Synergistic efficacy of extract of Jatropha curcas seeds and enzyme extract of Serratia marcescens against Spodoptera frugiperda under greenhouse conditions
Authors: Armando Valdez-Ramírez, María Eugenia de la Torre-Hernández, Antonio Flores-Macías, Juan Ramírez-Zamora, Norma Daniela García-Calderón, María Eugenia Nuñez-Valdez, Rodolfo Figueroa-Brito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study assessed the individual and combined effects of the acetonic extract from Jatropha curcas seeds (EAcJc) and the enzymatic extract of Serratia marcescens (EESm) on foliar damage caused by Spodoptera frugiperda larvae in maize plants under greenhouse conditions. In the first bioassay, maize plants were treated with EAcJc (0, 1000, and 2500 ppm), EESm (0, 150, and 300 ppm), and mixtures of EAcJc + EESm (0, 1000 + 300, and 2500 + 300 ppm) using a completely randomized design. In the second bioassay, the effect of varying application frequencies (0, 2, 3, and 4 times) of the EAcJc + EESm mixture at a fixed concentration of 2500 + 300 ppm was evaluated. By day 12, the treatments with EAcJc at 2500 ppm, EESm at 300 ppm, and the combinations of 1,000 + 300 ppm and 2500 + 300 ppm resulted in significant reductions in foliar damage of 51.5%, 50%, 49.0%, and 46.25%, respectively, compared to the control. Furthermore, the treatment mixture (2500 + 300 ppm) reduced damage by 58.0%, 57.5% and 56.5% after two, three and four applications, respectively. These findings highlight the potential of EAcJc and EESm, individually and in combination, as alternatives to synthetic chemical insecticides in controlling of S. frugiperda larvae.Keywords: Physic nut, Fall armyworm, Enzymatic extract, Botanical extract, Damage controlSubject terms: Agroecology, Agroecology

Introduction

The fall armyworm, Spodoptera frugiperda (J.E. Smith) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae), is an economically important pest that affects major cereal crops, causing significant damage and considerable losses in agricultural production1. It is a polyphagous and migratory pest, native to North and South America, which in recent decades has become an invasive species of major global significance2.

The primary method for controlling this pest is based on the application of synthetic chemical insecticides, with cypermethrin, a broad-spectrum pyrethroid, being the most widely used by producers in different regions of the world3. However, the prolonged reliance on pesticides for its control has contributed to the development of resistance to various insecticides by S. frugiperda in multiple regions4. In addition, these compounds exhibit high toxicity, posing serious risks to both human health and the environment. Among agricultural workers, direct or indirect exposure to these substances has been associated with various health issues, including diabetes, reproductive disorders, neurological dysfunction, cancer, and respiratory diseases5.

Given these challenges, contemporary research should prioritize the development of agroecological control strategies against Spodoptera frugiperda that ensure enhanced specificity, effectiveness, and environmental sustainability. Various studies have reported the use of botanical extracts and or secondary metabolites from plant species belonging to the Euphorbiaceae family as potential insect pest control agents.

Jatropha curcas L. (Euphorbiaceae) is a woody shrub widely distributed in tropical and subtropical regions. This species is capable of synthesizing flavonoid compounds and diterpenes that have demonstrated various biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, antiviral, antimicrobial, antifungal, and insecticidal properties6. It has been observed that the oil fraction of J. curcas, enriched with phorbol esters, exhibits contact toxicity, reduces food consumption, relative growth, and food conversion efficiency in third-instar larvae of S. frugiperda7. Additionally, hexanic and acetonic extracts obtained from J. curcas seeds have shown effective insecticidal and insectistatic activity for the control of S. frugiperda8. Moreover, methanolic extracts from fresh and dried J. curcas leaves have demonstrated insecticidal activity against S. frugiperda larvae9. Furthermore, the use of entomopathogenic bacteria that secrete chitinases has been investigated as a promising alternative for the development of bioinsecticides targeting S. frugiperda10.

One example is the potential of Serratia marcescens (Yersiniaceae), a bacterium with high genetic plasticity that allows it to adapt to and persist in multiple environments, including soil, water, and plants11,12. This bacterium secretes a wide range of chitinolytic enzymes, among which chitinases ChiA, B, C, and D are particularly notable13. These enzymes degrade the peritrophic membrane and the intestinal epithelium of insects, leading to the disruption of the gut barrier14. An example is the recombinant chitinase BL21 from S. marcescens, which has been shown to cause significant developmental disruptions and malformations in S. frugiperda15. Likewise, the endochitinase protein SmChiC, naturally present in S. marcescens strain Bar86 and overexpressed in transgenic plants, may play a crucial role in defense against herbivorous insects such as S. frugiperda, acting as an oral insecticide16.

Various studies have demonstrated the efficacy of botanical extracts from J. curcas and chitinases, or enzymatic extracts, derived from the bacterium S. marcescens in the biological control of S. frugiperda. However, to date, there is no documented evidence of a possible synergistic activity resulting from the combination of both types of extracts in the control of the fall armyworm. Therefore, the objective of the present study was to evaluate, both individually and in combination, the acetonic extract from J. curcas seeds and the enzymatic extract from S. marcescens in their effectiveness at reducing the percentage of damage to maize plants caused by S. frugiperda larvae under greenhouse conditions.

Materials and methods

Insect rearing

The larvae used in the bioassays were collected by random sampling from corn fields located in the “Los Limones” ejido, municipality of Yautepec, State of Morelos, Mexico (18°53′09″N, 99°03′38″W). The specimens showed no visible signs of disease or parasitism. Throughout their larval development, the larvae were fed an artificial diet consisting of 800 mL of distilled water, 60 g of commercial diet (Product No. F0635; S.W. Corn Borer®, Bio-Serv, Frenchtown®, NJ, USA), 20 g of sterile corn ear, 100 g of ground maize, 40 g of brewer’s yeast, 10 g of a vitamin mix specific for Lepidoptera (Bio-Serv®), 10 g of agar, 1.7 g of sorbic acid dissolved in 17 mL of ethanol, 2.5 mL of formaldehyde, 1.7 g of methyl p-hydroxybenzoate, and 0.6 g of neomycin sulfate17.

Adult insects were kept in pairs inside No. 3 Kraft paper bags (15 × 30 cm) and were fed with a solution of water and honey. The females laid eggs on the inner walls of the bags, which were checked daily to collect the eggs. These were transferred to 4 oz plastic containers with snap-on lids and maintained at a temperature of 24 ± 2 °C and a relative humidity of 60 ± 5% for a period of four days until hatching. The newly hatched larvae were fed the same artificial diet previously described and kept in a growth chamber under the same conditions, with a 12:12 h light-dark photoperiod. Fourth generation (F4) larvae were used for this study18.

Acetonic extract of Jatropha Curcas seeds

J. curcas seeds were collected from wild plants at the experimental field of the Atencingo Sugar Mill, in the state of Puebla, Mexico (18°30′51.49″ N, 98°36′26.59″ W). The seeds were identified by plant taxonomy expert Gabriel Flores Franco, a biologist, and the reference specimen of this plant is publicly available in the herbarium of the Autonomous University of the State of Morelos, Mexico, with voucher number 436,644.

The seeds were then shade-dried at room temperature (26 °C) for 24 h in the Entomology Laboratory of the Center for the Development of Biotic Products of the National Polytechnic Institute (CeProBi). The seeds were disinfected with a 1% sodium hypochlorite solution and rinsed with distilled water. Next, 500 g of healthy seeds were selected and ground in a laboratory mill (KMF10 Basic®; Wilmington, USA) until a fine powder was obtained, with a final weight of 457 g.

The extract was obtained using the maceration method, employing technical-grade acetone (J.T. Bayer®; Aguascalientes, Mexico) as the solvent, with a seed-to-acetone ratio of 1:5. The mixture was left to rest for 72 h in a fume hood. After this period, it was vacuum filtered using Whatman No. 1 filter paper. Subsequently, the mixture was concentrated in a rotary evaporator (Büchi® R-205; Hamton, USA) at a reduced pressure of 530 mbar and 44 °C for 10 min to remove the solvent, yielding 250 mL of oily extract, which was poured into a 300 mL Erlenmeyer flask. Finally, the sample was left to dry in a fume hood for 12 h to ensure the complete removal of the solvent19.

Serratia marcescens

Strain 81 of S. marcescens was provided by the Molecular Pathogenicity Laboratory of the Center for Research in Cellular Dynamics at the Autonomous University of the State of Morelos, Mexico. This bacterium was cultured at 30 °C on solid Luria-Bertani (LB) medium, composed of 10 g L⁻¹ peptone, 5 g L⁻¹ yeast extract, 10 g L⁻¹ NaCl, and 15 g L⁻¹ agar, adjusted to pH 7 (Sigma-Aldrich®, USA).

Preparation of colloidal Chitin

For the preparation of the inducer medium for chitinase production, colloidal chitin was used as the sole carbon source. It was obtained through a three-step procedure: (1) Demineralization: Commercial chitin powder (Sigma-Aldrich®; Saint Louis, USA) was treated with a 0.6 N hydrochloric acid solution at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 11:1 (100 g of chitin powder in 1,100 mL HCl) for 3 h at room temperature, (2) Deproteinization: The demineralized chitin was treated with a 1% sodium hydroxide solution for 24 h, also at room temperature (24 °C). The resulting product was washed with distilled water to remove chemical residues and then dried at 32 °C for 30 minutes20 and (3) Colloidal chitin was prepared by a modification of the method proposed by Hsu and Lockwood21.

This procedure consisted of dissolving 80 g of previously demineralized and deproteinized chitin in 400 mL of concentrated hydrochloric acid (HCl), under constant stirring for 50 min. Subsequently, 2 L of cold distilled water was added, and the mixture was kept under continuous stirring for 18 h at room temperature. Then, vacuum filtration was performed using Whatman No. 1 filter paper, followed by successive washes with distilled water until the acid was completely removed and a neutral pH (pH 7) was reached. The resulting paste was sterilized for 30 min at 15 psi and stored at 4 °C in a 300 mL amber bottle22.

Production of chitinases from Serratia marcescens

The inoculum of S. marcescens strain 81 was activated in liquid Luria-Bertani (LB) medium, composed of tryptone (C11H12N2O2) 10 g L⁻¹, yeast extract 5 g L⁻¹, and NaCl 10 g L⁻¹. The culture was incubated at 30 °C with continuous orbital shaking at 250 rpm for 24 h. Subsequently, the inoculum was transferred to an enriched medium (50 mL) containing yeast extract 0.025 g L⁻¹, MgSO4 0.05 g L⁻¹, NaH2PO4 0.068 g L⁻¹, and colloidal chitin 1 g L⁻¹, adjusted to pH 7, which was maintained at 30 °C with constant orbital shaking at 250 rpm for 24 h.

The enriched culture was used as inoculum for the chitinase production medium (100 mL), composed of K₂HPO₄ 0.1 g L⁻¹, MgO₄ 0.05 g L⁻¹, and colloidal chitin 2 g L⁻¹, adjusted to pH 7. Incubation was carried out at 30 °C with constant shaking at 250 rpm for five days. At the end of the incubation period, the culture was centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min to obtain cell-free supernatant. The extracellular protein concentration was determined using the Lowry method23.

Chitinolytic activity assay

The Sigma-Aldrich® chitinase assay kit (St. Louis, MO, USA; Cat. No: CS0980-1KT) was used following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol to evaluate the chitinolytic activity of the enzymatic extract obtained from S. marcescens. The kit includes three soluble substrates: 4-nitrophenyl N-acetyl, N-acetyl-β-D-glucosaminide and 4-nitrophenyl N, N’-diacetyl-β-D-chitobioside, used to detect exochitinase activity, as well as 4-nitrophenyl β-D-N, N’, N’’-triacetylchitotriose to determine endochitinase activity.

Chitinase from Trichoderma viride (Hypocreaceae), included in the kit, was used as a positive control. Blank samples were prepared by mixing each of the soluble substrates with the stop solution (sodium carbonate). The test samples, corresponding to the enzymatic extract of S. marcescens, were analyzed in duplicate.

One unit of chitinolytic activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to release 1 µmol of p-nitrophenol per minute from each corresponding substrate. The assays were carried out at pH 4.8 and 37 °C for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 405 nm using an ELISA microplate reader (Angilent®; BioTek Epoch/2; Santa Clara, CA, USA). Enzymatic activity, expressed in units per milliliter of cell-free supernatant, was calculated using the formula provided by the manufacturer.

Greenhouse bioassays

The evaluation was carried out in the greenhouse of the Center for the Development of Biotic Products of the National Polytechnic Institute (CeProBi-IPN), located at coordinates 18°49’29.7’’N, 99°05’41.5’’W, during April 2024. For the establishment of the experiment, black polyethylene bags measuring 25 × 15 cm (height x width) were used, which were filled with a 1:1 (kg kg-1) mixture of perlite and compost soil. Two seeds of maize (PROASE®; Zapata 10 variety) were sown in each bag.

When the plants reached the V6 phenological stage, thinning was carried out, leaving one plant per bag. The plants were arranged in a completely randomized experimental design, with 10 plants per treatment and three replicates, resulting in a total of 30 plants per treatment. Once the maize plants reached an average height of 15 cm, corresponding to the V8 phenological stage, a 24-hour-old S. frugiperda larva was placed on the whorl of each plant. Subsequently, treatments were applied by spraying, using a 250 mL capacity hand sprayer, to which 50 mL of distilled water was added per treatment.

The treatments evaluated were as follows: acetonic extract of J. curcas seeds (EAcJc) at concentrations of 1,000 and 2,500 ppm; enzymatic extract of S. marcescens (EESm) at concentrations of 150 and 300 ppm; and the combination of both treatments (EAcJc + EESm) at concentrations of 1,000 + 300 ppm and 2,500 + 300 ppm. Distilled water (50 mL) was used as the negative control, and the botanical insecticide EPA90® at 200 ppm (20 µL in 100 mL), formulated with soybean seed oil (Biokrone®, Mexico), was used as the positive control.

The variable evaluated was the percentage of damage to maize plants caused by S. frugiperda larvae, following the criteria established in Mihm’s damage rating scale24. Evaluations were conducted every third day throughout the insect’s larval cycle.

In a second bioassay, conducted during May 2024, only the most effective treatment for protecting maize plants was evaluated. This treatment corresponded to the combination of acetone extract of J. curcas and enzymatic extract of S. marcescens (EAcJc + EESm) at a concentration of 2,500 + 300 ppm. This treatment was applied at three different frequencies: 2, 3, and 4 applications, carried out every third day by direct spraying onto the maize plants. The methodology used in this second bioassay was the same as that used in Bioassay 1.

Statistical analysis

The evaluated variable, corresponding to the estimated percentage of damage in maize plants, was subjected to tests for normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and homoscedasticity of variances (Bartlett’s test). However, since the assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity were not met, the data were analyzed using the non-parametric Kruskal-Walli’s test, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test to identify significant differences between the treatments and the control group. Dunn’s test was adjusted for multiple comparisons using corrected p-values, considering a significance level of p < 0.05. Additionally, a simple linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate the relationship between application frequency and the observed response. Statistical analysis, as well as the linear regression and graph generation, were conducted using Prism version 10 software (GraphPad, Inc., USA)25.

Results

Endo- and exochitinase activity

A total of 300 mL of cell-free enzymatic extract from S. marcescens was obtained, with a protein concentration of 427 µg mL-1. This extract demonstrated chitinolytic activity, exhibiting both endochitinase and exochitinase activities on different specific substrates. Regarding exochitinase activity, values of 0.0549 ± 0.0012 U mL-1 were observed on the substrates 4-Nitrophenyl N-acetyl β-D-glucosaminide and 0.1565 ± 0.0010 mL-1 on 4-Nitrophenyl N, N’-diacetyl-β-D-chitobioside. On the other hand, the recorded endochitinase activity was 0.0603 ± 0.0008 U mL-1 using 4-Nitrophenyl β-D-N, N’, N’-triacetylchitotriose as the substrate.

Greenhouse bioassays

At 4 and 6 days of exposure, maize plants treated with the combination of acetonic extract of J. curcas and enzymatic extract of S. marcescens (EAcJc + EESm) at concentrations of 2,500 + 300 ppm showed damage percentages of 19% and 30.5%, respectively. Similarly, the combination at 1,000 + 300 ppm exhibited values of 20% and 29%. The treatment with EESm at 300 ppm recorded 23.5% and 32%, while the botanical insecticide EPA® showed damage of 26.5% and 33% at 4 and 6 days, respectively. All these treatments showed statistically significant differences compared to the control group, which reached damage percentages of 33% and 41.5% during the same periods (Figs. 1a, b, and c; Table 1). Similarly, the individual treatments of EAcJc at 2,500 ppm and EESm at 150 ppm reduced leaf damage to 20.5% and 29% at 4 and 6 days, respectively, significantly different as compared to the control (Fig. 1a and b; Table 1).

A similar effect was observed at 12 days of exposure. Maize plants treated with the combination of acetonic extract of J. curcas and enzymatic extract of S. marcescens (EAcJc + EESm) at concentrations of 2,500 + 300 ppm and 1,000 + 300 ppm showed damage percentages of 46% and 49%, respectively, caused by S. frugiperda larvae. During the 8 and 10 days of exposure, the mixture of acetonic extract of J. curcas and enzymatic extract of S. marcescens (EAcJc + EESm) at a concentration of 2,500 + 300 ppm, applied four times, was the most effective, reducing the percentage of damage caused by S. frugiperda larvae on maize plants to 38% and 49.5%, respectively. These values were lower than those recorded with the botanical insecticide EPA® (47% and 59.5%) and significantly lower compared to the control group (64.5% and 75.5%) (Fig. 1d; Table 2). Ranked second in efficacy were the same concentrations of EAcJc + EESm, following three and two applications, respectively, which reduced damage to maize to 39% and 43% at 8 days, and to 50% in both cases at 10 days. These treatments were statistically similar to EPA®, but significantly different from the control group (Fig. 1d; Table 2).

During 12 days of exposure, maize plants sprayed with the combination of acetonic extract of J. curcas and enzymatic extract of S. marcescens (EAcJc + EESm) at a concentration of 2,500 + 300 ppm, applied two, three, and four times, showed damage percentages ranging from 56.5% to 58%, values comparable to 64% those observed with the botanical insecticide EPA®. In contrast, plants in the control group recorded damage exceeding 84.5% (Fig. 1d; Table 2).

Discussion

In the present study, the cell-free enzymatic extract of S. marcescens showed both endochitinase and exochitinase activity. The activity of these chitinase enzymes is related to their ability to degrade chitin by breaking down the polymer. Endochitinase acts on the internal bonds of the chitin chain, generating larger products, whereas exochitinase acts on the ends of the polymer, releasing smaller products. The combined activity of endo- and exo-chitinases can have a significant impact on S. frugiperda, as these enzymes degrade chitin, an essential component of the insect’s exoskeleton. This degradation causes direct damage to the pest, affecting its survival and development by weakening the structural integrity of the exoskeleton and increasing its vulnerability to other threats. Therefore, the application of S. marcescens strains that produce chitinases represents a potentially effective strategy for the biological control of this pest26.

Throughout the entire exposure period, the plants sprayed with the highest concentration of the combination of the acetonic extract of J. curcas and the enzymatic extract of S marcescens (EAcJc + EESm) showed the lowest percentage of damage caused by S. frugiperda (fall armyworm) larvae. This mixture proved to be even more effective than the botanical insecticide EPA®.

In the present study, the application of the highest concentration of the extract mixture on two, three, and four occasions on maize plants reduced the damage caused by S. frugiperda larvae by more than 26% compared to the control treatment, and by 8% compared to the botanical insecticide EPA®. These results demonstrate a progressive reduction in plant damage as both the concentration and exposure time to the J. curcas and S. marcescens extract mixture increase, highlighting its potential as a biological alternative for the control of S. frugiperda.

In this study, over the entire exposure period (12 days), corn plants treated with both concentrations (2,500 and 1,000 ppm) of the acetonic extract from J. curcas seeds (EAcJc) showed a reduction in damage caused by S. frugiperda larvae of more than 27% and eighteen%,18ively, compared to the control. Similar plant protection was observed with the application of an aqueous extract of J. curcas with an emulsifier. Plants in the experimental plots showed S. frugiperda larval infestations of twenty-two and twenty-four%, resulting in low infestation rates per plant27.

Likewise, the application of a formulation based on J. curcas oil at a concentration of 15 mL L⁻¹ resulted in a mortality rate of 34% after 48 h and 50% after 72 h in S. frugiperda larvae28. A low percentage of plant damage has also been reported with the use of the acetonic extract from J. curcas kernel, which showed significant control of damage in cabbage plants, with a 28% reduction attributed to larvae of Copitarsia decolora (Guenée) (Noctuidae)18.

In the present study, treatment with EAcJc at a concentration of 1,000 ppm maintained a low percentage of damage in maize plants, recording only 13% compared to the negative control on day twelve of observation. In the previous study, GC-MS analysis demonstrated the presence of seven major fatty acid compounds in oils derived from J. curcas seeds: linoleic, oleic, palmitic, capric, lauric, stearic, and myristic29. S. frugiperda larvae can be affected by the fatty acid composition of their food sources, making the study of fatty acid profiles important for agricultural practices, as the fatty acids caused significant insectistatic activity, reduced larval and pupal weight, and delayed their development by 4 to 10 days8. On the other hand, it has been demonstrated that the aqueous extract of J. curcas seeds reduces populations of Helicoverpa armigera (Hübner) and Thysanoplusia orichalcea (Fabricius) (Noctuidae) larvae in chickpea crops30. Similarly, antifeedant activity has been observed in Spodoptera litura (Fabricius) (Noctuidae) and Spilarctia obliqua (Walker) (Arctiidae) larvae using hexanic and diethyl ether extracts obtained from J. curcas seeds31.

In the present study, the enzymatic extract obtained from S. marcescens (EESm) showed both endo- and exochitinase activity on the substrates glucosamine, chitobiose, and triacetylchitobiose. Similar activities have previously been reported in the SEN strain of S. marcescens32. Exochitinase chitobiose activity has also been observed in the microorganism Chitiniphilus shinanonensis SAY3T (Neisseriaceae)33. The simultaneous production of various chitinolytic enzymes by a single bacterium enhances the synergistic and efficient degradation of chitin34.

The enzymatic extract of S. marcescens (EESm) at concentrations of 150 and 300 ppm showed greater control over S. frugiperda larvae, reducing plant damage in maize by more than 29% by the sixth day of observation. Furthermore, by day twelve, a reduction of over 50% in plant damage was observed compared to the control.

It can be speculated that S. frugiperda larvae, upon ingesting the chitinases present on the treated leaves, may have experienced alterations in the peritrophic membrane, which could have interfered with their feeding capacity. This type of toxic effect has also been reported for the enzymatic extract with chitinolytic activity from Bacillus thuringiensis (Bacillaceae), strain T04A001, in larvae of Spodoptera exigua (Hübner) (Noctuidae)35. Similarly, it has been demonstrated that the endochitinase ChiAII from S. marcescens degrades the peritrophic membrane of Spodoptera littoralis (Boisduval) (Noctuidae)36. In another study, greater control of damage in maize plants was observed through the use of a recombinant type B chitinase from S. marcescens strain NRC408, which was able to reduce S. frugiperda larval and pupal populations by sixty-five and eighty-eight%, respectively15. Similarly, it has been reported that chitinases produced by S. marcescens are capable of degrading both the exoskeleton and the intestinal lining of S. litura, acting as part of its pathogenicity mechanism. These enzymes affect the digestive system of the larvae, significantly increasing their mortality rate37.

On the other hand, a significant reduction in the feeding capacity of S. frugiperda larvae has been reported in maize plants inoculated with Bacillus amyloliquefaciens (Bacillaceae), an organism that produces hydrolytic enzymes such as protease, pectinase, chitinase, and lipase highlighting its potential as a bioprotective agent38.

In the present study, it was shown that the combined application of EAcJc + EESm significantly reduced foliar damage, mainly due to the synergistic interaction of their bioactive components (fatty acids and chitinases). This synergy was also observed in a previous in vitro study where both treatments showed insecticidal and insectistatic activity on S. frugiperda29. Furthermore, treatments based on plant extracts have been shown to alter larval feeding behavior, resulting in reduced consumption or total elimination of treated plant tissues19. For example, oleic and linoleic acids derived from J. curcas have been reported to significantly reduce S. frugiperda larval weight8, indicating a potential antifeedant effect. Furthermore, several fatty acids present in J. curcas, such as oleic, palmitic, and stearic acids, can exert direct toxic effects on the larvae. These compounds can affect the nervous or metabolic system, causing immediate or progressive inhibition of feeding and ultimately a reduction in larval viability when ingested in high concentrations19,29,39.

The results obtained in the present study demonstrate a synergistic effect between EAcJc and EESm in the control of S. frugiperda, which is consistent with findings from other studies. For example, synergistic activity has been observed between the acetonic extract of J. curcas seeds and the metabolites of Beauveria bassiana (Cordycipitaceae), which affected the development of larvae and pupae and caused 100% mortality in S. frugiperda39. The synergistic combination of the botanical extract of J. curcas and the enzymatic extract of the entomopathogenic microorganism S. marcescens represents a promising strategy for the control of the fall armyworm, S. frugiperda. This joint formulation integrates the insecticidal activity of the plant extract with the enzymatic capability of the bacterium to degrade key insect structures, even contributing to its death.

Synergism has also been reported between chitinolytic enzymes from S. marcescens strain SU05 doped with nanosilica, in combination with ethanolic extract of Azadirachta indica A. Juss. (Meliaceae) leaves, achieving a cumulative mortality of 100% in third and fourth instar larvae of S. litura (Noctuidae)40. Additionally, J. curcas oil has shown the ability to synergize synthetic pyrethroids such as cypermethrin, lambda-cyhalothrin, and alphamethrin by increasing their insecticidal activity and reducing LT₅₀ values, indicating an accelerated action of these compounds. This finding highlights the potential of J. curcas vegetable oil to enhance the toxicity of synthetic insecticides against S. litura41.

The combined effect of both agents produces a response superior to that achieved individually, demonstrating a synergism that enhances the effectiveness of biological control. This strategy not only increases the efficiency of pest management but also reduces dependence on synthetic pesticides and may help mitigate the development of resistance in S. frugiperda. To achieve sustainable and successful long-term control, it is essential to explore innovative approaches that integrate biocontrol agents with different modes of action42. In this regard, the combination of tools with distinct mechanisms of action has proveen effective in delaying the evolution of resistance in pest insects43.

Conclusions

The results obtained in the present study determined that treatments with the acetonic extract of Jatropha curcas (EAcJc) and the enzymatic extract of Serratia marcescens (EESm), when applied individually, were effective in reducing the percentage of damage to maize plants caused by Spodoptera frugiperda larvae. Likewise, the combination of both treatments (EAcJc + EESm) exhibited a synergistic effect, increasing the efficacy in controlling plant damage over a two-week period. Therefore, the joint application of EAcJc and EESm can be considered a viable alternative for the agroecological control of the fall armyworm, offering effective protection to maize plants under greenhouse conditions. However, further research is needed, including bioassays under controlled and field conditions, as well as studies aimed at elucidating the specific mechanisms of action of these treatments on S. frugiperda.
