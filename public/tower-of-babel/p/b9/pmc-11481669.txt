Evaluating the impact of treatment sequencing on outcomes in hepatocellular carcinoma: a comparative analysis of TACE and systemic therapies
Authors: XingRong Zheng, Xin Song, BoXiang Zhang, XiYao Chen, YeQiong Zhang, QiuMin Luo, ZhiPeng Li, ZheXuan Deng, RuiXuan Xu, Liang Peng, Chan Xie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate how the timing of transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) relative to systemic therapy (tyrosine-kinase inhibitors [TKIs] and immune checkpoint inhibitors [ICIs]) influences oncological outcomes in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). A retrospective analysis was conducted on HCC patients treated with TACE plus TKIs and ICIs from January 2018 to February 2023. We compared objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), overall survival (OS), and progression-free survival (PFS) between patients receiving TACE before versus after systemic therapies. Multivariate Cox regression analyses identified potential prognostic factors. Of the 194 patients enrolled, 111 received TACE before systemic therapies, and 83 after. The median age at diagnosis was 52.8 years. There were no significant differences in ORR (40.72% vs. 30.41%, p = 0.989) or DCR (48.45% vs. 35.57%, p = 0.770) between the groups. Likewise, OS (18.73 vs. 18.20 months, p = 0.091) and PFS (11.53 vs. 10.05 months, p = 0.336) were similar regardless of treatment sequence. In the result of Cox analysis, a 20% decrease in AFP from baseline at one month was associated with improved OS (HR = 0.35, 95% CI 0.17–0.70, p = 0.003) and PFS (HR = 0.69, 95% CI 0.49–0.96, p = 0.028). Large tumor size (≥ 10 cm) was a poor prognostic factor for OS (HR = 2.12, 95% CI 1.07–4.21, p = 0.032), and the presence of portal vein tumor thrombus adversely affected PFS (HR = 2.31, 95% CI 1.47–3.62, p < 0.001). The sequencing of TACE and systemic therapies does not significantly impact the prognosis of advanced HCC. Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most prevalent primary liver cancer and the third cause of cancer-related death worldwide [1]. There were approximately 906,000 new cases and 830,000 deaths worldwide in 2020, ranking the third in morbidity and the second in mortality among digestive malignancies while more than half of the cases were found in China [1]. The locoregional treatment of transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) has been recommended for the treatment of intermediate and advanced-stage HCC in clinical practice guidelines [2–4]. The efficacy of TACE could be infected by tumor burden, liver function, liver cirrhosis and other factors with a median overall survival (OS) of 19 ~ 37 months [5]. Although TACE rapidly reduces tumor burden, it can also induce hypoxia of tumor environment which leads to up-regulation of HIF-1/2α and expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and promotes angiogenesis and tumor intrahepatic metastasis [5, 6]. TACE can regulate the apoptosis of tumor cells and inhibit the proliferation of tumor cells, but it can also change the tumor microenvironment, so it is necessary to combine targeted and immunotherapy to further improve the therapeutic effect [7].

Tyrosine-kinase inhibitors (TKIs) including sorafenib, lenvatinib, donafini, target protein kinases such as VEGF and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) to inhibit the tumor proliferation and metastasis. These agents are approved as first-line treatments for advanced HCC (aHCC) [2]. Multiple studies have showed sorafenib or lenvatinib plus TACE can prolong OS and progress free survival (PFS) in aHCC [8–10]. However, the survival benefits remain modest. Recent advancements in immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have demonstrated promising efficacy and safety in phase I and II clinical trials for aHCC treatment. [11–13]. Yet, phase III studies such as Checkmate 459 and KEYNOTE-240 did not find anti-programmed death-1 (anti-PD-1) monotherapy superior to standard therapy [14, 15]. Therefore, exploration of combined therapies including TKIs and ICIs were conducted with encouraging outcomes. Atezolizumab combined with bevacizumab as well as sintilimab plus a bevacizumab biosimilar (IBI305) has shown better survival outcomes than sorafenib with controllable adverse events in unresectable HCC which has been recommended as first-line option [16, 17].

Moreover, while TACE causes local tumor necrosis enabling effector T cells to recognize and destroy tumor cells, it also increases VEGF levels and PD1/PDL1 expression in the hypoxic environment. [18]. TKIs can normalize blood vessels and inhibit the VEGF signaling pathway, enhancing dendritic cell (DC) function and promoting immune cell infiltration, thereby boosting the effectiveness of immunotherapies. [19]. ICIs blocks the immunosuppressive response mediated by blocking PD1/PDL1 pathway, activates the exhausted T cells, and restores the anti-tumor effect of T cells. Numerous studies suggest a potential synergistic anti-tumor effect when combining TKIs and ICIs with TACE [20–22]. However, the impact of the sequence of TACE and systemic therapies on prognosis and tumor response in HCC has not been thoroughly investigated.

Herein, this retrospective study aims to analyze the differences in tumor response and survival outcomes among HCC patients who received TACE in combination with TKIs and ICIs in real-world settings.

Patients and methods

Study design and patient screen

This was a retrospective study that evaluated HCC patients from January 2018 to February 2023 who were treated with triple therapies including TACE, TKIs plus ICIs. Patients had a follow-up period of over one year, with the data cutoff on February 1, 2024. The study protocol conformed to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki as reflected in a priori approval by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients diagnosed with BCLC stage C of HCC with pathological or radiology tests and available biochemical data; (2) patients aged between 18 and 75 years; (3) treated with TACE, TKIs plus ICIs with Child–Pugh class A or B at baseline. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who received surgery or ablation or radiotherapy at baseline; (2) the interval time between the first course of TACE and systemic therapies was more than 30 days; (3) with other malignancies; (4) patients who were administered with other immunosuppressive drugs at baseline; (5) patients who had hepatic encephalopathy, severe ascites, esophageal gastric variceal bleeding; (6) patients with other autoimmune diseases; (7) patients with incomplete laboratory data.

Definition and treatment assessment

HCC diagnosis was confirmed after comprehensive tests which included pathological, radiological, and biochemical tests according to the HCC guidelines of the European Association for the Study of Liver or American Association for the Study of Liver Disease guidelines [23, 24]. Albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) grade, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) were also defined based on previous reports [25, 26]. In addition, radiological imaging was used to assess tumor response by modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST) based on contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scans or MRI every two months after the triple therapies by two diagnostic radiologists [27]. The survival outcomes including PFS, which was defined as time from the first treatment of TACE or TKIs or ICIs to the first report of progressive disease according to mRECIST or report of death from medical records. OS was defined as the time from the first treatment of TACE or TKIs or ICIs to death. The exact time of OS was collected from medical records or following up by telephone. The definition of objective response rate (ORR) was defined as the proportion of patients achieving complete response (CR) or partial response (PR). Disease control rate (DCR) was defined as the rate of objective response plus stable disease (SD).

Treatment procedure

TACE procedures

The TACE procedures were performed with a 4-F RH catheter (Cook, Bloomington, Ind) and microcatheter (Renegade, Boston Scientific, Natick, Mass; Progreat, Terumo, Tokyo, Japan). Hepatic arteriography was performed to identify the accessory arteries, then the TACE procedure was performed super-selectively in the tumor feeding arteries. Intra-arterial administration consisted of a 100–300 μm Calisphere vial (Jiangsu Hengrui Medicine Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) loaded with 40–60 mg of epirubicin (Pharmorubicin; Pfizer, Wuxi, China). Embolization was stopped following stasis of the contrast agent flow. When needed, further embolization was performed with Embosphere microspheres (100–300 mm) (Merit Medical Systems, Inc., South Jordan, UT, USA) to achieve stasis.

Administration of systemic therapies

Systemic therapies, including oral TKIs and intravenous ICIs, were initiated within 30 days before or after TACE therapy based on the liver function with Child–Pugh class A or B. Oral targeted drugs included sorafenib (Bayer AG Co., Ltd., German), lenvatinib (Weicai Medicine Co., Ltd., Japan), donafenib (Suzhou Zejing Medicine Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China), apatinib (Jiangsu Hengrui Medicine Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China), anlotinib (Jiangsu Zhengda Tianqing Medicine Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) and intravenous drug bevacizumab (Roche Registration GmbH, Switzerland) targets at VEGFR were administered based on the recommended dosage. Intravenous administration of ICIs prescribed in the study cohort included pembrolizumab (MSD Ireland (Carlow), USA), nivolumab (Bristol-Myers Squibb Pharma EEIG, England), camrelizumab (Jiangsu Hengrui Medicine Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China), sintilimab (Innovent Biologics, Suzhou, China), toripalimab (Shanghai Junshi Medicine Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), and tislelizumab (Guangzhou Baiji Medicine Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) with 200 mg every 3 weeks. Atezolizumab (Roche Registration GmbH, Switzerland) was also prescribed with 1200 mg every 3 weeks.

Data collection and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) (version 24.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), R software (version 4.0.2, Vienna University of Economics and Business, Vienna, Austria). The measurement data in accordance with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), the comparison between two groups was performed by independent sample t test. The data in non-normal distribution were expressed by Median (Q1, Q3), and the comparison between two groups was performed by ANOVA or Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages, and differences between groups were analyzed by ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test. Survival curves of OS and PFS were visualized by the Kaplan–Meier method with the log-rank test. Clinical data including age, gender, liver cirrhosis, HBeAg status, interval time between TACE and systemic therapy, AFP level and other values were analyzed in the univariate and multivariate Cox analyses to determine the potential prognostic factors. The factors with a statistically significant p < 0.10 in the univariate analysis were further entered into a Cox proportion hazards regression model to reveal the independent predictors. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant based on the two-tailed hypothesis tests.

Survival outcomes and subgroup analysis

The median follow-up time was 16.30 months. Out of 194 patients, 110 had complete OS data, and all had PFS data. At one-year follow-up, 133 patients (68.56%) survived. Median OS was 18.73 months (95% CI 16.587–21.521) for the 'TACE before' group and 18.20 months (95% CI 17.562–23.416) for the 'TACE after' group, with no significant difference (p = 0.091, Fig. 2A). Median PFS was 11.53 months (95% CI 9.936–13.119) for the 'TACE before' group versus 10.05 months (95% CI 8.291–11.811) for the 'TACE after' group (p = 0.336, Fig. 2B).

To further understand whether the interval time between first treatment of TACE and systemic therapies, whether treatment of anti-PD1 and anti-PDL1, small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors and anti-VEGFR monoclonal antibody could have any impact on HCC survival prognosis, we performed subgroup analysis. From the results in Fig. 3, we can find that whether the interval time between TACE and systemic therapies more or less than one week, more or less than two weeks, there were no significant differences in OS and PFS (all p > 0.05, Fig. 3A–D).

In our HCC cohort, 32 (16.49%) patients received anti-PDL1 drugs. We further explored the differences of OS, PFS in patients treated with or without anti-PDL1. To what we expected, no differences were revealed (all p > 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). In addition, 35 (18.04%) patients received anti-VEGFR monoclonal antibody (bevacizumab or bevacizumab biosimilar (IBI305) drugs. We also found no differences of OS and PFS whether patients received anti-VEGFR monoclonal antibody (all p > 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 1C, D).

Further subgroup analysis was focusing on baseline AFP level, tumor stage and other factors on tumor prognosis. The results showed that presence of PVTT (Fig. 4A, B) and a 20% decrease in AFP (Fig. 4C, D) were associated with OS and PFS. Patients with PVTT had worse OS (HR 2.45, 95% CI 1.02–5.89, p = 0.044) and PFS (HR 2.23, 95%CI:1.43–3.50, p < 0.001). The prognosis was better in patients whose AFP decreased by 20% at 1 month (OS, HR 0.44, 95% CI 0.23–0.83, p = 0.012; PFS, HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.51–0.98, p = 0.040).

However, we found no differences of OS and PFS in patients with TNM III or IV stage (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B), baseline AFP level more or less than 400 ng/ml (all p > 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 2C, D) as well as tumor size more or less than 10 cm or tumor numbers more or less than three (all p > 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 2E–H).

Univariate and multivariate Cox analyses

Clinical parameters (shown in Table 3) were analyzed to identify potential risk factors affecting survival prognosis. Since the collinearity between the two indicators (anti-PDL1, anti-vEGFR) was greater than 0.8, one of them as anti-vEGFR was selected for further analysis. In the univariate analysis, variables such as age, baseline HBVDNA level, TACE cycles, tumor numbers, anti-vEGFR therapy, TNM stage, CTP class or NLR, PLR, ALBI grade (all p > 0.1 in the univariate analysis) did not show any significant association with OS. However, eight parameters: gender, presence of cirrhosis, treatment sequence of TACE and systemic therapies, HBeAg status, baseline AFP level (greater than 400 ng/ml vs. 400 ng/ml or less), 20% decrease in AFP from baseline at one month, tumor size (10 cm or larger vs. less than 10 cm), and presence of portal vein tumor thrombus (PVTT), showed p-values less than 0.1 in the univariate analysis and were included in the multivariate analysis. Multivariate analysis revealed that a 20% decrease in AFP from baseline at one month was a protective factor for HCC survival prognosis (HR = 0.35, 95% CI 0.17–0.70, p = 0.003). Conversely, a tumor size of 10 cm or larger was identified as a poor prognostic factor (HR = 2.12, 95% CI 1.07–4.21, p = 0.032). No significant associations were found between OS and the use of anti-PDL1 or the presence of PVTT (Table 3).

Discussion

The rapid evolution of targeted and immunotherapeutic strategies has significantly impacted the management of advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). The integration of these therapies with transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) offers a promising approach but also introduces complexities regarding optimal sequencing and combination strategies. This study aimed to address a critical gap in current clinical practice guidelines: the optimal sequence of TACE and systemic therapies. Our findings suggest that the sequence of initiating TACE relative to systemic therapies (targeted drugs and immunotherapies) does not significantly impact overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), objective response rate (ORR), or disease control rate (DCR) in patients with advanced HCC who have not received prior radical or other local treatments. These results challenge the hypothesis that the order of these interventions might influence clinical outcomes and suggest a more flexible approach to treatment planning based on patient-specific factors rather than a fixed sequence.

To our knowledge, the “Cooperative Model” of TACE in combination with targeted, immunotherapy is superior to either TACE alone or targeted immunotherapy alone in both random clinical trials and the real-world practices [8, 20, 28, 29]. Firstly, TACE can induce tumor cell necrosis which inducing hypoxia microenvironment as well as increasing expression of VEGF [30]. As a result, anti-angiogenic therapy (TKIs or antibodies against VEGF) may delay revascularization and recurrence of tumors after TACE. Secondly, TACE can induce the release of tumor antigen by inducing immunogenic cell death locally in HCC and further enhance the immune response in the immunosuppressive environment of liver [31]. Thirdly, angiogenesis is closely related to anti-tumor immunosuppression. VEGF can contribute to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by hindering dendritic cell maturation and function, increasing recruitment of T regulatory cells and myeloid suppressor cells [32]. Targeting VEGF restores the antitumor activity of immune checkpoint inhibitors and enhances the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors. Therefore, there may be synergistic effects between TACE and systemic therapy, and the combination of them may present the effect of “1 + 1 + 1 > 3”, thus achieving better therapeutic effect.

Based on the theories described above, patients who received TACE first and systemic therapy later may have better outcomes. In a research led by professor Cheng Shuqun which enrolled 21 HCC patients in stage B of BCLC not suitable for surgical treatment receiving TACE for 5–10 days followed by initiation with atezolizumab combined with bevacizumab (fixed dose of atezolizumab 1200 mg injection, bevacizumab 15 mg/kg, once every 21 days), they found that patients showed an encouraging outcome with an ORR of 61.9% and a DCR of 100% assessed by mRECIST with median OS and PFS have not been reached [33]. And in the CHANCE001 research, the results showed that TACE combined with PD-(L)1 inhibitor plus TKIs significantly improved PFS, OS and ORR in patients with advanced HCC compared with TACE alone [20]. But the time frame criteria for combination therapy were defined as TACE administered up to 60 days before the administration of a PD-(L)1 inhibitor or concurrent treatment, and TKIs used in conjunction with TACE or a PD-(L)1 inhibitor. In a single-arm, multicenter, prospective study which performed in HCC patients treated with TACE in 3 days before lenvatinib and camrelizumab had demonstrated that the ORR was 76.4% and the DCR was 85.5% with a manageable safety [34]. Xin et al. [29] conducted a study in patients with unresectable HCC and found triple therapy with lenvatinib, a PD-1 inhibitor, and TACE may confer a greater survival benefit than dual therapy with lenvatinib and a PD-1 inhibitor with systemic therapy was performed within one month before and after the first TACE if the patient received triple therapy. In these studies, although the authors had confirmed the efficacy of triple therapy, but they did not further analyze whether the time sequence of TACE and systemic therapies would affect the outcomes.

However, some scholars had put forward different hypotheses that target drugs such as TKIs or anti-VEGFR or anti-PD-1 should be used before TACE. The main reasons are as follows. Firstly, after the administration of targeted drugs or anti-PD-1, the abnormal hepatic tumor blood vessels will gradually normalize, which is beneficial to the delivery of drugs and embolic agents during the follow-up TACE treatment [35, 36]. ICI can also have vessel-normalizing effects in preclinical models of breast, colorectal cancers [37, 38]. Secondly, TACE can stimulate the angiogenesis of patients, and the use of targeted drugs can inhibit the angiogenesis, so on the basis of the use of targeted drugs, the use of TACE treatment is more conducive to the treatment of cancer [32, 36]. In some HCC clinical trials, patients who received TACE one day after oral administration of lenvatinib [9, 39] showed improved clinical outcomes due to the adjustment of treatment regimens, but not concentrating on the sequence of treatments. In clinical practices, as TACE is still the most effective treatment in advanced HCC, many patients are treated first with TACE for tumor control and then with targeted and immunotherapy. In a word, at present, the sequence of TACE and targeted, immunotherapy has not been determined. According to guidelines, it is generally recommended that targeted drugs and immunotherapy should be used simultaneously or at short intervals, if TACE combined with systemic therapy is selected, the interval time is determined according to the patient's condition [23].

Consistent with prior research, our study reaffirms the prognostic significance of AFP levels. A decrease in AFP levels early in treatment was associated with improved survival outcomes, echoing findings from studies on sorafenib and other ICIs [9, 22, 40, 41]. This reinforces the utility of AFP as a dynamic biomarker to guide treatment adjustments and prognostication in clinical practice There have also been reports of the predictive role of reduced AFP in HCC response to treatment including local therapy and transplantation [42, 43]. In sorafenib-treated HCC patients, a > 20% decrease in AFP from baseline after 4–8 weeks of treatment is considered a valid predictor of treatment response and survival benefit [44]. Also, it has recently been reported in the literature that patients with an AFP reduction of more than 20% at 6–10 weeks was independently predictive of overall survival who were treated with either nivolumab or atezolizumab and bevacizumab with AFP > 20 ng/ml at baseline [45]. Hsu WF reports a decrease of > 15% in AFP levels within the first 3 months of ICI treatment can serve as a biomarker to predict the outcome of patients with unresectable HCC receiving ICIs treatment with or without TKI or local therapy [46]. Shao YY found that HCC patients with AFP levels decreased > 20% in the first 4 weeks had better OS and PFS who received ICI alone with AFP > 20 ng/mL at baseline [47]. These results suggest that the early decrease of AFP is helpful to predict the efficacy of immunotherapy for HCC. In our results, we also found that patients with a greater than 20% decrease in AFP at 1 month of treatment compared with baseline had a better survival outcome, similar to the results of the above studies. Except that, our study included patients treated with target-immune combination TACE therapies while previous studies reported mainly HCC patients with ICI therapy. The early decline of AFP and the predictive role of its magnitude in the combination therapy based on systemic therapy need to be further studied.

Despite these insights, our study has limitations inherent to its retrospective design, including potential selection bias and confounding factors. Prospective studies are necessary to validate our findings. Furthermore, the diversity in the types of TKIs and ICIs used, combined with the absence of analysis on their individual prognostic impacts, suggests that future research should explore the differential effects of specific systemic therapies in combination with TACE.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our results suggest that the sequence of TACE and systemic therapies did not universally impact clinical outcomes in advanced HCC. HCC patients treated with TACE and systemic therapies with a 20% decrease in AFP level compared with baseline at one month had better survival outcome. Future studies should aim to delineate more clearly the circumstances under which specific sequencing of therapies might confer a survival advantage, supporting more personalized and effective treatment strategies in advanced HCC.
