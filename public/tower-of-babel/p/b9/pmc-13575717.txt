Zebrafish as a practical and convenient model for the study of photodynamic and photothermal therapy of solid tumors: A scoping review
Authors: Ethan Wilson, Alexander Fiorentino, Brian W. Booth, Valery Bliznyuk, Diana Ivankovic, Luigi Boccuto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThanks to incalculable efforts by teams of scientists and physicians over the past half‐century, many cancer diagnoses that would have previously seemed terminal are now considered treatable or even curable with optimization of existing treatment strategies and the introduction of novel treatment methodologies. One rising treatment paradigm with the potential to improve patient outcomes and experiences is photomedicine, a group of light‐activated treatments that can be applied systemically and activated locally. As the work of developing new phototherapeutic treatments and agents for use in cancer patients progresses, in vivo experimentation is a necessary hurdle, costing both time and resources. The use of zebrafish as a vertebrate tumor model for early‐stage animal testing has the potential to increase the overall efficiency of the research process. Zebrafish are a model organism that allow for numerous tumor presentations and drug‐delivery options to be tested at scale with a low turnaround time relative to other in vivo vertebrate models. The transparency of an embryonic or larval zebrafish's body allows for real‐time observation of drug dispersal and effects of treatment. These qualities allow zebrafish to serve as a useful screening tool for identifying promising candidate photosensitizers before embarking on mammalian studies.

INTRODUCTION

The past half‐century has witnessed impressive improvements in the diagnosis and treatment of cancer. Unfortunately, despite such great strides, cancer remains the second most common cause of death in the US, below heart disease, accounting for approximately 600,000 deaths per year. 1 The current standard of care treatments are most commonly chemotherapy, radiation therapy, or surgery, used independently or in combination. Most chemotherapy is designed to be administered systemically, which allows chemotherapies to reach and treat even undiscovered metastases. The typical mechanism of action relies on chemotherapeutics having an outsized toxic effect on rapidly growing tumor cells versus healthy cells. Ideally, this results in the death of the malignant tissue, but it often results in toxicity to off‐target tissues, especially those characterized by physiologically high rates of cell division and turnover (i.e., the lining of the gut, hair, and bone marrow). Severe adverse effects of chemotherapy may lead to organ failure and even death. Surgery, on the other hand, can be an effective treatment for localized tumors but is not capable of addressing simultaneously primary lesions and metastases, rendering it relatively ineffectual against established late‐stage disease. 2 Surgery also carries the weakness of resulting in scarring at the site of excision and causing permanent structural and functional alterations in the targeted tissue. Radiation presents the benefit of being able to destroy tumors in a less invasive manner than surgery, but has a lowered utility in the case of metastasis and cannot differentiate between healthy and cancerous cells, potentially causing excessive damage to healthy tissue near the targeted tumor, as well as tissues between the tumor and skin in the case of external beam radiotherapy. 3

Such side effects of the standard therapeutic approaches have encouraged searching for less invasive treatment options. Photomedicine (PM), here referring to both photodynamic therapy (PDT) and photothermal therapy (PTT), is an emerging strategy for the treatment of cancer that promises to address many of the weaknesses of standard treatment modalities. A typical course of anti‐tumor PM involves the administration of a photosensitizing pigment to the patient intravenously and illuminating the targeted area with light of an appropriate wavelength and intensity sometime later. 4 The delay allows for the photosensitizer to be distributed around the body and for the photosensitizer to accumulate in tumor tissue via the enhanced permeability and retention effect. When a photon thus presented is absorbed by the photosensitizer, it is energized from a ground state to an excited singlet state. Depending on the properties of the photosensitizer and light used, the method by which excess energy is shed as the pigment returns to ground state corresponds to the therapeutic effect observed. The photosensitizer may simply eject the excess energy as heat into the environment, producing a photothermal effect. Another option is for the excited singlet state to convert to an excited triplet state through intersystem crossing. 4 , 5 From this state, the pigment can return to a ground state by emitting a photon (phosphorescence). Energy transfer from the excited triplet state may also produce free radicals, producing a photodynamic effect in two general pathways. If the excited triplet photosensitizer transfers an electron to a local substrate (e.g., cell membrane or other cellular structures), a type I reaction occurs and results in the production of reactive oxygen species. A type II reaction occurs when the excited triplet photosensitizer transfers energy directly to molecular oxygen, resulting in the production of singlet oxygen. 5 The net effect of the production of ROS/singlet oxygen is the oxidation of local cellular structures, damaging them and ultimately resulting in cell death.

In the PTT process, light energy absorbed by a near‐infrared (NIR) dye (photothermal agent, PTA) is converted into thermal energy, resulting in thermal damage (ablation) of the cancer cells. Several recent reviews in the field focused on engineering of the PTA to achieve better efficiency of the treatment, as well as on expanding possible applications beyond just the PTT process. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14

Several stages of thermally induced biodegradation can be distinguished on a cellular level, depending on the temperature rise during PTT treatment. 13 At mild temperatures of 41–43°C, protein denaturation and enhanced vascular permeability occur after several minutes of irradiation. Moderate temperatures of 43–45°C cause reorganization of the cytoskeleton and alterations in gene expression regulation. Higher temperatures (45–55°C) lead to the leakage of intracellular content due to the destruction of cellular membranes, followed by apoptosis and necrosis of the tumor cells. At temperatures exceeding 55°C, necrosis occurs instantly.

Many researchers draw attention to the fact that similar processes happen during PDT and PTT methods. In fact, two routes can be distinguished in the latter case. 6 , 11 , 13 , 15 In a type 1 process, nonradiative relaxation of light‐excited PTA occurs with the generation of singlet oxygen and other ROS species, like in the PDT process. In a type 2 process, conversion of the light energy into heat happens through radiation of IR photons, which are then absorbed by surrounding tissues. Though the two processes happen simultaneously, the advantage of the second type process, as well as of the PTT as a whole, is that the latter does not require the presence of oxygen in the irradiated tissues and proceeds even under hypoxic conditions. 6 , 13

Photosensitizers may also have additional features in addition to a pigment, allowing the drug to access and localize cancer cells. These may include lipophilic shells that allow passage through plasma membranes or ligands that can preferentially bind to the surface of cancer cells (folic acid, for example). In many cases, the energy that is not used in the creation of ROS or thermal energy is shed as fluorescence, which allows for imaging or diagnostic capabilities. The combination of therapeutic and diagnostic functionality of many photosensitizers has led them to be dubbed as theranostic agents. 16 The combination of systemic administration, the ability to target cancerous cells at a sub‐cellular level, and localized activation limits systemic off‐target effects associated with traditional chemotherapy. Additionally, phototherapies have higher selectivity potential than radiotherapy and can result in less scarring than surgery. 4 , 17

The nature of PM imposes limitations on the application. The requirement for illumination generally limits applicability to tumors positioned close to the skin or near a body cavity accessible by an endoscope, as most visible light only penetrates human tissue millimeters deep. Most PDT drugs require the presence of available molecular oxygen for efficacy. Additionally, even FDA‐approved PDT/PTT drugs can cause patients uncomfortable side effects, especially light sensitivity, and may remain in the patient's system for days or even weeks after administration. 4 As a relatively novel treatment modality, PDT/PTT anti‐cancer treatments lack the track record and breadth of research of more established anti‐cancer strategies. Given their inherent limitations, it is not expected that phototherapies will replace the established standard of care in most cases but may find application in supplementing treatment strategies.

Current work is being done to address these drawbacks by creating new and better photosensitizers and a PDT treatment regimen. The penetration of light can be accounted for by designing photosensitizers that react to NIR light, which penetrates tissue deeper than visible light, or by internal radiation delivery mechanisms. Researchers are also developing photosensitizers that are less reliant on molecular oxygen, instead creating ROS using oxygen harvested from surrounding tissues. Photosensitizers modified to have a higher degree of specificity and lower half‐lives could minimize patient discomfort and inconvenience. 18

As with most therapeutics, once a photosensitizer is conceptualized and synthesized, it is exposed to in vitro and in vivo models of increasing complexity before being tested in humans. Typically, if promising results are seen in vitro, mice are the next step in the testing process. Relative to cell culture, mice require much more time and resources to work with, resulting in limited data due to the practicalities of the model. Further, being a mammalian in vivo model, experimentation on mice invokes significant ethical considerations and oversight. The use of an intermediate model that could reproduce quickly in large numbers, with less ethical implications, and low growth and maintenance costs, while still retaining a high level of genetic and physiological homology with humans could be valuable to researchers looking to strengthen hypotheses and validate assumptions before starting trials with more costly models, including mice.

Zebrafish (Danio rerio) is a model organism with rising prominence in cancer research as an intermediate step between in vitro research and mammalian models. Zebrafish go through multiple, distinct developmental stages (Figure 1). From fertilization until hatching, zebrafish are considered embryos (about 72 hours post‐fertilization, hpf). The larval stage lasts from hatching through 30 days post‐fertilization (dpf). Throughout the embryonic and larval stages, the young zebrafish lack an adaptive immune system, allowing for the facile creation of xenografts in the young fish (though at the cost of interrogating immunological responses). From 30 to 90 dpf, the zebrafish are considered juveniles before finally achieving sexual maturity and being classified as adults. An adult zebrafish may live up to 2 years and produce up to 200 embryos per week, allowing for high‐throughput experimentation. 19

Additionally, zebrafish are relatively simple and inexpensive to maintain compared with other vertebrate models, provided the infrastructure is already in place. 20 , 21 , 22 Approximately 70% of human genes have at least one zebrafish ortholog (a gene with common ancestry), with that number rising to more than 80% for disease‐associated genes. 23 Of specific interest for PDT researchers, zebrafish are optically clear as both embryos and larvae, allowing for direct monitoring of organ and tumor development via light microscopy as well as monitoring of fluorescently labeled treatments and structures via fluorescent microscopy in real time in a living system. 24

Given the characteristics that recommend zebrafish for PM research, a critical overview of the assays and experimentation already demonstrated in zebrafish may prove useful to PDT/PTT researchers with access to a zebrafish facility in fully leveraging their resources and minimizing the use of higher‐order animal models. Therefore, in this review, we looked at how zebrafish have been used to study PM therapies and agents within the scientific literature to illustrate the value of zebrafish as a model organism. The resulting document will hopefully inspire future researchers to formulate their research plans and maximally utilize their resources.

METHODS

We utilized PubMed and Web of Science as the databases for our search. The search string used was (“Danio rerio” OR “zebrafish”) AND (“photodynamic therapy” OR “photothermal therapy” OR “PTT” OR “PDT”) with no date or other restrictions applied to the search. A list of all resultant articles was downloaded as a .csv file, including title, year of publication, and full abstract text. Duplicate results were discarded. Our inclusion criteria required that the articles must (1) utilize zebrafish as an in vivo model, (2) have zebrafish exposed to PM or a photosensitizer candidate to be used in PM treatment of cancer, and (3) have a full‐text English version available. Article abstracts were evaluated for relevance. Abstracts were screened for duplicate or review articles, which were not analyzed further.

Full‐text versions of the remaining articles were then accessed and evaluated against our inclusion criteria. Any concerns regarding whether an article met the criteria for inclusion were resolved by a conference between the authors. Of the 136 unique results returned from the initial search, eight were excluded for being review articles, three could not be retrieved, and two were unavailable in English. One hundred twenty‐three articles were subjected to full‐text assessment. Of these, 35 did not contain references to either zebrafish or PDT, one did not pertain to cancer, and one presented work already represented in another article; these were all discarded. Eighty‐seven articles were subjected to deeper assessment (see Figure 2 for details on the selection process).

PRISMA flow chart depicting record retrieval and selection process.

Data collection

For each included article, independent assays involving PDT/PTT or associated photosensitizers and zebrafish were identified. For each assay, the following information regarding the zebrafish used was recorded as applicable: strain of zebrafish, age at photosensitizer exposure, age at illumination, xenograft/transgenic modifications, age at xenograft (if appropriate), xenograft origin, photosensitizer name, activation radiation wavelength, light source, irradiance, and light duration were all noted. Finally, notes regarding the analysis were taken, including the assay's endpoints, sample size, purpose, and general methodology.

RESULTS

Overview of articles

Most of the analyzed articles performed multiple assays using zebrafish, often being able to use the same cohort to collect multiple data points, such as evaluating biocompatibility and imaging potential in a single drug exposure. Less intuitive or common techniques mentioned in this section may be elaborated on or given additional context in the following section.

Assays

The use cases for zebrafish in the reviewed work addressed questions that can roughly be divided into three basic categories: toxicity, efficacy, and bioimaging. As the name implies, toxicity assays assessed the effects of the photosensitizer on the host (zebrafish). Efficacy assays looked at whether the photosensitizer displayed its predicted functions in a physiological environment, including tumor destruction, fluorescence, and ROS production in situ. Bioimaging studies looked at the capability of using the photosensitizer for its fluorescent properties independent of anti‐tumor capabilities.

Toxicity/biocompatibility studies in embryonic zebrafish were the most common case, occurring in 62 of the reviewed articles. While the majority (40/62) of these tests followed the basic structure of the established fish embryo acute toxicity (FET) test 25 (with the common modification of not testing drug concentrations higher than may be used for subsequent testing rather than finding LC50), a variety of other variations were also included. Assays included both dark and light photosensitizer toxicity studies, light exposure studies, dosing studies, and biocompatibility assessments. Endpoints measured included morbidity/mortality, developmental anomalies, behavioral data, histological data, teratogenesis, and gene regulation.

Efficacy assessments were also numerous, occurring in 60 of the reviewed articles. In addition to performing PM on tumor model zebrafish, other tests included assessments of angiogenesis, pro‐apoptotic and anti‐metastasis functionality, evaluation of cytotoxicity in non‐tumor tissue, and in vivo ROS production. Assays and measured endpoints focused on the action of the photosensitizer rather than the response elicited from the model organism. Secondary effects of interventions (such as immunomodulatory responses) were not evaluated; instead, they were reserved for mammalian models following assessment in zebrafish. Endpoints measured included evidence derived from fluorescent microscopy, immunofluorescence, RT‐PCR, and transferase dUTP nick‐end labeling (TUNEL).

Bioimaging studies comprised the last major group and were performed in 39 articles. To qualify as a bioimaging assay, the author must have stated that the purpose was to evaluate the photosensitizer as an imaging agent explicitly in addition to other effects. This is a necessary delineation as the use of light and fluorescent confocal microscopy was highly prevalent across all studies. Specific aims in bioimaging studies included assessing drug kinetics (uptake, retention, and regression), fluorescent capability, tumor localization, and potential as an MRI contrast agent.

Employed zebrafish represented embryos, larvae, and adults. Toxicity/biocompatibility studies primarily relied on wild‐type fish strains, especially strain AB. Imaging studies predominantly used either wild‐type (WT) or Casper (a pigment‐deficient strain). Sixteen zebrafish xenograft tumor models were utilized throughout the assessed material (see Table 1), as well as two transgenic hepatocarcinoma zebrafish models, which were used in nine publications (see Table 2). An additional four transgenic lines were employed. One of these, Tg(fli1:EGFP) (a transgenic strain with fluorescently labeled vasculature) was utilized in five publications, whereas the other three made a single appearance each.

Common method motifs using zebrafish

Within the reviewed articles, zebrafish were used to demonstrate the utility of potential PM therapies as well as to demonstrate the lack of toxicity of photosensitizers and to optimize the effectiveness of therapeutic protocols. Treatment of implanted or induced tumors utilized steps comparable to PM treatment of a potential patient. In the following section, this paper will walk through the steps involved, highlighting options open to the researcher while conducting the PDT and commonly observed methods of optimizing protocols seen within the reviewed work. The general steps in PDT treatment of a zebrafish are: (1) initiate tumors within the zebrafish, (2) allow tumor growth, (3) administer photosensitizer, (4) allow for photosensitizer absorption, (5) illuminate the treated fish, and (6) assess effects on tumor development.

Initiating tumor growth

The establishment of a tumor model is the predominant first step in using the zebrafish platform to test the efficacy of PM treatments, though some researchers have reported results derived from intentionally damaging healthy tissue in zebrafish in lieu of tumor cells. 42 , 47 , 48 , 49 Though simple, this method lacks the ability to determine drug specificity as it relates to targeting malignant tissue.

There are two main methods for establishing tumors in zebrafish: using transgenic strains in which tumors can be controllably induced and implanting xenografts within zebrafish embryos/larvae.

Establishing xenografts is the more flexible option in terms of tumor variety, as zebrafish, in their first 30 days of life, are generally accommodating to implants of foreign tissue. Across the assessed studies, eight distinct human cancer cell lines were used and included cells originating from the breast, liver, cervical, lung, brain, and prostate. The injected cells have typically been dyed (e.g., CM‐Dil) or modified to express fluorescent proteins (e.g., mCherry or GFP) to allow for easy visualization throughout the remainder of the procedure. The choice of fluorescent marker is determined by the ability to differentiate it from the photosensitizer, which is to say, the differences in the absorption and emission spectra of the marker and photosensitizer. Human cells are microinjected into zebrafish between 0 and 96 hpf and may be deposited in several areas depending on the model type and the age of the zebrafish (some common sites are shown in Figure 3). For the creation of ectopic models, cells are injected directly into the circulation. For embryos aged about 24 hpf, the Duct of Cuvier, a circulatory sinus through which blood enters the yolk sac, is often a target of choice. 38 , 39 , 50 For more developed embryos, the cardinal vein serves the same function. 51 Orthotopic models are created by injecting cells into a zebrafish cavity orthologous to their human equivalent. Breast and liver cancer cells have been injected into the pericardial cavity, 52 , 53 , 54 perivitelline space, 55 , 56 or yolk sac 57 , 58 , 59 , 60 (again, dependent on developmental stage), whereas cells were injected retro‐orbitally to establish an orthotopic model with Conjunctival Recurring Malignant Melanoma‐1 (CRMM1). 38 , 39 A summary of the xenografts found within the reviewed articles can be found in Table 1.

To aid the growth of the implanted cells, newly injected zebrafish are typically kept at a warmer temperature than standard to allow the implanted cells to grow in an environment that more closely resembles that of a human body. In the literature reviewed in this work, post‐xenotransplantation water temperatures ranged from 28°C 53 , 58 (optimal/standard zebrafish growth temp) to 34°C. 38 , 39 , 50 , 62 Of the highest temperature group, one protocol held the fish at the elevated temperature for 6 days after injection without deleterious effects on the host. 39 It is worth noting that the above observations notwithstanding, according to a relevant review of zebrafish as a cancer model, standard growth conditions for zebrafish bearing xenografted human tumors are 36°C, indicating an additional margin for the well‐being of the zebrafish. 63 Conversely, within the protocols that did not elevate post‐injection water temperature above the standard 28°C zebrafish growth condition, the implanted tumors were still observed to grow despite the low temperature relative to optimal conditions for human cells. 53 , 58

An alternative method of developing tumor models in zebrafish uses transgenic zebrafish lines with inducible tumorigenesis. Using transgenic zebrafish has a few advantages relative to xenografting human cells. Using a transgenic model creates a tumor native to the fish, eliminating the question of using elevated temperatures to accommodate the human cells. Additionally, the injection step is skipped, removing an additional stressor to the fish and lessening the technical workload for the researcher. Using a transgenic model sacrifices the flexibility of the xenograft method and the resulting tumor is composed of fish cells rather than implanted human cells, adding a degree of separation from the human tumors it is meant to model. The transgenic zebrafish lines used in the reviewed work are summarized in Table 2.

Two inducible hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) models were used in the reviewed papers. The more commonly used strain, appearing in seven of the reviewed papers, was Tg(fabp10:LexPR; LexA:EGFP‐kras V12 ), abbreviated to krasV12. This strain features an inducible expression of K‐Ras‐driven HCC via exposure to mifepristone, allowing researchers to induce tumor development at a targeted stage of development. 46 By linking Kras with EGFP, the induced tumor may be easily visualized in zebrafish larvae via fluorescent microscopy. One downside to this model is the requirement to maintain and cross driver and effector transgenic lines to obtain the desired double‐transgenic fish. The other utilized transgenic HCC model, Tg(fabp10:rtTA2s‐M2; TRE2:EGFP‐kras G12v ), aka kras G12V, was developed by the same team and may be seen as an iteration on krasV12 rather than an entirely independent strain. It improves the convenience of krasV12 by not requiring a cross of driver/effector lines. Additionally, krasG12V uses doxycycline as the tumor induction agent, which is less toxic to zebrafish and has a smaller effect on the zebrafish transcriptome. 34

Allowing tumor growth

In most cases, after establishing a tumor, malignancy is allowed to proliferate before administration of the photosensitizer. The time allotted varied from 6 to 48 h for the xenograft models, with the most common development time being 24 h (see Table 1). Transgenic models typically require more time, typically requiring weeks to develop mature tumors after induction. 26

Administering photosensitizer

PDT treatment begins after the tumor is established. The first step, the introduction of the photosensitizer, presents the researcher with choices, namely the dosage and method of administration.

The dosage used on the tumor model zebrafish is typically informed by prior in vitro toxicity experiments on cultured cancer cell lines or some variation of the FET test. In this experimental framework, zebrafish embryos are grouped into separate wells of a multi‐well plate and exposed to varying concentrations of substance dissolved in their water for 96 h, with researchers assessing mortality/morbidity every 24 h. 25 Other indicators, including developmental and behavioral anomalies, may also be assessed. The most commonly assessed behavior in toxicity studies is spontaneous swimming or bend frequency, assessed via infrared motion trackers. 64 , 65 , 66

The most common goal of assessing toxicity is establishing biocompatibility at a predicted dose, wherein a positive result is equated with the drug having no significant impact on the assessed metrics at or above the predicted therapeutic dose. Biocompatibility of a given concentration of drug assessed via an FET test can be generalized from a water‐administered dose to an IV‐injected dose, as a given IV dose is considered benign relative to water exposure. 27 , 28

Alternatively, a similar experimental design can be used to resolve a quantitative measure of overall toxicity in the form of a lethal concentration 50 (LC50) or maximum tolerated dose (MTD). An LC50 describes the dose that causes death in 50% of test animals, whereas MTD is a researcher‐predefined metric that refers to the highest dose achievable before induced morbidity/mortality, which significantly impacts the experimental design. Both metrics are found by increasing the dose to which zebrafish embryos are exposed until the threshold is reached. 67 In the case of Chen 2020, the MTD was defined as 10% mortality at 4 days post administration. Once a desired dosage has been proven adequately safe, or MTD has been determined, that dosage is used for subsequent assays.

Once toxicity levels are established and a dose is decided upon, the route of drug administration remains to be considered. In the reviewed publications, administration methods took three main forms: water exposure, injection, or pre‐treatment of xenograft. Water exposure is the simplest method, as the drug is added to the fish's system water and passively absorbed by the fish over time through ingestion or absorption. Injection allows for local administration of photosensitizer directly to the tumor or for systemic administration via intravenous injection. Finally, in the case of pre‐treatment, cancer cells destined to become a xenograft are exposed to a photosensitizer before being implanted into their host and thus are pre‐localized to the tumor. This ensures that there is no difficulty in the photosensitizer reaching the tumor but precludes assessment of a drug's ability to migrate to and target tumor cells in vivo.

Undrugged controls are typically used to account for the confounding effect of injection or vehicles.

Allowing for PS absorption

After administering the photosensitizer, some amount of time is required for the photosensitizer to be absorbed into the tissues of the fish. A standard method of determining the drug/illumination interval is to perform a preliminary uptake and retention assessment by administering the drug to a non‐tumor‐bearing zebrafish and monitoring the kinetics of the photosensitizer by taking a series of fluorescent microscopy images of the living, dosed zebrafish.

The absorption/emission properties of PDT photosensitizers, coupled with the transparent/translucent bodies of zebrafish, allow them to be observed within living zebrafish in real time. This allows the researcher to know with certainty when the drug's accumulation within the tumor tissue has peaked, enabling maximum effectiveness by illuminating the photosensitizer at the optimal time point. Direct observation may also be used to determine a rough drug half‐life as it is eliminated from the body.

Fluorescent imaging of the photosensitizer within a dosed zebrafish may be performed in WT zebrafish prior to a PDT experiment to pre‐establish the therapeutic timing window. More commonly, imaging of the photosensitizer is performed in parallel with other experiments, be they PDT experiments or toxicity experiments, as a way of validating the drug's presence in the zebrafish's system. Illuminate treated zebrafish

After the prescribed absorption interval, the next step of the PDT treatment is to activate the photosensitizer by illumination. There are multiple options for light sources that may be used for illumination: white light, tuned LEDs, continuous laser, or pulsed laser.

Lasers have the advantage of allowing for direct targeting of a tumor while using a defined wavelength of light. Targeting of a tumor can also be achieved by masking non‐targeted areas, 27 though groups that used LEDs typically illuminated individual fish or multi‐well plates in their entirety. Assess effects on tumor development

After PDT treatment, damage to the tumor may not be immediately apparent, so some amount of time is allotted to allow for inflicted changes in the tumors' development to manifest. Most commonly, the allotted time was 24 h, but it ranged from 6 h to 4 days.

The most common method of assessing the effects of tumor development is to visualize the zebrafish using fluorescent microscopy. Because both implanted and transgenically engineered tumors usually have some variety of markers, this is a facile procedure, as images of the tumors may be taken directly from the living zebrafish after anesthetizing. To account for individual variance in tumor progression, images of the tumor are compared with images taken of the same tumor either directly before or directly following illumination. Because zebrafish assays are commonly treated as pilot studies anticipating further research in mouse models, a change in tumor size is often presented qualitatively, with representative images shown side‐by‐side rather than using quantitative data, presenting a dramatic visual discrepancy. In other papers, software such as Image‐J is used to estimate the size of the tumors from images before and after irradiation, creating quantitative measures of tumor size change.

Alternative metrics besides tumor size that may be measured following PDT treatment include ROS production rates, cell death pathway markers, and tumor metastasis.

In the reviewed articles, ROS production was visualized using dihydroethidium (DHE) or dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCF‐DA), both of which fluoresce in the presence of ROS. As with photosensitizers, both DHE and DCF‐DA may be administered to a living zebrafish via water exposure. 48 , 69 , 70 The zebrafish may then be subjected to PDT and imaged. Overlaying a fluorescent microscopy image of the photosensitizer with an image of the distribution of ROS can establish a geographic correlation between the two. DHE has a peak emission at 590 nm (red), while DCF‐DA's peak emission is at 530 nm (green). 71 The choice of which to use is informed by which compound has less emission spectra overlap with the photosensitizer being evaluated.

As the controlled cell destruction resulting from apoptosis is less deleterious to future patients than destruction via necrosis, establishing the mechanism of cell death is a worthwhile endeavor after subjecting it to PDT, typically in an efficacy experiment. Immunofluorescence staining of Caspase‐3 in recently euthanized fish is the most common method and is often paired with acridine orange staining to support the conclusion. 50 , 56 Another method used is terminal deoxynucleotidyl TUNEL assay, which fluorescently tags fragmented DNA, allowing for visualization of apoptotic foci within the treated zebrafish. 39

The use of tagged or fluorescent protein‐expressing tumor cells allows metastasis to be directly visualized via fluorescent microscopy. The presence of fluorescent foci outside the primary tumor indicates tumor metastasis. Simple image analysis may be used to quantify the number and distribution of metastases.

ADVANCES IN PHOTODYNAMIC THERAPY

The specific aims of the PM‐related projects that utilized zebrafish tumor models represent a range of goals. To illustrate this diversity, some selected examples are presented below.

Both high levels of glutathione and low molecular oxygen concentration in the tumor microenvironment can decrease the efficacy of PDT as a treatment strategy. Y. Wang et al. addressed this by designing a photosensitizing agent, dubbed TBD‐Pt(IV)@MOF‐199, which consists of nanoparticles (NPs) of a metal–organic framework with the ability to absorb glutathione (MOF‐199). These frameworks are bound to an aggregation‐induced emission photosensitizer, dubbed TBD, forming a shell around the prodrug platinum (IV)‐diazido complex (Pt(IV)), which produces oxygen as it is metabolized into Pt(II). In combination, the resultant nanoparticles theoretically possessed the ability to fluoresce and remove glutathione from the local environment while accumulating in hyperplastic tissue and then supply molecular oxygen and Pt(II) upon illumination. The team used krasv12 transgenic zebrafish to assess both the safety and efficacy of the nanoparticles in vivo. Their results showed a larger reduction in the size of liver tumors compared with controls. Additionally, the transparency of the zebrafish larvae allowed the team to assess intracellular glutathione and hypoxia levels in the living zebrafish using confocal laser scanning microscopy, validating the theorized efficacy of the nanoparticles in remediating the tumor microenvironment. Such induced remediation is one avenue into expanding the use of PDT to larger tumors and other hypoxic environments. 28

Zhang's group published work involving the combination of photothermal and chemotherapies as a method of more effectively eliminating tumor cells. In their paper, Zhang et al. created nanoparticles of a polymeric prodrug (PDOX) loaded with photosensitizer IR825. The addition of folic acid served as a targeting ligand. The resulting nanoparticle was designed to accumulate in tumorous tissue, release doxorubicin upon entry into the relatively acidic environment of the cytoplasm, and generate heat upon irradiation with NIR light. Transgenic zebrafish larvae were used to verify the selective accumulation of the drug within tumors and observe the release of effectors and subsequent destruction of the tumor. Fluorescent microscopy revealed that, while both free doxorubicin and unirradiated PDOX/IR825 nanoparticles caused tumors to shrink, irradiated PDOX/IR825 treatment shrank tumor volume to a significantly greater degree (−18% for DOX vs. −47% for irradiated PDOX/IR825 NPs). 29

Rather than chemotherapy, Maurellio‐Jimenez's group experimented with using a porphyrin‐based organosilica nanoparticle (PMOsPOR‐NH2) as a vector to silence gene expression by introducing siRNA into the targeted cells in addition to photodynamic effects. The nanoparticles were designed to remain within lysosomes following uptake by cells until illumination by the NIR laser generates ROS, breaking the lysosome. In addition to demonstrating a decrease in tumor size and an increase in Caspase‐3 expression when treated with both PMOsPOR‐NH2 and irradiation (relative to controls), zebrafish were also used to verify the particle's ability to deliver siRNA. To test the system's gene silencing capability, single‐cell zebrafish embryos (0–1 hpf) were injected with GFP mRNA into the cytoplasm, as well as PMOsPOR‐NH2 loaded with anti‐GFP siRNA, empty PMOsPOR‐NH2, or free anti‐GFP siRNA. As the nanoparticles were injected into the cytoplasm, no illumination was necessary to release and activate the anti‐GFP effects. After 6 h of development, the researchers quantified GFP mRNA in the treatment groups using RT‐PCR, followed by larval fixation and fluorescent imaging at 28 h post‐fertilization (hpf). Among the treatments, free siRNA showed the greatest decrease in mRNA and fluorescent intensity, with the loaded nanoparticles exhibiting a comparable magnitude. Both treatments resulted in significant reductions compared with other controls. 50

Mezghrani, experimenting in the same field, utilized a similar photosensitizer‐siRNA system (periodic mesoporous ionosilica nanoparticles; PMINPs) and was also able to induce tumor shrinkage in a zebrafish xenograft model injected with MDA‐MB‐231 breast cancer cells expressing red fluorescent protein. Following exposure to PMINPs and subsequent irradiation, fluorescent microscopy showed a decrease in tumor volume. This contrasted with an increase in tumor volume in unirradiated controls. Further in vitro work with MDA‐MB‐231 showed the magnitude of the anti‐tumor effect increased when PMINPs were loaded with anti‐apoptotic siRNA. 54

While several PDT/PTT photosensitizers have been evaluated for their potential as fluorescent imaging agents, both Y. Chen and M. Poss have demonstrated PDT photosensitizers, which can be used as magnetic resonance imaging contrast agents in zebrafish, thanks to the integration of gadolinium. In Y. Chen's work, folic acid‐nanoscale gadolinium‐porphyrin metal–organic frameworks (FA‐NPMOFs) were applied to WT zebrafish to evaluate their imaging capabilities, and then to krasG12V larvae to assess their ability to target and destroy HCC tumors. The WT zebrafish embryos showed dose‐dependent fluorescent and magnetic signals without causing notable mortality or malformations. One‐month‐old krasG12V zebrafish with mature HCC tumors were exposed to the FA‐NPMOFs via their water for up to 96 h. Subsequent liver dissection and imaging of treated fish from different time points showed the concentration of FA‐NPMOFs peaking at 48 h post‐exposure, overlapping the GFP signal produced by the tumor. This overlap suggests effective targeting of the tumor thanks to the folic acid portion of the FA‐NPMOFs particles. When krasG12V fish that had been exposed to FA‐NPMOFs for 48 h were further imaged, it was found that the tumor could be visualized with both MRI and thermal imaging. The volume of the dissected tumors was calculated, and krasG12V zebrafish treated with FA‐NPMOFs, which were irradiated 48 h after initial exposure, were found to have significantly smaller tumors relative to a non‐irradiated control group. The effectiveness of the PDT treatment was further supported by histological evidence as well as a decrease in GFP fluorescent and thermal signals. It is worth noting that ROS production was not evaluated in FA‐NPMOFs. The thermal data suggest that the photothermal effect may have played at least a part in any demonstrated efficacy, though the article credits the photodynamic mechanisms exclusively. 35

M. Poss instead looked at the prosaically dubbed Gd4 3+[AlPCS4]3 4− IOH‐NPs (gadolinium aluminum (III) chlorido phthalocyanine tetrasulfonate inorganic–organic hybrid nanoparticles), a composition promising both high photodynamic activity from the [AIPCS4]4− anions and magnetic detection from the Gd3+ cation. The magnetic imaging capabilities of Gd4 3+[AlPCS4]3 4− IOH‐NPs were not evaluated in this work, however ectopic injection of Gd4 3+[AlPCS4]3 4− IOH‐NPs treated, GFP‐expressing HeLa cells into zebrafish embryos' cardinal veins was used to demonstrate the phototoxicity of the nanoparticles. After 24 h, the spreading of both HeLa cells and Gd4 3+[AlPCS4]3 4− IOH‐NPs throughout the embryos could be observed via fluorescence microscopy, with discreet green and red foci, respectively. After illuminating a portion of the zebrafish tail for 10 min, most green foci within the irradiated area were eliminated; after 20 min, no green foci remained. The untreated portion of the zebrafish retained its green foci, indicating the destruction of HeLa cells within, and only within, the irradiated area. This experiment highlights the localized effects possible with PM. M. Poss cited zebrafish as a useful model “to easily translate test‐tube experiments into in vivo systems” citing their small size and capability for “direct readout and imaging”. 62

ADVANCES IN PHOTOTHERMAL THERAPY

The requirements for the PTA agent used in PTT include biocompatibility, high photostability, and low cytotoxicity. 15 , 72 , 73 The majority of PTT‐related publications report on a combination of organic dyes with some nanomaterials (multiplicators of the effect), which ensure strong absorbance in the infrared region and enhanced photothermal conversion efficiency (PCE). 74 , 75 Commonly used PTT nanomaterials include Au or Pd nanoparticles of various shapes, 76 , 77 , 78 carbon nanomaterials such as graphene oxide or carbon nanotubes 79 , 80 and conjugated polymers (polyaniline, polythiophenes, polydopamine, and some other 81 , 82 , 83 , 84 ). Moreover, recent reports show advantages of combining several types of such agents (bimetallic nanoparticles, graphene oxide NP decorated with Au, etc.) and their successful application for the ablation of cancer cells both in vitro and in vivo. 85 , 86

The role of metallic nanoparticles in this case is determined as a multiplication of the light absorption due to surface plasmon resonance (SPR). 87 , 88 The shape of the nanostructures plays a crucial role in the SPR effect and, therefore, a lot of research has been dedicated to the synthesis of different nanostructures, including nanorods, nanoplates, nanoshells, nanocages, and some other shapes with high PCE. 89 , 90

In vitro studies are valuable for establishing biocompatibility of the PTT agents; thorough better understanding of the mechanisms of PTT of the cancer cell can be obtained using an in vivo process. Zebrafish (Danio rerio) were used as a good experimental vertebrate model due to their high genetic homology with humans (~70%). 91

P. Das et al. demonstrated that nanocomposites based on a combination of bimetallic AuPd NP with reduced graphene oxide and polydopamine carrier AuPd‐rGO/PDA exhibited excellent photothermal properties when illuminated with NIR laser (λ = 915 nm). 92 They observed a temperature rise of more than 50° within 3 min of irradiation with a relatively low concentration of PTT agent (25 μg/mL), resulting in local ablation of the MDA‐MB‐231 cancer cells. Additionally, the applied system had high biocompatibility towards L929 cell lines and exhibited low toxicity rates in zebrafish embryos. The authors proved that in their case PTT‐induced cell death was partially accomplished due to Au‐Pd/rGO/PDA nanocomposite mediated generation of ROS, leading to oxidative stress damaging cellular macromolecules (DNA, proteins and lipids). 92 Therefore, the observed elimination of cancer cells can be considered as a dual action of PTT and PDT. 93 , 94

Generally, at least three main mechanisms of PTT treatment have been recognized and documented. First, a local rise of the temperature in the tumor area affects the permeability of the tumor cell membranes, which reduces the activity of intracellular enzymes and proteins and leads to the death of tumor cells. 77 Second, it is believed that the heat dissipation within the tumor cells is poorer in comparison to normal cells due to poor capillary development and slower blood circulation in the tumor. This lower tolerance of the cancer cells to high temperature results in local overheating and cell death. 94 Moreover, local heating in the focus area (the place of maximal intensity of the laser beam) leads to modulation of some genes related to apoptosis, causing the death of the cancer cells. 77

The same PS molecules can be used in PDT and PTT treatment and therefore the two techniques are often combined, that is, ROS species are generated simultaneously with local heat during irradiation. However, a proper design of a PTA allows several more combinations of noninvasive therapies. This consideration highlights an important advantage of the PM methods, which can be applied in line with other therapeutic techniques to achieve a synergistic improvement of cancer treatment efficiency. As reported in several recent reviews, PTT can be successfully combined with chemical therapy (photo‐stimulated drug delivery), radiotherapy, immunotherapy, gene‐therapy, or chemodynamic therapy (Fenton‐type oxidation). 12 , 77 , 84 , 94 Though many of these combinations have been studied in vivo, their application with zebrafish has not been reported yet.

In addition to the therapeutic treatments, some PTA nanocomposites offer the possibility of MRT, photoacoustic, or fluorescence imaging of the tissues, thus creating a way for their more sophisticated use as efficient PTT agents for cancer theranostics. 95 , 96 In the case of zebrafish research, this possibility is especially attractive due to the high transparency of the tissues and the small size of the animal.

STATE OF THE FIELD

Both the use of zebrafish in cancer research and the use of phototherapies in cancer treatment are relatively novel techniques just coming into their respective maturities. In the case of PM, the potential benefits are tantalizing. As of the time of writing, the US Food and Drug Administration has approved PDT treatments for actinic keratosis, cutaneous T‐cell lymphoma, Barrett's esophagus, basal cell skin cancer, throat cancer, non‐small cell lung cancer, and early‐stage squamous cell skin cancer. The treatment of skin cancers and precancers typically involves the topical application of aminolaevulinic acid (5′‐ALA, Levulan®) or methyl aminolevulinate (MAL, Metvix®) to the affected area, followed by irradiation using a blue LED, though researchers are currently experimenting with the use of daylight to reduce patient discomfort and shorten treatment times. 97 Esophageal and endobronchial tumors are treated by intravenous administration of porfimer sodium (Photofrin®) or Chlorin‐e6, followed by radiation with a NIR laser delivered by an endoscope. 98 Cutaneous T‐cell lymphoma can be treated using extracorporeal photopheresis, a method that involves oral administration of a photosensitizer (8‐methoxypsoralen, 8‐MOP), removing a lymphocyte‐enriched fraction of the patient's blood, irradiating the blood with UVA light, and reinfusing the blood back into the patient. 99 , 100 PDT treatments with regulatory approval by non‐FDA agencies include treatments for breast and prostate cancer. 4

There are several PDT protocols currently going through the clinical trial process as researchers continue to expand the application of existing drugs and introduce new photosensitizers with enhanced targeting and applicability. The use of implanted radiation sources and photosensitizers, which can operate in hypoxic or anoxic environments, allows for the treatment of deep tumors, while the use of lipophilic structures may allow photosensitizers to localize to previously inaccessible locations, including crossing the brain–blood barrier and seamlessly integrating with targeted cells via photochemical internalization, delivering both photosensitizers and other effectors to the heart of malignant tissue.

Further research will provide additional indications for PM, giving more options to more patients and their care teams. Though the most common use of zebrafish in PM research is limited to demonstrating a proof‐of‐concept prior to utilizing higher‐order models, zebrafish can serve a valuable role in screening promising drug candidates. As an early‐stage vertebrate model, zebrafish are convenient, cost‐effective, resource‐efficient, and offer quick turnaround. 20 , 101 , 102 Zebrafish are already employed in the assessment of toxicity via the FET test, but their uses continue to expand. Within this review, teams have taken advantage of the transparency of larvae to observe the distribution, localization, and dispersal of photosensitizers introduced by various means throughout the living fish in real time to evaluate the drug's ability to reach tumors 30 , 50 , 52 , 54 and the specificity of the systems to target tumors, leaving healthy tissue unaffected. 31 , 103 , 104 Zebrafish tumor models, including both transgenic strains and xenografted fish, are also being used to evaluate phototherapeutic agents for their ability to stymie tumors in vivo and have been demonstrated to be able to create dramatic, direct visualizations of their effectiveness, lending them narrative utility.

Zebrafish still face limitations as a test platform, however, both on a practical and a meta level. Practically speaking, one major area of interest to the PDT research community is expanding the reach of phototherapies to deeper tissue and larger tumors using NIR‐sensitive photosensitizers and two‐photon emission PDT. These approaches use light that falls within the phototherapeutic window, a range of light wavelengths that can penetrate deeper into human tissue than most visible light (e.g., 10 mm @ λ = 800 nm vs. 1 mm @ λ = 475 nm). 105 Due to their size (and, in the case of some strains, lack of pigment), most visible light can penetrate the majority (or all) of an adult zebrafish, much less the more transparent larvae or embryos. Accordingly, zebrafish are not well‐suited to determining the penetrative ability of a given PDT protocol. In one reviewed study in which the authors were unable to replicate a therapeutic success in zebrafish when moving to a mouse xenograft model, the proposed explanation was their light source's inability to activate photosensitizer within the relatively large and opaque mouse tumor. It was this same quality, however, that the author cited in choosing to use zebrafish for early‐stage testing, as their transparency allowed for light saturation, removing one possible variable from the efficacy equation in testing. 57 The same reasoning may be applied to research on the effects of photosensitizers in hypoxic environments, as zebrafish tumors may not grow large enough to foster a hypoxic environment. 63 In terms of meta difficulty, in a 2020 review of xenograft zebrafish models of human cancers, the authors acknowledge the utility and potential zebrafish offer cancer researchers, but pointed out that a lack of standardization and established best practices in using xenografts was a major limitation on the ability to compare data and thus make inferences using zebrafish as a model. 63 This was illustrated when reviewing the confusing variation in zebrafish water temperatures following xenograft implantation. A lack of standardization is, however, an inevitable difficulty for any developing field to correct itself with maturity and iteration. Within the reviewed articles, Chen et al. authored two papers directly furthering this cause, analyzing treatment effectiveness based on tumor location (ectopic vs. orthotopic) and drug administration method (local injection, systemic injection, and water administration). This data was used to demonstrate the amount of variation that may result from choosing one method or model over another. Q. Chen further proposed performing a similar battery of experiments as best practice at the onset of evaluating a drug's potential. 38 , 39

It was interesting that so few transgenic cancer models were employed in the reviewed articles. Two closely related HCC models were utilized repeatedly and exclusively, juxtaposed against a 2021 review of zebrafish cancer models, which lists more than 90 transgenic zebrafish cancer models, including gliomas, neuroblastomas, lymphomas, sarcomas, and melanomas, among others. 106 It seems clear that there are untapped resources and opportunities for the PM researcher, perhaps especially among the melanomas and other models of shallow tumors present as more practical targets for PM in a clinical setting.

CONCLUSION

The use of zebrafish as an effective cancer model for the study of PM offers much potential for researchers. As an intermediary model straddling the in vitro/in vivo line, using zebrafish as a screening tool allows relatively rapid assessment of functionality and biocompatibility, allowing researchers to focus time and resources more efficiently. As standard practices emerge and are defined, the applications of zebrafish for PDT/PTT research will increase in variety and translational potential as many of the existing difficulties associated with their novelty fade. Though practical limitations associated with fish as a model mean that the use of mammalian models will likely always remain a necessity, zebrafish offer significant advantages for those who can integrate their use as part of a larger research strategy.
