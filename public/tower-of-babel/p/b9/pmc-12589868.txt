Antisense oligonucleotide-based switches for conditional translation of synthetic mRNA
Authors: Francis Combes, Frida J Pettersson, Thanh-Huong Bui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSynthetic messenger RNA (mRNA) therapeutics requires precise control over their translation to minimize off-target effects. We introduce an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO)-based switch concept that regulates synthetic mRNA translation based on the presence of a complementary trigger RNA (trigRNA). We evaluate variations in ASO hybridization length, position, and chemistry in rabbit reticulocyte lysate (RRL) assays and HEK293T cells. In addition, we explore multiple ASO delivery strategies, including lipid and dendrimer conjugation. Our experiments demonstrate that increased ASO hybridization length and concentration enhance suppression while also improving the OFF/ON dynamic range in the presence of trigRNA. Cell experiments revealed that cholesterol or dendrimer-conjugated phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligonucleotides (PMOs) improved uptake leading to increased translational suppression which facilitated moderate trigRNA-dependent activation. Alternatively, PMOs that exhibit higher complementarity for the trigRNA than for the mRNA reached a dynamic range of 86.4 in RRL and 1.79 in HEK cells. We conclude that the presented ASO-based switch concept provides a novel mRNA translation control strategy, which does not rely on expressing exogenous proteins. While RRL assays demonstrate robust switching, intracellular efficacy is currently constrained by ASO delivery and intracellular dilution. Future optimization of ASO stability and maintaining intracellular suppression will be critical for therapeutic applications.Keywords: MT: Oligonucleotides: Therapies and Applications, mRNA therapeutics, switch, antisense oligonucleotides, ASO, phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligonucleotides, PMO

Introduction

Synthetic messenger RNAs (mRNAs) have garnered attention for their ability to make host cells produce any protein of choice, with the most notable application being their global use as SARS-CoV-2 vaccines. This novel class of medicine also bears the potential to be therapeutically employed, e.g., for replacing malfunctioning or absent proteins. Therapeutic use of synthetic mRNAs to their full potential will, however, require an additional solution to achieve cell-specific delivery. This would ensure that the synthetic mRNA is only translated at the intended tissue and hence reduces the chance of adverse effects in off-target tissues. Current attempts to address this challenge are primarily focused on changing the nanoparticle composition1 or conjugating targeting moieties to delivery vehicles.2 The outcomes of these efforts have been variable, with some studies demonstrating clear benefits3,4 while others question the improvements over untargeted delivery, especially to solid tumors.5,6 An alternative strategy that is gaining momentum involves the implementation of molecular switches which modulate the ability of ribosomes to translate a synthetic mRNA into proteins when a specific condition is met.7,8 This condition can range from exogenously administered small molecules9 to endogenous RNAs.10,11,12 The latter forms the basis of recently reported strategies to achieve cell type-specific production of therapeutic proteins. For example, introducing miRNA target sites in the synthetic mRNA is a well-known strategy to minimize off-target translation in scavenging organs such as the liver.13 This de-targeting strategy, however, requires knowledge of a suitable trigger molecule in all off-target cells. A more captivating strategy is to use triggers that are selectively present in the targeted cell type. For example, target cell miRNAs can be utilized to selectively suppress co-transfected inhibitory molecules such as RNA-binding proteins.7,14,15,16 The problem with such repressor proteins is that they are typically non-human and hence potentially immunogenic. To avoid this issue, recent reports describe the use of reprogrammable adenosine deaminase acting on RNA (ADAR) sensors.10,11 These are synthetic mRNA transcripts which contain a premature stop codon (UAG) that can be converted to a tryptophan codon (UIG, read as UGG) upon engaging a trigger RNA and a constitutively expressed ADAR in human cells. Unfortunately, this approach requires 160–200 base pairs (bp) duplex formation between the mRNA and its trigger RNA, an interaction known to elicit inflammation through innate immune sensors such as melanoma differentiation-associated protein-5 (MDA5).17,18 In addition, reprogrammable ADAR (RADAR) switches also produce potentially immunogenic non-endogenous proteins in their default off state. Eukaryotic toeholds (eToeholds) are another type of recently reported mRNA OFF-ON switches.12 These are complex RNA structures known as internal ribosomal entry sites (IRES) that can change conformation upon engaging a specific trigger RNA. It is, however, unclear to what extent the intricate design of IRES-based switches can accommodate variations in trigger RNA.

Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) are a well-established class of nucleic acid therapeutics that can suppress the production of endogenous proteins by modulating splicing, promoting mRNA degradation, or inhibiting translation.19 Instead of targeting an endogenous RNA, the current work explores how ASOs can be employed to suppress the translation of synthetic mRNA in cell-free translation assays and in a cell line. The ASOs were designed to also competitively bind a predetermined trigger RNA, essentially allowing the ASO to operate as a translational OFF-to-ON switch in the absence or presence of the trigger RNA. This versatile approach provides a novel alternative to previously published molecular switch strategies without requiring potentially immunogenic exogenous proteins. While multiple ASO chemistries demonstrate good clinical safety profiles, we focus on phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligonucleotides (PMOs) because their uncharged backbone offers excellent hybridization and nuclease resistance, and their clinical track record make them particularly attractive for translational applications.19,20 In an attempt to improve cellular uptake, we co-formulate the synthetic mRNA and PMOs into lipoplexes using charged DNA leashes and conjugation of the PMO to either cholesterol or charged dendrimers.

Results

Conceptually, ASOs can be hybridized with the 5′ UTR of the synthetic mRNA prior to encapsulation into delivery vehicles (Figure 1A). This allows unhindered binding during production and ensures that both molecules reach the cytoplasm simultaneously so that the ASO immediately exerts its suppressive effect on mRNA translation. The 5′ UTR region where the ASO:mRNA interaction occurs can be designed so that the ASO also exhibits affinity for an endogenous trigger RNA (trigRNA) of choice. In fact, by introducing overhangs or different chemistries, the relative affinity of the ASO for the intracellular trigRNA can be higher than the ASO’s affinity for the synthetic mRNA. This should facilitate OFF-to-ON switching upon contact with the trigRNA. Analogous to electrical relays, we named these ASO-dependent switches “riborelays” (RR). Accordingly, the 5′ UTR regions that are complementary to the ASOs are named “RR modules”.

Using rabbit reticulocyte lysate (RRL) assays and PMO-based ASOs, we first investigated the translational suppression at different ASO:mRNA molar ratios and ASO:mRNA hybridization lengths (Figure 1B). Different lengths (16–25 nt) of a target trigRNA sequence were inserted in the 5′ UTR of a reporter firefly luciferase (fluc) mRNA. The chosen trigRNA sequence was based on an eGFP region that is accessible to siRNA.21 Next, a complementary 25 nt-long PMO was annealed to these 5′ UTR sequences at different concentrations, exposed to RRL lysate, and luminescence was measured after 60 min. The RRL assay demonstrates that the RR blocking strength increases with RR module length (i.e., the region of ASO:mRNA hybridization) and PMO1 quantity (at an mRNA concentration of 31.7 nM, IC50 values for RR16-25 are, respectively, 119.7 nM, 61.6 nM, 57.0 nM, and 37.6 nM), whereas absence of an RR module in the 5′ UTR did not lead to translational suppression. Note that the mRNA was fixed at 780 fmol per reaction (∼500 ng). We then evaluated whether the suppressed translation can be recovered by adding short trigRNA1 complementary to PMO1 during the RRL reaction (Figure 1C). Again, RR modules with the longest mRNA-ASO hybridization length resulted in the highest suppression but also demonstrated the highest dynamic range between OFF and ON states in an RRL assay. This relation was also seen when a structured version22 of trigRNA1 was used (−30.50 kcal/mol), albeit at a lower dynamic range. We confirmed this interaction on m1ΨUTP-modified mRNA (Figure S1).

To verify that the riborelay switching strategy is also applicable to other trigger sequences, we exchanged the first RR module for a new RR module which targets a different trigger RNA (trigRNA2). Positional and additive effects of these RR modules were investigated by positioning the RR module either close to the 5′ cap or close to the AUG start codon of the fluc mRNA. We also tested double and triple repeats in the 5′ UTR (Figure 1D). The results demonstrate an additive suppressive effect when increasing the number of RR modules in the 5′ UTR. Both cap-proximal and cap-distal positioning of the RR module resulted in translational suppression and, similar to increasing the length of hybridization, better suppression also results in a higher dynamic range. The dynamic range between OFF/ON states can further be adjusted by employing ASOs with longer sequence complementarity for their trigRNA compared to the sequence complementarity for their binding region in the 5′ UTR. For example, we employed a 5′ UTR RR module of 25 nt long but tested PMOs of 25 nt or 30 nt in length with a trigger RNA of 30 nt (trigRNA2-30, Figure 1E). The longer PMO 30 demonstrated better suppression (3.6% ± 0.8%) and better triggering (19.5-fold, p < 0.0001) than the shorter PMO 25 (6.1% ± 4.1%, p = 0.000156). Lastly, to model the functionality of PMO 25 or PMO 30-based RR switches at low concentrations, we evaluated their behavior in RRL dilution experiments (note that the trigRNA concentration was kept constant at 1.1 μM). This experiment confirms the enhanced functionality of PMO 30 over PMO 25 but also highlights that lower concentrations of the mRNA:PMO duplex lead to loss of translational suppression and decreased dynamic range (Figure 1F).

As mentioned, we are primarily interested in using PMO-based ASOs due to their selectivity, low toxicity, clinical maturity, and resistance to degradation, which enables persistent translational suppression of the synthetic mRNA. However, we also evaluated ASOs of other chemistries for comparison. Interestingly, uncharged PMOs consistently outperformed other ASO chemistries such as 2′-O-methylation (2′OMe), and 2′-O-methoxyethyl with a phosphodiester backbone (2′MOE-PO). However, 2′-O-methoxyethyl with a phosphothioate backbone (2′MOE-PS) also demonstrated suppression and triggerability in this assay (Figure 1G). Although inclusion of locked nucleic acid (LNA) modifications in the ASO is expected to drastically increase the affinity to the RR modules,23 3 different LNA designs demonstrated only moderate (48.5%–74.2%) translational suppression and almost no triggering on RRL assays (Figure 1G). Additional gel experiments suggested that ASO dimerization might be partially responsible for this result in LNA2, but not in LNA1 and LNA3 (Figure S2). Moreover, we noticed that LNA2 demonstrates lower binding to mRNA containing an RR module and that off-target binding to other regions on the mRNA is an issue for these ASOs (especially LNA1).

Summarized, PMO-based ASOs can be annealed to complementary regions in the 5′ UTR to suppress the translation of a fluc reporter mRNA. Increasing the number of ASOs, the level of complementary, and the number of complementary sites in the 5′ UTR results in better suppression. We observed that stronger translational suppression is associated with higher dynamic range between off and on states upon adding a complementary trigRNA. This switching effect can be further enhanced by using an ASO that binds more strongly to the trigRNA than to the fluc reporter mRNA (see PMO 30). PMOs appear to outperfom other ASO chemistries for suppressing mRNA translation in the RRL assay. However, dilution of the mRNA:PMO duplexes leads to loss of switch functionality.

Next, we wanted to evaluate how PMO-based RR switches behave in cells. To ensure that the mRNA and PMOs simultaneously reach the cytoplasm in the desired stoichiometric quantities, they must be co-formulated into the same complex or delivery vehicle. However, uncharged PMOs are notoriously difficult to transfect into cells. We therefore assessed three delivery strategies: (a) hybridizing the PMOs to short DNA oligos (“leashes”) to facilitate complexation with lipofectamine messenger max (LFMM),24 (b) conjugation of positively charged groups to the PMOs,25 and (c) cholesterol-PMO conjugation to facilitate delivery in lipid carriers.26 In the first delivery strategy, we started by annealing PMOs to the mRNA at 250:1 molar ratio to ensure complete saturation of the mRNA with PMOs. Due to the lack of charge, we do not expect the unhybridized PMOs to be efficiently complexed with lipofectamine and therefore additionally added a 1000-fold molar excess PMOs (compared to the mRNA) that were hybridized to DNA leashes. DNA leashes are negatively charged DNA oligos that can be annealed to PMOs thereby allowing electrostatic interaction with lipofectamine and subsequent transfection of cells.24 Six leashes were designed so that they can easily be outcompeted by trigRNA1 (Figure 2A). The mixture of mRNA, PMOs, and PMO:leashes was complexed with LFMM, and finally transfected into HEK293T cells (Figure 2A). Addition of leashes to the PMOs exerted variable but non-significant effects on luminescence. In addition, leash 2–5 appeared to suppress luminescence in the absence of PMOs. We have therefore abandoned this delivery strategy.

We also explored conjugating chemical moieties to PMO 25 to facilitate its cellular uptake. Both cholesterol (PMO-chol) and cleavable octoguanidine dendrimer (PMO-vivoX) conjugates were tested along unconjugated ASOs in trigger dose-response RRL assays using a fixed 42 nM mRNA annealed to 50-fold molar excess ASOs (Figure 2B, chemical structures of the PMO conjugates can be found in Figure S3). Similar to the RRL results in Figure 1G, 2′MOE-PS ASOs suppressed translation to about 21% in absence of trigger. However, complete restoration of signal was achieved at 200:1 trigger excess (i.e., 4 times more trigger than ASO). For PMO 25 and PMO 30, luciferase translation was suppressed to 1.3% and 0.2%, respectively. However, both these ASOs could only restore the signal to 7.5% and 24.2%, respectively. This corresponds to a dynamic range of 5.1-fold for PMO 25, and 86.4-fold for PMO 30. Whereas PMO-chol demonstrated an almost identical dose-response pattern to PMO 25, we observed a very different profile for PMO-vivoX. Indeed, PMO-vivoX was able to very strongly suppress the translation to 0.03% and maintained this suppression (0.04%) even in the presence of 50-fold molar excess trigger (i.e., equimolar to the number of ASOs). Although the 400-fold molar excess trigger only restored the luminescence signal to 0.97%, this corresponded to a dynamic range of 21.7-fold.

Next, we annealed the PMOs, PMO conjugates, or 2′MOE ASOs to mRNA containing or lacking 3xRR modules in a 50:1 molar ratio. These complexes were subsequently transfected in HEK293T cells with increasing concentrations of LFMM (Figure 2C). After 24 h, almost no suppression was detected in the constructs that lacked the RR modules. In contrast, very strong suppression was seen in all wells that received the RR mRNA hybridized to ASOs, demonstrating that the translational suppression is sequence-specific and functional in cells. The largest differences between 3xRR mRNA without and 3xRR mRNA with ASO was detected at relatively high volume of 0.5 μL LFMM (Figure S4). As in the RRL, the lowest signal was detected in PMO-vivoX (3.4%), followed by PMO-chol (5.3%), and 2′MOE-PS (8.6%). Less potent suppression was seen for PMO 30 (16%) and PMO 25 (26%). Despite that 0.5 μL LFMM resulted in the strongest translational suppression, a WST-8 viability assay performed on these samples also indicated considerable cytotoxicity at this volume of LFMM (Figure 2C). Moreover, the relative luminescence of 3xRR mRNA (“only mRNA”) suggests signal saturation between 0.25 and 0.50 μL LFMM. We have therefore chosen to use 0.125 μL LFMM to investigate triggering events in cells (Figure 2D). HEK293T cells were first transfected with different quantities of chemically stabilized trigRNAs. This was followed by a second transfection with mRNA:ASOs approximately 3 h later and medium exchange after another 3 h was used to mimic lowering of ASO:mRNA concentration over time. Trends of modest concentration-dependent triggering could be observed for all ASOs. However, only PMO 30 demonstrated a statistically significant difference between no triggering and 1 μM trigRNA (about 3.3-fold molar excess compared to the ASOs), resulting in a dynamic range of 1.79 (p = 0.00009).

Discussion

To date, about a dozen ASO technologies have received market approval for indications ranging from neurological disorders to familial hypercholesterolaemia and amyloidosis.27 Decades of research has culminated in a wide variety of design rules and chemical modifications that result in short nucleic acid(-like) sequences that are both efficacious in suppressing endogenous target mRNA and sufficiently safe to be used in the clinic. At the same time, mRNA vaccines rapidly evolved to a clinically mature technology during the COVID-19 pandemic. Like ASOs, however, there is a huge unrealized therapeutic potential of synthetic mRNAs which remains inaccessible due to current limitations in achieving tissue-specific expression. The current investigation aimed to employ known ASO chemistries to target synthetic (exogenous) instead of endogenous transcripts. Moreover, we provide insight into how this strategy can potentially be used as translational switches that regulate therapeutic protein production in response to endogenous trigRNA concentration. While several ASO chemistries are safe and clinically established, we focused on using PMOs due to their excellent hybridization, nuclease resistance, and well-documented clinical safety.19,20 For example, weekly intravenous infusions of up to 30 mg/kg eteplirsen, a PMO-based ASO to treat Duchenne muscular dystrophy, were well-tolerated and considered safe to use in children.28 To a large extent, this advantageous safety profile can be attributed to the PMO’s uncharged backbone, which prevents their interaction with proteins—a principal cause of toxicity in ASOs with a charged backbone.29 However, the lack of charge also makes it difficult to complex and transfect PMOs into cells.30

In a cell-free environment like the RRL assay, PMOs behaved in a predictable fashion. More extensive PMO:mRNA hybridization and higher PMO concentrations lead to more potent translational suppression. Additional suppression can be achieved by either increasing the RR module length or by inserting more copies of a given RR module into the 5′ UTR. Interestingly, the RRL experiments revealed that better suppression widens the dynamic range between OFF and ON states of the RR switch, both when using structured and unstructured trigger RNA. Although stronger translational suppression generally comes at the expense of lower protein production in the ON-state, the required expression level fully depends on the therapeutic context. For example, expressing cytotoxic enzymes, such as DNase or granzyme B in target cells will require much lower protein concentrations to achieve functionality compared to e.g., secreted therapeutic cytokines like IL-2. Introducing ASO overhangs which increase the total hybridization length to a trigger RNA further improved triggerability, as seen for PMO 30 in Figures 1E, 1F, 2B, and 2D. Employing different chemical modifications also had a clear effect on both the translational suppression and on the triggerability of an RR switch. Surprisingly, despite their high affinity, LNA-modified ASOs demonstrate only modest translational suppression and virtually no triggering capabilities in RRL assays. Denaturation experiments indicated decreased LNA activity due to off-target binding on the reporter mRNA (LNA1) or due to self-dimerization (LNA2), thereby suggesting that higher affinity modifications come at the expense of unintended Watson-Crick base pairing with non-target regions. Although more extensive optimization of the LNA-modified ASOs can potentially result in better translational suppression, charged ASOs (like LNAs) also interact with countercharged proteins in e.g., RRL assays, serum, and in cells.31 In fact, charge-dependent protein binding potentially provides an explanation to why the PMOs consistently outperformed modifications that depend on a negatively charged backbone. When more ASOs are engaged in unwanted interactions with proteins, the effective concentration of unbound ASOs decreases and hence, this dilution effect results in a lower capacity to suppress translation of the target mRNA.

To overcome PMO’s delivery challenge, we tested three strategies. First, we explored DNA “leashes” to enhance complexation of PMOs with cationic lipids. However, we abandoned this delivery strategy because leashes did not significantly improve translational suppression in HEK293T cells and demonstrated inconsistent effects on luminescence, even in absence of PMOs. Moreover, using DNA raises concerns about immune activation and interference with trigger engagement.

Next, we conjugated cholesterol to PMOs to facilitate lipophilic interaction with lipofectamine. In previous publications, ASO-lipid conjugation resulted in the formation of micelles32 which can increase the effectiveness of gapmers in rat models of neuropathic or inflammatory pain.33 Similar improvements of PMO function were seen for lipopeptide-PMO conjugates.34 While cholesterol-PMOs performed similarly to unconjugated PMO 25 in RRL (likely due to lack of lipid structures), they significantly improved suppression in HEK293T cells (Figure S4, p = 0.0003) with minimal toxicity in a WST-8 assay. Still, cholesterol conjugation may disrupt membranes26 and sequester ASOs in lipid environments, reducing cytosolic availability. This supports using cleavable linkers that release the ASO after endosomal escape.

We implemented this approach using octoguanidine-conjugated PMOs (PMO-vivoX) with cathepsin B-cleavable linkers. These linkers are cleaved in acidic lysosomes and result in increased intracellular availability of the PMOs (patent US20230357763A1). While PMO-vivoX conjugates don’t readily complex with lipofectamine, their positive charge likely enhances membrane interaction and improves uptake.35 It is also likely that charge-charge interactions will also form strong interactions between PMO-vivoX and mRNA during annealing. Indeed, Figure 2B demonstrates that PMO-vivoX was very potent in suppressing translation. PMO-vivoX was also the only ASO that exhibits resistance to triggering upon adding 50-fold molar excess trigger. A possible explanation for this phenomenon is that PMO-vivoX both selectively (complementary binding to the RR module) and non-selectively (interactions between the RNA phosphodiester backbone and the ocotguanidine dendrimers) binds mRNA. In this scenario, PMO-vivoX demonstrates higher avidity for mRNA and its local concentration around the RR module increases, both resulting in an apparent resistance to engage with trigRNA. In HEK293T cells, PMO-vivoX exhibited negligible toxicity at lower LFMM volumes and were similar to cholesterol-PMOs with regard to translational suppression (Figures 2C and S4) and triggering efficiency (Figure 2D). Given the consistent concentration-dependent response in cells, the absence of statistical significance for both PMO conjugates (p = 0.054 for PMO-chol and p = 0.148 for PMO-vivoX) appears to stem primarily from technical variability rather than a true lack of biological effect. To better understand the switch dynamics, future studies should aim to precisely determine the number of PMOs that are co-encapsulated with mRNA in the delivery vehicles, the extent of cellular uptake, and the fraction of mRNA and PMOs that are released from the endosomal compartment.

Unconjugated PMOs suppressed translation of target mRNA in cells when supplemented at 50:1 molar excess of mRNA molecules (Figure 2C), particularly with 0.5 μL LFMM per well (Figure S4). Yet, the suppressive effect of PMO 25 and PMO 30 was weaker compared to that of the PMO-conjugates and even 2′MOE-PS, supporting the notion that either transfection-enhancing conjugates or backbone charge improve ASO uptake. Nonetheless, PMO 30 was the only ASO that reached statistically significant triggering in Figure 2D. Assuming that the intracellular molar ratios were maintained (50:1 ASOs-to-mRNA), 1 μM trigger RNA represents about 3.3-fold molar excess compared to the quantity of ASOs. In Figure 2B, this molar excess already approaches the trigger saturation point of PMO 30, reaching a dynamic range of over 86. Unlike these static conditions, however, extracellular mRNA:ASO concentrations will lower over time. To mimic this effect, we have exchanged the medium 3 h after transfection in Figure 2D. This resulted in a loss of translational suppression and a concurrent loss of dynamic range between off and on states of the switch. Collectively, these results indicate that increasing the dynamic range in cells will require better suppression instead of higher quantities of trigRNA. However, due to the competitive and dose-dependent nature of the riborelay switch, achieving higher suppression requires higher intracellular ASO concentrations which, in turn, necessitate the presence of even higher concentrations of trigRNA. Such quantities of trigger RNA might be found in virus-infected cells. For example, the Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) stably integrates into the genome of human B cells which can subsequently lead to Burkitt’s lymphoma, Hodgkin’s lymphoma, and nasopharyngeal carcinoma.36 EBV infected cells are known to produce very high quantities (105–106 copies per cell) of small non-coding RNA named EBER1 and EBER2 which can potentially be used as trigRNA.37,38

Unexpectedly, PMO 30 exhibited greater cytotoxicity than other ASOs. This may result from increased off-target binding or lysosomal accumulation causing endosomal stress.39 At 0.125 μL LFMM, this could explain why PMO 30 causes both higher toxicity while demonstrating lower translational suppression in cells despite its higher potency in RRL assays. However, size alone is unlikely the cause, as shown by Sarepta’s use of 30-mer PMOs like eteplirsen.40 The cytotoxicity issue can therefore potentially be addressed by selecting a different trigger sequence.

Interestingly, while 2′MOE-PS underperformed in RRL assays and trigger assays, it yielded strong suppression in Figure 2C. The key difference was serum: transfections in 2c were performed in full serum, while 2d used low-serum OptiMEM. This suggests that serum proteins may enhance uptake of phosphorothioate-modified ASOs.41

As touched upon earlier, a key challenge with riborelay switches is the dilution effect: lower ASO concentrations reduce mRNA binding and translational suppression (see Figure 1F). This forms an obstacle because higher quantities of ASOs needed to maintain the off-state will, in turn, require higher quantities of competitive trigger RNA to facilitate on-switching. However, this property might be exploited to suppress mRNA translation in tissues of high accumulation. For example, extrahepatic translation is an unsolved problem of high priority in the mRNA therapeutics field. Co-encapsulation of both the mRNA and suppressive ASOs into delivery vehicles will, upon intravenous injection, often result in high accumulation in the liver. Due to the dilution effect, the translation of the mRNA will be mostly suppressed in the liver while allowing translation in (target) tissues of low accumulation. This liver protective mechanism (or other tissues/cells of high accumulation) might be useful in e.g., patients suffering from liver disease.

In conclusion, we developed a modular switch using ASOs to conditionally suppress synthetic mRNA translation in response to trigger RNAs. PMOs emerged as strong candidates that balance safety and potency. In cells, higher translational suppression can be achieved by conjugating PMOs to cholesterol or cationic dendrimers. Furthermore, PMOs with higher affinity for the trigger RNA enhance triggerability in cell-free and cellular assays but may need optimization to reduce toxicity. The functional dependency on intracellular ASO concentration, however, was identified as a main obstacle to overcome but might also provide opportunities to dampen the effects of mRNA therapeutics in sites of high accumulation.

Materials and methods

In vitro transcription

pDNA templates were derived from the pCDN3.1(+) vector and ordered at Eurofins Genomics. The plasmids were linearized with BbsI and subsequently used in a NEB HiScribe T7 high yield RNA synthesis reaction (E2040) using sequence-weighted nucleotides42 and modified uridine where indicated. After 2 h incubation at 37°C, the IVT mRNA was treated with DNase I (10 min at 37°C) to remove template DNA. Silica columns (NEB #T2040) were used to purify the mRNA, followed by 5′ capping (Scriptcap Cap 1) and an additional silica purification. The quality of each IVT mRNA was evaluated on agarose gel.

RRL assays

Nuclease-treated RRL kits were purchased at Promega (#L4960). DNA leashes were annealed to PMOs at 1:1 molar ratios using a thermocycler as described in ref. 24.24 Unless indicated otherwise, 500 ng mRNA was first annealed to a 50-fold molar excess of ASOs in 10 μL nuclease-free water for 5 min at 60°C. After centrifuging 30 s at 16000 × g, 5 μL nuclease-free water was added containing the trigRNA (100-fold molar excess compared to the number of mRNA molecules, unless specified otherwise). Next, 10 μL of RRL master mix (containing all amino acids, see manufacturer’s instructions) was added to the mRNA:ASO:trigRNA mixture and incubated for 1 h at 30°C (a final ASO concentration of ∼1.8 μM was used). Next, the RRL reactions were briefly centrifuged and 24 μL was transferred to 46 μL nuclease-free water in a white NUNCLON 96-well plate where an additional 10 μL D-luciferin (7.5 mg/mL, Promega #P1041) was added. After 5 min of incubation at room temperature, the luminescence was measured using a Tecan Spark plate reader.

Cell transfections

After thawing, HEK293T cells (ATCC #CRL-3216) were cultured for at least 2 weeks according to ATCC’s instructions (supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin). In Figure 2A, 50k HEK293T cells were plated on a flat bottom tissue-culture treated 24 well plate in 500 μL complete medium (DMEM, 2 mM L-glutamine, 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin). After 24 h, complete medium was replaced by 500 μL medium without antibiotics. PMO 25 was first annealed to each DNA leash in a 1:1 molar ratio in PBS. The annealing was carried out on a thermocycler by first heating the complexes 5 min at 95°C followed by lowering the temperature 10°C per minutes until reaching a temperature of 5°C. PMO 25 was also separately annealed to 1xRR mRNA at 250:1 molar excess using the same protocol. The annealed PMO:leash complexes were then added to the annealed PMO:mRNA complexes at 1000:1 molar ratio (leash-to-mRNA). An equivalent of 250 ng mRNA (complexed with PMOs and leashes) was mixed with 4 μL Lipofectamine Messenger Max (LFMM, ThermoFisher #LMRNA015) in OptiMEM at a total transfection volume of 30 μL per well. After 24 h, 15 μL D-luciferin (Promega #P1042, filter-sterilized 15 mg/mL stock solution in PBS) was added to each well and luminescence was measured 5 min later on a Tecan Spark plate reader (3000 ms integration time). In Figure 2C, 10k HEK293T cells were plated on a 96 well plate in 100 μL complete medium. After 24 h, complete medium was replaced by 100 μL medium without antibiotics and 3xRR mRNA:ASO complexes (annealed at 1:0 or 1:50 molar ratio) were transfected in 0–1 μL LFMM. Luminescence was determined after 24 h. In Figure 2D, 10K HEK293T cells were plated on a 96 well plate in 100 μL complete medium. After 24 h, complete medium was replaced by pre-heated OptiMEM and 0–100 nM (final concentration) trigRNA2-30F trigger was transfected using 0.1 μL LFMM per 50 ng trigRNA (note that trigRNA was 2′-fluoro modified at both terminal ends for increased intracellular stability). After 3 h, the cells were washed twice with medium (without antibiotics) which was finally replaced by 100 μL OptiMEM. Next, 3xRR mRNA:ASO complexes (annealed at 1:50 molar ratio) were transfected using 0.125 μL LFMM. After another 3 h, the supernatant was replaced with complete medium to simulate lower ASO:mRNA concentration over time. Luminescence was measured after 21 h.

WST-8 assay

HEK293T cells were transfected as described. Immediately after luminescence readout (24 h post transfection), we performed the WST-8 assay as suggested by the manufacturer (Cayman Chemical #10010199). Briefly, electron mediator solution and WST-8 developer reagent were mixed in a 1:1 ratio and 10 μL of this mixture was added to each well. The receiving plates were gently shaken and subsequently placed in a cell incubator for 2 h at 37°C. Finally, the plate was gently shaken again before reading out the absorbance at 450 nm on a Tecan Spark plate reader. Untransfected cells were incubated for 15 min at room temperature with 1% Triton X-100 and used to determine the background signal in the WST-8 assay after 2 washes with PBS and restoring the original volume of cell culture medium. This background signal was subtracted from all absorbance values.

Statistics

Graphpad Prism 10.3.0 was used to perform statistics on samples where n ≥ 3. Unless specified otherwise, a one-way ANOVA was first performed to identify significant differences between conditions. Using Dunnett’s or Tukey’s multiple comparison test, p values for the differences were then assigned according to the following thresholds: < 0.05∗, < 0.01∗∗, < 0.001∗∗∗, and < 0.0001∗∗∗∗.
