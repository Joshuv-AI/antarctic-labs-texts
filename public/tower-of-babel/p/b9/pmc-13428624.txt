Occupational Licensing and Psychologist Supply
Authors: Daniel Goetz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDoes reducing interstate licensing barriers increase the supply of mental health providers? We study this question using the U.S. Psychology Interjurisdictional Compact (PSYPACT), which allows psychologists to practice across state lines via telemedicine without obtaining additional state licenses. Because interstate telemedicine entry is difficult to track using physical practice locations, we utilize data from a large mental health provider search platform, where providers choose which zipcodes they are listed in as search results. Leveraging states' staggered accession timings for identification, we find a nuanced supply response: although joining PSYPACT increases listings in a state by 7.7% within a year, the response is entirely driven by incumbent psychologists within a state listing for the first time. We find evidence consistent with incumbents responding to a perceived threat of entry, as states with ex ante high licensing costs exhibit the largest treatment effects, and incumbents choose to list in underserved, high‐income areas within those states.

Introduction

Access to mental health care within a country is often dictated by geography, with large, persistent supply gaps across regions (Organization for Economic Co‐operation and Development, 2023). 1 As a result, policymakers have sought ways to increase the supply of providers in underserved areas (World Health Organization 2022; V. Patel et al. 2023; McGinty et al. 2024). One recent policy initiative with this explicit aim in the United States is the Psychologist Interjurisdictional Compact, or PSYPACT, which allows psychologists licensed in a PSYPACT member state to serve patients in any other member state using telemedicine. By lowering interstate barriers to virtual care, PSYPACT is meant to help psychologists “reach populations that are currently underserved, geographically isolated or [that] lack specialty care.” (PSYPACT 2024).

Despite its intent to facilitate external entry, PSYPACT may also affect patient access through another key channel—incumbent responses. In‐state psychologists may be particularly concerned about the increase in out‐of‐state entrants that compact membership would bring, as exemplified by the following quote about PSYPACT expanding to a new state:Why would we want a bunch of therapists from out of state who charge half what we charge to start poaching from our patient pool […]?.User GreyMeade, post on reddit.com/r/therapists, Sept. 2025

Why would we want a bunch of therapists from out of state who charge half what we charge to start poaching from our patient pool […]?.

In response, in‐state psychologists may advertise more (Ellison and Ellison 2011), differentiate themselves (Salop 1979; Shaked and Sutton 1982), or drop hourly rates (Thomas 1999), all of which could improve patient access. Alternatively, they may try to start serving out‐of‐state patients themselves, or even exit the market (Mulligan and Shier 2022). Given these competing forces, the impact of PSYPACT on psychologist supply and affordability is an empirical matter.

In this paper, we analyze the effect of PSYPACT on psychologist supply decisions. We address three questions: first, does PSYPACT accession increase the supply of active psychologists in a state? Second, if there is an increase, how is it shaped by external entry versus incumbent responses? And third, does PSYPACT help alleviate geographic inequality in access to mental health care from the supply side? Despite growing academic and policy interest in the economics of mental health care (Abramson et al. 2024; Cronin et al. 2025), there has been relatively little work on how lowering licensing barriers affects supply. In part, this may stem from data availability challenges. Many psychologists in the U.S. shifted to virtual talk therapy after the COVID shock (Barnett et al. 2018; Appleton et al. 2021; Ettman et al. 2025), making it difficult to track their market entry decisions using their physical location.

To address the empirical challenges, we collect a novel, granular dataset of over 40,000 U.S.‐based psychologists' decisions on which geographic markets to enter. The data comprises monthly observations from July 2022 through December 2024 of all mental health care providers listed on PsychologyToday.com, the largest therapist search platform in the U.S. Importantly, we observe providers' choice of the zipcode(s) in which they will appear as search results, which captures where they are soliciting patients. Compared to data on physical practice locations (Deyo et al. 2024) and home addresses (Peterson et al. 2014), this data can capture if psychologists wish to serve multiple, distant markets through telemedicine. Moreover, we also observe information on pricing, insurance acceptance, and psychologist attributes, which will allow us to assess price and quality effects of PSYPACT. We combine this data with information on staggered, state‐by‐state accession to PSYPACT, along with detailed records of which psychologists acquired a PSYPACT license and when.

We first use a generalized synthetic control approach to estimate the effect of PSYPACT accession on state‐level outcomes. Our main finding is that PSYPACT causes a 7.7% increase in new psychologist listings in a state in the 12 months following accession. We find no direct pro‐competitive effect on prices: average hourly rates do not fall and psychologists do not become more likely to offer discounts for low‐income patients. However, the average number of accepted private insurance providers increases, suggesting an increase overall in accessibility. We conduct substantial validation of our results using placebo outcomes, including non‐psychologist mental healthcare provider listings and employment numbers in healthcare fields outside of mental health.

To better understand why listings increase in treated states, we decompose the supply response. We find no evidence that out‐of‐state providers increased listings in acceding states, and strong evidence of an increase in listings by incumbent psychologists who are already physically based within a state. Moreover, this increase is driven entirely by psychologists without a PSYPACT license who cannot access out‐of‐state markets themselves. Listings increase the most where the threat of entry is largest—in states that have the ex ante highest licensing barriers, and in affluent, underserved zipcodes within acceding states. Together, the evidence points towards incumbents focusing on the threat of entry, and responding to that threat by increasing their online footprint with a PsychologyToday.com listing (Thomas 1999; Ellison and Ellison 2011). Our finding that accession increases insurance acceptance is consistent with in‐state providers focusing on patients for whom there is less competition, since out‐of‐state providers face significant barriers to become credentialed with insurers in‐state.

Finally, we assess whether PSYPACT leads more psychologists to enter underserved geographic areas. We find directional but insignificant evidence of a larger increase in listings in underserved states. Within states, we find no evidence of an increase in listings in Healthcare Provider Shortage Areas (HPSA). Overall then, the evidence is weak that psychologists focus on listing themselves in areas with greater need in response to PSYPACT.

This study makes three main contributions to our understanding of healthcare provider supply decisions. First, we provide novel, well‐identified evidence that reducing inter‐state barriers does not lead to cross‐state entry for health care providers in private practice. 2 A handful of other papers have looked at the effect of licensure compacts on location decisions: DePasquale et al. (2016); Shakya et al. (2022) examine the Nurse Licensure Compact in the U.S. and find mixed results on mobility, while Deyo et al. (2024) examine the Interstate Medical Licensure Compact in the U.S. and find some evidence that it facilitated out‐of‐state practice. 3 Our findings are relevant to the many ongoing and proposed licensure compacts in the U.S. 4 : because entering new markets through telemedicine is relatively frictionless for psychologists once licensing barriers are removed, if there are supply responses to such policies in any medical field, one would expect them to appear here.

Second, we provide evidence that psychologists pay attention to both competition as well as the observable characteristics of the markets they enter. We contribute to a growing literature that it is difficult to induce entry to underserved areas. Doctors, urgent care clinics, and other providers all tend to locate in higher income areas (Asaria et al. 2016; Nguyen and Chernew 2019; Nenow et al. 2022), and on average, providers are not responsive to financial incentives to serve areas of deprivation (Kulka and McWeeny 2019; Khoury et al. 2025), but tend to prioritize the amenity value of locations (Falcettoni 2021). We meaningfully enrich our understanding of location decisions: since psychologists in our dataset can list themselves in any PSYPACT market through telemedicine, their decision of which region to enter is not necessarily tied to the amenity value of that area or the cost of serving that region, but must purely reflect favorable demand‐side and competition variables.

Lastly, we connect to a growing literature on the economics of providing health care through telemedicine. This research has focused on whether telemedicine can lower costs (Zeltzer et al. 2023, 2024; Cantor et al. 2022; Busso et al. 2022) and increase quality (Dahlstrand 2024), and whether it has pro‐competitive effects (Goetz 2023). Our paper sheds light on whether reducing licensing barriers can increase market entry through telemedicine for psychologists, a group of medical professionals who are uniquely well‐suited to providing care at a distance.

Background

Psychologist Licensing

In the United States, psychologists (including both clinical and counseling psychologists) are graduate‐degree holding professionals who can provide talk therapy, but who do not prescribe medication. To practice, psychologists must obtain a license which is administered at the state level (Stigall 1983), a consequence of which is that a psychologist licensed in one state is not automatically permitted to practice in another. Prior to PSYPACT, for a psychologist to become licensed in an additional state typically required applying and paying licensure fees to that state, irrespective of how many other states the psychologist was licensed in. 5

In recent years, there has been a growing interest in reducing barriers to cross‐state practice (Goodstein 2012), particularly as telemedicine has become more prevalent (Barnett et al. 2018). The COVID‐19 pandemic accelerated this shift (Perrin et al. 2020; Appleton et al. 2021). During the public health emergency, many states temporarily waived licensing requirements for out‐of‐state providers and expanded reimbursement for telehealth services under Medicare and Medicaid (Mullangi et al. 2021; Hamadi et al. 2022). These emergency measures highlighted both the potential benefits and feasibility of cross‐state psychological care through telemedicine.

PSYPACT, or the Psychology Interjurisdictional Compact, is an interstate agreement designed to facilitate the practice of telepsychology and temporary in‐person psychological services across state lines. Psychologists who meet eligibility criteria in a PSYPACT member state can apply for an Authority to Practice Interjurisdictional Telepsychology (APIT), which allows them to offer telepsychology services to any patient residing in any PSYPACT state without having to obtain full licensure in each of those states. 6 To practice using PSYPACT, a psychologist must have their APIT and be physically present in their “home state”, which is the PSYPACT member state in which they are licensed. 7 The APIT does not give the psychologist the ability to physically move to, and practice from, a PSYPACT member state in which they are not licensed.

PSYPACT is meant to serve two primary purposes for patients. 8 First, it ensures continuity of care by allowing psychologists to continue working with clients who move across state lines, or who temporarily reside in another state. Second, PSYPACT helps address regional provider shortages by enabling qualified psychologists to offer telemedicine services in states facing high demand or underserved populations, thereby expanding access to mental health care.

Psychologist Search Platform

PsychologyToday.com is a large, online search platform that connects patients seeking talk therapy services with providers. On the provider side, licensed therapists, including psychologists, counselors, social workers, and marriage and family therapists, pay a monthly fee to list a profile on the platform to be found by searching patients. The profile information they upload typically includes whether they offer care in‐person, online, or both, their credentials, specialties, prices, and unstructured information such as photos and a description of their practice.

A single provider profile includes at least one and up to two zipcodes. An example of a psychologist profile is provided in Supporting Information S1: Web Appendix A.1. The zipcode a psychologist chooses to list themselves in has a direct impact on which patients are likely to find them. This is because patient search on PsychologyToday.com is primarily geographic: the initial prompt to search is based on zipcode or city, with additional filters available after this initial geographic search (see Supporting Information S1: Web Appendix A.1). Assuming that most patients in a zipcode are likely to search in or near that zipcode, a provider's choice to list themselves in a zipcode implies that they are looking to acquire clients from in and around that zipcode.

For a psychologist who wishes to be found by patients from a new state, they can add a zipcode in that state as a secondary location for no additional fee on their existing profile. Alternatively, they can create another profile on PsychologyToday.com that includes zipcodes in that state and pay the monthly fee for the additional profile. They will then appear in searches within that state. 9

Data

PsychologyToday.com Listing Data

In each month from July 2022 through December 2024, we collect information on all mental health providers listed on PsychologyToday.com. In total, we gather over 500,000 unique provider IDs operating in 13,784 zipcodes, which includes 43,028 psychologists, 142,813 social workers, 163,314 counselors, 63,502 marriage and family therapists, and assorted other mental health care providers.

Because a single provider may have multiple PsychologyToday profiles, they could be associated with multiple provider IDs. Starting from the total set of PsychologyToday profile IDs for psychologists, we set all IDs with identical names and identical graduation dates to be the same provider. Using this procedure, we identify 41,086 unique psychologists. 10 Note that when we code different IDs as the same provider, we retain all information in the unique profiles. For example, a provider with two profiles each serving two distinct zipcodes would be associated with serving four distinct zipcodes.

Our primary interest is in how many psychologists are listed in a state, which we directly code using psychologists' listing decisions from the PsychologyToday.com data. A psychologist who lists themselves in at least one zipcode that is present in a state counts as a listing in that state; if they list themselves in two or more different states we count it as a listing in each state.

To evaluate whether PSYPACT accession reduces prices, we collect the average hourly private‐practice rate and whether psychologists offer income discounts. We also collect the number of insurers that psychologists accept (Zhu et al. 2024), since greater acceptance translates into greater affordability for patients. To measure the quality composition of psychologists, we will look at the average graduation year of listed psychologists in a state as a proxy for experience. To measure psychologists' tendency to expand their practices across state lines, we include the average number of states served by psychologists listed within a state as outcomes.

We will also examine what locations within states psychologists choose to list themselves in. From the 2022 American Community Survey 5 years sample, we source average household income level in a zipcode, and group psychologists into those listed in above median income zipcodes and below median income zipcodes within a state. Moreover, from PsychologyToday.com listings, we compute the number of psychologist listings per 100,000 population in each zipcode in July 2022, and group psychologists into those listed in zipcodes above and below the median value of listings per 100,000 population in a state. 11 Finally, the Center for Medicare/Medicaid Services (CMS) Healthcare Provider Shortage Areas (HPSA) for mental health capture geographic areas that lack access to resources, with definitions based on the ratio of psychiatrists to the overall population (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2024). We code whether a zipcode is in such an area, and group psychologists into those serving such areas and not serving such areas within a state. HPSAs boundaries may change and an HPSA may lose its status as a shortage area over time; we detail in Supporting Information S1: Appendix A.3 our procedure for mapping HPSAs to zipcodes in each month of the sample.

Do PsychologyToday.com Psychologists List in Multiple States?

For each unique psychologist, we code a dummy for whether they operate in multiple states. We then compute the average propensity that a psychologist operates in multiple states, using the set of all psychologists operating in at least one of our treated or control states, listed in Table 1. In July 2022, we find that 6.6% of psychologists are listed in multiple states, which increases to 11.2% of psychologists by December 2024. These data suggest that choosing to list in multiple states is a relevant margin for a significant fraction of providers, and that listing in multiple states is a growing trend for PsychologyToday.com providers during the sample period. If PSYPACT incentivizes multi‐state entry, then we may reasonably expect it to show up in provider decisions in the PsychologyToday.com listing data.

PSYPACT Data

For our treatment variable, we will use the timing of states' accession to PSYPACT, listed in the final column of Table 1. For most states the enactment date (the date on which the accession is signed into law) and the effective date (the date at which psychologists can begin signing up) are fairly close together; the longest gap is Rhode Island, with a span of 1 year.

In addition to state‐level join times, we also code which psychologists acquire an APIT, the PSYPACT license that allows them to practice across state borders. PSYPACT makes a list available of all psychologists who have successfully applied for an APIT, as well as the date on which the APIT was granted. We match psychologists on the PSYPACT directory to psychologists on PsychologyToday.com using first, middle and last names and primary states (home state on PSYPACT.) 12 Of the 14,363 unique psychologists who had attained an APIT license on PSYPACT by December 2024, we match 7093 with PsychologyToday.com profiles.

Self‐Selection Onto PsychologyToday.com

Since the PsychologyToday.com sample is self‐selected and does not cover the universe of psychologists, we assess whether it captures a relevant population for analyzing the effect of PSYPACT. From the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), there were 131,600 practicing psychologists with Ph.D. or Psy. D degrees in the United States in 2024, compared to 41,086 in our sample. 13 In Supporting Information S1: Web Appendix A.4, we show that the distribution of PsychologyToday.com listings tend to be overrepresented in New York, New Jersey, and California, which are not PSYPACT members, and Michigan, which became a member in 2023. Psychologists in our sample exhibit relatively high PSYPACT participation: 11% of BLS psychologists participate in PSYPACT (14363/131600), whereas 17% (7093/41086) of the PsychologyToday.com psychologists participate in PSYPACT.

We also compare PsychologyToday.com psychologists' practice details to the general psychologist population using the American Psychological Association's annual survey (American Psychological Association 2024). Comparing our population of 41,086 psychologists to the 853 respondents to the survey, we find that our psychologists are more likely to be more recent graduates (0.27 graduated in past 10 years vs. 0.13 in APA), less likely to accept insurance (0.57 accept any insurance vs. 0.66 in APA), equally likely to provide care via telemedicine (0.86 vs. 0.87), and slightly less likely to identify as female (0.61 vs. 0.65). Overall, psychologists in our sample are highly likely to engage with PSYPACT and to provide care virtually, which are two key pre‐requisites required to observe an effect of PSYPACT.

Final Sample and Summary Statistics

Data from PsychologyToday.com aggregated to the State × Month level will comprise our outcome variables. We choose to aggregate to the State × Month level since a psychologist‐level analysis would miss the effect of PSYPACT on new entrants, especially those that are listing themselves on PsychologyToday.com for the first time.

In our baseline analysis, we will drop the treated states Wyoming, North Dakota, and South Dakota. This is because from Table 1, these states all have fewer listed psychologists compared to any of the control states. The synthetic control approach we will use to conduct our analysis models a treated unit's counterfactual as a convex combination of untreated controls, and this assumption is not credible for states whose pre‐treatment outcomes fall below the control states' outcomes (Abadie 2021). We also drop Connecticut from our baseline, since this state was treated in October 2022 and has too few pre‐treatment periods for the synthetic control approach to match pre‐treatment outcomes. Our baseline sample consists of the acceding states of Florida, Michigan, Mississippi, Rhode Island, South Carolina, and Vermont, as well as the set of control states that have not joined PSYPACT. We include the 4 dropped acceding states in robustness checks in Section 4.5 using a different causal model.

Analysis

Model

Our objective is to estimate the effect of joining PSYPACT on psychologist outcomes in a state. We specify a model that follows the generalized synthetic control approach of Xu (2017): (1) yjt=δjtDjt+Xjt′β+αj+αt+λj′ft+ϵjt where yjt is one of the State × Month level outcomes in Table 2, Djt is a dummy variable that equals 1 if PSYPACT has taken effect in state j in month t, Xjt is a vector of time‐varying controls, αj is a state‐level fixed effect, αt is a month‐level fixed effect, and λj′ft is the interactive fixed effect (IFE) term.

The IFE term λj′ft is an r‐dimensional vector that interacts r unobserved common factors ft1,…,ftr with rj‐specific weights λj1,…,λjr that capture the extent to which each unit j depends on each common unobserved factor. This approach augments traditional two‐way fixed effects (TWFE) formulations as the interactive fixed effect accommodates the standard unit and time fixed effects, which we explicitly write separately in Equation (1) for clarity. Compared to TWFE, the IFE term allows for much more flexibility in modeling unobserved time trends that may be common among a subset of the treated and control units.

Xjt includes controls that vary within states over time. To control for changing Medicare reimbursement rates that may influence the relative attractiveness of entering private practice, we take the Medicare reimbursement rate for the therapy‐related CPT code 90837, which covers a one hour block of talk therapy, and which changes in January of each year. To control for relative shifts in in‐state demand for talk therapy that may drive entry, we collect state‐specific monthly trends in the search volume from Google Trends for the mental‐health terms “anxiety” and “depression.” Finally, to control for the Medicaid “unwinding” that took place in 2023 as Medicaid rolls were purged after the end of the COVID continuous coverage provision, we control for the total number of individuals enrolled in Medicaid in a state in each month (KFF 2026).

Estimation

Estimation of this model proceeds in two steps (Xu 2017). First, the control units are used to recover the parameters β, the time‐varying factors ft, and the factor loadings for the control units λj. Second, the vector of factor loadings λj for each treated unit is chosen to minimize the sum of squared pre‐treatment residuals given βˆ and fˆt from the first stage. This vector of factor loadings for treated units, combined with the time‐varying factors, yields the counterfactual synthetic controls for each unit. The δjt parameters are then estimated for each treated unit by comparing post‐treatment outcomes to the predicted outcomes of the treated unit's synthetic control.

Compared to Abadie (2021), where synthetic control weights for a treated unit are chosen to minimize the distance between the treated unit and control unit pre‐treatment outcomes, the procedure in Xu (2017) first projects control unit outcomes into a lower‐dimensional space of time‐varying factors, and fits the synthetic control weights for treated units to this space. The advantage of this approach versus standard synthetic control estimation is that it supports multiple treated units, staggered treatment times, and frequentist inference through bootstrapping. In the next section, we discuss the causal identification assumptions.

Identification

The treatment effects δjt are identified by the difference between the assumed counterfactual outcomes, and the actual observed outcomes of treated units. Compared to TWFE the identification assumptions are slightly weaker, as treated units need only exhibit parallel trends with synthetic controls conditional on the latent unobserved time‐varying heterogeneity captured by the estimated factors ft1,…,ftr. Moreover, only never‐treated units are used for comparisons with treated units post‐treatment, so treated units' post‐treatment outcomes do not contaminate the comparison group (Roth et al. 2023). We next describe the identification assumptions in detail and provide evidence that they hold.

First, conditional on the fixed effects, factors ft, weights λj, and observables Xjs, treatment assignment must be conditionally random. If treatment assignment is indeed random and exogenous to the set of estimated factors, then each treated unit should exhibit no pre‐trend in outcome variables relative to its synthetic counterfactual control. In Figure 5, we show that the model does well in matching the predicted log number of psychologists for the synthetic counterfactual to the pre‐treatment patterns in log psychologists for each of the treated states. In Supporting Information S1: Web Appendix B.1, we plot the event studies for the five basic outcomes (log number of psychologists, average costs, average share offering income discounts, average number of insurers accepted, and average graduation year) with standard errors and show that there are no pre‐trends.

Synthetic control fit: normalized logged number of listed psychologists. Each graph depicts the normalized log number of listed psychologists for the treated state (solid), and the predicted synthetic control for each treated state (dashed). The synthetic control is found by (1) projecting the treated state outcome onto the estimated time‐varying factors in the pre‐treatment period to find weights, and then (2) using the weights with the time‐varying factors to simulate the counterfactual post‐treatment outcome for that treated state. We normalize by the value of each treated state's data in the first period to facilitate comparison of magnitudes across states.

Second, there should be no anticipation. For Florida, Mississippi, and South Carolina, there is almost no gap between when PSYPACT is enacted and when it is effective, and thus there is no scope for anticipation. For Rhode Island, Vermont, and Michigan, there is no visual evidence of anticipatory changes in either Figure 3 or Figure 5.

Third, the data generating process should satisfy the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA), which requires that the outcome for a particular state depends only on its treatment (or non‐treatment) status and not on the status of other states. 14 In theory, SUTVA may be violated in our setting: for example, if an out‐of‐state psychologist is deciding between entering Florida as a second market or entering New York as a second market, then when Florida joins PSYPACT it may attract the entrant at New York's expense, leading the control state's number of psychologists to be contaminated. We conduct an analysis in Section 5 that suggests that such violations are not likely to occur in practice.

Fourth, there should be no confounding contemporaneous state‐month shocks that are correlated with both PSYPACT accession and psychologist decision‐making. In robustness checks in Section 4.5, we use the behavior of counselors, social workers, and marriage and family therapists on PsychologyToday.com to conduct placebo checks, under the assumption that their decisions are plausibly unaffected by PSYPACT but would reflect more general state‐level mental health shocks that might threaten identification. We also try placebo regression using non‐mental healthcare providers in outpatient care, including optometrists, chiropractors, and physical therapists.

Finally, similarly to standard synthetic control analysis, treated units' pre‐treatment paths should lie within the convex hull of control units' pre‐treatment paths for the estimated counterfactual control paths to be credible (Abadie 2021). To satisfy this requirement, in the data construction section, we selected only those treated states in Table 1 whose number of listed psychologists lie between control states' numbers of listed psychologists in July 2022.

Our main finding is that the number of psychologists is 7.7% higher after treatment in states that accede to PSYPACT, on average. The result suggests that PSYPACT caused psychologists who did not previously list themselves in a state pre‐accession to begin listing themselves after accession.

With respect to psychologist pricing, we find no differential effect of the accession on the average log hourly rate. Note that prices go up in all states over this time period (from $155 per hour on average in July $2022 to $188 per hour on average in December 2024), but there is no differential impact of the accession on prices in treated states. Intuitively, in this credence‐good market, the psychologist's posted price may be used to indicate quality, an incentive which is heightened by the fact that there are no reviews or paid boosts on the platform that psychologists can use as a signal. Similarly, we find no effect of PSYPACT accession on the average propensity to offer income discounts.

We find a positive effect of PSYPACT on the number of insurance providers psychologists accept. This change increases affordability, as many psychologists choose not to accept insurance because private pay rates are higher than insurance reimbursement (Zhu et al. 2024; American Psychological Association 2024). We explore whether this expansion in acceptance is driven by an increase in the propensity to accept public options in Supporting Information S1: Web Appendix B.2, and find no effect on psychologists' propensity to accept Medicare, and a slightly negative, marginally significant effect on the propensity to accept Medicaid.

Finally, we find a positive, significant effect of PSYPACT accession on the average graduation year of the psychologists operating in a state. This directly implies that new listings in PSYPACT states tend to be slightly less experienced compared to control states.

Overall, the increased number of providers, and the increase in the average number of insurers accepted, suggests that for patients searching within the state, not only the variety of providers on the platform but the ease of accessing those providers increased after PSYPACT accession. The lack of price effects suggests that PSYPACT was not pro‐competitive on this dimension, while the increase in graduation year implies that the reduction in geographic barriers attracted entry by less experienced psychologists.

Placebo and Robustness Checks

We conduct placebo tests where we replicate Table 3 using three other sets of mental health care providers on PsychologyToday.com: counselors, social workers, and marriage and family therapists. Intuitively, if psychologists are responding to PSYPACT accession and not some other contemporaneous broader mental health shock, these other mental health care providers should exhibit no change in outcomes after PSYPACT accession. We believe that these groups should be relatively unaffected by a psychologist‐specific policy like PSYPACT, since they outnumber psychologists 9 to 1 in our sample and compete much more strongly on price ($130 per hour vs. psychologists' $174 per hour). For all three groups of providers we find no significant increase in listed providers in a state after PSYPACT accession, and no change in any other outcome variable (see Supporting Information S1: Web Appendix B.3). Mechanism Analysis

There are three plausible mechanisms by which PSYPACT could increase listings in an acceding state. First, out‐of‐state providers may now find it worthwhile to list in the acceding state since the fixed cost of getting licensed and certified is lower. Second, in‐state providers may respond to a perceived change in the competitive environment by newly listing themselves online—a defensive advertising response. Third, in‐state providers may begin listing themselves in‐state if they perceive that the in‐state market has expanded. In the following sections we evaluate the evidence for each mechanism by disaggregating the increase in listings and conducting heterogeneity analysis.

Out‐Of‐State Entry

We first consider whether the increase in listings in acceding states is driven by outside psychologists entering. We estimate Equation (1) using the average number of states served by each provider operating in a state as the outcome. A positive treatment effect could reflect an increase in out‐of‐state entrants, or an increase in the number of states served by in‐state providers. In Column 1 of Table 4, we find that the average number of states served by psychologists operating in a treated state does not significantly change by 1 year after accession compared to the synthetic control (see Supporting Information S1: Web Appendix C.1 for the event study plot). This null result suggests that entering psychologists are not more likely to be multi‐state, consistent with the preliminary evidence in Figure 4. This regression also rules out that incumbent psychologists are adding markets after accession.

To provide more direct evidence on the external entrants channel, we consider the set of 7093 APIT holders that we matched to PsychologyToday.com profiles. For each treated and control state j in each month t, we count the number of APIT‐holding psychologists whose APIT home state is outside of state j, but who are observed to operate in state j. 16 For example, for the state of Florida in January 2024, we add up all psychologists who list themselves in a Florida zipcode on PsychologyToday.com that month but who have an APIT based out of a non‐Florida PSYPACT state. If a state joining PSYPACT leads to an influx of virtual providers operating under PSYPACT licenses, then it should be reflected in an increase in this group of psychologists after accession. Moreover, this count will capture any “single‐state entrants”—psychologists based in PSYPACT states other than j who list themselves exclusively in a treated (or control) state j—who would not have been captured by the multi‐state regression.

We estimate Equation (1) using this count of psychologists (transformed using log(1+x)). 17 In Column 2 of Table 4, we show that there is no increase in the number of external APIT holders in a state after it accedes to PSYPACT. Together, the null results in Column 1 and 2 of Table 4 show that when a state joins PSYPACT, the increase in listed psychologists is not driven by out‐of‐state psychologists entering. The increase must therefore come from in‐state psychologists beginning to list themselves on PsychologyToday.com. This analysis also suggests that the SUTVA violations discussed in Section 4.3 are unlikely to be a problem. Recall that if out‐of‐state entrants decide between treatment and control states, then there will be spillovers from treatment to control as treatment states become a more attractive prospect post‐accession. Because entry by out‐of‐state psychologists is very minimal for the treated states during the sample period, the practical consequences of multi‐state entry for SUTVA violations is also likely minimal.

In‐State Entry: Defensive Advertising Versus Market Expansion

Since there is no entry by out‐of‐state psychologists, the increase in psychologists must reflect in‐state providers beginning to list themselves. 18 There are two conceivable rationales for doing so, which may both be operating. The new listings may be psychologists who believe that they are now facing greater competition, and who engage in defensive advertising (Thomas 1999). The new listings may also be psychologists who perceive that PSYPACT has expanded their market. In particular, an in‐state psychologist can now serve patients who travel across state lines into other PSYPACT states, as long as the psychologist gets an APIT.

To evaluate these mechanisms, we split in‐state psychologists into two groups. First, we examine psychologists operating in state j who do not have an APIT. Since this group cannot take advantage of PSYPACT, if listings increase for this group then it must reflect defensive advertising. We estimate Equation (1) using the number of psychologists in this group (transformed using log(1+x)) as the dependent variable, and report the results in Column 3 of Table 4. We find a significant increase in listings by non‐APIT holders in PSYPACT states after accession relative to the synthetic control constructed from non‐PSYPACT states.

Second, we examine psychologists operating in state j who have an APIT with their home state set as state j. If listings for this group increase after accession, then it must reflect a desire to use PSYPACT to serve patients in their state who are mobile across state lines. In Figure 2, we already established that there is substantial take up of APITs in treated states; however, this does not establish that the cause of the increase in listings is new entrants holding APITs. To check this mechanism, we examine whether new listings based in state j have high APIT adoption relative to incumbent listings in state j. Intuitively, incumbent behavior captures the baseline attractiveness of getting an APIT in state j, and if entrants get APITs at higher rates than that, it suggests PSYPACT is actually driving the new listings. 19

In Figure 7, we compare the propensity of new entrants into treated states to have an APIT, relative to the propensity of incumbents to have an APIT. New entrants are psychologists whose listing in state j appears for the first time that month, while incumbents are all remaining psychologists listed in that state. We find that entrants are not more likely to acquire an APIT than incumbents. While there is a desire to serve mobile patients, as evidenced by the moderate APIT take‐up rate among incumbents, this propensity is not especially high among entrants, suggesting that this desire is not driving the increase in entry in acceding states.

If incumbents are strategically responding to the perceived threat of entry, then in states and zipcodes where that threat is larger, we should expect a greater incumbent response.

We first check whether states that have higher ex ante licensing barriers experience greater increases in listings. Intuitively, after PSYPACT, a state that had higher barriers ex ante experiences a greater relative reduction in the fixed cost of entry, and so is more exposed to the threat of external entry. In such a state, incumbents should therefore respond more aggressively. Mechanism Discussion

Our analysis shows that incumbent responses, not external entry, drives the overall effect of PSYPACT. The idea that incumbents may wish to undertake costly “fighting” actions—such as increasing advertising—in response to the threat of entry is well‐established in industrial organization (Dixit 1980; Milgrom and Roberts 1982; Thomas 1999; Ellison and Ellison 2011). Incumbents have also been shown empirically to use advertising to buffer against increased external competition when international markets open (Ramani and Srinivasan 2019; Ramani 2025). Incumbents' choice to increase the number of insurers they accept after PSYPACT accession also speaks to increased differentiation: out‐of‐state providers face large hurdles in getting credentialed with local insurance providers, which makes insured patients a market where in‐state providers do not need to compete with external entrants. 21

The null impact of PSYPACT accession on out‐of‐state entry from Section 4.4 may imply an equilibrium outcome, where incumbents' defensive advertising was successful in discouraging entrants. Yet the absence of an entry effect is not proof positive of the equilibrium, and the average number of new in‐state listings is relatively small to have a strong discouraging impact on new out‐of‐state entrants.

There are several other explanations for why a psychologist may wish to list their practice after their state joins PSYPACT, even if virtual entrants do not materialize in local search results on PsychologyToday.com. First, as in the quote in Section 1, psychologists may hold strong beliefs about competition and react based on those beliefs. This may be an example of businesses not holding correct beliefs (Goldfarb and Xiao 2011; DellaVigna and Gentzkow 2019), or simply not being aware of the lack of entry spurred by PSYPACT. Second, patients in that state may now realize that they have a wider set of options available online. If patients rely less on referrals and more on online search, incumbents would benefit from starting to list themselves online as well. Third, hospitals and clinics based in a joining state may now start to hire psychologists from other states to remotely serve their patients. If these entrants displace incumbent psychologists, then these psychologists may turn to listing themselves online in order to grow their private practice clients.

Accessibility of Care

A stated goal of PSYPACT is to increase access to care in underserved areas. We explore two dimensions to this issue: whether underserved states exhibit greater increases in listings, and whether underserved zipcodes within states experience greater increases in listings. Since we find in Section 5 that there is no external entry in response to PSYPACT, the present analysis explores whether existing incumbent providers in a state become easier to find by patients in underserved areas by listing themselves on PsychologyToday.com. Any welfare improvement should therefore be conceptualized as a reduction in search costs.

We first take the state‐level estimates of treatment effects from Section 5.3 and correlate them with measures that capture whether a state is underserved. In Figure 9, we show that whether using (1) the number of listed PsychologyToday.com psychologists per 100,000 residents, (2) the share of the state population in an HPSA, or (3) the number of mental healthcare providers per 100,000 residents required to remove all HPSA areas in a state, the evidence is directionally consistent with underserved areas experiencing larger treatment effects. This may reflect an altruistic desire by incumbent psychologists. However, it may also reflect that measures of being underserved are correlated with higher ex ante licensing fees, which we found to be positively correlated with new entry in Section 5.3.

Discussion and Limitations

A key finding in our research is that PSYPACT did not induce cross‐state entry by external providers. While it did induce in‐state providers to list themselves on the search portal for the first time, this is likely only a marginal improvement to patient accessibility. It is important to understand this limitation of PSYPACT, since there have recently been pushes to establish similar compacts for counselors (Counseling Compact Commission 2025) and social workers (Council of State Governments 2025), partly on the basis of increasing accessibility.

For policymakers, a consequence of our findings is that they should not rely soley on the supply‐side behavior of mental health providers to alleviate local shortages—either for PSYPACT, or for other in‐progress licensure compacts. Providing information on how the compact works to patients, primary care physicians, and hospitals may be more fruitful, as it can help patients in compact states access a much wider pool of providers than what is available locally or in‐state.

We also find that there is substantial heterogeneity across states in the treatment effect of PSYPACT. Mississippi, South Carolina, and Rhode Island saw clear post‐accession increases in total listings, while Florida saw only moderate gains and Michigan and Vermont saw minimal effects. In the causal estimates, state‐specific treatment effects on total listings span roughly −1%–15%. In states that are well‐served prior to accession we find minimal treatment effects, which should serve to set expectations for policymakers who wish to implement licensure compacts in the future.

A limitation of our study is that the data on psychologist market entry does not cover states that acceded to PSYPACT prior to July 2022. Having time‐restricted outcome data that does not cover early policy changes is typical in the licensure literature (Markowitz et al. 2017; Alexander and Schnell 2019). A special consideration with PSYPACT is that the more states that have already joined, the more psychologists a potential joining state will gain access to, and the greater the perceived threat of entry among in‐state providers: this creates a setting where the treatment effect on late‐joining states is likely to be larger than the effect on early joining states. We acknowledge this limitation, and caution against interpreting our findings as the overall average effect of PSYPACT on psychologists' supply decisions in all states.

Conclusion

In this paper, we estimate how reducing psychologists' inter‐state licensing barriers affects market entry. Using the staggered rollout of PSYPACT, an interjurisdictional compact that allows psychologists licensed in one member state to practice telemedicine in any other member state, we find that a state's entry into PSYPACT leads to 7.7% more psychologist listings in that state, but no new external entrants. We argue that these new in‐state listings are motivated by the perceived threat of entry, which is consistent with their choices to list in areas where the threat of entry is highest.

While we find a limited impact on cross‐state entry on the supply side, future research should explore whether PSYPACT sparks an increase in cross‐state utilization of mental health resources from the demand side—potentially by analyzing claims data. Moreover, more work on psychologists' decisions to enter markets is needed to understand exactly what barriers there are to serving patients out‐of‐state through telemedicine on the supply side. Future research can also explore the potential for informational or peer/referral interventions to help patients in underserved areas in PSYPACT states actually access the deep pool of available telemedicine providers available to them.
