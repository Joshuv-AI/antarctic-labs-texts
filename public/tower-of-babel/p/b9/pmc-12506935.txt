Ligand-receptor interactions induce and mediate regulatory functions of BATF3+ B cells

ABSTRACT
B cells express many protein ligands, yet their regulatory functions are incompletely understood. We profiled ligand expression across murine B sublineage cells, including those activated by defined receptor signals, and assessed their regulatory capacities and specificities through in silico analysis of ligand-receptor interactions. Consequently, we identified a B cell subset that expressed cytokine interleukin-27 (IL-27) and chemokine CXCL10. Through the IL-27–IL-27 receptor interaction, these IL-27/CXCL10-producing B cells targeted CD40-activated B cells in vitro and, upon induction by immunization and viral infection, optimized antibody responses and antiviral immunity in vivo. Also present in breast cancer tumors and retained there through CXCL10-CXCR3 interaction–mediated self-targeting, these cells promoted B cell PD-L1 expression and immune evasion. Mechanistically, Il27 and Cxcl10 transcription was induced by synergizing Toll-like receptor (TLR) and CD40 signals and driven by coinduced transcription factor BATF3, which directly targeted these genes. By applying a discovery framework focusing on regulatory cells, our findings expand the recognized scope of B cell regulatory functions.

INTRODUCTION

The host immunity controls infections and cancers through effector cells and molecules. B lymphocytes secrete antibodies with diverse effector functions, particularly upon engagement of Toll-like receptor (TLRs) by microbe-derived ligands or CD40 by CD4+ T cell–expressed CD154 (1–3). Subsequent receptor signals induce B effector cell differentiation processes, e.g., immunoglobulin (Ig) class switch DNA recombination (CSR) and generation of germinal center (GC) B cells, plasma cells, and memory B cells. B cells also play antibody-independent immune regulatory roles (4, 5). Secreted and surface protein ligands (such as cytokines, chemokines, adhesion molecules, and Ig superfamily proteins) and their receptors are major immune modulators and highly expressed by immune cells (6). Interleukin-10 (IL-10), IL-12, IL-27, IL-35, and CCL22 can be produced by B cells to regulate antibody response, antiviral immunity, cancer, and inflammation (7–13). Despite these and other seminal findings as well as previous ligand profiling study (14, 15), the regulatory capacity and specificity of B cells remained murky due to lack of systematic investigations into their ligand expression and target cell engagement. In addition, none of the ligand-producing B cells have been shown to have a hallmark transcription factor (TF), hindering the definition of their sublineage identities. This contrasts the well-defined B cell subsets at different development and effector cell differentiation stages and other regulatory cells, e.g., CD4+ T cells. Last, previous in silico analyses suggest that B cell regulatory functions were not confirmed by functional analyses, leaving a knowledge gap (6, 16).

Here, we determined the scope and specificities of B cell regulatory functions by profiling the expression of protein ligand-encoding transcripts in different B cell subsets and other immune cells [Fig. 1A (1)] and using a bioinformatics pipeline to catalog interlineage and intra–B lineage cell-cell communications (CCCs) and underpinning ligand-receptor interactions (LRIs) [Fig. 1A (2)]. We further provided direct evidence for the relevance of selected CCC-LRI predictions by “reconstituting” inter–B cell communications in vitro and using animal models of the antibody response, antiviral immunity, and breast cancer (BCa) [Fig. 1A (3,4)]. Last, we investigated the mechanisms underlying specific ligand expression in B cell subsets by identifying hallmark TFs and transcription regulation modalities [Fig. 1A (5-1)]. In particular, we uncovered a B cell subset that expressed BATF3 as the characteristic TF, induced cytokine IL-27 and chemokine CXCL10 as the unique ligands, used Il27 → Il27ra and Cxcl10 → Cxcr3 LRIs to target different B cell subsets in silico, and played a regulatory role in diverse pathophysiological conditions in vivo.

RESULTS

Profile ligands as relevant to B cell regulatory functions

Ligands and receptors made up one-third of murine “communication factors,” defined as secreted proteins and membrane-associated proteins with at least one extracellularly oriented domain (Fig. 1B). Their locus accessibility and expression profiles across B cell developmental stages and immunization-induced differentiation stages in vivo are publicly available [Immunological Genome Project (ImmGen)]. Building on this, we expanded ATAC-seq and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analyses to freshly isolated B cells (B.Nil) and B cells that were activated by defined stimuli in vitro, reasoning that such activation would polarize B cells to produce specific immune modulators, similar to the defined in vitro polarization of CD4+ T cells. The B cell stimuli included TLR4-engaging lipopolysaccharide (LPS; yielding B.LPS cells) to mimic a dominating signal during the early extrafollicular (EF) responses in vivo, CD154 (B.CD154) as the main T cell–dependent signal, or both (B.LC) to mimic sequential activation, either alone or in the presence of IL-21 (B.LC21), the signature cytokine of T follicular helper (TFH) cells within GCs (data tables S1 and S2).

B.Nil and stimulated B cells together expressed 517 ligand/receptor-encoding genes (table S1). While 356 of these genes were constitutively expressed, 104 showed little expression in B.Nil cells but were up-regulated by at least one stimulation condition and thus considered inducible (table S1). Their frequency (20%) was higher than that of inducible communication factor genes and genes in general (11% each), likely reflecting their readiness to respond to environmental changes (table S1). Based on the degree of their locus accessibility up-regulation in stimulated B cells compared to B.Nil cells (data table S3), inducible ligand and receptor-encoding genes could be segregated into two clusters (Fig. 1C), with one cluster showing over 250-fold increases, particularly in the promoter region (fig. S1A). By contrast, ligand/receptor genes that were constitutively active or silent rarely displayed up-regulation in their locus accessibility or transcription (Fig. 1C). B.LPS, B.CD154, and B.LC cells each displayed signature inducible ligands and receptors (Fig. 1D and data table S4). Notably, B.LC cells expressed inducible ligand-encoding genes at an overall higher level than B.LPS and B.CD154 cells, in contrast to the comparable expression of inducible communication genes and all genes across different B cells (fig. S1B).

In summary, B cells induce specific ligands upon activation, potentially enhancing their regulatory capacities. In particular, B cells coengaged through TLR4 and CD40 are likely to acquire new and augmented regulatory functions.

Identify IL-27 and CXCL10 as specifically induced ligands

Among 71 genes expressed at higher levels in B.LC and B.LC21 cells combined (B.LC/21) compared to B.LPS and B.CD154 cells combined (data table S5), Il27p28 and Cxcl10 were the ligand genes showing the most significant changes (Fig. 1E). In B.LC/21 cells, the accessibility of the Il27p28 locus was highly induced, while that of the Cxcl10 locus showed moderate up-regulation compared to B.Nil cells (Fig. 1C, fig. S1C, and data table S6). Il27p28 transcript levels peaked at 36 to 48 hours after stimulation in B.LC cells (fig. S1D) and were further elevated by IL-21 (fig. S1E). These, along with Ebi3 up-regulation, led to the secretion of IL-27 [a heterodimer of IL-27p28 and Epstein-Barr virus-induced gene 3 (EBI3)] at high levels, comparable to those produced by LPS-activated macrophages (Fig. 1F). CXCL10 was also secreted at levels similar to those induced by interferon-γ (IFN-γ) (Fig. 1F), the prototypical CXCL10 inducer (17). By contrast, Il10 was induced only by LPS, while Il12a (encoding IL-12α, which forms IL-12 with IL-12β as well as IL-35 with EBI3) was highly expressed in B.Nil cells and thus not up-regulated (fig. S1, C and D).

Like costimulation, priming B cells with LPS for 6 hours followed by CD154 and IL-21 stimulation induced Il27p28 and Ebi3 transcription, IL-27 secretion (fig. S1F), and green fluorescent protein (GFP) expression driven by the Tg(Il27p28-Gfp) reporter transgene (Fig. 1G). GFP+ B.LC21 cells were CD11c−CD11b−CD1d− and showed heterogeneous expression of IgD, the GC B cell marker FAS, and the plasma cell marker CD138 (fig. S1G). Priming with any TLR ligand up-regulated CD40, whose engagement by an agonistic antibody (αCD40) led to its internalization and GFP expression (fig. S1, H and I). By contrast, priming with B cell receptor (BCR) cross-linking resulted in poor Il27p28 induction (fig. S1J), as did reversing the order of LPS and CD154 stimulation (fig. S1F).

In vivo, Il27p28 transcripts were expressed by B cells within bronchial-associated lymphoid clusters upon intranasal infection with vaccinia virus (Western Reserve strain; VVWR) in C57 mice (Fig. 1H). In VVWR-infected Tg(Il27p28-Gfp) mice, GFP+ and IL-27p28+ B cells emerged in the draining mediastinal lymph node (mLN) and spleen, beginning at day 3 and markedly increasing by day 7 (Fig. 1I). Within GFP+ B cells, IL-27p28 and EBI3 were up-regulated (fig. S1K). The transcriptome of sorted spleen CD19+IgD−GFP+ B cells most closely resembled that of plasmablasts among B sublineage cells (fig. S1L and data table S7). Such B.IgD−GFP+ cells expressed inducible ligand and receptor genes at higher levels than their B.IgD+ and B.IgD−GFP− counterparts (Fig. 1J). Compared to B.IgD−GFP− cells, their up-regulated genes included Il27p28 and Cxcl10, indicating coexpression of IL-27 and CXCL10, and were enriched (47/71) of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) found at higher levels in B.LC/21 cells compared to B.LPS and B.CD154 cells (fig. S1M). Thus, IL-27 and CXCL10 are specifically induced in B cells by coengagement or sequential engagement of TLR and CD40 receptors in vitro and are up-regulated in B cells upon virus infection in vivo.

Determine ligand expression specificity in the immune system

To analyze regulatory functions of B cells mediated by protein ligands, including IL-27 and CXCL10, we first developed a bioinformatics pipeline to predict LRIs underpinning CCCs between immune regulatory and target cells [Fig. 1A (2)]. The pipeline used normalized bulk RNA-seq data of 225 samples representing 46 immune cell lineages/sublineages (i.e., four in vitro activated B cells and three B subsets from VVWR-infected mice profiled here, and six combined B cell subsets and 33 other cell types from ImmGen; data table S8). To analyze CCCs between a ligand-producing regulatory cell (CellL) and a receptor-expressing target cell (CellR), the pipeline focused on high-confidence LRIs involving ligands with cell type–specific expression. Only the 474 ligands capable of engaging in high-confidence LRIs were included for further analysis (fig. S2A and data table S9). For each cell type, the expression specificity of a given ligand was calculated by counting the number of the other 45 cell types in which the ligand’s expression levels were lower than in the cell type of interest (data table S10), resulting in a metric termed the “specificity score” for the given ligand in the cell type (data table S11). The top 100 ligand specificity scores per cell type were visualized using radar plots (fig. S2B). A large plot area indicated marked distinctiveness of the corresponding cell, e.g., group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s), macrophages, neutrophils, endothelial cells, and stroma cells. The Il27p28 and Cxcl10 specificity scores were both high in B.LC/21 and B.IgD−GFP+ cells as well as in neutrophils, dendritic cells (DCs), and epithelial cells (Fig. 2A). In total, 199 ligands had at least one top 10 specificity score and paired with 289 receptors to form 489 LRIs, which were included in further analysis (fig. S2A).

We next calculated the “interaction scores” of the 489 LRIs in unidirectional CellL:CellR pairs, excluded LRIs with scores near zero due to low receptor expression, and computed the ratio of each remaining 418 LRI’s interaction score in a given CCC to its average score across all 2116 CCCs among the 46 cell types. This resulted in a CCC-LRI enrichment score matrix (data table S12), which could be visualized directly as a global heatmap or simplified to highlight top CCCs for a given LRI using a bubble plot and to highlight top LRIs contributing to a specific CCC using a bar graph (fig. S2A). As shown by the global heatmap (Fig. 2B), nonhematopoietic innate cells (stroma and endothelial cells) exhibited the greatest number of high-enriched LRIs when acting as CellL, followed by hematopoietic innate cells (ILCs, polymorphonuclear cells, macrophages, DCs, and monocyte cells) and then adaptive cells [B, T, natural killer (NK), and NKT cells]. Activated CD4+ T cells and regulatory T (Treg) cells used Il21 → Il21r and Ctla4 → Cd28/Cd80/Cd86, respectively, to function as CellL, as also shown in the bubble plots (fig. S2C). Such LRIs would play a major role in the T cell:B cell CCC, as illustrated by the bar graph (fig. S2D).

Predict scope and specificity of B cell regulatory functions

As shown by the global enrichment score heatmap, B sublineage cells, when acting as CellL, shared enriched LRIs with other adaptive immune cells, such as T, NK, and NKT cells (Fig. 2B). B.IgD−GFP+ cells and, to lesser extents, plasma cells and B.Nil cells used additional LRIs (Fig. 2B, dashed rectangle). For specific LRIs, Il10 → Il10ra was used by B.LPS cells to engage B cells, T cells, DCs, and macrophages, while Il12a → Il12rb1 was used by multiple B subsets to engage B cells, T cells, NK cells, and ILCs (Fig. 2C), consistent with and extending known regulatory functions of IL-10– and IL-12/IL-35–expressing B cells. Representing previously unrecognized regulatory functions, Gh → Ghr and Efbn1 → Epha4 were used, although not exclusively, by B cells to target endothelial and stroma cells (Fig. 2C).

To predict LRIs that would underpin specific CCCs involving B sublineage cells as CellL, we prioritized B.IgD−GFP+ cells, which exhibited a high number of enriched LRIs, and their in vitro counterparts, B.LC/21 cells. Both B.IgD−GFP+ and B.LC/21 cells used Il27 → Il27ra as one of their top three LRIs to target naïve CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2D). This, along with their use of Il27 → Il27ra to target central memory and memory precursor CD8+ T cells (fig. S2E) as well as Treg cells (fig. S2F), would support a role of IL-27+ B cells in CD8+ T cell responses through IL-27 receptor (IL-27R) and a role of IL-27 in promoting Treg functions (18, 19). Both B.IgD−GFP+ and B.LC/21 cells also used Cxcl10 → Cxcr3 (encoding CXCL10 receptor CXCR3) as one of their top five LRIs to target central memory and memory precursor CD8+ T cells (fig. S2E). As further illustrated by the bubble plots, Il27 → Il27ra and Cxcl10 → Cxcr3 were used by B.IgD−GFP+ and B.LC/21 cells, as well as other immune cells, to engage NKT and ILC2 cells (Fig. 2C), consistent with the top positions of both LRIs in the bar graphs involving NKT cells as CellR (fig. S2G) and of Il27 → Il27ra in the bar graphs involving ILC2 cells as CellR (fig. S2H).

We next focused on intra-B lineage CCCs, which would be prevalent in B cell–rich secondary lymphoid follicles and tertiary lymphoid structures. Including Il27 → Il27ra and Cxcl10 → Cxcr3, 36 LRIs were used by both B.IgD−GFP+ and B.LC/21 cells as CellL to target 13 B sublineage cells as CellR (Fig. 2E). These LRIs clustered into two major groups. The first cluster was characterized by Il27 → Il27ra, Vegfa → Itgb3, and Vegfa → Itgav as the major LRIs and developing, naïve, B.CD154 and B.GC cells as the main CellR. The second cluster was defined by Cxcl10 → Cxcr3, Ccl3/Ccl5 → Ccr1/Ccr3, Efna2/Efna4 → Epha2/Epha4, and Vegfa → Nrp1/Kdr with B.LPS cells, plasma cells, and IL-27+CXCL10+ B cells themselves as CellR (Fig. 2E). Thus, B cells exhibit a substantial regulatory capacity among adaptive immune cells, although less than that of innate cells, with different subsets engaging specific target cells through distinct LRIs, including Il27 → Il27ra and Cxcl10 → Cxcr3.

Validate IL-27+ B cell–B cell CCC in vitro and its role in antiviral immunity

Informed by the enrichment score–based predictions (Fig. 2E), we next validated the targeting of B cells (CellR) by B.LC/21 cells (CellL) and the LRIs involved [Fig. 1A (3)]. B.LC cells per se displayed reduced IgV(D)J gene transcription (Fig. 3A), Ca2+ flux in response to BCR cross-linking (Fig. 3B), CSR to different Ig isotypes (Fig. 3C), and plasma cell differentiation (Fig. 3D) compared to B.LPS and/or B.CD154 cells. These results suggested that B.LC/21 cells mainly functioned not as antibody-producing effector B cells, but rather as ligand-producing regulatory B cells that affected the differentiation of target B cells. To test this, we reconstituted the B.LC21:B.CD154 CCC in vitro. In the presence of CD45.1+ B.LC21 cells, CSR to IgG2a was induced in CD45.2+ B.CD154 and B cells stimulated with CD154 plus IL-21 (B.C21 cells), but less so in B.LPS cells; the response of B.Nil cells as targets could not be evaluated due to their inactive state (Fig. 3E and fig. S3A). CD45.1+ B.LC21 cells also synergized with IFN-γ to promote induction of CSR to IgG2a in B.C21 cells (Fig. 3E), with the synergy observed across most cell divisions (fig. S3B). Likewise, B cells isolated from CD45.2+ C57 mice and stimulated with LPS plus αCD40 and IL-21 (B.LA21 cells, equivalent to B.LC21 cells), together with IFN-γ, induced CSR to IgG2a in CD45.1+ B.C21 cells (Fig. 3F). When CD45.2+ Il27p28−/− and Ebi3−/− B.LA21 cells were used as CellL, CSR induction was reduced, showing an important role of the Il27 → Il27ra LRI in mediating the B.LA21 cell:B.C21 cell CCC, which might also be augmented by additional LRIs involving ligands up-regulated by autocrinic IL-27 in CellL. Consistent with its role in directing CSR to IgG2a, IL-27 induced Iγ2a-Cγ2a germline transcription in B.CD154 cells (fig. S3C) and up-regulated genes known to be induced by IFN-γ, such as Tbx21, which encodes TF T-bet (Fig. 3G and data table S13). IL-27 also collaborated with IFN-γ to further up-regulate Iγ2a-Cγ2a and Tbx21 transcript levels (fig. S3C). Last, IL-27 promoted IL-21–dependent plasma cell differentiation in B.C21 cells (fig. S3, D and E) and enhanced the expression of Prdm1, which encodes B lymphocyte-induced maturation protein 1 (BLIMP1), the master TF of plasma cell differentiation (fig. S3F).

To address the functional relevance of Il27 → Il27ra LRI-mediated intra-B lineage communications in vivo, we analyzed B effector cell differentiation in mice with B cell–specific deficiency in IL-27 or its receptor. Upon VVWR infection, mb1creIl27p28fl/fl mice exhibited impaired secretion of virus-specific IgG2a, the murine IgG isotype that appears earliest during viral infections and has strong antiviral functions (20), and failed to clear the virus (Fig. 3H). This was associated with reduced plasma cell differentiation and CSR to IgG2a, but normal CSR to IgG1, which indicated no gross B cell defects. Likewise, mixed bone marrow chimera mice with B cell–specific deficiency in Ebi3 (μMT:Ebi3−/− mice; Fig. 3I) or IL-27R (μMT:Il27ra−/− mice; Fig. 3J and fig. S3G) showed impaired virus-specific IgG2a responses, reduced production of neutralizing IgGs, defective virus clearance, and increased lung pathology. These findings together confirmed the important role of IL-27+ and IL-27R+ B cells as CellL and CellR, respectively. In summary, IL-27+ B cells target effector B cells through the Il27 → Il27ra LRI to promote CSR to IgG2a and plasma cell differentiation in vitro and mediate the antiviral IgG2a antibody response in vivo.

Validate the role of IL-27+ B cell–B cell CCC in antibody responses

Similar to virus infection, immunization of Tg(Il27p28-Gfp) mice with 4-hydroxy-3-nitrophenyl acetyl (NP)–chicken γ-globulin (CGG) combined with LPS and alum as adjuvants elicited GFP+ B cells, which, like their in vitro counterparts, displayed variable IgD, FAS, and CD138 expression levels and showed loss of surface CD40 (fig. S4A). The GFP+ B cells, which were moderately enriched in NP-binding BCRs, accounted for 5% of all B cells (fig. S4B), a proportion comparable to that of IL-27p28+ B cells induced in immunized C57 (fig. S4C) and Il10IRES-Gfp mice (Fig. 4A). In the latter, some B cells showed concentrated IL-27p28 signals in plasma membrane protrusions resembling secretory vesicles. In both immunized (Fig. 4A) and VVWR-infected (fig. S4D) Il10IRES-Gfp mice, IL-27p28+ B cells were distinct from Il10-GFP+ B cells, reflecting different induction pathways for Il27p28 and Il10, e.g., in B.LC/21 and B.LPS cells, respectively (fig. S1, C and D, and 4E).

IL-27p28+ B cells substantially contributed to the overall IL-27p28 expression (fig. S4F), consistent with reduced Il27p28 and Ebi3 expression in B cell–deficient C57-μMT (Fig. 4B) and Balb/C-JH mice (fig. S4G). Furthermore, Il27p28 transcript levels and IL-27 secretion were decreased in mb1creIl27p28fl/fl mice (Fig. 4C), and IL-27 production was impaired in μMT:Ebi3−/− mice (Fig. 4D). These mutant mice (Fig. 4, C and D), along with μMT:Il27ra−/− mice (fig. S4H), failed to mount full IgG2a responses following NP-CGG immunization, in association with reduced frequencies of IgG2a+ B cells and plasma cells, but normal (GL-7+) GC B cells, confirming the important role of IL-27+ B cells and their IL-27R+ B cell targets in supporting effective antibody responses.

Consistent with their synergy in inducing CSR to IgG2a, IL-27+ B cells and IFN-γ mediated residual CSR to IgG2a and IgG2a production in mice with B cell–specific knockout (KO) of the IFN-γ receptor (IFN-γR) and IL-27R, respectively, a redundancy eliminated in B cell–specific double KO μMT:Il27ra−/–Ifngr1−/− mice (Fig. 4E and fig. S4I). Unexpectedly, these double KO mice also exhibited broad defects in GC B cell and plasma cell differentiation, TFH cell development, and even IgG1 production. These findings suggested that the GC fitness required activation of the B cell–intrinsic IL-27R and IFN-γR signaling. Together, IL-27+ B cells promote the differentiation of IL-27R+ target B cells and optimize the antibody response, validating the CCC-LRI prediction.

Validate IL-27+CXCL10+ B cell self-targeting in vitro through Cxcl10 → Cxcr3

To validate the second enrichment score analysis–based CCC-LRI prediction that IL-27+CXCL10+ B cells targeted B.LPS cells and themselves via Cxcl10 → Cxcr3, we first confirmed that recombinant CXCL10 could affect both B.LPS and B.LA21 cells. Specifically, CXCL10 activated AKT in both cell types but suppressed extracellular signal–regulated kinase (ERK) activation in B.LA21 cells (Fig. 5A). In a Transwell assay, when CXCL10 was placed in the bottom chamber, it induced migration of B.LPS cells, B cells stimulated with αCD40 and IL-21 (B.A21), and, to a lesser extent, B.LA21 cells (fig. S5A). We reasoned that the increased retention of B.LA21 cells might be due to their coexpression of CXCL10 and CXCR3 leading to “autochemotaxis” within the top chamber that competed with chemotaxis toward exogenous CXCL10. This reasoning was prompted by the higher Cxcl10 and Cxcr3 transcript levels in B.LC21 cells compared to other B cell subsets (fig. S5B) and by the greater enrichment of H3K27ac (a histone posttranslational modification mark associated with active transcription) at the Cxcl10 and Cxcr3 loci in B.LA21 cells compared to B.LPS cells (fig. S5C). In a modified Transwell assay using LPS as the chemoattractant in the bottom chamber, fewer B.LA21 cells migrated from the top chamber than B.LPS and B.A21 cells (Fig. 5B). Such restrained migration was partially relieved when B.LA21 cells were Cxcl10−/− or treated with AMG487, a highly selective CXCR3 antagonist (Fig. 5B, right) (21, 22). While recombinant CXCL10 did not enhance IL-27 production by B.LA21 cells (Fig. 5C), IL-27 secreted by B.LA21 cells did contribute to their CXCL10 production, as evidenced by reduced CXCL10 secretion in Il27p28−/− and Il27ra−/− cells (Fig. 5D). Accordingly, Il27ra−/− B.LA21 cells exhibited less restraint in the top chamber and increased migration (Fig. 5D, right).

B.LA21 cells formed large clusters after undergoing cycles of merging and rounding-up (Fig. 5, E and F, and movie S1). Similar clustering was observed in B cells primed with any TLR ligand followed by stimulation with αCD40 and IL-21 (Fig. 5G). B.LA21 cell clusters moved slowly and remained confined to their immediate vicinity (Fig. 5H), consistent with their restrained migrating pattern. By contrast, B.LPS and B.A21 cells, despite having comparable proliferation and viability (fig. S5D), formed smaller clusters that moved more rapidly and over greater distance (Fig. 5, E and H). Both IL-27 and IFN-γ up-regulated Cxcl10 and Cxcr3 transcription (Figs. 3G and 5I and data table S13) and acted collaboratively to further increase CXCR3 protein levels (Fig. 5J). Accordingly, the cluster size of IL-27–secreting B.LA21 cells increased with the IFN-γ treatment (Fig. 5E) without further reduction in the individual cluster’s mobility (Fig. 5H), likely reflecting already fully restrained migration. Last, coexpression of chemokine/receptor pairs in B cells and other immune cells (fig. S5, D and E) suggested that autochemotaxis was a widespread phenomenon. In summary, IL-27+CXCL10+ B cells use Cxcl10 → Cxcr3 for self-targeting and migration restraint.

Characterize the role of IL-27+ B cells in tumoral PD-L1 expression

To explore the in vivo relevance of the migration restraint exhibited by IL-27+CXCL10+ B cells, we selected a cancer model, prompted by the continuous migration of immune cells through tumors as part of the cancer-immune cycles (23). Specifically, we used murine BCa models, based on the extensive B cell infiltration observed in orthotopic tumors derived from the estrogen receptor–positive (ER+) E0771 BCa cell line engrafted into C57 mice (Fig. 6A). In E0771 tumors, IL-27p28 was expressed by tumor-associated B cells (TAL-Bs) (fig. S6A). A similar proportion (30%) of TAL-Bs was GFPhi in Tg(Il27p28-Gfp) mice (fig. S6B). Most TAL-Bs were activated, as they down-regulated IgD, CD38, and CD21/CD35 and up-regulated FAS compared to naïve B cells in draining inguinal lymph node (inLN) (fig. S6C). GFPhi TAL-Bs in Tg(Il27p28-Gfp) mice were all IgD− and coexpressed CXCL10, which was also expressed by GFP−IgD− and GFP−IgD+ TAL-Bs, likely due to the potent induction by IFN-γ that was prevalent in the tumor microenvironment (TME) (Fig. 6B). IL-27p28hi and GFPhi TAL-Bs frequently coexpressed the immune checkpoint molecule PD-L1 (Fig. 6A and fig. S6D), but not CD5 (fig. S6A).Tumor progression was hampered in mice with B cell–specific deficiency in IL-27 production (μMT:Il27p28−/− and μMT:Ebi3−/−), but not in mice deficient in B-cell production of IL-35 or IL-12 (μMT:Il12a−/−) (Fig. 6C and fig. S6E). This, along with increased CD8+ T cell infiltration in residual tumors in μMT:Il27p28−/− mice (Fig. 6D), showed that IL-27+ TAL-Bs promoted tumor immune evasion in murine BCa.

Lymphocytes in residual E0771 tumors from μMT:Il27p28−/− mice showed reduced proportions of not only IL-27p28+ cells (as expected) but also PD-L1hi cells (Fig. 6D), suggesting an important role of IL-27+ B cells in promoting tumoral PD-L1 expression. Supporting this notion, and consistent with the coexpression of IL-27 and PD-L1 in TAL-Bs, IL-27–producing B.LA21 cells expressed PD-L1 (fig. S6F). This likely reflected the influence of a superenhancer in the Cd274 locus (SE-Cd274), which spanned a broader region and was more enriched for H3K27ac in B.LA21 cells than B.LPS cells (fig. S6, G and H, and data table S14). PD-L1 expression in B.LA21 cells was further up-regulated by IFN-γ (fig. S6F). IL-27 also enhanced IFN-γ–induced PD-L1 induction in both B.LPS and B.CD154 cells (fig. S6I). Thus, in the presence of IFN-γ, IL-27+ B cells not only expressed PD-L1 at high levels themselves but could also promote PD-L1 expression in other B cells through their secreted IL-27.

A key role of PD-L1+ TAL-Bs in BCa progression was shown by the impairment, comparable to that observed in μMT mice, of E0771 tumor growth in AicdacreCd274fl/fl mice, in which Cd274 KO in activated B cell led to fewer PD-L1hi tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and an increased proportion of CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6E and fig. S6J). In addition, E0771 tumors pregrown to ~400 mm3 in AicdacreCd274+/+ mice responded to the treatment by a PD-L1–blocking antibody (αPD-L1); by contrast, tumors that eventually reached a similar size in AicdacreCd274fl/fl mice were resistant to αPD-L1 (Fig. 6F and fig. S6K). Likewise, E0771 tumor growth was delayed in mb1creIl27p28fl/fl mice, as compared to that in wild-type controls (fig. S6L), and tumors that eventually grew to 400 mm3 in mb1creIl27p28fl/fl mice responded less effectively to αPD-L1 than those in wild-type mice (fig. S6M), also reflecting the loss of PD-L1hi TAL-Bs, a key target of αPD-L1.

In summary, IL-27+ B cells promote PD-L1 expression in TAL-Bs and immune evasion. In addition, PD-L1hi TAL-Bs are direct targets of αPD-L1 therapy that restores antitumor immunity in murine BCa.

Determine the impact of CXCR3 on the retention of IL-27+ B cells within syngeneic BCa tumors

Like PD-L1, CXCR3 was highly expressed by TAL-Bs in E0771 tumors (fig. S6, C and N), reflecting the cooperative induction of both molecules by IL-27 and IFN-γ (Fig. 5J and fig. S6I). TAL-Bs were also an important source of tumoral CXCR3 and PD-L1, in part due to their high abundance (fig. S6O). Accordingly, Cxcr3 and Cd274 expression in E0771 tumors were reduced in μMT mice (fig. S6P), in which tumor progression was impaired (Fig. 6G and fig. S6Q). This tumor suppression was accompanied by PD-1 induction (indicative of T cell receptor activation) in CD8+ T cells and production of IFN-γ and granzyme B by CD8+ T cells as well as increased IFN-γ production by NK cells (fig. S6R). Similarly, tumor development from engrafted syngeneic D2A BCa cells in B cell–null Balb/C-JH mice was inhibited (Fig. 6G and fig. S6Q).

Treating E0771 and D2A1 tumors pregrown to 100 mm3 with the CXCR3 antagonist AMG487 reduced the proportion of IL-27p28+ TAL-Bs by 50%, without affecting B cells in the inLN or spleen that were largely IL-27p28− (Fig. 6H and fig. S6S). The AMG487 treatment also delayed tumor progression (Fig. 6, I and J, and fig. S6, T and U), likely due to the reduction of PD-L1hi TAL-Bs in E0771 tumors (Fig. 6I). Combining AMG487 with αPD-L1 further suppressed E0771 and D2A1 tumor growth (Fig. 6, I and J, and fig. S6, T and U), suggesting that the reduced number of PD-L1hi TAL-Bs were more efficiently targeted by the same dose of αPD-L1. This interpretation was supported by the comparable efficacy between a full dose of αPD-L1 alone and the combination of AMG487 with a half dose of αPD-L1 in treating D2A1 tumors (Fig. 6J and fig. S6U).

As whole-body Cxcr3 KO has been shown to impair CD8+ T cell infiltration into tumors (24), likely due to the defective influx of Cxcr3−/− CD8+ T cells during early tumor establishment (e.g., as tumors grew to 100 mm3), we analyzed CD8+ T cells infiltrating into D2A1 BCa tumors. These cells were only marginally affected, if at all, by the AMG487 treatment alone (Fig. 6J). By contrast, their infiltration was even increased when AMG487 was combined with a half dose of αPD-L1, reaching levels comparable to those induced by a full dose of αPD-L1 (Fig. 6J), consistent with the observed tumor growth inhibition. Thus, CXCR3 played an important role in the accumulation of TAL-Bs. This role was intrinsic to B cells, as evidenced by impaired E0771 tumor progression in mice with B cell–specific Cxcr3 deficiency (Fig. 6K and fig. S6V). The importance of CXCR3 in the tumor residence of B cells was also consistent with its higher expression in TAL-Bs compared to infiltrating CD8+ T cells; relative to their respective counterparts in the inLN and spleen, TAL-Bs up-regulated CXCR3 and tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells down-regulated CXCR3 (Fig. 6L). In summary, IL-27+ TAL-Bs coexpress CXCL10 and CXCR3, which promotes their accumulation in syngeneic BCa tumors, reflecting their migration restraint mediated by the Cxcl10 → Cxcr3 LRI (Fig. 6M).

Identify BATF3 as a hallmark TF candidate of IL-27+CXCL10+ B cells

To identify a hallmark TF(s) of IL-27+CXCL10+ B cells, we used a three-step approach using multiple datasets (fig. S7A). Most B cell TFs were constitutively expressed (table S1), although their protein levels varied (fig. S7B and data table S15). In B.Nil and stimulated B cells, TFs from the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB), ETS, activator protein-1 (AP-1), and interferon regulatory factor (IRF) families displayed high PageRanks (Fig. 7A and data table S16), as calculated by Taiji based on the accessibility of these TFs’ binding motifs across the genome and the expression correlation of these TFs with target genes harboring accessible motifs (25). Among these TFs, Batf3, which was the only AP-1 family gene induced specifically in B.LC/21 cells (Fig. 7B), showed the largest PageRank increase when comparing B.LC21 cells to B.Nil, B.LPS, or B.CD154 cells (Fig. 7C and data table S17). Supporting the PageRank findings, the AP-1-binding TRE site TGA(G/C)TCA was the most enriched motif in differentially accessible regions (DARs) in stimulated B cells relative to B.Nil cells (fig. S7, C and D, and data table S18), and particularly in DARs unique to B.LC/21 cells, but not in B.LPS or B.CD154 cells (Fig. 7D; fig. S7, C and E; and data table S18). In addition, B.LC/21 cells showed high cumulative BATF3 target gene weights (Fig. 7E and data table S19).

Batf3 induction in B.LA21 cells slightly lagged behind that of Il27p28 (Fig. 7F). Once induced, BATF3 maintained the expression of the 125 genes that were down-regulated in Batf3−/− cells (data table S20), which exhibited defects in Il27p28 and Cxcl10 induction and IL-27 secretion (Fig. 7G and fig. These 125 genes were enriched among those up-regulated in B.LC/21 cells compared to B.LPS, B.CD154, and/or B.C21 cells (Fig. 7H), identifying them as characteristic genes of IL-27+CXCL10+ B cells. They were also enriched for genes involved in antiviral immunity (Fig. 7I) and up-regulated by IFN-γ and IL-27 in B.CD154 cells (fig. S7H). Consistent with these findings, Batf3 was coexpressed with Il27p28 in B cell clusters in VVWR-infected lungs (fig. S7I) and was coinduced with Il27p28 and Cxcl10 in GFP+ B cells from VVWR-infected Tg(Il27p28-Gfp) mice (fig. S1M). Conversely, the 392 genes up-regulated in Batf3−/− cells were enriched for genes that were down-regulated in B.LC/21 cells compared to B.LPS, B.CD154, and/or B.C21 cells (fig. S7J). These genes were associated with leukocyte migration and myeloid cell functions (Fig. 7I), mediated B effector cell differentiation (e.g., Ig, Aicda, Prdm1, and Jchain), and included Il10 (fig. S7K). Last, mice with B cell–specific Batf3 KO displayed impaired generation of IL-27+ B cells, reduced IL-27 secretion (Fig. 7J), and defective IgG2a responses (fig. S7, L to N). Thus, BATF3 is a strong candidate for the hallmark TF of IL-27+CXCL10+ B cells, as it promotes their regulatory cell functions and associated gene expression, while inhibiting genes that could compromise their identity or features, such as migration restraint and effector B cell differentiation.

Define BATF3 as a function modulator of B cell–relevant TFs

A cell’s hallmark TFs directly target genes that define its identity and functions. In B.LA21 cells, BATF3 bound the canonical AP-1 motif TGA(G/C)TCA (Fig. 7D and data table S21), including those in the Il27p28 and Cxcl10 loci as well as its own gene, suggesting a positive-feedback mechanism similar to that observed with JUN (Fig. 8A) (26). To quantify the likelihood of each gene being a direct BATF3 target, we integrated multiple criteria: the gene’s expression correlation with Batf3, its Taiji-predicted gene weight for BATF3, its differential expression in Batf3−/− cells, and its direct binding by BATF3 (Fig. 8B and data table S22). Batf3 (#3-ranked), Il27p28 (#4) and Cxcl10 (#15) were among the top directly activated genes, while S1pr1, Ciita, and Twsg1 were among 60% of direct target genes whose transcription was repressed by BATF3 (Fig. 8, B and C). By contrast, Cr2, Ccl22, and Cd80 were identified as the top genes indirectly up-regulated by BATF3 (Fig. 8C and fig. S8A).

BATF proteins all have a high-affinity DNA binding domain but lack a transactivation domain. As such, they can outcompete JUN and FOS to inhibit transcription yet also stabilize nearby TFs to enhance transcription (27, 28). BATF3-expressing B.LA cells displayed strong activation of NF-κB p65 (RELA; fig. S8B). BATF3 and RELA could co-occupy DNA, as indicated by the enrichment of the RELA-binding motif in BATF3-bound regions (Fig. 7D) and reciprocal enrichment of the AP-1 motif in RELA-bound regions (Fig. 8D and data table S23). They also co-occupied 80% of the 596 SEs and 9% of the 10,629 typical enhancers in B.LA21 cells (Fig. 8E, fig. S8C, and data table S24). Notably, both factors bound “Region 1,” a sequence located 280 base pairs (bp) upstream of the Il27p28 transcription start site (TSS) and containing a Taiji-predicted AP-1 and κB site, in B.LA21 cells (Fig. 8F and data table S25). This binding was associated with a shift in H3K27me3 peaks (likely reflecting nucleosome reorganization) and acquisition of H3K27ac marks in B.LA21 cells, relative to B.LPS cells. Likewise, BATF3 and RELA cobound a 50-bp region located just 50 bp upstream of the Cxcl10 TSS that also contained a Taiji-predicted AP-1 and κB site, with the promoter virtually devoid of H3K27me3 and highly enriched in H3K27ac (Fig. 8G and data table S25). Il27p28 and Cxcl10 promoters were also predicted by Taiji to be bound by other TFs (fig. S8, D and E).

The activity of RELA at the ll27p28 and Cxcl10 loci was reduced in Batf3−/− B cells (Fig. 8H). It was also decreased in 1526 genes and abrogated in 3474 genes genome-wide (Fig. 8I and data table S26). This, along with the BATF3 and RELA co-occupancy, indicated that BATF3 stabilized NF-κB to enhance transcription. By contrast, the activities of JUNB and JUND were increased in Il27p28 and globally (Fig. 8, H and I, and fig. S8F), suggesting that BATF3 outcompeted these TFs. The activity of IRF8, as reported to mediate IL-27 expression (9), was decreased in Il27p28 and Cxcl10 (Fig. 8H) but increased globally (Fig. 8I and fig. S8F) in Batf3−/− B cells. Il27p28, Cxcl10, and 18 other genes were directly targeted by BATF3 for up-regulation and displayed BATF3-enhanced RELA and IRF8 activities (Fig. 8J and data table S27). Collectively, BATF3 fulfills the criteria of a hallmark TF. It is recruited to the IL-27 and CXCL10 gene loci, modulates the activities of multiple TFs there and promotes the expression of these key ligands.

DISCUSSION

Here, we used a discovery framework to unveil that B cells extensively used CCC-LRIs with high enrichment scores, thereby expanding the recognized scope of B cell regulatory functions. These functions were comparable to, and in some cases exceeded, those of other adaptive immune cells. In particular, we identified a B cell subset with reduced capacity for effector cell differentiation and up-regulated expression of ligands, such as IL-27 and CXCL10. The target cell selection and regulatory roles of these IL-27+CXCL10+ B cells were predicted through in silico enrichment score analyses and validated in vitro and in vivo using mouse models of immunization, viral infection, and BCa tumor progression. Our CCC-LRI analyses also confirmed many previously established immune regulation modules. This approach, however, did not capture communication mechanisms involving nonprotein factors or antigen receptors or major histocompatibility complexes. Notably, B cells are also known to produce small-molecule communication mediators, such as γ-aminobutyric acid, acetylcholine, and thioredoxin (29–31) and respond to metabolites, such as 25-hydroxycholesterol (through EBI2) and S-geranylgeranyl-l-glutathione (through P2RY8) that regulate B cell migration (32–34). The current framework adopts a reductionist strategy by analyzing pairwise CCCs, without accounting for more complex multicellular interactions, a limitation that could be addressed in future studies using deep single-cell RNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics. In addition, the enrichment score analysis excluded biologically important LRIs involving broadly expressed ligands, such as Cd274 → Pdcd1 (PD-L1 → PD-1). Last, the accuracy of CCC-LRI predictions would be further improved through the integration of proteomic data and validation using the LIPSTIC approach (35).

BATF3, identified through the framework’s three-step multiomics approach, functioned like a master TF, as it targeted its own locus to potentially enable positive feedback induction and influenced the activity of other TFs. This role also suggests that BATF3 would confer a sublineage identity to IL-27+CXCL10+ B cells, distinguishing them from other regulatory B cell populations, which lack a defining TF (7–13). The residual IL-27 in Batf3−/− B cells might reflect a compensatory role of BATF. The BATF3–IL-27 axis, along with BATF and EBI3 induction in human B cells upon Epstein-Barr virus infection (36), suggests the existence of a conserved TF-ligand module. Its induction depended on bridging innate TLR signaling and adaptive CD40 signaling, with CD40 up-regulation serving as the critical link. This sequential signal integration could bridge the EF B cell response, driven primarily by TLR signals during bacteria and virus infections (37–39), and the GC response (40). This study did not dissect the individual contribution of the TLR, CD40, and IL-21R signaling pathways to the emergency of BATF3+ B cells in vivo or how these pathways intersect. It is also possible that non-TLR innate receptors can prime B cells for the induction of BATF3+ B cells.

B cells organize into follicles or clusters in vivo, a phenomenon recapitulated in vitro by the formation and restrained migration of spheroids when B cells were costimulated via TLR and CD40 engagement in the presence IL-21 and IFN-γ. These B cell–rich structures may facilitate inter–B cell communication, such as interactions between IL-27+ regulatory cells and effector B cells. Given that IFN-γ can be derived from TFH cells, IL-27+ B cells might interact and collaborate with IL-21+IFN-γ+ TFH cells to optimize the differentiation of their target B cells, similar to the cooperative roles of IL-27 and IFN-γ in the differentiation of Treg cells and ILCs (18, 41–43). B cell–intrinsic IL-27R and IFN-γR signaling synergized to enhance the GC reaction, likely through multiple mechanisms that warrant further investigation. These may include the timing and magnitude of signal transducers and activators of transcription 1 (STAT1) and/or STAT3 activation and their functional interactions at the molecule level as well as the effects on B cell proliferation, viability, and differentiation at the cellular level. It remains to be determined whether IL-27+ B cells can act on TFH cells to up-regulate IL-21 expression (44), thereby establishing a feedback loop that synchronizes IL-21 and IL-27 production. It is also worth exploring whether adding prepolarized IL-21+IFN-γ+ TFH cells to B cell spheroids could generate organoid structures capable of supporting full antibody responses.

Mirroring the general B cell functions, TAL-Bs can act as effector cells that produce tumor antigen–targeting IgG and IgA antibodies (IgG/IgA TAAs) (45–47), as antigen-presenting cells (APCs) that activate tumor-specific T cells, and as regulatory cells that either enhance or dampen antitumor immunity. TAL-B–derived IgA TAAs could inhibit tumor progression when transferred into NK1.1-depleted Rag1−/− mice (45). Conversely, BCa tumors in mice lacking antibody secretion but having otherwise normal B cells (IgMi mice) exhibited impaired responses to a checkpoint inhibitor (48). Similarly, Cd19crePrdm1fl/fl mice, in which B cells failed to differentiate into plasma cells, showed reduced survival in a glioblastoma model (49). Notably, B cells from both IgMi and Cd19crePrdm1fl/fl mice displayed higher APC potential and promoted tumor clearance (50). The GC-like B cell differentiation pathway, as characterized by hypermutated IgG/IgA TAAs and evidence of positive selection, was associated with the favorable responses to therapies (51). By contrast, a greater proportion of TAL-Bs followed an EF-like differentiation pathway, as characterized by polyreactive and minimally mutated TAAs and linked to worse clinical outcomes (51). It is possible that IL-27hi TAL-Bs represented a transitional stage from protumor EF-like B cells to antitumor GC-like B cells, as suggested by the induction of IL-27+ B cells through sequential TLR and CD40 engagement. IL-27hi TAL-Bs might also temporarily lose the TAA-producing function, as suggested by the reduced ability of IL-27+ B cells to become effector cells, thereby mainly playing a protumor role.

As exemplified by the role of IL-27+ B cells in murine BCa tumors, the protumor function of TAL-Bs was underpinned by the ligand expression in specific subsets and the LRI-mediated tumor residence and immune cell targeting of such subsets. While syngeneic BCa tumors contained a high proportion of IL-27+CXCL10+ TAL-Bs, the frequency of this TAL-B subset may vary across tumor types due to both B cell–extrinsic factors (e.g., TIL compositions) and B cell–intrinsic factors (e.g., gene-inherent properties). Regarding TIL compositions, the CD40 signaling required for IL-27 induction in B cells could be provided in BCa tumors by (CD25+FoxP3−) activated CD4+ T cells, whose frequencies, however, might vary in other cancer types, leading to different IL-27 levels. IL-27 induction required BATF3, which, as shown here, suppressed Il10 transcription, suggesting that BATF3 might inhibit the generation of protumor IL-10+ TAL-Bs, highlighting the functional complexity and heterogeneity of TAL-Bs. Notably, BATF3 could also be expressed by DCs to mediate IL-27 production (52). Regarding gene-inherent properties, the presence of SE-Cd274 in activated (B.LPS) cells and its further intensification in IL-27+ (B.LC/21) cells likely underlay IL-27+ TAL-B expressing PD-L1 themselves and up-regulating PD-L1 in other B cells, thereby positioning B cells as a key source of PD-L1 in BCa tumors. The intensified SE-Cd274 might also be present in other immune cells, such as DCs, which are known to produce PD-L1 for immune evasion (53); the intensified SE-Cd274 includes a shorter SE located between Cd274 and Pdcd1lg2 that has been shown to coordinate PD-L1 and PD-L2 expression in cancer cells in vitro (54).

The retention of IL-27+ TAL-Bs within syngeneic BCa tumors was impaired by the CXCR3 inhibitor AMG487, leading to delayed tumor progression that was further suppressed by αPD-L1 treatment. These findings provide a rationale for repurposing AMG487 in combination with PD-L1 blockade to treat BCa. Notably, such a combination may reduce immune-related adverse effects by allowing for lower αPD-L1 dosing. In addition, the mechanisms of action of AMG487 suggest a new therapeutic strategy, i.e., by manipulating efflux, instead of influx, of lymphocyte. This approach could be refined once the kinetics of B cell trafficking into and out of tumors is elucidated. The observed synergy between AMG487 and αPD-L1 in halting tumor progression contrasted with the lack of response to αPD-L1 by BCa tumors that were eventually well established in mb1creIl27p28fl/fl mice. The difference might reflect the partial reduction in but persistent presence of IL-27+ TAL-Bs in AMG487-treated tumors, as opposed to the long-term and more complete loss of IL-27+ TAL-Bs in mb1creIl27p28fl/fl mice, along with uncharacterized changes in the TME resulting from prolonged IL-27+ B cell deficiency. Last, B cells acted as both the source and target of a ligand, a feature they share with other immune cell types, such as Treg cells producing and responding to IL-27 (55, 56), CD8+ T cells with CXCL10 (57), and ILC3s with CCL20 (58).

IL-27 has been widely reported to induce PD-L1 up-regulation. However, this might not be the sole mechanisms underlying the function of IL-27+ B cells. These cells also coexpressed Vegfa and likely other immune checkpoint molecules (59), suggesting broader immunoregulatory capabilities. In addition to engaging B cells, IL-27+ B cells may also regulate non–B immune cells, most notably CD8+ T cells, as predicted by in silico analyses and likely occurring in the TME. Upon the removal of CD8+ T cell suppression mediated by B cell–derived PD-L1 through either genetic KO or αPD-L1 blocking, IL-27+ B cells might switch from a protumor to an antitumor role by activating their CD8+ T cell targets (Fig. 6M), a model aligning with the synergy of IL-27 and PD-L1 blockade in inhibiting E0771 tumor progression (60).

As a pleotropic cytokine produced by multiple cell lineages and known for its Janus-faced activities, IL-27 has been shown to exert both proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory effects across a variety of pathophysiological conditions, including cancer (60–62), infection (63), inflammatory bowel disease (56), and autoimmunity (64). Traditionally, insights into IL-27’s function have come from models involving global inhibition of IL-27 or its receptor, through whole-body gene KO or blocking antibodies, or from boosting systemic or local IL-27 levels using recombinant protein or different gene delivery strategies. More recently, an increasing number of studies have used mice with cell type–specific KO of Il27p28 or Il27ra to uncover the complexity of IL-27 signaling. These studies have revealed, for instance, anticancer roles for IL-27 produced by myeloid cells in targeting CD8+ T cells (60) and procancer roles when IL-27 acts on Treg cells (62). In the future, once CCC networks involving the IL-27 → IL-27R LRI are sufficiently deconstructed, research will likely shift toward reconstructing such CCCs to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of IL-27’s functions. One promising approach is mathematical modeling. A recent theoretic modeling has suggested that IL-27 treatment, when combined with PD-1 blockade, can be anticancer at low doses and procancer at high doses (65). Last, as any regulatory cell–target cell interaction can be broken down into a combination of key signature LRIs, therapeutic strategies [such as bispecific antibodies targeting PD-1 and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)] that simultaneously target multiple LRIs may improve the specificity and reduce off-target effects to enhance drug efficacy and safety.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Mice

C57BL/6J (C57, CD45.2+, stock #000664) as well as Batf3−/− (#013755), CD45.1+ (Ptprca, #002014), Cxcl10−/− (#006087), Ebi3−/− (#008691), Cxcr3−/− (#005796), Ifngr1−/− (#003288), Il10IRES-Gfp (#008379), Il27ra−/− (#018078), and μMT (IghμMT/μMT, #002288) mice, all on the C57 background, were originally from the Jackson Laboratory (JAX). Balb/C-JH (IghJH/JH) mice were originally from Taconic (stock #1147). C57BL/6N-Atm1Brd Il27tm1a(EUCOMM)Wtsi homozygotic mice, as rederived from frozen sperms (European Mouse Mutant Archive mouse repository, strain #10189), were unable to express Il27p28 transcripts or secrete IL-27, due to the interference by the LacZ-Neor cassette between exons 1 and 2, and were, therefore, used as Il27p28−/− mice. Il27ra−/− and Ifngr1−/− mice were crossed to generate Il27ra−/–Ifngr1−/− double KO mice.

Il27p28fl/fl, Tg(Il27p28-Gfp), and Il27rafl/fl mice were as described (66–68). Cd274fl/fl mice carrying two loxP sites flanking exon 2, which encodes the AUG translation initiation codon, were originally from Taconic (model #TF0103) and further backcrossed with C57 mice for nine generations. Il27p28fl/fl mice were crossed with mb1+/cre mice (JAX, stock #020505; Cd19+/cre mice were not used, as the Il27p28 and Cd19 loci are <200 kb apart on the chromosome 7) to generate B cell–specific KO mb1creIl27p28fl/fl mice. Cd274fl/fl mice were crossed with Aicdacre mice (JAX, stock #018422) to generate AicdacreCd274fl/fl mice.

To generate mice with B cell–specific KO in Il27p28, Ebi3, Il27ra, Ifngr1, Cxcr3 or Batf3 or double KO in Il27ra and Ifngr1 using the mixed bone marrow chimera mouse approach, age- and sex-matched recipient C57 mice from the same breeding batch in JAX were treated with neomycin sulfate (2 mg/ml in drinking water) for 1 week before being γ-irradiated with a lethal dose (10 Gy, 12.5 min) via a cesium source. After 24 hours, mice were randomized into different groups and injected through the tail vein 2.5 × 106 mixed bone marrow cells, as comprised of 80% (2 × 106) cells from a single μMT mouse and 20% (0.5 × 106) cells from either a KO/double KO mouse or its age- and sex-matched C57 mouse counterpart (“wild-type”). Before mixing, bone marrow cells isolated from the tibia and fibula of donor mice were depleted of T cells by incubation with biotinylated anti-CD3 monoclonal antibody (mAb) and MagniSort Streptavidin Negative Selection Beads (eBioscience). Chimeric mice were monitored for CD45, CD19, CD4, CD8, CD11b, CD11c, and/or Gr1 expression of mononuclear cells in the circulation for at least 6 weeks to confirm the immune system reconstitution. To verify B cell–specific gene deficiency in mice, CD19+CD4−CD8−, CD19−CD4+CD8−, and CD19−CD4−CD8+ peripheral blood cells were sorted (BD FACSAria II) for genomic DNA extraction and genotyping using polymerase chain reaction (PCR). While female mice were used in most experiments, in experiments in which male mice were also used, they did not display significant differences from female mice in the same group.

Vaccinia virus

The wild-type VVWR stock has been maintained and regularly used in Y. Xiang’s laboratory since 2000. HeLa cells [ATCC (American Type Culture Collection) CCL2.2] were maintained in monolayer in 150-cm2 tissue culture flask in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS; 10% v/v, Invitrogen) and penicillin-streptomycin/amphotericin B (1% v/v). At the time of infection, 109 cells growing at the log phase were infected with VVWR according to a standard protocol at a multiplicity of infection of 1 for 72 hours at 37°C in the presence of DMEM and FBS. Infected cells were pelleted and resuspended in 14 ml of buffer (10 mM tris-Cl, pH 9.0). After homogenization in a glass Dounce homogenizer, nuclei were removed by centrifugation (900g, 5 min, 5°C). The supernatant was sonicated and layered onto a cushion of 17 ml of 36% sucrose. After centrifugation (32,900g, 80 min, 4°C), the virus pellet was resuspended in 1 ml of 1 mM tris-Cl (pH 9.0). After titration, viruses were stored in 100-μl aliquots at −80°C until thawed to be used only once for infection.

Syngeneic BCa cells

E0771 BCa cells (CH3 Biosystems, #94A001) are one of the 10 syngeneic mammary tumor models in C57 mice and the only one arising from spontaneous tumors. They resemble the luminal B type of BCa and, as shown by our data, also express ERα (69, 70). D2A1 BCa cells were established from Balb/C mammary alveolar hyperplastic nodule (D2 HAN/D2 tumor). They express a constitutively activated int-5/aromatase gene, resulting from mouse mammary tumor virus integration within the 3′ untranslated region, and synthesize local estrogen via aromatase induction. Also expressing ER, D2A1 cells represent a model of intratumoral estrogen-driven mammary cancer (71). E0771 and D2A1 cells were maintained in low passages in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin/amphotericin B.

Mouse immunization and virus infection

Ethics statement

All mice were maintained in a pathogen-free vivarium and were used for experiments at 8 to 12 weeks of age, without any apparent infection or disease. All procedures were approved by the University of Texas at San Antonio Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol #20150003AR and #20170219AR).

For immunization, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 100 μg of NP-CGG (average 16 molecules of NP conjugated to one molecule of CGG; Biosearch Technologies) in the presence of 100 μl of alum (Imject Alum adjuvant, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the central-left abdomen area and 10 μg of LPS (from Escherichia coli, serotype 055:B5 and deproteinized; Sigma-Aldrich) in 100 μl of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) in the central-right abdomen area. For intranasal VVWR infection, 5 × 104 plaque forming units of infectious viral particles (in 20 μl of PBS) were delivered into the nostrils (10 μl each) of infected mice, as anesthetized by ketamine and xylazine (50 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg of body weight, respectively). Infected mice were kept on a warm pad until waking up and being transferred to original cages. Each mouse was weighed 1 day before infection and daily after infection until moribund (e.g., losing 25% or more of the initial body weight) or euthanized at predetermined end points.

Syngeneic BCa cell engraftment, tumor size measurement, and drug treatment

E0771 or D2A1 cells (4 × 105 or as indicated) in 100 μl of full DMEM mixed with an equal volume of Matrigel matrix (Corning) were implanted in the fourth mammary fat pad of female mice on the C57 or Balb/C background. Tumor growth was monitored by the measurement of the longest diameter (L) and perpendicular diameter (W) using a digital caliper initially at 3- to 4-day intervals and every other day when tumor grew to over 600 mm3 in volume (V), which was calculated using the formula V = ½ × L × W × W. Tumors that failed to develop to 100 mm3 in volume in wild-type mice were rare and excluded from analyses. Mice were euthanized when tumor grew either to over 2000 mm3 in volume as the primary end point or to 400 to 600 mm3 for analysis of TILs as the secondary end point. After euthanasia, the tumors were removed, weighed, and processed for fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), transcript level analysis and imaging analysis, as described below.

For treatment with CXCR3 inhibitor AMG487 (Tocris, #4487), the stock solution of AMG487 (3 g/ml in dimethyl sulfoxide) was diluted to 3 mg/ml with 50% 2-hydroxpropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HPβCD; Caymen Chemical, #16169), mixed in a sonicating water bath for 2 hours with occasional vortexing every 30 min, and further diluted with sterile ddH2O to the final concentration of 1.2 mg/ml in 20% HPβCD, as to be used for injection. For treatment with αPD-L1 blocking antibody (clone 10F.9G2, BioXCell, #BE0101), αPD-L1 stock solution (6.7 mg/ml in PBS) was diluted with Ca2+- and Mg2+-free Dulbecco’s PBS (Cytiva) to 0.75 mg/ml, as to be used for injection. After tumor establishment from engrafted BCa cells (i.e., average of 100 mm3 in volume unless indicated, e.g., when tumors grew to 400 mm3), mice were injected subcutaneously with AMG487 at 5 mg/kg body weight (about 100 μl) or the vehicle control (20% HPβCD) every 3 days, and intraperitoneally with αPD-L1 or an isotype-matched rat IgG2b antibody (anti–keyhole limpet hemocyanin, clone LTF-2, BioXCell, #BE0090) as the control at 75 μg (or half of this dose when indicated) every 5 days. Mice receiving no treatment at all were also included in the same experiments to ensure that treatment with the vehicle or isotype control had no impact. Irrespective of their (reduced) tumor growth as compared to mice in the nontreated or control group, treated mice were euthanized together with control mice at the end of each experiment for analyses, including FACS, transcript level analysis and imaging analysis.

B cell purification and culture in vitro

To purify murine B cells, spleen and lymph node cells were subjected to negative selection (against CD43, CD4, CD8, CD11b, CD49b, CD90.2, Gr1, or Ter-119) using the EasySep Mouse B Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies). Cell preparations were stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies specific for CD45 or CD45.2, CD19, CD4, CD8, CD11b, CD11c, and/or Gr1 at 4°C for 30 min to be verified by FACS analysis to contain >95% CD19+CD11b−Gr1−CD11c− B cells and no CD4+ or CD8+ T cells. To purify CD19+ B cells by positive selection, single cell suspensions containing 5 × 106 splenocytes were incubated with 2 μg of biotinylated anti-CD19 mAb before being reacted with MagniSort Streptavidin Positive Selection Beads (eBioscience) following the manufacturer’s instructions. To sort IgD+, IgD−GFP−, and IgD−GFP+ B cells from the spleen of VVWR-infected Tg(Il27p28-Gfp) mice, splenocytes were stained with antibodies specific for CD19 and IgD in the presence of mAb clone 2.4G2, which blocks FcγII and FcγIII receptors, and 7-aminoactinomycin D to exclude dead cells before sorting (BD FACSAria II).

To culture mouse B cells purified from the spleen or lymph nodes, or total splenocytes, cells were cultured (3 × 105 cell/ml, 2 ml in a 24-well plate or 1 ml in a 48-well plate) in RPMI 1640 medium (Invitrogen) supplemented with FBS (10% v/v, Invitrogen), penicillin-streptomycin/amphotericin B (1% v/v) and 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol (RPMI-FBS) with the following stimuli at the dose indicated unless specified otherwise: CD154 (3 U/ml), agonistic anti-CD40 mAb (clone FGK4.5, 16 μg/ml, BioXCell), LPS (3 μg/ml), TLR1/2 ligand Pam3CSK4 (100 ng/ml, Invivogen), TLR4 ligand lipid A (1 μg/ml, Invivogen), TLR7 ligand R-848 (30 ng/ml, Invivogen), TLR9 ligand ODN1826 (sequence 5′-TCCATGACGTTCCTGACGTT-3′) with a phosphorothioate backbone (CpG, 1 μM; Eurofins), F(ab′)2 of a goat anti-mouse μ chain antibody [anti-μF(ab′)2, 1 μg/ml; SouthernBiotech], which cross-links IgM BCR, or anti-Igδ mAb (clone 11-26c) conjugated to dextran (anti-Igδ/dex, 100 ng/ml; Fina Biosolutions), which cross-links IgD BCR, alone or in combination. CD154 was prepared as membrane fragments isolated from Sf21 insect cells infected by CD154-expressing baculovirus, as we described (72, 73); membrane fragments from noninfected Sf21 cells failed to activate B cells to proliferate. Other stimuli used included recombinant IL-21 (2 ng/ml, BioLegend), IL-27 (100 ng/ml, R&D Systems), IFN-γ (30 ng/ml, BioLegend), IL-4 (4 ng/ml, BioLegend), and CXCL10 (20 nM, R&D Systems) at the indicated dose unless specified otherwise. In sequential stimulation experiments, B cells were cultured in the presence of the first stimulus (or stimuli) for 16 to 24 hours before the second stimulus (or stimuli) was added to the culture.

In B cell coculture experiments, B cells prepared from CD45.1+ or CD45.2+ mice were stimulated with LPS, CD154 (or αCD40), and/or IL-21 for 48 hours, washed twice with RPMI-FBS to remove the stimuli and resuspended in RPMI-FBS before being used as stimulating B cells. Target B cells were labeled with CellTrace Yellow and activated for 16 to 24 hours by stimuli, as indicated, before being mixed with stimulating B cells with a different CD45 congenic marker at 1:1 ratio (3 × 105 cells/ml each) and cocultured for additional 72 hours.

RNAScope

VVWR-infected mice were euthanized at the predetermined end point by cervical dislocation and infused with 2 ml of 4% paraformaldehyde through intratracheal injection. The fixed lung tissues were harvested and then paraffin embedded for the detection of mRNA and/or target proteins by RNAscope [Advanced Cell Diagnostics (ACD)]. All reagents were from ACD unless otherwise indicated, including the RNA probes for Mm-Il27 (#557471), Mm-Batf3 (#496201), and V-VACV-E3L-A3L-K2L-A26L (#466831), as used in conjunction with the RNAscope Intro Pack 2.5 HD Reagent Kit (#322373) or RNA Protein Co-Detection Ancillary Kit (#323180), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, fresh sections (5 μm) from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded lung tissues were deparaffinized by heating at 60°C for 1 hour in the HybEZ oven (#310010) and then washed twice with fresh xylene for 5 min, washed twice again with 100% ethanol for 1 min, and then air dried for 5 min. Tissues were then covered with H2O2 (#322330) and, after washing twice with ddH2O, incubated with antigen retrieval buffer at 98° to 102°C for 15 min. After the hydrophobic barrier was drawn and dried, the slide was directly hybridized with an RNA probe or first incubated with an anti-CD19 primary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #90176, 1:800) at 4°C overnight in a humidity-controlled tray. After washing twice with PBST, the slide was fixed with 10% neutral buffered formalin at 25°C for 30 min, washed four times with PBS, and then incubated with protease at 40°C for 30 min. After washing twice with ddH2O, the slide was hybridized with prewarmed RNA probes at 40°C for 2 hours in a humidity-controlled tray. The slide was then reacted with Hybridize Amplify Reagent 1 through 6 and washed with RNAscope wash buffer (#310091) in between. RNA signals were detected by incubating the slide with the RNA-red solution at 25°C for 10 min and rinsed with tap water. For co-detection of CD19, the slide was incubated with Co-Detection Blocker (#323170) at 40°C for 15 min before reacting with a horseradish peroxidase–conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Abcam, #ab214880) at 25°C for 50 min followed by color development using the ImmPACT DAB Substrate (Vector Laboratories, #SK-4105) at 25°C for 10 min. The slide was then counterstained with 50% Modified Mayer’s hematoxylin (Richard-Allan Scientific, #72804) at 25°C for 2 min. After washing with ddH2O for 2 min, the slide was dipped in 0.02% ammonia water for 10 s and immediately washed with ddH2O. After drying at 60°C for 1 hour and cooling for 5 min, the slide was mounted with VectaMount Permanent Mounting Medium (#321584).

B cell chemotaxis

B cell chemotaxis assays were carried out by a method adapted from one previously described (74). Wild-type or KO B cells, freshly isolated or stimulated as indicated, were washed, counted, resuspended in serum-free RPMI-1640 (3 × 105 cells/ml in 100 μl), and seeded in the upper chamber of the Transwell culture insert in a 6.5-mm-diameter 24-Transwell chemotaxis chambers (Costar). Filters with a pore size of 5 μm were transferred into the wells containing RPMI-FBS in the presence of CXCL10 at the indicated dose or LPS (3 μg/ml). After incubation in 5% CO2 at 37°C for 4 hours, B cells that remained in the upper chamber or had migrated (or “escaped”) into the lower chamber were both collected and analyzed by FACS with counting beads for accurate numeration. The proportion of B cells that had migrated into the lower chamber (equaling those that had escaped from the upper chamber) was calculated, as inversely correlated with the ability of such B cells to undergo autochemotaxis. To analyze the effect of the CXCR3 inhibitor AMG487, AMG487 (200 nM) or the vehicle was added to B cells 1 hour before seeding. It was also added to the medium after the seeding until the end of assay.

Specificity score analysis

After batch removal, the read count matrix was trimmed to contain only protein-encoding genes and normalized by the total count of each sample, yielding the master gene expression file for further analyses. The expression of each of the 474 ligand genes (see above) in any of the 46 cell types/subsets was compared to that in each of the rest 45 cell types/subsets, with the P values calculated by the one-sided Wilcoxon rank sum test. If a gene’s mean readcount was over 20 in all cell types/subtypes and displayed significantly higher expression, i.e., log2 fold change > 2 and P < 0.05, in a cell type/subset than one of the rest 45 cell types/subtypes, a positive score was tabulated and the cumulative score was defined as the “specificity score,” which ranged from 0 to 45, for this gene in this cell type/subset. In total, 411 (out of 474) ligand genes were expressed by immune cells. For any ligand gene, the cell types/subtypes with top specificity scores would be those that were most likely to express it. Likewise, for any given cell type/subset, the genes with top specificity scores would be those that were most likely to be its signature genes and used by it to communicate with and regulate other immune cells. The genes with top 100 specificity scores in a cell type/subset were visualized in a radar plot, whose area would be determined by both the number of genes with high specificity scores and such scores themselves.

LRI interaction score and enrichment score in CCC analyses

To identify characteristic LRIs in pairwise immune cell/subset samples (totaling 225 × 224 = 50,400 pairs), we focused on the 199 ligand genes that had at least one top 10 specificity score in the 46 immune cells/subsets. These ligands, together with the corresponding 289 receptor genes, formed 489 high-confidence LRIs in silico. The interaction score of each LRI between the ligand in cell i (as the ligand-expressing cell, CellL) with the expression level of Expi Ligand and its receptor in cell j (as the receptor-expressing cell, CellR) with the expression level of Expj Receptor, was determined as “(Expi Ligand × Expj Receptor)/1000”. This yielded an interaction score matrix of 489 × 50,400 cell pairs, totaling 25 million data points. The interaction score matrix was also used to calculate the “enrichment score” of any selected LRIs within each CCC, defined as the ratio of the interaction score of the LRI to the average of the interaction score in all 50,400 CCCs. As 71 LRIs involved receptors that were virtually not expressed, they were not included in the enrichment score analysis. The enrichment score of each of the remaining 418 LRIs within each CCC involving major immune cell types (totaling 2116 CCCs among the 46 cell types) was calculated as the ratio of this LRI’s interaction score in a given CCC to its average score across all 2116 CCCs. All analyses were performed with the Python packages scikit-learn, and data visualization was performed with seaborn, Orange3, or Prism.

Identification of BATF3 target genes

To identify and rank target genes of BATF3, datasets from four independent analyses were integrated, as follows: dataset-1, correlation between BATF3 and each expressed candidate gene, as calculated using RNA-seq data of wild-type B.Nil, B.LPS, B.CD154, B.LC, and B.LC21; dataset-2, the weight score of each candidate gene for BATF3, as calculated by Taiji using matched ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data in the same B cells as in dataset-1; dataset-3, differential BATF3 binding in the genome in Batf3+/+ B cells, as subtracted by that in Batf3−/− B cells, upon stimulation by LPS plus αCD40 and IL-21; dataset-4, moderated t statistics of DEG analysis in Batf3+/+ and Batf3−/− B cells stimulated with LPS plus αCD40 and IL-21. The “scale” function in R was used to scale each of the four criteria based on minimum and maximum of differences, set as “0” and “100,” respectively. The ggradar package in R was used to visualize the results in the radar plot format. The area of the radar for each gene was calculated using an R script to rank all potential target genes based on their areas.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Excel (Microsoft), GraphPad Prism, or R software environment. Differences in RNA transcript expression were determined by DESeq2, which estimates variance-mean dependence in count data and tests for differential expression based on a model using the negative binomial distribution. Spearman correlation analysis was used to measure the strength and direction of gene expression correlations. Student’s t test and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to analyze the group differences, assuming normal variances. The mixed linear effect model test was used to analyze the time course of tumor growth or dose responses.
