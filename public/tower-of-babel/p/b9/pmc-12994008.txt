Development of Celiac Disease After Immunotherapy: A Case Series
Authors: Alyyah Malick, Benjamin Lebwohl, Peter H.R. Green, Stephen M. Lagana, Suneeta Krishnareddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsCeliac disease results from a complex immune response to gluten in genetically susceptible individuals. It has been noted to occur with immune checkpoint inhibitor therapies and other novel therapies in prior case reports. In this case series, we report 3 cases of celiac disease in patients receiving therapy for psoriasis, breast cancer, and renal cell carcinoma.MethodsThree cases of celiac disease occurring in patients after novel medications were identified by gastroenterologists at a tertiary care center. Informed consent was obtained from all 3 patients. Medical history and clinical data were obtained by review of the electronic medical record.ResultsCeliac disease developed in 3 patients after initiating biologic therapy: a 21-year-old female with psoriasis receiving ixekizumab (interleukin-17 antagonist) and risankizumab (interleukin-23 antagonist); a 49-year-old female with invasive ductal carcinoma of the breast after pertuzumab (human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 antagonist); and an 82-year-old male taking cabozantinib (multityrosine kinase inhibitor) for renal cell carcinoma.ConclusionWe have identified 3 cases of celiac disease that presented after initiating new immune-modulating medications. It is important to consider celiac disease and nonceliac villous atrophy in the differential for diarrhea developing during or after a new therapy as it heavily impacts management and may eliminate the need for alternate therapies that include steroid courses, cessation of therapy, or even gluten-free diet.

Introduction

Emerging immunotherapies and small molecule inhibitors have revolutionized treatment of cancers and various autoimmune diseases. As these therapeutics become widespread, reports have risen of associated immune-related adverse events, such as pneumonitis, colitis, hepatitis, hypophysitis, thyroiditis, and nephritis. These conditions have been reported most frequently with immune checkpoint inhibitors, which target programmed death-1 (PD-1), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4)-B7 to blockade inhibitory pathways in T cell activation.1 The most common luminal gastrointestinal immune-related adverse event is enterocolitis, which can range in severity from mild diarrhea to fulminant colitis with bowel perforation.2 The incidence of enterocolitis with immune checkpoint inhibitors is approximately 13%.3 The pathogenesis of immune checkpoint inhibitor colitis is hypothesized to involve activation and expansion of CD8+ tissue resident memory T cells, production of interferon gamma, and alterations in the gut microbiome.1,4 In addition to enterocolitis, there are rare reports of gastritis, enteric neuropathy, nonceliac villous atrophy, and celiac disease after immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy.5,6 Celiac disease has been associated with immune checkpoint inhibitors such as ipilimumab, pembrolizumab, and nivolumab.5,7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 The mechanism is unknown but, based on mouse studies, may involve reduction in tolerance and expansion of self-reactive CD4 T cells from PD-L1 and CTLA-4 blockade.9

While immune checkpoint inhibitors remain the most studied with regards to gastrointestinal immune-related adverse events, case reports of colitis associated with other immunotherapies and small molecule inhibitors have started to emerge. Interleukin (IL)-17 inhibitors and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) antagonists have both been associated with colitis and unspecified gastrointestinal toxicities.15, 16, 17, 18 In this case series, we now report 3 cases of celiac disease after nonimmune checkpoint inhibitor biologic agents.

Methods

Three cases of celiac disease occurring in patients during or after immune-modulating therapies were identified by gastroenterologists at the Celiac Disease Center at Columbia University Irving Medical Center. Informed consent was obtained from all patients. Medical history and clinical data were obtained by review of the electronic medical record.

Results

Case 1

A 21-year-old female presented with abdominal pain, diarrhea, and bloating. Her medical history was notable for psoriasis diagnosed at 8 years old; she was formerly on methotrexate and adalimumab. For the 4 years prior to presentation, she was taking ixekizumab (IL-17A antagonist) with remission of psoriasis. She then developed acne on her neck and was switched to risankizumab (IL-23A antibody). She received only the loading dose and had clearance of dermatologic findings. This coincided with her development of gastrointestinal symptoms.

Her initial workup revealed selective immunoglobulin A (IgA) deficiency (IgA < 5), elevated tissue transglutaminase immunoglobulin G (IgG) greater than 100 (normal <6), and gliadin peptide IgG of 64 (normal <20); human leukocyte antigen DQ2 was positive and human leukocyte antigen DQ8 was negative. Stool calprotectin was elevated at 161 (normal <50). Gastrointestinal pathogen polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was negative. Colonoscopy showed diffuse nodularity of the ileum and colon. Pathology showed changes consistent with celiac disease in duodenal biopsies and lymphocytic colitis in colonic biopsies. She was started on budesonide and a gluten-free diet with some improvement in diarrhea. Repeat labs showed improvement in gliadin peptide IgG to 32; tissue transglutaminase IgG remained greater than 100. Endoscopy after 6 months on gluten-free diet showed minimal inactive chronic gastritis negative for Helicobacter pylori and small bowel mucosa with subtotal villous blunting and intraepithelial lymphocytes consistent with celiac disease. She continued to have symptoms, including abdominal pain, bloating, and diarrhea. She was instructed to stop ixekizumab due to reports of association with inflammatory bowel disease and microscopic colitis. She concurrently trialed a low fructose diet, and then reintroduced fructose. She was feeling well and self-discontinued budesonide after 1 month.

She had normal bowel movements for 4 months, and then developed watery stools and lower abdominal cramps, so was restarted on budesonide. She developed recurrence of psoriasis and received 2 doses of risankizumab. She self-discontinued budesonide and developed recurrence of diarrhea, bloating, and lower abdominal pain, as well as possible bloody stool. Infectious studies were negative and she restarted budesonide, with diarrhea persisting. Repeat endoscopy and colonoscopy showed normal appearance of the esophagus, stomach, and duodenum, as well as nodular mucosa in the entire colon and terminal ileum, consistent with nodular lymphoid hyperplasia. There were increased intraepithelial lymphocytes in the duodenum with normal villous architecture, no evidence of T cell lymphoma by flow cytometry, and prominent lymphoid aggregates in the colon. The patient was instructed to take ciprofloxacin (10 day course) followed by rifaximin (2 week course). She then had a small bowel capsule enteroscopy performed which showed mild inflammation in the terminal ileum and no other lesions throughout the small intestine. She is now without symptoms on a gluten-free diet and risankizumab, which is controlling her psoriasis.

Case 2

A 49-year-old female presented with diarrhea and weight loss. One year prior, she was diagnosed with invasive ductal carcinoma of the right breast, pT2N0 ER+ PR+ HER2+. She had no other past medical history. She was treated with lumpectomy and re-excision (month 1) and received chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and radiation. Her chemotherapy regimen was ddAC-T (months 2–6): doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, and paclitaxel (reduced dose due to peripheral neuropathy and pain). Additional therapy included trastuzumab (months 3–9) and pertuzumab (months 3–8) (anti-HER2 monoclonal antibodies). She received adjuvant radiation therapy (months 7–8). She was then started on leuprolide and exemestane (month 8). Exemestane was stopped after 1 month due to suspected gastrointestinal side effects, and she underwent extensive workup as described below.

At month 7 of treatment (receiving only trastuzumab and pertuzumab), she developed diarrhea, initially 1 watery, nonbloody bowel movement per day but progressing over several months to 10 bowel movements per day. She also reported poor appetite, heartburn, nausea, and rare emesis along with 20 kg weight loss. She denied abdominal pain. Diarrhea was unresponsive to antidiarrheal agents. She required hospital admission at month 10 due to weakness and worsening diarrhea with resulting electrolyte abnormalities (hypomagnesemia and hypokalemia). She was found to be malnourished with low folate, vitamin C, vitamin D, and zinc. Gastrointestinal pathogen PCR testing and Clostridioides difficile testing were negative. She required total parenteral nutrition. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography revealed circumferential mural edema of the gastric antrum and first part of the duodenum with trace surrounding free fluid and no cholelithiasis or choledocholithiasis. She was given intravenous antibiotics (details unknown) and discharged without further interventions.

Endoscopy and colonoscopy at month 11 revealed localized atrophic mucosa in the gastric body, patchy mild inflammation in the gastric antrum, and 2 superficial duodenal ulcers (1 cm each) with surrounding erythema and erosions and with no stigmata of bleeding in the duodenal bulb and immediate postbulbar area. Diffuse mucosal flattening, effacement of folds, and severe nodularity were noted in the second and third part of the duodenum. Pathology showed duodenal mucosa with partial to total villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, and active chronic inflammation with increased intraepithelial lymphocytes, suggestive of celiac disease. Gastric antrum mucosa showed mild chronic inactive gastritis and mildly increased intraepithelial lymphocytes; H pylori testing was negative. The colon and terminal ileum were normal. Serology results supported the diagnosis. Tissue transglutaminase IgA was greater than 100 (normal <4), gliadin peptide IgA was 122 (normal <20), and gliadin peptide IgG was 126 (normal <20).

Pertuzumab was hypothesized to be related to development of celiac disease and was discontinued. She was started on a gluten-free diet with resolution of diarrhea and 4 kg weight regain within 1 month. Exemestane was resumed and trastuzumab was reloaded at that time (months 12–15). She tolerated these well. Repeat serologies 2.5 years later showed improvement in tissue transglutaminase IgA to 5, gliadin peptide IgA to 9, and gliadin peptide IgG to 3.

Case 3

An 82-year-old male with metastatic renal cell carcinoma presented with watery diarrhea. He had no significant past medical history. He was diagnosed with clear renal cell carcinoma (pT3N1M0, PD-L1 0%, mismatch repair proficient) 4 years prior to presentation. At time of cancer diagnosis, he presented with pulmonary embolism treated with tissue plasminogen activator and heparin, complicated by upper gastrointestinal bleeding requiring endoscopy that revealed 3 duodenal ulcers but otherwise normal esophagus and stomach (no biopsies taken). Tumor thrombus invaded the right renal vein through the suprarenal inferior vena cava to the cavoatrial junction. His renal cell carcinoma was treated with right nephrectomy and inferior vena cava thrombectomy and reconstruction.

Repeat imaging 9 months after diagnosis showed metastasis to the liver, and he was started on pembrolizumab; he received 4 doses (months 10–12) with interval increases in hepatic metastases. He was then started on axitinib 5 mg 2 times a day (month 12) with improvement in hepatic metastases. He had chronic irregular bowel habits that worsened on axitinib requiring a dose reduction after 7 months of therapy with subsequent improvement. Pembrolizumab was added back to his regimen (month 21). He also received ciprofloxacin during microwave ablation of a liver metastasis (month 21). He then developed worsening diarrhea thought to be secondary to colitis from pembrolizumab or antibiotics. Gastrointestinal pathogen PCR and C difficile testing were negative; diphenoxylate/atropine and loperamide treatment were ineffective. Pembrolizumab was discontinued, and diarrhea improved on a prednisone taper.

When he developed new and increased hepatic metastases (month 44), he was switched to cabozantinib 40 mg daily. After switching to cabozantinib, he developed more frequent watery diarrhea, accompanied by abdominal cramps, tenesmus, and bleeding. Cabozantinib was reduced in frequency and then dose-reduced to 20 mg. He presented to gastroenterology for ongoing symptoms. He had no family history of inflammatory bowel disease, celiac disease, or colorectal cancer. He had 1 brother with gastrointestinal stromal tumor. Laboratory tests showed a tissue transglutaminase IgA of 32 (normal <4), gliadin peptide IgA of 26 (normal <20), and gliadin peptide IgG of 44 (normal <20). Upper endoscopy was performed and showed 2 mm erosions with no stigmata of bleeding in the gastric antrum, a normal duodenal bulb, and decreased folds and dilated lacteals in the second part of the duodenum. Biopsies of the duodenum (Figure) showed subtotal to total villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, severe chronic active duodenitis with erosion, focal intraepithelial lymphocytosis, foveolar metaplasia, and patchy increase of subepithelial collagen, consistent with celiac disease complicated by collagenous sprue (Marsh pattern 3c). One month later, he had persistent diarrhea and ongoing weight loss, and he was started on budesonide 9 mg daily (2 capsules open, 1 closed) for an 8-week course to treat collagenous sprue. Symptoms improved significantly. Repeat laboratory tests showed tissue transglutaminase IgA of 6, gliadin peptide IgA of 14, and IgG of 17. At a follow-up visit 4 months later, he reported continued improvement in symptoms, with formed bowel movements on a gluten-free diet alone. Cabozantinib was continued at the reduced dose of 20 mg.

Discussion

Immune checkpoint inhibitors have been associated with celiac disease in a few case reports. Some cases are difficult to distinguish from nonceliac villous atrophy or immune checkpoint inhibitor enterocolitis given absence of 1 or more characteristics of true celiac disease: positive celiac serologies, consistent histologic findings, and response to gluten-free diet. Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor-Associated Celiac Disease

EMA, endomysial antibody; F, female; ICI, immune checkpoint inhibitor; IELs, intraepithelial lymphocytes; M, male; TTG, tissue transglutaminase.

Proposed mechanisms for immune checkpoint inhibitor-associated celiac disease include disruption of immune tolerance through inhibition of CTLA-4 and PD-1, which may then lead to autoreactive T cells targeting the small intestine.9,35 Badran et al9 characterize the immunophenotypic features of immune checkpoint inhibitor-associated celiac disease compared to immune checkpoint inhibitor-associated duodenitis and typical celiac disease. In evaluating intraepithelial lymphocytes, they found that there were increased CD3+ CD8+ T cells in immune checkpoint inhibitor-associated celiac disease and duodenitis but decreased γδ T cells compared to celiac disease. PD-L1 percentage was higher in immune checkpoint inhibitor-associated celiac disease and duodenitis compared to celiac disease. Their findings suggest that immune checkpoint inhibitor-associated celiac disease may have a different immune etiology than conventional celiac disease.9 It is difficult to distinguish whether cases of celiac disease that present after immunotherapy represent de novo disease or unmasking of latent previously undiagnosed celiac disease. However, consideration of this diagnosis in patients who present with persistent diarrhea after immunotherapy is important as management differs significantly from other immune-related adverse events (gluten-free diet rather than steroids).

We now report 3 cases of celiac disease after novel immune-modulating therapies (Table 2). Case 1 received ixekizumab, an IL-17 antagonist, and risankizumab, an IL-23 antagonist, for psoriasis. The IL-23/IL-17 signaling pathway is involved in activation of Th17 cells that cause a proinflammatory cascade affiliated with psoriatic diseases. This signaling pathway has also been implicated in inflammatory bowel disease. IL-23 inhibitors have been shown to help treat inflammatory bowel disease; ustekinumab, an IL-12 and IL-23 antagonist, is approved for use in both Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis.36 However, the role of IL-17 is much more unclear.37 IL-17 antagonism has been ineffective as a treatment in inflammatory bowel disease and counterintuitively may induce a drug-associated colitis.15,38 Upregulation of IL-17 in small intestine mucosa has been found in celiac disease and levels decrease with gluten-free diet. IL-17 may be involved in mucosal protection, which may explain increased gastrointestinal adverse events with IL-17 antagonists.39,40 Ixekizumab specifically has been associated with colitis, typically with features of Crohn’s disease or ulcerative colitis.15,41,42 Risankizumab is currently in phase III trials for use in inflammatory bowel disease. To our knowledge, this is the first case report of celiac disease and lymphocytic colitis diagnosed after ixekizumab and risankizumab therapy.

In case 2, pertuzumab was hypothesized to be related to celiac disease. Diarrhea is a common effect of pertuzumab, affecting 67% of patients,43 and may involve dysregulation of ion channels, altered motility, changes in the intestinal microbiome, or small intestinal bacterial overgrowth.18 Another case report details a 53-year-old female receiving trastuzumab and pertuzumab for breast cancer who developed watery diarrhea and was found to have positive tissue transglutaminase IgA, duodenal mucosa with villous blunting and intraepithelial lymphocytes, and colitis which improved with discontinuation of pertuzumab.16 The mechanism may be mediated by the microbiome. Celiac disease is associated with changes in microbiome composition, and the microbiome has also been associated with variation in HER2 antagonist response.44,45

Case 3 was at 1 point on pembrolizumab, a PD-1 antagonist that has been associated with celiac disease.13 Possible mechanisms include reduction of T cell immune tolerance and enhancement of autoantibody secretion.46 Typically, immune checkpoint inhibitor-associated celiac disease occurs at a median of 1.6 months after immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy.9 However, the patient’s initial symptoms resolved with discontinuation of pembrolizumab and prednisone therapy, implying a more common immune checkpoint inhibitor colitis. His subsequent presentation that yielded a diagnosis of celiac disease was temporally associated with cabozantinib. It is possible for immune-related adverse events to occur after cessation of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, or there may be an association of celiac disease with cabozantinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor affecting vascular endothelial growth factor, MET, AXL, and KIT pathways involved in cell proliferation and angiogenesis. Cabozantinib produces diarrhea in 75% of patients undergoing treatment that is severe enough to result in dose reductions in 16% of patients.47 It is not known to what extent celiac disease was investigated in these patients, if at all. Our patient had a tissue transglutaminase IgA 8 times the upper limit of normal, which strongly supports the diagnosis. Cabozantinib has an immune modulating effect, increasing CD8+ T cells, which could provide a mechanism for autoimmune causes of diarrhea.48

Conclusion

In summary, diarrhea, during or after immunotherapy, is a frequent complication that often requires dose-reduction or cessation of therapy. Celiac disease is known to be a rare immune-related adverse event of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. We have now reported cases of celiac disease that are potentially associated with ixekizumab, pertuzumab, and cabozantinib. It is important to consider celiac disease and nonceliac villous atrophy in the differential for diarrhea after starting a new therapy as it heavily impacts management and may eliminate the need for alternate therapies, including steroid courses, cessation of therapy, or even gluten-free diet.
