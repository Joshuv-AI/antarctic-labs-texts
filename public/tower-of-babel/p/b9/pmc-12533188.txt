Controlled laboratory measurements of dune erosion under variable waves, water levels and dune pore-moisture
Authors: S. Conti, K. D. Splinter, I. L. Turner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Controlled laboratory wave flume experiments of dune erosion can assist researchers to explore the physical processes of dune erosion and improve numerical predictive models. This paper presents a dataset of controlled laboratory measurements of unvegetated dune erosion under vary waves, water levels, and initial dune moisture contents. Three different water levels in the wave flume, two irregular wave conditions, and three initial dune pore-moisture contents were explored. In total, twenty-nine (29) tests were conducted in a 36 m long by 1.6 m high and 0.9 m wide wave flume. Bathymetry, dune morphology, offshore and onshore wave conditions, wave runup, water table and volumetric water content data within the dune were recorded across all tests, as well as video footage for qualitative purposes. This combined dataset can be used to: (1) explore nearshore morphodynamic processes under varied forcing conditions, (2) elucidate dune face erosion and failure mechanisms tying the observed erosion to internal groundwater dynamics and external wave and water level forcing, and (3) validate numerical models of dune erosion processes.

Background & Summary

Sandy coastal dunes provide essential protection against storm-induced erosion for communities and infrastructure worldwide1,2. With future sea level rise3 and intensification of coastal storms4, understanding and predicting the mechanisms and extent of dune erosion is increasingly crucial. Present numerical models for predicting dune and beach erosion during storms primarily rely on semi-empirical equations5–9 to determine the magnitude and rate of dune erosion. These models often neglect geotechnical and groundwater processes, and this omission can introduce significant uncertainties in model predictions that may contribute to the need for site specific (and even storm specific) model calibration10–12. Several studies have hypothesized the role of geotechnical and groundwater processes in dune face failure and erosion13–16, yet experimental data for model validation remains lacking. Controlled laboratory measurements of dune erosion, including groundwater dynamics and time-varying interstitial moisture contents more broadly, are crucial to establish a comprehensive understanding of the physics driving the dune erosion process.

This paper describes and makes available detailed controlled laboratory wave flume measurements of dune erosion in the “collision regime”17 under varied water levels, wave conditions, and initial dune pore-moisture content. The experiments were conducted at UNSW Sydney’s Water Research Laboratory 36 m long wave flume during a 9-month period. A total of 29 tests combining three water levels, two wave conditions, and three initial dune pore-moisture content were conducted, collecting detailed hydrodynamic, morphological and groundwater data. The three water levels were chosen to simulate increasing levels of storm surge and wave collisions with the dune face. The water levels were combined with two increasingly energetic irregular wave conditions to re-create realistic erosive wave events. A further variable that was controlled and quantified for the first time was the moisture content within the dune face above the water table. Three initial pore-moisture contents within the dune face, encompassing a ’dry’ initial dune through to a fully saturated moisture content were considered.

The forcing wave conditions and their nearshore transformation, nearshore bathymetry, detailed wave-to-wave foreshore and dune profile morphology, wave runup, pore-water moisture content, and groundwater observations were quantified and recorded using a range of instrumentation. It is envisaged that this combined dataset can be used by researchers to: 1) explore nearshore morphodynamic processes under varied forcing conditions, 2) elucidate dune face erosion and failure mechanisms tying the observed erosion to internal groundwater dynamics and external wave and water level forcing and 3) validate numerical models of dune erosion processes.

Methods

Experimental design and test program

The complex interactions between nearshore hydrodynamic processes, beach and dune morphology, groundwater and pore-water dynamics, and prevailing weather conditions (e.g., rainfall, air humidity, etc.) renders causal relationships between the key forcing variables and dune face erosion difficult to observe and quantify in the field. Further, within the coastal engineering literature, in wave flume experiments that focus on replicating coastal erosion it is common to take a ‘realistic’ storm wave and water-level hydrograph, scale this appropriately to the size of the flume, and impose these conditions to a representative beach profile9,18,19. A difficulty encountered when adopting this approach is the covariation of water levels and wave conditions throughout each test run, meaning that it is not possible to identify and quantify the effect that a specific variable may have on the measured dune erosion. To circumvent this, in the experimental program outlined below three key variables were controlled and varied individually: wave conditions; still water levels; and the initial pore moisture content within the dune. This approach offers the opportunity to simulate dune erosion under much more controlled conditions, carefully varying the forcing variables, isolating the effect of each and rendering causal relationships with the observed dune erosion clearer.

Furthermore, as the main interest of these experiments was in quantifying the geotechnical and groundwater processes affecting dune face erosion under high waves and water levels, conventional scaling of sediment transport and wave conditions using the dimensionless fall parameter and Froude number, respectively, was not done18. This is in virtue of the fact that this sort of scaling would have distorted the geotechnical and groundwater properties of the dune face. To counter this, we maximized the scale of the dune to the dimensions of the wave flume and ensured that the wave conditions employed were erosive in nature and were also as big as possible within the constraints of the flume size.

Wave Flume and Sediment Characteristics

The experiments were performed in a 36 m × 1.6 m × 0.9 m (length x height x width) wave flume located at UNSW Sydney’s Water Research Laboratory (Fig. 1a). The wave flume has a 20 m long glass section which aids in visually observing cross-shore processes of sediment transport and dune erosion, as well as offering the chance to measure bathymetry during testing without having to interrupt the waves or drain the tank. The sediment used to construct the beach and dune profile was ‘Sydney fine sand’, sourced in Kurnell, NSW. This sediment was chosen based on two main criteria: 1) it was sufficiently fine to ensure ‘erosive conditions’ based on the dimensionless fall parameter and the wave conditions used (as discussed in the next section), and 2) detailed geotechnical characterisation of the same sand source was performed in previous PhD work20. Sediment settling velocity was also calculated using Stokes Law. Wave Conditions and Initial Beach Profiles

The nearshore, beach and dune profile was constructed in the wave flume as shown in Fig. 1a. In total, 8.6 m3 or 13 metric tonnes of sand was placed in the flume. The initial beach and dune profile was determined by first setting the dune toe elevation relative to the flume floor (as detailed below) and then forming an equilibrium beach profile and dune face above and below this reference point. This process was repeated for both wave conditions, with the corresponding initial profile and dune shape recorded along the glass wall of the tank and reconstructed before each test. After reshaping and prior to filling the flume with water, a mechanical compactor was used to achieve a consistent porosity of 0.4 for each test. Preliminary tests were conducted to establish the necessary compaction duration to achieve this porosity. This porosity of 0.4 matches the measured laboratory porosity and approximates the observed porosity ranges within the natural dunes from where the sediment for these experiments was sourced (Table 1).

The two irregular wave conditions employed are summarized in Table 2, defined by their combination of significant wave height (Hs) and peak wave period (Tp). These two wave conditions were selected using the erosion-accretion criterion outlined in Larson and Kraus5 based on the calculation of the dimensionless fall parameter Ω = Hs/wTp21,22 where the sediment fall velocity w (m/s) is a function of sediment grain size. The dune toe elevation was set at 0.85 m above the flume floor to ensure that, for the tested waves and water levels, the intersection of the sandy profile and flume floor remained deeper than the expected depth of closure24. Landward of the dune toe, the initial dune face was shaped with a 45° planar slope (1:1), approximating the natural angle of repose for the range of unsaturated moisture contents examined. To prevent overtopping under the tested wave conditions, the dune crest was constructed to a height of 0.55 m above the dune toe. The dune crest was extended landward at this same elevation by a horizontal distance of approximately 3 m, to ensure sufficient available sand volume during testing (Figs. 1a and 3).

After establishing the preliminary beach and dune profile, the two wave conditions (Table 2) were individually applied to “season” this idealized profile for six hours at a water level of 0.7 m relative to the flume floor. This duration was sufficient for each wave condition to reach equilibrium, as no further subaqueous profile changes were observed. The resulting equilibrium profile was then marked on the flume wall for reference. The equilibrium beach profiles for the two wave conditions are shown in Fig. 1b. In each case a bar formed in the nearshore area where most of the wave breaking occurred. For the smaller wave condition A, the bar was located 2.5 m seawards of the dune toe resulting in a more reflective beach profile, whilst for the more energetic wave condition B (refer to Table 2) the bar formed with the crest 5 m seawards of the dune toe resulting in a more dissipative beach profile. The dune face remained unaltered due to the lower water level used for profile seasoning, and as anticipated, negligible sand was transported seawards of the mobile sand profile for both wave conditions during testing. On this basis, this initial profile corresponding to each of the two wave conditions was manually constructed in the wave flume prior to every test. By this approach, the observed dune face erosion and associated profile adjustment were a direct function of the specific wave, water-level, and initial dune pore-moisture conditions.

Water Levels

The three still water levels used for testing are summarised in Table 3. These water levels were selected based on Sallenger’s17 widely used dune erosion regime framework. Each regime is defined by the elevation of the Total Water Level or ‘TWL’ (i.e., the sum of the still water level, wave setup, and wave runup) relative to the vertical elevations of the dune toe and dune crest. The still water level was selected to ensure that the dune face remained within the “Collision Regime,” where the maximum vertical extent of wave runup surpasses the dune toe elevation but remains below the dune crest. As a result, dune overtopping and overwash processes were not considered. The lower water level was set below the dune toe level, the mid water level to match the elevation of the dune toe at 0.85 m, and the higher water level to intersect the dune face at one-third of the dune height. Dune Moisture Content

Sand moisture content within the dune was a key variable in these experiments that in previous work has not been varied and continuously measured to the extent used here. After constructing the initial beach and dune profile, the flume water level was raised to one of the test still water levels (Table 3) and left overnight to allow the steady-state water table to equilibrate at this elevation within the beach and dune. During this period, the capillary fringe naturally formed, extending 15–20 cm above the still water level, where the sand matrix remained fully saturated. See Conti, et al.16 for a more detailed review of the geotechnics and moisture dynamics of dunes during wave erosion.

Table 4 outlines the three distinct sand moisture contents that were imposed within the dune, which were established and controlled in the laboratory above the height of the saturated capillary fringe. These contrasting soil moisture conditions were achieved by wetting the dune to varying extents from above, both prior to and during testing, using a spray system applied to the horizontal surface of the dune. The lower moisture content was achieved by applying no additional water to the dune matrix. This volumetric water content, ranging from 0.03 to 0.08, corresponds to the background atmospheric humidity. The ‘field capacity’ moisture content was achieved by applying limited amount of water to the dune. The term ‘field capacity’ describes the maximum amount of water that a soil matrix can retain after it has been fully saturated and then allowed to drain under gravity. This volumetric water content simulates natural conditions where the dune reaches its maximum water-holding capacity, without altering groundwater conditions relative to the prevailing wave and water levels at the beach face. Watering tests were performed to determine the field capacity of the dune and apply the correct amount of water during experimental testing. The two lower moisture conditions reflect identical groundwater states but differ in the moisture content above the capillary fringe. Finally, the ‘saturated’ dune condition (achieved through continuous application of water to the dune from above) represents a scenario where the sand dune matrix is fully saturated, elevating the water table within the dune and decoupling it from the waves and water levels at the beach face. Test Program

The test program comprised 18 unique combinations of the two wave conditions, three water levels, and three dune moisture contents. These tests, labelled T01–T18 in Table 4, were each run for 4000 waves (approximately 1.1–1.75 hours, depending on the wave condition, refer Table 2) to observe and quantify erosion of the dune face, water table dynamics and internal pore-moisture conditions. Additionally, a series of 9 supplementary tests (T19–T27) were carried out, repeating the conditions of tests T01–T06 and T10–T12 but extending the duration to 8000 waves. The objectives of these extended tests were twofold: firstly, to assess the repeatability of the experimental results; and secondly, to ensure that the maximum extent of dune erosion was reached, characterized by waves no longer impacting the dune face and further recession ceasing. Two final tests T28-T29 were performed with the rising water levels (Fig. 2) for a duration of 4000 waves combined with the more energetic wave condition B and the minimum pore-moisture content.

Morphodynamic Measurements

Offshore and nearshore wave conditions were recorded using two arrays of capacitance wave gauges (Fig. 4b). These wave probe arrays were positioned along the centreline of the 0.9 m wide wave flume. The morphological evolution of the submerged profile seaward of the shoreline was monitored throughout all tests. Positioned within the glass-walled section of the wave flume (Fig. 1a), the active profile was measured using vertical rulers attached to the external side of the glass wall, spaced at 0.5 m intervals along the flume (Figs. 3a, 4c). The submerged profile was recorded every 600 waves, corresponding to 11- and 15-minute intervals for the two wave periods tested (Table 2).Fig. 4Pictures of Instrumentation.

Pictures of Instrumentation.

A 2-D scanning LIDAR (SICK LMS111 – Fig. 4a) was installed above the flume located 0.3 m from the side of the tank and aligned with the sphere of influence of the moisture probes. This system was used to simultaneously record wave runup and the evolving subaerial beach and eroding dune profile, scanning at a frequency of 25 Hz. The use of laser scanning devices has become increasingly common in coastal and hydraulic research over the past decade due to their non-intrusive nature, which avoids interference with hydrodynamic or morphological processes (e.g.25–27). To assess any three-dimensional variations in the beach and dune profile, three additional LIDAR scans were conducted before and after each test, with the flume drained of water. These scans captured the full profile at different cross-sectional positions across the width of the wave flume.

Groundwater and Pore-Moisture Measurements

The elevation of the water table at the location immediately landward of the erosion-affected region of the dune was continuously monitored using a capacitance water level logger (Odyssey Xtreem, Fig. 4d) installed within a slotted well (Fig. 3b), recording data at 0.1 Hz. The logger was situated 1.92–2.42 m landward of the dune toe, where no erosion was anticipated. The use of a slotted well effectively served as a low-pass filter (see28), smoothing out rapid fluctuations in the phreatic surface (i.e., instantaneous pore pressure) caused by individual wave runup in the swash zone. To prevent sand ingress, the slotted well was wrapped in geotextile fabric, and data were retrieved via a Bluetooth connection to the logger.

To further measure the groundwater dynamics and how this oscillated on a wave-by-wave basis, two pairs of high-sensitivity, low-range, vented pressure transducers (Keller PSI 46S, Fig. 4e) sampling at 8 Hz were deployed within the dune. The instrumentation was identical to that detailed by Turner and Masselink29 for this same purpose. For each test, the sensor pairs were repositioned to ensure they were situated directly beneath the water table surface (Fig. 3b).

Two types of soil moisture probes (Meter Group TEROS 10 and Sentek Drill & Drop) were embedded throughout the dune profile to monitor initial pore-moisture content (Table 4) and track how this evolved within the actively eroding dune during each test. A total of twelve probes, six of each type, were deployed. Both instruments operate on the same principle, estimating moisture content within a small control volume by measuring the apparent dielectric permittivity of the surrounding medium using Frequency Domain Reflectometry (FDR). The dielectric permittivity, which reflects a material’s ability to store electrical charge, is directly related to its moisture content. Raw probe output was converted into moisture content using appropriate calibration curves (see Technical Validation Section).

The TEROS 10 probe is a compact (~5.0 × 2.5 × 7.5 cm) single sensor designed for direct burial in the sand matrix (Fig. 4f). In contrast, the Drill & Drop is a 90 cm long probe containing nine individual moisture and temperature sensors evenly spaced along its length (Fig. 4g). Due to its more intrusive design, the Drill & Drop probes were installed further landward in the dune, away from the actively eroding region, and were mainly used to control the initial moisture content of the dune. The TEROS 10 probes were used to measure time-varying pore-moisture content within the actively eroding dune face. Their placement was adjusted for each test, depending on the wave conditions and water levels, to ensure the sensors remained above the initial capillary fringe and did not remain in the saturated zone for the entire test duration. Both probe types were connected to a Campbell Scientific CR300 data logger, with data recorded at frequencies of 1 Hz (TEROS 10) and 0.066 Hz (Drill & Drop).

Test Conditions.

Wave data

Wave data was collected using two three-probe arrays that were along the centerline of the wave flume, located offshore close to the wave paddle and onshore of the sandbar. Raw and analysed wave data for each test are present in the Raw Wave Data and Analysed Wave Data folders. The raw wave data consists of.csv files saved under the name of Txx_WP.csv. The raw wave data include calibration constants, three probe array spacing, wave timeseries from each probe and incident and reflected timeseries for the onshore and offshore three probe array using the method of Mansard & Funke31. All data was sampled at 25 Hz. See technical validation for further information on the accuracy and calibration of the probes.

The analysed data was processed using the WAFO toolbox32 and consists of offshore and onshore wave energy spectra data (Txx_energy_spectrum_Offshore.csv & Txx_energy_spectrum_Onshore.csv), wave timeseries (Txx_time_series_Offshore.csv & Txx_time_series_Onshore.csv) & wave statistics (Txx__wave_statistics_Offshore.csv & Txx__wave_statistics_Onshore.csv) present in a folder with the corresponding name.

Bathymetric data

Bathymetric data was collected for each test using vertical rulers attached to the glass wall of the flume. The data is saved as Txx_Bathy.txt files for each test. Note that the location of the vertical ruler shifts by half a meter between tests with wave condition A and wave condition B. This is because the dune for all tests using wave condition B was translated half a meter landwards to better fit the dissipative equilibrium profile. Accuracy of the bathymetric data is described in the Technical Validation Section.

LIDAR data

The LIDAR was used to collect high frequency data of dune and foreshore morphology, wave runup and complete initial and final beach profiles. Two subfolders called Initial and Final Beach Profiles and Morphology and Runup Data are present each with RAW and processed data.

In the Initial and Final Beach Profile there are the raw initial (Txx_Initial_x_RAW.txt) and final (Txx_Final_x_RAW.txt) line scans performed at three locations along the width of the wave flume. The raw data is in the form of x-y coordinates and is untransformed. Processed initial (Txx_Initial_x.txt) and final (Txx_Final_x.txt) data are found in the corresponding folder and consist of the raw data cleaned and transformed to the origin coordinates.

In the Morphology and Runup Data folder there are the raw LIDAR measurements performed during each test named Txx_LID_DATA.txt. The raw data was acquired at a frequency of 25 Hz and an angular frequency of 0.25° and has been transformed into cartesian coordinates. The time array was measured in Epoch time.

From the raw data, dune and foreshore morphological data was extracted and saved as DuneProfile_Txx.txt files for all tests (see Technical Validation for how this was done). This data was resampled at 1 Hz and is in the form of x-y coordinates per time. The time array was transformed to Australian Eastern Standard Time (AEST) and all the other data sets were synced to the processed LIDAR data.

Wave runup timeseries were extracted from the raw data using a maximum threshold method (see Technical Validation for specifics) and saved in the Wave Runup folder inside the timeseries folder as Txx_Swash_Time_Series.txt. Interference due to watering and other instrumentation falling in front of the LIDAR led to some of the runup timeseries being compromised, particularly for the saturated moisture content conditions. In these instances, wave runup elevation is saved with NaN values. Nonetheless, a majority of wave runup was captured, rendering statistical analysis possible. Wave runup timeseries were collected at a frequency of 25 Hz. Further data containing wave swash peak elevation (Txx_Swash_Peaks.txt) of the swash timeseries and probability density function of the swash peak elevation (Txx_Swash_Stats.txt) to perform swash statistics are present in the swashstats folder.

For specifics on how the LIDAR data was processed and its accuracy, refer to the Technical Validation Section.

Pore-moisture data

Pore-moisture dynamics data was collected for all tests using the Teros10 and Drill and Drop probes. A separate folder is available in the data repository for each of these.

The calibrated data for both was collected and saved in the processed folder as Txx_TEROS10_Data_Processed.txt and Txx_DD_Data_Processed.txt files for the Teros 10 and Drill and Drop data, respectively. The Teros10 probes sampled data at 1 Hz whilst the Drill and Drop probes had a sampling rate of 0.06 Hz. The moisture data takes the form of volumetric water content, with a value of 0 indicating completely air ‘dry’ conditions and a value 0.4 indicating a fully saturated pore moisture content. Uncalibrated data is also present in the folders and is named Txx_TEROS10_DATA.dat and Txx_DD_DATA.dat for the Teros10 and Drill and Drop data, respectively.

For the calibration procedure and accuracy of the probes, refer to the Technical Validation.

Groundwater data

Groundwater data was collected using an Odyssey water level logger and Keller pressure transducers. A separate folder is available in the data repository for each of these.

In the Odyssey WLL Data folder there is the processed Odyssey data saved as Txx_ODYSSEY_Water_Level_Logger.txt which consists of average water table elevation from the flume bottom in centimeters across time. This data was sampled at 0.1 Hz.

In the Keller PT Data folder there is the raw (TxxPTDATA.csv) and processed (Txx_PT_Data_Processed.txt) instantaneous water table data across time from the pressure transducers. All data was acquired at 8 Hz. The raw data is measured in KPa and is not transformed into the origin coordinate system. It includes measurements of faulty probes (see Data Gaps section below). The processed data is in the form of centimeters of water from the flume bottom and has only been saved for the active probes. Instrument location is specified as a separate file for all test cases. See Technical Validation for how this data was processed and the accuracy and calibration of the probes.

Technical Validation

Instrument calibration and accuracy

Wave probes

The wave probes use capacitance transmitters to determine the difference in the dielectric constant at the water - air interface and by transforming this to a reference voltage at a frequency of 25 Hz, instantaneously measuring the water surface elevation. The probes were calibrated by immersing them at varying fixed depths in a water column and deriving a linear relationship between the output voltage and immersion depth. All wave probes were re-calibrated every week to minimise the effects of offset drift and to account for any changes in the dielectric constant of the water used. The wave probes had a standard error of ±2 mm.

Three-probe wave arrays were used to determine incident and reflected wave components in the flume. A least-squares method, following the methodology introduced by Mansard and Funke31, was used to separate the incident and reflected components of the wave spectrum. The method requires simultaneous measurement of waves at three predefined positions in the flume within close proximity to each other. For an in-depth mathematical description of the least-squares method used, refer to Mansard and Funke31.

LIDAR and beach profiles

The Lidar used was a 2-D SICK LMS111 20100 LIDAR camera, shown in Fig. 4a. LIDAR technology operates by emitting pulsed light wave against objects that bounces off and returns to the sensor. Spatial characterization of the emission plane is performed by comparing the return frequency of the light waves. The LMS111 LIDAR operates with a maximum field of view of 190° and angular resolution of 0.25°. A scanning frequency of 25 Hz was utilized, producing high frequency, continuous spatial data33. According to the manufacturer specifications, the sensor is reported to have a systematic measurement error of ±25 mm when measuring objects at a distance from 1–10 m (refer to SICK manual33), and this was found to be consistent with its use in these experiments. The LMS111 was operated through the SOPAS software developed by SICK and was post-processed in Matlab using the methodology outline below to extract wave runup timeseries and continuous morphological data of the eroding dune and foreshore profile. The raw data was smoothed using a Savitsky Golay filter with a polynomial order of 3 and a frame length of 11.

Wave runup was calculated using a maximum threshold method. Initially, the uneroded dune and beach profile served as a baseline for comparison. At each timestep (25 per second), the difference between the raw data and the baseline profile was computed, and the swash tip was identified as the most landward location where this difference exceeded 5 mm. As the swash tip was tracked throughout each swash cycle, a new baseline profile was established at moments of minimum wave runup. By this approach the changes in foreshore and dune morphology due to wave runup were accounted for. A low pass filter (>1 Hz) was applied to the data to get rid of noise in the runup timeseries. An extract of wave runup timeseries and swash statistics during T10 are shown in Fig. 5b-c. Dune and foreshore morphology was then extracted from the raw data at each swash cycle minima and averaged to create one profile per second (Fig. 5a).Fig. 5(a) Raw LIDAR data from T10 shown in grey with the extracted dune and foreshore morphology colored by time, (b) Wave runup timeseries snapshot from T10 and (c) histogram and normal distribution of wave runup from T10.

Water level loggers and pressure sensors

The mean water table inside the dune system was measured in real time using one Odyssey Xtreem Capacitive Water Level Logger installed inside a slotted well. The sensor (Fig. 4d) measured the capacitance acting inside a volume of fluid. The capacitance is directly related to the volume of fluid sitting on top of the sensor and was calibrated to determine the water level above the sensor. The Odyssey WL logger spanned 1.5 m in length and was connected to an internal in-situ memory unit that saved data continuously for a fixed span of time. The logger was set up for the data acquisition frequency of 0.1 Hz while connected to a PC and started using the data acquisition software Odyssey, then deployed autonomously inside the slotted well. After testing, the logger was re-connected to the PC and the data was downloaded from its internal memory. The calibration procedure consisted of hanging the logger in a water column at known depths and calibrating the raw output to the observed water level. A calibration check of the logger was undertaken every 1–2 weeks, to confirm its accuracy of ±1 mm.

The pressure transducers (Fig. 4e) used to measure the oscillating phreatic surface (Fig. 3b) were calibrated to measure a gauge pressure range corresponding to −300 mm to +700 mm of hydraulic head. To protect the sensing diaphragm, a 40 µm stainless steel screen was mounted over the pressure port, preventing sand grains from exerting mechanical force on the sensor. The void between the protective screen and the diaphragm was filled with silicon oil, which has the same specific gravity as fresh water, enabling the rapid transmission of pore pressure fluctuations from the adjacent sand matrix to the sensor. The pressure sensors were installed in two vertical pairs with a fixed spacing between them. This configuration allowed for the measurement of the instantaneous phreatic surface elevation and detection of any vertical flow within this zone. The probes were re-calibrated every week with new silicon oil placed inside the sensor diaphragm to avoid the probes drifting. Pore-moisture probes

Soil-specific calibration curves are necessary to obtain accurate pore-moisture content measurements. Changes in mineral composition, grain size distribution, wetting or drying cycles and, to a lesser extent, dune density, can all affect the dielectric permittivity of a sand matrix. Therefore, different soils will present distinct moisture content vs dielectric permittivity curves. The Teros 10 probes were calibrated in-house, whilst the Drill and Drop probes were calibrated using an equation found in literature34.

To calibrate the Teros10 probes, a curve was produced relating the raw output of the probes to controlled moisture conditions in a sand sample in the laboratory. Dry sand samples of around 20 kg or approximately 0.015 m3 were placed in a 25 L bucket and progressively wet from air-saturated to water-saturated. The samples were prepared to suffice the following criteria: (1) The volume of the sand sample was large enough to fully encapsulate the moisture probe and the sphere of influence over which it acted, (2) the sand samples were compacted equally to guarantee dry bulk density homogeneity, and (3) each time water was added to the sample, the sand-water matrix was thoroughly mixed to ensure that the water content was homogenous throughout. After each water addition, raw output readings were recorded for each probe, and a core sample collected to determine the volumetric water content by the oven-drying technique35. For each probe, 8 different pore-water moisture measurements were obtained, and these were combined to form the soil calibration curve shown in Fig. 8 from which the linear calibration curved was obtained.Fig. 8Soil Calibration Curve for the Teros 10 moisture probes.

Usage Notes

All the data record folders include README.txt files that explain how to load and interpret the data. If any other query remains concerning the datasets, we recommend to contact the authors. It should be noted that the LIDAR used had a blind spot of 5° in its field of view from 95–100°. During testing, no data was recorded past the 90° mark, whilst in the initial/final full profile scans, profile data in the blind spot was interpolated.
