Case report of seminal vesicle schwannoma with synchronous prostate cancer
Authors: Mitali Kini, Joshua Cabral, Marlene Gallegos, Cristina Arruza-Frau, Safiya-Hana Belbina, Jonathan Aguiar, Vinaik Sundaresan, Gregory Raster, Faraz Longi, Larissa Bresler, Joel Cornfield
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeminal vesicle schwannomas are rare entities, reported in only a handful of cases since 2002. These benign neoplasms originate from the peripheral nerve sheath and are typically found incidentally on imaging or during the workup of nonspecific urologic symptoms. The case presented here describes the incidental finding of a seminal vesicle schwannoma, initially believed to be “acute seminal vesiculitis” during a gross hematuria evaluation. The patient was diagnosed with synchronous prostate cancer for which he underwent open radical retropubic prostatectomy, with excision of bilateral seminal vesicles, resulting in the diagnosis of seminal vesicle schwannoma on final pathology review.Keywords: Seminal vesicle, Schwannoma, Histopathology, Prostate cancer, Prostatectomy

Introduction

Primary malignant neoplasms of the seminal vesicles (SV) are exceedingly rare, with approximately 30 cases reported in the literature since 2002.1 The most common malignant seminal vesicle masses are those of secondary malignancy origin, typically of adjacent organs such as the prostate. Primary neoplasms can occur rarely and include sarcomas such as leiomyosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, angiosarcoma, or entities such as seminomas, and squamous cell carcinomas.1,2 Benign neoplasms are the most frequently noted masses of the SVs, including fibromas, leiomyomas, schwannomas, cystadenomas and papillary adenomas. Schwannomas are benign nerve sheath tumors of peripheral nerves. These typically occur in the head and neck but can be found rarely in the pelvis. Most schwannomas are sporadic and most commonly present between 20 and 50 years of age.1,2

Here, we report a clinical case of a patient with a seminal vesicle schwannoma, with synchronous prostate cancer. This is only the second case reported in the literature of seminal vesicle schwannoma diagnosed and resected at the time of open radical retropubic prostatectomy during the evaluation and treatment of co-occurring localized prostate cancer.3

Case presentation

This is a case report of a 69-year-old man who was initially seen by Urology for management of gross hematuria and urinary retention. His medical history consisted of hypertension, substance use disorder and a history of a brachial deep vein thrombosis for which the patient was on chronic anticoagulation. He underwent a thorough gross hematuria workup with a urine culture and cytology, commuted tomography (CT) urogram and cystoscopy which were negative for renal or bladder pathology. Workup was significant for findings of an asymmetrically enlarged right seminal vesicle concerning for “acute seminal vesiculitis” as well as a large thickened bladder with prostatomegaly.

On further evaluation, the patient was noted to have a prostate specific antigen (PSA) of 4.26 for which he underwent a prostate magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) study (Fig. 1). The MRI demonstrated moderate benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) as well as a heterogenous T2 hyperintense and mildly T1 hypointense lesion of the right seminal vesicle measuring 3.6 cm in greatest dimension. The lesion was described to be thick-walled with peripheral enhancement on post-contrast imaging with no significant restricted diffusion. The radiographic differential diagnosis at this time was cystadenoma versus primary seminal vesicle neoplasm. The patient was then scheduled for transrectal ultrasound and prostate needle biopsy given his elevated PSA. Digital rectal exam demonstrated an indurated right seminal vesicle, fixed at the superior portion, with no nodularity or induration of the prostate gland. The prostate gland was biopsied using the standard template (six cores from the right hemigland and six cores from the left hemigland). Additional cores were obtained targeting the right SV.

Pathology demonstrated Grade Group 2 (Gleason 3 + 4 = 7) prostate cancer in the right anterior medial prostate biopsy core. Pathology from the seminal vesicle biopsy was noted to be “atypical stromal cells, favoring prostatic stromal tumor of uncertain malignant potential (STUMP).” This new diagnosis of favorable intermediate risk prostate cancer as well as STUMP of the right SV was discussed with the patient extensively. The patient then opted for treatment of the prostate cancer with radical prostatectomy.

The patient was counseled regarding the risks, benefits, and alternatives to surgical resection. Due to the patient's history of hypertension in the setting of recent substance use, and higher risk in the Trendelenberg position required for a robotic-assisted approach, we opted to pursue open retropubic radical prostatectomy. Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in a supine position. Following pelvic dissection, the right seminal vesicle was exposed and noted to be firm on palpation. A frozen section was sent to pathology intraoperatively in order to determine if the patient had evidence of SV invasion of his known prostate cancer, prior to proceeding with prostatectomy. The frozen section was reported to contain “minute fragments of spindle cells” without concern for prostatic adenocarcinoma invasion. Radical prostatectomy was performed with removal of bilateral SVs intact. Following surgery, the patient recovered well and was discharged on post-operative day three.

The patient's immediate postoperative course was without complication. The patient was seen for catheter removal one week following discharge. On six-week follow up, the patient's PSA was undetectable and he reported doing well and voiding without issue with no incontinence episodes.

On gross pathology, the specimen was described as a “tan-pink, smooth to shaggy prostate with attached bilateral seminal vesicles.” The right SV was measured at 3.5cm in greatest dimension and described to have a “soft pale-tan-yellow, focally hemorrhagic and cystic well-circumscribed mass, directly adjacent to the right seminal vesicle. The cystic space contains transparent pale yellow fluid.”

On microscopic pathology, the prostate was noted to contain Gleason 7 (3 + 4) prostate adenocarcinoma, pT2, involving 5–10 % of the tissue with negative margins and absent extraprostatic extension or seminal vesicle invasion. The right SV lesion was reported to be a “spindle cell lesion” which was sent to the Joint Pathology Center for further evaluation. Fig. 2 depicts the microscopic hematoxylin and eosin (H and E) images. The specimen shows a spindle cell lesion with areas of degenerative change and pseudocyst formation. The tumor has both higher cellularity collagenous areas with nuclear palisading consistent with Antoni A areas, and lower cellularity myxoid areas consistent with Antoni B areas. Some of the lesional Schwann cells exhibit nuclear enlargement consistent with degenerative change. Of note, the specimen demonstrates intense S100 protein reactivity consistent with schwannoma, and CD34 positive fibroblasts in the Antoni B areas. Ki-67 shows a low proliferative rate. The specimen was negative for AE1/3, desmin, NKX3.1, STAT6, myogenin and myo-dl immunohistochemical stains. The final diagnosis was schwannoma of the seminal vesicle.

Discussion

Tumors of the seminal vesicle are very rare. Malignant SV tumors typically arise from contiguous spread from adjacent organs such as the bladder or prostate, or disseminated spread such as renal cell carcinoma, testicular neoplasms or melanoma.1,2 Schwannomas are benign tumors of the peripheral nerves. Schwannoma of the seminal vesicle has only been reported in a handful of cases.

These tumors are usually asymptomatic, but have the potential to cause nerve compression due to mass effect. Rarely, these masses can cause chronic pelvic pain or hematospermia. In a small number of case reports, patients have presented with ejaculatory obstruction or even ureteral compression resulting in unilateral hydronephrosis.4,5 In the majority of cases, such as the case reported here, these masses are noted incidentally on imaging at the time of workup for other urologic symptoms or conditions.6

While MRI and CT scan can provide anatomical information regarding size, location, and characteristics of a suspected schwannoma, the final diagnosis relies on tissue sampling and histology. Tissue sampling can also assist in distinguishing malignant from benign peripheral nerve sheath tumors due to major differences in morphologic and immunohistochemical features.4,5 Transrectal ultrasonography (TRUS) with seminal biopsy can assist in obtaining tissue sampling of the suspected lesion, but this technique can be limited depending on size and location of the mass and the presence of cystic versus solid components.4 In the present study, TRUS biopsy was performed at the time of prostate biopsy, but the pathology was equivocal.

Histologically, seminal vesicle schwannomas are composed of alternating regions of dense cellularity, otherwise known as “Antoni-A″ regions, with regions of myxoid matrix, known as “Antoni-B″ regions1,3 On immunohistochemical staining, these lesions are most characteristically positive for S-100, negative for CD34, and negative for epithelial markers or smooth muscle antigens.1 It should be noted that in the present study, the initial transrectal ultrasound-guided biopsy of the SV mass had demonstrated “STUMP” on pathology. This is typically characterized by a high density of spindle cells and atypical stromal proliferation on pathology and is usually positive for CD34 and progesterone receptors on immunohistochemical analysis7. There is no literature to suggest that there is a pathologic relationship between STUMP and schwannomas of the seminal vesicle. However, given the wide array of histologic patterns encompassed by STUMP, these lesions are often difficult to distinguish from other spindle cell tumors such as schwannomas. STUMPs may appear as spindle cell proliferation with phyllodes-type pattern with leaflike growth. Therefore, the discrepancy between the initial TRUS biopsy findings and the final pathology result are presumed to be due to minimal or poor sampling of the SV tissue to allow for adequate distinction of this pathology or potential off-target sampling of the surrounding prostatic stromal tissue which can often displace the SVs laterally8.

The mainstay of treatment for cases of SV schwannomas is surgical excision9,10 In the case presented here, the patient had synchronous prostate cancer for which he had opted for surgical resection. Due to patient factors, this was performed using an open approach. However, the literature has described SV schwannoma excision using open, laparoscopic and robotic approaches with similar outcomes.6,9 Use of the open approach in the present case allowed for tactile assessment of the mass and directly targeted intraoperative biopsies to be obtained. The robotic-assisted laparoscopic approach provides the advantage of favorable cosmetic outcomes, improved visualization of pelvic anatomy and faster recovery time postoperatively.2,4 Another consideration is the use of intraoperative sonography to more reliably identify tumor borders, similar to how this is performed during a robotic partial nephrectomy, and to ensure complete resection and negative margins.4 In the present case, ultrasonography was not utilized, as resection of the entire prostate was being performed for the purpose of prostate cancer control and therefore the SVs were resected in their entirety in tandem.

Conclusion

Seminal vesicle schwannoma is a rare, benign neoplasm that may be diagnosed incidentally on imaging in patients presenting with non-specific symptoms or undergoing evaluation for various urologic conditions. The use of CT and MRI can provide a differential diagnosis but tissue sampling in the form of biopsy or surgical resection is the most definitive means of diagnosis. At this time, surgical resection remains the mainstay of treatment, but future studies are needed to understand the role of conservative measures, such as imaging surveillance, as an option for patients who may be poor surgical candidates. In the case reported here, the patient opted to undergo surgical resection of his prostate for the purpose of cancer treatment for newly diagnosed favorable intermediate risk prostate cancer. This allowed for concurrent excision of the SV schwannoma and provided a definitive diagnosis of this rare condition.
