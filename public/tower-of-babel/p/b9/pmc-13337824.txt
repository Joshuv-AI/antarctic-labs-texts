The Editor,

We read with interest the study by Dhenki et al., which compared 6-zone and 12-zone lung ultrasound (LUS) protocols with high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) in critically ill patients and demonstrated improved sensitivity and agreement with HRCT using the extended 12-zone approach.1

While increasing the number of scanning zones may enhance detection of posterior and subpleural abnormalities, this approach does not address a fundamental limitation of LUS as a regionally dependent imaging modality. Lung ultrasound provides a localized assessment of lung aeration, and expanding scan zones may improve coverage but does not overcome its inherent inability to detect deeper or nonsubpleural pathology.2,3 Furthermore, multiple LUS protocols exist without clear evidence demonstrating the superiority of one method over another, suggesting that incremental expansion of scanning zones alone may not translate into meaningful clinical impact.2

An important methodological consideration is the temporal separation between LUS and HRCT acquisition. In the present study, LUS was performed within 24 hours of HRCT.1 Although this approach improves feasibility and standardization, in critically ill patients with rapidly evolving lung aeration, even this interval may permit clinically meaningful changes in recruitment, edema, or consolidation, potentially influencing agreement between modalities.

More importantly, the clinical relevance of improved diagnostic agreement remains uncertain in the absence of outcome-based validation. While LUS demonstrates good diagnostic performance across multiple clinical settings, with variability depending on specific sonographic patterns and diagnostic algorithms, its role has evolved beyond static diagnosis toward real-time monitoring of lung aeration and guiding bedside management.4–6

In contemporary critical care practice, LUS should be viewed as a dynamic physiological tool rather than merely a diagnostic surrogate. Integrating LUS-derived aeration assessment with physiological indices of ventilator-induced lung stress may provide a more meaningful framework. For instance, combining LUS with measures such as mechanical power—an integrative marker of ventilatory energy delivery—may better capture the interaction between ventilatory load and evolution of lung injury, with potential implications for both prognosis and individualized ventilatory strategies.7

In conclusion, while extended LUS protocols improve diagnostic sensitivity, future research should move beyond protocol expansion and focus on integrating LUS within physiological and outcome-based frameworks to better define its role in critically ill patients.
