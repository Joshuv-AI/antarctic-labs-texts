Predictors of in-hospital mortality among adults with chronic kidney disease at a national tertiary hospital in Sierra Leone
Authors: Foray Mohamed Foray, Henry Sankoh, Joshua Coker, John Abdul Koroma, Norman M Farama-Bangura, Fatima Jalloh, Mark I Kapuwa, Alhassan Sumah, Soccoh Kabia, Gibrilla Fadlu Deen, Bamba Gaye, Mohamed B Jalloh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn-hospital outcomes among adults with chronic kidney disease (CKD) in low-resource settings are poorly characterized. We evaluated predictors of in-hospital mortality among adults admitted with CKD to Sierra Leone’s national tertiary hospital, which houses the country’s only public dialysis unit. We retrospectively reviewed case notes for all adults with CKD admitted to Connaught Hospital, Freetown, from January 1, 2022, through December 31, 2024. CKD stage was assigned according to KDIGO estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) categories. The primary outcome was in-hospital death. Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine prespecified predictors of mortality. Among 385 admissions (median age, 48 years [interquartile range, 38 to 57]; 57.1% men), 64.2% presented with KDIGO G5 CKD and 41.0% received dialysis. In-hospital death occurred in 95 patients (24.7%). Each 10 mL/min/1.73 m² lower eGFR was associated with higher odds of death (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.94; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.42 to 2.66), as were heavier proteinuria (aOR, 2.23; 95% CI, 1.39 to 3.58 per one-category increase on dipstick) and diabetes (aOR, 1.97; 95% CI, 1.10 to 3.52). Receipt of dialysis was associated with lower adjusted odds of death (aOR, 0.55; 95% CI, 0.31 to 0.95). Age, sex, and hypertension were not statistically significant. Hospitalized adults with CKD in Sierra Leone typically present with advanced disease and heavy proteinuria, and approximately one in four died during hospitalization. Lower eGFR, greater proteinuria, and diabetes identify patients at highest risk, and access to dialysis was associated with lower short-term survival in this low-resource setting.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, in-hospital mortality, estimated glomerular filtration rate, proteinuria, hemodialysis, low-resource settings

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major noncommunicable condition that contributes substantially to premature mortality worldwide. In the most recent CKD-specific analysis by the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Chronic Kidney Disease Collaboration, an estimated 697.5 million people were living with CKD and approximately 1.2 million deaths were attributable to CKD in 2017 [1]. These figures underscore CKD’s global reach and rising toll. Beyond these headline numbers, CKD imposes cascading risks of cardiovascular events, infection, and progression to kidney failure, often necessitating kidney replacement therapy (KRT) to sustain life. Health system capacity and financing determine who receives such treatment, and where gaps persist, preventable morbidity and mortality accrue [2].

The burden of CKD is disproportionate. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), late recognition and constrained access to diagnostics and KRT intensify risk, while comorbid conditions such as hypertension and diabetes remain highly prevalent and often inadequately controlled [3,4]. In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), hospital cohorts report a younger CKD population, late presentation with advanced disease, and high mortality [3]. Sierra Leone exemplifies these constraints: sustained hemodialysis capacity was established only in 2016, and care continues to be shaped by out-of-pocket costs and workforce limitations [2,5,6]. At a national adult referral center (Connaught Hospital), a study found renal admissions comprised 2.7% of medical inpatients, with many patients presenting with advanced CKD and financial barriers to dialysis [6].

In-hospital mortality among patients with CKD varies widely by setting and severity. Global studies consistently identify low estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), proteinuria, older age, diabetes, and cardiovascular comorbidities as independent predictors of death [7–9]. In high-income settings, in-hospital mortality for patients with advanced CKD ranges from 5% to 15%, with access to dialysis substantially modifying outcomes [10,11]. Data from SSA reveal considerably higher mortality rates, often exceeding 30% among hospitalized patients with kidney failure, in the context of presentation, limited dialysis availability, and co-infections such as tuberculosis and HIV [12–14]. In Sierra Leone specifically, there is high inpatient mortality among those with kidney disease [6], but granular risk stratification using standardized CKD classification and contemporary dialysis access patterns has not been reported. This knowledge gap limits clinicians’ ability to triage patients effectively and impedes efforts to allocate scarce KRT resources based on objective prognostic data.

Against this backdrop, there is a need for contemporary, locally derived evidence to guide triage and treatment decisions for adults hospitalized with CKD in Sierra Leone. International guidelines provide a framework for staging and risk stratification: the KDIGO 2024 CKD guideline endorses classification by GFR category (G1 to G5), and albuminuria category (A1 to A3), and emphasizes routinely available measures, including eGFR and proteinuria, for clinical decision-making across settings [15]. In resource-limited hospitals, quantitative albuminuria assessment through albumin-creatinine ratio (ACR) or 24-h urinary protein estimation is either unavailable or prohibitively expensive for most patients, making dipstick proteinuria a pragmatic alternative for risk assessment [15]. Similarly, while eGFR is not routinely autoreported by most hospital laboratories in Sierra Leone, it can be calculated at the bedside using mobile applications or standard formulas, enabling rapid severity assessment. Both measures are immediately interpretable and map directly to KDIGO risk grids used worldwide [15]. Yet the independent contributions of kidney function, proteinuria, and common comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes) to in-hospital mortality remain incompletely characterized in this setting. Similarly, how receipt of dialysis during admission relates to mortality risk under real-world constraints of cost and capacity is unclear.

We sought to identify independent predictors of in-hospital mortality among adults admitted with CKD to Sierra Leone’s national referral hospital during the contemporary period when dialysis is available but not universally accessible. Using KDIGO-based staging and routinely collected admission measures (eGFR and dipstick proteinuria), we quantified associations with mortality to inform bedside risk stratification and health system planning for KRT.

Materials and methods

Study design and reporting

We conducted a retrospective cohort case-note review at a national tertiary hospital in Sierra Leone of consecutive adult admissions and report according to STROBE recommendations for observational studies [16].

Setting and study period

The study was conducted at Connaught Hospital, Freetown, Sierra Leone. We reviewed consecutive adult admissions from 1 January 2022 to 31 December 2024, sampling medical records from the cardio-renal ward, intensive care unit, and general medical wards.

Eligibility and case definition

We included patients ≥18 years with a clinician-documented diagnosis of CKD and a recorded in-hospital outcome for the index admission. We excluded patients <18 years, isolated acute kidney disease without evidence of chronicity, admissions without CKD, and records lacking outcome documentation. CKD followed KDIGO criteria (abnormality ≥3 months): reduced eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m², markers of kidney damage (albuminuria/proteinuria, hematuria of renal origin, or structural abnormalities), or KRT. In this retrospective design, chronicity was ascertained from clinical notes, laboratory records showing persistently reduced eGFR over ≥3 months where available, imaging evidence of chronic changes (e.g., reduced kidney size, increased echogenicity), and explicit clinician documentation consistent with KDIGO criteria or maintenance dialysis. Patients with isolated suspected acute kidney injury without evidence supporting chronicity were excluded. We acknowledge that AKI-on-CKD may have been present in some patients; however, our inclusion criteria required documented evidence of underlying CKD.

Variables and data sources

Demographics (age, sex, residence) and comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension) were abstracted from structured fields. Kidney function variables included serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen as recorded, and eGFR auto-reported by the hospital laboratory; the specific equation used was not systematically documented in clinical records, but laboratories in Sierra Leone typically employ creatinine-based equations such as the CKD-EPI 2009 or MDRD formula; we were unable to verify whether a single equation was used consistently across the study period. We reclassified KDIGO G stages (G1–G5) from recorded eGFR; patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis were classified as G5 in accordance with KDIGO criteria; in this cohort, all such patients had eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m², consistent with standard dialysis initiation thresholds. Urine dipstick protein (negative/trace, 1+, 2+, 3+, 4+) was analyzed as an ordinal predictor (per +1 category). Presenting symptoms were abstracted to characterize the clinical phenotype of the cohort but were not included as predictors in the primary multivariable model. This decision was prespecified based on: (1) our focus on objective, guideline-endorsed predictors (eGFR, proteinuria) that can be systematically measured; (2) the subjective and variably documented nature of symptom recording in retrospective case notes; and (3) the potential for symptoms to mediate rather than confound relationships between kidney function and mortality. Free-text symptoms were mapped to a predefined lexicon and summarized per symptom. Age categories were prespecified as <50 years (reference), 50–64, 65–74, and ≥75 years, based on clinically meaningful thresholds commonly used in nephrology research and aligned with prior CKD outcomes studies. These categories reflect working-age adults (<50), middle-aged adults approaching typical retirement age (50–64), younger elderly (65–74), and older elderly (≥75), each potentially having distinct risk profiles related to comorbidity burden, physiological reserve, and competing mortality risks.

Data management and quality assurance

Data were abstracted into a standardized template. Range checks (e.g., creatinine >15 mg/dL) and logic checks (e.g., dialysis → G5) were implemented. Missingness patterns were reviewed, and free-text fields (residence, symptoms, comorbidities) were normalized with documented codebooks.

Outcome

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, defined as death documented in the medical record during the index admission.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were two-sided with α = 0.05. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and summarized as median (IQR); groups were compared using Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables are n (%), compared with χ² or Fisher’s exact tests where expected counts were <5.

We prespecified a multivariable logistic regression with death (Yes/No) as the dependent variable and the following predictors based on clinical relevance and prior literature: (1) Age category (<50 [ref], 50–64, 65–74, ≥75), (2) sex (female [ref], male), (3) diabetes (yes/no), (4) hypertension (yes/no) [5], eGFR modeled continuously per 10 mL/min/1.73 m² decrease [6], urine protein per +1 dipstick category, and [7] dialysis (yes/no).

We excluded serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) from the model to avoid collinearity with eGFR, consistent with KDIGO guidelines that prioritize GFR over BUN for risk stratification [15]. BUN is influenced by non-renal factors including dietary protein intake, catabolism, and volume status, making eGFR the more specific and guideline-endorsed measure of kidney function for prognostic modeling. Dialysis was ascertained as a binary exposure (received vs. not received during the index admission) and was not modeled as time-varying; we did not capture the exact timing of dialysis initiation relative to admission or death.

Collinearity was evaluated with variance inflation factors (VIF), considering VIF > 10 as problematic.

Model size and events-per-variable (EPV)

All eligible cases were included. With 95 events and 7 predictors, the EPV was approximately 13.6, exceeding Peduzzi’s conventional threshold of 10 [17], which supports reasonable stability of effect estimates [18].

Functional form and assumptions

Linearity in the logit for continuous predictors was examined graphically and with Box–Tidwell checks.

Missing data

Complete data for all primary analysis variables were available for 383 of 385 patients (99.5%). Two patients had missing urine dipstick protein values and were excluded from the primary multivariable analysis. Missingness was minimal (<5%) for all predictor variables. Given the near-complete data availability, the primary complete-case analysis is unlikely to be substantially biased. Multiple imputation sensitivity analysis (20 imputations) was conducted to confirm robustness and yielded materially unchanged results (Supplementary Materials).

Sensitivity analyses

To test robustness of the primary findings, we ran two complementary sensitivity analyses reported in the Supplement. First, we fit a parsimonious logistic model that retained only core, clinically essential and minimally collinear predictors: diabetes (yes/no), eGFR modeled continuously per 10 mL/min/1.73 m² decrease, urine dipstick protein per +1 category, and dialysis (yes/no), to respect events-per-variable constraints and assess stability of effect estimates. Second, we conducted an exploratory multiple-imputation sensitivity analysis (20 imputations) including all predictors and the outcome, and repeated the parsimonious model; estimates were materially unchanged. All sensitivity analyses used two-sided α = 0.05.

Analyses were performed in Python (3.x) using statsmodels and scikit-learn. The de-identified dataset analyzed during this study and the complete analytical code are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Ethics approval and consent

Permission to conduct this study was obtained from the Hospital Care Manager at Connaught Hospital. The study received ethical approval from the Sierra Leone Ethics and Scientific Review Committee (SLESRC No: 020/01/2025). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The Committee approved a waiver of individual informed consent because the study used retrospectively collected, routinely recorded clinical data that were extracted in de-identified form, involved no direct contact with patients, posed no more than minimal risk to participants, and it would have been impracticable to obtain consent from all individuals. Data were handled in accordance with applicable ethical and confidentiality standards.

Kidney dysfunction at presentation was advanced: eGFR (median 16.0 mL/min/1.73 m², IQR 8.0–24.0) yielded KDIGO G5 in 247 (64.2%), G4 in 78 (20.3%), and G3 in 52 (13.5%). Common symptoms at presentation included fatigue (278 [72.2%], p = 0.061) and edema (265 [68.8%], p = 0.615), with nausea (75 [19.5%], p = 0.399), vomiting (56 [14.5%], p = 0.996), and oliguria (12 [3.1%], p = 0.079) less frequent. Proteinuria was heavy: 2+ in 174 (45.2%) and 3+ in 170 (44.2%), with negative/trace in 5 (1.3%). Hypertension was near-ubiquitous (326 [84.7%]), and diabetes was present in 139 (36.1%).

Compared with those who did not receive dialysis, the dialysis group was older (median 50 vs 47 years; p = 0.030), more often male (63.9% vs 52.4%; p = 0.025), had lower eGFR (median 14.0 vs 18.0 mL/min/1.73 m²; p < 0.001), and a higher prevalence of diabetes (47.5% vs 28.2%; p < 0.001), with similar BUN distributions (p = 0.652) (Table 1).

In-hospital mortality

The primary outcome occurred in 95 of 385 patients (24.7%). Crude mortality did not differ significantly by dialysis status (26.0% in no-dialysis vs 22.8% in dialysis; p = 0.473; Table 1).

Multivariable analysis of mortality

Lower eGFR, greater proteinuria, and diabetes were independently associated with higher odds of in-hospital death, whereas dialysis was associated with lower adjusted odds. Specifically, each 10 mL/min/1.73 m² decrease in eGFR was associated with a 94% increase in the odds of death (adjusted OR [aOR] 1.94, 95% CI 1.42–2.66; p = 3.4 × 10⁻⁵), and each +1 dipstick increase in urine protein was associated with a 2.23-fold higher adjusted odds (aOR 2.23, 95% CI 1.39–3.58; p = 9.0 × 10⁻⁴). Diabetes remained independently associated with mortality (aOR 1.97, 95% CI 1.10–3.52; p = 0.023). Dialysis showed a protective association (aOR 0.55, 95% CI 0.31–0.95; p = 0.032), interpreted with caution given potential confounding by indication.

Age, sex, and hypertension were not independently associated with death after adjustment (all p > 0.21). Adjusted estimates are displayed in the forest plot (Figure 1).

Notably, crude mortality rates did not differ significantly by dialysis status (26.0% in the no-dialysis group vs. 22.8% in the dialysis group; p = 0.473), yet the adjusted odds ratio showed a protective association (aOR 0.55; p = 0.032). This apparent reversal reflects confounding by indication: patients who received dialysis had more severe disease at baseline (lower eGFR, higher prevalence of diabetes) that would predispose them to higher mortality. Once these severity markers are controlled in the multivariable model, the independent association of dialysis shifts toward a protective direction. This pattern underscores that unadjusted comparisons can obscure treatment effects when indication for treatment correlates with severity.

Model discrimination and calibration

Discrimination was good (AUC 0.791, bootstrap 95% CI 0.738–0.842), indicating the model ranks patients well by risk (Figure 2A). However, calibration showed deviation at higher predicted risk deciles, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (p = 0.0066) suggested miscalibration, meaning absolute predicted probabilities may not perfectly match observed event rates, particularly at the extremes of predicted risk (Figure 2B).

Sensitivity analyses

In sensitivity analyses, a parsimonious model (diabetes, eGFR per-10 decrease, urine protein per +1, dialysis) and multiple imputation (20×; pooled estimates; parsimony set), confirmed the primary findings with materially unchanged effect sizes and model performance (Supplementary materials).

Discussion

In this consecutive cohort of 385 adults hospitalized with clinician-documented CKD at Sierra Leone’s main national referral hospital, nearly two thirds presented with KDIGO Stage 5 and half had ≥3+ heavier dipstick proteinuria. One in four patients died during the recorded episode. After adjustment, mortality was independently associated with lower eGFR, diabetes, and higher proteinuria. Dialysis was associated with lower odds of death, an estimate we interpret cautiously given potential confounding by indication. Age, sex, and history of hypertension were not significant (Graphical Abstract). These findings underscore the clinical value of simple bedside information for short-term risk stratification in resource-limited settings.

The pattern of late presentation with advanced CKD in this cohort is concordant with the global risk gradients that underpin contemporary staging systems. KDIGO emphasizes classification by GFR and albuminuria because each dimension confers independent and additive prognostic information; our results align with that framework and with large meta-analyses from the CKD Prognosis Consortium showing graded increases in adverse outcomes with lower eGFR and higher albuminuria across diverse populations [7,15,20]. Beyond laboratories that can quantify albuminuria, dipstick protein, used here as an ordinal predictor, has repeatedly tracked mortality and cardiovascular risk in population-based cohorts, supporting its pragmatic use when quantitative assays are -unavailable [21,22].

Because dialysis during admission is not randomly assigned and timing varies, the observed association (aOR ∼0.55) is associational and susceptible to confounding by indication and immortal-time bias; patients must survive to the point of dialysis initiation, which may inflate the protective estimate; causal inference would require time-dependent analyses or target-trial emulation. Our interpretation follows this guidance and treats the dialysis coefficient as suggestive rather than causal [23]. The magnitude of the observed association should not be interpreted as an estimate of treatment effect. Nevertheless, the direction of association is clinically plausible and consistent with prior local experience that documented high mortality among patients unable to access dialysis at Connaught Hospital [6].

Dialysis in Sierra Leone is centralized in Freetown. The residence data show that patients traveled from multiple districts to access care, which may point to structural barriers to timely kidney replacement therapy. International evidence indicates that distance and travel time to dialysis facilities are associated with worse outcomes, including higher mortality and more unplanned catheter starts; these associations have been reported elsewhere [24,25]. Globally, the International Society of Nephrology’s Global Kidney Health Atlas documents substantial gaps in dialysis availability and financial protection in LMICs, and Liyanage and colleagues estimate that millions who require dialysis die prematurely because they cannot access it [2,26]. These realities map directly onto our context: a single urban site, out-of-pocket costs, and long travel distances may be associated with delayed presentation, advanced metabolic derangements at admission, and unequal receipt of dialysis. Findings from the earlier Connaught Hospital series, which predated our observation period, underscore that inability to obtain dialysis has been persistently associated with poor outcomes locally, providing essential context for our contemporary analysis [6].

The association between higher education and dialysis receipt (p < 0.001) suggests potential socioeconomic barriers to kidney replacement therapy access. In Sierra Leone, where dialysis requires substantial out-of-pocket expenditure, educational attainment may serve as a proxy for financial capacity and health literacy that facilitate navigation of the healthcare system. These findings highlight equity concerns: patients with less education, who may already face compounded disadvantages, appear less likely to receive dialysis. Addressing these disparities requires policy interventions including subsidized dialysis programs, community education initiatives, and decentralization of dialysis services.

Males comprised a modest majority of admissions in this cohort, but sex was not independently associated with in-hospital mortality after adjustment. This pattern is noteworthy given the global literature showing that females often have higher CKD prevalence, particularly in stage G3, while males more commonly initiate KRT and have higher CKD-attributed mortality. Explanations include biological differences in progression as well as social and structural determinants that influence recognition, referral, and treatment access. Our null adjusted association suggests that, once hospitalized and severity is accounted for, sex did not explain residual variation in short-term mortality in this setting. It does not exclude upstream sex-linked inequities in who reaches dialysis-capable care and when [27,28]. Continued monitoring for sex-specific barriers is needed in Sierra Leone as dialysis capacity and referral systems evolve. The present findings build on local observations from the same institution and provide updated context for equity assessments [5,6].

Hypertension was present in 84.7% of patients, and this near-universal prevalence substantially limits its utility as a discriminating predictor of mortality in multivariable analysis. With insufficient variation in exposure, statistical power to detect an association is reduced even if a true effect exists. Furthermore, hypertension may exert its prognostic influence through chronic pathways (cumulative vascular damage, progression to kidney failure) rather than through acute mechanisms detectable during a single hospitalization. The null finding should not be interpreted as evidence that blood pressure control is unimportant; rather, it reflects limited within-cohort heterogeneity and the retrospective ascertainment of hypertension as a binary diagnosis rather than as measured blood pressure levels or control status at admission.

Policy implications

Two routinely obtained admission measurements, eGFR and dipstick proteinuria, may help identify high-risk patients who could benefit from early nephrology consultation, closer monitoring, and timely dialysis evaluation when indicated. These markers align with international staging systems and could potentially inform triage protocols, shared decision-making with patients and families, and quality improvement initiatives. As Sierra Leone develops strategies to expand dialysis capacity and strengthen referral networks, these simple assessments might offer a practical starting point [15].

Given the near-universal prevalence of hypertension in this cohort, blood pressure control may be best positioned as a foundational element of CKD management rather than as supplementary care. Health facilities could consider screening blood pressure at every clinical visit using validated automated devices. Adopting standardized treatment protocols with clear titration steps may be beneficial. Reliable access to affordable first-line medications and single-pill combinations is likely important where feasible. Facilities might consider ensuring uninterrupted supply chains for antihypertensive drugs and loop diuretics for volume management. Task-sharing models that engage nurses and community health workers in follow-up and adherence support could be valuable. Monitoring hypertension control rates monthly may serve as a useful quality indicator for CKD care.

At the health system level, hypertension programs may benefit from integration with CKD services and noncommunicable disease clinics. Incorporating sodium reduction initiatives and public education campaigns could further support these efforts. Establishing measurable targets across the detection, treatment, and control cascade may be helpful [15]. Linking these targets to financing and supervisory mechanisms could strengthen accountability, though further research is needed to confirm effectiveness.

The findings underscore the need for upstream investment in diabetes prevention, detection, and control, which remains independently associated with in-hospital mortality and a leading cause of kidney failure globally [1]. Priority actions include expanding affordable access to essential diagnostic tests such as serum creatinine and urine protein assessment, embedding CKD and hypertension screening within diabetes and hypertension clinics, advancing equitable dialysis access, and developing explicit ethical frameworks for resource allocation to reduce bedside rationing and mitigate clinician moral distress in settings where dialysis availability is limited [2].

Future directions

Future studies should build on these findings using prospective or quasi-experimental designs to strengthen causal inference, particularly regarding the effect of dialysis on short-term mortality. In this setting, observational data may be leveraged to emulate a target trial by explicitly defining eligibility criteria, treatment strategies (dialysis vs. no dialysis), time zero at hospital admission, and appropriate follow-up windows, while accounting for time-varying confounding and immortal-time bias. Such an approach would allow more robust estimation of the real-world effectiveness of dialysis under conditions of constrained access and nonrandom treatment allocation. Target-trial emulation using routinely collected hospital data may represent a pragmatic and scalable methodological pathway for evaluating kidney replacement therapy in low-resource settings where randomized trials are infeasible.

Strengths and limitations

Key strengths include a consecutive, real-world cohort from the country’s main national referral hospital; standardized KDIGO staging based on recorded eGFR; prespecified modeling decisions that avoided collinearity; and comprehensive diagnostic evaluation of discrimination, calibration, and overall accuracy.

Limitations are inherent to the retrospective design: residual confounding (particularly for dialysis), measurement error in medical record abstraction, the study did not capture timing of dialysis initiation, precluding time-to-event or time-dependent analyses that could better address immortal-time bias, absence of granular dialysis process data (timing, modality, adequacy), reliance on dipstick rather than quantitative albuminuria, and lack of longitudinal outcomes beyond index hospitalization. The specific eGFR equation used by the laboratory was not captured, introducing potential measurement heterogeneity; different equations (CKD-EPI 2009, CKD-EPI 2021, MDRD) can yield systematically different values, particularly at higher GFR ranges, potentially affecting staging classification in a minority of patients near category boundaries. However, the majority of our cohort presented with G5 disease (eGFR <15), where equation-related differences are minimal. Misclassification of AKI-on-CKD as pure CKD is possible given the retrospective design; however, explicit documentation of chronicity markers was required for inclusion. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated imperfect calibration (p = 0.0066), suggesting that while the model discriminates well between high- and low-risk patients (AUC 0.79), the absolute predicted probabilities may deviate from observed outcomes, particularly at higher risk levels. This limits the model’s utility for precise individual risk quantification but does not undermine its value for risk stratification and triage purposes. Presenting symptoms were characterized descriptively but not evaluated as prognostic predictors; future prospective studies could examine whether specific symptom profiles add incremental predictive value beyond eGFR and proteinuria. Educational attainment was associated with dialysis receipt, suggesting unmeasured socioeconomic confounding that our model could not fully address. Selection bias is possible in a single-center tertiary hospital where severe cases concentrate; findings should not be interpreted as population incidence or mortality.

Conclusion

At Sierra Leone’s national referral hospital, adults hospitalized with CKD commonly presented with advanced kidney failure and heavy proteinuria, and approximately one in four died during the index admission. In multivariable analysis, lower eGFR, heavier proteinuria, and diabetes were independently associated with in-hospital mortality, whereas dialysis was associated with lower adjusted odds of death. Patients presenting with KDIGO G5 disease and ≥3+ dipstick proteinuria represent a readily identifiable, ultra–high-risk subgroup with markedly elevated short-term mortality. These thresholds, available at admission using routine testing, provide a pragmatic basis for bedside risk stratification, early nephrology review, and prioritization for dialysis evaluation in settings with constrained capacity. Aligning clinical decision-making with such operationally simple risk thresholds may support more transparent, equitable, and effective allocation of limited kidney replacement therapy resources. Prospective validation of threshold-based triage strategies is warranted.
