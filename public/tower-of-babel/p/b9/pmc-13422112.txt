Perineuromal hydrodissection for acute postamputation pain? An observational study in a time of war
Authors: Dmytro Dmytriiev, Winnie Liu, Maksym Barsa, Andreii Khomenko, Andreii Strokan, Paul F Pasquina, Steven P Cohen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis exploratory study investigates the potential of perineuromal hydrodissection as an adjunct to opioid therapy for postamputation pain, specifically focusing on residual limb and phantom limb pain (PLP). Given the correlations between acute pain and the development of chronic pain, the primary aims were to estimate the effect size of early hydrodissection of scar tissue around residual limb neuroma(s) and to identify the best time frame for treatment.MethodsSeventy-four patients with war-related limb amputations and painful neuromas were included in this observational analysis. Thirty-eight Ukrainian patients with war-related limb amputation and a painful neuroma(s) who underwent hydrodissection and opioid therapy within 6 months of amputation were compared with 36 patients who received opioids alone. Co-primary outcome measures were median reduction from baseline in average residual limb and PLP at 12 weeks. The composite positive outcome was designated as a ≥2-point decrease or 30% reduction in average residual limb and PLP, satisfaction with treatment, and not requiring an increase in analgesics.ResultsHydrodissection as an add-on to opioids resulted in a greater reduction in average residual limb pain at 12 weeks (−2.00±1.00 vs −1.00±1.00; p<0.001) and earlier time periods, but PLP only through 4 weeks. At 12 weeks, Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale anxiety (10.00±2.00 vs 11.00±1.00; p<0.001) but not depression score was lower in the hydrodissection group. Opioid use in the hydrodissection group significantly declined from 41.32±9.63 to 33.42±8.78 morphine equivalents per day (p=0.001) over the study, but not in the opioid-only group (p=0.20). Differences in 12-week satisfaction rates were not significant.ConclusionsThis exploratory study suggests perineuromal hydrodissection may improve residual limb pain and to a lesser degree phantom limb pain, particularly when implemented early in the course of postamputation pain. The study provides preliminary effect size estimates and identifies acute pain as a potential characteristic of patients who may respond more favorably to this intervention. Randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings and control for the confounding variables identified.Keywords: OUTCOMES; Opioids; Acute Pain; Analgesics, Opioid; Pain Management

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE, OR POLICY

Although the effectiveness of hydrodissection was limited to certain populations, the safety, low cost, and ease of use warrant further investigation. Introduction

Amputations are a signature injury of war. In a recent systematic review that included 31 studies (n=14 738), the authors estimated the pooled prevalence rates of phantom limb pain (PLP) and residual limb pain (RLP) to be 57% and 61%, respectively, among combat-injured service members.1 In addition to pain, amputation is associated with an increase in mortality and reduction in quality of life.2–4

There are no reliable treatments for postamputation pain (PAP), which stems from the fact that it represents a syndrome rather than disease, with a myriad of different mechanisms. There is significant overlap in PLP and RLP, indicating the likelihood of overlapping mechanisms and risk factors.5 These mechanisms include peripheral (eg, neuroma), spinal, and central etiologies (eg, central sensitization), with a presumed higher contribution of peripheral mechanisms influencing RLP.3 For PLP, a review found level 2 evidence for opioids, gabapentin, and direct current stimulation and level 3 evidence for anti-depressants, N-methyl-D-asparate receptor antagonists and peripheral nerve stimulation.3 There are no systematic reviews for RLP, but small randomized trials support opioids, intravenous lidocaine, and nerve stimulation, with anecdotal evidence in favor of targeted muscle re-innervation, perineural injections, and ablation.6 For persistent postsurgical pain in general, there is a correlation between severe acute postsurgical pain and chronification, with efforts to reduce chronic pain by mitigating acute pain yielding mixed results.7

One treatment that has not been previously studied for PAP is hydrodissection. Hydrodissection involves the injection of fluid into the tissue planes surrounding nerves. In addition to releasing entrapped nerves, hydrodissection may also suppress ectopic nerve discharges, exert local anti-inflammatory effects by decreasing cytokine levels, prevent or even wash out early heterotopic ossifications, block nociceptive signaling, and promote tissue healing. When dextrose solutions are added, it may reverse the hypoglycemic milieu around ischemic nerves that sensitize c-fibers.8–11 Randomized and uncontrolled trials examining hydrodissection support its efficacy for entrapment syndromes and cervical radiculopathy, with emerging evidence for other conditions.11–13

In the Ukraine-Russian war, it is estimated that over 100 000 Ukrainians have sustained war-related amputations. Unfortunately, there remains an absence of data on disease burden, treatments or outcomes.14 Prewar, Ukraine’s economy ranked 59th in the world, with less than 8% devoted to healthcare translating to per capita spending of less than $300 per person. This renders advanced treatments inaccessible to most war casualties, while therapies such as opioids carry risks in a population with high co-prevalence rates of psychopathology.15

To evaluate the effect of hydrodissection on acute PAP, we performed an observational study comparing hydrodissection and opioids to opioids alone. Our objectives were: (1) to determine the effectiveness of hydrodissection as an add-on therapy to opioids for phantom limb and residual limb pain; (2) determine whether hydrodissection reduces the incidence of persistent PAP; and (3) identify factors associated with treatment response.

Patients

Permission to conduct this observational study was granted by the Institutional Review Board at Vinnytsia National Pirogov Medical University (IRB_LEC_№5\13052022) and the Public Affairs Department at Walter Reed National Military Medical Center. On 13 May 2022, it was registered as a database designed to collect data on war-related chronic pain conditions with the central government committee (https://nddkr.ukrintei.ua/, №0122U002551). All patients received treatments that were already being provided as standard clinical care and provided informed written consent.

Seventy-four patients with PAP treated with either oral opioids alone or opioids and ultrasound-guided hydrodissection between 1 November 2023 and 1 April 2024 at Vinnitsa Regional Clinical Hospital or Prometheus Pain Treatment and Rehabilitation Center were evaluated. Analysis criteria were major limb amputation; treatment with opioids with or without hydrodissection; stable dose of opioid and other analgesics for >1 week; numerical rating scale pain score ≥4/10 for either RLP or PLP over the past week; duration of pain between 2 weeks and 6 months; and failure to respond to physical therapy. Excluded from analyses were those with systemic or local residual limb infection or an open wound that precluded a block and represented a treatable pain generator; patients missing 4-week follow-up data; previous procedure within 4 weeks of hydrodissection; pain in other location greater than RLP and PLP; and concomitant injection therapy received before 4-week follow-up.

Nerve hydrodissection technique

Painful neuromas were identified by history (eg, determining whether phantom pain occurred in a specific nerve distribution) and physical exam (eg, Tinel’s sign). Depending on the clinical presentation, either one or two end neuromas were identified using ultrasound guidance. These included the sciatic and femoral nerves proper and their main branches, the common peroneal, tibial, and saphenous nerves. After anesthetizing the skin with lidocaine 1%, a 21- or 22-gauge ultrasound needle was inserted in-plane. In cases where the end neuroma was obscured by scar tissue, an out-of-plane technique was employed. Once the needle tip appeared near the neuroma, a 5% dextrose in water solution was injected, with total volumes ranging between 5 and 40 mL depending on the number and diameter of targeted nerves and the extent of encasing scar tissue.

Initial opioid titration with immediate-release morphine or oxycodone was performed by an evaluating physician starting at 10 mg oral morphine equivalents every 6–12 hours as needed and titrating up or down at 3- to 7-day intervals based on patient response. There were no limits on opioid dose, and opioid stability was defined as no changes in opioid dose or of another analgesic for at least 1 week. No patient was started on adjuvants during the study period, but doses of previously prescribed adjuvants were titrated as clinically indicated.

Outcome measures

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics including location of amputation(s), average and worst PLP and RLP scores, Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) score, Somatic Symptom Scale-8 (SSS-8) V.2 score, analgesic medications, number of nerves and limbs targeted, and volume of injectate were recorded. For opioid dose, oral morphine equivalents per day were recorded based on equianalgesic conversion. In between the procedure and follow-ups, no additional procedures were performed, though patients could receive the non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug dexketoprofen as needed, titration of pre-treatment adjuvants, or additional physical therapy.

Follow-up evaluations occurred at the 48-hour, 1-week, 4-week, and 12-week time points. The coprimary outcome measures were median reduction from baseline in average RLP and PLP scores at the 12-week endpoint. Secondary outcome measures included change and absolute scores for RLP and PLP at earlier time points; change and absolute scores for HADS and SSS-8 at follow-up; medication reduction (eg, opioid dose and a categorical medication reduction outcome defined as >20% reduction in opioid consumption) at follow-up16; satisfaction with the procedure rated as highly satisfied, somewhat satisfied, or dissatisfied; and a binary outcome measure whereby success was defined as a ≥2-point or 30% improvement in average RLP and PLP score, being at least somewhat satisfied with the procedure’s results, and not requiring an increase in analgesic consumption (>20% increase in opioid dose or starting a new non-opioid analgesic).

Statistical analysis

Our sample size had sufficient power to detect a minimal clinically important difference of 1.0 point between the “opioid-only” and “hydrodissection + opioid” treatment groups based on the following assumptions: baseline average pain score of 5.0; a medium-to-large effect size of 0.7; (α) level of 0.05; and statistical power >80%.17–20

As no preliminary data were available on the use of hydrodissection + opioids for PAP, the effect size was estimated based on prior studies of hydrodissection suggesting a large effect. Based on these assumptions, we estimated that 37 patients in each treatment group were needed. After dropouts, we achieved final sample sizes of 36 patients in the “opioid-only” group and 38 patients in the “hydrodissection and opioid” group, providing 85% power. In subgroup analyses, patients were stratified by duration of pain into acute (1 month, 2 months, and 3 months) or chronic (>3 months).

All analyses were performed in RStudio 2024.04.01. χ² Fischer’s exact (if n<5) tests were applied for comparisons between hydrodissection+opioid versus opioid-only categorical outcomes. For continuous variables, Wilcoxon rank-sum was used. Frequency (%) and mean (SD) were reported for variables that met normality, while median (IQR) was reported for variables that did not per Shapiro-Wilk tests, Q-Q plots, and frequency histograms.

Results

Between 1 November 2023 and 1 April 2024, 92 patients with extremity amputations were screened. Fifteen patients were excluded for infection over the site (n=4), open wound (n=2), cognitive impairment (n=8), or refusal of participation (n=1), with another three excluded for failure to report 4-week outcomes. In sum, 38 patients received both hydrodissection+opioids, and 36 patients received opioids only (figure 1). These patients all completed 12-week follow-up.

Study flow chart.

Baseline characteristics differed for some variables between the two treatment groups (table 1). Patients receiving hydrodissection+opioids were younger than the opioid-only group (mean±SD age: 35.13±9.50 vs . 40.39±9.38 years old, p=0.02), had a shorter duration of pain (median (IQR): 2.00±1.88 vs . 3.75±3.00 months, p<0.01), and used less opioids at baseline (p<0.01). Patients receiving only opioids were more likely to be receiving antidepressants (86% vs 34%), gabapentin (53% vs 0%), and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (100% vs 89%) (p<0.001). Injuries in the hydrodissection + opioid group were more likely to result in unilateral amputations above the knee (63% vs 36%, p<0.01). There was no difference between treatment allocation and outcomes stratified by military status (data redacted).

Secondary outcome measures

At 48 hours post-treatment, the hydrodissection+opioid group had lower average RLP (4.00±2.75 vs 6.00±1.25, p<0.001) and worst RLP scores (5.00±2.00 vs 6.00±2.00, p<0.01). One-week post-treatment, the hydrodissection group had lower average RLP (3.00±2.00 vs 5.00 1.00, p<0.001) and worst RLP scores (5.00±1.00 vs 5.00±0.00, p<0.01) as well. At 4 weeks, average RLP was lower (3.00±3.00 vs 5.00±1.00, p<0.001), and reduction in average RLP score was greater (−2.00±2.00 vs −0.50±1.00, p<0.001) in the hydrodissection group. For RLP, the proportion of patients with a categorical positive outcome was higher in the hydrodissection group than the opoid-only group across all timepoints. There was no significant difference between groups at any timepoint for PLP (figure 2).

Aside from 12 weeks where the hydrodissection+opioid group had lower SSS-8 scores compared with the opioid-only group (12.00 (2.00) vs 13.50 (1.50), p=0.03), there were no significant differences in SSS-8 scores at other timepoints. HADS depression domain score was not significantly different between treatment groups at any timepoint except 4 weeks when patients receiving hydrodissection had lower depression subscale scores (8.00±1.00 vs 9.50±2.00, p<0.01). Patients in the hydrodissection group had lower HADS anxiety subscale scores at 4 weeks (10.00±3.00 vs 11.00±1.00, p<0.001) and 12 weeks (10.00±2.00 vs 11.00±1.00, p<0.001).

Patients who received opioids only started at a higher morphine equivalent dose than those who received hydrodissection (58.19±28.64 vs 41.32±9.63; p<0.01). The hydrodissection group reduced their opioid consumption at 12-week follow-up compared with baseline (41.32±9.63 to 33.42±17.50 morphine equivalents per day; p=0.001), while the opioid-only group did not. For satisfaction, 24% and 32% of patients who received opioids and hydrodissection were highly and somewhat satisfied with the treatment results at 12 weeks, respectively, versus 14% and 44% in the opioid-only group (p=0.44 for highly satisfied, p=0.37 for somewhat satisfied).

No patient in either group reported a complete absence of RLP or PLP at 12 weeks. Neither did any patient report a 12-week average RLP score <2, though one patient, in the opioid-only group, reported a 12-week average PLP score of <2/10.

Effect of pain acuity on main outcome measures

When patients were stratified into acute (<3-month duration) and chronic (>3 months) pain, differences emerged. The hydrodissection+opioid group patients with acute pain had lower 4-week (median 3.0 IQR 3.0 vs 5.0 (2.0); p=0.001) and 12-week (3.5 (2.0) vs 5.0 (0.0); p=0.001) average RLP pain scores than the opioid-only group, but the difference between groups among those with chronic pain did not reach statistical significance. For average PLP, the difference among those with acute pain was significant at 4 weeks (4.0 (1.0) vs 5.0 (2.0); p=0.04) but fell shy of significance at 12 weeks. In the chronic pain subgroup, this same pattern of significance at 4 weeks (4.5 (2.0) vs 5.0 (0.0); p=0.01) but not at 12 weeks held true (table 3).

Patients with acute pain were further stratified by duration of pain into 2–4-week, >4–8-week, and >8–12-week groups. In patients who were >8–12 weeks out from pain onset, the hydrodissection + opioid group had a greater difference in average RLP from baseline at 12 weeks (−2.0 (1.0) vs −0.5 (1.5), p<0.04). None of the outcomes were significant for PLP.

Among patients who were 2–4 weeks out since pain onset, those in the hydrodissection + opioid group had lower worst RLP scores at 4-week study follow-up than those in the opioid-only group (4.0 (1.0) vs 5.0 (0.5), p=0.048). All other outcomes were not statistically significant.

Adverse events

In the hydrodissection group, one patient developed what appeared to be new-onset phantom pain in his amputated leg, which resolved over several weeks. Three patients in the opioid-only group developed a diffuse pain phenotype and sensitivity over the stump, indicative of opioid-induced hyperalgesia versus possible complex regional pain syndrome.

Discussion

The main finding of this study was that hydrodissection was associated with a significant reduction in RLP and less influence on PLP across the study, though unequal baseline measures including age, duration of pain, and opioid dose limit the conclusions one can draw on effectiveness. For average RLP, this effect was only significant in amputees with acute pain.

Comparison to other studies

There are no studies evaluating hydrodissection for PAP, but clinical studies have reported effectiveness for neuropathic pain, though the addition of corticosteroid to a high-volume injectate in one double-blind study may have contributed to benefit.12 17 18 For acute pain, one randomized trial that added hydrodissection to local anesthetic and distilled water during an infraclavicular block reported less postoperative pain in the hydrodissction group.21 High postoperative pain is a strong predictor for persistent postsurgical pain, including after amputation.7 22 23

Explanation of findings

Our results are cautiously optimistic, with hydrodissection+opioids reducing RLP and to a lesser degree PLP scores compared with opioids only. For the coprimary outcome measures (average RLP and PLP at 12 weeks) and categorical outcomes stratified by acuity, patients in the hydrodissection group with a duration of pain ≤3 months experienced greater decreases in average RLP score than those in the opioid-only group and those with chronic pain. Disease burden in the opioid-only group was greater on many counts than that observed in the hydrodissection group. In the pain literature, measures of disease burden such as duration of pain, opioid consumption, and pain scores are associated with negative outcomes.7 24 In patients whose PAP was due to lingering postoperative pain, which likely occurred more often in the hydrodissection group, contributions from the natural course of healing may partially explain stronger responses.

Putative hydrodissection mechanisms

As noted above, there are numerous mechanisms by which hydrodissection may alleviate pain.8–10 Since PAP is a syndrome with disparate mechanisms, it is impossible to tease out which mechanisms, if any, were addressed by hydrodissection. However, the observation that positive outcomes were more frequent in RLP than for PLP, and that cortical mechanisms are more relevant for PLP than RLP, suggests peripheral mechanisms.3 It is also possible that the peripheral and systemic treatment of PAP in the forms of hydrodissection and opioid therapy, respectively, beneficially alter cortical reorganization, a likely mechanism for PLP.25 26

The contribution of a placebo effect cannot be discounted. The placebo effect is stronger for pain and psychiatric indications (which are highly coprevalent) than for more objective outcome measures, being at least as great as the intrinsic effect for most therapies.27 Moreover, this effect is larger for procedures such as hydrodissection than for medications.28 It is therefore probable that even an interventional treatment that lacked efficacy would be more effective than having no procedure in an uncontrolled study. In one systematic review evaluating long-standing perineural local anesthetic infusions for PAP, Laloo et al found a modest effect on postoperative pain and opioid requirements when all studies were evaluated, but the effect on opioid requirements was no longer significant when only randomized trials were included.29

Confounding effects of psychiatric variables, amputation burden, pain duration, opioid dose, and adjuvant use

As alluded to above, there were baseline differences in duration, opioid dose, adjuvant usage, amputation classification, and psychiatric variables that may have played a role in outcomes. In clinical studies, longer duration of pain is consistently associated with poor treatment results.7 24 Although one systematic review on PAP found only small differences in prevalence rates of RLP and PLP stratified by duration, the authors did not sub-analyze results for shorter durations of pain, and chronic pain tends to be stable after 6 months.1 An observational study comparing PLP prevalence in 85 amputees found the highest incidence at the earliest surveillance point at 6 months, declining slightly over the 3-year follow-up.30 A cross-sectional survey study found that 48% of 65 amputees reported that both RLP and PLP decreased over time.31 In our study, the lack of a significant effect for RLP in the chronic pain cohort may signify that the postinjury inflammatory phase returned to baseline. Other explanations include more intractable scar tissue less amenable to hydrodissection, smaller numbers, lower power to detect a difference, and collinearity with other variables accounted and unaccounted (greater attention and number of visits for newer patients) for.

There were higher baseline opioid doses in the opioid-only group. This may be because those with a longer duration of pain were more refractory to treatment or had more time to develop tolerance. Opioids have been shown to strongly predict the chronification of pain after iatrogenic and non-iatrogenic traumatic injuries, including during war, as well as procedural treatment failure.7 32 33 Reasons might include opioid hyperalgesia, conscious or subconscious secondary gain, non-pain-related use, and that higher opioid doses tend to be prescribed for those who have failed multiple other therapies, especially in veterans who engender a more tolerant approach than what guidelines recommend.7 34 Explanations as to why opioid use did not predict treatment results are that they also effectively reduced pain, anxiety, and sleep disturbances, the latter of which are associated with poor outcomes, and that not enough time elapsed before hyperalgesia and dependence developed.7 24

There were a higher proportion of hydrodissection patients who had unilateral lower extremity amputations compared with those in the opioid-only group. This might have accounted for part of the reason why hydrodissection was not used in the opioid-only group (ie, procedures are best utilized in those with more localized pain), but also could pose as a confounding variable to explain the poorer outcomes in the opioid-only group (ie, greater disease burden translates to poorer outcomes).35 36

Adjuvant use, particularly gabapentin usage which was 0% in the hydrodissection+opioid treatment group, was higher in the group that did not undergo hydrodissection. This may reflect the desire for non-hydrodissection patients to avoid procedures, or the reluctance of practitioners to trial multiple interventions simultaneously in an attempt to determine individual effectiveness. Some studies have found adjuvant use to predict poor interventional treatment outcomes, which may reflect therapeutic recalcitrance.37 Other studies suggest that gabapentin may work better for neuropathic pain phenotypes associated with central sensitization (dynamic mechanical hyperalgesia), which we did not measure, but is more likely to be associated with long-standing pain.38 Finally, we did not control for psychiatric comorbidities which are highly associated with pain outcomes.7 24

Limitations and future research

There are limitations to this study that warrant consideration. First, the design resulted in differences in baseline variables that could adversely affect outcome including the possibility of unmeasured variables such as catastrophization, coping skills, central sensitization, and opioid use disorder. Those in the hydrodissection group also were younger, which is independently associated with persistent postsurgical pain.7 Second, the placebo effect may have played an outsized role in the hydrodissection group, who probably had higher expectations. This is supported by the observation that pain itself decreased more in the treatment group than secondary outcome measures such as anxiety that tend to move in concert with pain. Recording objective outcome measures such as pain pressure thresholds, activity levels, or biomarkers in future studies may provide better information on outcomes and mechanisms. Third, hydrodissection is likely to work better for pain secondary to neuroma formation rather than central mechanisms. Whereas both RLP and PLP can be secondary to neuromas, treatments that address peripheral pathology, including inflammation and ectopic discharges, may be more likely to treat the former.39 Future studies should consider identifying painful neuromas via diagnostic blocks or adding local anesthetic and/or steroid to the injectate to reduce spontaneous neuroma firing and decrease the inflammatory milieu in early amputation. Finally, the treatments compared were not standardized, and some non-procedural co-interventions such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and physical therapies were permitted which could have confounded results.

Conclusions

In this study, hydrodissection added to opioids improved PAP, particularly RLP, compared with opioids only. However, this effect was primarily driven by better outcomes in those with acute pain who were disproportionately represented in the hydrodissection group. Although these results suggest hydrodissection might benefit a subpopulation with PAP, randomized trials that control for confounding variables are needed to demonstrate efficacy.
