Pulmonary vascular complications in children who survive long-term after Kasai portoenterostomy for biliary atresia: a narrative review
Authors: Jie Zhang, Gong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract Following Kasai portoenterostomy for biliary atresia (BA), long-term survival in children is associated with chronic liver cirrhosis leading to hepatopulmonary syndrome (HPS) and portopulmonary hypertension (PoPH). This further causes a diverse array of respiratory and circulatory symptoms. The incidence of these complications in children with BA is higher than that seen in other chronic liver diseases. With a faster progression, BA is life-threatening in some severe cases. Moreover, HPS and PoPH may coexist in the same child. Enhancing our understanding of HPS/PoPH, making an early diagnosis, rational selection of medication, and appropriate timing of liver transplantation underscore the need for multidisciplinary collaboration.Keywords: Jaundice, Pediatrics

Introduction

With widespread implementation of the Kasai portoenterostomy for biliary atresia (BA) in China, autologous liver survival rate is rising. Indeed, there are increasing numbers of BA children achieving jaundice-free long-term survival. However, a significant proportion of these children may go on to develop liver fibrosis-related pulmonary vascular complications, namely, hepatopulmonary syndrome (HPS) and portopulmonary hypertension (PoPH). The average age of onset of HPS is approximately 4 years old with an incidence in BA children of 3%–20%. The average age of onset of PoPH is around 15 years old, with an incidence of 1%–3%.1 Children with HPS or PoPH may present with the symptoms of long-term severe hypoxia such as dyspnea, chest tightness, or finger clubbing. These symptoms have failed to receive attention from both follow-up physicians and families. For those with severe symptoms, liver transplantation poses a high risk. It is therefore crucial to assess the occurrence of pulmonary vascular complications after Kasai portoenterostomy in BA patients with native liver survival during follow-up or consultation (figure 1).

Hepatopulmonary syndrome (HPS)

Definition and mechanisms

HPS is a condition characterized by hypoxemia and intrapulmonary vascular dilation induced by an increase in vasodilatory factors associated with chronic liver disease (CLD). The diameter of normal pulmonary capillaries is less than 10 µm. In contrast, in HPS patients this can increase to 100 µm. A considerable proportion of blood flow from the right ventricle into the pulmonary capillaries flows back into the left heart in the middle of the widened pulmonary capillaries without being well oxygenated. This forms a right-to-left shunt triggering the series of symptoms of hypoxia. Swanson et al. reported that the median survival time of children with HPS is only 24 months without liver transplantation, and the 5-year survival rate is just 23%.2 Anatomically, HPS can be divided into two types: type I, a diffuse lesion (functional shunt) with significant dilation of precapillaries and capillary beds, presenting with widespread spider-like, even diffuse sponge-like or nodular changes; type II, the formation of anatomical shunts due to the communication between pulmonary arteriovenous vessels.3 BA is the most common underlying disease causing HPS in children with metabolic liver diseases, acute liver failure, and liver tumors.

An increase in vasodilatory mediators plays a vital role in the development of HPS. The sources of these vasodilators after Kasai portoenterostomy in children with BA are complex. Endothelin-1 (ET-1) in the liver binds to endothelin receptors on the surface of pulmonary vascular endothelial cells, activating endothelial nitric oxide synthase, increasing local nitric oxide (NO) and activating cyclic guanosine monophosphate, resulting in vasodilation. When liver function is severely impaired, enterogenous and non-enterogenous pulmonary vasodilators such as glucagon, vasoactive intestinal peptide, 5-hydroxytryptamine, atrial natriuretic peptide, P substance, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) cannot be effectively metabolized by hepatocytes, resulting in a relative increase.4 In recent years, it has been found that translocation of intestinal bacteria leads to the aggregation of macrophages in the lungs. These produce TNF-α and stimulate an increase of inducible NO synthase.5 In addition, heme oxygenase-1 produced by macrophages can also promote the formation of a collateral circulation via new shunt vessels and increase pulmonary venous blood flow and left ventricular returned blood volume. Collectively, these contribute to a hyperdynamic circulatory state to worsen patient condition.6

Diagnosis of HPS

CLD and hypoxemia are the two key factors in the diagnosis of HPS. The European Respiratory Society has provided a strict definition of hypoxemia in HPS: at sea level and when breathing air, an alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient [P(A−a)O2=PAO2−PaO2] exceeding 15 mm Hg (1 mm Hg=0.133 kPa) or PaO2<70 mm Hg is defined as hypoxemia.

PAO2 = FiO2 × (atm — PH2O) — PaCO2/R

where, PAO2, alveolar oxygen partial pressure; FiO2, fractional inspired oxygen, FiO2=0.21 (indoors); atm, standard atmospheric pressure, atm=760 mm Hg; PH2O, water vapor pressure, PH2O=47 mm Hg (37℃); PaCO2, arterial carbon dioxide partial pressure; R, respiratory quotient, R at rest=0.8.7

The diagnostic cut-off value of P(A−a)O2 is not fixed at 15 mm Hg for children of different ages. Indeed, Snehavardhan et al.8 proposed a modification to the diagnostic cut-off value of P(A−a)O2 based on age: P(A−a)O2 cut-off value =10+(0.26×age in years−0.43). If the actual difference exceeds this cut-off value, hypoxia is considered to be present. According to this standard, the incidence of HPS is 87.5% in children with BA and persistent jaundice; this can reach 46.2% even in the absence of jaundice. The alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient P(A−a)O2 is relatively complex to calculate. However, this parameter comprehensively considers both oxygen (PaO2) and carbon dioxide partial pressures (PaCO2), thus avoiding the inaccurate appearance of PaO2 compensation due to hyperventilation. PaO2 is another important indicator in the diagnosis of hypoxemia. In clinical practice, HPS is usually classified into mild (PaO 2>80 mm Hg), moderate (PaO2 60–80 mm Hg), severe (PaO2 50–60 mm Hg), and extremely severe (PaO 2<50 mm Hg) for graded diagnosis and treatment.

Determination of both the alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient and the arterial oxygen partial pressure requires arterial puncture to obtain relevant data. Peripheral arterial puncture is invasive, and there are technical difficulties, especially in younger children. Research aimed to identify less invasive diagnostic methodologies is paramount. Oxygen saturation (SpO2), as a non-invasive parameter, has gradually attracted attention for its diagnostic value in HPS. Cut-off values of SpO2 for the diagnosis of HPS range from 92% to 97%.9 10 Govindan et al.11 reported that the sensitivity of SpO2 <97% in judging severe hypoxemia is 100%. Studies on liver transplant candidates and general screening subject controls from three centers in the USA showed that when SpO2 was lower than 96%, even in patients with an oxygen partial pressure lower than 60 mm Hg, the diagnostic sensitivity was only 71%; the negative predictive value was 0.82. This implies that only 18% of children with SpO 2>96% had HPS. Therefore, it can be posited that the accuracy of diagnosing HPS using a single SpO2 value is not high.12

Contrast-enhanced echocardiography (CEE) is an important auxiliary examination for HPS. When 9 mL of normal saline and 1 mL of air are respectively drawn using two 10 mL syringes connected to a three-way stopcock and mixed repeatedly, a large number of microbubbles with a diameter of about 10 µm are generated. Following injection of these microbubbles into children without atrial or ventricular septal defects or intrapulmonary shunts, they travel through the right ventricle and their passage is blocked by small intrapulmonary vessels. If microbubbles appear in the left atrium and ventricle within 3–10 cardiac cycles, intrapulmonary shunts are considered to exist. This relatively simple and accurate method can assist in determining the presence of intrapulmonary shunts and can distinguish the severity of HPS by assessing the number of bubbles (figure 2)

Using the diagnostic methods mentioned above, different strategies can be adopted for different populations: ordinary patients with hypoxia symptoms can be initially screened using SpO2. When SpO2 is <97%, arterial blood gas and CEE should be further examined. For children waiting for liver transplantation, regardless of their oxygen saturation, arterial oxygen partial pressure and CEE should be combined.12

The mechanism of 99mTc macroaggregated albumin (MAA) isotope lung perfusion imaging is similar to that of CEE. Labeled albumin is usually only taken up by and imaged in the lungs. If extrapulmonary imaging exceeds a certain proportion, it proves the existence of abnormal shunts. Uneven distribution of pulmonary vascularity, with more arteriovenous shunts at the lung base than that at the lung apex, makes hepatopulmonary syndrome more easily detected in the upright posture.8 This examination can serve as a supplement to arterial blood gas and CEE.

HPS-related factors in BA

Identifying the factors that render BA children susceptible to the development of HPS is a matter of great clinical concern. Using univariate and multivariate analyses, it has been shown that splenic malformation and interruption of the inferior vena cava are predisposing factors for HPS after Kasai portoenterostomy for BA. In contrast, age, transaminase, bilirubin, prothrombin time, and Pediatric End Stage Liver Disease Score do not correlate significantly with the incidence of HPS.13 Therefore, it can be hypothesized that there may be potential factors related to embryonic development in BA with HPS. The incidence of BA with HPS increases rapidly as follow-up time after Kasai portoenterostomy increases. Pandey et al.14 conducted a 2-year follow-up on groups of BA and non-BA CLD children over 6 months old. It was found that the median age at HPS diagnosis in the BA group and the non-BA groups was 14.4 and 90 months, respectively. The initial incidence of HPS in the BA group was 57.1%, while that in the non-BA group was 32.2%. In the BA group, there were 1–2 new HPS cases every 3 months. By the end of the observation period, the prevalence of HPS in the BA group had increased to 73.8%. Among patients with HPS, seven died and one underwent liver transplantation. There were no new cases in the CLD group during the follow-up period. It should be noted that the proportion of BA patients in this study with a diagnosis of HPS is relatively high. The authors used a combination of blood gas analysis and CEE for diagnosis; fractional exhaled NO was collected as a non-invasive index in some patients. However, important diagnostic parameters such as P(A-a)O2 were not reported. Discrepancies in diagnostic methodologies may account for the elevated diagnosis rate observed in this study.

Treatment of HPS

Drug therapies for BA with HPS include NO synthase inhibitors such as methylene blue and methyl-L-arginine, pentoxifylline (a TNF-αinhibitor) and antibiotics.15 16 However, drugs are usually used as adjuvants and are rarely used alone. Transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunts can effectively reduce portal vein pressure, decrease the release of vasodilators such as NO, improve pulmonary blood flow and ventilation/perfusion ratio, and relieve the severity of right-to-left shunt.17 Liver transplantation is a radical strategy. Most children with BA and HPS gradually experience a reversal in intrapulmonary shunts 1 year after liver transplantation; the results of CEE and isotope lung perfusion imaging improve accordingly. However, children with PaO2<40–50 mm Hg prior to liver transplantation have a significantly increased risk of complications and death post transplantation. Children with polycythemia before transplantation and those who require long-term oxygen support should be closely monitored in the intensive care unit for an extended period in the early stage after transplantation.18

Portopulmonary hypertension (PoPH)

Definition and mechanism

The pathophysiological mechanisms of PoPH in children with BA after Kasai portoenterostomy are driven by constriction of small pulmonary arteries, accompanied by vascular remodeling, intimal hyperplasia, medial hypertrophy, fibrosis, and in-situ thrombosis of the pulmonary artery.19 Endothelin (ET) is an important vasoconstrictor in disease development. ET enters the lungs from the portal vein, leading to a further increase in pulmonary vascular resistance. As PoPH progresses, vascular tone becomes disordered, and this is accompanied by visceral vasodilation and a decrease in total peripheral resistance, along with a drop in systemic blood pressure. A compensatory hyperdynamic circulatory state exists where the shear stress of high-speed blood flow damages the vascular intima, further aggravating pulmonary hypertension (figure 3).20 21

Diagnosis and treatment

BA with PoPH has an insidious onset, for which there may be a genetic susceptibility. In the initial stage, children with PoPH could be asymptomatic. However, with disease progression, dyspnea may occur; this is accompanied by fatigue, weakness, dizziness, orthopnea, or even aggravated exertional dyspnea. Arterial blood gas in children with PoPH can also show hypoxia and an increase in the arterial-alveolar oxygen gradient. In contrast to HPS, children with PoPH can show right ventricular hypertrophy, right atrial enlargement, and right axis deviation on the ECG. Echocardiography can infer an increase in right ventricular systolic pressure based on tricuspid regurgitation, but up to one-third of PoPH patients may be misdiagnosed when echocardiography alone is used. Right-heart catheterization is the gold standard for diagnosing PoPH. With respect to liver disease, when the pulmonary artery wedge pressure is normal with a mean pulmonary artery pressure >25 mm Hg, the pulmonary capillary wedge pressure is <15 mm Hg and the pulmonary vascular resistance is >3 wood units, PoPH can be diagnosed.1 An emphasis on no significant increase in pulmonary capillary wedge pressure is used to rule out a secondary pulmonary artery pressure increase caused by left-heart dysfunction. In addition, it is also essential to rule out other possible causes of pulmonary hypertension such as high-flow states, intravascular volume overload, diastolic dysfunction, obstructive/restrictive lung diseases, and sleep-disordered breathing during diagnosis.

Right-heart catheterization for pressure measurement is invasive and requires intervention. Echocardiography is often used to measure systolic pulmonary artery pressure (SPAP) to indirectly (and roughly) diagnose PoPH. SPAP <30 mm Hg can rule out PoPH, while SPAP >50 mm Hg is highly suggestive of PoPH. Right-heart catheterization for pressure measurement should be further performed at this time. The European Respiratory Society classifies PoPH into mild (25–35 mm Hg), moderate (35–45 mm Hg), and severe (greater than 45 mm Hg) based on mean pulmonary artery pressure to guide drug use and determine the timing of liver transplantation. In addition to echocardiography and right-ventricular catheter-based detection, plasma brain-type natriuretic peptide can be used as a non-invasive test in children over 5 years old. In this regard, with a cut-off value of 29.1 pg/mL, the diagnostic sensitivity is 100%, the specificity is 84.6%, the positive predictive value is 60% and the negative predictive value is 100%22 (figure 4).

Once PoPH is diagnosed, suitability for liver transplantation should be evaluated as early as possible. A range of vasoactive drugs is needed as a transition during the perioperative period. The primary drugs used are endothelin receptor antagonists, such as bosentan, ambrisentan, and macitentan. Phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors, such as sildenafil and tadalafil, are also suggested. In addition, long-term intravenous use of the prostacyclin analogue epoprostenol can also effectively reduce pulmonary vascular resistance. A combination of macitentan and sildenafil is often used to gradually reduce pulmonary artery pressure to <35 mm Hg before liver transplantation. After liver transplantation, two vasoactive drugs in combination are still needed for protracted periods to control pulmonary artery pressure. In the early postoperative period when oral medication is not possible, inhaled NO is an alternative for transition.23 Recovery time after liver transplantation for PoPH is surprisingly long. It has been reported that mean pulmonary artery pressure in some patients remains elevated even 2 years after surgery; the remission stage is about 5 years post surgery.24

Co-existence of HPS and PoPH

In children with BA, HPS and PoPH can change into each other or co-exist in the same patient. An interesting case has been reported by Umeda et al. of a girl with BA who underwent her first liver transplantation at the age of 11 due to HPS. A chronic rejection reaction occurred in the fourth post-operative year, and she was diagnosed with PoPH the following year. At the age of 16, the patient underwent a second liver transplantation.25 When HPS and PoPH occur simultaneously, PoPH leads to the activation of vascular endothelin A receptors causing pulmonary vasoconstriction, vascular remodeling, and an increase in vascular resistance. Conversely, HPS leads to an increase in the expression of vascular endothelin B receptors, causing pulmonary vasodilation and right-to-left shunts. Hemodynamically, the two conditions offset each other to a certain extent and may create an illusion of improvement in clinical symptoms and some monitoring parameters. Indeed, our hospital once admitted a child with severe PoPH, with an average pulmonary artery pressure >50 mm Hg and SpO22 less than 80% lasting 2 years. A few months before she was re-admitted, her oxygen saturation improved, but examination revealed HPS. Sadly, liver transplantation failed to save this child. Therefore, it is necessary to remain vigilant for the occurrence of combined HPS and PoPH. There is no clear sequence or pattern in the occurrence of HPS and PoPH in the same patient. A systematic review analyzed 11 cases of co-existing HPS and PoPH. Among them, eight cases had HPS first and then developed PoPH, one case was the opposite, and two cases occurred simultaneously.26

Conclusion

HPS and PoPH represent relatively severe pulmonary vascular disorders in children with BA after Kasai portoenterostomy. Impairments of gas exchange, along with alterations in circulatory dynamics, can give rise to serious consequences. Notably, in a small subset of patients, liver transplantation may prove ineffective as a life-saving procedure. The pathogenic mechanisms underlying differential susceptibility to HPS or PoPH in BA patients post-Kasai portoenterostomy are not fully elucidated, and the lack of reliable biomarkers for early risk stratification hinders timely intervention. Cohort studies are needed to identify genetic, epigenetic, or environmental factors that predispose BA patients to HPS/PoPH with the potential for targeted prevention. Development of non-invasive diagnostic tools, such as serum microRNAs or advanced imaging techniques, is necessary for early detection. From diagnostic invasiveness to age-adapted drug dosing and growth-related transplant risks, disease management demands tailored strategies. Pediatric surgeons and hepatologists should pay extra attention to oxygen saturation and examination of echocardiograms during the follow-up of these patients. For children suspected of these conditions, it is advisable to perform CEE or right-heart catheterization. Once the diagnosis of the two pulmonary vascular diseases is established, multiple disciplines, including cardiology, interventional radiology, pediatric surgery, and transplantation need to be engaged. This can facilitate the formulation of a rational plan encompassing liver transplantation and an appropriate medication regimen to minimize the occurrence of more severe clinical events. This integrated approach is crucial to optimize patient outcomes and reduce morbidity and mortality associated with HPS and PoPH in BA patients.
