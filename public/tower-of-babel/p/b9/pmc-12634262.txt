Computational pathology in precision oncology: Evolution from task-specific models to foundation models
Authors: Yuhao Wang, Yunjie Gu, Xueyuan Zhang, Baizhi Wang, Rundong Wang, Xiaolong Li, Yudong Liu, Fengmei Qu, Fei Ren, Rui Yan, S. Kevin Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the rapid development of artificial intelligence, computational pathology has been seamlessly integrated into the entire clinical workflow, which encompasses diagnosis, treatment, prognosis, and biomarker discovery. This integration has significantly enhanced clinical accuracy and efficiency while reducing the workload for clinicians. Traditionally, research in this field has depended on the collection and labeling of large datasets for specific tasks, followed by the development of task-specific computational pathology models. However, this approach is labor intensive and does not scale efficiently for open-set identification or rare diseases. Given the diversity of clinical tasks, training individual models from scratch to address the whole spectrum of clinical tasks in the pathology workflow is impractical, which highlights the urgent need to transition from task-specific models to foundation models (FMs). In recent years, pathological FMs have proliferated. These FMs can be classified into three categories, namely, pathology image FMs, pathology image–text FMs, and pathology image–gene FMs, each of which results in distinct functionalities and application scenarios. This review provides an overview of the latest research advancements in pathological FMs, with a particular emphasis on their applications in oncology. The key challenges and opportunities presented by pathological FMs in precision oncology are also explored.

Introduction

With the advancement of whole-slide imaging technologies, digital histopathology images have become increasingly accessible. Pathologists can now interact with these images on a computer screen in much the same way that they examine tissue samples under a microscope. Whole-slide images (WSIs) offer new insights into prevalent diseases, such as neurodegenerative conditions and cancers,[1] and enable preventive screenings for data-rich cancer types, including breast,[2] colorectal,[3] and prostate cancers.[4] The integration of computational techniques to interpret digital histology data may lead to the development of more accurate, practical, and scalable clinical decision-making tools, thus potentially improving therapeutic strategies. Typically, WSIs have extremely high resolution, with a single WSI image reaching 100,000 × 100,000 pixels. Additionally, tissue sections have a certain thickness, so scanning at different focal planes results in varied images. These characteristics result in a computing paradigm for pathological images that differs from natural image analysis approaches.

The development of artificial intelligence (AI) technology, particularly in computer vision and natural language processing, facilitates the analysis of heterogeneous multimodal data, including image, text, and sequence data. Deep learning (DL) has achieved significant success in health care.[5] Pathological images, which are more complex, also present unique opportunities for DL analysis. DL technologies have broad applications in digital pathology and provide automated clinical decision support in tasks such as nucleus detection and segmentation, disease diagnosis, gene mutation prediction, survival analysis, treatment response evaluation, gene expression prediction, and biomarker discovery. AI-based digital pathology techniques overcome the limitations of subjective visual assessments by integrating various detection methods to explore tissue structural complexity. Furthermore, combining histopathological features with radiology, genomics, and proteomics leads to more objective, multidimensional, and functionally relevant diagnostic reports, thereby advancing precision medicine.[6]

Task-specific computational pathology models typically require large, well-annotated datasets. However, the high costs associated with acquisition and labor, along with the relatively limited availability of task-specific datasets, have constrained the development of task-specific models for each application scenario. Additionally, the lack of diversity in the datasets used to train task-specific computational pathology models, which are often derived from one or a few centers, leads to a narrow applicability that is constrained to a single organization, thus resulting in limited functionality. With the advancement of AI technologies and improvements in data collection, foundation models have demonstrated significant advantages in the field of computational pathology. Foundation models are highly versatile and can be applied across various tasks because of their pretraining on large-scale, diverse data.[7] They offer strong generalization ability, which makes them suitable for multitask or exploratory applications. Recent foundation models in health care are large-scale, pretrained AI models designed to understand and process complex medical data across diverse tasks.[8,9] After training on vast datasets that include medical images, clinical notes, and biomedical literature, these foundation models can perform a range of functions, from diagnosing diseases to predicting patient outcomes and assisting in drug discovery. Their ability to generalize knowledge across multiple domains enables them to be fine-tuned for specific health care tasks, thus improving accuracy and efficiency in medical decision-making while reducing the need for extensive task-specific training. In this review, we discuss the evolution of computational pathology from task-specific models to foundation models in precision oncology and highlight their key applications [Figure 1], recent methodological advances, major challenges, and future opportunities.

Task-Specific Models in Computational Pathology

Existing task-specific computational pathology models typically require large-scale clinical data and expert annotations. Through extensive data annotation, DL models assimilate sufficient expert knowledge from human experts, thus enabling effective model training. Despite the high data annotation costs and considerable data collection challenges, task-specific computational pathology models generally possess the flexibility to address real-world clinical problems effectively. This paper discusses task-specific computational pathology models across five areas: subtype prediction, biomarker prediction, survival analysis, treatment response prediction, and other applications.

Subtype prediction

AI-based diagnosis[10–13] and grading[4,14] at the WSI level are among the most extensive research areas in computational pathology. Pathologists generally agree that both WSIs and their specific regions must be examined for comprehensive decision-making in pathological image analysis. In recent years, several studies have explored the use of AI to classify cytopathology images. However, the small number of cytological samples and the absence of tissue structural features present significant challenges in identifying the pathological type of cancer. AI systems have shown potential in detecting and grading prostate cancer in biopsy samples, with performance comparable to that of international experts. This could reduce workload, automate cancer measurements, provide second opinions, and offer expert-level support in regions where such expertise is scarce. For example, AI-assisted Gleason grading has shown significantly improved accuracy compared with both unassisted pathologists and standalone AI, which underscores the advantages of combining AI with pathologist expertise.[14] A deep convolutional neural network developed by Coudray et al[10] accurately classifies lung cancer subtypes and predicts gene mutations from histopathology slides. This model achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.97, demonstrating performance comparable to that of pathologists. The tumor origin assessment by DL (TOAD) algorithm identifies the origins of tumors from histology slides and achieves high accuracy in differentiating primary from metastatic tumors.[15] This provides a valuable tool for diagnosing cancers of unknown primary (CUP) origin, thereby potentially reducing the need for extensive ancillary tests. Additionally, a study developed a deep convolutional neural network for gastric cancer histopathological diagnosis that achieved nearly 100% sensitivity and 80.6% specificity.[16] By testing on a large dataset from multiple sources, this system has the potential to assist pathologists by improving diagnostic accuracy and to address the shortage of pathologists. Besides, it performs consistently across medical centers, demonstrating its feasibility for routine clinical use. Another study used AI-driven histopathological image analysis to distinguish between p53abn and NSMP endometrial cancer subtypes, and a distinct ‘p53abn-like NSMP’ group with worse survival outcomes was identified.[17] Through the use of discovery and validation cohorts, this study demonstrated the potential of AI to identify prognostically significant subgroups within endometrial cancer that may be missed by traditional methods.

Biomarker prediction

Molecular changes are fundamental for determining the specific growth patterns of tumor tissues and influencing the composition of surrounding tumor microenvironment. As a result, a strong intrinsic relationship exists between molecular alterations and histological morphology. Extensive researches has demonstrated that WSIs can be used to predict individual gene alterations, microsatellite instability, and gene expression.[18–21] This research has also linked genetic variations with morphological patterns (i.e., genotype–phenotype correlations), which aids in predicting patient prognosis. As targeted therapies have been increasingly applied in clinical settings, determining whether a mutated tumor is sensitive to these therapies or whether the tumor expresses molecular markers has become critical for selecting appropriate treatment regimens. However, the testing methods for these markers are often complex, time-consuming, and costly. Consequently, the prediction of molecular markers using pathological images has emerged as a significant research focus. HE2RNA can predict transcriptome profiles from histology slides, thus providing virtual spatialization of gene expression and transferable predictions for molecular phenotypes.[22] Deep residual learning models can predict microsatellite instability directly from hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained images, thereby potentially broadening access to immunotherapy for gastrointestinal cancer patients by eliminating the need for additional genetic or immunohistochemical tests.[23] Using a dataset of 30,612 gene expression profiles from breast cancer patients, the method proposed by He et al[2] can predict local gene expression from H&E-stained images with high spatial resolution. Additionally, Diao et al[24] introduced a novel approach that enhances the interpretability of computational pathology by using human-interpretable image features (HIFs) to predict molecular phenotypes from WSIs. An analysis of H&E-stained images from 28 cancer types, along with genomic, transcriptomic, and survival data, revealed that computational pathology can accurately distinguish tumors from normal tissue and correlate pathological features with genetic aberrations and gene expression.[25] DPCT predicts the expression levels of various genes on the basis of H&E-stained images.[26] By analyzing differences in the expression of genes related to inflammatory activation, cell proliferation, and cell differentiation pathways, lung adenocarcinoma and lung squamous cell carcinoma can be categorized into phenotypes.

The successful integration of DL into clinical practice for knowledge discovery is exemplified by the identification of biomarkers across various cancer types and patterns.[27] PathFinder,[28] which is an interpretable human-centered DL-guided framework, was proposed to assist pathologists in discovering novel tissue biomarkers. By combining sparse multiclass tissue spatial distribution information from WSIs with attribution methods, PathFinder enables the localization, feature description, and validation of potential biomarkers while ensuring state-of-the-art prognostic performance. In the case of hepatocellular carcinoma, this framework revealed a long-overlooked factor—the spatial distribution of necrosis correlates strongly with patient prognosis. As a result, two clinically independent metrics were proposed: necrosis area fraction and tumor necrosis distribution. These metrics were validated as practical prognostic tools and were shown to meet the recommended standards for tumor biomarker prognostic studies. However, currently known biomarkers rarely exhibit sufficient robustness to provide true analytical or clinical value. While computational pathology based on DL can serve as a prediction strategy for survival, its limited interpretability and generalizability remain barriers to its widespread clinical application.

Survival analysis

The biological characteristics of tumor cells are the primary determinants of patient prognosis, with high tumor heterogeneity often correlated with poor outcomes. DL methods are particularly well suited for extracting features related to tumor heterogeneity.[25,29] For example, Kleppe et al[3] created a clinical decision support system that combines the DoMore-v1-CRC marker with pathological staging to enhance risk stratification in patients with stages II and III colorectal cancer, outperforming existing methods. This approach accurately identifies patients with a good prognosis who might avoid unnecessary adjuvant chemotherapy, thereby potentially reducing treatment-related morbidity, mortality, and costs. Similarly, Skrede et al[30] developed a DL-based prognostic biomarker for early-stage colorectal cancer by analyzing over 12 million image tiles from H&E-stained sections. Ten convolutional neural networks were trained to classify image tiles and integrate the findings to predict patient outcomes. The biomarker, validated across cohorts, outperformed traditional markers in identifying low- and high-risk patients. Compared with unimodal survival analysis, multimodal methods have shown improved prediction accuracy. For example, Chen et al[31] employed multimodal DL to integrate pathology whole-slide images and molecular profiles from 14 cancer types, aiming to develop joint prognostic models and identify key features associated with patient outcomes. Consequently, morphological and molecular correlationship are now available in an interactive open-access database, facilitating further exploration and biomarker discovery. A study optimized HoVerNet[32] for tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) detection,[33] demonstrating that the model significantly outperformed other TIL detection models and that its results surpassed even those obtained from CD8 staining on tissue microarrays (TMAs). By predicting the expression levels of various molecular markers, they generated multiplex molecular expression maps for each WSI and used entropy to calculate a tumor heterogeneity index. This index was used to classify each WSI into high-heterogeneity and low-heterogeneity groups, and further revealed that patients with high tumor heterogeneity had significantly lower survival rates than those with low tumor heterogeneity.

Therapeutic response prediction

In addition to tasks such as risk stratification and prognosis prediction, computational pathology models have also been applied in the field of treatment response prediction in recent years. For example, Zhang et al[34] utilized DL to analyze H&E-stained histopathological images of small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and identified 50 histomorphological phenotype clusters (HPCs) as potential pathomic features. Two of these HPCs were found to have significant prognostic value. By integrating these HPCs into a pathomics signature (PathoSig) using Cox regression, this study demonstrated that PathoSig can effectively stratify patients based on the overall survival and disease-free survival. This study also identifies patients who may benefit from chemoradiotherapy and provides prognostic information that extend beyond those provided by traditional TNM staging and molecular subtyping methodologies. Validation across independent multicenter cohorts highlights PathoSig’s potential as a valuable tool for personalized treatment planning in SCLC. Another study predicted genome-wide tumor mRNA expression from H&E-stained slides and predicted response to therapies based on the inferred expression, achieving high prediction accuracy across multiple cancer types and treatments without direct training on treatment data.[35] Computational pathology models can effectively predict treatment responses, which highlights their broad potential applications in treatment planning and strategy development.

Other applications

AI-based pathological slide analysis tools have been widely integrated into pathological analysis workflows. These models typically do not make clinical decisions but assist in tasks such as object detection and semantic segmentation. This subsection introduces commonly used clinical pathology analysis tools, including cell segmentation[11,32,36,37] and nucleus counting on pathological slides, which aid in accurate diagnoses. For example, histopathological cancer diagnosis often relies on observing structural abnormalities. In cases of highly differentiated cancers that closely resemble normal tissues, detailed observation is necessary. Semantic segmentation helps identify nuclei, stroma, and glands, thereby enabling precise cancer diagnosis. Similarly, segmentation techniques can extract features from the region of interest (ROI) while excluding irrelevant image parts, thus accelerating model training. NuClick,[37] which is a convolution neural network (CNN)-based approach, efficiently generates precise annotations for nuclei, cells, and glands in histology and cytology images with minimal user interaction; it demonstrates strong performance across various object scales and domains and achieved the top rank in the LYON19 challenge.[38] Another challenge in pathological image analysis is staining variability across hospitals, which affects the accuracy of DL models. Virtual staining, powered by DL, offers a sustainable, rapid, and cost-effective alternative to traditional histological staining by generating stained images from unstained tissue. However, it is still in the early development phase and requires further validation. de Haan et al[39] proposed a new method that transforms H&E staining of WSIs into virtual immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining based on cytokeratin, thereby significantly reducing the uncertainties associated with IHC slides. Wong et al[40] used hyperspectral fluorescence microscopy and AI to generate high-quality virtual H&E and prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia-4 stains from unstained prostate tissue.

The absence of certain modalities poses a significant challenge in multimodal learning. Denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) and other generative approaches provide effective means for mitigating this limitation. For example, in RNA-CDM,[41] the authors developed a model that consists of two main components. First, a β-diffusion autoencoder architecture was trained using RNA data from the The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset, which contains the expression levels of 17,655 genes. This training produced a pretrained encoder that compressed the RNA data from 17,655 to 200 dimensions. Two cascaded DDPMs were subsequently trained using the compressed RNA data and WSIs as inputs. A well-trained model can start from random noise and generate pathology slide images guided entirely by RNA data. By inputting different RNA profiles, the model can generate pathology slide images that correspond to various cancer types. Additionally, Ding et al[42] developed a large-scale virtual clinical image dataset named “Synthetic Nuclei and Annotation Wizard (SNOW)”, which provides both synthetic images and annotated nuclei labels. The SNOW dataset was created by using existing image generation and nuclear annotation models and includes a total of 20,000 virtual image patches with 1,448,522 annotated nuclei. The workflow for generating this virtual dataset involved a synthetic image generator (SIG) and a nuclei annotator (NA) that were designed to produce virtual pathology images of breast cancer. The SIG, trained on a limited real pathology dataset such as BreCaHAD,[43] is capable of generating large numbers of virtual pathology image patches. Moreover, the NA can generate nuclei annotations for these virtual images without requiring manual correction. This approach offers an efficient and reproducible method for creating paired virtual pathology image samples and nuclei annotations, thus significantly expanding the data available for nuclei analysis in pathology images without increasing the manual annotation workload.

Foundation Models in Computational Pathology

The training of models from scratch faces significant limitations, which are due primarily to the difficulties in collecting expert annotations and large histological datasets for specific diseases. To overcome these challenges, researchers have increasingly turned to transfer learning techniques in computational pathology,[29,44–48] which has proven effective in tasks such as tumor detection, mutation prediction, cancer grading, and survival prediction. Foundation models[7] are general-purpose AI models trained by self-supervised learning[49,50] on large and diverse datasets, which enables them to be transferred to a wide range of downstream tasks. This represents a significant shift from the traditional approach of designing AI models specifically tailored for individual tasks.

Recently, foundation models have gained considerable attention in health care, particularly within oncology, where cancer research often necessitates the integration of multimodal data, such as pathology slides,[51] medical records, lab tests, and spatial transcriptomics (ST).[8] The capabilities of foundation models are driven by the expansion of datasets, scaling of model sizes, and advancements in DL methods. Unlike task-specific models, foundation models are typically trained on large datasets from multiple centers, which gives them superior generalization capabilities. This robustness enables them to be flexibly deployed in various clinical workflows, which is a feature known as zero-shot/few-shot inference. In other cases, these models only require minimal additional data for further fine-tuning to achieve satisfactory clinical performance. In summary, foundation models provide a new paradigm for addressing a wide range of clinical tasks. Pathology image foundation models

Initially, pathological foundation models were typically trained on datasets of single-modality images. Datasets such as TCGA,[51] which contains approximately 29,000 formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE), and fresh frozen (FF) H&E whole-slide images of 32 cancer types, serve as foundational resources for many self-supervised models. Coupled with other public and private pathological datasets, prior research has made significant strides in learning meaningful representations of histological structures for clinical pathology tasks. For example, UNI is a universal self-supervised model for computational pathology that has been trained on over 100,000 diagnostic WSIs across 20 major tissue types.[45] UNI is trained using the self-supervised learning algorithm DINO-V2. This algorithm enables the model to learn embeddings by analyzing both global and local regions of tissue sections, which can subsequently be aggregated across slides for downstream tasks. UNI performs well across a range of tasks, including common cancer type classification and rare cancer type identification. In weakly supervised classification tasks, UNI demonstrated remarkable performance, with a top-5 accuracy of 93.8% and an AUC of 0.976 in classifying 43 cancer types using data from Brigham and Women’s Hospital (BWH). UNI represents a pioneering approach in the development of patch-level pathological foundation models. However, since the ultimate goal is to perform WSI-level tasks, WSI-level foundation models are needed.

Subsequently, GigaPath[52] effectively achieved both patch-level and WSI-level representations through a two-phase training process and the LongNet[42] method for ultralong sequence modeling. GigaPath,[52] which was pretrained on over 1.3 billion pathology image tiles (256 × 256) from a large U.S. health network, which covered 31 major tissue types and included 171,189 WSIs from more than 30,000 patients. By employing autoregressive pretraining using masked autoencoders, GigaPath facilitates the reconstruction of certain image patches, thereby achieving effective representation at the WSI level. This approach enables the model to demonstrate superior performance in downstream tasks. GigaPath achieved the best performance in nine cancer subtype classification tasks, with AUC values of 0.9 or higher for six cancers, namely, breast, kidney, liver, brain, ovarian, and central nervous system cancers. The model also excelled in biomarker prediction tasks from WSIs and significantly outperformed competing methods in 17 out of 18 tasks.

Moreover, various studies have further improved performance by increasing the training dataset size. Paige introduced Virchow,[47] which is a large-scale foundational model in pathology that was trained on approximately 1.5 million H&E-stained WSIs from approximately 100,000 patients at the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC). The model achieved an impressive AUC of 0.95 across nine common and seven rare cancers. Notably, Virchow embeddings outperformed other models on untrained tissue data, including those from the cervix, testis, and head and neck. This pancancer detection model demonstrated strong generalization ability. The model’s success was particularly evident in the classification of rare cancer variants, where it surpassed several production-grade, tissue-specific clinical models despite the use of significantly less training data. Moreover, Virchow embeddings excelled in predicting the status of biomarkers within samples. Although single-modality pathology image foundation models have achieved remarkable results, foundation models pretrained on multimodal data have demonstrated broader clinical applications. By integrating text and genomic data, these multimodal approaches have further advanced the development of foundation models in computational pathology.

Pathology image–text foundation models

Building on a vast dataset that consists of 208,414 pathological images along with their corresponding textual descriptions, Huang et al[44] developed the Pathology Language–Image Pretraining (PLIP) model based on a contrastive learning framework (CLIP). Unlike traditional pathology models, PLIP integrates natural language annotations, which enables it to comprehend image semantics and perform various downstream tasks. Experiments demonstrate that PLIP outperforms the baseline CLIP model across all evaluation datasets, particularly in identifying key tissue categories such as fat, background, and colorectal cancer epithelium. The study indicates that PLIP can adapt to new medical texts through zero-shot learning and can serve as a versatile pathology image encoder that enhances representation capabilities for various tissue types. Additionally, PLIP offers flexible pathology image search functionality, thus assisting clinicians and pathology trainees in education and information sharing.

Moreover, several studies have enhanced foundation model training by curating higher-quality datasets. CONCH was used to construct a multimodal dataset by collecting more precise medical literature.[44] It automatically cleaned certain educational resources (EDUs) and data from PubMed Central Open Access to generate a dataset of 1,786,362 image–text pairs. The model’s visual-language pretraining setup includes an image encoder, a text encoder, and a multimodal text decoder, which are jointly trained with contrastive and captioning objectives. The contrastive objective aligns encoders by maximizing the cosine similarity between image and text embeddings, whereas the captioning objective enhances the likelihood of generating correct texts on the basis of images. CONCH excels in various cancer-related image classification tasks and particularly outperforms state-of-the-art models in zero-shot and few-shot learning scenarios. In zero-shot classification, it achieved accuracies of 90.7% and 90.2% for TCGA-non-small cell lung cancer and TCGA-renal cell carcinoma, respectively, thus outperforming PLIP. For the TCGA breast invasive carcinoma task, CONCH reached a zero-shot accuracy of 91.3%, which was clearly ahead of those of other models. The study highlights CONCH’s potential in few-shot supervised learning. CONCH achieved outstanding classification performance through weak supervision with the ABMIL algorithm and linear probing, with average accuracies of 86.7%, 94.2%, and 93.3% in BRCA, RCC, and NSCLC image classification, respectively.

Some approaches do not employ a contrastive learning pretraining paradigm; instead, they use the text encoder solely as an auxiliary input to facilitate pretraining on various types of pathological datasets. For example, CHIEF offers a versatile weakly supervised machine learning framework that significantly enhances the analysis of histopathological images.[29] Unlike traditional models optimized for specific tasks, CHIEF has broad applicability across various diagnostic fields, with excellent performance in cancer detection and prognostic analysis.

In addition to diagnostic classification tasks, various foundational models have been designed to streamline clinical services for medical professionals and even patients, including the generation of diagnostic reports and the resolution of associated issues. PathChat is a multifunctional visual-language AI assistant specifically designed for pathology.[46] The authors combined a visual encoder for pathology with a pretrained large language model, and subsequently fine-tuned the system with over 456,000 visual-language instructions. PathChat can process both visual and natural language inputs and can assist medical students and junior pathologists in enhancing their diagnostic skills and bridging health care gaps. When evaluated using the diagnostic task benchmark PathQABench, PathChat achieved an accuracy of 89.5%, outperforming leading AI models such as LLaVA[53] and GPT-4V. The results of expert evaluations indicate that PathChat has significant advantages in pathology-related tasks that require detailed examination and accuracy, thus establishing it as an efficient medical diagnostic tool that can enhance the clinical decision-making process.

Pathology image–gene foundation model

Recent studies have shown that multimodal biological data (e.g., transcriptomics + pathology) can be used to achieve effective self-supervised contrastive learning by bimodal pretraining, even without manual annotations. Inspired by vision-language models, Jin et al[57] introduced a gene-induced multimodal pretraining (GiMP) framework for image–omics classification. GiMP pioneers unsupervised multimodal learning in biology through its dual-module architecture, which integrates a multihead self-attention gene encoder with masked patch modeling to extract and fuse high-order features effectively from WSIs. TANGLE[55] employs contrastive learning to integrate WSI and expression data and demonstrates that expression-guided representation yields more discriminative features than does slide augmentation or masking alone. TANGLE also excels in few-shot slide-level classification and demonstrates strong retrieval capabilities.

Additional endeavors have been dedicated to the development of a unified model for multiple tasks to serve as a foundation model. Tu et al[59] incorporated unimodal biomedical AI into a multimodal system and addressed 14 clinical tasks across language, imaging, and genomics via the Multi-MedBench evaluation framework. Built upon the robust foundation of the pathway language model (PaLM), vision transformer (ViT), and PaLME, Med-PaLM enables comprehensive multimodal context prediction with a solitary prompt. It achieves state-of-the-art performance across all multi-MedBench tasks and often outperforms specialized models by wide margins. Current limitations include restricted clinical applicability and an overemphasis on image–text pairing at the expense of molecular data integration. This is due mainly to the limited accessibility of paired images and molecular datasets. To address this, Xu et al[48] developed mSTAR, which is a novel pretraining framework that leverages 11,765 TCGA WSIs across 32 cancers to advance pathology modeling from unimodal/patch-level to multimodal/slide-level analysis. mSTAR leverages UNI-encoded image patches and a pretrained slide aggregator to generate slide representations aligned with bulk RNA-sequence data, thereby significantly boosting performance in diagnostic classification, survival prediction, and pathology report generation tasks.

Spatially resolved transcriptomics (ST) enables subcellular molecular profiling with spatial coordinates that naturally align with pathology images and offers unique advantages over bulk/scRNA-seq for spatial-domain analysis. However, high costs and limited datasets have prevented the development of ST-specific foundation models. These methods yield an integrated embedding from both the image and the sequence text (image–ST fused embedding) that captures both morphological and transcriptional profiles simultaneously. One notable approach is Multimodal Structured Embedding (MUSE),[58] which adopts a multiview autoencoder neural architecture augmented with both self-reconstruction and self-supervision losses, the latter derived from pseudolabels generated by clustering methods. The fused embeddings outperformed single-modality approaches in identifying tumor biomarkers, tissue subpopulations, and spatial heterogeneity across multiple ST platforms, and demonstrated both generalizability and novel biological discovery potential. Several studies have approached modality fusion through contrastive learning between image and transcriptome data.[60,61] These methods bridge gene expression and histopathology through robust low-dimensional embedding techniques (CNNs, GNNs, and MLPs) and generate unified representations while emphasizing distinct learning objectives. Gui et al[61] focused on domain-specific knowledge awareness. ConST[62] leverages expressive and interpretable model capabilities, and CongGL[60] employs multiple contrastive losses to enhance learning across and within modalities. These methods achieve state-of-the-art performance in spatial domain identification, thus demonstrating the transformative potential of image-ST foundation models. Their minimal human intervention requirement enables seamless extension to spatial proteomics and other molecular modalities, which will enable enhanced tissue heterogeneity analysis and clinical applications.

Conclusions and Prospects

Foundation models leverage self-supervised learning algorithms, which enables them to generate high-quality data representations (embeddings) without the need for meticulously labeled datasets. These embeddings can be generalized well across various downstream tasks, which makes them highly applicable in the field of computational pathology. In clinical applications, foundation models significantly increase the efficiency and accuracy of pathological analysis and reduce the reliance on specialized and costly techniques such as IHC and genomic testing. This not only accelerates diagnostic processes but also lowers health care costs and improves patient treatment experiences. In summary, the introduction and application of foundation models are paving new frontiers in computational pathology and precision oncology, thus highlighting the enormous potential and promising future of foundation models in the medical field. With ongoing technological innovation and exploration, computational pathology is poised to bring more groundbreaking advances to clinical practice in the future.

Real-world performance evaluation

Foundation models in computational pathology frequently lack validation with real-world datasets. Consequently, employing larger, more diverse, multicenter, and prospective datasets is essential for ensuring the performance and generalizability of these models. Moreover, training models on datasets that reflect historical health disparities and aim to represent underrepresented groups (considering factors such as race, gender, and socioeconomic background) poses a risk of introducing discriminatory selection bias. Before these models can be fully integrated into routine clinical practice, external validation, prospective and multicenter clinical trials, and assessments of the correlation between model accuracy and real clinical efficacy are essential.

Interpretability

DL models are typically highly complex, as are foundation models. Our understanding of their mechanisms remains limited. Consequently, enhancing the interpretability of these models and improving their credibility are critical issues in the field of computational pathology, with significant implications for the clinical adoption of these models. Interpretability is also a key factor that influences the translation of foundation models into clinical practice. Therefore, exploration of the biological interpretability of DL models will further deepen our understanding of model predictions and accelerate their clinical translation.

Multimodal information mining

Pathology images reflect the histological characteristics of tumors, whereas radiological features and genomics provide anatomical and molecular-level insights. The integration of these different modalities can offer a more comprehensive representation of tumor characteristics. While different modalities provide complementary information, they may also contain overlapping data, thus leading to redundancy during integration. Moreover, decision-level integration may fail to capture the interactions between modalities. The key challenge in current research on pathology foundational models is to effectively model multimodal information to jointly represent the biological characteristics of tumors, thereby assisting clinicians in making precise diagnostic and therapeutic decisions.

Long sequence modeling

The current foundation models collectively demonstrate the efficacy of harnessing multiple modalities, which not only enhances the performance of tasks within each available modality but also expands the capabilities for tasks from a single modality to those of other modalities and even to tasks beyond the involved modalities. However, the scalability of current models remains constrained by the limitations of representation learning for long sequences, even though mSTAR[48] successfully uses a contrastive pretrained aggregator to bridge this gap. Moreover, alternative architectures for managing long sequences in both omics and WSI data, such as LongNet[42] and Mamba[63], are emerging.

In conclusion, foundation models have demonstrated tremendous potential in transforming computational pathology by offering scalable, label-efficient, and high-performing solutions across diverse clinical tasks. Nevertheless, their widespread clinical deployment hinges on addressing several critical challenges. These include ensuring robust generalization through real-world and diverse datasets, enhancing model interpretability for greater transparency and clinical trust, and advancing the integration of multimodal data to fully capture the biological complexity of disease. Furthermore, innovations in long-sequence modeling architectures will be pivotal in enabling foundation models to process high-resolution WSIs and complex omics data more effectively. As research in these directions continues to mature, foundation models are poised to play a central role in the evolution of precision medicine, bridging the gap between computational advances and clinical impact.
