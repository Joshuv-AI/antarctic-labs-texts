Effectiveness and Feasibility of Interventions for Weight Management of Young Adults in Low‐ and Middle‐Income Countries (LMICs): A Systematic Review With Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Sujata Shakya, Subas Neupane, Shanti Bajracharya, Pilvikki Absetz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionOverweight and obesity rates are rising globally, including among young adults, posing significant health risks. However, prevention programs in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) are limited, and little is known about their applicability in these settings. This systematic review aimed to assess the effectiveness and feasibility of overweight and obesity prevention and management interventions among young adults in LMICs.MethodsDatabases, including Medline, Scopus, and Web of Science, were systematically searched to identify trials published between 2014 and February 2026. Studies were included if they targeted young adults in LMICs and reported outcomes such as body mass index (BMI) and/or waist circumference. Publications reporting feasibility outcomes were manually searched. A random‐effects meta‐analysis estimated the mean difference in outcomes by gender and intervention duration. A narrative synthesis complemented the random‐effects meta‐analysis. The Cochrane risk‐of‐bias tool and Grades of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework evaluated the evidence quality.ResultsSixteen intervention studies met inclusion criteria, but none reported on feasibility. Nine studies were included in the meta‐analysis on effectiveness. Pooled estimates show that mean BMI (kg/m2) (−1.13; 95% CI: −2.10, −0.17) and waist circumference (cm) (−1.66; 95% CI: −2.35, −0.96) were significantly lower in the intervention group compared to the controls. Greater effects were observed in longer durations of interventions (≥ 6 months). The quality of evidence was moderate.ConclusionObesity control interventions may benefit young adults in LMICs, but evidence is limited and moderate in quality. Future studies should evaluate feasibility using established implementation frameworks.

Introduction

The prevalence of overweight and obesity has increased significantly worldwide, posing a significant global health burden and contributing to what has been described as a “global epidemic” [1, 2, 3]. If current trends persist, it is projected that by 2035, over 4 billion people worldwide will be affected [3]. Over the past decade, obesity rates have surged significantly, especially in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) [4, 5, 6]. South Asia has experienced the highest burden and the most rapid increase in overweight and obesity compared to other regions [7, 8, 9]. Notably, nine out of the top 10 countries expected to see the most significant increase in obesity rates among both adults and children are LMICs, and all located in Asia or Africa [3]. An expert committee convened by the World Health Organization (WHO) considered this rising trend an impending worldwide threat and an escalating issue in public health [10].

Obesity, once regarded as the problem of middle‐aged adults, is now common among adolescents and young adults too [11]. Young adulthood is a critical stage of life accompanied by significant lifestyle changes which will continue even later [7]. Evidence shows the rapid increasing trends of overweight and obesity among young adults [7]. Rapid urbanization and high‐calorie food intake combined with a sedentary lifestyle, especially among the young adult group, pose various health issues, posing consequences during the early stage [6].

According to WHO, the overweight and obesity prevalence in Nepal in 2014 was 16.7% and 2.9%, respectively [5]. Different studies in Nepal showed a high prevalence of overweight and obesity among the young adult group [12, 13, 14]. The occurrence of overweight among young adult women (18–29 years) exhibited a consistent uptrend from 1996 (1.5%) to 2019 (14.6%). Likewise, during this timeframe, the prevalence of obesity also showed a steady rise to 3.3% in 2019 [15]. Overweight and obesity are the major contributors to various non‐communicable diseases (NCDs) that are the leading causes of preventable premature deaths [6, 16, 17, 18]. Obesity in young adults is often associated with the presence of metabolic syndrome, asymptomatic cardiovascular disease, impaired respiratory functions, and infections such as COVID‐19 [7, 19, 20, 21]. The increasing prevalence of overweight and obesity among young adults in Nepal underscores the urgent need to design and implement prevention strategies that are feasible within the constraints of a low‐income country such as Nepal.

Effective prevention of overweight and obesity necessitates an integrated approach that strengthens and accelerates preventive programs including early screening for obesity, its potential risk factors, and comorbidities [10]. There are country‐based national obesity prevention guidelines in high‐income countries like Australia, Canada, and many European countries [22]. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) of the United States has endorsed the State‐based Nutrition and Physical Activity Program to Prevent Chronic Diseases and Obesity, which guides program managers, policymakers, and others in planning appropriate strategies [23, 24]. Earlier research suggests that the intervention packages for specific risk factors for NCDs can be implemented effectively and economically in LMICs [25, 26]. Obesity control interventions are less targeted toward the young adult group in LMICs [7]. For example, while Nepal—a lower‐middle‐income country—has emphasized reducing NCD‐related preventable morbidity, avoidable disability, and premature mortality, under a multisectoral action plan in which one of the targets is to halt the rise in obesity [27], the governmental policies and strategies in the country are primarily concerned with the control of undernutrition and target‐oriented actions. Therefore, earlier scoping review recommended reinforcement of governmental efforts to control the obesity epidemic in Nepal through target‐oriented actions [22]. Drawing on the demonstrated success of similar interventions in other LMICs, effective and contextually feasible prevention strategies can be designed and implemented in Nepal. There are numerous studies assessing the effectiveness and feasibility of obesity control interventions among children, adolescents, and adults above 18 years in high‐income countries. However, there is limited knowledge on the effectiveness and feasibility of the intervention measures, specifically among young adults, in LMICs taking into account the socio‐cultural and other contextual factors. Therefore, evaluating the effectiveness and feasibility of obesity control interventions in LMICs is crucial to bridging existing evidence gaps and to inform the design of context‐appropriate strategies for low‐income settings such as Nepal.

A synthesis of existing evidence on overweight and obesity control interventions targeting young adults in LMICs is needed to inform evidence‐based policy development. This will aid in designing an effective framework for intervention planning. This study had a double aim: (i) To systematically identify and synthesize the available evidence on the effectiveness of interventions in reducing overweight and obesity among young adults in LMICs and (ii) to assess the feasibility of the interventions in real‐world settings with the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance (RE‐AIM) framework [28].

Materials and Methods

This review is set to combine two complementary parts, one for effectiveness with meta‐analysis and the other for feasibility with a narrative analysis. The review protocol of this study was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024524158). The study adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines (Figure 1) [29] for reporting the information.

PRISMA flow diagram of the systematic review process of finding relevant studies.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

We included studies (1) conducted among young adults (preferably among 18–29 years), (2) conducted in LMICs defined by the World Bank [30], (3) randomized or non‐randomized trials with a control group including trials registered, (4) evaluating any type of behavior change interventions aiming at preventing/managing overweight or obesity, and (5) assessing BMI and/or waist circumference as an outcome. We excluded studies that test different treatment regimens or anti‐obesity drugs, and all those that did not fall under the above inclusion criteria.

Literature Search

A comprehensive literature search was performed using electronic databases, including Medline (PubMed), Cumulative Index to Nursing & Allied Health (CINAHL Complete EBSCO), Scopus (Elsevier), Web of Science, and Joanna Briggs Library (JBI Complete Ovid), to find relevant studies. The search strategy using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) combined using Boolean operators such as “OR,” “AND,” or “NOT” was used (sample strategy in Table S1). The search was restricted to publications in the English language from the past 10 years (since 2014 until February 2026) to capture contemporary relevance of the intervention. We also manually searched for publications on feasibility outcomes from the studies identified for effectiveness evaluation. All the studies retrieved through the database search were exported into Rayyan (Rayyan‐QCRI 2016) software in which the duplicate records were removed.

Screening and Data Extraction

The titles and abstracts were independently screened for eligibility by two reviewers (S.S. and S.B.), and the studies not fulfilling the inclusion criteria were excluded. Discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (S.N.). The full texts of the included articles were further screened for eligibility.

The data extraction checklist was developed and revised after discussion and consensus between the reviewers and coauthors. The checklist was pretested on a sub‐sample (10%) of the selected studies to ensure consistency. The first reviewer (S.S.) independently performed data extraction, and 20% of which were cross‐checked by the second reviewer (S.B.) and agreed through discussion and consensus.

The data extraction items included authors, country, year of publication, study design, study population, interventions, reported outcome, and results. The data extraction table is presented in Table S2. The outcomes for the effectiveness were BMI and waist circumference, which are the indicators of overweight and obesity. Most of the studies measured BMI as the outcome (the ratio of weight [kg] and square of height [m2]), and in some studies, waist circumference (cm) was measured as the indicator of overweight/obesity.

The corresponding authors were contacted via email to request additional data if sufficient information was not provided in their articles. If no response was received within 2 weeks, follow‐up emails were sent twice at 2‐week intervals. Of the 11 authors contacted by email, seven replied with additional data, while one responded stating that they no longer had data available for their study.

Outcomes for feasibility assessment were defined based on the relevant constructs of the RE‐AIM framework [28, 31]. Reach on the target population, with indicators such as number, proportion, and representativeness of participants; Adoption, as the proportion and representativeness of the settings that deliver the intervention, or the number of eligible and invited sites; Implementation with indicators such as acceptability, fidelity, and enabling factors and barriers to implementation; and Maintenance, referring to the extent to which the intervention becomes institutionalized or sustained, with high levels of reach, adoption, effectiveness, and implementation over time [32].

Assessment of Risk of Bias

Assessment of risk of bias was performed for all the 16 studies included in the review. The revised Cochrane risk‐of‐bias tool for randomized trials [33, 34] was used to determine the risk of bias in randomized trials. It includes five domains: (i) bias due to randomization, (ii) bias due to deviations from the intended interventions, (iii) missing outcome data, (iv) bias in the measurement of the outcome, and (v) bias in the selection of the reported result. Each was rated either a high, low, or unclear risk [33]. We adapted the Risk Of Bias In Non‐randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS‐I) tool for non‐randomized studies, which covers seven domains [34], during the pre‐intervention stage (bias due to confounding and bias in the selection of participants), intervention stage (bias in the classification of interventions), and post‐intervention (deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported result). Risk of bias assessment was performed independently by the first reviewer (S.S.) and cross‐checked by the second reviewer (S.N.). All differences in opinion between the reviewers were settled through discussion and mutual agreement.

Data Analysis

Studies were included in the meta‐analysis if they reported the mean, standard deviation (S.D.), or standard error (S.E.), and sample size for both intervention and control groups during the final follow‐up, or if they provided the change in mean and SD from baseline to follow‐up. A narrative synthesis on effectiveness was conducted for the remaining studies. The overall effect size was reported as the pooled estimates (difference between intervention and control) and the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) by using random‐effects models. Chi‐square test was used to find out heterogeneity (0%–100%) among studies showing low (25%), moderate (50%), or high (75%) heterogeneity at p < 0.05 [35]. The analysis was stratified by duration of intervention and sex when there were sufficient studies. For meta‐analysis, we used Cochrane's Review Manager (RevMan Version 8.7.0).

Our initial aim was to conduct a narrative synthesis of the feasibility findings based on the RE‐AIM framework [28]. However, due to inadequate reporting on feasibility, this analysis could not be carried out.

Assessment of Quality of Evidence

We used the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) rating system to analyze the quality or certainty of the evidence for the studies considered for meta‐analysis. The “Summary of findings” tables were generated to summarize the outcomes. The GRADE rating is based on eight GRADE criteria (risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias, large magnitude of effect, dose–response, and effect of plausible confounding factors) [36]. The rating started from “HIGH” to “VERY LOW” (downgraded) in case of RCTs, whereas from “LOW” to “HIGH” (upgraded) in case of non‐randomized evidence. We prepared a summary table for the quality of evidence based on the Cochrane [37].

Results

Our database searches yielded 71,765 results. After removing duplicates and screening of abstracts and full‐text, 16 articles reporting on 16 unique interventions were included in the final review. The detailed screening procedure is presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1). Nine studies [38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46] were included in the meta‐analysis which includes two RCTs [42, 44]. The remaining seven studies were included in the narrative synthesis of effectiveness. All the 16 identified articles reported exclusively on effectiveness. The additional manual search identified no reports on feasibility from the 16 unique interventions.

Characteristics of Included Studies

The characteristics of the 16 studies that were included for effectiveness evaluation covering a total of about 14,526 young adults with overweight or obesity are presented in Table S2. Six studies were conducted among women [39, 42, 43, 46, 47, 48]. Seven studies were conducted in Asia [38, 39, 42, 44, 45, 49, 50, 51], six in the Middle East [40, 43, 47, 48, 52, 53], and two in Africa [41, 46]. The study population includes individuals aged 19–25 years in one study [38], adults aged 45 years and below in three studies [39, 43, 44, 46], and adults aged 18 years and above in the remaining studies. Data specifically for young adults (18–29 years) were obtained from five studies [39, 40, 41, 42, 45].

Among the 16 studies, five involved educational interventions [38, 41, 43, 49, 53], two focused on diet therapy [42, 50], three on counseling and motivation [45, 47, 48], three on multiple interventional activities [39, 40, 52], and three on the use of the Smart Care app or social media [44, 46, 51]. Five studies implemented health facility‐based interventions [41, 43, 48, 51, 53]. All studies included usual care control groups, except for two that compared two different intervention groups [44, 48]. Six studies assessed only BMI as an outcome [39, 43, 45, 46, 47, 52], while the remaining assessed both BMI and waist circumference. Nine studies had longer interventions for 6 months or more [40, 41, 43, 44, 45, 46, 48, 49, 52].

Nine out of 16 studies were included in the meta‐analysis. Among these, four studies were conducted only among women [39, 42, 43, 46], and one was conducted among men [44]. Three studies included different types of educational interventions [41, 43, 44], two included multiple interventions [39, 40], and one each included exercise [38], diet therapy [42], motivation and counseling [45], and social media use [46].

Assessment of Risk of Bias

The risk of bias assessment was performed for 16 studies that were included in the review and is summarized in Figure 2a,b. Out of seven RCTs, six [42, 44, 47, 48, 50, 53] had some concerns of risk of bias in two to four domains, whereas one study [51] had high concern in one domain. Bias due to deviations from the intended interventions (the investigators were aware of the interventions received by the participants) and bias due to missing outcome data (losses to follow‐up) were common in most of the studies. Similarly, most of the non‐randomized studies were assessed as having low to moderate risk of bias, except one [41], which had high risk of bias due to missing data (losses to follow‐up). Studies exhibited bias due to confounding [38, 40] (some participants claimed a probability of controlling their dietary intake once they had initiated the exercise intervention), deviations from interventions [41, 45, 46, 49, 52] (because interventions could not be completed for all as a result of losses to follow‐up), and missing outcome data (due to drop‐outs) [41, 45, 46, 49, 52]. One study had high risk of bias due to missing data and large drop‐outs [41].

Meta‐Analysis

The findings of the quantitative synthesis of nine studies are presented in the forest plot (Figures 3 and 4). The pooled estimate of the mean difference of BMI after the interventions was significantly favoring the intervention group (mean difference in BMI −0.87 [95% CI: −1.57, −0.18]) kg/m2 than the controls. Three individual studies [39, 43, 45] showed BMI significantly lower in the intervention group with high heterogeneity between studies (I 2 = 90%) (Figure 3). The pooled estimate of the mean difference of the waist circumference (cm) in the intervention group showed lower BMI compared to the control group (−1.66; 95% CI: −2.35, −0.96) with no heterogeneity between studies (Figure 4).

Stratified Analysis by Duration of Interventions and Sex

The pooled estimate of the mean difference of BMI showed only a small difference (favoring intervention) between interventions and the controls for the studies with short‐term intervention (< 6 months), while the magnitude of the difference for the studies with longer (≥ 6 months) interventions was higher (pooled mean difference −2.13; 95% CI: −2.97, −1.28 vs. −0.70; 95% CI: −1.91, 0.51) and statistically significant (Figure 5), whereas for waist circumference outcome, the studies with longer interventions showed significantly lower waist circumference in the intervention group during post‐test (pooled mean changes −2.13; 95% CI: −2.97, −1.28) with no heterogeneity between studies (Figure 6).

Forest plot of the standard mean difference between the intervention and control groups with their 95% CIs for “BMI” outcome by the duration of intervention.

Forest plot of the standard mean difference between the intervention and control groups with their 95% CI for “waist circumference” outcome by duration of intervention.

While the estimates were not statistically significant, we found a reduction in the mean BMI (pooled mean change −1.39; 95% CI: −2.77, −0.00) and mean waist circumference (mean change −2.49; 95% CI: −5.47, 0.49) among females (Figures S1 and S2). While smaller differences in BMI (mean change 0.19; 95% CI: −0.06, 0.45) and waist circumference (mean change −0.54; 95% CI: −1.37, 0.30) were found among males (Figures S3 and S4). Studies with longer duration interventions (≥ 6 months) among females had a reduction in mean BMI after intervention (mean change −1.78; 95% CI: −3.50, −0.06) (Figure S5). Similar significant results were also found for waist circumference (mean change −3.88; 95% CI: −5.20, −2.56) (Figure S6).

Quality of Evidence

The GRADE assessment of the studies included in the meta‐analysis indicated a moderate quality of evidence of the effectiveness of intervention based on two randomized trials, meaning that it is likely that additional research will have an important impact on the effect estimate or its precision. Similarly, the non‐randomized trials also showed moderate quality of evidence for BMI and waist circumference outcomes (Table 1).

Narrative Synthesis of Effectiveness Evaluation

Seven studies [47, 48, 49, 50, 51, 52, 53] for which no data was available exclusively for the young adult group were excluded from the meta‐analysis and assessed for narrative synthesis. Except for two studies [49, 52], all were RCTs, with one being a randomized parallel‐group trial involving two intervention groups [47]. In all studies with control groups, the interventions had a significant effect on obesity outcomes (BMI or waist circumference). The effect size ranges from 0.70–1.40 for BMI and 6.10–8.00 for waist circumference. Two studies supported the effectiveness of mobile phone interventions [44, 51] and three supported the effectiveness of health facility‐based interventions [48, 51, 53]. One was a pragmatic study on lifestyle interventions in community and family settings [52]. These findings indicate that obesity prevention interventions can be effective in controlling overweight and obesity among adults.

Discussion

We found 16 studies that reported on the effectiveness of interventions to reduce overweight and obesity among young adults in LMICs. Despite our manual search, we found none of these interventions reporting on feasibility. The evidence suggested a positive intervention effect as measured by BMI and waist circumference. However, the quality of the evidence was only moderate. The interventions used in these studies were mainly education, exercise therapy, dietary modification, motivational counseling, and multiple interventional therapy.

We found a significant difference in BMI and waist circumference in the intervention group compared to the control group, with heterogeneity between studies, indicating greater diversity of the interventions and their impact on obesity prevalence. This finding aligns with an earlier systematic review which detected a significant and heterogeneous effect of intervention on waist circumference [54]. Similarly, other reviews also showed a positive effect of the obesity prevention and management interventions on obesity outcomes [55, 56]. One study in our review found a significant difference among the intervention group in waist circumference, but not in BMI [40].

Our analysis provided only limited evidence to support different modes of intervention delivery. Five health facility‐based studies [41, 43, 48, 51, 53] on weight management included in this review demonstrated a positive effect on obesity outcomes. In line with these findings, other reviews in LMICs have also concluded that weight management interventions delivered in health care settings are effective in controlling obesity [54, 56]. This suggests that healthcare facilities could serve as effective platforms for implementing obesity control programs, particularly by engaging health professionals.

Two RCTs included in our narrative review [44, 51] and a quasi‐experimental study included in meta‐analysis [46] revealed that the use of mobile phone applications and social media can be effective ways to control obesity. This is consistent with findings from two systematic reviews that demonstrated the effectiveness of e‐ & mHealth interventions promoting physical activity, healthy diets, and weight loss in LMICs [57, 58]. Therefore, utilizing mobile applications and e‐technology presents a viable approach to managing overweight and obesity, as mobile phones have been increasingly popular and accessible in the modern era, also in LMICs [59].

Evidence from high‐income countries has also shown that interventions aimed at reducing or preventing obesity among adults can be effectively implemented. A systematic review conducted among Australian adults reported that only a limited number of weight‐loss dietary and supportive interventions demonstrated meaningful effects on weight [60]. Similarly, a systematic review among immigrant populations in the United States found that community‐based interventions multi‐component programs incorporating nutrition, physical activity, and education were effective in improving obesity‐related outcomes [61]. Another review from developed countries showed that behavioral weight management interventions delivered in primary care settings were effective [54]. This findings align with our review, which demonstrated the effectiveness of multi‐component [39, 41, 47, 50] and community‐based interventions [47, 50, 52] in reducing obesity. Mobile app‐based health interventions have also been shown to promote health‐enhancing behaviors among adults [59], consistent with the results of our study. Furthermore, a systematic review that included studies from high‐income countries as well as several from LMICs found that 9 out of 30 interventions were effective in reducing obesity, primarily those using digital technologies or delivered through health care settings [62]. Overall, the evidence suggests that no single type of intervention is consistently more effective than others in reducing weight in high‐income countries, which is consistent with our findings from low‐income settings. While such multi‐modal interventions may be more expensive and less sustainable in resource‐constrained LMICs, obesity control strategies implemented at the primary or community level through the use of digital technologies could represent more appropriate and feasible options.

Our review showed that interventions lasting 6 months or longer were more effective than shorter ones (< 6 months) in lowering waist circumference. A similar pattern was observed in female‐specific analyses when stratified by duration of interventions. However, these subgroup findings based on a few studies can only be the basis for future studies.

Possible barriers or challenges to implementing obesity control programs in low‐income settings, from the implementer or organizational perspectives, could not be assessed from this review. However, barriers from the recipient's perspectives included personal, family, and social factors, as well as the perception that dietary modifications were ineffective or unaffordable [63]. This suggests that appropriate and feasible interventions should be planned after a careful assessment of the societal challenges and through active engagement of the target population.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

This is the first systematic review to evaluate effectiveness of overweight and obesity prevention and control interventions targeting young adults across different types of settings in low‐ and middle‐income countries. Earlier reviews predominantly focused on the effectiveness of obesity prevention interventions among children and adolescents [64, 65] or among adults aged 18 years and older [60, 66, 67]. The majority of these reviews were from high‐income countries. The only review of studies from low‐ and middle‐income countries focused on school settings [64]. Consequently, evidence specific to the young adult population, where various behavioral risk factors are prevalent, was lacking [6, 7]. Additionally, a meta‐analysis was done to estimate pooled effects and their statistical significance, enhancing the robustness of the study.

However, in this study, firstly, the studies were limited, especially those assessing the feasibility components. Age groups were not specified in all studies except two studies [39, 45], which makes it difficult to draw any firm conclusion, particularly for young adults. Two studies included in the meta‐analysis also include adults aged 30 and above (up to 45) [43, 44]. All outcomes measured in the studies lack extended follow‐ups, which created difficulty in assessing the long‐term impact of interventions. In addition, most of the subgroup analyses were performed based on only two studies per group due to the limited availability of relevant studies. Consequently, the results of these subgroup analyses cannot be considered as contributing substantive evidence to the overall findings of the review. Rather, they should be interpreted cautiously and regarded primarily as exploratory, serving to generate hypotheses for future research. We set out to evaluate intervention feasibility with the RE‐AIM framework; however, no studies reported on both effectiveness and feasibility of obesity prevention and management. Furthermore, none had published separate secondary analyses on feasibility outcomes, making robust evaluation impossible to fully assess the applicability of obesity control interventions. Therefore, the feasibility evaluation could not be performed in this study. The studies did not report anything about the cost of the interventions, which could serve as an indirect indication of their feasibility. One study identified the hidden cost associated with a weight management mobile application as a reason for discontinuing its use [63]. Furthermore, two other studies assessing the impact of sugar‐sweetened beverage tax policies found that, although some monetary costs were incurred due to tax imposition, these were much lower compared to the government revenue generated and therefore considered to contribute to reducing national health care costs [68, 69]. The studies included in this review did not specifically examine social determinants or their role in interventions despite the fact that these increasingly influence obesity. We believe this may be because such factors are difficult to modify and to demonstrate effective outcomes. Nevertheless, these social determinants could play various roles in the interventions, such as policy‐level interventions targeting social determinants. Two studies in our review have examined the effect of taxation and regulation policies on behavior change and obesity [68, 69].

Conclusion

This systematic review indicated that obesity control interventions can be effective in reducing overweight and obesity; however, the overall quality of evidence was only moderate. The use of mobile phone technology might be a promising and cost‐effective approach for low‐income countries such as Nepal, offering potential to improve dietary and physical activity behaviors and thereby reducing overweight and obesity among young adults. Our findings signify a lack of high‐quality effectiveness and implementation research that would identify successful and feasible obesity prevention and management strategies for young adults having a sustainable impact. This highlights the need for continuous and sustained intervention efforts to achieve significant and lasting impacts on obesity outcomes. Given the limited availability of studies and those focusing specifically on young adults, further research is recommended to evaluate the feasibility of effective interventions, especially in resource‐constrained settings. Further research tailored to LMIC contexts, including Nepal, is needed to better understand how social determinants influence obesity and the effectiveness of its prevention strategies. This research could also serve as a foundation for developing a national strategy to address overweight and obesity among youth in Nepal and other LMICs.
