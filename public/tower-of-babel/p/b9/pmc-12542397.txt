Lack of evidence for viral etiology in symptomatic chronic kidney disease of unknown etiology (CKDu) in Sri Lanka
Authors: Nilantha Pushpakumara, Paul Freund, Shakila Premarathne, Pasan Hewavitharane, Naduni Erandika, Dulanjali Herath, Thilini Weerakoon, Sulochana Wijethunga, Neelakanthi Ratnatunga, Abdul Wazil, Christopher M Skopnik, Diana Metzke, Julia Melchert, Tobias Bleicker, Kerstin Amman, Christoph Daniel, Victor M Corman, Terry C Jones, Sabrina Weiss, Kai-Uwe Eckardt, Philipp Enghard, Nishantha Nanayakkara

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic kidney disease of unknown etiology (CKDu) is a major health problem in Sri Lanka. A transient symptomatic episode, characterized by fever, dysuria, and arthralgias, accompanied by kidney dysfunction, has been reported in endemic areas of CKDu. Sym-CKDu has shown with histological features consistent with acute interstitial nephritis and is considered a potential initiating event of CKDu. Among various potential etiologies, viral infections such as Hantavirus, Cytomegalovirus (CMV), and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) have been implicated in the causation of CKDu.MethodsHere, we performed a prospective observational cohort study of symptomatic patients presenting with at least three of symptoms - i.e. arthralgia, myalgia, fever, back pain or feeling unwell an outpatient clinic in a CKDu-endemic region of Sri Lanka. Background

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is characterized by a reduced GFR, increased albuminuria, or other signs of kidney damage lasting more than 3 months duration, irrespective of the etiology. While CKD can frequently be attributed to a defined cause such as diabetes, glomerulonephritis, infection, vascular disease or genetic disorders [1], the etiology of chronic kidney disease of unknown etiology (CKDu) remains unclear. A high prevalence of CKDu has been reported in farming communities in tropical countries worldwide, including Sri Lanka [2]. In Sri Lanka, CKDu is primarily identified in and around north-central region of the country [3].

CKDu has been an emerging cause of mortality during the past two decades among people with low socio-economic status in the dry zone of Sri Lanka. The disease is mainly considered to be asymptomatic until stages with severely impaired GFR are reached. However, recently a transient Sym-CKDu with histological features of acute interstitial nephritis has been reported [4]. Common symptoms associated with Sym-CKDu include fever, dysuria and arthralgias [4, 5]. Kidney biopsy studies in symptomatic patients have demonstrated early tubulointerstitial changes and active inflammation [5, 6]. These findings led to the hypothesis of a sequel of events: an agent yet to be identified initiates acute tubulointerstitial injury, which leads to irreversible lesions and subsequently progresses towards kidney failure. Symptoms during the acute stage are nonspecific; hence this transient illness could be camouflaged among other nonspecific febrile illnesses or even attributed to strenuous work in a hot, humid climate.

Presently, among the possible etiologies mainly environmental toxins and/or heat stress with repetitive dehydration are primarily discussed and investigated [2, 7]. Conversely, several reports also point towards an infectious cause, in particular a viral infection [8–10]. However, such a viral etiology has never been proven, nor has it been formally ruled out. The presentation with symptoms such as fever, arthralgia and myalgias seems suggestive of an infectious cause, but both toxins and heat stress may cause similar symptoms. Most of these insults could also induce acute histological responses similar to the lesions described in Sym-CKDu, as well as chronic interstitial nephritis. Arguably, analyzing CKDu at an earlier stage could provide a better chance of identifying the injurious agent.

Here we performed an observational prospective cohort study of symptomatic patients with a feverish syndrome presenting in an outpatient clinic in a CKDu endemic region of Sri Lanka. We report eGFR trajectories in this cohort, a detailed description of patient characteristics of individuals losing eGFR over time and the absence of any evidence supporting a viral etiology.

Methods

This cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted in Girandurukotte, which is a sentinel area in CKDu surveillance in Sri Lanka [4]. Ethical approval was obtained by the ethical review committee of the National Hospital Kandy, Sri Lanka. Patient recruitment and initial sample collection was performed for one year and four months starting from June 2018 with a follow-up until November 2021.

Participant recruitment and inclusion criteria

Patients who fulfilled the inclusion criteria and self-presented to the district hospital Girandurukotte were recruited after obtaining informed consent. Inclusion criteria were age above 18 years and presence of three or more of the following symptoms: arthralgia, myalgia, fever, back pain, or non-specific symptoms such as general malaise, fatigue, or weakness as reported by the patient. No adjustments were made to the inclusion or exclusion criteria due to the COVID-19 pandemic, as participant recruitment had already been completed prior to the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. The pandemic only affected the timing of follow-up visits, some of which occurred later than originally planned. In total, 151 patients were included, and 85 patients returned for follow-up visits and had blood sampled at least twice. This was a pragmatic trial that recruited all consenting patients during the recruitment phase. Therefore, a prior sample size calculation was omitted.

Exclusion criteria

Patients who were below 18 years of age and patients who were previously diagnosed as CKDu or CKD were excluded from the study.

Assessment of demographics and patient history

Demographics and patient histories were systematically assessed using a standardized questionnaire in all patients. Responses were collected by well-trained research assistants after obtaining informed consent. A specific part of the questionnaire gathered basic demographic information of participants including age, gender and site of residency. Furthermore, clinical and occupational data, agrochemical exposures, tobacco and alcohol consumption, drinking water supply and details of medication usage were recorded as a part of the questionnaire. Details on weather conditions of the study area were retrieved from the national weather information system.

Sample collection

Blood and urine specimens were collected at the initial presentation as well as during follow-up appointments. After voiding, samples were stored at -80 °C. Five participants from the prospective study cohort underwent kidney biopsies based on clinical indications (primarily reduced GFR). Additionally, 15 kidney biopsies were obtained from Sym-CKDu patients outside the study cohort; no urine or blood samples from these patients were included in this study. Only the remaining biopsy tissues from these individuals were used for histological analysis. Statistical analyses, including Chi-square tests, ANOVA, and two-sample proportion tests, were performed to evaluate the data collected from the biopsies and other specimens (see Table 3 for more detailed characteristics of patients).

Routine laboratory and histology analysis

Blood samples analysis

A total of 170 blood samples were analyzed at Labor Berlin in Berlin, Germany, for serum creatinine levels, serum cystatin C, and CRP concentrations using the Jaffe method (creatinine) or turbidimetry (Cystatin C, CRP), respectively. These samples were collected from 151 patients, with some patients providing multiple samples at initial presentation and follow-up visits.

Urine samples analysis

Urine samples were analyzed for protein levels using conventional urine dipsticks in Sri Lanka.

Histological analysis

Light microscopy assessments were performed using hematoxylin and eosin staining, along with relevant special stains on formalin fixed paraffin embedded specimens. Immunofluorescence studies included IgG, IgA, IgM, and C3 staining and performed on fresh samples using direct method. The main histological parameters assessed were glomerular pathology, tubulitis, tubular atrophy, interstitial inflammation, interstitial fibrosis, vascular pathology and any other changes if present. Additionally, these parameters were quantified using a standardized scoring system established by the pathology department of the University of Peradeniya [11]. Histological evaluation was conducted by experienced Pathologists with special interests in renal pathology.

RNA/DNA extraction and Hanta-PCR

For RNA/DNA extraction of organ samples, a piece of tissue of approximately 2 mm of diameter, 350 µL of PBS, and a steel ball were first transferred to a 2 mL tube. The sample was then homogenized using the TissueLyser II (Qiagen) for 3 min at 30 Hz. After centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 1 min, 250 µL of supernatant was transferred to a deep-well plate and mixed with 250 µL tissue lysis buffer. Samples were extracted using the DNA and Viral NA Volume Kit on a MagNA Pure 96 system (Roche) according to manufacturer’s instructions and eluted into 100 µL. RNA was used for library preparation for high-throughput sequencing and tested specifically for Hantavirus using a PAN-Hanta-PCR [12]. RNA was reverse transcribed using M-MLV (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and random hexamer primers. The resulting c-DNA was used as PCR template to target a 347 nucleotide (nt) long region of the Hantavirus genomic L segment.

Virome data

A total of 32 serum-samples (loss of eGFR group [n = 7], control group [n = 25]) were analyzed for antibodies against the human virome based on a PhIP-Seq pipeline (VirScan™) at CDI laboratories (USA) as described previously [13]. Briefly, protein sequences from all annotated viruses with human tropism were extracted from UniProt library to create a T7 phage library after tiling overlapping sequences. Serum samples were then incubated with the library leading to immunoprecipitation of antibodies using protein A/G coated magnetic beads. The precipitate was PCR-amplified and the sequences quantified by a next-generation sequencing and analysis pipeline. Samples were compared to negative controls with no antibody input (mock-immunoprecipitations).

Data from VirScanTM-samples were analyzed and plotted with R (v4.3.0) using RStudio. For a detailed description of all used packages, see Supplemental Table S1.

High-throughput sequencing (HTS) and data analysis

DNA-Libraries were generated using the KAPA RNA HyperPrep Kit (Roche Diagnostics) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Nucleic acids were fragmented for 6 min at 85 °C and subsequently amplified for 13 cycles. Resulting libraries were quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Agilent TapeStation with the HS D1000 Kit (Agilent). Equimolar pooled libraries were paired-end sequenced using the NextSeq 500/550 High Output Kit with 150 cycles (Illumina, USA).

For virus identification from HGTS runs, we applied a computational pipeline that consists of a collection of bash shell (C. Ramey, GNU bash, available at https://www.gnu.org/software/bash/) scripts that invoke third-party and custom in-house programs. Overall execution is coordinated by a Slurm Pipeline Python package (T. C. Jones, Slurm Pipeline, available at https://github.com/acorg/slurm-pipeline) Briefly, human reads and sequence duplicates are removed. All remaining reads were matched at the amino acid level against protein databases using DIAMOND (version 2.1.7) [14]. Two databases were used: (i) National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) viral reference sequences [15] (downloaded June 2023); (ii) Protein sequences from a collection of insect virus genomes [16].

Protein match results were converted into an HTML summary and then checked independently by two virologists. All potential viral hits were manually reviewed using Geneious Prime v2022.0.1 (Biomatters, Auckland, New Zealand, https://www.geneious.com).

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

After screening for suitable individuals 151 patients with acute febrile syndrome were recruited into the study. However, only 85 patients returned for follow-up visits and had their blood sampled at least twice. Therefore, our analysis of eGFR trajectories focuses on these 85 patients. Baseline characteristics for the initial 151 patients are given in Supplemental Table S2, detailed baseline characteristics of the 85 analyzed patients are displayed in Table 1. The most frequent symptoms at presentation were back pain (97.6%) and myalgia (89.4%), 49.4% of the patients reported fever. Median eGFR was 88.0 ml/min/1.73 m2 (Range 17.3–130.8 ml/min/1.73 m2). Only a small fraction of 5 patients exhibited low-level proteinuria (1 + on dipstick), while the remaining patients had either none or only trace amounts of proteinuria. As expected, we observed a high prevalence of reduced eGFR at presentation, especially in men. Of the 46 included men, 15 individuals (32.6%) had an eGFR in the range of < 90 –60 min/min/1.73 m2, 16 individuals (34.8%) had an eGFR in the range of < 60 − 30 ml/min/1.73 m2, and 2 individuals (4.3%) had an eGFR below 30 ml/min/1.73 m2. Among females (n = 39) 9 individuals (23.1%) had an eGFR of < 90 − 60 ml/min/1.73 m2 (Fig. 1A). We observed a significant association between eGFR and age (p = 0.0066 for males; p < 0.0001 for females). Assuming a linear loss of eGFR with aging, a yearly decline of -1.17 ml/min/1.73 m2 for females and − 1.02 ml/min/1.73 m2 for males was calculated using linear regression statistical method.

Change of renal function over time in the follow-up

The patients were revisited after a median follow-up of 2.3 years (range 0.8–3.3 years). Patients were grouped into three categories, according to their eGFR at the last follow-up. The first group maintained a normal eGFR of ≥ 90 ml/min/1.73 m2 (n = 36); in this group, eGFR changes were not further analyzed because current eGFR formulas exhibit increasing imprecision at higher GFR levels and making changes in eGFR values ≥ 90 ml/min/1.73 m2 is potentially meaningless. The second group had an eGFR < 90 − 60 ml/min/1.73 m2 (n = 33), the third group had an eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m2 (n = 16) at last follow-up.

In the eGFR group < 90 − 60 ml/min/1.73 m2, 5 patients had gained at least 10 ml/min/1.73 m2 eGFR when comparing initial visit and follow-up. These patients are likely to have had an acute kidney injury (AKI) during the initial presentation, however, we cannot formally confirm whether AKI criteria were fulfilled.

In the two groups with impaired eGFR (group < 90 − 60 and group < 60 ml/min/1.73 m2 at last follow-up), patients showed varying degrees of eGFR decline since their first visit. In the absence of an established cut-off for pathological loss of eGFR, we defined a decrease of at least 4 ml/min/1.73 m2 per year as a significant loss of eGFR. According to this, a total of 16 patients out of 49 patients in the reduced eGFR groups had a significant loss of eGFR over the follow-up period with a median loss of 6.3 ml/min/1.73 m2 eGFR per year (range − 4.1 to -42.1 ml/min/1.73 m2). Thus 18.8% of all 85 patients included showed an unexpected loss of eGFR during the observation period (Fig. 2).

A total of five (n = 5) patients enrolled in the study underwent kidney biopsy during the observation period for clinical indication (loss of GFR). All biopsies were consistent with CKDu. All of these five (n = 5) patients had reduced eGFR, three of them had a loss of eGFR over the observation period (details see below, Table 2). No clear risk factors associate with loss of GFR

Next, we investigated whether any patient characteristic predicted loss of eGFR in our cohort. For an overview of analyzed features please refer to Table 2. No significant differences were observed regarding initial symptoms, work habits, reported symptoms, or weather conditions on the day of presentation. Likewise, no differences were observed in CRP values at initial presentation or urinary findings (proteinuria and sediment). Next, we analyzed water exposure and compared individuals who drank and/or bathed in well water compared to the usage of water sources regarded to be of better quality (public supply, reverse osmosis, spring water, bottled water, bowser source). Individuals with well water exposure had a numerically higher risk of loss of eGFR (23% of patients with well water exposure had loss of eGFR of at least 4 ml/min/1.73 m2 per year compared to 11% of the non-well users); however, this finding did not reach statistical significance. Interestingly, when mapped, the patients with loss of eGFR appeared to cluster geographically in a more confined area compared to patients without loss of eGFR (Fig. 3). Furthermore, we analyzed CRP values from serum samples collected at the first and last visit, respectively (Fig. 4A). At first presentation we analyzed 85 samples (median CRP 1.3 mg/l; min. 0.5 mg/l, max 70.5 mg/l), at last follow-up 84 samples were measured (median CRP 0.9 mg/l; min. 0.6 mg/l, max. 13.3 mg/l). There was no correlation between CRP at first visit and changes in eGFR (Pearson’s r = 0.1220, p = 0.266) (Fig. 4B).

Absence of evidence for viral mediated kidney injury in CKDu

The initial febrile syndrome and the loss of eGFR in a considerable fraction of study participants were consisted with a potential viral infection leading to kidney inflammation, as has been suggested previously [8, 10]. To screen for a potential viral etiology for the loss of eGFR serum samples from the follow-up visits were analyzed using the VirScan platform. VirScan detects the presence of IgG antibodies against all known human-pathogenic viruses. A total of 32 serum samples were analyzed, including seven samples from patients with loss of eGFR of at least 4 ml/min/1.73 m2 per year during the observation period. No difference in any virus reactivity was observed between the groups with and without loss of eGFR (Fig. 5).

Heatmap of VirScanTM-analysis from 32 serum samples. Patients were grouped into Loss of eGFR (n = 7) and control (n = 25). No difference in any virus reactivity is detectable between the two groups

As the screening for virus-reactive antibodies did not yield any potential candidate viruses causing eGFR decline, we next attempted to directly detect viral genome in kidney biopsies from patients with CKDu. To this end, freshly frozen fractions of kidney biopsies were analyzed with Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) for the presence of viral pathogens. A total of 19 kidney biopsies were analyzed, 4 from our prospective cohort and an additional 15 collected from Sym-CKDu patients outside our prospective study population. All included biopsies were from symptomatic individuals at initial presentation, with the most frequent symptoms being fever/feverish feeling (78.9%), joint pain (63.2%) and back pain (57.9%). All biopsies were consistent with CKDu, however, four biopsies showed alternative/additional pathologies (diabetic nephropathy [n = 2], IgA nephropathy [n = 1], hypertensive nephropathy [n = 1]). Importantly 52.6% of the biopsies showed acute inflammation and 78.9% signs of any inflammation (see Table 3; Fig. 6).

High-throughput sequencing resulted in 2.1 to 39.4 million generated reads per sample (mean: 26.6 million, median: 29.2 million, (See Supplemental Table S3). After removing human and bacterial genomes, the remaining sequences were compared against the NCBI database. We were not able to detect any virus with known or likely possible pathogenicity to humans. Occasionally, endogenous retroviral sequences could be detected, as well as known kit contaminations [17]. Furthermore, using a PAN-Hanta-PCR we could not detect any specific Hantavirus RNA.

Discussion

The current study, the first observational cohort study on suspected Sym-CKDu, focused on patients presenting with an otherwise unexplained feverish syndrome in an area endemic for CKDu in Sri Lanka [4, 5]. Our study revealed three main findings: First, a relevant proportion of patients with this syndrome had reduced eGFR at the time of first presentation and had histological lesions compatible with Sym-CKDu. Second, a considerable proportion of these patients had a rapid loss of eGFR within 2 to 3 years after initial presentation. Third, using a combination of antibody screening and genome testing in kidney biopsies we found no evidence for a viral infection as a causative trigger.

All included patients had at least three of the previously described symptoms of Sym-CKDu - i.e. arthralgia, myalgia, fever, back pain or feeling unwell - which were serious enough to prevent them from regular work so that they sought health care advice in a regional hospital. A relevant proportion of 21.1% (18 of 85) had an eGFR below 60 ml/min/1.73m2 at presentation, while none had had gross proteinuria, consistent with CKDu [18]. The majority of patients were male, with almost 40% presenting with an eGFR below 60 ml/min/1.73m2, which is also consistent with a known gender imbalance of CKDu in Sri Lanka [4, 19]. Almost one in five patients (18.8%) revealed a rapid loss of eGFR of at least 4 ml/min/1.73 m2 per year during a median follow-up of 2.3 years. In this calculation, we excluded individuals with an eGFR >90 ml/min/1.73m2 at the last follow-up, as in this range the used formulas are imprecise and the relevance of a nominal loss of eGFR is uncertain. However, we might have excluded patients with truly progressive CKDu at an early stage. Likewise, the chosen threshold of 4 ml/min/1.73 m2 per year to define rapid loss of eGFR is high and the overall proportion of patients with eGFR decline was potentially higher. However, our cohort with symptomatic presentation does not seem to have a completely different eGFR course than a general CKDu populations in Central America [20] or Sri Lanka [21, 22], in whom the majority did not present with symptoms. Altogether, we did not find any epidemiological characteristics that identified Sym-CKDu as distinct subgroup of CKDu patients.

Several infectious agents have been implicated in the etiology of CKDu. An association between viral nephritis and progression of CKD has been well established in BK-viral nephropathy in kidney transplant recipients. Recently, an association between Hantavirus infections and CKDu has been reported [8, 10]. However, our analysis of virome data using the VirScanTM-pipeline did not indicate a specific viral infection as trigger of symptomatic CKDu. Consistently, our NGS-evaluation on biopsies with confirmed CKDu and acute lesions were unable to reveal any signals from viruses and a PAN-Hanta-PCR performed on biopsy samples was negative. The combination of the lack of detection of viral genome in kidney tissue of Sym-CKDu patients and the absence of specific anti-viral antibodies makes a viral etiology of CKDu very unlikely. Nevertheless, the possibility of a novel, yet unidentified virus or below the detection limit viral load or a self-limiting viral infection causing kidney inflammation without persistent antibody response cannot be completely ruled out.

Despite rigorous investigations the etiology of CKDu remains uncertain. Single or episodic exposure to an infection or nephrotoxin that does not leave identifiable evidence at chronic, irreversible stages could be a viable explanation for inconclusive results. The same scenario has been described for other toxin-mediated nephropathies such as drug-induced interstitial nephritis where patients diagnosed with chronic interstitial diseases without any information on specific agent. Similarly, if not detected at the acute inflammatory stage, patients may remain in the community with residual kidney dysfunction until detected through screening with no obvious evidence of etiology. therefore, comprehensive evaluation at an acute stage may be crucial for identifying the etiology. Our results support the possibility of a tip-of-the-iceberg phenomenon in CKDu, where the majority of patients go unnoticed whilst only a minority is presenting with detectable kidney injury on routine evaluation or on occasion of a symptomatic episode. However, it was noteworthy to observe a decline of eGFR which aligned with natural progression of CKDu in one group and improvement of eGFR, consistent with recovery from AKI in another group. These findings may indirectly reflect the level of exposure, severity of kidney injury and degree of residual kidney damage. Faster progression in otherwise comparable individuals suggest the possibility of continuous low-grade exposure to a nephrotoxin. An observed numerical association with well water and geographical clustering further supports this possibility.

This study has several limitations. The study included patients presenting to a single regional hospital creating an inclusion bias. In particular some patients presumably had kidney function tests before enrolment, enriching our cohort for patients with CKD and advanced stages. The study was conducted during the COVID 19 pandemic resulting in only half of the patients returning for follow-up visits. Only five patients received a kidney biopsy due to clinical indication, which was not part of the protocol. The limited number of biopsies was mitigated by including 15 additional biopsies from patients outside the study for further analysis. As the present study focused on possible viral infections we did not include analyses for toxicology. Virome analysis was performed only in a subgroup due to financial and logistical limitations. On the other hand, we present one of the largest cohorts of symptomatic CKDu (Sym-CKDu) patients. By using state-of-the-art methodology combining routine laboratory testing, histology, virome analysis and high-throughput sequencing we created a holistic approach to test for viral infections.

Lastly, the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Therefore, our analysis cannot rule out a viral cause of CKDu, though the current evidence makes it very unlikely.

Conclusions

In summary, focusing on CKDu patients presenting with acute symptoms of unclear cause we generated consistent evidence against an infection with a known virus causing the symptoms or playing a role in the pathogenesis of kidney disease and its subsequent progression.
