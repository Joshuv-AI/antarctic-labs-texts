Predictors of depression trajectories during the COVID-19 pandemic in adults with rheumatoid arthritis: results from the Canadian Early Arthritis Cohort
Authors: Susan J Bartlett, Orit Schieir, Marie-France Valois, Gilles Boire, Diane Tin, Janet E Pope, Carol A Hitchon, Glen Hazlewood, Carter Thorne, Louis Bessette, Hugues Allard-Chamard, Bindee Kuriya, Vivian P Bykerk, CATCH Investigators
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesGrowing evidence points to mental health impacts of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). We explored trajectories of depressive symptoms in the year pre- and 2 years post-pandemic onset in adults with RA.MethodsData are from the Canadian Early Arthritis Cohort (CATCH), a prospective, multicentre, early RA cohort of Canadian adults treated by rheumatologists. Pre-pandemic, participants completed patient-reported outcome measures and rheumatology exams in person. After March 2020, patient-reported outcomes were collected at in-person and virtual visits. We used group-based trajectory modelling to explore longitudinal patterns of depressive symptoms prior to and throughout the COVID-19 pandemic and multinomial regression to identify factors associated with depression trajectory group.ResultsA total of 989 participants had a mean age of 60 years (s.d. 14), RA for 6 years (s.d. 4) and were mostly white (84%) and female (73%) with some college education (60%). Most (77%) were in Clinical Disease Activity Index remission/low disease activity prior to the pandemic. We identified four trajectories: resilient (no symptoms throughout: 60%), worsening (none–mild: 22%), improving (mild–minimal: 8%) and persistent (moderate–severe throughout: 9%). Age, sex, race, education, pain, physical and social functioning, fibromyalgia and history of anxiety/depression were associated with different trajectories.ConclusionAlthough 60% had a consistent affect during the first 2 years of the pandemic, mood worsened in greater than one in five, suggesting a cumulative impact over time. Biological, psychological and social factors and worse pre-pandemic symptoms and function were associated with a greater risk of depression during the pandemic. Identifying at-risk groups impacted by major stressors like the pandemic may offer new opportunities to personalize treatment, allocate resources, reduce disease flares and improve outcomes.Keywords: depression, mood, affect, RA, COVID-19 pandemic, coping, arthritis, rheumatic diseases

Introduction

Growing evidence points to the considerable mental burden of the prolonged coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. In a representative sample of Canadians, the prevalence of major depressive disorder was more than two times higher during COVID-19 than in the 4 years before (i.e. 15% vs 7%) [1]. In the UK, mental distress increased from 19% to 27% by the end of April 2020 [2]. As compared with 2019, between April 2020 and August 2021 in the general US population, rates of depression increased 4-fold [3]. In a systematic meta-regression of longitudinal and cross-sectional studies with a pre-pandemic baseline, it was estimated that worldwide the prevalence of major depressive disorder increased by 28% (95% CI 25–30) [4].

The mental health impacts of COVID-19 in adults with RA may be considerable given that depression is a common comorbidity (17%) and is linked with worse disease activity, comorbidities, disease management, health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and outcomes [5]. In RA, depression is also associated with a poorer response to treatment and reduced odds of reaching remission [6]. In RHEUMAVID, a cross-sectional study of 1800 patients with rheumatic diseases (29% with RA), 46% were classified as at risk of depression [7]. However, data from longitudinal studies on mental health impacts in rheumatic diseases are sparse.

Our goal was to explore and characterize longitudinal trajectories of depressive symptoms and predictors in Canadians with RA leading up to and throughout the first 2 years of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Participants

Our sample consisted of adults enrolled between January 2017 and August 2022 in the Canadian Early Arthritis Cohort (CATCH) who had completed the 29-item Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS-29) Depression measure at least once in both the pre-pandemic (March 2019–February 2020) and pandemic (March 2020–January 2022) time periods. CATCH is a multicentre inception cohort of RA patients seen across 18 sites in Canada; details are available elsewhere [8]. CATCH has been approved by research ethics boards at study sites and participants provide written informed consent.

Measures

At enrolment, patients completed a questionnaire asking self-identified sex, race and education. Comorbidities were assessed by calculating the Rheumatic Disease Comorbidity Disease Index (RDCI). Disease activity was assessed with the Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI) [9].

Patients completed the PROMIS-29 V2.0 Adult Profile at study visits. The PROMIS-29 includes four-item scales for anxiety, depression, fatigue, pain interference, sleep disturbance, physical function and ability to participate in social roles and activities (hereafter called participation). Higher scores on symptoms reflect greater symptom burden, whereas higher scores on physical function and social participation reflect better function (i.e. less impairment). The PROMIS-29 uses a t-score [population mean 50 (s.d. 10)] to reliably and precisely capture symptoms across the full continuum [10]. Depressive symptoms were classified using the recommended cut-offs for mild (55–59.9), moderate (60–69.9) and severe (≥70) levels of symptoms [11].

Statistical methods

Group-based trajectory modelling (GBTM) was used to identify distinct subgroups of patients that follow similar trajectories of depressive symptoms in the period of observation. GBTM assumes a population is composed of distinct groups with differing trajectories and individuals are assigned a probability of belonging to each latent group. Multiple models were developed and fitted examining three to seven latent classes of depressive symptoms. Model fit was compared using the unadjusted and sample size–adjusted Bayesian (BIC) and Akaike (AIC) information criteria, sigma, assigned and theoretical proportions and mean posterior probabilities ≥0.8 by group. We also compared trajectory outputs graphically to examine whether differing numbers of latent classes allowed a clearer interpretation.

Established trajectories were characterized and compared using analysis of variance to identify mean differences in continuous variables across groups and chi-squared for categorical variables. Multivariable multinomial regression models were used to identify individual and clinical characteristics and PROs associated with trajectory membership accounting for all variables in the model. Candidate predictors in multivariable models were selected based on significance in univariate analyses, factors in the literature and expert opinion and included sociodemographic (age, sex, race, education), health (obesity, comorbidities, history of depression, anxiety and/or fibromyalgia), RA (pre-pandemic CDAI and/or advanced therapies) and a 0.5 s.d. change in pain, physical and social functioning. SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) was used for data analysis.

Results

The 989 participants had a mean age of 60 years (s.d. 14) and a disease duration of 6 years (s.d. 4); 73% were women, 84% were White and 77% were in CDAI remission/low disease activity at the visit prior to the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic (March 2020). Two groups (17%) were classified with mild (i.e. improving) or moderate (i.e. persistent) depressive symptoms in the year prior to the pandemic.

The best fitting model of the 15 tested was linear and included four groups: resilient (no symptoms throughout: 60%), worsening (none–mild symptoms: 22%), improving (mild–no symptoms: 8%) and persistent (moderate–severe symptoms throughout: 9%) (Fig. 1). The best model had the smallest BIC and AIC values (BIC-A −10 331.21, BIC-B −10 324.31, AIC-A −10 294.93 and AIC-B −10 282.93), the smallest difference between theoretical proportions and calculated group sizes (0.07–1.22%) and the highest mean posterior probabilities by group (0.80–0.96).

Trajectories of depressive symptoms in the year preceding and first 2 years of the COVID-19 pandemic

In univariate analyses, as compared with the resilient trajectory, those in the worsening trajectory were younger women with worse symptoms (depression, anxiety, fatigue, pain and sleep) and poorer physical and social functioning (Table 1). As compared with the resilient trajectory, improving participants were more likely to be middle-aged, non-obese men with more active RA pre-pandemic, including worse physician and patient global scores, RA symptoms and physical and social functioning. Participants in the persistent trajectory had the highest proportion of racially diverse obese women, lowest proportion of postsecondary education, with more active RA pre-pandemic, high physician and patient global scores, worst symptoms and physical and social functional impairments and were more often treated with advanced therapies and steroids.

In fully adjusted multinomial regression models, higher pre-pandemic pain, lower social functioning and a history of anxiety or depression were associated with being in any of the depression trajectories (Supplementary Table S1). Additional predictors of the worsening trajectory were younger age, female sex and better RA control. Predictors of the Persistent trajectory were non-White race, lower education, fibromyalgia and worse pre-pandemic physical function.

Discussion

In our longitudinal cohort of Canadian adults with RA with pre-pandemic data, most participants (60%), whom we termed resilient, reported consistently good affect during the year preceding and first 2 years of the COVID-19 pandemic. Further, 8% who were initially depressed reported their mood steadily improved such that they no longer reported depressive symptoms by the end of the first 2 years. In contrast, 22% reported deteriorating mood and 9% had persistent depressive symptoms. Higher pain, lower social functioning and a history of depression or anxiety were associated with being in the worsening, improving or persistent trajectories, along with other biopsychosocial factors.

Our results suggest that symptoms and function in the pre-pandemic phase were important indicators of emotional coping during the first 2 years of COVID-19. Notably, prior to the pandemic onset, resilient participants reported symptoms and physical and social functioning at or better than expected levels (i.e. PROMIS scores <55 for symptoms and >45 for physical and social functioning); their mean PROMIS depression score was >0.5 s.d. below norms, suggesting no evidence of depression. This is consistent with the low mean CDAI (5.4) of the resilient trajectory and offers additional evidence their RA was in good control at pre-pandemic visits. Conversely, the 22% whose mood steadily deteriorated (worsening) reported worse pain and greater impairments in social function pre-pandemic, as did those in the persistent group who experienced moderate–high depressive symptoms throughout the 3 years of observation. As compared with resilient participants, even those with improving mood (whose mean scores pre-pandemic reflected mild symptoms) experienced worse pain and social functioning pre-pandemic. These results suggest that suboptimal pain control and poorer social functioning were important risk factors associated with the 40% of individuals at risk for higher depressive symptoms going into the pandemic.

Another common factor among participants not classified as resilient was a prior history of anxiety and/or depression. Participants in the worsening, improving and persistent symptom trajectories had from three to six times the odds of a history of emotional distress. Recurrence of depression is common and a previous history may reflect a genetic predisposition to be more sensitive to stressors that trigger depression or the effects of early stress, deprivation and trauma [12]. However, loneliness and social isolation, which increased during the pandemic for many, are also potent risk factors for depression [13]. Conversely, the impact of disease activity on depressive symptom trajectory appears very small relative to the impact of psychosocial factors.

Social determinants of health are associated with a greater likelihood of depression and were implicated in our findings. For example, the resilient group had the highest proportion of men who were White. Studies show that women are at higher risk of depression, especially those with chronic diseases [14]. Resilient participants also reported the best HRQoL, with less than one-third reporting even mild anxiety, depression, fatigue, pain or sleep disturbance, despite 40% reporting impairments in physical functioning and 24% in social roles and functioning. Participants in the improving trajectory also had the highest proportion of participants who were well educated. In contrast, less than half of those with persistent symptoms had a college education. Notably, depressive symptoms were highest among individuals 40–65 years of age, a time when demands from family, career and social roles peak for many.

UK researchers also identified similar patterns in the general UK population across the first 6 months of the COVID-19 pandemic. Pierce et al. [15] used latent class analysis to examine trajectories of depression in a random probability UK sample across the first 6 months. Five distinct trajectories emerged where three-fourths of participants had consistently very good or good mental health throughout; 12% who were worse initially steadily improved, 7% had sustained worsening and 4% had persistently elevated depression scores. Living in a deprived neighbourhood, remaining isolated for health reasons and a previous history of mental illness were more common in individuals whose mental health worsened, whereas those who were male, older and living in an affluent area were most likely to have maintained good mental health during the first 6 months of the pandemic.

In Canada, identifiable waves of the pandemic were associated with a range of social restrictions resulting in greater social isolation for many. Overall, rates of major depression doubled during the pandemic, and the risk of depression increased exponentially with the number of risk factors [1]. Two predictors of good mental health were a sense of mastery or perceived control over life circumstances and a sense of community belonging [1]. The higher rates of depressive symptoms (22% worsening and 9% persistent symptoms) we observed in the first 2 years of the pandemic may, in part, reflect the burden of living with RA, growing loneliness from social isolation and a cumulative response to increased stressors in the environment. Public health authorities recommended Canadians stay home as much as possible, restrict non-essential travel, avoid gatherings (both indoors and outdoors), maintain a distance of at least 2 m, wear well-fitted masks in public indoor spaces, frequently wash hands or use hand sanitizer and improve ventilation in homes/workplaces by opening windows [16]. Early in the pandemic, Canadians with RA also faced potential shortages of antirheumatic medications, limited access to in-person rheumatology visits and admonitions to remain isolated due to a greater perceived vulnerability to COVID-19 infection and more severe outcomes.

Depression is important to identify and address in RA given that when mood declines, so too does physical health, and these effects are reciprocal. Depression in RA is associated with worse symptoms, self-care and treatment adherence response to RA medications and greater healthcare usage. However, pain, fatigue, poor sleep and greater disability also negatively impact mental health in RA [17]. Treatment guidelines from the EULAR and ACR emphasize the importance of a holistic approach to managing RA, including minimization of psychological distress. While depression is treatable and treatment results in better control of RA disease, and HRQoL, many adults experiencing major depression remain untreated and experience worse outcomes as a result.

Strengths of this study include the use of a large number of well-characterized adults with RA for whom pre-pandemic information was available to compare with measures prospectively collected during the pandemic. The use of PROMIS measures, which have been validated in RA and assess symptoms and functional impacts that RA patients have identified as important to them, allowed us to monitor changes in a broad set of HRQoL indicators. The standardized collection of PROs prior to the onset of COVID-19 facilitated ongoing collection from patients even when face-to-face visits with rheumatologists were not possible, particularly in the earliest months of the pandemic. There are also limitations. Data collection was more sporadic during the pandemic and the representativeness of the subset of patients for whom data are available is unclear. The PROMIS assesses depressive symptoms and does not confer a clinical diagnosis. We did not have information available on whether patients were taking antidepressant medications.

Conclusions

In summary, in this large cohort of adults with RA, 60% had a consistent mood in the year preceding the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic and during the first 2 years. This is reassuring given the essential role that depressive symptoms play in overall HRQoL and RA outcomes. However, mood worsened in more than one in five, suggesting a cumulative impact of pandemic-related stressors over time. Pain, poor social functioning and a history of depression or anxiety were predictive of deteriorating mood during the pandemic, along with several biopsychosocial indicators. Given the impact of depression on HRQoL and RA management, the ability to identify vulnerable groups impacted by stressors such as the COVID-19 pandemic may offer better opportunities to personalize treatment, allocate resources effectively and improve outcomes in RA.
