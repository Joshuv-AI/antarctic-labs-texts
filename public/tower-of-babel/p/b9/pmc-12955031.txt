Cognitive behavioural therapy is not universally evidence-based: implications for eating disorders
Authors: Laurence Cobbaert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is widely described as an evidence-based psychological treatment, including within eating disorder (ED) care. However, evidence claims are only valid within the boundaries of the populations, outcome measures, and service contexts represented in the underlying trials. This paper argues that CBT is not universally evidence-based and that extending its evidentiary status to neurodivergent people and Indigenous, First Nations, and Māori communities is not currently justified. Drawing on decolonial and neurodiversity scholarship and lived experience-led literature, the paper shows how research designs prioritising internal validity have contributed to an evidence base that functions as a methodological echo chamber, reproducing findings drawn predominantly from white, Western, largely neurotypical samples. As a result, CBT trials often lack external validity for communities whose experiences of distress are shaped by sensory processing differences, executive functioning difficulties, systemic discrimination, food insecurity, and the colonial legacy of trauma, erasure, and dispossession. In ED care, these limitations are amplified by diagnostic frameworks and outcome measures that privilege weight-centric approaches and neuronormative and Eurocentric cognitive models while overlooking sensory, somatic, cultural, and socio-political drivers of illness. Evidence from autistic and Indigenous participants documents mis-attunement and iatrogenic harm within standard CBT-based pathways for EDs. The paper concludes that universal claims about CBT are not supported by the current evidence base and are better understood as population-specific evidence claims grounded in co-designed trials, culturally valid measurement, and implementation research demonstrating safety, relevance, and benefit for diverse communities.Keywords: Cognitive behavioural therapy, CBT, Psychotherapy, Eating disorders, Colonialism

Introduction

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) became central to modern psychological practice because manualised protocols and randomised controlled trials (RCTs) make possible internally valid causal inferences in the specific populations studied, and because clinical trials have demonstrated benefit for some individuals in specific contexts and circumstances [1]. However, the existence of benefit within defined samples does not justify unqualified generalisation beyond those samples’ characteristics [1]. The production of mental health-related evidence is not a purely technical activity. It embodies choices about which populations are included or excluded, which outcomes are measured or prioritised, and which narratives and epistemic frameworks are treated as authoritative. Decolonial critiques emphasise that universalist claims commonly obscure alternative ways of knowing and meaning making and demand that evidence generation attend to political, cultural, and ethical contexts [2].

This paper argues that CBT’s claim to be “evidence-based” must be qualified. A therapy can be evidence-based for the specific populations, circumstances, and service ecologies represented in the trial literature, while still failing to be evidence-based for other populations and contexts. Extending evidence claims to neurodivergent people or to Indigenous, First Nations, and Māori communities requires representative trials, co-designed outcome measures, and implementation evidence that demonstrates safe, culturally valid, and acceptable delivery in the real-world. I first describe the general limits on universal claims, then consider neurodivergent and Indigenous priorities, and finally funnel these arguments into a focused critique of CBT in the context of eating disorder (ED) care.

CBT is not universally evidence-based

CBT’s evidentiary base rests heavily on trials that optimise internal validity. That design logic commonly limits heterogeneity because it controls for comorbidity and contextual complexity in order to detect treatment effects. The consequence is an evidence corpus concentrated in white, Western and largely neurotypical samples, a distribution that reduces the external validity of trial findings for many, often marginalised, communities [1, 3–5]. Meta-analytic work on cultural adaptations indicates that adaptation can improve outcomes, but adaptations vary widely in depth, theory, and governance, and meta-analytic averages cannot be assumed to validate universal transfer without attention to fit and implementation [6].

Neurodivergent people

Neurodivergence involves heterogeneity in perception, sensory processing, emotional and relational expression, and executive functioning, and these differences shape both how distress is experienced and which forms of support are safe and effective. Recent evidence indicates that CBT does not reliably meet these needs for many autistic people and can be harmful [3, 4]. Similarly, emerging work on attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) documents that CBT is often ineffective for adults with ADHD and, in some cases, experienced as harmful [7]. More broadly, critics argue that CBT is not neurodiversity-affirming because it prioritises normalisation and masking over acceptance, relational and sensory safety, and structural supports [8, 9].

Indigenous peoples and culturally diverse communities

Indigenous, First Nations, and Māori scholarship foregrounds alternative conceptualisations of mental illness and recovery that are relational, spiritual, communal, and/or land-based rather than primarily intrapsychic. Decolonial methodological work emphasises that Western individualist models and symptom scales often fail to capture these domains [2]. Empirical scoping reviews and Indigenous-authored reports document that Indigenous-run services measure outcomes such as cultural reconnection, community safety, and relational repair—outcomes that conventional symptom-focused RCTs typically do not measure [10, 11].

Implementation realities intensify these problems. Workforce shortages, centralised specialist services, and lack of Indigenous governance make it unlikely that adapted therapies can be delivered with fidelity and safety at scale without structural investment [12, 13]. Policy analyses for First Nations Australians and Māori show elevated need for mental healthcare combined with the absence of culturally validated screening instruments and treatment pathways, which undermines triage and early detection and further reduces the relevance of mainstream trial evidence [14, 15].

CBT and EDs

ED care concentrates these measurement and implementation challenges. ED services rely heavily on Western diagnostics, rigid triage thresholds, and manualised psychosocial packages rooted in CBT; this architecture privileges low body mass index and acute medical risk and tends to overlook binge-spectrum, night-eating or sensory-driven presentations that may be more common in structurally disadvantaged or culturally distinct populations [16, 17]. When diagnostic logic and outcome measurement are misaligned with local presentations, trials that rely on these instruments cannot establish relevance for communities whose phenomenology differs.

CBT approaches for EDs assumes the primacy of maladaptive cognitions and avoidance behaviours as maintenance processes, and that modifying these intra-individual processes is sufficient for recovery. Lived experience-led research, especially among neurodivergent people, show that sensory processing differences, gastrointestinal disorders, systemic discrimination, and experiences of iatrogenic harm can be proximal drivers of restrictive and binge behaviours [18, 19]. When the primary illness drivers are somatic, sensory, or socio-politically mediated, cognitive techniques may not only fail but also exacerbate iatrogenesis, shame, and disengagement from care [18, 20, 21].

Neuronormativity

Neurodivergent people with EDs commonly report that CBT-focused treatments are alienating, ineffective, or actively harmful [18, 20, 22, 23]. A recent empirical study of autistic adolescents with EDs documents this core finding in striking terms: participants described being “misunderstood,” with some expressing that they left treatment feeling traumatised, and the authors report that “Some specific treatment modalities such as FBT and CBT were mentioned as particularly mis-attuned to what was needed as an autistic person by way of attempting to override autistic traits and subsequently increasing distress” [24].

Recent lived experience-led scholarship further amplifies this message. Elwyn [18], writing as a neurodivergent person with longstanding illness, describes how standard psychotherapies, including CBT, were experienced as harmful and traumatising, and worsened ED symptoms: “Clinicians’ conceptualisations of ED in treatment often directly erased and invalidated the meaning of my experiences, and these experiences reinforced my AN.”

Importantly, Miskovic-Wheatley et al. [25] note that co-design in ED research remains uneven and that neurodivergent people are frequently under-represented in clinical trials. This observation strengthens the argument that the ED evidence base lacks the representative samples and co-designed, neurodiversity-affirming outcome measures required to judge CBT’s effectiveness for neurodivergent populations. When that systematic gap is read alongside qualitative and lived experience-led scholarship documenting mis-attuned and traumatising experiences [18, 24] and narrative syntheses identifying unmet sensory, executive, and relational needs [20], the conclusion is clear: without deliberate recruitment, transparent reporting, and genuine co-design for neurodivergent participants, claims that CBT is evidence-based for neurodivergent people with EDs are not supported [18, 20, 24, 25].

Indigenous peoples, First Nations Australians, and Māori

Among Indigenous peoples the mismatch between ED measurement and lived reality is stark. Scoping reviews and Indigenous-led projects consistently document that ED presentations in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander and Māori communities are shaped by food insecurity, disrupted traditional food systems, and the legacies of colonisation, and that mainstream diagnostic and treatment logics centred on low body mass index and rigid categorical thresholds routinely fail to detect many Indigenous presentations and support needs, producing delayed and often crisis-driven care [14, 15, 17, 26].

Work led by and with Māori demonstrates that ED recognition, language, and measurement require active cultural translation rather than superficial adaptation. Fraser et al. [14] show how developing te reo Māori terminology for ED-related phenomena can reduce stigma and improve early recognition, because concepts of social-and-emotional wellbeing and whakapapa foreground relational and community dimensions of distress that Western psychiatric nosology misses. Pettie et al. [26] further emphasise the substantial gaps in knowledge about Māori experiences of EDs and argue for Kaupapa-Māori research and co-governed trials that prioritise cultural reconnection, whānau safety, and community-defined outcomes. Together these contributions indicate that evidence for CBT-based ED pathways in Aotearoa cannot be established without Indigenous leadership of ED measurement, trial governance, and translational implementation.

Scoping reviews focusing on First Nations Australians document parallel problems and point to actionable priorities for ED research and services. Burt et al. [15] synthesise evidence of diverse ED presentations among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples and highlight the striking absence of culturally validated ED screening instruments, which undermines detection and produces late or crisis-driven care. Gall et al. [17] map policy and service gaps and call for culturally responsive prevention and screening and management approaches that address food insecurity and the colonial disruption of traditional food systems. For First Nations Australians, the ED evidence requirement is therefore concrete: co-governed research that produces culturally specific screening tools, trials that test interventions against Indigenous-defined outcomes, and implementation investments in decentralised, culturally safe services that can deliver adapted ED care at scale [15, 17].

ED care and colonialism: beyond Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand

Although the illustrative cases in the previous section draw on First Nations Australian and Māori experiences, the structural critique advanced here is not geographically bounded. Rather, it reflects broader dynamics of coloniality in psychological science, whereby Eurocentric knowledge systems position themselves as neutral, universal, and exportable, while Indigenous epistemologies and those rooted in minoritised racial and ethnic communities are positioned as culturally specific deviations from an assumed norm [27]. In this framing, the problem is not simply that some populations are excluded from research samples, but that the underlying ontological and epistemic premises of mainstream psychology are shaped by colonial histories.

There is clear evidence in the ED field that dominant epistemic systems shape what is studied, who is represented, and which populations are implicitly treated as the default reference group. Egbert et al. [28] systematically reviewed studies published in the International Journal of Eating Disorders across 2000, 2010, and 2020 and found that fewer than half of papers (44.3%) reported race and ethnicity. Even among studies that did report these data, white participants consistently constituted the majority of research samples, comprising approximately 70% of participants. Egbert et al. [28] explain that such patterns limit the contextualisation and generalisability of findings across racial and ethnic groups and may perpetuate assumptions that EDs primarily affect white people.

These representational gaps are not trivial methodological omissions but have downstream implications for how evidence-based ED treatments are conceptualised, evaluated, and implemented. Egbert et al. [28] explicitly argue that the lack of race and ethnicity reporting restricts the field’s ability to understand ED etiology, maintenance processes, and treatment needs within racialised and ethnically minoritised populations. This concern aligns with broader ED scholarship highlighting persistent disparities in recognition and access to care. In their scoping review across Australia, Canada, Aotearoa New Zealand, and the United Kingdom, Williams-Ridgway et al. [29] identified evidence of systemic inequities in ED recognition, diagnosis, and treatment for minority ethnic populations, including disparities in referral pathways and barriers linked to culturally insensitive service provision. Relatedly, Becker et al. [30] found that ethnically diverse healthcare consumers described barriers to ED care that included stigma, stereotyping, and concerns about clinician understanding and cultural competence, suggesting that treatment systems shaped by dominant norms may alienate those whose experiences do not align with mainstream clinical assumptions.

This broader pattern is also reflected in literature emphasising that ED research has historically marginalised Black and Indigenous populations. Egbert et al. [28] note that only a small proportion of ED research has directly focused on Black or Indigenous individuals, a concern also highlighted in the virtual issue commentary by Mikhail and Klump [31], which underscores the limited visibility of Black and Indigenous experiences within the ED evidence base. This is particularly significant because ED prevalence and risk are increasingly understood as shaped by intersecting structural factors. For example, Burke et al. [32] found that ED risk varied substantially at the intersections of socioeconomic status, race and ethnicity, gender identity, and sexual orientation, demonstrating that demographic effects cannot be reduced to single-axis categories. Such findings reinforce that the external validity of ED evidence cannot be assumed when dominant samples and dominant frameworks fail to capture intersectional lived realities.

Taken together, this literature supports the argument that the concern raised here is not culture-specific but structural. When ED-related evidence is generated within dominant epistemic systems and then generalised outward, “evidence-based” claims may function as epistemic authority that marginalises alternative frameworks of distress, support needs, healing, and recovery [27]. Egbert et al. [28] argue that inadequate reporting and limited inclusivity constrain the generalisability of ED research and may perpetuate the assumption that EDs primarily affect white populations, discouraging research and service development for underrepresented communities. In colonised and racially stratified contexts, such epistemic exclusions may contribute to delayed diagnosis, iatrogenic harm, and reduced treatment engagement, particularly where wellbeing is conceptualised through relational, collective, spiritual, or culturally embedded frameworks that diverge from the assumptions embedded in standardised CBT models [29, 30, 33]. In this sense, the structural critique is not limited to First Nations Australian or Māori contexts but reflects broader systemic risks that arise whenever ED interventions are presented as universally evidence-based despite persistent inequities in representation, conceptual framing, and epistemic authority within the ED research literature [28, 31, 34].

Conclusions

The literature reviewed here converge on a single, pragmatic conclusion: CBT is evidence-based only within the boundaries of the populations, outcome measures, and service ecologies represented in the clinical trials; it is not universally evidence-based. When trials, diagnostic instruments, and governance assume neurotypical norms and Eurocentric conceptualisations of illness and recovery, they systematically misrepresent the lived realities and support needs of neurodivergent people and Indigenous, First Nations, and Māori communities. Lived experience testimony and recent qualitative work make this point vividly: CBT and related ED treatments are frequently mis-attuned, sometimes iatrogenic, and often experienced as invalidating by those whose sensory, executive functioning, or cultural contexts and needs are sidelined or ignored [18, 20, 24, 25].

Clinically, the ethical imperative is clear. Practitioners must not invoke the shorthand “CBT is evidence-based” without specifying the populations and circumstances to which that claim applies. In ED care this means clinicians should recognise when standard protocols are poorly matched to clients. Where adaptation is attempted, it must be co-designed with lived experience partners and accompanied by practice changes that create relational safety and sensory-accessible environments. Moreover, services must measure and report iatrogenic harm [18, 21, 25]. For research and policy, trials must purposively recruit neurodivergent, First Nations, and Māori participants, pre-specify neurodiversity-affirming and culturally relevant outcome measures, and embed mixed methods process evaluations that test whether adapted components engage the mediators that matter [13, 15, 25].
