Aortobronchial Fistula: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Zhenghan Liu, Mingyu Li, Haomei Yu, Wei Chen, Xiaoyun Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis report details the urgent management of a life‐threatening aortobronchial fistula (ABF) in a 61‐year‐old male with a history of esophageal cancer surgery, who presented with massive hemoptysis, underscoring the critical role of rapid diagnosis and endovascular intervention in this rare vascular complication. Emergency digital subtraction angiography (DSA) confirmed the ABF, prompting immediate thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) with successful deployment of a stent graft, which achieved complete hemostasis, restored aortic integrity, and resulted in an uncomplicated postoperative course without recurrent bleeding. The case reinforces TEVAR as a minimally invasive and definitive therapeutic strategy for ABF, particularly in high‐risk surgical candidates, while highlighting the indispensable role of angiographic imaging in acute settings and the need for heightened clinical suspicion in patients with prior thoracic interventions to avert catastrophic outcomes.

Summary For massive hemoptysis in patients with prior thoracic surgery, promptly suspect aortobronchial fistula. Emergency DSA is crucial for diagnosis, and TEVAR provides immediate, life‐saving minimally invasive repair.

Introduction

Aortobronchial fistula (ABF) is a lesion characterized by the formation of a sinus tract between the thoracic aorta and the bronchus, leading to bleeding. It is a rare condition clinically [1, 2]. ABF is associated with critical morbidity and high mortality, necessitating emergency intervention. Recently, we successfully treated a case of ABF using TEVAR. To deepen the understanding of this disease and improve the diagnostic and treatment levels, we report the etiology, clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and treatment of ABF based on this case and a literature review.

Case History

The patient was a 61‐year‐old male. He was admitted to the hospital due to “hemoptysis for 1 day”. The patient had onset of hemoptysis without obvious predisposing factors 1 day ago. The blood was bright red, approximately 700 mL in volume. He was treated at a local hospital. Enhanced chest computed tomography (CT) showed postoperative changes of esophageal cancer, tumor recurrence, tracheoesophageal fistula (Figure 1), multiple aspiration pneumonias in both lungs, and localized pulmonary emphysema in both lungs. After treatments such as hemostasis, there was no obvious improvement. He was then transferred to our department for further diagnosis and treatment.

Enhanced chest CT scan demonstrates a left main bronchus–mediastinum fistula, associated with posterior bronchial wall destruction, luminal stenosis, loss of mediastinal fat planes, and adjacent gas‐containing soft tissue infiltration suggesting communication with aerated spaces. However, no significant signs of aortobronchial fistula are demonstrated.

The patient underwent surgical resection for esophageal cancer 2 years prior, with no postoperative complications. The tumor recurred half a year ago, and he received radiotherapy and chemotherapy.

Physical examination revealed a body temperature of 36.6°C, a pulse rate of 102 beats per minute, a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute, and a blood pressure of 115/62 mmHg. Laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 12.00 × 109/L, a red blood cell count of 2.39 × 109/L, and a hemoglobin level of 73 g/L. There were no significant abnormalities in coagulation function, liver and kidney function, or electrolytes.

Methods

The initial diagnosis was massive hemoptysis, recurrence of esophageal cancer after radiotherapy and chemotherapy, tracheoesophageal fistula, pneumonia, and pulmonary emphysema. The patient's family was informed that an emergency interventional embolization surgery was required for hemostasis, and they agreed to the treatment and signed the informed consent form for the surgery.

After rapid and thorough preoperative preparation, the patient was placed in a supine position on the DSA table. Local anesthesia was administered at the right groin, followed by puncture of the right femoral artery. After successful puncture, an arterial sheath was inserted, and a pigtail catheter was introduced into the aorta for angiography. The angiography did not show any obvious abnormal blood vessels (Figure 2). The catheter was then changed to a celiac catheter and carefully advanced to the descending aorta to further explore for abnormal blood vessels. When the catheter tip reached the aorta at approximately the level of the tracheal bifurcation, there was a sense of entrapment. Contrast injection demonstrated simultaneous opacification of the arterial system and unexpected visualization of the left main bronchus, definitively confirming the diagnosis of aortobronchial fistula (Figure 3), and thus ABF was diagnosed. The catheter was secured in place to prevent dislodgment, which could precipitate massive bleeding.

Contrast injection demonstrated simultaneous opacification of the arterial system and unexpected visualization of the left main bronchus, confirming the diagnosis of aortobronchial fistula (black arrow).

The patient's family was informed again about the intraoperative situation. It was explained that further treatment required the implantation of an aortic stent graft to seal the fistula, and they agreed to the treatment plan. The patient was then given general anesthesia, and a local incision was made at the right femoral artery. The angiography catheter was withdrawn, and an aortic stent graft (32 × 160 mm, Xianjian Company) was quickly implanted. The proximal end of the stent was close to the origin of the left subclavian artery, and the distal end reached the middle segment of the descending aorta. Postoperative angiography showed that the stent adhered well to the vessel wall, was in the correct position, and there were no complications such as endoleak (Figure 4). Finally, the stent delivery system was removed, and the incision of the right femoral artery was sutured. The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU).

Results

Seven days after the operation, there were no bleeding or surgery‐related complications, and the patient was discharged to continue subsequent treatment at a local hospital. Telephone follow‐up 3 months after the operation revealed no recurrence of hemoptysis.

Discussion

ABF is a rare complication resulting from a pathological communication between the aorta and the bronchus [3]. In 1914, Girardet first described ABF, and in 1934, Keefer and Mallory reported 6 cases of ABF [4]. ABF is classified into two subtypes: primary and secondary [5, 6].

Primary ABF is caused by primary lesions of the aorta, mediastinum, lungs, and esophagus. The etiologies include aortic aneurysm, malignant tumors, tuberculosis, syphilis, fungal infections, trauma, and atherosclerosis. Secondary ABF is caused by postoperative complications, such as erosion of the bronchial tree by the stent graft and local sepsis caused by graft infection of the aorta. In this case, ABF was attributed to direct invasion of the aorta and bronchial tree by recurrent esophageal cancer. Due to anatomical relationships, most ABF cases clinically involve the descending aorta and the left tracheobronchus [7], as was also the case in this study. Written informed consent for publication of the case has been obtained from the patient.

Most ABF patients present with intermittent hemoptysis [8]. Taking local malignant tumors as an example, as the tumor progresses and invades surrounding tissues, inflammation occurs, leading to adhesion and necrosis, and ultimately the formation of a fistula. In the early stage of the fistula, a small amount of bleeding associated with it usually causes mild hemoptysis. Once a blood clot forms, the hemoptysis stops [9]. The patient in this study was likely in the early stage of fistula formation. The patient's intermittent hemoptysis may have led to the formation of blood clots obstructing the fistula orifice. Consequently, no obvious fistula tract was visualized on contrast‐enhanced CT or conventional DSA angiography. Only when the catheter was used for superselective angiography and the catheter tip was embedded in the fistula could the left main bronchus be visualized. However, if the blood clot dissolves or detaches, hemoptysis will recur, and this process may repeat, resulting in intermittent hemoptysis in the patient. Intermittent hemoptysis is the most common symptom, occurring in 95% of patients [10].

As the tumor progresses, the fistula continues to grow, and eventually becomes large enough to cause life‐threatening bleeding, with a mortality rate approaching 100% [3]. Therefore, for patients presenting with massive hemoptysis for the first time, a high level of suspicion and comprehensive diagnosis are required, especially for those with esophageal cancer, mediastinal tumors, etc. Unfortunately, massive and fatal hemoptysis is sometimes the first and only manifestation of this disease. Other clinical symptoms include chest pain, cough, and dyspnea [11].

Multiple imaging examinations can assist in the diagnosis of ABF, including plain chest radiography, CT, and angiography. Bronchoscopy is another option to consider [12]. Chest radiography can only show an aneurysm in approximately 50% of patients. The X‐ray findings are usually nonspecific. CT is often the preferred examination method. CT can partially clarify the etiology, distinguish whether the lesion is due to the aorta itself or a local tumor, and even visualize the fistula [7]. In addition, CT can detect soft tissue density representing a blood clot. Bronchoscopy can identify the source of bleeding, but the fistula may not be easily detected. This invasive procedure carries risks such as perforation and detachment of the blood clot, which may lead to hemorrhagic shock [2]. Therefore, if necessary, an emergency surgical plan must be prepared before the examination.

Angiography is considered the gold standard for diagnosis as it can detect vascular leakage and bronchial visualization. However, blood clots usually prevent the contrast agent from flowing from the aorta into the bronchus, so angiography may produce false‐negative results in some cases. If there is a high level of suspicion, superselective angiography techniques should be used to carefully explore. Once the fistula is found, the catheter should not be withdrawn to avoid detachment of the blood clot during the operation and subsequent massive bleeding. The catheter should be withdrawn at the last moment when the surgical treatment is ready.

With the development of interventional techniques, TEVAR provides a safer, simpler, and faster method for repairing life‐threatening ABF. The implantation of an aortic stent graft can effectively seal the fistula and prevent fatal massive bleeding, showing good perioperative outcomes [7]. Canaud et al. [13] conducted the largest study on TEVAR for ABF: reviewing 134 patients, reporting a technical success rate of 93.2%, a 30‐day mortality rate of 5.9%, and an aortic‐related mortality rate of 14.3% after an average follow‐up of 17.4 months. In this case, no bleeding occurred after TEVAR, also demonstrating the definite efficacy of the stent graft.

In conclusion, ABF is a rare disease with a critical condition and extremely poor prognosis. Successful treatment depends on timely diagnosis and repair. Massive hemoptysis is usually the initial symptom. The mortality rate is 100% in undiagnosed patients. This certainty emphasizes the importance of maintaining a high level of vigilance when facing patients who may have ABF and minimizing diagnostic delays. Based on the treatment experience of this patient and literature review, we believe that TEVAR is a minimally invasive, safe, rapid, and effective treatment for ABF and should be the preferred treatment method for this disease.
