Isolation of major bacterial species associated with equine skin wounds and in-vitro antibacterial activities of selected medicinal plants
Authors: Aliy Beshir, Jelalu Kemal, Bruk Abraha, Eyob Hirpa Tola
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWounds on the skin are a common health issue affecting working equines. This study aimed to evaluate the in-vitro antibacterial properties of crude methanolic extracts from selected medicinal plants against pathogens isolated from equine skin wounds in Merti district. Agar well and disc diffusion tests were used to determine the mean zone of inhibition, while broth dilution methods were used to determine the minimum inhibitory concentrations and minimum bactericidal concentrations (MBCs), further confirming the potent antibacterial effects of the selected medicinal plant extracts. One way analysis of variance was used to compute the mean zone of inhibition (mm ± SEM) using SPSS version 20. The results showed that Staphylococcus aureus was the most frequently isolated bacterial pathogen. The medicinal plants Zingiber officinale (ginger), Allium sativum (garlic), Croton macrostachyus, and Solanum incanum exhibited significant antibacterial activity against S. aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, and Klebsiella pneumoniae. On agar well diffusion method, the highest and lowest zone of inhibition was recorded in C. macrostachyus (28.0 ± 1.2 mm for S. aureus standard strain) and Z. officinale (12.7 ± 0.7 mm for K. pneumoniae wound isolate), respectively at 780 mg/mL concentration. Similarly, C. macrostachyus showed the highest (28.3 ± 0.9) zone of inhibition using the disc diffusion test for S. aureus standard strain. In combined extracts, the highest zone of inhibition was found against standard strain of S. aureus with Croton macrostachyus + Zingiber officinale (33.0 ± 0.6), followed by both Allium sativum + Zingiber officinale and Allium sativum + Croton macrostachyus at 32 ± 1.2 mm at 780 mg/mL concentration. The minimum inhibitory concentration ranged from 12.18 mg/mL for A. sativum (against standard S. aureus) to 390 mg/mL for S. incanum (against P. aeruginosa), while MBC ranged from 24.38 mg/mL for A. sativum (against S. aureus and E. coli) to > 390 mg/mL for S. incanum against K. pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa. The findings demonstrate the potential of these natural resources as alternative or complementary treatments for managing bacterial infections associated with equine skin wounds, which is particularly relevant in the context of the growing global challenge of antibiotic resistance, and provide valuable baseline information for future in-vivo investigations and the potential development of herbal-based wound management strategies in equine veterinary practice, warranting further in-vivo studies.Keywords: Antibacterial activity, Equine, In-vitro, Medicinal plant, MIC, Skin woundSubject terms: Biological techniques, Drug discovery, Microbiology

Introduction

According to the Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia Central Statistical Agency1 in 2020, Ethiopia has the largest equid population in Africa, with approximately 2.11 million horses, 8.98 million donkeys, and 0.38 million mules. Equines play a crucial role as working animals for rural and urban communities, transporting people and goods across the country2. Despite the significance of equines in Ethiopia, they are susceptible to a variety of infectious and non-infectious diseases that negatively affect their wellbeing. One of the most prevalent health issues facing working equines worldwide is skin wounds3.

Skin wounds in horses can lead to serious complications if not properly managed, as the bacterial species that colonize these wounds play a significant role in the healing process2. Identifying the predominant bacterial species associated with equine skin wounds and evaluating the antibacterial potential of medicinal plants can provide valuable insights for developing effective wound management strategies2,3.

Ethiopia is renowned for its rich biodiversity, including a wide variety of medicinal plants with potential antimicrobial properties4,5. Exploring the antibacterial activities of these medicinal plants against the bacterial pathogens found in equine skin wounds could lead to the development of alternative or complementary treatments for wound management in equine veterinary practice4,5.

Skin wounds in equines can provide a suitable environment for microbial growth, especially in cases of saddle sores, as they facilitate infection risk by creating optimal conditions for microorganism propagation6. Common bacterial pathogens isolated from equine wounds include Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella spp., Streptococcus spp., Proteus spp., Enterobacter, Enterococci, Clostridium, Candida, Peptostreptococcus, Fusobacterium, and Aeromonas7,8. The emergence and spread of antibiotic-resistant bacterial strains have further exacerbated the global threat posed by infectious diseases.

Ethiopia is renowned for its rich biodiversity, including a wide variety of medicinal plants with potential antimicrobial properties4,5. Exploring the antibacterial activities of these medicinal plants against the bacterial pathogens found in equine skin wounds could lead to the development of alternative or complementary treatments for wound management in equine veterinary practice4,5. Medicinal plants have gained increasing attention as alternative treatments to combat the growing challenge of antibacterial resistance in pathogenic microorganisms9. These natural resources have traditionally been utilized for treating various infectious diseases and wound types, including chronic, deep suppurative, lacerated, incised, and ulcerated wounds10.

In Ethiopia, medicinal plants have a long history of serving as a source of traditional medicine for treating human and livestock ailments11,12 Several plant species, such as Zingiber officinale (ginger), Allium sativum (garlic), Croton macrostachyus, and Solanum incanum, have been employed to manage different health conditions due to their reported antimicrobial, antifungal, anti-inflammatory, and other pharmacological properties13,14

The antimicrobial potential of these medicinal plants holds promise for their application in managing bacterial infections associated with equine skin wounds. Equine skin wounds can provide a suitable environment for the proliferation of various bacterial pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and other opportunistic microorganisms7. The emergence and spread of antibiotic-resistant bacterial strains have further exacerbated the global challenge of infectious diseases, making the exploration of alternative treatment options a crucial step in addressing this public health concern8.

Bacterial pathogens have demonstrated an increasing ability to develop resistance against commercially available antibacterial agents, posing a significant challenge in the field of antimicrobial therapy15. This growing concern has necessitated the search for new raw materials to develop novel antibacterial agents that can effectively combat the rising issue of microbial resistance.

Extracts from medicinal plants have been utilized by pharmaceutical companies in manufacturing herbal antibacterial agents, providing an alternative approach to combat infectious diseases12. However, over time, some pathogenic microorganisms have acquired resistance against these commercially produced herbal antibacterials, an issue that is prevalent worldwide, including in Ethiopia.This study evaluated the antibacterial potential of local medicinal plants against pathogens isolated from equine skin wounds in Merti district. In the district, people medicate their equine in nearby clinics as well as by application of medicinal plants such as S. incanum (in oromic hiddii), C. macrostachyus (in oromic makkanniisaa), A. sativum (in oromic qullubbii) and Z. officinale (in oromic zingbila). The research aimed to provide evidence-based insights into traditional practices of using plant-based remedies for treating equine skin infections, which are a common issue in the region. Investigating the antimicrobial properties of locally available medicinal plants can help identify effective natural alternatives to complement or potentially replace conventional antibiotics, contributing to the management of antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections in the equine population.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The study was conducted in Merti district, which is located in the Arsi Zone of the Oromia Regional State in Ethiopia. The district is bordered by the districts of Jaju, Boset, Asako, and Guna. Merti district has 18 peasant associations and 5 towns, with Abomsa serving as the administrative center, situated 202 km from the capital city of Addis Ababa. Merti district has a substantial livestock population, comprising 120,770 cattle, 71,695 goats, 37,205 sheep, 18,692 donkeys, 7685 horses, 3270 mules, 29,647 camels, and 146,707 poultry. The district is served by 7 veterinary clinics, and the predominant agricultural practices include mixed crop-livestock and mobile livestock production systems Fig. 1.

Map study area.

Study design

The study was conducted from October 2021 to January 2022 using both cross-sectional and experimental research methodsThe study first identified bacteria present in equine skin wounds and then assessed the antibacterial activity of medicinal plant extracts against the identified pathogens.

Approval for animal experiments

Before initiating the study, Ethical Clearance was obtained from the Haramaya University Research Ethics Review Committee in September 2019 (approval number: HURG-2019-01-01-53) to conduct experiments on equines. All experiments were carried out in accordance with Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines and regulations, and the authors adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines.

Study population

The study focused on the equine population in the Merti district, specifically targeting animals with skin wound infections. Equines without any visible skin wounds were excluded from the sampling and data collection for this research.

The researchers conducted their investigations within the framework of the established veterinary clinics in the district. This allowed them to access the equine population and gather data on the common bacterial pathogens involved in the skin wound infections affecting these animals.

Sampling strategy and sample size determination

The study employed purposive sampling of equines with skin wounds from veterinary clinics. The sample size was based on the availability of relevant cases presented to the clinics during the study period.

Test organism

The study used a combination of bacterial strains, including both clinical isolates and standard laboratory strains of known pathogens. The clinical isolates were obtained from skin wound samples taken from horses and included the bacterial species S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, E. coli, and K. pneumoniae. The standard laboratory strains were acquired from reputable culture collections, such as the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and the Ethiopian Public Health Institute, and also included these same bacterial species. These particular bacteria were selected for the study due to their established potential to cause skin infections in horses.

Clinical sample collection

The samples carefully collected from the equine skin wounds, disinfecting the area and using sterile cotton swabs to obtain the specimens. The samples were immediately placed in nutrient broth and transported to the laboratory for analysis, following proper procedures to prevent contamination.

Bacterial isolation

The collected samples were cultured on various agar media to isolate and identify the bacterial species. Gram staining and biochemical tests were performed following standard procedures7,16 to determine the genus-level identification. Further species-level identification was achieved through selective culturing and additional biochemical tests, as detailed in the study’s appendices.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antibiotic susceptibility testing was conducted using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method. Discs of 6 mm diameter were prepared from sterile Whatman number 1 filter paper by a perforator and each of them was impregnated with 0.01 ml of the prepared test extracts. Antibiotic-impregnated discs were placed on agar plates inoculated with the bacterial isolates. The inhibition zones were measured, and the results were interpreted as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant, following CLSI guidelines17. The detailed procedures are described in the study’s appendix.

Medicinal plant collection and extraction of active ingredients

The study followed appropriate protocols and obtained the necessary approvals for the collection of plant materials. Specifically, the plant collection and sampling procedures complied with international guidelines and legislation. Plant identifications were conducted by a botanist, Hymanot Bizuneh, and voucher specimens were deposited at the Haramaya University Plant Science Department Herbarium. . Prior to the study commencement, approval was obtained from the Haramaya University Research Ethics Review Committee in September 2019 (approval number: HURG-2019-01-01-53).

The plant materials used in the study included leaves of S. incanum and C. macrostachyus, bulbs of A. sativum, and rhizomes of Z. officinale. These were collected, cleaned, dried, and identified using herbarium keys. The dried, powdered plant materials (100 g) were extracted with methanol (500 ml) by maceration, filtered, and concentrated using rotary evaporation. The extracts were stored at 4 °C. Phytochemical screening of the extracts was conducted to detect the presence of various secondary metabolites.

For the antibacterial testing, both standard (EPHI) and clinical isolates of S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and K. pneumoniae were used. The bacterial inocula were standardized to 0.5 McFarland turbidity (~ 108 CFU/mL) per CLSI guidelines. Antimicrobial assays were performed using disc diffusion and agar well diffusion methods, with extract concentrations of 780, 390, and 195 mg/mL. Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) and Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC) were determined by broth microdilution. The antimicrobial activity of the plant extracts was evaluated following the CLSI guidelines. Bacterial suspensions were inoculated onto Mueller Hinton Agar (MHA) plates, and antimicrobial testing was conducted using both disc diffusion and agar well diffusion methods. All tests were performed in triplicate. Additionally, the most effective individual extracts were combined and tested using the agar well diffusion method.

Preparation of different concentrations of the crude extracts

The crude plant extracts were used to prepare stock solutions at a concentration of 780 mg/mL, which was done by dissolving 3.9 g of each dried extract in 5 ml of distilled water. From these stock solutions, two additional lower concentrations of 195 mg/mL and 390 mg/mL were then prepared for each individual plant extract. Combinations of the medicinal plant extracts were also tested, for which the individual extracts at each concentration (195 mg/mL, 390 mg/mL, 780 mg/mL) were combined in a 1:1 ratio to create the combination extracts. In total, the antimicrobial testing was carried out using the individual plant extracts at three different concentrations as well as 1:1 combinations of the extracts at the same three concentrations.

Minimum inhibitory concentration inhibition determination

The minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the effective plant extracts was determined using a broth dilution method. Serial dilutions of the extracts were prepared in nutrient broth, and bacterial inoculums were added. After incubation, the lowest extract concentration that showed no visible bacterial growth was recorded as the MIC, following the procedures described by Usman and Osuji18. This MIC determination was conducted for any extracts that had shown ≥ 8 mm inhibition zones in the initial screening.

Minimum bactericidal concentration determination

To determine the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC), the test tubes from the MIC assay that showed no visible bacterial growth were subcultured onto fresh agar plates. After incubation, the MBC was recorded as the lowest extract concentration that did not allow any visible bacterial colony growth, following the method described by19.

Data analysis

The data was analyzed using SPSS software. Categorical data was analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test, while the antimicrobial activities and MICs of the plant extracts were analyzed using descriptive statistics. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test was used to assess the statistical differences in the mean zones of inhibition, with a significance level of P < 0.05.

Staphylococcus aureus was the most commonly found pathogen, comprising 38.7% of the total isolates. There was a significant difference in the zone of inhibition among the various extract concentrations. Discussion

A study conducted in the Merti district of Arsi Zone investigated the major bacterial species associated with equine skin wounds. The researchers found four key bacterial species: Staphylococcus aureus (38.7%), Escherichia coli (20%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (8.7%), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (5.3%). These findings are consistent with a previous study from East Nile locality in Khartoum state, which reported S. aureus (40%), K. pneumoniae (5%), and P. aeruginosa (5%) as the main pathogens in equine skin wounds20.

Staphylococcus aureus was the most commonly encountered bacteria in the equine skin wounds, which aligns with the findings of other researchers20,21. This may be attributed to the virulence factors of Staphylococcus species, which allow them to secrete enzymes and toxins that facilitate colonization, invasion, and spread within wounds, giving them a competitive advantage over other bacteria22.

In contrast, the prevalence of K. pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa was lower compared to the other isolated species20. This is consistent with a previous study that reported prevalence of 7.6% and 8.9%, respectively, for these bacteria21. The lower prevalence of K. pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa may be due to inhibition by the animal’s defensive mechanisms or competition from more virulent microbial species20.

The study found that the majority of the isolated bacterial species were sensitive to the antibiotics kanamycin and sulphamethazole, while many showed intermediate susceptibility to other commercially available antibiotics20. The extensive antimicrobial sensitivity to kanamycin and sulphamethazole may be due to their infrequent use in veterinary practice, in contrast with other antibiotics20. This aligns with previous research that found variation in the efficacy of different antibiotics against the isolated bacteria, with no single antibiotic being effective against all the wound pathogens23. This could be attributed to the uncontrolled selling and use of veterinary drugs, leading to improper administration and the development of antibiotic resistance20.

Given the rise in antibiotic resistance, evaluating the antibacterial potential of herbal medicines is crucial for addressing animal health issues24,25 In this study, both individual and combined crude plant extracts exhibited considerable antibacterial activity against the tested pathogenic bacteria, including S. aureus, E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and P. aeruginosa20.

Specifically, the garlic (Allium sativum) extract showed promising antibacterial activity, with the ability to inhibit the growth of all the tested pathogens at a concentration of 780 mg/mL20. This finding is consistent with previous studies that reported the antibacterial effects of A. sativum against S. aureus, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa26,27. The presence of phytochemicals such as saponins, flavonoids, and phenolic compounds in the plant extract is likely responsible for its antimicrobial properties28

Croton macrostachyus, Zingiber officinale, and Solanum incanum also demonstrated considerable antibacterial activity against the tested26,29,30). The combinations of the medicinal plant extracts, such as Allium-Zingiber, Allium-Croton, and Croton-Zingiber, showed higher inhibitory activity against the tested bacterial isolates compared to the individual extracts27,31. The synergistic effect of the combined extracts was likely due to the sequential inhibition of a common biochemical pathway and the disintegration of the outer membrane32,33

The study found that the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) and Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC) values of the tested medicinal plants ranged from 12.18 to 390 mg/mL, with gram-positive bacteria generally being more susceptible to the crude extracts compared to gram-negative bacteria31. The difference in susceptibility may be due to the outer lipopolysaccharide membrane in gram-negative bacteria potentially hindering the passage of active phytochemicals34 However, the crude extracts did show antibacterial activity against both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, suggesting the presence of broad-spectrum bioactive metabolites in the studied plants34.

Conclusions and recommendations

The study found that Staphylococcus aureus was the most prevalent bacteria isolated from equine skin wounds, and these isolates exhibited drug resistance. The crude extracts of Zingiber officinale (ginger), Allium sativum (garlic), Carissa macrostachyus, and Solanum incanum showed promising antibacterial activity against S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and K. pneumoniae in laboratory assays.

The antibacterial effects were dose-dependent, and may be due to the presence of bioactive compounds acting through different mechanisms. Extracts with lower minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) generally had higher inhibitory activity against the tested pathogens.

The results indicate these medicinal plant extracts have potential for treating equine skin wound infections, including those caused by drug-resistant bacteria. The authors recommend further research to investigate different plant parts, extraction solvents, plant sources, and growth stages, as well as to conduct toxicity testing and in vivo antibacterial studies to confirm the safety and efficacy of these extracts for treating equine wound infections. Particularly, it is important to elucidate the impact of these plant extracts on the wound-healing process and their neurotoxicity in laboratory animals.
