Stepwise Procedural Workflow of the Modified Guide Anchoring “UNICORN” Technique

Background

The clinical success of transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has naturally led to its expansion into the management of degenerated surgical bioprostheses. However, valve-in-valve (ViV) TAVI is not a uniform procedure; its complexity is dictated by the specific design of the primary surgical valve.1, 2, 3, 4 Among these, the Perceval S represents a unique technical subset. As a sutureless, self-expanding bioprosthesis,5 it uses a nitinol alloy stent frame designed to exert radial force against the aortic annulus and sinuses, rather than being anchored by a traditional surgical sewing ring.6

Although this design facilitates rapid surgical deployment, it introduces significant challenges during subsequent ViV procedures. The Perceval frame features a sinus segment and an upper crown designed to conform to the patient's anatomy, which often results in the bioprosthetic metal frame sitting in extreme proximity to the aortic wall and the sinotubular junction (STJ).6 When these valves fail, the displaced bioprosthetic leaflets can sequester the coronary ostia during transcatheter heart valve (THV) expansion.

The risk of iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction is particularly acute when the valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance is <2 mm.2, 3, 4,6,7 In such hostile aortic roots, standard rescue strategies are frequently limited by the rigid nitinol architecture. For instance, chimney stenting relies on a gutter between the THV and the bioprosthesis; in the Perceval S, the upper crown petals may eliminate this space, leading to mechanical sandwich compression of a coronary stent against the aortic wall.8 Similarly, intentional leaflet laceration (Bioprosthetic Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Artery obstruction [BASILICA]) requires sufficient sinus width to allow the split leaflet to splay open, a requirement often unmet in the narrow sinus dimensions of Perceval valve failure.3 Given the shallow sinuses for some anatomy and eccentricity of the coronary artery ostium, the results of chimney and BASILICA can also be difficult to predict.

Case Presentation

A 71-year-old woman (body surface area: 1.85 m2, Society of Thoracic Surgeons score: 6.07%) presented with NYHA functional class II heart failure and exertional dizziness. Echocardiography confirmed severe mixed failure of a Perceval S valve (mean gradient: 56 mm Hg; aortic valve area: 0.8 cm2). Preprocedural cardiac computed tomography revealed a critical right coronary artery (RCA) VTC of 1.8 mm and a superior, eccentric RCA takeoff (Figure 1). Most significantly, the valve-to-STJ distance was measured at 1 mm, with the nitinol crown petals crowding the aortic root. The valve-to-sinus distance was also limited to 1 mm, leaving virtually no room for leaflet splaying (Figure 1). The left main height was 16 mm and the leaflet length was 12 mm, making the risk of left coronary obstruction much lower than right coronary obstruction.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with transesophageal echocardiographic guidance. Cerebral embolic protection was established via the right radial artery. Primary access was obtained through the right femoral artery using a 14-F eSheath. Given the critical VTC distance, bilateral coronary protection was prioritized with 0.014-inch wires. A 6-F MPA1 guide catheter supported a 1.8-F Fincross microcatheter for leaflet traversal. Using an Astato 30 wire, the mid-scallop of the right coronary cusp was punctured. Once the wire crossed into the left ventricle (LV), we executed the modified UNICORN maneuver; the microcatheter was removed and an over wire balloon 2.0 × 12 was used to open the space to anchor the 6-F MPA1 guide, which was advanced directly across the bioprosthetic frame and deep into the LV. This seated the guide as a stable anchor, allowing for a 1-wire transition. A 0.035-in Safari preshaped high-support wire was then placed directly through the MPA1 guide into the left ventricular apex. A 10.0- × 20-mm peripheral balloon was advanced and positioned across the right coronary cusp. High-pressure inflation achieved a controlled mechanical laceration of the bioprosthetic tissue to protect the eccentric RCA ostium (Figure 2). A 23-mm balloon-expandable valve was then successfully implanted (Figure 3). The total traversal-to-deployment window was 32 minutes. Postprocedural transesophageal echocardiography showed no paravalvular leak and a peak transvalvular gradient of 9 mm Hg. Six-month follow-up confirmed a stable mean gradient of 13 mm Hg (Figure 4, Table 1).

Discussion

The management of a failing Perceval S sutureless bioprosthesis represents a paradigm shift in procedural planning. Unlike traditional stented bioprostheses, the Perceval S uses a full-length nitinol frame that exerts continuous radial force against the aortic root. This case illustrates a perfect storm of anatomic contraindications where the design of the surgical valve precluded the 2 most common coronary protection strategies: chimney stenting and the BASILICA technique. Although both techniques have been described for the Perceval valve, the decision to avoid the chimney stent technique was rooted in the mechanical interplay between the THV, the Perceval frame, and the aortic wall. In standard ViV TAVI, a chimney stent is often supported by the offset created by a surgical sewing ring. However, the Perceval S lacks a ring; its upper crown petals flare outward to stabilize the valve within the STJ. With a measured 1-mm clearance, any coronary stent would be subjected to sandwich compression.1, 2, 3, 4,6, 7, 8,9,10 The expansion of the THV would have inevitably crushed the chimney stent against the rigid nitinol petals, leading to acute failure. BASILICA success is dependent on leaflet splaying, the lateral migration of lacerated segments into the sinuses.3 Our patient's anatomy presented insurmountable hurdles: the 1-mm valve-to-sinus distance represented an effaced sinus with no dead space, and the RCA takeoff was eccentric. A traditional longitudinal midline split would have been ineffective because the undisrupted portion of the leaflet would have remained in the path of flow on THV expansion.

Conclusions

The modified UNICORN maneuver with guide anchoring is designed to minimize the duration of the vulnerable window between crossing and deployment. By advancing the 6-F MPA1 guide into the LV immediately post-traversal, we created a stable anchor that bypassed the instability of wire exchanges.11 This maneuver successfully minimized procedural complexity, timing, and avoidance of possible associated complications during wire exchange, preventing the loss of access in complex root anatomy.
