Associations between sarcopenia and its developed status with chronic lung disease in the middle-aged and older Chinese people
Authors: Yi Zhang, Kemeng Zhang, Sui Huang, Mengchen Liu, Wenhan Li, Bijin Luo, Ping He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship between sarcopenia and chronic lung disease (CLD) has not been widely studied. We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) data to investigate the relationship between sarcopenia status and its developed status and CLD in the middle-aged and older Chinese people.MethodWe selected 4444 participants over the age of 45 from CHARLS in 2011. Sarcopenia status was defined according to the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia 2019 (AWGS 2019) criteria. CLD was defined as the presence of physician-diagnosed chronic lung disease. We retrospectively assessed their sarcopenia during 2011 and 2013, which were classified into never and newly developed sarcopenia, and these participants were followed from 2013 to 2018.ResultsDuring the 2011–2018 follow-up, 554 (12.4%) CLD events were identified. The new incidence rate of CLD in the sarcopenia group, newly developed sarcopenia group, low muscle mass alone group respectively 17.9% (54/302), 17.3% (39/226), and 13.8% (80/576). In the longitudinal analysis, individuals with sarcopenia [HR (95% CI): 1.468(1.068–2.017)] (P<0.01), newly developed sarcopenia [HR (95% CI): 1.486(1.031–2.141)] (P<0.05) and low muscle mass alone [HR (95% CI):1.555(1.174–2.059)] (P<0.01) were more likely to have new onset CLD.ConclusionSarcopenia, newly developed sarcopenia, and low muscle mass alone were associated with higher CLD risk among middle-aged and older Chinese adults. Therefore, early prevention and treatment of sarcopenia and avoiding its progression may help decrease the incidence of CLD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03192-4.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Sarcopenia conversion patterns, Chronic lung disease, Low muscle mass, Middle-aged and older Chinese people

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a serious older patients syndrome and a major public health problem for middle-aged and old people [1]. The 2014 consensus of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) defined sarcopenia as “age-related muscle mass loss, combined with low muscle strength and/or physical performance”, and established a threshold for each diagnostic component [2]. AWGS 2019 introduced “possible muscle loss syndrome”, defined as low muscle strength or physical performance, which is particularly suitable for primary healthcare or community-based health promotion to achieve early lifestyle interventions [3]. Sarcopenia is closely related to various adverse outcomes, including weakness, disability, poor quality of life, and increased mortality [4–7]. With the impact of the global aging wave, the incidence of sarcopenia will gradually increase. This will seriously affect the healthy quality of life of the older patients.

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is the third leading cause of death globally, and according to records from the World Health Organization, the incidence continued to rise by 2019 [8]. COPD Gene, an ongoing multicenter, observational study of the progression and genetic susceptibility to COPD, used the reduction of pectoralis muscle area (PMA) as a substitute for sarcopenia, and reported that the increase of PMA was related to the reduction of incidence rate and mortality in patients with mild to severe COPD [9]. A meta-analysis involving 2565 COPD patients showed a higher incidence of sarcopenia among COPD patients, with an overall incidence of 21.6% [10]. A meta-study of different national populations indicated that the overall prevalence of sarcopenia in COPD patients ranged from 15.5–34% [11]. In addition, a cross-sectional study showed that chronic bronchitis was associated with a high prevalence of sarcopenia [12]. A prospective study that monitored participants for four years demonstrated a significant association between COPD and new-onset sarcopenia [13], whereas a six-month follow-up study of patients with clinically stable COPD did not detect any appreciable rise in sarcopenia incidence [14]. However, most current research focuses on the prevalence of sarcopenia in CLD. There is relatively little research on the prevalence of CLD in sarcopenia. Therefore, further research is needed on the relationship between sarcopenia and CLD.

At present, the relationship between sarcopenia status and CLD is not yet clear, and there are few articles about the relationship between sarcopenia and CLD. This provides a prerequisite for us to explore the relationship between sarcopenia and CLD in the middle-aged and older Chinese people. In this study, we conducted a longitudinal analysis using nationally representative data from 2011 to 2018 of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to investigate the relationship between sarcopenia, its developed status and sarcopenia parameters with CLD in the middle-aged and older Chinese people.

Methods

Study population

CHARLS is a nationally representative prospective cohort study that covers 150 counties, 450 villages, over 10,000 households, and approximately 18,000 people aged 45 and above in China. The first round of the CHARLS national sample survey was conducted in 2011, and the second, third, fourth and fifth rounds were successfully conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020.

This study mainly reviewed data from 2011 to 2013. The inclusion criteria for the study are: (1) Individuals over 45 years old in CHARLS in 2011; (2) There is data on the description of sarcopenia status. The exclusion criteria are: (1) missing data on CHARLS sarcopenia status in 2011 and 2013; (2) People under the age of 45; (3) Lack of blood test data.

We used population data published in 2011 as a baseline for follow-up until 2018. People with sarcopenia data in 2011 and 2013 are considered to be the target population. Excluding people with a history of disease at baseline, we established the cohort in 2011 and followed it up to 2018 to obtain new incidence data on CLD. Assessment of sarcopenia status

Sarcopenia was evaluated according to the 2019 criteria of the Asian Sarcopenia Working Group (AWGS 2019), which includes three parts: appendicular skeletal muscle (ASM), muscle strength, and physical performance [3]. According to the AWGS 2019, low muscle strength is defined as a grip strength of less than 28 kg for males and less than 18 kg for females. ASM is estimated through anthropometric equations validated among Chinese residents [15]. There is research indicating strong consistency between the ASM equation model and the dual X-ray absorptiometer (DXA) [12]. The cut-off for defining low muscle mass was based on the sex-specific lowest 20% of the height-adjusted muscle mass (ASM/Ht2) among the study population. In this study, women with an ASM/Ht2 value of < 5.33 kg/m2 and men with an ASM/Ht2 value of < 7.01 kg/m2 were considered to have low muscle mass in the 2011 population. In 2013, the cut-off values for low muscle mass for women and men were 5.35 kg/m2 and 7.01 kg/m2, respectively. Low body performance is defined as 5 chair standing experiments for ≥ 12 s or gait speed < 1.0 m/s or the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) ≤ 9 [16]. Sarcopenia is diagnosed when low muscle mass plus low muscle strength or low physical performance are detected [11].

Assessment of CLD

The assessment of CLD is based on answering the question “Has the doctor told you that you have been diagnosed with CLD such as chronic bronchitis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and emphysema?” If the answer is “yes,” it is defined as having CLD.

Assessment of developed sarcopenia status

Combined the sarcopenia in 2011 and 2013, there were four potential conversion patterns of sarcopenia during that time:

Never sarcopenia: no sarcopenia in 2011 and no sarcopenia in 2013;

Never sarcopenia: no sarcopenia in 2011 and no sarcopenia in 2013;

Developed sarcopenia: no sarcopenia in 2011 and developed sarcopenia in 2013;

Developed sarcopenia: no sarcopenia in 2011 and developed sarcopenia in 2013;

Relieved sarcopenia: sarcopenia in 2011 and relieved sarcopenia in 2013;

Relieved sarcopenia: sarcopenia in 2011 and relieved sarcopenia in 2013;

Persistent sarcopenia: sarcopenia both in 2011 and in 2013.

Persistent sarcopenia: sarcopenia both in 2011 and in 2013.

Due to the small number of people in the third and fourth groups, we will only discuss the relationship between the second group and CLD.

Covariates

The covariates include: (1) Basic social information: age, gender, marital status (married and others); (2) Health status: body mass index (BMI), smoking and drinking status, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, cancer, liver disease, heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, digestive disease, psychiatric disease, memory-related diseases, asthma; (3) Blood test indicators: total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), CRP, creatinine, Cystatin C, glycated hemoglobin. BMI is divided into three groups: underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (BMI = 18.5 kg/m2 to 24 kg/m2), and overweight or obese (BMI ≥ 24 kg/m2).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are represented by mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median interquartile spacing, while categorical variables are represented by percentages. First, baseline characteristics of the longitudinal analysis samples were summarized based on sarcopenia status, and participants were compared using Chi-square tests, ANOVA, Tukey tests, or Kruskal-Wallis tests, as appropriate. Second, we used univariate Cox regression to analyze the relationship between variables and CLD, and included variables with P ≤ 0.05 into the multivariate Cox regression model. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of 2013 sarcopenia, 2011–2013 sarcopenia developed group and parameters of sarcopenia for the risk of new-onset CLD were estimated by Cox regression models and adjusted by covariates. Finally, we performed a mediation analysis to assess the direct and indirect association between sarcopenia and CLD. ALL statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and R version 3.5.1, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all cases.

Longitudinal association between sarcopenia parameters with new-onset risk of CLD at follow-up from 2011 to 2018

In the longitudinal analysis, we excluded 302 individuals with sarcopenia. Our final sample included a total of 4142 participants, among which 2473 (57.9%) had no sarcopenia parameters decreased, 576 (13.9%) had low muscle mass alone, 188 (4.5%) had low muscle strength, 750 (18.1%) had low physical performence, and 155 (3.7%) had the latter two. The new incidence of CLD for five groups was respectively 8.8% (218/2473), 13.8% (80/576), 9.5% (18/188), 10.6% (80/750), 13.5% (21/155). After adjusting for covariates in model 1–4, low muscle mass alone were an independent risk factors for CLD [HR (95% CI):1.555(1.174–2.059)], but low physical performance alone were not significantly associated with an increased risk of CLD [HR (95% CI): 0.998(0.748–1.333)]. After adjusting for covariates in model 1–2, low muscle strength with low physical performance had a higher risk of new CLD [HR (95% CI):1.555(1.026–2.359)], but low muscle strength was no significant difference [HR (95% CI):1.028(0.658–1.605)] (Table 4; Fig. 4). Discussion

This study suggests that the association of sarcopenia status and newly developed sarcopenia status with the risk of new CLD. The results of this study are as follows: (a) This study suggests that sarcopenia is an independent risk factor for new chronic CLD. (b) Developed sarcopenia are risk factors for new chronic lung disease compared to never sarcopenia. (c) Low muscle mass alone is an independent risk factor for chronic new lung disease. There may be a potential association between low muscle strength with low physical performance and new incidence of CLD.

Multiple cross-sectional studies have shown that the presence of sarcopenia is associated with a higher risk of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and that sarcopenia is associated with the severity and prognosis of COPD [17, 18]. In a study targeting Koreans, Chul HyunPark et al. [19] pointed out that skeletal muscle mass index is associated with decreased lung function in populations without obvious lung disease. In the study by Weng et al. [20] they used data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to investigate the relationship between skeletal muscle quality index (MQI) and lung function. The study showed that MQI was significantly correlated with forced vital capacity (FVC) and peak expiratory flow rate (PEF). As MQI increased, forced expiratory volume in the first 1.0 s (FEV1), FVC, and PEF seemed to increase. In addition, they also found a more significant relationship between MQI and lung function index in the older age group. Wang Jingmei [21] also reached a similar conclusion that compared with the no sarcopenia, the sarcopenia group showed a significant decrease in lung function (FEV1, FVC, PEF), and indicated a negative correlation between age and lung function related indicators, regardless of gender. A longitudinal study of Chinese people showed a significant association between sarcopenia and the development of chronic lung disease [22]. The results of these studies are similar to our study, although there are differences in the diagnostic criteria used for sarcopenia.

Some studies have found a correlation between limb muscle strength and respiratory muscle strength. Bahat et al. [23] showed a positive correlation between grip strength and maximum inspiratory and expiratory muscle strength. Some research has shown that exercise training can increase the skeletal muscle mass, grip strength, and PEF [24]. This also indirectly indicates a certain relationship between limb muscle strength and respiratory muscle strength as a whole. A study targeting old women living in Brazilian communities [25] found that maximum expiratory pressure (MEP) and handgrip strength (HGS) are independent factors for sarcopenia in the older patients, and suggested that simple muscle strength tests, including HGS and MEP, could be considered when identifying sarcopenia in old Brazilian community residents. As age increases, both respiratory muscles and skeletal muscles throughout the body will experience muscle atrophy. The Japan Rehabilitation Nutrition Association’s Japan Respiratory Muscle Deficiency Working Group defines respiratory muscle deficiency as “systemic sarcopenia and low respiratory muscle mass, accompanied by low respiratory muscle strength and/or low respiratory function.“ [26] These all indicate a close correlation between respiratory muscles and limb muscles. Some studies have shown that sarcopenia and CLD share common pathogenesis, including chronic inflammation [27, 28], oxidative stress [29] and mitochondrial dysfunction [28, 30].

This study has the following advantages: (a) It is a large sample study of middle-aged and older patients in China, providing evidence for the relationship between sarcopenia and CLD; (b) This study is different from other studies. Most studies have focused on the prevalence of sarcopenia in COPD, while few studies have examined the relationship between sarcopenia and CLD. The purpose of this study was to explore the relationship between sarcopenia and CLD, and to deepen the understanding of the relationship between sarcopenia and other diseases. (c) This study also explored the relationship between newly developed sarcopenia and the new-onset CLD, and found that the sarcopenia is an independent risk factor for the occurrence of CLD, and active management of sarcopenia can reduce the risk of CLD; (d) We also found that muscle mass loss alone was an independent risk factor for the development of CLD, suggesting that early onset of muscle loss in middle-aged and older adults may increase the risk of developing CLD and should be given attention early.

There are still some limitations to this study: (a) Due to many missing SPPB score data, there may be biases in the evaluation of sarcopenia; (b) Our method of determining CLD is mainly based on the Q&A on DA007 in the CHARLS database, which is a self-report of the respondents, and its accuracy may be affected by many factors; (c) Due to the lack of detailed classification data for CLD, we are unable to explore the relationship between sarcopenia and COPD; (d) The population we have chosen is mainly the middle-aged and older population in China, without involving populations and races from other countries. Further research is needed to determine whether this conclusion can be applied to other races. (e) The exclusion of participants with incomplete data may introduce selection bias.

In conclusion, our study shows that sarcopenia, its developed status, and low muscle mass alone are associated with a higher risk of developing CLD in middle-aged and older adults, which suggest that prevention and treatment of sarcopenia may be beneficial to reduce the incidence of CLD and promote healthy living among the middle-aged and older Chinese people.
