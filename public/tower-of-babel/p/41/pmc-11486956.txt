A 30-m annual grassland dataset from 1991 to 2020 for Inner Mongolia, China
Authors: Min Liu, Yue Qu, Jie Wang, Yixin Liao, Guotai Zheng, Yubin Guo, Luo Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Grassland is a widely distributed land use type that provides essential resources for livestock production and serves as a crucial ecosystem component. Over the past decades, grassland has significantly degraded and shrunk due to climate change and human activities, with ongoing changes in its area. This study utilized the Google Earth Engine (GEE) and the Res-UNet++ model to analyze the phenological and spectral characteristics of grasslands in Landsat images for long-term annual monitoring. Additionally, the LandTrendr algorithm was utilized to correct long-term time series data for grasslands, yielding a map of grassland distribution in Inner Mongolia from 1991 to 2020. The results indicate that the overall spatial accuracy from 1991 to 2020 exceeded 96%, with a Kappa coefficient over 0.92, demonstrating high monitoring accuracy. In particular, the 2019 grassland monitoring area showed high consistency with data from the Third National Land Survey (TNLS), with a coefficient of determination (R^2) reaching 0.97, reflecting the high accuracy and reliability.

Background & Summary

Grassland primarily consists of herbaceous plants and semi-shrubs with coverage exceeding 5%, whereas trees and shrubs are relatively sparse1. In China, grassland covers about 30% to 40% of the national territory, playing an integral role in maintaining ecological balance and supporting human livelihoods2,3. Inner Mongolia serves as a significant grassland region in China, where grassland resources are essential for the ecosystem and the livestock industry4. However, reckless reclamation and overgrazing have caused land desertification and a decline in grassland quality, thus creating a vicious cycle of short-term economic gains and grassland degradation thus creating a vicious cycle of short-term economic gains and grassland degradation5. This degradation urgently requires modern methods for monitoring and management to ensure the sustainable use of grassland resources.

In the 1980s, China used initial remote sensing technology and field surveys to create a 1:1,000,000 grassland resource map, but it has not been updated to reflect current changes5. With the accumulation of satellite images from Landsat, MODIS, Sentinel, and others, numerous updated land use and grassland mapping products have emerged. For example, the MCD12Q16 land cover dataset based on MODIS imagery and the grassland mapping in Tibet7, facilitated comprehensive low-spatial-resolution monitoring, whereas PROBA-V data facilitated grassland mapping in the Zambia region8. However, although low-spatial-resolution products perform well in spatial coverage, their low-spatial-resolution makes it difficult to effectively identify grasslands in fragmented landscapes and does not meet the practical needs of livestock farming7,9. Conversely, high-spatial-resolution datasets such as the World Cover land use/land cover dataset from Sentinel-2, the 10-metre resolution land cover dataset for China in 202010, and the first 1-metre resolution land cover map in China (SinoLC-1)11, provide more detailed information on grassland distribution. Although high-spatial-resolution products provide substantial spatial detail, their complex data processing and limited coverage challenge their use in large-scale, long-term grassland monitoring12. To address these issues, medium-to-high spatial resolution datasets such as GlobeLand30, the Annual Global Land Cover (AGLC)13 and the China Land Cover Dataset (CLCD)14, which are based on Landsat, balance broad spatial coverage with higher resolution, improving the accuracy of grassland detection.

Medium to high-spatial-resolution grassland mapping include three main types of datasets, each constructed by analysis of multi-temporal remote sensing images of the same region to accurately capture the spatial layout and trends of grassland changes. The first dataset type is based on single-temporal remote sensing images, where datasets are developed through the analysis of multi-band images annually to identify grassland features and monitor changes by comparing grassland distribution maps over consecutive years15. Factors such as cloud cover, cloud shadows, and snow can lead to spectral anomalies, causing identification errors and anomalies in long-term series16,17. Additionally, the spectral variability of grassland and their similarity to cultivated lands increase the uncertainty of classification results18,19. To reduce inconsistencies in inter-annual identification results and bolster the robustness of the method, it is necessary to thoroughly consider the connections between inter-annual changes across the complete time series.

The second dataset type is based on grassland identification results from a reference year, it explores changes in grassland in the target year and updates the annual grassland distribution maps based on these changes. By comparing the NDVI or other spectral indices between the reference year and subsequent years, changes in the grassland are determined20,21. If the spectral index in the target year exceeds a set threshold relative to the reference year, that pixel is identified as a changed area. Overlaying the change maps from all years facilitates the monitoring of annual changes in grassland22,23. This dataset mitigates the disadvantages of using single-temporal imagery for independent identification. However, the accuracy of this method greatly depends on the quality of the data from the reference year; any inaccuracies in the reference year can accumulate, thereby impacting the accuracy of the entire time series.

The third dataset type is constructed based on long-term trajectory monitoring. This dataset utilizes annual characteristic images to identify changes in grassland and assess the impacts of changes in neighboring years on current grassland status. Factors including sensor errors, cloud and shadow coverage, and mixed pixels may cause alternating changes between grassland and non-grassland states24. Grassland changes are typically slow and gradual, difficult to switch frequently in the short term, but influenced by disturbances such as fires, human intervention, and climate change. Therefore, long-term analysis of grassland evolution is crucial25,26. This dataset utilizes threshold segmentation and change vector analysis to identify and correct fluctuations between grassland and non-grassland states based on signal variations within distinct time windows27–30. Selecting an appropriate time window is essential, as a window that is too small may not capture long-term changes, while one that is excessively broad can introduce substantial noise, leading to overlapping change events and elevating the risk of erroneous assessments and confusion31,32.

In summary, creating datasets merely by overlaying annual identification results is unstable in long-term series grassland mapping. Although datasets relying on a specific reference year theoretically account for interannual fluctuations, their quality heavily depends on the accuracy of the reference year data. Over time, accumulated errors can reduce the effectiveness and utility of the entire dataset. Furthermore, although methods based on long-term series trajectories are theoretically more robust, they frequently encounter limitations in addressing complex long-term changes induced by human or natural processes, owing to the limited data duration. These issues not only impact the precision of the data, but also limit its potential for effective interannual change analysis.

Addressing the inadequacies that existing grassland datasets, particularly their lack of consideration for the continuity and rationality of interannual changes in long-term monitoring. This study has developed a new grassland dataset, this dataset is based on the correlation between grassland phenological characteristics and spectral features, effectively identifying key phenological periods and spectral characteristic indicators. Building upon the integrated spatial distribution features and temporal change patterns, this study proposes a long-term interannual monitoring algorithm for grasslands, integrating deep learning convolutional neural networks with the LandTrendr temporal segmentation technique. The algorithm has enabled continuous monitoring of Inner Mongolia grasslands from 1991 to 2020, significantly improving data quality and effectively eliminating unreasonable data fluctuations, thereby substantially enhancing the accuracy and reliability of time series analysis.

Methods

Study area

Inner Mongolia spans the northeastern, northern, and northwestern regions of China (97°12′~126°04′E, 37°24′~53°23′N), making it the provincial-level administrative region with the widest longitude range. Based on administrative and natural geographic characteristics, Inner Mongolia is divided into Western, Central, and Eastern Mongolia, covering about 118.3 million hectares or 12.3% of the total land area of China (Fig. 1). The average elevation of the region is between 1000 and 1200 meters, and it features a temperate continental climate with hot summers, cold winters, and ample sunshine. Annual precipitation ranges from around 700 mm in the east to just 10 mm in the west33. Inner Mongolia is rich in grassland resources, accounting for over 22% of the national pastures and serving as a crucial base for the livestock industry in China. The topography transitions from the forests of Greater Khingan in the east to grasslands and deserts in the west, supporting a diverse range of vegetation and favourable conditions for drought-resistant herbaceous plants. Furthermore, Inner Mongolia is situated at the climatic intersection of semi-humid and semi-arid regions, marked by a farming-pastoral ecotone with dynamically varying precipitation. Temporally, the agricultural and pastoral structures alternate with fluctuations, while spatially, farming and grazing territories are interwoven34.Fig. 1Study area in Inner Mongolia, the distribution of a farming-pastoral ecotone in North China and field survey area (each area includes a few samples).

Data production process

We collected Landsat images from 1991 to 2020 using the GEE cloud platform, excluded abnormal pixels, calculated spectral indices, identified key phenological periods for grassland detection, and synthesized images. By calculating key spectral indices and constructing time series curves of the spectra, we determined key phenological periods for grassland identification and synthesized images for these periods, based on comprehensive phenological and spectral analysis of grasslands and other land classes. Concurrently, using field survey data and the ESA World Cover Dataset 2020, typical landmarks were identified through manual visual interpretation to draw ground truth labels. These were combined with images from key phenological periods to generate grassland samples that were divided into training and testing groups. A temporal segmentation algorithm and its parameters were developed to conduct long-term series monitoring of grassland identification results and produce annual distribution maps of grasslands in Inner Mongolia from 1991 to 2020. The accuracy of the long-term annual grassland distribution maps was evaluated from spatial and area perspectives. Vegetation indices

To advance this study using high-quality Landsat images from 1991 to 2020, four key remote sensing indices were selected: the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), the Green Chlorophyll Vegetation Index (GCVI), the Bare Soil Index (BSI), and the Land Surface Water Index (LSWI). These indices serve as the primary spectral characteristics for identifying grasslands in Inner Mongolia. NDVI, a widely used index, measures vegetation growth and coverage by comparing differences between NIR and Red wavelengths39–41. GCVI has a linear relationship with vegetation chlorophyll content and Leaf Area Index (LAI), exhibiting high sensitivity and effectively reflecting vegetation growth conditions, while not being affected by the saturation of dense vegetation LAI42,43. BSI assesses land exposure by measuring the brightness of bare soil. As the brightness increases, the BSI value becomes higher44,45. LSWI measures vegetation water content by reflecting differences between NIR and SWIR wavelengths, higher values indicate more water content46–48. Field survey and sample data

To understand the spectral characteristics of Inner Mongolia, this study conducted field surveys in Hulunbuir City in Eastern Mongolia, Hohhot City in Central Mongolia, and Baotou and Bayannur Cities in Western Mongolia. The surveys were exclusively conducted from July to September during 2022 and 2023. Data collection, involving on-site photography and consultation, yielded a total of 144 sample points. There were 79 grassland sample points and 65 non-grassland sample points. 94 sample points were distributed in Hulunbuir City, where grass grows most vigorously, while 38 sample points were distributed in Baotou City and Hohhot City. 12 sample points were distributed in Bayannur City (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Distribution of field surveys.

Distribution of field surveys.

Field survey is indispensable for grassland identification. Firstly, on-site surveys facilitate the direct collection of information about grassland shape and texture, supporting subsequent visual interpretation and sample validation. Secondly, although the spectral characteristics of land cover in remote sensing imagery may appear similar, the ground-truth information obtained through field data can significantly reduce misclassification. Additionally, field consultations contribute to understanding the growth cycles and physiological characteristics of grasslands, thereby enhancing the analysis of spectral features and interannual variations. During field surveys in Inner Mongolia, we used 2020 Landsat-8 imagery, ESA World Cover dataset47 and high-resolution Google Earth imagery to collect key grassland sampling points to create a feature image library for grassland types in the study area. Considering the vast expanse of Inner Mongolia, which largely consists of sparsely populated deserts and forests beyond grasslands, relying solely on field surveys or interpretation of remote sensing images is insufficient to obtain numerous, accurate samples. Therefore, we used images from the key phenological periods for grassland detection in 2020 and the established feature image library to create a 2020 grassland sample database with 3877 images through visual interpretation and manual delineation method.

To ensure diversity and rationality in data during model training, Inner Mongolia was divided into 1° × 1° grid units, with eight grids selected to uniformly cover various land types including forest, grassland, and farmland, effectively representing regional land use. To enhance efficiency, each grid was divided into 128 × 128 pixel slices with a 2% overlap, ensuring data consistency during model training and reducing GPU resource consumption (Fig. 5). Additionally, the K-Fold method was used to divide the dataset into five folds. In each iteration, one portion of the data was used as the testing dataset, and the other four as the training dataset. This process was repeated five times to ensure comprehensive data utilization and a stable evaluation of model performance49.Fig. 5Distribution of training sample.

Phenological characteristics and time-Series spectral analysis

Spectral data were collected throughout 2020, and all spectral indices were normalized. By analysing the smoothed time series of spectral data, we revealed distinct differences in the spectral curves across various land use types at the original bands. As illustrated in Fig. 6, our comprehensive analysis of the phenological characteristics and time series spectra for six land use types revealed distinct vegetation cover differences during the peak growth period (days 100 to 250), attributable to varied phenological traits. During this peak period, the NDVI and GCVI indices both reached their maximum values nearly simultaneously. Particularly, the advantage of GCVI in avoiding saturation from dense canopy LAI led us to define the peak of GCVI as the “Vegetation Peak Period.” Forests reached their growth peak around day 200, exhibiting average GCVI and NDVI values of approximately 0.4 and 0.8, respectively. Cropland and grassland peaked around day 240, with croplands recording average GCVI and NDVI values of 0.3 and 0.7, respectively, while grassland showed lower values at 0.2 and 0.6, below those of forest, built-up, other land, and water. Therefore, during this period, NDVI and GCVI enabled significant differentiation of grassland from forest, built-up, other land, and water, and to some extent, from cropland as well. Owing to varying water content in vegetation, LSWI significantly distinguished grassland from water, forest, and croplands. Similarly, BSI, which reflects different levels of vegetation bareness, clearly differentiated grassland from other land types, cropland, forest, and water.Fig. 6Spectral curves of various land use types derived from all available Landsat imagery.

Spectral curves of various land use types derived from all available Landsat imagery.

Classification algorithm

The semantic characteristics of grassland in the study area exhibit complex semantic characteristics, comprising both extensive, continuous areas and numerous interspersed patches among cropland, desert, and forest. These grasslands have irregular shapes that often blend with other land types, lacking distinct boundaries. Additionally, the spectral characteristics of grasslands closely resemble those of croplands. Therefore, we introduce a deep learning model named Res-UNet++, which integrates the complex spectral and semantic features of grasslands to extract characteristic information at various levels and scales. The Res-UNet++ model utilizes the original encoder-decoder structure of UNet and enhances it by incorporating residual modules, improving the original UNet++ to more effectively integrate feature information across scales and reduce information loss50,51. Figure 8a illustrates the framework of the grassland recognition network using the Res-UNet++ model, which features a five-layer deep structure with four downsampling layers52,53. To address the issue of gradient vanishing with increasing network depth, this study incorporated residual modules inspired by the ResNet architecture (Fig. 8b), enhancing feature extraction capabilities and mitigating gradient loss via identity mapping. To bolster attention to spatial and channel features, the csSE-Block spatial-channel attention module is introduced, enhancing the model perceptual ability regarding various features (Fig. 8c). Furthermore, the model utilizes deep supervision to calculate feature loss at the output of each 3 × 3 deconvolution layer. This approach accounts for feature loss at varying semantic levels, thereby speeding up model convergence and enhancing prediction accuracy.Fig. 8The overall framework of the Res-UNet++ model.

The overall framework of the Res-UNet++ model.

Algorithm for long-time-series grassland correction

The acquisition of high-quality observational data for grassland identification is impeded by winter snow cover and summer cloudiness or rainfall, complicating the maintenance of seasonal consistency in annual imagery of the same location. Atypical spectral features of grassland during non-growing seasons, combined with image noise and mixed-pixel boundary issues, contribute to unstable identification results over long time series, leading to unreasonable shifts between grassland and non-grassland areas. Therefore, we proposed a time-segmented long-term grassland monitoring algorithm that corrects annual grassland predictions to address these variations and eliminate unreasonable interannual discrepancies in identification results, achieving consistent long-term interannual mapping of grassland.

The LandTrendr algorithm analyzes annual Landsat time series imagery to detect surface changes, utilizing subtle difference detection and trend fitting methods to effectively eliminate noise and track vegetation cover changes. This algorithm operates independently of predefined models, relying on long-term data and using linear segment fitting to precisely model spectral trajectories, preserving detailed information while filtering out noise24. Initially, the LandTrendr algorithm was utilized to study disturbance and recovery trends in forest. Subsequently, it was widely applied to track changes and recoveries in vegetation, water, and built-up24,54–56. Therefore, improvements have been made to the LandTrendr algorithm to utilize its ability to detect sudden and gradual changes, and a long-term grassland correction algorithm has been introduced. This enhancement comprehensively optimizes the accuracy of grassland predictions and effectively corrects unreasonable changes.

The LandTrendr algorithm functions by initially modelling time series data of grassland prediction probabilities into multiple linear segments, thereby eliminating significant annual fluctuations due to noise. This process is iterated until fluctuations fall below a designated threshold, ensuring the data remains within a reasonable variation range. A residual criterion identifies spikes, while regression analysis employs the start and end years of the time series as segmentation points. The point exhibiting the highest residual in the regression results is chosen as the next segmentation point. Regression calculations are repeatedly applied to segments on both sides of each vertex, focusing further regression on those with higher mean squared errors (MSE), until the segment count decreases to below a predetermined threshold. If the number of potential vertices exceeds the threshold, the vertex with the smallest angle is eliminated to refine the set of potential vertices. The ultimately determined vertices are regarded as indicators of potential changes between grassland and non-grassland areas. Fitting between vertices is achieved by directly connecting points or using regression-based methods, with the weakest vertices removed to simplify the model and smooth the time series, thus capturing authentic disturbances from abnormal spikes. Throughout the fitting process, p-values are computed to prevent overfitting, and the model with the lowest p-value is selected as optimal, ensuring it accurately represents the trends in grassland probability changes within the study area. Data validation method

This study evaluates the accuracy of the distribution map in Inner Mongolia, utilizing 30 × 30 m pixels as sampling units. Stratified random sampling based on interannual mapping results was performed, resulting in the calculation and random generation of 377 grassland sample points and 447 non-grassland sample points from predicted grassland and non-grassland areas respectively (Fig. 10). The reliability of the algorithm based solely on one year of accuracy validation results is not precise. Hence, the validation process was repeated every five years over a 30-year period. These validations were based on Landsat and Google Earth imagery, combined with field surveys and image interpretation markers, and sample point validations were completed every five years. Based on the validation results, confusion matrices for each year were compiled, and evaluation metrics were calculated, including Overall Accuracy (OA), User’s Accuracy (UA), Producer’s Accuracy (PA), and the Kappa coefficient, to assess the spatial accuracy of the grassland monitoring results.Fig. 10Spatial distribution of validation sample points.

Data Records

The grassland datasets for Inner Mongolia in China spanning from 1991 to 2020 are named by year and stored in GeoTIFF format. With a spatial resolution of 30 meters, these non-dimensional datasets allow for scalable analysis. They can be imported into remote sensing applications (e.g., ENVI) and GIS platforms (e.g., ArcGIS) for further analysis. Grassland delineation and classification follow the industry standard NY/T 2997-2016. Detailed information on these datasets is available for download at 10.6084/m9.figshare.25709268.v258.

Annual maps of grassland from 1991 to 2020

Based on a long-term time series monitoring algorithm, we generated grassland distribution maps of Inner Mongolia covering 1991 to 2020, depicted in five-year intervals (Fig. 11). Analysis shows that grasslands primarily cluster in central and southeastern Inner Mongolia, mirroring precipitation patterns, indicating a strong correlation between grassland vegetation distribution and rainfall. Specifically, in Western Mongolia, grasslands are extensively found around Ordos City, while in Bayannur City and Baotou City, they are more fragmented. In Central Mongolia, grasslands are most concentrated, especially in Xilingol League and Hohhot. These areas, located on the flat terrain of the Inner Mongolian Plateau, receive ample rainfall influenced by marine air currents, fostering grassland growth. Ulanqab City, positioned at the transitional zone between grasslands and deserts, features a scattered grassland distribution. In Eastern Mongolia, grasslands mainly occupy the western parts of Hulunbuir City, while in Hinggan League and Tongliao City, they are highly fragmented and intermingled with forests and croplands.Fig. 11Grassland maps of Inner Mongolia every 5 years from 1991 to 2020.

Grassland maps of Inner Mongolia every 5 years from 1991 to 2020.

Over the past 30 years, grasslands across three regions of Inner Mongolia have undergone various changes. In Western Mongolia, the grasslands of Ordos City have continuously expanded, especially westward. Conversely, the grasslands in Baotou City contracted from 1991 to 2005 but expanded from 2005 to 2020. In Alxa League, the grasslands have also begun to expand since 2015, particularly in the southern regions. In the Central Mongolia region, the grasslands in Xilingol League and Ulanqab City have seen significant increases since 2005, while those in Hohhot have shown little change. In Eastern Mongolia, the grasslands in Hulunbuir City have expanded eastward, with significant increases in the Greater Khingan Range since 2005. Hinggan League witnessed expansion in 2000, whereas Tongliao City and Chifeng City have experienced a decline in grassland areas.

Interannual Changes in grassland area from 1991 to 2020

Figure 12 shows the annual total grassland area and the annual increased and decreased areas of grassland in Inner Mongolia from 1991 to 2020. Over the past 30 years, the grassland area in Inner Mongolia has exhibited a trend of gradual recovery. From 1991 to 2005, fluctuations in grassland area were primarily driven by climatic factors. During the 1990s, nearly half of the decade faced drought conditions under global warming, severely impacting grassland vegetation growth. Droughts led to reduced vegetation and gradual desertification. Although the total grassland area did not significantly expand during this period, annual fluctuations were evident due to climatic variability. Grassland identification area accuracy assessment

To assess the reliability of the grassland distribution maps from this study, we analyzed grassland areas across 12 prefectural-level divisions in Inner Mongolia for 2019, using the maps alongside administrative division maps. This analysis was compared with data from TNLS for the same year (Fig. 13a). Results indicate that Xilingol League possesses the largest grassland area, at 1,798,670 hectares, while Wuhai has the smallest, at 4,340 hectares, closely matching TNLS findings. Hulunbuir and Ordos cities have grassland areas exceeding 500,000 hectares each, ranking second and third in the region respectively. However, significant discrepancies were noted between the mapping results and TNLS for Alxa League, with TNLS reporting 903,470 hectares, but the mapping showing only 126,080 hectares. This discrepancy may stem from the location of Alxa League in the harsh desert regions of western Inner Mongolia, where sparse vegetation yields less distinct spectral features in remote sensing imagery. According to the grassland interpretation rules of this study, such sporadically distributed desert grasslands are not cl-assified as grassland. Bayannur and Wuhai, located at the interface between desert grasslands and deserts, also showed lower areas in the mapping results compared to TNLS. Overall, except for Wuhai, Alxa League, and Bayannur, the grassland area statistics for the other nine regions in Inner Mongolia were slightly higher than those reported in TNLS. This difference is primarily due to the varying definitions of grassland between our study and TNLS; sparse forested grassland was classified as grassland based on the industry standard NY/T 2997-2016, while TNLS classified it as forest. Additionally, the methods of investigation differ; our study relies on field surveys and manual delineation of grassland, whereas TNLS employs both indoor and outdoor surveys, emphasizing on-site verification. Discussion of limitation and future work

This study utilized Landsat data and the GEE platform to develop a long-term, interannual monitoring algorithm for grasslands in Inner Mongolia, producing a grassland dataset spanning from 1990 to 2020. Due to limitations such as data quality and manpower costs, there is some uncertainty in the study that needs improvement in the following areas: (1) This study used Landsat-4/5/7/8 images sourced from the Landsat Collection 2 Level-2 products on Google Earth Engine. The selected images underwent radiometric calibration and high-precision orthorectification, with inter-calibration performed across different Landsat sensors. However, due to the limited amount of high-quality observational data from Landsat-4/5, and the striping issues with Landsat-7, the identification results are still somewhat affected by data quality. In the future, imagery from different sensors could be incorporated, and a small amount of noise could be introduced to improve accuracy. Meanwhile, we plan to integrate additional sensor imagery into the retraining process and refine the data processing and validation workflows to further bolster the robustness and consistency of the model across different sensor applications. (2) Firstly, the visual interpretation and manual delineation processes are prone to subjective biases, which could potentially lead to inaccuracies in identifying land cover types, thereby possibly compromising the precision of the sample data. Secondly, the intricate topography and heterogeneous vegetation in the study area, especially in the desert and forest regions beyond the grasslands, present significant challenges in acquiring a sufficient number of accurate samples via field surveys or remote sensing imagery, potentially leading to insufficient sample representativeness. (3) The spectral characteristics of grasslands, farmlands, and deserts in Inner Mongolia are similar and easily confused. Future studies could incorporate information such as texture, temperature, and precipitation to avoid confusion and clearly delineate the boundaries between grasslands and deserts.

Usage Notes

We developed a deep learning and time-segmentation-based algorithm using Landsat time-series data and the GEE platform for long-term monitoring of interannual changes in the grassland of Inner Mongolia. Our algorithm has generated a continuous long-term time series dataset essential for monitoring grassland ecosystems. This method enhances data continuity and accuracy, making it particularly suitable for the analysis of complex interannual changes. However, variations in classification standards can influence grassland definitions and data interpretation. We recommend users consider these differences when utilizing this data. Different classification standards can result in significant variations in monitoring results. Accurate understanding and application of these standards are crucial for precise data interpretation and providing a scientific basis for managing and sustainably utilizing grassland resources in Inner Mongolia.
