Cutaneous Collagenous Vasculopathy: Report of First Korean Case and Review of the Literature
Authors: Dea Kwan Yun, Kyujin Yeom, Uri Shon, Byung Cheol Park, Mi Soo Choi, Myung Hwa Kim
Published: 2023

Dear Editor:

Cutaneous collagenous vasculopathy (CCV) is rare microangiopathy1. Few cases have been reported among Asians.

A 60-year-old man presented with a 10-year history of symmetrically well-demarcated, telangiectatic patches without any symptoms (Fig. 1A, B). These patches appeared on the anterior chest, but they gradually progressed to affect his back, arms, and face. Sun exposure exacerbated the lesions. His past medical history included hypertension, for which he had been taking telmisartan and amlodipine. His family history was negative for telangiectasias. Dermoscopy revealed dilated vascular structures, appearing as red dots and serpentine vessels (Fig. 1C, D). Histopathologic examination revealed vessel dilatation and marked thickening of only superficial blood vessel walls (Fig. 2A). There were scanty lymphocyte-dominant inflammatory infiltrations around vessel walls without other vasculopathic changes, such as red blood cell (RBC) extravasation, fibrinoid necrosis and leukocytoclasia (Fig. 2B). There were amorphous, eosinophilic materials on the vessel walls, which were highlighted with D-PAS stain and Masson-trichrome (Fig. 2C, D). These findings allowed us to diagnose CCV.

CCV is a rare primary vascular disorder that involves the development of idiopathic generalized telangiectasias without systemic involvement. Typically, the clinical features are described in the progressive development of well-demarcated telangiectasias that affect the lower extremities and spread over the trunk without mucosa1,2,3. As in our case, there were rarer cases affected only in the upper body3.

On dermoscopy, CCV is characterized mainly by vascular structures appearing as red dots, serpentine vessels, comma-like vessels, and reddish patches. Dermoscopic findings correspond histopathologically with dilated papillary blood vessels4. Histopathological examination is essential for the diagnosis of CCV. The main finding is dilated superficial blood vessels with thickened walls due to the deposition of eosinophilic hyaline material. This material is highlighted with PAS diastase, iron colloidal stain, and Masson trichrome. CCV can be associated with minimal lymphocyte infiltration within the affected blood vessels1.

The etiopathogenesis of CCV is unknown, but it is associated with damage to the microvasculature. Like our patient, one reported patient suffered an extension of telangiectatic lesions, which was more noticeable in the summer2. Sun exposure is a possible risk factor. About 62% of CCV patients had comorbidities with cardiovascular and related disease, such as hypertension, atrial fibrillation, myocardial infarction, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes mellitus3,5. Cardiovascular disease induce ultrastructural changes of cutaneous vessels, and such changes potentially contribute to the development of CCV. Many CCV patients take statins and beta-blockers. So, it is impossible to rule out the role of drugs3.

CCV lesions are usually left untreated, and the lack of treatment is not associated with any non-cosmetic negative effects. However, 585-nm pulsed-dye laser have been reported with some success5.

CCV could be underreported because its clinical features are similar to those of generalized essential telangiectasia (GET), hereditary benign telangiectasia (HBT), and hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT). But unlike CCV, HBT, and HHT are autosomal dominant disorders, so excluded clinically by family history. Also, GET and HHT sometimes involve mucosal membranes. Histologically, they show dilatation of the capillaries and postcapillary venules without collagen deposition in the vessel walls5.

In conclusion, this was the first Korean case of CCV, highlighting its clinical findings, etiopathogenesis, histologic findings, and differential diagnosis.
