Implicit Bias and Recognition of Biases Among Vascular Surgeons
Authors: Kerry A Howard, Brian Witrick, Ashley Clark, Karen Atkinson, Avery Morse, Pranav Kapoor, Katharine L McGinigle, Samantha Minc, Olamide Alabi, Caitlin W Hicks, Andrew Gonzalez, Crystal W Cené, Samuel Cykert, Corey A Kalbaugh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:Implicit bias can influence behavior and decision-making, such as treatment decisions in clinical settings. Given documented Black—White disparities in vascular care, the purpose of this study was to describe the prevalence and degree of implicit bias and recognition of bias among vascular surgeons.Methods:The sample included vascular surgeons in the Vascular Quality Initiative. Participants completed a survey of demographic questions, race implicit association test, and six bias recognition questions. Implicit bias was categorized as no preference, or slight, moderate, or strong pro-White or pro-Black. We stratified implicit and bias recognition findings by physician race/ethnicity, sex, years of experience, and region; and examined the relationship between implicit bias and bias recognition.Results:The survey was completed by 304 participants. Most participants (71.6%) showed a pro-White bias with 73.0% of this group in moderate and strong categories. Bias magnitude varied based on physician race/ethnicity, years of experience, and region. Although 77.5% of respondents showed recognition of biases, of those whose results showed lack of recognition, 67.8% had moderate or strong bias. Women were more likely than men to report recognition of biases and of the potential impact of bias on decision-making.ConclusionsMost people have some level of implicit bias, developed from early life reinforcements, social stereotypes, and learned experiences. However, these are important findings in a profession that takes care of a high proportion of individuals from structurally vulnerable groups. Given the lack of association between implicit bias and recognition of biases, recognition may be an important first step to minimize documented disparities.Keywords: Bias recognition, Implicit bias, Peripheral artery disease, Quality of care

Introduction

Racial disparities in health care and resultant poor outcomes have been documented for patients suffering from peripheral artery disease (PAD).1 Patients with PAD represent a vulnerable cohort of patients with high comorbidity burden, management that requires a complex interplay of medical and interventional specialties and decision-making, and high risk for amputation and early mortality.2-8 Although all people with PAD are at a high risk of poor outcomes, patients from minoritized communities represent a structurally vulnerable group whose PAD-related care may be a result of intersecting social, cultural, and structural hierarchies, as opposed to individual-level behaviors.9 Studies focused on PAD-related care delivered to Black and White patients have shown that Black patients are offered different procedures than White patients, including a lower incidence of limb-sparing revascularizations and a higher incidence of primary limb amputations (i.e., amputation without attempt at revascularization).5 In addition, Black patients with PAD have a 2-5 times greater lifetime risk of lower extremity amputation than White patients.4 Black patients are also less likely to receive guideline-directed medical therapy such as antiplatelet medications and lipid-lowering therapy.10 Vascular surgery represents a specialty that can offer the full complement of treatments for PAD, from guideline-directed medical therapy to peripheral revascularization and lower limb amputation. Given that factors that could reasonably explain disparities, such as disease severity and access to care, are examined and disparities remain 11, it is critical to consider the ways that social and structural factors may influence decision-making of vascular surgeons.

The etiology of these disparities in care is unclear; however, one potential contributing factor to inequities is implicit bias, defined as an automatic bias that may influence behavior, cognitive processes, and decision-making.12,13 As described in several recent systematic reviews, health care providers have implicit biases that may impact the delivery of health care.13-16 Notably, physicians often rate Black patients more negatively (less intelligent, less compliant, etc.) than White patients.17 In fact, Black patients report lower ratings of care when their clinician has stronger pro-White biases, suggesting noticeable differences in the delivery of care based on bias.18 Furthermore, issues of bias may be further compounded by a physicians’ lack of recognition because an individual’s implicit biases can be outside of their awareness.13 Prior studies have shown that conscious biases and implicit biases are often not associated with one another.19,20 Although studies have examined whether or not physicians admit to explicit biases,21,22 intervention for implicit bias may require recognition of bias and its potential to influence decision-making, particularly among physicians tasked with managing care for structurally vulnerable patients with PAD. Implicit bias and recognition of bias remain an under-investigated potential factor in PAD-related Black—White treatment and outcome differences.23

Given evidenced differences in care based on race, including notable differences among patients with PAD, we conducted a descriptive study to examine the prevalence and degree of implicit bias and recognition of biases among vascular surgeons. We hypothesized that there would be implicit White preferences among surgeons, as well as lack of recognition of such biases.

Materials and Methods

Study sample and source population

The Society for Vascular Surgery Vascular Quality Initiative (VQI) is a national vascular procedure-based registry24 intended to document and improve the quality of care delivered to patients with vascular diseases. All clinicians that deliver vascular care in the United States are eligible to participate in the VQI. As of June 2024, the VQI includes 1028 sites and has collected over 1.2 million procedures. The VQI has 18 regional quality groups across the Unites States: South (5), Northeast (4), Midwest (4), and West (5). Centers include academic medical centers, teaching hospitals, community hospitals, and private practice sites.

Recruitment strategy

We invited all 2512 vascular surgeons who practice at VQI participating centers to take the race Implicit Association Test (IAT)25 to categorize their level of implicit bias. Participation was voluntary and all participants provided informed consent before starting the survey. Vascular surgeons who consented to participate in the study were able to take the survey on any digital device with a keyboard or a touch screen. A $100 gift card was offered as an incentive for completion of the survey. The survey remained open for 12 mo and no duplicate participants were identified. The institutional review boards at Clemson University (IRB 2020-096) and Indiana University (IRB 16325) approved this study. All data were administered and stored on a secure Secure Sockets Layer (SSL)-encrypted website managed by Project Implicit, the company that created the survey.

Survey components

The survey included three components: physician demographics, the race IAT, and bias recognition questions.

Demographics

Survey participants were asked to provide their age, sex assigned at birth (male or female), gender identity (male, female, trans male/trans man, trans female/trans woman, genderqueer/gender nonconforming, or a different identify), race, ethnicity (Hispanic, non-Hispanic, or other), years of experience (0-10, 11-20, and more than 20 y), and the VQI regional group to which they contribute. For the purposes of this study, self-identified race and ethnicity categories were combined into Hispanic, non-Hispanic Asian, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and non-Hispanic other. Participants were also asked to identify their specialty and data analyses were restricted to vascular surgeons.

Race implicit association test

Created in 1998, the race IAT (developed by Project Implicit) is a validated instrument that measures the degree of implicit bias a participant has for individuals who are European American (White) or African American (Black).26 Participants are shown a series of White and Black individuals as well as “good” and “bad” words and are then asked to group the individuals shown with each word presented on the screen as quickly as possible. The underpinning of the IAT is that participants will match a group (images of Black or White Americans) to a characteristic, evaluation, or stereotype (“good” and “bad” words such as excellent and angry, respectively) more quickly if there is already an automatic connection. The IAT calculates the difference in reaction time between two blocks of the trial. A positive score indicates pro-White bias, whereas a negative score indicates pro-Black bias. Based on previously established guidelines,15,27 scores are reported as “no bias,” “slight bias,” “moderate bias,” or “strong bias,” depending on the difference in reaction times.

Bias recognition measure

Following completion of the IAT, six self-administered survey questions were used to measure participant recognition of biases: “I am biased,” “Everyone, including me, has biases toward other people,” “Unwanted biases probably influence my decisions about other people,” “Unwanted bias may affect the way I make decisions without my realizing it,” “Unwanted biases may influence my perception of the world around me,” and “Unwanted bias may influence my interactions with other people.” Each question included strongly agree, moderately agree, slightly agree, slightly disagree, moderately disagree, strongly disagree, and prefer not to answer as response options. The bias recognition measure has been used in previous studies and shows high internal reliability (α = 0.91).28

Statistical analysis

Based on IAT results, physicians were grouped according to one of seven categories based on the degree of implicit bias: no bias, slight bias toward White, moderate bias toward White, strong bias toward White, slight bias toward Black, moderate bias toward Black, and strong bias toward Black. We also examined both IAT results and bias recognition results by strata of physician race/ethnicity, physician sex, and physician years of experience. In these analyses, IAT results were grouped in two ways. The first grouping was based on the direction of bias: pro-White bias, pro-Black bias, and no bias. The second grouping was based on the magnitude of the association: no bias, slight bias, moderate bias, and strong bias. Cutoff points for the magnitude of implicit bias were consistent with those that been used in prior literature.26,29 Bias recognition results were grouped into three categories based on the majority of a participant’s answers: generally agree, generally disagree, and a combination of agree and disagree. Regional VQI group were grouped to reflect the US Census Regions (Northeast, Midwest, West, and South). For this descriptive study, chi-square analyses tested the distribution of bias results across strata. We used Spearman correlation to test for an association between implicit bias magnitude and bias recognition, as well as chi-square for the distribution of implicit bias results across two levels of recognition: average of recognition answers and average of lack of recognition answers.

To reduce the risk of nonresponse biasing the results, data were weighted before analysis based on demographic characteristics of the vascular surgeons in the VQI sampling frame.23 Surveys often face the problem of low response rates, which can bias results and affect the representativeness of the study to the population of interest.30-32 In an effort to minimize the potential impact of nonresponse bias, we applied a weighting process consistent with documented survey-handling procedures which include studies that use survey methodology with surgeons.23,32-34 In addition, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using unweighted data and results closely aligned with the weighted analyses. Further details on weighting and the results of the sensitivity analysis can be found in the Supplementary Material. All analyses were run using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Among 2512 VQI-participating vascular surgeons, 368 (14.6%) physicians started the survey and 304 (12.1%) physicians completed the entire survey, including obtaining results from the IAT. The average age of participants was 44 y. The majority of the sample were non-Hispanic White (61.8%), male (66.8%), and early in their career (0-10 y of experience, 62.2%). Although the survey asked about both sex and gender and provided options other than sex assigned at birth for gender, all respondents selected their sex assigned at birth as their gender. The ethnoracial distribution of participants included non-Hispanic Black (4.6%), Hispanic (4.6%), non-Hispanic Asian (23.0%), and individuals who identified as another non-Hispanic race (5.9%). The most represented region of the United States was South (39.8%), followed by Midwest (24.3%), Northeast (19.7%), and West (16.1%). Overall implicit bias and recognition results

We found that 18.8% (n = 57) of vascular surgeons had no bias for Black or White individuals. The majority, 71.6% (n = 218), of physicians had a pro-White bias and 9.7% (n = 29) had a pro-Black bias. Of the approximately (due to weighting) 218 vascular surgeons with a pro-White bias, 27.0% were found to have a slight bias, 45.3% had a moderate bias, and 27.7% had a strong bias for White compared to Black individuals. Of the approximately 29 surgeons with a pro-Black bias, 36.3% were found to have a slight bias, 52.7% had a moderate bias, and 11.0% had a strong bias for Black compared to White individuals. Overall, 39.2% tended to show strong recognition of biases and the potential for biases to affect decision-making, whereas 10.3% tended to strongly indicate lack of recognition, and 50.5% gave mixed responses. Based on the average of a person’s responses, 77.5% generally showed recognition and 22.5% generally showed lack of recognition. Implicit bias and recognition by physician race and ethnicity

Based on results of the IAT, the majority of Hispanic (64.5%), Non-Hispanic Asian (75.0%), and Non-Hispanic White (73.1%) physicians had an implicit pro-White bias. Non-Hispanic Black physicians had a more even distribution across pro-White (32.5%), pro-Black (26.8%), and no bias (40.8%). There was a statistically significant difference in the distribution of IAT result magnitude (none, slight, moderate, strong) across physician race/ethnicity categories (χ2 = 47.44, P = 0.003). Non-Hispanic Black physicians were most likely to have no bias (40.8%), followed by a moderate bias (31.0%), slight bias (21.1%) and strong bias (7.8%). Hispanic (74.7%), Non-Hispanic Asian (37.2%), and Non-Hispanic White (36.6%) physicians were most likely to have moderate bias. The majority of physicians identifying as Non-Hispanic Other had a slight bias (50.8%). Strong bias was the least likely finding for all races/ethnicities except Non-Hispanic White, for whom it was second most likely (24.5%). Non-Hispanic White physicians were also least likely of physician race/ethnic categories to have no bias (17.5%). There was no evidence of a difference in the distribution of bias recognition results by physician race/ethnicity (χ2 = 8.03, P = 0.431).

Implicit bias and recognition by physician sex

Male (71.7%) and female (70.1%) vascular surgeons were both highly likely to have a pro-White bias. We found no evidence of a detectable differences in implicit bias magnitude by surgeon sex (χ2 = 1.10, P = 0.777). Male and female surgeons were similarly likely to have no bias (18.9% versus 17.6%), slight bias (22.3% versus 27.4%), moderate bias (37.2% versus 40.4%), and strong bias (21.6% versus 14.6%). There was a difference in recognition of bias (χ2 = 6.22, P = 0.044) by surgeon sex. Female vascular surgeons (89.1%) were more likely than male vascular surgeons (76.1%) to generally agree with the bias recognition questions, indicating greater recognition of the existence of bias and the effect of bias on decision-making.

Implicit bias and recognition by physician years of experience

The results showed a statistically significant difference in magnitude of implicit bias by physician years of experience (χ2 = 24.36, P = 0.004). Physicians, regardless of years of experience, were most likely to have a moderate bias (0-10 y: 36.1%; 11-20 y: 39.3%; more than 20 y: 37.5%). Those with more than 20 y of experience showed less likelihood of no bias (13.1%) compared to 0-10 y (21.5%) and 11-20 y (23.3%). Those with 11-20 y of experience showed least likelihood of strong bias (5.7%) compared to 0-10 (23.7%) and more than 20 y (29.4%). There was no evidence of a difference in bias recognition between groups of years of experience (χ2 = 4.82, P = 0.307).

Implicit bias and recognition by physician region of practice

Across all regions, most vascular surgeons had an implicit pro-White bias (Northeast: 72.2%; Midwest: 82.4%; West: 71.8%; South: 63.7%). For Midwest (15.9%), West (21.9%), and South (22.7%), this was followed by no bias; Northeast had higher pro-Black directionality (16.5%) than no bias. There was a statistically significant difference in the distribution of implicit bias magnitude by region (χ2 = 19.35, P = 0.022). Physicians in the Midwest were most likely to have a slight bias (36.7%), followed by moderate (28.0%), strong (19.5%), and none (15.9%), whereas physicians from the Northeast (44.7%), West (46.3%), and South (36.3%) regions were most likely to have moderate bias. Strong bias was second most likely for the South (25.3%) and the Northeast (20.9%), and least likely in the West (13.9%). A no bias result was more likely of physicians in the South region (22.7%), compared to West (21.9%), Midwest (15.9%), and Northeast (11.3%) regions. There was no evidence of a difference in the distribution of bias recognition results by physician region of practice (χ2 = 0.59, P = 0.997).

Association between implicit bias and bias recognition

We examined the association between magnitude of implicit bias (none, slight, moderate, strong) and recognition of bias. Overall, there was no evidence of an association (ρ = −0.03, P = 0.600). There was a trend in which those with lack of recognition of bias had a higher percent of moderate and strong implicit bias results than those who displayed recognition (χ2 = 3.19, P = 0.074). Of those whose bias recognition results showed lack of recognition, 67.8% had moderate or strong bias, compared to 55.7% for who displayed recognition. Among levels of race/ethnicity (Fig. 1A), sex (Fig. 1B), years of experience (Fig. 1C), and region of practice (Fig. 1D), this trend was particularly the case for physicians with more than 20 y of experience (recognition, moderate or strong: 62.1%; lack of recognition, moderate or strong: 91.5%; χ2 = 6.12, P = 0.013) and for male physicians (recognition, moderate, or strong: 55.6%; lack of recognition, moderate or strong: 69.0%; χ2 = 3.69, P = 0.055).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to explore and describe the level of implicit bias and recognition of biases among vascular surgeons that contribute to a national quality registry. Consistent with our hypothesis, we found that the majority of vascular surgeons had some degree of pro-White bias. Important differences were identified by physician race/ethnicity, years of experience, and region of practice. There were also differences in bias recognition answers, such that female surgeons were more likely than male surgeons to generally agree that biases exist. Overall, there was no evidence of a relationship between magnitude of implicit bias and bias recognition responses. Therefore, those with stronger implicit biases are not necessarily more aware of the existence of biases and that biases may impact decision-making. These findings offer important considerations for attentiveness to both implicit biases and enhancement of recognition of the existence of biases among a surgical community tasked with managing care for structurally vulnerable patients with PAD.

The majority of people have some level of implicit bias: an analysis of 14 y of IAT data found that 65% of respondents showed a pro-White bias.35 Implicit bias is developed from early life reinforcements, social stereotypes, and learned experiences from oneself and those around them.15,36,37 Bias is not always negative, as automatic pattern recognition is the key for making rapid and efficient decisions that can be necessary for survival (e.g., identifying danger) and health care (e.g., treating acutely ill patients).12 Within the intersection of health care and race, however, significant problems may result from implicit bias associated with negative stereotyping of individuals which, in turn, can impact decision-making. Our finding that 71.6% of vascular surgeons have a pro-White bias, although relatively consistent with the general population,35,38 is particularly noteworthy given the high prevalence of vascular diseases in Black patients as well as the poorer outcomes in Black patients with PAD compared to White patients.

Racial disparities in PAD care have been substantially documented.39-41 Such disparities in outcomes persist even in studies that have adjusted for comorbid conditions and clinical severity at presentation, suggesting that other factors, such as structural racism and implicit bias, may be independently associated with adverse outcomes.8,11 Structural racism has been identified as an important contributing factor to racial disparities in health care in the United States.8,42 Racial minorities, particularly Black individuals, have historically experienced disproportionate disadvantages in income, education, housing, rate of incarceration, and access to quality health care.8,43 As a result of these existing inequalities, whether or not they are intentional, Black individuals may have less awareness of PAD warning signs and symptoms, contributing to worse health outcomes.43 The threat of racial stereotyping may also lead patients to avoid medical care, where implicit bias may perpetuate and amplify the risk of lower quality treatment.23,43-45

Previous research has shown that conscious biases and implicit biases are often dissociated from each other.19,20 Our results were consistent with these results. In fact, 67.8% of those who indicated that they are not aware of, or admit to, the existence of biases and their role in decision-making showed moderate or strong implicit bias, compared to 55.7% among those who indicated recognition of biases. Although more overt discrimination is easier to identify, implicit bias is present whether it is realized or not. An arguably more pressing issue than the existence of implicit biases is the lack of awareness of one’s own bias and the potential need to address or manage bias.12,46 When examining trends in moderate and strong biases in our study, specifically, there was some evidence that those who report lack of recognition are more likely to have moderate or strong implicit biases. Therefore, recognition and understanding of susceptibility to implicit biases may be a critical first step in mitigation efforts.44,47

The present study offers practical implications based on stratification across physician race/ethnicity, sex, years of experience, and region of practice. First, differences in implicit biases based on physician race/ethnicity and region of practice offer opportunities for programs to address awareness on a personal level, targeting training and evaluation based on individualized strategies. Furthermore, similar to our results, studies that seek to understand differences in implicit bias by the sex of the physician have found no observable difference in bias by sex.14,15,48 That said, in also examining bias recognition, we did find that female vascular surgeons reported more recognition of the existence of biases than male vascular surgeons. Second, our analysis showed that those with 0-10 y of experience and those with 20 or more years of experience had a similar frequency of strong magnitude of bias. Given that vascular surgery training follows a group apprenticeship model, vascular surgery trainees may be subject to a variety of behaviors that may shape their own bias.49,50 In addition, there are different training pathways (5 + 2 and 0 + 5) physicians may take to be trained to treat individuals with PAD that may differently influence practice. Although we are unable to ascertain if bias is linked to mentor—mentee relationships or training pathways, incorporating information on the recognition of biases and structural changes to facilitate behavior change may be helpful within training programs.44,45,48,51 This idea could be further examined in future studies by distributing the IAT and bias recognition questions in training programs and to those in different training pathways to promote early understanding of one’s susceptibility to implicit bias.45,48,51

Bias training programs have been developed with mixed effectiveness for mitigating the impact of implicit biases in clinical settings.12 Our findings indicated that vascular surgeons show levels of bias similar to those found in numerous health care fields, including general physicians and other surgical specialties.38,52-55 Previous studies have shown that implicit bias is present early in medical training.52,56,57 A large meta-analysis of IAT results in the medical community showed that medical students average a slight-to-moderate pro-White bias.52 That said, there is also some evidence that medical residents are more cognizant than faculty of implicit bias and the role it may have in influencing patient care.57 Incorporation of the findings from the present study can enhance focused strategies and early interventions to aid in elevating recognition of biases.58 However, although institutional and training-level interventions are important, they cannot overcome all biases and should be part of a broader, multilevel strategy to reduce the impact of bias in health care.

Our study has limitations that are important to discuss. The IAT was developed in the field of psychology where there is an ongoing debate over its appropriateness in measuring implicit bias.59 There are also questions about the usefulness of numeric cut points for determining the severity of the test-taker’s bias.60 In addition, the IAT only allows for comparison of White and Black faces, which is not representative of society or all patients who may be treated by vascular surgeons. Structural racism and disparities in vascular care have been most extensively studied in Black compared to White characteristics which led to the scope of the current study. Project Implicit has created a variety of IATs for different races/ethnicities that could be examined in future studies. The IAT remains the most widely used test for studying implicit bias with good reliability and validity and is superior to self-reported measures of bias.13 In contrast to the automatic processes used by the IAT, the self-reported measures of bias recognition may be subject to social desirability effects, as is true for many survey measures where participants may not want to report information that will paint them in a negative light.

Our study population may not be representative of all US-based vascular surgeons, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. The study’s sampling frame was vascular surgeons who participate in the VQI and we feel confident that our findings are representative of bias in this group. The sample of individuals who completed the study survey included more women and younger surgeons than the overall frame. The study also encountered limitations in response rate that are common in survey methodology.30-32 We anticipated these challenges and, therefore, we were able to compare our sample to the overall VQI sampling frame and provide weighting to account for nonresponse and demographic differences in the final participating sample. Thus, we are still able to make inferences to the broader VQI surgeon population. However, the VQI may not be representative of all vascular surgeons and although these participants identified as vascular surgeons and have been trained as vascular surgeons, the participants for this study may not currently treat PAD. Similarly, there are specialties other than vascular surgery that may treat PAD (e.g., cardiology faculty, general surgery) that future studies could explore to have a comprehensive understanding of implicit bias as it relates to the PAD treatment. Finally, there may be other variables, including previous implicit bias training or community factors, that we were unable to correct for in our weighting that could bias estimates, and a larger sample size may uncover statistically significant trends that we are unable to discern with the present sample.

Conclusions

The present study provides evidence that pro-White bias is present in a significant proportion of vascular surgeons in our sample. Although these findings are similar to what has been observed in studies of the general population and of medical providers in other specialties, these are important findings in a profession that takes care of patients with PAD due to a heavy burden of chronic comorbid conditions and a high proportion of individuals from structurally vulnerable groups. Given a lack of relationship between implicit biases and recognition of biases, and their potential to impact clinical outcomes, these findings offer considerations for delivering care to structurally vulnerable patients, such as those with PAD. With recognition as an important first step in mitigation, cognizance of the existence of biases, as identified by this study, can aid efforts to minimize racial disparities in health care.
