Optical spin-orbit interaction induced by magnetic textures
Authors: Martin Luttmann, Mauro Fanciulli, Pietro Carrara, Maurizio Sacchi, Thierry Ruchon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Contrary to the optical spin angular momentum (SAM), the role played by the orbital angular momentum (OAM) of light in magneto-optics remains largely unexplored. However, the SAM and OAM are known to be coupled when light interacts with non-homogeneous and non-isotropic materials. Here we predict that the OAM carried by each photon in a light beam is modified upon reflection on magnetic textures like skyrmions, and that the sign and magnitude of this variation are governed both by the SAM of the incident field and by the topology of the texture. Our predictions can be readily tested by performing circular dichroism measurements, and may provide new routes to read the topology of magnetization optically, or to shape light’s angular momentum with magnetism.

Introduction

Spin-orbit interaction (SOI) is a phenomenon occurring in many areas of quantum physics, for instance in condensed matter in the case of noncollinear magnetism1, magnetocrystalline anisotropy2, Rashba3 and Dresselhaus effects4, anomalous Hall effect5, spin Hall effects6, topological materials7 and the electronic structure of heavy element compounds such as transition metal dichalcogenides8, or in dilute matter for gas phase spectroscopy9. More recently, this concept was extended to the spin and orbital angular momenta (SAM and OAM) of light, opening the field of optical spin-orbit interactions (OSOI). Through OSOI, light angular momentum of one type – spin or orbital – may be controlled by the value of the other type10; in this respect, OSOI is a quite distinct concept from electronic SOI in condensed-matter systems. OSOI has been identified upon strong focusing conditions, or through refraction or diffraction. However, OSOI was also reported when light propagates in a medium that is both anisotropic and inhomogeneous, with important applied perspectives for the control of the SAM and OAM of light. This is the working principle of optical elements like q-plates11,12, whichexhibit spatially varying birefringence), metasurfaces13,14 or light-shaping nanostructures15,16. These examples all rely on a structural patterning of matter, possibly controlled by electric fields. On the contrary, while the coupling between light polarization and magnetism is well established17–21, the research effort aiming at identifying the role of OAM in magneto-optical interactions is only very recent22–26 and studies of OSOI induced by magnetism are very scarce today27,28. However, as the ensemble of stable magnetic structures, with fine control on their manufacturing and innumerable applications, is growing, it is timely to investigate their specific impact on light beams carrying SAM and OAM. Some promising textures like magnetic vortices and skyrmions29–33 are particularly attractive for these investigations. They are patterned in dots of swirling magnetization, while their typical size ranges from a few microns, to a few nanometers, which is comparable to focal spot sizes of visible to soft X-ray beams, a spectral range where light beams with angular momentum are now available34,35. In this context, identifying magnetically-induced OSOIs might open interesting perspectives and new avenues for technological applications. These effects may be used as new platforms for probing non-trivial magnetic topologies, similarly to the techniques using vortex beams for chiral sensing36,37. The ultrafast control of the angular momentum of light38,39 may also benefit from such a study.

Here we harness the theoretical framework proposed in Ref40. to demonstrate how the average OAM per photon in a light beam can be modified upon reflection on magnetic textures. In particular, we show that the sign of the OAM variation is controlled by the SAM of the incident field, while its magnitude depends on the specific magnetic structure considered. This magnetically-induced OSOI originates from the azimuthal interference between the longitudinal, transverse and polar magneto-optical Kerr effect (MOKE) components, that get coupled for incident elliptically polarized light fields. Taking the example of the X-ray spectral range, we show that magnetic vortices and skyrmions are an ideal test-bed for the observation of this form of OSOI.

If the incident light is circularly polarized, we find11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Delta \ell = s_{\text {in}}N_{\text {Sk}} \frac{\mathcal {W}_{\text {in}}}{\mathcal {W}_{\text {out}}}\cdot \frac{ |m_0|^2}{2}\left| r_{pp}r_0^t r_{ps}^l\right| \,. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}We thus unveil a form of OSOI, since the OAM change can be positive or negative depending on the helicity of the incident field \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_{\text {in}}$$\end{document} and the value of the magnetic winding number \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_\text {Sk}$$\end{document} (Fig. 2.b,c), and cancels out if one of the two is equal to zero. Returning to Eq. 4, we note that this change of OAM only occurs in the p-polarized component of the beam, whereas the s-polarized component retains an OAM equal to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell _{\text {in}}$$\end{document}. Indeed, the p component of the reflected field contains the interference between the transverse and longitudinal magnetization terms, coupled through the presence of both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon _p$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon _s$$\end{document}, resulting in a variation of OAM. On the other hand, the s component of the reflected field is simply given by a term proportional to the longitudinal magnetization, up to a constant term. Note however that in the presence of azimuthally-varying longitudinal and polar magnetization components, an OAM variation can also occur in the s component of the light field. We can also compute the mean outgoing SAM quantum number as12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} s_{\text {out}}&=2 \frac{\mathcal {W}_{\text {in}}}{\mathcal {W}_{\text {out}}}\left[ \mathcal {I}\left( \epsilon _p^*\epsilon _s r_{pp}^*r_{ss} \right) - \frac{|m_0|^2}{2} \mathcal {I}\left( \epsilon _p \epsilon _s^*\right) \left| r_{ps}^l\right| ^2 \right] \nonumber \\&=\frac{\mathcal {W}_{\text {in}}}{\mathcal {W}_{\text {out}}}\left[ -s_{\text {in}} \mathcal {R}\left( r_{pp}^*r_{ss} \right) + s_{\text {in}} \frac{|m_0|^2}{2} \left| r_{ps}^l\right| ^2 \right] \,. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The first term on the right hand side is the well-known change of polarization upon reflection on a surface due to the dephasing between the s and p polarization components. For instance, setting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{pp}=-r_{ss}=1$$\end{document}, which would model a perfect metallic mirror at 45\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document} incidence, we retrieve the expected inversion of SAM. We notice the strong analogy between the second term and the OAM change in Eq. 11, which are similar up to the replacement of one \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left| r_{ps}^l\right|$$\end{document} term by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left| r_{pp}r_0^t\right|$$\end{document}. However, the difference in magnitude prevents them from compensating one another, and we conclude that the total angular momentum of light is not conserved during the interaction. This results from the fact that the sample is not at a normal angle with respect to the incident beam’s propagation axis, and thus does not show rotation symmetry about this axis. This is again analog to non-rotationally symmetric q-plates, which do not allow a pure SAM to OAM conversion11.

Simulation of the magneto-optical interaction

To test our predictions, we perform simulations of a realistic MOKE experiment, not at normal incidence, using the magneto-optical constants at the L\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_3$$\end{document}-edge of Fe. The photon energy of the incident beam is 711.2 eV, and the incidence angle is 45\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document} (Brewster’s angle). We express the spin texture of the magnetic skyrmions using the following ansatz46,47:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\begin{matrix} {\textbf {m}}_{\text {Sk}} & = (m^l, m^t, m^p)\\ & = \bigg ( C(\phi ) \sin \{f(r)\}, S(\phi ) \sin \{f(r)\}, \cos \{f(r)\} \bigg ) \,, \end{matrix}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C(\phi )=\cos (N_{\text {Sk}}\phi +h)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S(\phi )=\sin (N_{\text {Sk}}\phi +h)$$\end{document}. The helicity h determines the type of the skyrmion. h = (0, π ) corresponds to Néel-type solutions (with zero azimuthal magnetization component) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h =\pm \pi /2$$\end{document} yields Bloch-type solutions (a magnetization vortex). \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\text {Sk}}$$\end{document} is the skyrmion charge, equal to 1 for the Bloch and Néel skyrmions considered here. We will later comment on the case of textures with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\text {Sk}}>1$$\end{document}. A good approximation of the radial function f(r) is4714\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} f(r) =4 \tan ^{-1}\big (e^{-\frac{r}{\lambda }} \big ) \,, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where λ is the extension of the skyrmion. In numerical applications, we will set λ = 100nm, in order to match the size of typical skyrmions. The incident beam waist is also set to 100 nm. The other parameters of the simulation (in particular the exact modelling of magnetic sample and substrate) are the same as in Ref25,40.. The reflected field at the sample’s plane (Eq. 4) is numerically propagated to the far field using the Fraunhofer operator.

Figure 3 displays the results for an incident LG mode of OAM \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell _{\text {in}}=1$$\end{document}, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_{\text {in}}=\pm 1$$\end{document}, impinging on a Bloch skyrmion. For \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_{\text {in}}=-1$$\end{document}, inspecting the intensity and phase of the p and s components of the reflected field at the detector (Fig. 3.a,c), we observe that the p component has been almost fully converted into the ℓ =0 mode, as it exhibits a strong on-axis intensity and flat spatial phase. As expected, the s component retains the ℓ =1 helical phase of the incident beam. On the other hand, for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_{\text {in}}=+1$$\end{document} an increase in OAM is manifest in the phase of the p component, which shows a 4π azimuthal variation, typical of an OAM of 2 (Fig. 2.b). Similar observations can be made for a Néel-type skyrmion (Fig. S1 of the supplementary material). The reflected field is a superposition of different modes, and thus does not exhibit the perflectly round, donut-like profile typical of pure LG modes.

To get a more quantitative insight of the OAM variation, we compute numerically the local OAM value in the far field using Eq. S24 (see Fig.S1.e-h). This quantity is then weighted by the local intensity and averaged over the beam profile, yielding the average OAM value per photon in the reflected beam. Subtracting the incident OAM \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell _{\text {in}}$$\end{document}, we obtain the mean OAM variation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \ell = \ell _{\text {out}} - \ell _{\text {in}}$$\end{document} caused by the reflection on the skyrmion. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \ell$$\end{document} is shown as a function of the photon energy for various winding number \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\text {Sk}}$$\end{document} in Fig. 4. In particular, at the resonance frequency, we find \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \ell \approx \pm 0.3$$\end{document} for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\text {Sk}}=1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \ell \approx \pm 0.6$$\end{document} for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\text {Sk}}=2$$\end{document} (corresponding to a higher order skymrion33). On-axis magnetic circular dichroism

For a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell _{\text {in}} = 1$$\end{document} beam incident on a skyrmion, the reflected beam may contain a ℓ =0 contribution, depending on the incident SAM. Inspecting Fig. 3.a, we observe that the absence of optical singularity in the p component leads to a Gaussian-like on-axis intensity profile, whereas the presence of an optical singularity results in a very weak on-axis intensity (Fig. 3.b). Thus, a MCD measurement, consisting in subtracting the far field images obtained with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_{\text {in}}=1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_{\text {in}}=-1$$\end{document}, performed with a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left| \ell _{\text {in}}\right| =1$$\end{document} incident OAM, should exhibit a measurable on-axis signal. Yet, as predicted, they exhibit an on-axis positive signal for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell _{\text {in}}=1$$\end{document} and negative signal for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell _{\text {in}}=-1$$\end{document} incident polarization (see the line-outs in Fig. 5.c), independently of the magnetic skyrmion helicity, as the OSOI is independent of the sign of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_0$$\end{document} (Eq. 11). Discussion

We identified a form of OSOI occurring in magneto-optics with structured magnetic samples. Our study focused on reflection off a magnetic interface, described by the MOKE formalism. This effect arises from the interference between the transverse, longitudinal and polar MOKE effects, coupled by the electric field’s components of the incident light. We have shown that the helicity of the incident beam determines the OAM content and the average OAM quantum number of the reflected field. Remarkably, the magnitude and sign of the OAM variation is also governed by the winding number of the magnetic texture, and cancels out for a winding number equal to zero. This indicates that this effect could be used to implement all-optical ultrafast skyrmion readers, which would encode the magnetic state on light’s spatial degrees of freedom. OAM demultiplexer could then be used to decode the information. In this paper, we numerically computed the effect for X-ray light, but it is expected to exist at all wavelengths, including in the visible, to the cost of a reduced magnitude.

This modification of the OAM of the light is not accompanied by an opposite change of the SAM, that would yield a null total angular momentum modification. Therefore, part of light’s angular momentum is coherently transferred to matter. How this transfer exactly takes place in our case – is a mechanical torque exerted on the texture, perhaps via the excitation of spin waves? – will have to be investigated in future works. Conversely, the Faraday effect17, wherein the incident beam is transmitted by a magnetic sample, should allow to observe full SAM to OAM transfer, since, in that case, the sample could be invariant under rotation about the beam axis.

We argue that an experimental setup similar to the one reported in Refs25,26 could be used to observe this OSOI effect by performing MCD measurements. Our work allows to anticipate that magnetic textures with appropriate topology could serve as re-configurable OAM beam shapers, similarly to q-plates and metasurfaces in the visible domain, or patterned spintronic emitters in the Terahertz range39. Compared to these passive devices, magnetic textures present the promising advantage of being controllable by external magnetic or electric fields, and exhibit a fast dynamical response. One could also imagine shaping or controlling magnetic skyrmions with beams of light carrying SAM and/or OAM23,48,49, with applications in data encoding and processing33. Finally, the capability of free-electron lasers50 or high harmonic generation sources34,35,51,52 to synthesise ultrashort femtosecond or attosecond light pulses carrying various kinds of angular momentum lets us envision to perform the MCD experiment described here at the natural time-scales of spin dynamics in matter.
