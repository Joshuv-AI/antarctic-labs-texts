A Rare Case of Adenoid Cystic Carcinoma in the Male Breast: Clinical Presentation and Histopathological Evaluation
Authors: Nastaran Tayebi, Behnaz Jahanbin, Aysan Nozheh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAdenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC) of the male breast is an exceptionally rare neoplasm with a distinct clinical behavior. We report a case of a 40‐year‐old man who presented with ACC of the right breast. The patient was initially treated with surgical excision; however, a local recurrence was identified just 4 months postoperatively. This rapid recurrence underscores the aggressive potential of ACC in this unique demographic. The case highlights the critical importance of rigorous postoperative surveillance and a more extensive consideration of adjuvant therapies to mitigate recurrence risk. Further research is essential to establish optimal management protocols for this rare disease.Keywords: adenoid cystic carcinoma, male breast cancer, rare breast malignancy, surgical excision, tumor recurrence

Introduction

Adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC) of the breast is a rare malignancy, representing less than 0.1% of all breast cancers, with its occurrence in male patients being exceptionally uncommon [1]. Histologically, it is characterized by a biphasic proliferation of luminal epithelial and basaloid myoepithelial cells, typically forming cribriform, tubular, and solid architectures [2]. Immunohistochemically, breast ACC typically presents as triple‐negative—lacking expression of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and HER2—which distinguishes it from the more common subtypes of breast cancer and renders hormonal and anti‐HER2 therapies ineffective [2, 3, 4]. While female breast ACC is often associated with a favorable prognosis and an indolent clinical course, emerging evidence suggests that its behavior in males may be more aggressive, characterized by a higher potential for local recurrence and distant metastasis [3]. This underscores the critical need for a deeper understanding of its clinicopathological behavior in this unique demographic. We report a case of a 40‐year‐old male with ACC of the breast who experienced an early local recurrence 4 months after surgical excision, a finding that challenges the conventional perception of this tumor's indolence and highlights critical questions regarding its optimal initial management.

Case History/Examination

A 40‐year‐old man with no significant past medical or family history presented with mild right breast enlargement. A CT scan revealed multiple hypodense lesions in the right pectoral subcutaneous area. An excision of the right breast mass was subsequently performed at an outside institution. The pathology report at that center confirmed adenoid cystic carcinoma. The patient received no adjuvant therapy postoperatively due to personal decision. Approximately 4 months later, a surveillance breast MRI at our institution revealed a new non‐mass enhancement at the primary site. The patient remained asymptomatic systemically, reporting no weight loss or fever.

Physical Examination in Our Center

Previous surgical scar site in the right breast, mild skin changes, and no nipple discharge. There was no evidence of axillary lymphadenopathy.

Differential Diagnosis

Breast abscess or inflammatory lesion post‐previous surgery, recurrence of tumor, metastatic carcinoma, invasive breast carcinoma.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Pathology Results

Previous excision pathology report in another center: Multiple pieces of irregular brown tissue, totally measuring 8 × 6 × 3 cm, with histomorphology consistent with adenoid cystic carcinoma, without any further details. The surgical margins in the report cannot be assessed due to the fragmentation of the specimen described in the gross report. The IHC report indicates that ER, PR, and HER2 are negative, and Ki‐67 is approximately 17%. (The IHC staining of ER, PR, and HER2 is performed in the first surgery center and we don't have access to it; we only have access to the pathology report and we don't repeat these markers at our center.)

The current mastectomy specimen sent to our center consists of an oriented breast tissue specimen partially covered by skin, breast tissue measuring 17 × 17 × 2.5 cm, skin measuring 12 × 5 × 0.3 cm, an areola measuring 2.5 cm and a nipple measuring 0.5 cm in diameter. On the cut sections, an infiltrative creamy‐white mass measuring 1.5 × 1.3 × 1.2 cm was located at the retro‐areolar region with a distance of 1 cm from the closest (deep) surgical margin. The histopathologic sections demonstrate infiltration of the breast by neoplastic cells arranged in characteristic cribriform, tubular and solid patterns. Basaloid tumor cells are seen encircling pseudo‐luminal spaces filled with basophilic secretions and consistent with recurrent adenoid cystic carcinoma (Figure 1). Lymph vascular invasion and necrosis were not identified, but perineural invasion is present. Right sentinel and non‐sentinel lymph nodes were also sent as frozen section and they are free from tumor. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed to confirm the diagnosis. The tumor cells showed diffuse positivity for CD117 and a peripheral layer of cells with strong nuclear positivity for p63 (Positive in myoepithelial basal‐like cells) (Figure 2).

The patient, who had previously refused adjuvant therapy after the first surgery, decided to accept it upon experiencing this recurrence, and treatment was commenced accordingly.

Discussion

The management of adenoid cystic carcinoma of the breast remains a subject of debate due to its rarity and the consequent lack of large‐scale randomized trials. Current strategies are primarily derived from retrospective series and institutional experiences. The cornerstone of treatment is surgical excision, with studies demonstrating that both breast‐conserving surgery (BCS) followed by adjuvant radiotherapy and mastectomy achieve excellent and comparable long‐term local control rates [5, 6]. The critical importance of radiotherapy following BCS is well established, with meta‐analyses and cohort studies consistently showing a significant reduction in local recurrence risk, achieving local control rates exceeding 95% with this multimodal approach [6, 7].

A notable feature of breast ACC is its low propensity for axillary lymph node metastasis, reported in fewer than 5%–10% of cases [6, 7, 8]. This biological characteristic supports a conservative approach to axillary staging. Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) is now widely accepted as the standard of care for clinically node‐negative patients, making extensive axillary lymph node dissection largely unnecessary and minimizing associated morbidity [5, 6, 7, 8].

The role of systemic chemotherapy is less clearly defined. Its use is generally contemplated for patients with high‐risk features, including lymph node involvement, tumors larger than 3 cm, high histological grade (particularly a predominant solid growth pattern), or the presence of perineural invasion [6, 7, 8, 9]. However, the evidence supporting the use of adjuvant chemotherapy is limited to small, retrospective studies and remains inconclusive. Hormonal therapy has no role in the management of classic ACC due to its consistently triple‐negative phenotype [2, 3, 4, 5].

Our case illustrates a critical clinical lesson. The patient experienced a rapid local recurrence 4 months after the initial surgery, a scenario likely influenced by the lack of adjuvant therapy and a fragmented excision that prevented definitive margin evaluation.

The diagnostic challenge in our case, compounded by the initial fragmented specimen, mirrors the known difficulties in cytologically diagnosing adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC), particularly its solid basaloid variant. As highlighted by Noda et al., such variants can present with subtle features—such as scant cellularity, ribbon‐like basement membrane material, and peripheral nuclear palisading—that may be overlooked or lead to diagnostic uncertainty, especially in limited sample sizes [10].

This aggressive behavior is atypical for the generally indolent course of female breast ACC; it aligns with reports suggesting a more virulent phenotype in male patients and in tumors with high‐risk histological features [3, 4, 9]. The patient's early recurrence powerfully illustrates the potential consequences of inadequate initial local therapy. It strongly suggests that for a subset of patients, which may include males or those with specific pathological risk factors not initially apparent, a more aggressive upfront approach is warranted. This case reinforces the argument that deviating from the established multimodal paradigm of BCS plus radiotherapy or mastectomy can carry a significant risk of early treatment failure.

This experience underscores the necessity of a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach to this rare tumor from the outset. It also highlights the imperative for vigilant, scheduled follow‐up to enable the early detection of recurrence. Future efforts must focus on collaborative, multi‐institutional studies to better identify the clinicopathological and molecular predictors of aggressive behavior. Such insights, potentially leveraging molecular profiling as suggested by recent research [11], are essential for moving beyond a one‐size‐fits‐all approach and developing risk‐adapted, personalized management strategies to optimize outcomes for all patients with this rare disease.

In conclusion, adenoid cystic carcinoma of the breast is a rare and distinct subtype of triple‐negative breast cancer known for its relatively slow progression and generally favorable prognosis. Despite its potential for local recurrence, especially after incomplete surgical removal, patients typically experience better outcomes compared to other triple‐negative breast cancers. Optimal management involves complete surgical excision (with free surgical margins), adjuvant therapy and careful long‐term monitoring to detect recurrences early. Given the rarity of this tumor, particularly in men, documenting such cases is essential for enhancing clinical knowledge and improving treatment approaches.
