An approach to automatic fault detection in four-point system for knitted fabric with our benchmark dataset Isl-Knit
Authors: Avishek Das Gupta, Zafar Sadek, MD Shakhawat Hossain, Tarik Reza Toha, Anupom Mondol, Sultana Umme Habiba, Shaikh Md Mominul Alam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractKnit fabric is one of the dominating fabric types for wearable textile across the whole world and during the production of knit fabric, faults created by different reasons cause difficulties in the subsequent process. The existing fault detection process in the knitting industry is done manually by the human naked eye. To detect faults automatically, deep learning-based models are very efficient and can reduce the workload for fabric inspection. However, implementing a deep learning-based model for fabric fault detection needs a large number of images with different types of knit fabric faults from real-world scenarios to train and for better performance. Most of the existing fabric fault datasets are based on woven fabric, which cannot be used for training because the faults of woven fabric and knit fabrics are completely different. In this research, we propose a new dataset named ISL-Knit. It is a benchmark dataset for knit fabric faults collected from different knit dyeing industries in Bangladesh. Our dataset contains high-resolution images of grey fabric and dyed fabric annotated with 7 types of faults. To provide correct annotation, all images of faults are annotated and verified with the proper reference which can be a great tool for boosting up deep learning-based models for automatic knit fabric fault detection in the textile field. A comparison of the accuracy of the deep learning-based model by training with our proposed dataset and existing knit fabric fault dataset is also done. To develop an automatic fault detection system, we trained the YOLOv5 models with our dataset, considering different image sizes. For real-world implementation, the best models are selected considering mAP and inference time. YOLOv5 models are implemented in Raspberry Pi with a Raspberry Pi camera, a laptop with no dedicated GPU, and a laptop with a dedicated GPU with a web camera to observe the performance. Finally, an automated four-point inspection system is developed by calibrating the camera to generate the score representing the quality of fabrics. The implementation represents the feasibility of a deep learning-based model in knit fabric fault detection, considering the cost and accuracy.Keywords: Knit fabric, Fault detection, Deep learning, Dataset, Real implementation, Four-point inspection

Introduction

In the field of wearable textiles, woven and knit fabrics are the most dominating and popular fabric types for their versatile use and inherent properties. The process of creating the woven fabric is known as weaving, where two sets of yarn are interlaced at the right angle, and knit fabric is produced by the process called knitting, where one or more than one set of yarn is used to create interlocking loops. Generally, the knitting process can be classified into two major types: warp knitting and weft knitting. From the perspective of wearable textiles, the weft-knitted fabric is more popular and demanded worldwide for its flexibility and comfortability than warp-knitted fabric. In this research, we focus on the weft knitted fabric. Bangladesh is a significant manufacturer of garments in the global textile market, especially in knitwear. In the RMG export, the contribution of knit and woven products is respectively 54.48% and 45.52%, in the 2021-22 financial year. The growth rate of knit fabric manufacturing and woven fabric on total export is respectively 36.88% and 33.82% by BKMEA [1]. From the trade information, we can see the impact of knit fabric production. In 2018, the number of total registered knitting industry was 2500 [2]. This number is rising to meet the rising demand for knit fabric worldwide. Except for Bangladesh, the producers of knit fabric are also increasing in China, India, Vietnam, Pakistan, Korea, etc. [3]

Due to different reasons like raw material quality, machine settings, surrounding environment, and operator mistakes, different types of faults are created in fabric. After production, the fabric is sent to the inspection section, where humans check faults manually. This inspection system is laborious and causes eye fatigue for continuous attention on the illuminated board. According to research by Jing et al. [4], The quality of the inspection decreases along the working time of the operator, and quality varies on the experience and personal point of view of the operator. After inspection, fabrics are sent to the finishing section for heat-set or dyeing section for dyeing. Faults unchecked from the inspection section create problems in the dyeing process and increase the possibility of rejection at the final inspection of the garments section. About 45%-65% of the price reduction is caused due to fabric fault, which is ultimately a loss for a company mentioned by Kumar et al. [5].

An automatic fault detection system can be a great tool to reduce the operator's workload and ensure consistent inspection quality. Several approaches have been made for automatic fault detection systems, and deep learning-based approaches are becoming more popular for their learning ability, feature extraction, and better performance. However, implementing a deep learning-based model with high accuracy needs a large number of images with different types of faults, which should be in high resolution. Available datasets are based on woven fabric, which can not be used to train a model to detect knit fabric faults as woven and knit fabric faults are different. A few faults, like oil stains and holes, can be similar in both knit fabrics. However, most of the faults like carrying, broken end, knot, feet, starting mark, double end, broken pick, miss pick, and temple mark only can be found in woven fabric. These types of faults cannot be found in knit fabric. In knit-fabric common faults are needle mark, set up, yarn missing, loop mark, etc. So, to develop an automatic fault detection system for knit fabric using a deep-learning-based method, we need a dataset of knit fabric faults.

There are several challenges to building a knit fabric fault dataset. As the faults are not created intentionally, the occurrence of the faults is not so high in industry. So, building a dataset with original images captured from the industry environment is a time-consuming process. The environment of training images should be the same as the testing environment for higher accuracy. Computer-generated images will not perform well in real-time fault detection in an industrial environment.

To build the dataset, we collect images of knit fabric faults from different knit dyeing industries in Bangladesh. We try to maintain a constant fabric distance to the camera to keep the fault in the same dimension and avoid critical angles from where the fault cannot be understood. Most of the existing deep-learning-based models are also based on woven fabric faults. These models cannot be implemented to detect knit fabric faults. In this research, we try to develop an automatic fault detection system for knit fabric based on our dataset, and finally, we implement our system in the industry for real-time fault detection.

Related work

This section discusses the dataset-related work on fabric faults, deep learning-based approaches for fabric fault detection, and the four-point inspection system.

Dataset on fabric fault

The preliminary and most vital requirement for developing a deep learning-based model for automatic fabric fault detection is fabric fault datasets. Most of the fabric fault datasets available on the internet are based on woven fabric. Chetverikov and Hanbury [6] proposed a dataset named “TILDA”, which contains 3200 images on 8 fabric types. This dataset was published in 1994, and it is freely accessible. DAGM (Deutsche Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Mustererkennung), proposed by Timm and Barth [7], is a fabric fault dataset containing a total of 6900 images published in 2007. However, the images of this dataset are artificially generated by computers. HKU-Fabric by Ngan and Pang [8] is another popular woven fabric fault dataset consisting of 166 images and 3 fabric types. Silvestre-Blanes et al. [9] proposed a PUBLIC FABRIC DATABASE consisting of 245 images where 140 images are defect-free. This dataset contains 12 types of woven fabric faults. Zhang et al. [10] proposed ZJU-Leaper, a benchmark dataset in the woven fabric fault dataset. This dataset contains 19 types of fabric and a total of 98777 images, where 27650 images are defective fabric. All of these datasets are based on woven fabric fault. The only available dataset on knit fabric fault on the Internet is Fabric Stain Dataset [11]. This dataset contains 68 defect-free images and 398 stained images. The types of stains are ink stain, oil stain, and dirt stain, and the fabric types are cotton and polyester. However, all types of stains are labeled and annotated under a single class. The images in the Fabric Stain Dataset are grayscale and do not contain any machinery-related fault.

To overcome the lack of knit fabric datasets, we propose ISL-Knit, which contains 3018 images of knit fabric faults and 357 images of defect-free fabric. All the defective fabric images are real faults that occurred in circular knitting machines or finishing machinery.

Fabric fault detection

Researchers have made several approaches to fabric fault detection. Structural, statistical, spectral, model-based, and learning-based detection methods are some significant approaches by Hanbay et al. [12]. Spectral approaches like Wavelet transform, Fourier transform-based approaches, and Gabor transform-based approaches were very popular. However, spectral approaches have some limitations, such as high computational cost and poor performance in small defect detection. These approaches also face difficulties in the case of random Hanbay et al. [12] and diverse fabric texture Liu et al. [13]. Recently, researchers have applied deep learning-based models on fabric fault detection, and satisfactory results have been achieved. Wei et al. [14] proposed a fabric fault detection method with modified Faster RCNN and achieved 95.8% accuracy. They built a woven fabric fault dataset with 6 different types of faults, where each class contains 135 images. Sujee et al. [15] proposed YOLOv2 and YOLOv3 Tiny for fabric fault detection. In this research, they use their dataset of woven fabric fault with 6 classes of defects. They showed YOLO provides better FPS than the Faster R-CNN detection framework. Zhang et al. [16] applied YOLOv2 for fabric fault detection. They also used their dataset containing 276 images of yarn-dyed fabric with three defect classes. However, they didn't mention whether the fabric is knit or woven. Jing et al. [17] proposed an improved YOLOv3 model for fabric fault detection. The error rate in detection is lower than 5% and more effective than YOLOv3. However, their model is based on woven fabric fault detection. Liu et al. [18] proposed a fabric defect detection model based on YOLOv3 trained with 2000 images of woven fabric fault. They fine-tuned the model by freezing the layer, which was previously trained by the COCO dataset. Zheng et al. [19] proposed an improved YOLOv5 model for fabric fault detection with 0.9552 accuracy. This research used the TIANCHI [20] fabric defect dataset containing 4371 images of printed woven fabric faults. In research work, [19] 2239 images were used for training from the TIANCHI dataset. However, they didn't implement them at the industry level or mention the types of fabric faults. Song et al. [21] proposed a fabric fault detection system with a lightweight algorithm named EfficientDet-D0. They trained their model with 5 different woven and printed fabric datasets and implemented it in the NVIDIA Jetson TX2 device.

Mobile-Unet is a deep learning-based defect segmentation network approach by Jing et al. [22] trained with 1790 images of yarn-dyed fabric. They compared the computational cost with different platforms considering FPS. However, the number of images in the dataset is not sufficient. Beljadid et al. [23] developed a convolutional model named DetectNet using TILDA [6] dataset and achieved 96% accuracy. However, they used a small dataset with 1000 images and increased the number of training images by applying data argumentation. Ouyang et al. [24] proposed a woven fabric defect detection system using CNN with the activation layer. They used TILDA and DFG datasets to evaluate their model and achieved 95.34% accuracy. A plain weave fabric defect detection system was developed by Guan et al. [25] using the VGG model. In this research, they consider 6 types of woven fabric faults and evaluate the model with 12000 samples. Gao et al. [26] proposed a defect detection system for woven fabric based on CNN. They built a fabric fault dataset with 600 defect and 600 defect-free images. Their proposed model showed 96.52% accuracy on their dataset. In recent work, Pourkaramdel et al. [27] proposed a novel algorithm for woven fabric fault detection. The algorithm will learn the pattern of fabric from a defect-free training set and then can be used for fault detection. The experimental evaluation of their approaches showed 97.66% accuracy.

Chakraborty et al. [28] introduced a printed fabric database including both defect and defect-free images of knit fabric. Their dataset contains 380 images of fabric collected from the industry, and they train their custom CNN model with 760 images, which are increased by image augmentation. However, this is not enough for the deep learning-based model. Samit Chakraborty and Parrillo-Chapman [29] also proposed another approach for fault detection in printed fabric with a deep convolution neural network. The library of images used for training and testing consists of 400 images where defect images are caused by color spots and misprint faults. The study compares the performance of different types of activation functions, such as simple CNN, VGG19, and VGG16. Hanbay et al. [30] proposed their fault detection system for single jersey knit fabric with the ANN model. They considered four types of knit fabric faults and implemented their device in a circular knitting machine. Their device is able to detect faults with 94% accuracy. However, their system is limited to use inside the circular knitting machine.

Four-point inspection system

It is an international standard system to express the quality of fabric calculated from the penalty points given for each fault. The system is described under ASTM D5430-93 [31]. Knit fabric manufacturing industries use this method to express the quality of fabric and garments in case of quality control, fabric purchasing, and buyer-seller agreements. By the four-point inspection system, fabric inspectors give penalty points for fabric faults. After the summation of total points, points per 100 square yards are calculated, and the score is compared to the acceptance level. Existing automatic fault detection systems didn't mention any types of approaches for automated four-point inspection systems. In our research, we developed a prototype of the four-point inspection system for knit fabric.

System design and dataset creation

The automatic fault detection system in the four-point inspection system is based on three major works. They are dataset creation, which is the core of automatic fault detection, fabric fault detection model development, and four-point module development. Image collection was the first challenging task in this research before doing these major tasks. The block diagram of our proposed methodology is shown in Fig. 1.

Block diagram of proposed methodology.

To collect the real knit fabric fault images, our data collectors collect the images of faults from different industries. The names of industries from which we collect fabric fault images are Amex Knitting, Pakiza Knit Composite, Masco Export Ltd., GMS Textile Limited, and Knit Concern Group. Our data collectors collect images of the fabric faults with the smartphone camera. As the images are collected with different cameras, the resolution size of images is not fixed in our dataset. However, all the cameras can take high-resolution images, and the average resolution size is much higher than the existing knit fabric dataset. The images are collected from two sections of the industries: the inspection machine of the knitting section and the stenter or compactor machine of the finishing section. These two sections are chosen based on the industry process sequence. When knitting is done, the fabric is sent to the inspection section for fault detection. In this stage, the fabric is generally grey fabric. However, yarn-dyed fabrics can also be found in this stage based on orders by the buyer. The target is to detect and report all knitting faults in the inspection machine before sending the knit fabric to the dyeing or finishing section. If a faulty fabric is passed through the inspection section, the fabric will be dyed by the dyeing unit and finally will be rejected after finishing. If this happens, it will create an unrecoverable loss of time, chemicals, dyestuff, and utilities. To avoid this phenomenon, the highest priority is given at the inspection section after knitting by checking every single fabric roll. For this reason, we also prioritize collecting the images of fabric faults from the inspection section. In the dyeing section, the fabric is generally not checked again. The finishing process begins after the dying fabric is sent to the finishing section. In the finishing section, the fabric is passed through the dryer-compactor or stenter-compactor according to the characteristics of the fabric. In the finishing section, the fabric can be checked at different stages, varying in different industries. The general practices of finishing inspection in the industry are given below:

1. All the fabric rolls are checked during unloading from the stenter or compactor machine. After that, the whole batch is stored. 30% of the fabrics in one batch are examined by the inspection machine. If the fabric lot cannot fulfill the necessary quality requirements, then 50% of the fabric is tested

2. All of the fabric rolls are unloaded from the compactor without checking, and batches are created. After batching, every fabric from the batch is checked in the inspection table or inspection machine.

We collect images of fabric faults from the stenter or compactor if the industry follows practice 1 and collect faults from the inspection table if the industry follows practice 2. The number of faults depends on the industry and also varies day by day. Generally, about 25 fabric fault images can be taken after 1-hour observation.

Fault types

Various faults can occur in the knit fabric due to yarn quality, machine-related issues, the condition of the surrounding environment, operator negligence, and material handling-related problems. In ISL-Knit, we don't cover all types of faults. Some faults occur very rarely, and some faults cannot be captured by the camera from the top view of the fabric. To build the dataset, we select the most common faults that occur for all fabric types and are common among the industries. Our selected fault types can be observed from the top view of the fabric because, in our approach, the camera is placed approximately at the right angle to the fabric surface, which is similar to human inspection. The selected fabric faults are described.

Loop mark

A loop mark is a knitting fault that occurs due to yarn quality, the machine's high rpm, and excessive fabric tension. Visually, the loop mark fault is similar to a hole joint with a drop stitch. The hole is the starting point of the faults, shown in Fig. 2. The drop stitch is a type of fabric fault that occurs due to a closed latch, broken needle hook, or if the hook cannot catch the yarn. Generally, the drop stitch seldom occurs faults. The loop mark is selected as a fault class for its higher occurrence in ISL-Knit. This fault is also known as long loop and drop stitch.

Loop Mark.

Needle mark

A needle mark causes a vertical line along the length or wales of the fabric, which can occur due to broken needles, bent needles, and faulty latches. The needle mark is shown in Fig. 3. In a circular knitting machine, single jersey fabric is produced in a tubular shape if the machine doesn't have a fabric cutter. After dyeing, the tubular fabric is opened by a slitting machine to make an open-width form. To make this process easy and uniform slitting, a needle is intentionally dropped in the circular knitting machine. Dropping the needle causes a vertical mark along the length of the fabric. This mark is not considered a fault for single jersey fabric, but for double jersey fabric, this type of vertical mark is a fault. Vertical marks are faults for single and double jersey fabric if multiple needles become inactive due to dust or damage. In ISL-Knit, all types of vertical marks due to needles are annotated as needle mark, which can be either intentionally or by damaged needles.

Needle Mark.

Set up

This fault is also known as set off in different factories. The larger hole is classified under this class. Some types of knit fabric, like single jersey and single lacoste, have a course constructed with all knit loops. It becomes set up if the yarn is cut in all knit loops. Sometimes, to check the fabric's GSM, the fabric is cut by a GSM cutter, which also creates a large circular hole. In the finishing section, the fabric is cut for the GSM check and shade check. However, these types of larger holes or tears are also classified under set up, though this process is necessary. Several practices can be used to check the GSM. The operators are allowed to check the GSM from the definite portion of the fabric roll. The portion is decided by the industry authority or buyer recommendation. The fabric may be rejected if the operator cuts fabric with a GSM cutter without the predefined portion. For this reason, the hole due to the GSM cutter is classified under set up because it is necessary to detect the hole caused by the GSM cutter, even if it is a necessary process. Examples of set up from our dataset are shown in Fig. 4.

Set Up.

Oil spot

Oil spot is a common type of fabric fault that occurs due to the excess number of oil drops, faulty lubrication system, and sometimes from the operator's hands. No particular shape was observed for the oil spot; however, in most cases, it is circular or oval. This fault is also known as an oil stain. Sometimes, the oil spot becomes a thin line shape. This type of oil spot is known as the oil line. The oil line can be created for the same reason as the oil spot, additionally, oil licks and passes through the needle to the fabric. Though both oil lines and oil spots are created because of oil, the shape is different for oil lines and oil spots. If both oil spot and oil line are classified under the same class, the mAP value is decreased. To overcome this problem, the oil line is not classified under the oil spot. Fig. 5 are examples of oil spots.

Oil Spot.

Hole

Holes can be created due to high machine rpm, problems in the positive feeder, improper knotting, yarn tension variation, and high tension in fabric takedown. According to size, holes are classified under several types, like pin hole, star, pin star, etc., in different factories. These types of faults are created for almost the same reason for creating holes, and the size and shape of these faults are similar to those of holes. In ISL-Knit, these faults are classified under one class: the hole shown in Fig. 6.

Hole.

Contamination

Contamination is also called fly conta, conta, or fly among different industries. The main reasons for contamination are when any foreign particle, like dyed fiber, dead fiber, or husk, deposits on yarn. If any foreign fiber, like polyester or other man-made fiber, becomes a deposit of cotton yarn flying from another feeder or another running machine, the fiber will be trapped on the yarn after knitting. The shape of contamination is like an oil line, which is like a line along a course. Another fabric fault is slub, which is caused due to unevenness of yarn thickness. This fault originated from the spinning of yarn. The shape of the slub is also like contamination. It is difficult to detect separately contamination and slub from the image. If we classify contamination, slub, and oil lines under different classes, the mAP value becomes very low, and the model cannot predict well between these classes. For better detection, we classify contamination, oil line, and slub under one class: contamination Fig. 7.

Contamination.

Yarn missing

Yarn missing is a knitting fault that occurs when a yarn cannot pass the yarn feeder due to yarn breakage or tension variation. In the case of fleece fabric production, the breakage of yarn using in tuck loop causes yarn missing fault. The shape of the yarn missing is like a horizontal line along the course of the fabric. Sometimes, it can be seen along the whole width of the fabric, and sometimes, it is only 30 to 40 cm long. It is also known as apply. Another common fault is lycra break or lycra out. Many knit fabrics are produced using lycra yarn with cotton or polyester yarn to provide elasticity properties. If lycra yarn breaks and the machine does not stop, the lycra break fault arises. The shape of the lycra break is also similar to yarn missing, however, it is thinner than yarn breakage. In our dataset, both yarn missing and lycra break are classified under the yarn missing class. Comparison with fabric satin dataset

In the case of knit fabric fault, the only available public dataset is the Fabric stain dataset. In this section, we compare the Fabric Stain Dataset with the ISL-Knit. The main lack of the fabric stain dataset is that this dataset contains only one type of fault, which is stain, and the number of images is low for deploying an efficient deep learning model. Another problem in the dataset is that the stain pictures are taken too close to the fabric. For this reason, the stain covers the larger portion of images. Some pictures from the Fabric Stain Dataset are shown in Fig. 10. However, for real-time implementation in the machine, the camera attachment is convenient from a distance, the same as the distance of the operator's eye from the fabric. From that distance, the size of the fabric stain will not be as large as in the Fabric Satin Dataset. For real-time implementation, if the camera is attached to the machine at a distance too close to the fabric, the camera will not be able to capture the images of the fabric across its full width. This phenomenon is shown in the Fig. 11. Table 5 compares the Fabric Stain Dataset with ISL-Knit on different points like image information, fault characteristics, and other information. We also train YOLOv5 models with the Fabric Stain Dataset and ISL-Knit. We train nano, small, medium, and large models considering different sizes of images. The mAP value of each test is presented in Table 6. Before training, we split the fabric stain dataset into 60% for training images, 10% for validation, and 30% for training as ISL-Knit. As the Fabric Stain Dataset is a single-class dataset, we train and test ISL-Knit as a single class. The table shows that the mAP value for Fabric Stain Dataset is higher than ISL-Knit for every size of the image and model. The higher mAP in the Fabric Stain Dataset is because it has only one class where defects cover a large portion of images, and most importantly, all the defects are similar in shape. YOLOv5 description

The YOLO (You Only Look Once) is a real-time object detection model which is proposed by Redmon et al. [32]. Several versions of YOLO have been launched. In this research, we chose the YOLOv5 model for its lightweight, moderate training time and easy implementation in low computational devices such as Raspberry Pi. YOLOv5 is a single-stage object detection model consisting of 3 major parts. They are the backbone, neck, and head. New CSP-Darknet53 is used for designing the backbone which is the main part of the model. SPPF (Spatial Pyramid Pooling Fusion) and New CSP-PAN are used as the neck of the model. The head of the YOLOv5 is similar to YOLOv3 made up of three convolution layers that forecast where the bounding boxes (x, y, height, and width), scores, and object classes will be. SiLU and Sigmoid activation functions are used as activation functions in YOLOv5. The YOLOv5 model computes 3 losses: class loss, objectness loss, and location loss. The class loss and objectness loss are computed by Binary Cross Entropy, and location loss is computed by CIoU (Complete Intersection over Union).

From this equation, we see that if we want to create an automated four-point inspection system, we should measure the faults by inches. However, the unit length of images is pixel. So, we must convert the pixel value to the inches to determine the length of faults for the four-point inspection system. We can convert the pixel value to inches by setting the camera to a fixed distance and measuring its horizontal and vertical coverage. However, this theoretical approach will contain some errors. By running the YOLOv5 program, we can detect faults with the bounding box and measure the pixel of the bounding box. The YOLOv5 creates bounding boxes by anchor. So, the dimension of bounding boxes is fixed. If we see the detection images, we can see that the dimensions of the bounding boxes are slightly larger than the actual faults. We do several tasks to determine the best possible relation between the bounding box dimension and the fault dimension. The steps taken for this process are illustrated in Fig. 16.

Block diagram of four-point system development.

Fabric and dataset preparation

We create artificial fabric faults with definite lengths for pixel-to-inch mapping. To measure the length of the fault correctly, we create square-shaped faults on a single jersey fabric, which is free from machinery faults. The fabric type is a single jersey, the GSM is 150, the stitch length of the fabric is 2.6 mm, the yarn type is 100% cotton, and the yarn count is 30/1 (Ne). The length we choose for faults starts from 0.5 inches to 12 inches with 0.5-inch intervals. We create several faults for one size to measure the average pixels. Fig. 17 shows the images of artificial faults. Then, the fabric is loaded into the inspection machine in our university lab. As the real fabric faults like setup or hole are not square-shaped, ISL-Knit trained model faces difficulty detecting these faults. We take images of faults from the inspection machine to make models familiar with these artificial faults. A total of 216 images are taken, annotated, and split into train and validation sets. These images are added to ISL-Knit, and we train the YOLOv5 medium model with it. These images with artificial faults are made only to measure the pixel value for specific lengths. The actual dataset will not contain any artificial faults. As single jersey knit fabric has edge curling property, the edge of the faults curls and the dimension of the faults changes slightly. We iron the fabric before taking pictures and pixel data to reduce the curling.

Fabric preparation.

Camera installation

We installed an ASUS ROG eye camera in the inspection machine for data collection. The installation setup is shown in Fig. 18 (A). The distance between the camera and the fabric surface is 15 inches. This distance is very important throughout the pixel-to-inch mapping process. If the distance between the camera and fabric changes, the pixel value for a specific fault length will also change. For industrial implementation, the camera must be calibrated for a fixed distance between the camera and the fabric surface for the specific structured inspection machine. The horizontal length coverage of the Asus webcam for 15 inches distance is 24 inches, and the vertical length coverage is 15 inches. These lengths are illustrated in Fig. 18 (B). Our webcam-based approach can cover 24 inches of fabric width. Generally, knit dyeing industries produce up to 72 inches of fabric in the width direction. To cover 72 inches, the system will require multiple cameras and more GPU power. This research has not done a comparative study of GPU consumption for multiple cameras.

Problem encountered and recommended solution

Table 18 indicates the performance of our dataset and model in a real scenario. From the table, we can observe that the f1 score of Yarn Missing is very low. There are two reasons behind the low score. One is that the yarn missing problem rarely occurs, so we have low instances of that fault, and another reason is the horizontal line because of the light source. The horizontal line for the light source causes the misclassification of yarn missing. The fluorescent light source causes horizontal lines in the camera shown in Fig. 27. Other types of light sources with flickering characteristics operating on AC electric systems can cause the same problems because of the high FPS of the camera. To overcome this problem, the camera FPS can be adjusted so as not to catch the light flickering. Non-flickering light sources such as DC electric system-powered light can also be a solution. As knit fabric has edge-curling properties, it causes temporary creases. Because of these creases, our model misclassified the background as needle marks and contamination (as they are vertical lines shaped like creases). The problem is shown in Fig. 27. The problem can be solved by maintaining proper tension on the fabric so that no temporary crease occurs. The tension should be optimal, as high tension can change the stitch length of the fabric.

Horizontal line and temporary crease.

Conclusion and future work

To reduce the workload and maintain consistent, accurate fabric quality, a deep learning-based fault detection system can be a great tool in the textile industry's automation field. The lack of knit fabric fault data is the primary motivation for our research. To overcome this problem, we build a benchmark dataset consisting of 3375 images and 20679 instances, considering seven types of knit fabric faults. The research also shows that it is possible to obtain mAP@0.5 0.69 from the YOLOv5 medium model. Collecting more images of knit fabric faults will lead to higher accuracy.

Existing related work is either based on woven fabric faults or trained with small datasets. The four-point system development is also a novel task in existing work. A problem for an automated four-point inspection system is that the model may count a fault multiple times, generating the wrong result. However, this problem can be solved by creating a region of interest or ROI. The area of ROI should be in such a way that the fault will only count a single time when it enters the ROI. In the future, we plan to complete the implementation of the four-point inspection system by synchronizing the area of ROI with fabric speed considering other factors like the FPS of the model, the computational power of GPU, FPS of webcam, model size, and type of model. In the future, we also plan to optimize hyperparameters in YOLO models or build novel fault detection models with higher accuracy or lower inference time using ISL-Knit.
