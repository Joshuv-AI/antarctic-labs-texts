Ethylene glycol toxicity presenting as a rapidly deteriorating focal neurological exam and reversible cerebral herniation syndrome
Authors: Andrea L Klein, Hayley H Rose-Inman, Paul E Stromberg, Allison N Seifert, Andrew B Moore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEthylene glycol toxicity is an exceedingly rare toxic encephalopathy. Ethylene glycol is the primary toxin in antifreeze and exhibits its toxicologic effects via metabolites glycolic and oxalic acids, driving a profound metabolic acidemia. Classically, ethylene glycol toxicity presents as inebriation without focal neurological deficits. Here we describe a case of a healthy older adult who ingested antifreeze and presented with 1 h of dysarthria, left-sided facial droop, and left-sided hemiplegia and rapidly progressed to a clinical cerebral herniation syndrome from which he ultimately fully recovered. This case is an example of the breadth of altered mental status presentations that are possible with ethylene glycol toxicity, while modeling the classic laboratory and imaging findings of the toxic encephalopathy. The epidemiology, pathophysiology, diagnosis, management, and prognosis of ethylene glycol toxicity will be discussed.

Introduction

Ethylene glycol toxicity is an exceedingly rare toxic encephalopathy. Ethylene glycol is the primary toxin in antifreeze and exhibits its toxicologic effects via metabolites glycolic and oxalic acids, driving a profound metabolic acidemia. Classically, ethylene glycol toxicity presents within 12 h of ingestion as inebriation with neurologic symptoms of altered mental status, slurred speech, ataxia, and Central Nervous System depression [1], [2]. As toxic metabolites accumulate in the lungs and heart, cardiopulmonary dysfunction develops 12–36 h after ingestion. This stage can overlap with neurologic symptoms depending on the severity of ingestion [2]. Finally, 24–72 h following ingestion, progressive renal injury occurs [2]. In terms of continued neurologic deficits, beyond the acute phase, cranial nerve damage can occur as a delayed complication, with facial nerve dysfunction being the most commonly reported [3]. Rarely, cerebral edema has been documented [3]. However, in practice, the presentation of ethylene glycol toxicity may be nonspecific and mistaken for other acute neurological events.

Here we describe a case of a healthy older adult who ingested antifreeze and presented with 1 h of dysarthria, left-sided facial droop, and left-sided hemiplegia and rapidly progressed to a clinical cerebral herniation syndrome from which he ultimately fully recovered. The epidemiology, pathophysiology, diagnosis, management, and prognosis of ethylene glycol toxicity will be discussed.

Case report

A 73-year-old male with past medical history of well-controlled chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) not on home oxygen and extensive remote spinal surgery was brought in by emergency medical services (EMS) as a field stroke alert for one hour of dysarthria, left-sided facial droop, and left-sided hemiplegia. Arrival vitals were 94.1 F rectally, pulse 80s-90s, blood pressure was in the 220 s systolic, respiratory rate in the twenties, and oxygen saturation in the 80 s requiring 6 L oxygen via nasal cannula. Neurological status for EMS was reportedly Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 14 losing 1 point for confusion and a positive Cincinnati stroke scale for left sided weakness. Neurological status reportedly declined en route. He rapidly decompensated further to a GCS of 7 (E4V2M1) and a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) of 42. He was audibly gurgling with visible upper respiratory secretions, was not protecting his airway, and had a declining neurological exam, so the decision was made to intubate immediately. Prior to intubation, his neurological exam further deteriorated into decorticate posturing with a markedly dilated and sluggishly reactive right pupil and the loss of cough, gag, or corneal reflexes. He was intubated successfully. He remained hypertensive and was started on a nicardipine drip to bring his systolic below 160 mmHg given concern for active herniation due to hemorrhagic stroke. Endotracheal tube placement was confirmed with x-ray, and he was taken to CT. Per institutional stroke protocol, non-contrast CT of the head, CT angiography of the head, and cerebral perfusion scans were performed; all three scans were negative for acute pathology. The patient’s children revealed critical pertinent history. The patient’s son reported that his father (who had previous alcohol use disorder but had been sober for twenty years) had seemed “off” that morning and sounded drunk, but he did not keep alcohol in his home. The siblings checked on their father three times that day and had requested a police well-check. Eventually, the son came home from work and found his father laying in his own waste, urinating copiously, and with left sided weakness and slurred speech, prompting the EMS call. The patient’s daughter revealed that the patient and his wife of fifty years recently separated, which she proposed as a potential trigger for an out-of-character drinking episode.

An intensive care unit (ICU) bed was procured as results of the work up returned. Notable findings included an initial pH of < 7.00 with an undetectable bicarbonate, serum osmolality gap of 58 (calculation: 363-(2[145]+244/18 +5.0/2.8), estimated ethylene glycol concentration of 378.2 mg/dL (formula: osmolal gap*6.2 = ethylene glycol mg/dL), serum lactic acid of 5.0, and urine containing calcium oxalate crystals. The patient was transferred to the ICU, where he received continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) in the form of continuous veno-venous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF), fomepizole (15 mg/kg as a loading dose, then 10 mg/kg), high dose thiamine (500 mg every 8 h), and a bicarbonate drip. A serum ethylene glycol level was obtained the next day by the ICU team and was 15 mg/dL 12 h after initiation of therapy, and 24 h after presentation. His ammonia was repeated and down to 51 μmol/L the day following his presentation. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed during his hospitalization demonstrated small scattered acute infarctions of the left parietal and occipital lobes, as well as subacute anoxic brain injury and/or toxic metabolic process in the bilateral thalamus, putamen, globus pallidus, and caudate, and restricted diffusion in the bilateral cerebellum (Fig. 1). The patient eventually made a full neurological recovery after seven days. His estranged wife confirmed with hospital staff that he had told her he was suicidal and purchased several gallons of antifreeze over the past week. He subsequently underwent outpatient psychiatric treatment. His renal function gradually improved and he was discharged on hemodialysis (HD) but has since recovered his renal function, no longer requiring HD.

Discussion

Ethylene glycol toxicity is an exceedingly rare toxic encephalopathy in the twenty-first century. According to the American Association for Poison Control’s National Poison Data System the annual incidence of ethylene glycol toxicity was approximately 6100 in 2022, with the majority of cases occurring as intentional suicidal ingestions in adults over 20 years of age [4]. Ethylene glycol toxicity mimics ethanol toxicity. It begins as clinical inebriation without significant laboratory abnormalities, progressing to varying degrees of altered mental status with an elevated osmolality gap, metabolic acidosis, and renal injury or failure [5]. The initial clinical manifestation of ethylene glycol toxicity is vague and should be considered in any patient presenting with altered mental status due to suspected ethanol intoxication, sedating medication overdose, or any number of other encephalopathies. Additionally, ethylene glycol toxicity may occur without clinical inebriation; lack of inebriation does not rule out ethylene glycol toxicity. Patients often have a pseudo-elevation in lactic acid due to glycolic acid being detected by some point-of-care analyzers as lactate, while serum lactic acid analyzers that use a lactate dehydrogenase assay do not cross react [6]. The majority of patients with toxic alcohol ingestion that is promptly identified and treated appropriately will recover clinically, with the worst outcomes occurring in those with delayed presentation or extreme ingestion [7]. In many hospital systems, ethylene glycol testing is not available in a timely fashion, making recognition and empiric management of ethylene glycol toxicity even more paramount.

The underlying pathophysiology of ethylene glycol toxicity is the generation of the toxic metabolites glycolic acid and oxalic acid, which drive the profound metabolic acidosis and renal injury, respectively. This metabolic process relies upon the same enzymes as ethanol metabolism. Treatment consists of fomepizole (an alcohol dehydrogenase inhibitor) for patients with a serum ethylene glycol level above 25 mg/dL, or in patients for whom the provider has strong clinical suspicion for toxic alcohol poisoning. Thiamine repletion can further assist, as it reduces the formation of oxalic acid and aids in the treatment of any alcohol-induced Wernicke’s encephalopathy [8]. Continuous renal replacement therapy or hemodialysis is reserved for patients with concurrent renal failure, severe metabolic acidosis (as in this patient), or severe electrolyte abnormalities.

Classically, the altered mental status associated with ethylene glycol toxicity ranges in severity from a mild dysarthria to severe confusion with altered sensorium. This case is particularly notable because of how rapidly and markedly the patient’s neurological status declined. The patient displayed the classic signs of “inebriation” throughout the day and only began to precipitously decline later in his course. Over the span of approximately 90 min, his neurological exam transitioned from mild confusion with a GCS 14 to a right middle cerebral artery (MCA) syndrome to decorticate posturing with a blown pupil and partial Cushing’s reflex, suggestive of impending herniation. However, his noncontrast CT, CT angiogram, and CT perfusion were all negative for any acute pathology. MRI has remarkable utility and consistency in identifying toxic-metabolic encephalopathies, and the diffuse insults to the bilateral thalamus, putamen, globus pallidus, and caudate, and restricted diffusion in the bilateral cerebellum are somewhat classic findings in toxic alcohol and chronic alcohol encephalopathies [9]. There is evidence to suggest that these MRI findings are reversible [10].

The most unique facet of this case is the focality of the patient’s neurological exam and rapidity of his neurological decline. Classically, ethylene glycol toxicity presents with a generalized altered mental status and does not have localizable deficits [1], [2]. To date, there have been two documented cases of rapidly declining neurological exams in patients with ethylene glycol toxicity. Both had nonfocal exams that progressed to clinical cerebral herniation syndromes with evidence of active herniation due to cerebral edema on brain imaging [11], [12]. Both of these patients were ultimately declared brain dead, however the patient in this case made a complete neurological recovery. There are documented case reports of focal cranial nerve deficits in ethylene glycol toxicity, however rarely manifesting as a full large vessel stroke-like syndrome [13]. There is evidence to suggest the localizable deficits in this case are due to calcium oxalate crystal deposition in the large cerebral vessels [14], [15], [16].

Conclusion

This case is an example of the breadth of neurological presentations that are possible with ethylene glycol toxicity, while modeling the classic laboratory and imaging findings of this toxic encephalopathy. Although there are case reports of focal cranial nerve deficits and rapidly deteriorating neurological exams from ethylene glycol toxicity, rarely has a patient presented with a focal, large-vessel stroke-like neurological exam with rapid deterioration [11], [12], [13]. In isolation, this patient’s presentation was concerning for a plethora of other encephalopathic etiologies, however in combination with collateral history and marked lab abnormalities, the appropriate diagnosis was made early (without waiting for send out labs) and treatment was rapidly initiated with a positive outcome.
