Early Structural Lung Changes in Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia (PCD)
Authors: Phil Robinson, Megumi Yokote
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionPrimary Ciliary Dyskinesia (PCD) is an inherited disorder commonly presenting as an autosomal recessive condition causing dysfunction of muco‐ciliary clearance, which can lead to bronchiectasis. As newborn screening is not available for PCD, diagnosis is frequently delayed, often resulting in a degree of structural lung disease being detectable at or soon after diagnosis. Once the diagnosis is established, initiation of therapies including airway clearance aim to prevent or retard any progression to irreversible structural damage.ObjectivesWe aimed to investigate the degree of structural changes evident in early life in PCD by examining chest CT scans in a cross‐sectional study in children with PCD aged 3 years of age.Methods15 young children with proven PCD (young cwPCD) had surveillance chest CT scanning when clinically stable. Introduction

Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia (PCD) is an uncommon, inherited disorder, characterised by defective motile cilial structure or function, leading to impaired muco‐ciliary clearance [1, 2]. This condition typically manifests as chronic productive cough and recurrent respiratory infections, which may lead to bronchiectasis and irreversible lung damage. In the upper airways, impaired muco‐ciliary clearance may manifest as chronic suppurative nasal discharge and recurrent otitis media, potentially resulting in hearing loss and sinusitis.

PCD is often compared to another inherited suppurative lung disease—Cystic Fibrosis (CF) [3]. While it is estimated that up to 80% of infants with PCD will have a form of neonatal respiratory distress, often commencing in the first day of life [4], such early respiratory symptoms are uncommon in infants born with CF [5]. Furthermore, while most cases of CF are diagnosed early in life by newborn screening programs, the diagnosis of PCD is often delayed, in part as the clinical presentation of PCD often overlaps with common paediatric respiratory symptoms such as productive cough, nasal congestion and recurrent otitis media [2]. Given its progressive nature, early diagnosis of PCD is important to minimise disease progression through regular surveillance and commencement of effective interventive therapies [6].

Monitoring Progression via Structural Changes

High Resolution chest CT scanning (HRCT) is considered by most to be the gold standard for the detection of early lung changes in suppurative lung diseases such as PCD and CF [7]. Previous studies have shown that people with PCD (pwPCD) show a range of structural lung disease including bronchiectasis, bronchial wall thickening, collapse/consolidation and air trapping on HRCT [8, 9]. Further studies have shown that PCD lung changes do not occur in the same regional lobar frequency as CF [10]. Specifically, newborns with PCD who develop neonatal respiratory symptoms, frequently have collapse/consolidation restricted to the upper lobes [4]. In contrast, in older children with PCD (> 5 years of age, (cwPCD), relative sparing of the upper lobes from structural lung disease has been described [9, 10]. Our research group has published findings that pwPCD have differing structural lung changes to those of people with CF, suggesting that CF‐derived HRCT scoring systems may not capture the full extent of PCD structural damage [10, 11, 12]. We have more recently described the SPEC (Specific PCD Evaluation by CT) score, a PCD derived, specific scoring system in an older group of pwPCD, which correlates well with lung function measurements allowing structure function correlations to be considered [13].

While the spectrum of HRCT structural lung changes in PCD has been described across a wide range of age groups, no detailed analysis of the spectrum of PCD lung disease in young children has been described. We therefore aimed to describe the range and frequency of structural lung changes in a cohort of young children with PCD (cwPCD) to determine the extent of structural damage present early in life. We believe such findings may enhance understanding of PCD lung disease and suggest appropriate timing for radiological screening in pwPCD.

Methods

We retrospectively examined the chest computed tomography (CT) images of 15 young cwPCD who were receiving their routine PCD clinical care at the Royal Children's Hospital in Melbourne, Australia and who had undergone a surveillance CT scan at around 3 years of age. Scans were conducted over a 6‐year period between 2018 and 2024. Chest CT scanning at 3 years of age has been part of our unit's clinical care program since 2016. All included patients had a definitive diagnosis of PCD according to diagnostic criteria as summarised by ATS and ERS guidelines [6, 14]. Patients required at least two of the major clinical symptoms of PCD: unexplained neonatal respiratory distress, laterality defects, evidence of a chronic upper, or lower airway suppurative disease, productive cough or recurrent sinusitis/coryza. In addition, patients had at least two of the following: presence of bi‐allelic PCD‐causing mutations, ultrastructural ciliary abnormalities on electron microscopy, or ciliary waveform abnormalities recognized in PCD seen on high‐speed video‐microscopy. At diagnosis all cwPCD in our service are prescribed twice daily airway clearance under instruction from an experienced pediatric chest physiotherapist. Inhaled mucolytic agents are not used in our PCD service at this age.

The study received ethical approval from The Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC), ethics approval number 35214 A. The study did not involve direct patient contact, as only retrospective CT scans performed for clinical use formed the basis for the study. As such, no patient consent was required as part of the ethics approval.

All scans were considered eligible for inclusion if patients had undergone scans at, or around, 3 years of age, at a time of clinical stability, and where the scans were of satisfactory quality (i.e. with greater than 100 separate contiguous images in the inspiratory phase).

The CT scanner and scanning protocol used has been previously described and was the same for all scans performed [10, 13]. All young cwPCD were clinically at baseline at the time of the scan and were scanned as part of our unit's biennial medical imaging surveillance program, which commences at 3 years of age. All scans were conducted under general anaesthesia. Additionally, while under the same anaesthesia, a single tracheal aspirate was obtained. A formal bronchial lavage was not performed.

HRCT scans were independently assessed by authors PR and MY. Scans were initially evaluated by MY, a medical student who had undergone training in reviewing CT scans from a paediatric radiologist with extensive experience in reporting paediatric chest CT scans. Emphasis in this training was in the identification of bronchiectasis, mucus plugging, bronchial wall thickening and air trapping. MY then completed additional training on a set of 20 CT scans from pwPCD and did not progress to assess the study scans until the scan scores achieved good correlation with scores made by PR, a paediatric respiratory physician with extensive experience in the use of CT scanning in describing structural changes in suppurative lung disease [7, 10, 12, 13, 15, 16]. Scans were deidentified before scoring.

Chest CT Analysis

CT scans were observed for presence and the degree of: (a) bronchiectasis, (b) bronchial wall thickening, (c) collapse/consolidation and (d) air trapping, with each lobe being scored separately using the PCD specific scoring system, the SPEC score [13]. Scores were assigned 0‐ absent, 1‐ for mild (< 50% of the lobe) severity, and 2‐ for severe (> 50% of lobe) severity. The degree of severity for each structural change was determined for each patient.

Bronchiectasis was defined when the bronchus was greater than 0.9 of the diameter to the corresponding blood vessel, or the bronchus was non‐tapering [16]. Bronchial wall thickening was assessed subjectively, when the bronchus was thicker than normal healthy airways [8].

In order to provide a more detailed description of changes, scans were then reassessed by PR on two separate occasions, using the following criteria: less than 15% lobar involvement, 15%–30% lobar involvement and 30%–45% lobar involvement for each structural change. As these are qualitative assessments it was not felt feasible to divide assessments into smaller proportions.

Results were then analysed by underlying structural abnormality, as well as results for upper versus lower zones. Upper zones were defined as the amalgamation of right and left upper lobes, while the lower zones were defined as the amalgamation of the right and left lower lobes, as well as the right middle lobe and lingula, as has been previously described by other authors [3, 17].

Statistical Analysis

Young cwPCD were described as a group, as well as within subtypes of defects. The subtypes include: “Inner Dynein Arm” defect (IDA), “Outer Dynein Arm” defect (ODA), and “Other”—which included transposition defects, central defects, as well as defects which could not be determined, or that did not have identifiable ultrastructural defects. Structural changes seen in patients with heterotaxy were also compared to those with situs solitis.

Differences between total right lung and left lung changes, right sided vs left sided upper lobe changes, right sided vs left sided lower lobe changes were described using non parametric methods. While the standard test for non‐parametric data is the Wilcoxon rank sum test, this is not discriminatory in our data set where over 95% of data score either 0 or 1. As such we described the data using the method of McNemar's statistical test for paired nominal data. This method employs 2 × 2 contingency tables with a dichotomous trait. Data is thus represented

where x is the number of patients where RUL = 0 and LUL = 0 and y is the number of patients where RUL = 0 and LUL = 1 etc. This allows description of the consistency or discrepancy between the two sets. Tables have been constructed comparing right upper lobes against left upper lobes, right lower lobes against left lower lobes and upper lobes against lower lobes. These tables are included in an attached supplement [18].

All scans were reviewed by PR on 2 occasions at least a week apart, blinded to the results from the original assessment. Intraobserver reliability was assessed using the ICC (two way mixed effects model) and Bland Altman analysis [19]. In general, ICC between 0.4 and 0.6 is considered moderate, 0.6–0.8 good, and above 0.8 excellent [19].

Results

Fifteen young cwPCD, who had surveillance lung HRCT at around 3 years of age, were included in this study. (Table 1) There were 8 (53%) male and 7 (47%) female patients. Five had IDA defects, 7 had ODA defects, and 3 had CP defects. Six patients had situs inversus totalis, 1 patient had dextrocardia and 8 patients had situs solitus. The median age at PCD diagnosis was 1.1 years (range: 4 months to 3.5 years), and the median age at HRCT was 3.6 years (range: 2.5–4.3 years).

Frequency of Lobar Structural Lung Changes

Structural changes were seen in all young cwPCD. All structural changes were more frequent in lower lobes and least frequent in upper lobes. (Table 2 and Supplement 1). Bronchial wall thickening was seen in at least one lobe in 12 (80%) patients, bronchiectasis was seen in 11 patients (73%), air trapping in 9 patients (60%) and collapse consolidation in 13 patients (87%). Bronchial wall thickening was present in 23% of upper lobes, 46% of middle lobes and 80% of lower lobes. Bronchiectasis was seen in 13% of upper lobes, 37% of middle lobes and 63% of lower lobes. Air trapping was seen in 10% of upper lobes, 17% of middle lobes and 33% of lower lobes. Collapse consolidation was seen in 10% of upper lobes, 43% of middle lobes and 43% of lower lobes. There was no difference between right and left sided lung changes.

When results of identified structural changes were correlated against the underlying structural abnormality, specifically, IDA defect, ODA defect, or CP defect, there was no difference in the frequency of either mild bronchiectasis or mild bronchial wall thickening. One young cwPCD with IDA defect had all lobes with mild bronchiectasis. Two patients with ODA defects had 4 lobes which showed evidence of bronchiectasis. There was no difference in the frequency of structural changes seen in patients with heterotaxy compared to those with situs solitus.

Intraobserver Reliability

The intraobserver reliability for the repeat scoring was 0.8 for bronchial wall thickening, 0.84 for bronchiectasis, 0.87 for air trapping and 0.87 for collapse consolidation.

Microbiology

In 12 young cwPCD, a lower airway sample was obtained by direct lower airway suctioning immediately after the CT scan procedure. In 4 young cwPCD, no bacterial pathogens were identified. In 8 infants, moderate to profuse growth of organisms were obtained including Streptococcus pneumonia in 4 cases, Haemophilus parainfluenza in 4 cases. In one case Haemophilus parainfluenza was isolated in conjunction with Pseudomonas aeruginosa. This patient had the most severe degree of structural lung disease.

Discussion

In this study, we have shown that in children as young as 3‐years‐old, there is evidence of structural airway changes, including the presence of mild bronchial wall thickening in 80% of patients and early bronchiectasis in over 70% of patients. These findings highlight the importance of early diagnosis as well as the need for early initiation of mucolytic therapy and airway clearance techniques for young cwPCD. In addition, the spectrum of abnormalities found in our study does, we believe, justify the use of CT scanning as an assessment tool in this young age group.

Approximately 80% of infants with PCD will manifest neonatal respiratory symptoms. Mullowney et al have described that in up to 70% of cases, upper lobe collapse is evident. In our present study of young cwPCD the spectrum of structural changes is more in keeping with previous studies in older patients with PCD showing relative upper lobe sparing and increased frequency of changes across the middle and lower lobes [8, 9, 10, 13].

While structural changes were seen in all patients, they were generally mild in nature affecting less than 15% of a lobe. More significant changes of collapse/consolidation were seen in 5 patients, while 2 patients showed bronchiectatic changes in 15%–30% of a lobe (1 patient) and 30%–45% (1 patient) in a single lobe. The small numbers of patients in each mutational class makes it impossible to draw meaningful relationships between structural changes and mutational class as has been previously described in a larger group of older pwPCD by Davis et al. [20]

As there are no community based newborn screening programs for PCD, as there are for CF, patients will typically present with clinical features of PCD before diagnosis is considered. It is estimated that up to 80% of infants born with PCD have neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, frequently associated with transient upper lobe collapse [4]. Therefore, there are often early suggestive signs of the need to consider PCD, but in many cases these suggestive symptoms are not recognised and diagnostic testing not performed. Similarly, the presence of heterotaxy, particularly when identified in the neonatal period as part of the investigation for neonatal respiratory distress, should suggest consideration of PCD. Following the immediate neonatal period, many young cwPCD present with recurrent productive coughs, nasal congestion and recurrent otitis media with reduced hearing. As these are often seen in otherwise healthy infants consideration of PCD may be further delayed [2]. While early diagnosis of PCD may be an important factor in preventing progressive lung damage there is little evidence of any effective therapy achieving this aim once the diagnosis of PCD is made. As a condition with impaired airway clearance, chest physiotherapy techniques would appear essential in optimising airway clearance and prevention of mucus retention in the airway and subsequent bronchial thickening and progression to bronchiectasis. All patients in our service are commenced on regular airway clearance at diagnosis. The prevalence of structural changes described in our study may suggest that further additional interventive therapies may be required to delay the onset of structural damage and bronchiectasis.

Studies comparing different techniques of airway clearance in PCD are lacking. Given the higher prevalence of lower lobe disease seen in our current study, and previously reported by other investigators, it could be postulated that this finding should be taken into consideration when structuring airway clearance programs for PCD. The evidence for a benefit of mucolytic therapy is also lacking in PCD, as opposed to CF however El‐Abiad et al described improvement in clinical status and lung function of 2 patients with PCD after the inhalation of rDNAse. The authors did not describe any assessment of structural lung changes pre and post treatment [21].

As our study was cross sectional in nature, the alteration over time of these structural changes cannot be determined. Mills et al in a study of 142 children with proven bronchiectasis (median age 5 years) found radiographic resolution on repeat CT scanning in 57 of 142 patients (40.1%), improvement in 56 of 142 (39.4%), and no change or worsening in 29 of 142 (20.4%) [22]. Only 6 of the 142 patients studied had PCD, with post infective bronchiectasis being the commonest cause of bronchiectasis in this group. As PCD is a progressive incurable condition it is unlikely that a similar degree of improvement over time would be observed. In a longitudinal study of 142 older pwPCD (mean age at first scan 8.5 years) Kinghorn and colleagues found progression of overall disease, degree of bronchiectasis and degree of mucus plugging in subsequent scans over a mean of 5 years [23].

There are several limitations to our study. While we describe the structural lung changes in a group of 3‐year‐olds with PCD, the 15 cases present a small group and limits conclusions relevant to the larger PCD population. Comparative studies addressing genotype or structural class changes (ODA vs IDA vs CP and other) against clinical outcomes such as structural changes on CT require much larger study numbers for definitive conclusions. Our original study methodology using the PCD specific SPEC score was insensitive in describing the milder degree of structural changes seen in this younger age group and future studies in this age group will need to consider more refined ways of describing early structural changes, as we have attempted to do in this present study. The increased radiation exposure related to CT scanning compared to that from plain chest X‐ray must be balanced against the additional detailed structural information obtained from CT scanning and will need to be decided by clinicians on an individual patient basis.

Considerable literature exists showing the deleterious effect of early bacterial infection of the lower airway in CF and associated structural changes seen on chest CT scanning [5]. 12 patients in our study had lower airway suction specimens obtained with bacteria being identified in 8 patients. While the one patient in whom Pseudomonas Aeruginosa was isolated had the most significant degree of bronchiectasis, it is difficult to draw direct conclusions between structural changes and the isolation of bacteria as the sampling was done by a suction specimen from the lower airway and not a direct bronchial lavage. The small number of bacteria isolated, limits our ability to draw specific conclusions on the role of bacteria on the development of structural lung disease in children with PCD.

In summary, we believe our findings identify that structural lung changes occur early in young children with PCD, emphasizing the critical need for early diagnosis and prompt implementation of effective airway clearance techniques. While all patients described in our current report had been prescribed airway clearance at diagnosis the spectrum of structural lung changes seen in this group would suggest that current airway clearance techniques may at best slow but not prevent the progression of structural changes, and suggest the need for further assessment of more effective airway clearance techniques in this young age group.
