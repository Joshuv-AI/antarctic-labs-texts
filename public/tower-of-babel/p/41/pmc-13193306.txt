Pilot Study: Vancomycin Calcium Sulfate Beads in the Prevention of Recurrent Capsular Contracture of Breast Implants
Authors: Rex E. Moulton-Barrett, Nicholas P. Jewell, Aashi Rathod
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Prevention and management of capsular contracture of the breast remain the most challenging complications after breast augmentation and reconstruction. The use of resorbable calcium sulfate beads containing antibiotics within the breast pocket has been found to reduce implant extrusion from infections. No previous studies have determined whether biofilm-related recurrent capsular contracture (RCC) of the breast can be reduced using similar antibiotic beads at the time of surgery.Methods:A retrospective case series study by 1 surgeon, conducted between 2006 and 2025, was performed to determine whether placing calcium sulfate beads with either 500 mg or 1 g of vancomycin within a breast pocket, versus no beads, following capsulectomy and implant exchange could prevent RCC. Thirty-eight patients underwent 40 procedures involving 57 breasts. RCC was defined as Spear-Baker grade III or IV.Results:The use of antibiotic beads was associated with a significant reduction in RCC (P < 0.001). Patients without beads, considering all other factors, had 15 times greater odds of developing RCC. This risk was reduced to 8 times greater odds when accounting for length of follow-up.Conclusions:This pilot study supports the use of calcium sulfate beads in combination with vancomycin for the prevention of RCC.

Takeaways

Question: Can dissolvable antibiotic beads reduce recurrent capsular contracture of the breast?

Findings: A retrospective clinical series compared vancomycin beads with no beads at the time of capsulectomy and implant replacement. The beads were associated with a significant reduction in recurrent capsular contracture (P < 0.001).

Meaning: This pilot study provides a simple, inexpensive alternative for reducing the recurrence of capsular contracture following surgery.

INTRODUCTION

Breast augmentation for cosmetic and reconstructive purposes is an established cornerstone of plastic surgery, with more than 430,000 procedures performed in the United States in 2024.1 The most common sequela of breast implantation that requires reoperation is capsular contracture.2 Although encapsulation of the implant is a normal physiological response, pathology arises when the capsule becomes thick and contracts, and may become painful, hard, and disfiguring. Various grading classifications have been proposed, including the Spear-Baker grading scale,3 with grades III and IV considered clinically significant (Table 1).

The true incidence of capsular contracture remains unclear owing to variability in reported rates and inclusion criteria, such as grade II versus III and/or unilateral versus bilateral contracture. According to premarket approval studies, capsular contracture was reported to be lowest among patients undergoing primary breast augmentation (11.4%–17.5% per breast) and higher after revision surgery or reconstruction (24.6%–35.7%).4–6

The etiology of capsular contracture is not fully understood; however, inflammatory and immunologic responses are thought to be associated with it.7 Subclinical bacterial infection, or biofilm, as a risk factor, provides the strongest supporting evidence. A strong association has been reported between the presence of coagulase-negative Staphylococcus (CNS) and Propionibacterium (PSS). and grades III and IV capsular contractures.8 Factors associated with higher rates of capsular contracture can be divided into before, during, and after surgery (Table 2),9–33 with varying and often disputed support in the literature.

Previously, the standard treatment for clinically significant capsular contracture of grades III and IV has been capsulectomy, site change wherever possible, and implant exchange. The use of acellular dermal matrix (ADM) has been reported to result in lower rates of recurrent capsular contracture (RCC).34 However, if the success of ADMs comes from mechanical protection of the implant surface from bacteria or from the locally active, persistent antibiotics soaked within the material, it would be useful to find a less expensive and less invasive alternative.

The purpose of this study is to determine whether the use of resorbable vancomycin-loaded calcium sulfate beads in the implant pocket of patients undergoing surgery for capsular contracture could reduce the rate of RCC.

METHODS

All patients included in the study were evaluated pre- and postoperatively by 1 surgeon, and long-term evaluation was performed in person and by telephone interviews and text messages. This retrospective clinical series evaluated 38 patients who underwent 40 procedures, which included 57 breast implants, for RCC Spear-Baker grade III or IV between 2007 and 2025. Inclusion criteria included breast augmentation or reconstruction, and patients were included regardless of prior chest radiation therapy, ADM use, implant type (saline or silicone; textured or smooth), or history of multiple breast procedures. Patients with comorbidity, including renal failure, or those who failed to provide informed consent for any reason were excluded from the study. Forty-two of the 57 breasts in 27 of the 38 patients had previously undergone breast augmentation, and 15 breasts in 11 patients had prior breast reconstruction. The patients were divided into 3 groups: 13 patients with 20 breast implants who did not receive any antibiotic beads between 2007 and 2015, 8 patients with 12 breast implants who received 500 mg of vancomycin beads per breast between 2011 and 2017, and a final group of 17 patients with 25 implants who received 1 g of vancomycin beads per breast between 2016 and 2025. Institutional review board exemption was obtained (Pearl Institutional Review Board: study no. 17-RMB-101); all patients signed written consent for the use of antibiotic beads; and the study complied with Public Health Institute and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act regulations, as set forth in the Privacy Rule 164.512(i)(ii)(iii).

The surgical procedures varied by case because patients had a unique history of breast disease and reconstructive challenges. Capsulectomy procedures were divided into total, subtotal, or partial (Table 3). The author’s choice was for total capsulectomy whenever possible. Partial capsulectomy was chosen in thinner patients with adherent capsules in the submuscular pockets, particularly when there was adherence to the intercostal musculature, to avoid a pneumothorax and/or unnecessary bleeding. All patients in this series had prior implants placed in a subpectoral plane, and after capsulectomy, no change of pocket to a submammary plane was performed. Following capsulectomy and before placement of vancomycin beads, gloves were changed, and between 2006 and 2014, the pockets were irrigated with a triple antibiotic solution (1 g cephazolin, 1 g vancomycin, and 100 mg gentamicin in 1 L of normal saline), including irrigation of the implants and instruments and replacement of surgeons’ and assistants’ gloves. After 2014, the irrigation solution for instruments and gloves remained the same, but pocket irrigation consisted of a 0.025% hypochlorous acid solution followed by 10% Betadine solution, and the implant was irrigated with 10% Betadine solution alone. The no-touch technique was used whenever possible. Calcium sulfate mini-bead sets (500 mg) were obtained from either Osteoset (Wright Medical/Stryker; Food and Drug Administration [FDA] 510(k) K053642, 2005) or Stimulan Rapid Cure (Biocomposites, Keele, UK; FDA 510(k), K141830, 2015). Their use in the breast is an off-label application, and the use of antibiotic-loaded beads is also off-label. Both bead sets were given FDA clearance based on predicated equivalent technology for orthopedic “filling of bone voids.” Beads were prepared and placed in a consistent manner. A slurry of calcium sulfate (500 mg powder), water, and vancomycin powder was created and subsequently delivered into a silicone rubber tray with 3-mm and 4.8-mm circular craters and allowed to cure for at least 8 minutes. The beads were removed from the tray and evenly placed in the pocket. Most patients had a 7- or 10-mm Jackson-Pratt drain placed intraoperatively. The patient data were retrospectively recorded in an encrypted database. These data included variables intended to illuminate potential risk factors for RCC, such as age, parity, reason for primary implantation (cosmetic versus reconstructive), postlumpectomy or mastectomy status, number of previous corrective procedures for capsular contracture, prior type of implant used, prior incision type, history of breast radiation, and duration since last surgery. For the current surgery, variables included incision type; method of capsulectomy; irrigation technique; use and duration of drains; use of ADM; type and surface of implant; dose of vancomycin (500 mg or 1g), if beads were used; unilateral or bilateral disease; and Spear-Baker grade of capsule before and after operation at the time of follow-up. Patients were classified into 2 groups: grades IA, IB, and II as nonrecurrent capsular contracture and grades III and IV as RCC. No pathological studies were performed on the removed capsules.

Statistical analysis to determine whether there was a difference in the incidence of RCC between all 3 groups included the naive χ2 test and naive Fisher exact test, and the latter was found to be more appropriate for small sample sizes. Logistic regression tests were then used to examine the null hypothesis that all 3 groups had the same frequency of RCC, adjusting for within-person correlation (where data included both right and left breasts and/or patients had repeated procedures) using generalized estimating equations. Note that this approach estimates marginal treatment effects, targeting the difference between 2 counterfactual scenarios: (1) where all women receive no-bead treatment and (2) where all women receive bead treatment. All statistical calculations were performed using Stata 17 software.

RESULTS

Four of the 40 procedures had less than 6 months of follow-up, 9 had longer than 6 months but less than 1 year, and 27 had longer than 1 year of follow-up. The ages of the patients ranged from 28 to 77 years, with a mean of 50.9 years (SD 10.8 y). Of the 57 breasts in total, the number of prior breast procedures for capsular contracture ranged from a minimum of 1 to a maximum of 22, with a mean of 2.3 (SD 3.0). The duration from the last surgery to the most recent surgery for all groups combined ranged from 3 to 480 months, with a mean of 133 months (SD 123 mo) and a median of 104 months. The mean duration of follow-up was 57, 22, and 11 months for the no-beads, 500 mg beads, and 1 g beads groups, respectively. The replacement breast implants were 35 saline and 22 silicone implants, of which 28 were textured and 29 smooth. Following surgery, 19 breasts (33%) developed symptomatic grade III or IV RCC, and 38 of the 57 (67%) did not develop capsular contracture. Of the 19 breasts with RCC, 4 had received radiation therapy and were from the group that did receive antibiotic beads. Two of the 4 breasts following prior lumpectomy or simple mastectomy did not develop RCC after surgery. Six breasts in 4 patients received ADM at the time of the procedure. Three of the 6 breasts that received ADM developed recurrent grade III capsular contractures. Seventeen of the 42 augmentation breasts developed RCC, whereas only 2 of the 15 reconstruction breasts developed RCC. Three of the 14 patients who developed RCC after surgery without beads subsequently underwent revision surgery with beads, and none of the 3 developed further RCC (Table 4).

RCC in the No-beads Group Followed by Revision Surgery With Beads

Of the 20 patients who underwent surgery for RCC without the use of beads, 14 developed a new capsular contracture. Three of the 14 patients then underwent a second surgery with antibiotic bead therapy, and none of them had RCC.

The use of vancomycin beads (500 mg or 1 g) was associated with a lower incidence of RCC compared with no beads (P < 0.001) (Table 5). The risk of RCC was 12% in the 1 g group, 17% in the 500 mg bead group, and 70% in the no-bead group (Table 5). The sample size was too small to statistically distinguish between the frequencies of RCC in the 2 bead treatment groups. Logistic regression analysis (allowing for within-person correlation through generalized estimating equation) estimated that the odds of capsular contracture were 15 times higher in the no-bead group compared with the combined 500 mg +1 g vancomycin bead group (P = 0.001; 95% confidence interval [CI], 3–71) using the bootstrap method (with 1000 repetitions) of computing precision. For context, the 95% CI for the odds ratio when ignoring clustering was 4–57, indicating that there was not a great deal of degradation of the standard error caused by ignoring within-person correlation.

When the 500 mg and 1 g vancomycin bead groups were combined as a single group and compared with the no-beads group, there was a statistically significant difference between the 2 groups in the incidence of RCC after surgery: naive χ2 test and Fisher exact test, P < 0.001. It could be seen that the risk of an RCC increased from 12% in the 1 g bead group, to 17% in the 500 mg bead group, and to 70% in the no-beads group, showing a qualitative dose–response effect.

Considering each covariate, RCC did not seem to be related to patient age, number of prior procedures, incisions used, drain duration, capsulectomy technique, bilateral surgery, or implant surface. However, 4 of the 5 failures in the combined groups who received vancomycin beads occurred in patients who underwent implantation for reconstructive surgery following lumpectomy or mastectomy with irradiation. Qualitative increases in the risk of RCC were observed for augmentation procedures and textured implant surfaces, but neither of these reached statistical significance due to small sample sizes. In all cases, none of the covariates indicated any confounding effects on our estimate of the increased risk of RCC associated with no-bead treatment. Furthermore, the lack of confounding by these variables was confirmed in the multivariable analysis.

One variable that appeared to affect the relative impact of bead treatment on RCC was the follow-up time in the data. As noted, the no-bead treatment group was followed up for longer periods of time than either of the 2 bead treatment groups. The data also reflect an empirical increase in the odds of RCC of 4% for every extra month of follow-up. A simple adjustment for this follow-up time variable reduced the estimated odds ratio (comparing the odds of RCC with no beads to that with either 500 mg or 1 g beads) to 8.2, with a 95% CI of 1.4–48.3, while remaining statistically significant (P = 0.02). Treatment efficacy associated with beads was apparent in the first 4 years of follow-up, when the follow-up periods overlapped for the treatment groups, although the sample sizes were too small for formal inference.

DISCUSSION

This pilot study suggests that vancomycin calcium sulfate beads are a useful and cost-effective method for preventing RCC. The typical cost of a Stimulan mini-set bead kit is US $1300, whereas the cost for a single AlloDerm Breast ADM is between $5000 and $7000.

Five articles described the use of antibiotic beads for the prevention or treatment of breast implant–related infection: 3 for prophylaxis and 2 for treatment. All studies involved non–FDA-approved “off-label use” of calcium sulfate beads in the breast and antibiotic-impregnated beads. Albright et al35 reported the removal of 14 grossly infected breast implants, followed by placement of 2 g of vancomycin with 1.2 g tobramycin in patients with polymethylmethacrylate beads, an expander, and a drain. Subsequent removal of the beads, drain, and expander was performed, followed by implant exchange, with no reported reoperations and a mean follow-up of 8.2 months. Sherif et al36 described the removal of 12 grossly infected implants, partial capsulectomy, placement of 1 g vancomycin and 1.2 g tobramycin with Stimulan beads, and immediate replacement of the implant. The procedure led to 75% successful salvage, with no specific discussion regarding subsequent capsular contractures.36 Kenna et al37 reported a reduction in the incidence of tissue expander loss from 11.9% to 1.5% with the use of 500 mg Stimulan mini-beads in combination with 500 mg vancomycin and 250 mg gentamicin per pocket.37 More recently, Nguyen et al38 reported the use of unspecified doses of gentamicin- and vancomycin-loaded antibiotic beads to prevent expander-related infections in 202 breasts of 127 patients. Infection in this group of patients was 1.5%, with loss of the expander, compared with 4.3% of patients who underwent a similar procedure with the use of nonabsorbable antibiotic poly methyl methacrylate beads.38 The authors suggested that oral antibiotics were not needed after surgery. Ahmed et al39 reported no statistical difference in a group of patients who received stimulant beads with 1 g of vancomycin and 240 mg of liquid gentamicin compared with a nonbead group; however, the risk of infection was not controlled.39

Previous studies suggest that 100% of women may have bacterial flora within breast tissue.40 A 2017 study analyzing culture results from the sonification of encapsulated breast implants concluded that the most effective prophylactic antibiotic was vancomycin because of its effectiveness against PSS and CNS.8 In our experience, all patients with grade III or IV breast capsular contracture developed a biofilm, but not all patients with a biofilm developed grade III or IV capsular contracture. Similarly, Virden et al41 reported that 18% of patients with culture-positive breast explants did not have a preexisting capsular contracture. This might be explained by the relative quantitative bacterial load within the breast pocket or the immunogenicity of individual strains of CNS and PSS.42

The average potency of the vancomycin-loaded calcium sulfate beads was between 14 and 20 days, with peak levels at 48 hours.43 Although it is important to reduce exposure to unnecessary antibiotics, there remains no other known risk associated with the use of the 500-mg mini-bead sets. Transient symptomatic hypercalcemia has been reported with calcium sulfate beads of 1 g or greater placed during an orthopedic surgery.44 Renal failure may lead to elevated serum calcium levels, especially in the 1 g or greater bead sets. In the current clinical series, the first 3 patients who received 500-mg calcium sulfate antibiotic beads were monitored for clinical signs of hypercalcemia, and serum calcium was measured on days 2 and 5 after surgery. None of the patients were found to be symptomatic or to have hypercalcemia.

It has been reported that the minimum follow-up period to objectively evaluate for RCC is 1 year.13 The current author did not determine the duration between the previous surgery and when grade III/IV contracture was first noticed by patients in this study. However, only 2 of the 19 breasts in our clinical series developed recurrent grade III/IV capsular contractures within 12 months of surgery. Previous studies have reported a higher incidence of capsular contracture following breast reconstruction than after augmentation. In the current clinical series, revision surgery for capsular contracture following breast augmentation was associated with a significantly higher incidence of RCC compared with reconstruction (42.5% versus 10.5%). The largest number of patients in the augmentation group was from the group that did not receive antibiotic beads and had a longer follow-up, which may explain these differences.

The authors’ technique for breast pocket irrigation was changed after 2014. Previously, Betadine irrigation was not approved by implant manufacturers. The use of 10% Betadine irrigation and hypochlorous acid irrigation is relatively new and may represent a confounding variable between the earlier group with no beads, in which 17 of the 20 breasts received only triple antibiotic irrigation compared with the more recent combined bead group of 37 breasts that received Betadine and hypochlorous acid irrigation. Prior clinical studies have not compared individual irrigant solutions in the prevention of RCC. 43,44 Jewell et al45 reported an in vitro study of cultured Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria that supported the use of Betadine over triple antibiotic or normal saline in terms of bacterial kill up to 10 minutes following irrigation.

The limitations of this pilot study include its retrospective case series design, the small number of patients, nonrandomization of groups and pocket irrigation techniques, and varying follow-up times. A large, long-term, multicenter prospective study using multivariate analysis would be helpful to better understand which factors (Table 2) are most influential in terms of providing the greatest efficacy of this new treatment for the prevention of RCC.
