Development of a machine learning model for early pulmonary tuberculosis diagnosis using blood test biomarkers
Authors: Liangqiong Chen, Cuiqi Yang, Yefeng Dong, Renmei Ge, Jianhao Xu, Rongman Xu, Haitao Zhang, Deping Dong, Feiyue Ji, Jiyang Lu, Jinliang Chen, Yongwei Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberculosis (TB) is a major global health threat, causing 10.6 million new cases and 1.3 million deaths in 2022. Early diagnosis is crucial, but current methods are often costly and slow for resource-limited settings. This study aimed to develop a rapid, low-cost diagnostic tool using routine blood indicators.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed data from 728 TB patients and 2,718 healthy controls. The training set was balanced using the ROSE technique. We trained seven machine learning models, using LASSO regression and forward selection to identify optimal features. The final model was interpreted with SHAP and deployed as an interactive Shiny application.ResultsThe Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) model performed optimally on the test set (AUC = 0.831, specificity = 0.855, sensitivity = 0.644). SHAP analysis identified platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), and platelet distribution width (PDW) as key predictors. Lowering the classification threshold to 0.24 increased sensitivity to 83.6% (specificity 59.9%), demonstrating its screening potential. An interactive web application was developed to enhance clinical utility.ConclusionThis study delivers a validated GBM model using routine blood tests as a cost-effective TB screening tool. Its high specificity can reduce unnecessary follow-up tests. The model’s core predictors are interpretable and provide biological insights into the inflammatory response in TB. The accompanying Shiny app increases accessibility, making it a promising tool for resource-limited settings.RecommendationsWe propose a phased diagnostic strategy: use this model with a low threshold (0.24) for high-sensitivity initial screening, followed by confirmatory molecular testing for positive cases. This approach balances high detection rates with resource optimization. Future work should include prospective validation with cohorts including other respiratory diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12029-4.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, remains one of the top 10 causes of death globally. It is also the deadliest infectious disease caused by a single pathogen. Although TB is preventable and treatable, its global burden remains high due to diagnostic delays, reporting gaps, and insufficient screening among high-risk populations [1]. According to World Health Organization (WHO), over 10 million TB cases and 1.5 million TB-related deaths are reported annually [2].

Conventional diagnostic methods such as sputum smear microscopy and bacterial culture fail to meet clinical needs for TB diagnosis due to limited sensitivity and time-consuming procedures [3, 4]. Numerous emerging omics-based approaches, including transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, have been developed for TB diagnosis [5, 6]. However, these tests are not yet applicable in clinical practice due to their high instrument dependency, poor reproducibility, and insufficient validation. Therefore, developing low-cost, scalable strategies for early TB screening has become a pressing public health need.

Although previous studies have attempted to apply machine learning (ML) models for TB auxiliary diagnosis, most rely on imaging or advanced molecular data [7, 8], which are often inaccessible in low-resource settings. Moreover, model interpretability is frequently overlooked, limiting practical utility and clinical acceptance. While some studies have utilized explainable AI (XAI) techniques such as SHAP to reveal model decision mechanisms, interpretable models based on routine blood indicators with clear biological explanations and practical feasibility remain scarce [9, 10]. This study aims to address this gap by constructing and validating an interpretable ML model using only routine hematological parameters, further develops it into a freely accessible web application. This tool facilitates early triage and public health decision-making in resource-limited settings, thereby aiding TB control efforts in high-burden regions [11].

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted from January 2022 to September 2024. Participants were recruited from Haian County People’s Hospital and Nantong University Xinglin College Affiliated Jianhu Hospital. Based on clinical and laboratory assessments, participants were categorized as TB-infected or TB-uninfected. TB infection was defined as positive mycobacterial culture and/or positive GeneXpert MTB/RIF results from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid or sputum specimens, alongside clinical symptoms such as persistent cough, chest pain, fever, and night sweats. TB-uninfected controls were required to meet all the following criteria: no history of contact with active TB patients, negative tuberculin skin test (PPD) results, and absence of TB-associated clinical symptoms such as persistent cough, fever, night sweats, or weight loss. Additionally, they had no history of other infectious diseases, immunodeficiency conditions, or malignancies. All healthy controls were selected from individuals undergoing routine health examinations at physical examination centers and were deemed clinically healthy based on physician evaluation (encompassing medical history, physical examination, and basic laboratory tests) to confirm absence of both active TB and latent TB infection. Patients were excluded if they had received anti-TB treatment within one month prior to enrollment. This study was approved by the Ethics Committees of Haian County People’s Hospital and Nantong University Xinglin College Affiliated Jianhu Hospital. Routine laboratory tests

Complete blood count (CBC) testing was performed for all participants. Briefly, peripheral blood samples anticoagulated with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) were collected for CBC analysis, yielding the following parameters: white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin concentration (Hb), hematocrit (Hct), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean platelet volume (MPV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), platelet count (PLT), plateletcrit (PCT), red blood cell distribution width-standard deviation (RDW-SD), red blood cell distribution width-coefficient of variation (RDW-CV), lymphocyte count (LYM), neutrophil count (NEU), monocyte count (MONO), eosinophil count (EOS), basophil count (BASO), lymphocyte percentage (LYM%), neutrophil percentage (NEU%), monocyte percentage (MONO%), eosinophil percentage (EOS%), basophil percentage (BASO%), platelet-large cell ratio (P-LCR). Additionally, multiple inflammation-related ratios were derived from hematological parameters, such as neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), neutrophil-to-platelet ratio (NPR), derived neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (dNLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio (NMLR), systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), and systemic inflammation index (SII). All measurements were conducted using the XN-9000 hematology analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan).

Lasso

LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) is a regression method ideal for datasets with many features, especially when there are more features than samples. By incorporating an L1 regularization term (sum of absolute coefficients) into the loss function, it imposes a penalty on regression coefficients, shrinking the coefficients of less important features to zero and thereby achieving feature selection. Compared to conventional regression methods, LASSO reduces overfitting while simplifying the model for enhanced interpretability. The regularization intensity is controlled by parameter λ, typically optimized through cross-validation. In R, the glmnet package is the standard implementation tool for LASSO, supporting various models including linear and logistic regression, with built-in cross-validation functions [12].

Establishment of diagnostic models

Seven classification algorithms were implemented in a standardized manner using the caret package: (1) logistic regression; (2) random forest; (3) Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM); (4) XGBoost; (5) support vector machine; (6) k-nearest neighbors; (7) naive Bayes. Model training employed 5-fold cross-validation with 3 repeats, using AUC as the primary evaluation metric. Hyperparameter optimization was conducted using grid search, with parallel processing supported by the doParallel package. The training process incorporated upsampling to balance class distribution.

Decision curve analysis

Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) is a method for evaluating the clinical utility of predictive models [13]. It calculates the net benefit of a model at different risk thresholds. This helps evaluate its practical usefulness in clinical settings. Net benefit comprehensively integrates the true positive rate and predicted positive rate, balancing the costs of false positives and false negatives. Unlike traditional metrics such as AUC, DCA focuses on a model’s practical impact in specific clinical scenarios, frequently comparing model performance against “treat-all” or “treat-none” strategies. In R, the rmda and DecisionCurve packages are commonly used to plot DCA curves and compute net benefit.

Calibration curve

Calibration curves are tools for assessing whether a predictive model’s estimated probabilities align with observed outcomes, reflecting the calibration performance of the model [14]. For binary classification models, they group predicted probabilities (typically into deciles), comparing each group’s mean predicted probability against its actual event rate. An ideally calibrated curve follows the 45-degree diagonal line, indicating perfect prediction-observation concordance. Significant deviations can be quantified using the Brier score [15]. In R, the calibrate function from the rms package, and calibration function from the caret package are standard tools for generating calibration curves.

SHapley additive exPlanations

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) is a method used to explain the predictions of machine learning models. It is based on the concept of Shapley values from game theory [16]. It works by assigning a contribution value to each feature for a given prediction. This clarifies how the model arrives at its decisions. SHAP generates visualizations including feature importance plots and dependency plots, applicable to diverse complex models (e.g., tree-based models, neural networks). Compared to alternative methods, SHAP possesses a more rigorous theoretical foundation and broader applicability. In R, the fastshap and SHAPforxgboost packages serve as primary tools for SHAP analysis, supporting multiple model types.

Nomogram construction

To generate a clinically practical and interpretable nomogram while retaining the high predictive performance of our Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) model, we employed a model approximation or surrogate model approach. The core methodology was as follows: first, a high-performance GBM model was trained and validated to act as a “teacher model.” Second, this model was used to generate predictions on a log-odds scale for each sample within the training set. Third, these predictions served as the new target variable for training a simple and interpretable Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) linear regression model, known as the “student model.” Finally, the nomogram was constructed entirely based on the regression coefficients of this student model. This method effectively transforms the complex “black-box” model into an intuitive visualization tool whose predictive logic stems from the more powerful GBM model, serving as a best linear approximation of its predictive capabilities and providing reliable support for clinical decision-making.

Shiny deployment

Shiny deployment refers to the process of converting data analyses or model outputs into interactive web applications using the Shiny framework within the R language. Shiny enables the creation of user interfaces and server logic using straightforward R code, without requiring expertise in advanced web development technologies. Deployed applications can display dynamic plots, tables, and model predictions while supporting real-time user interaction. Shiny applications can be run either locally or deployed to cloud platforms (e.g., Shinyapps.io), facilitating research dissemination. In R, the shiny package serves as the core for application development, while shinydashboard and shinyWidgets provide enhanced interface design options.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis evaluates how model outputs respond to variations in inputs, assessing model stability and identifying critical variables. This involves altering input value ranges, adjusting parameters, or testing models on different datasets, and observing output fluctuations. This method helps identify model constraints, optimizes design, and enhances decision-making support. In predictive modeling, it routinely evaluates model robustness against uncertainties. Within R, the caret package can be utilized for testing model performance across data subsets.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median values. Categorical variables are presented as frequency (%). Comparisons of continuous variables were performed using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Comparisons of categorical variables were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Linear regression analysis was performed to assess linear relationships between various indicators. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to evaluate the ability of indicators and models to discriminate between active tuberculosis (ATB) and latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI). The area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), positive likelihood ratio (PLR), negative likelihood ratio (NLR), and overall accuracy were calculated. All analyses were conducted in the R environment (v4.2.2), primarily utilizing the tidyverse, caret, ggplot2, patchwork, rms [17] packages.

Results

Characteristics of recruited participants

Prior to baseline characteristic analysis, missing values for each feature in the dataset were examined. Features with more than 30% missing data—specifically PCT, RDW-SD, EOS, BASO, EOS%, BASO%, and P-LCR—were excluded to ensure data integrity and reliability. Subsequently, boxplot analysis identified and removed significant outliers deviating from normal ranges. The final cohort comprised 3,446 participants, stratified into training (no-TB: n = 1,631; TB: n = 437), validation (no-TB: n = 543; TB: n = 145), and test sets (no-TB: n = 544; TB: n = 146). Baseline analysis revealed that TB patients were younger than non-TB (no-TB) counterparts (training set: 61.23 ± 17.15 vs. 64.35 ± 13.09 years, p < 0.001; test set: 61.54 ± 17.71 vs. 64.26 ± 13.45 years, p = 0.044) with significantly higher proportion of males (training set: 73.0% vs. 48.7%, p < 0.001). Hematologically, the TB group exhibited classical anemia patterns: hemoglobin (Hb: training set 123.13 ± 18.02 vs. 133.04 ± 17.30 g/dL), red blood cell count (RBC: 4.22 ± 0.60 vs. 4.40 ± 0.59 × 106/μL), and hematocrit (Hct: 0.38 ± 0.05 vs. 0.40 ± 0.05) were significantly reduced (p < 0.001). At the same time, platelets counts were elevated (PLT: training set 214.80 ± 86.64 vs. 183.38 ± 64.79 × 103/μL, p < 0.001) and absolute lymphocyte counts (LYM: 1.24 ± 0.58 vs. 1.74 ± 0.67 × 103/μL, p < 0.001) were reduced. Inflammation ratios were markedly aberrant in the TB group, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR: 5.23 ± 4.83 vs. 3.46 ± 3.87), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR: 216.24 ± 143.46 vs. 121.66 ± 72.15), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII: 1131.94 ± 1128.95 vs. 627.00 ± 793.33), all p < 0.001. Notably, total white blood cell count (WBC) did not differ significantly between groups (p > 0.3). Protective associations were mainly observed for higher levels of erythrocyte-related parameters (Hct, Hb, RBC, MCH, MCHC) and lymphocyte metrics (LYM, LYM%), while risk associations were associated with higher levels of inflammatory indicators (PLT, MONO, NEU%, MONO%) and inflammation ratios (NLR, PLR, MLR, SIRI, SII).This differential pattern remained highly consistent across the training, Data processing and feature analysis

To address class imbalance in the training set (original TB/no-TB ratio ≈1:3.7), the ROSE (Random Over-Sampling Examples) algorithm was employed for resampling [18]. After resampling, feature distributions remained stable: density curves maintained high consistency with original distributions (Supplementary Fig. 1). Quantitative analysis further revealed that the mean change rates of all features were < 5% (e.g., hemoglobin: 0.5887→0.5681, change rate 3.5%; lymphocytes: 0.3858→0.3468, change rate 10.1%), and the increases in standard deviation were controlled within 34% (maximum fluctuation: NLR 0.0958→0.1282, increase 33.8%). These results indicate effective preservation of data distribution characteristics while balancing class proportions (Supplementary Table 1). Multicollinearity diagnostics confirmed all variables were suitable for modeling: the mean variance inflation factor (VIF) was 1.86 (range: 1.14–2.39), well below the commonly accepted threshold of 5 (Supplementary Table 2). In the correlation matrix, 98% of feature pairs had correlation coefficients < 0.5 (e.g., Hb-WBC: r = 0.06; MCV-RDW-CV: r = −0.08). Only select inflammation ratios showed moderate correlations (e.g.,NLR-SIRI: r = 0.68; PLR-SII: r = 0.52), which did not reach levels indicative of problematic collinearity (Supplementary Figure 2).

Establishing diagnostic models using machine learning

A multi-stage modeling strategy was implemented for TB diagnostic model development. First, LASSO regression (L1 regularization) screened key features. Selecting lambda.1se parameter identified 15 core hematological indicators—Hct, Hb, PLT, MCH, MPV, MCHC, PDW, LYM, MONO, LYM%, MONO%, PLR, NPR, dNLR, and MLR—based on binomial deviance minimization (Figs. 2A, 2B). This feature subset trained seven machine learning models with hyperparameters optimized via grid search (Supplementary Table 3). Performance evaluation of GBM model

The calibration curve of the recalibrated GBM model illustrates the relationship between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. After recalibration on the validation set, the model demonstrated good calibration, achieving a Brier score of 0.1174 on the test set. To better contextualize this, the Scaled Brier Score was 0.2962, indicating an improvement in predictive accuracy over a baseline model (Fig. 3B). Decision curve analysis (DCA) revealed that the net benefit of the GBM model exceeded both the “treat-none” and “treat-all” reference lines across the 0.0–0.5 threshold range. At a threshold of 0.2, the net benefit reached approximately 0.25, declining to near 0.0 at threshold 0.5, signifying enhanced clinical utility at lower thresholds (Fig. 3C). Comparative analysis of GBM performance under two thresholding strategies showed: at the default threshold (0.5), sensitivity = 0.644 and specificity = 0.855; at the optimized threshold (0.24), sensitivity increased to 0.836 while specificity decreased to 0.599 (Fig. 3D). Confusion matrices further visualized these trade-offs, confirming significantly improved sensitivity at threshold 0.24 despite reduced specificity (Fig. 3E). These results demonstrate that threshold adjustment enables balancing sensitivity-specificity tradeoffs to accommodate diverse clinical requirements.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted a sensitivity analysis by constructing a case-control study through age- and sex-matching between non-TB and TB cases within the test set. After controlling for these potential confounders (age and sex), the GBM model maintained good discriminatory power in distinguishing TB from non-TB cases. Performance metrics were as follows: AUC = 0.8401, sensitivity = 0.6573, specificity = 0.8531, accuracy = 0.7552, positive predictive value (PPV) = 0.8174, negative predictive value (NPV) = 0.7135. These results demonstrate that the GBM model retained high diagnostic accuracy and discriminatory power even after accounting for these confounding variables that may influence predictions.

Shap explainability analysis

SHAP interpretability analysis quantified each feature’s contribution to the model’s output. Global analysis identified the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) as the most influential feature (mean |SHAP| = 0.588), followed by the monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), platelet distribution width (PDW), and lymphocyte count (LYM) (Fig. 4A). The beeswarm plot further revealed that PLR and MLR primarily drove risk upwards, while mean platelet volume (MPV) exhibited bidirectional effects (Fig. 4B). To illustrate the model’s logic for individual predictions, we selected three representative patients from the test set corresponding to high, median, and low predicted risk profiles. For a representative high-risk patient (predicted log-odds = 3.457), the prediction was primarily associated with inflammatory markers: the neutrophil-to-platelet ratio (NPR) had the strongest positive contribution to the risk (+1.32), followed by lymphocyte count (LYM, +0.717), while PDW showed a negative association with risk (−0.372). For a representative median-risk patient (predicted probability = 0.111), PLR was the feature with the main positive association (+0.501), and monocyte count (MONO) also had a slight positive correlation with the predicted risk (+0.23). Conversely, for a representative low-risk patient (predicted log-odds = −4.654), a strong negative association with risk was observed for platelet-related parameters, with MPV (−0.848) and PLR (−0.79) being the core contributors (Figs. 4C,D). These analyses indicate that PLR is the most important feature globally while also showing context-dependent, individualized associations (e.g., being associated with higher risk in one patient versus having a strong negative correlation in another). The model effectively separates patients into different risk levels by analyzing the complex relationship between PLR and other inflammatory markers. Discussion

Accurate early diagnosis of TB is critical for effective control of its transmission, yet current clinical practice faces substantial challenges. It is widely acknowledged that single biomarkers demonstrate limited value in distinguishing pulmonary TB from non-TB infections [19–22], with diagnostic performance frequently constrained by inadequate sensitivity or specificity. Consequently, exploring combined applications of multiple biomarkers has become an imperative trend for enhancing diagnostic efficacy [23–26]. However, previous studies have faced major challenges. They often do not make full use of existing data, thereby missing the potential combined effects of different indicators. Alternatively, some modeling methodologies may not fully capture the data’s complexity, leading to diagnostic models that do not meet expected performance. This complicates the identification and determination of optimal biomarker combinations and their application rules. Moreover, the diagnostic potential of novel biomarkers is often underestimated due to ineffective integration with established indicators. Recent advances in multidimensional data analysis have highlighted algorithm-based machine learning, particularly excelling in classification-centric applications [27–30]. Therefore, addressing increasing clinical demands—especially for cost-effective screening tools [31]—necessitates adopting more advanced data analysis methods to re-evaluate and maximize the diagnostic potential of both conventional and emerging indicators [10].

Machine learning can help maximize the value of each indicator and optimize the effective utilization of laboratory test data. In this study, we introduced machine learning techniques to explore their potential application in this field. The gradient boosting machine (GBM) model constructed herein achieved stable diagnostic performance on the test set (AUC = 0.831, sensitivity 64.4%, specificity 85.5%). When compared with previous TB diagnostic models, our approach—based solely on routine complete blood count (CBC) parameters—showed specific advantages. Luo et al. reported a higher AUC (0.978) using a flow cytometry-immunoassay combined strategy [28]; Chen et al.‘s GBM model based on routine laboratory parameters (including biochemical/coagulation tests) achieved AUC = 0.962 (sensitivity 84.38%) [27]; whereas our model—using only basic complete blood count (CBC) parameters—demonstrated superior specificity (85.5%) compared to a biochemical indicator-based KNN model (74%) [32], while showing comparable results to imaging-based SVM models (specificity 80%) [29], HIV-specific random forest models (specificity 81.0%) [33], and spectroscopy-based approaches (AUC = 0.82) [34]. Notably, our model’s continuous probability output supports clinical threshold adjustment: sensitivity increased to 83.6% at threshold 0.24—approaching Chen’s model (84.38%)—while maintaining high specificity (85.5%) at the default threshold 0.5. The model’s probability output allows for flexible thresholds. Combined with its ability to model complex relationships between blood parameters, our approach makes routine blood tests more useful in resource-limited settings. It offers a practical alternative where advanced diagnostic equipment is unavailable.

SHAP analysis identified platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), and platelet distribution width (PDW) as the most influential predictors in our model. PLR (highest SHAP value) reflects the coexisting states of inflammatory activation and immunosuppression in TB patients [35]. Platelets not only participate in coagulation but also exacerbate inflammatory responses through releasing mediators (e.g., platelet factor 4); concurrently, lymphocytopenia indicates impaired cellular immune function, potentially leading to immune exhaustion particularly in chronic infections. This dual dysregulation establishes PLR as a robust inflammation-immunity biomarker [36, 37]. Elevated MLR directly correlates with monocyte-macrophage pathway activation [35, 38]: systemic monocyte recruitment increases granuloma formation, while lymphocytopenia further compromises immune surveillance. As a sensitive indicator for active TB, MLR demonstrates consistent elevation across age, gender, and TB localization (pulmonary/extrapulmonary) [19]. Ranking second in global importance on the SHAP beeswarm plot, MLR proves critical for TB prediction. It exhibited a positive contribution (+0.0364) in high-risk samples on SHAP waterfall plots, indicating that a high MLR value strongly pushes the model’s prediction towards a higher risk of having active TB. Conversely, it showed a negative contribution (−0.00686) in a representative median-risk sample, where a lower MLR value helped reduce the predicted risk. These findings establish elevated MLR as a core signal for predicting TB risk. Separately, the clinical relevance of MLR is further supported by studies showing that its levels significantly decline following successful anti-TB therapy, validating it as a dynamic marker of disease activity [39, 40]. PDW reflects platelet size heterogeneity [41, 42]: its abnormal elevation may link to disordered megakaryocyte differentiation during inflammation. PDW differs significantly between osteoarticular TB and pulmonary TB patients [41] and is associated with increased treatment failure risk in TB-diabetes comorbidity [42], suggesting its role in reflecting inflammatory persistence and treatment response potential. Each indicator represents distinct immune-inflammatory features of TB infection: PLR highlights platelet activation-lymphocyte suppression imbalance; MLR emphasizes monocyte infiltration-immune exhaustion; PDW indicates disordered thrombopoiesis. This aligns with prior studies establishing inflammation markers’ diagnostic value in TB [43, 44]. The strength of the machine learning model is its ability to combine information from these routine indicators. This approach creates what is potentially a more reliable diagnostic framework than relying on any single biomarker. This offers a plausible biological interpretation for the model’s performance in risk prediction using routine blood tests.

As a practical early diagnostic tool, this model is particularly suitable for resource-limited settings. It utilizes only 11 routinely collected blood test parameters (e.g., CBC parameters) during patient admission for risk assessment, requiring no additional testing costs—aligning with World Health Organization (WHO) economic criteria for diagnostic tools in high-burden regions. The model significantly reduces diagnostic delays, thereby decreasing hospitalization duration and associated expenses. To optimize resource allocation, we recommend implementing a phased screening pathway: initial screening employs a low-risk threshold (0.24) to maximize sensitivity, with GeneXpert confirmation reserved for model-positive cases. This strategy has the potential to significantly reduce the volume of GeneXpert testing required. For grassroots implementation, a freely accessible Shiny application enables physicians to input routine blood test results for real-time risk scoring, helping to bridge diagnostic gaps in under-resourced areas.

The value and innovation of this study are demonstrated through a systematic comparison with previous machine learning-based research on tuberculosis diagnosis. Firstly, in terms of data, unlike models that rely on specialized tests such as flow cytometry [28], biochemical and coagulation parameters [27], or imaging examinations [29], our model requires only the most basic and inexpensive complete blood count (CBC) parameters. This significantly lowers the barrier to application, making it particularly suitable for primary healthcare settings with limited equipment. Methodologically, we moved beyond merely pursuing high AUC values by placing special emphasis on model interpretability. Using LASSO regression and SHAP analysis, we identified key predictors like PLR and MLR. We also explained the biological mechanisms behind their importance, such as imbalances in the immune and inflammatory response (e.g., platelet activation and lymphocyte suppression). This approach is designed to make the model’s decision-making process more transparent and understandable to clinicians. Finally, and most importantly, in terms of clinical application, we did not stop at algorithm development but deployed the final model as an interactive, open-source Shiny online tool. This step represents progress toward bridging the gap between research and clinical practice.It also provides a practical tool for resource-limited settings, which is a common challenge that many studies have not fully addressed. In summary, our work presents an interpretable model deployed as an accessible web application. It offers a potential tool for implementing phased screening strategies for tuberculosis in high-risk populations.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Samples were primarily sourced from two hospitals, limiting population representativeness. The model was developed and internally validated only on retrospective data, without prospective validation or extensive external validation in independent, diverse populations. Consequently, its generalizability, robustness, and potential clinical utility beyond the current dataset remain uncertain. Future studies should aim to validate the model in broader populations (e.g., HIV-coinfected individuals, pediatric TB patients) and across different regions. The model utilizes routine laboratory indicators without incorporating emerging biomarkers (e.g., omics or flow cytometry markers); future studies could explore integrating these or inexpensive inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein (CRP) to enhance performance. Additionally, the control group currently includes only non-infected individuals without incorporating patients with common respiratory diseases (e.g., pneumonia, lung cancer). Thus, the model’s ability to distinguish pulmonary TB from these conditions (specificity) warrants further evaluation. Future improvements should include: expanding validation in multi-ethnic populations, incorporating diverse comparator groups to assess differential diagnostic capability, and exploring integration of novel biomarkers with model parameter optimization.

Conclusions

This study suggests that the gradient boosting machine (GBM) model constructed using routine blood ratios (e.g., PLR, MLR) represents a cost-effective early screening tool for TB. Its companion Shiny application offers a user-friendly interface, providing an accessible tool for decision support that is particularly suited for resource-limited settings. With further validation, this tool holds promise as a complementary addition to existing TB diagnostic systems.
