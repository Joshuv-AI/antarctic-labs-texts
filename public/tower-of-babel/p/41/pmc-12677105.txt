Pharmacodynamics, Safety, and Tolerability of Pyridostigmine Bromide in Heart Failure
Authors: Randal Goldberg, Lucy Norcliffe-Kaufmann, Horacio Kaufmann, Ikoa Jeschke-Lopez, Yu Guo, Judy Zhong, Kenneth I Berger, Roberta M Goldring, David S Goldstein, Carey Pope, Lara Maxwell, Manushree Bharadwaj, Alex Reyentovich, Stuart D Katz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPyridostigmine bromide is a short-acting carbamate acetylcholinesterase inhibitor that has been shown to acutely augment parasympathetic signaling in cardiovascular disease populations.ObjectiveThis study was undertaken to characterize pharmacodynamics, safety, and tolerability of pyridostigmine during repeated dosing in patients with heart failure.MethodsA prospective ascending-dose, forced titration, double-blind Phase II randomized clinical trial was conducted to compare the effects of pyridostigmine bromide (15, 30, and 60 mg TID over 8 weeks) versus matching placebo on red blood cell (RBC) acetylcholinesterase activity, cholinergic side effects, and physiologic measures of parasympathetic heart rate modulation and sympathovagal balance in ambulatory patients with chronic systolic heart failure.ResultsFifty subjects were screened, and 33 eligible subjects were randomly assigned (mean age, 55 years; mean left ventricular ejection fraction, 23%). Pyridostigmine bromide significantly decreased RBC acetylcholinesterase activity (P < 0.02 vs placebo) and increased the frequency of participant-reported cholinergic excess symptoms (P < 0.001 vs placebo). Physiologic measures of parasympathetic heart rate modulation and sympathovagal balance did not differ between treatment groups. In the pyridostigmine bromide group, RBC acetylcholinesterase activity was not significantly associated with postexercise parasympathetic heart modulation.ConclusionsPyridostigmine bromide administered over 8 weeks was associated with a significant reduction of RBC acetylcholinesterase activity and relatively mild symptoms of cholinergic excess, but changes in parasympathetic signaling in the sinoatrial node previously reported after acute administration were not observed. Further investigations are needed to delineate pharmacodynamic and pathobiological factors contributing to these findings. ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT01415921.Key words: Autonomic nervous system, Cholinesterase inhibition, Heart failure, Human studies, Pharmacodynamics, Pyridostigmine, Toxicology

Introduction

Pyridostigmine bromide is an orally available, short-acting, carbamate acetylcholinesterase inhibitor approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of myasthenia gravis and for use in military combat personnel as prophylaxis against potential exposure to organophosphorus nerve agents.1,2 Pyridostigmine bromide primarily mediates its pharmacological effects in cholinergic neurons of the peripheral nervous system by reducing enzymatic breakdown of acetylcholine in the synaptic cleft and potentiating muscarinic and nicotinic receptor postsynaptic signaling.3,4 Both therapeutic benefits and toxic effects of acetylcholinesterase inhibition are attributable to the effects of excess postsynaptic cholinergic receptor-linked cell signaling in target organs.5 Chronic pyridostigmine bromide treatment at recommended doses for FDA-approved indications is generally well tolerated, with cholinergic gastrointestinal symptoms reported as the most common side effect.6

The effects of pyridostigmine bromide on cholinergic signaling in the autonomic nervous system of the heart may offer therapeutic benefit in patients with cardiovascular disease. In pilot studies of patients with coronary heart disease, postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome and heart failure, pyridostigmine bromide 30 to 45 mg in single-dose and repeated-dosing protocols for up to 48 hours was well-tolerated and was shown to slow resting and submaximal heart rate, reduce myocardial ischemia during submaximal exercise, and increase postexercise heart rate recovery, all pharmacological effects consistent with enhancement of parasympathetic cholinergic neurotransmission in the muscarinic receptors of the sinoatrial node of the heart.7, 8, 9, 10 The pharmacodynamics, safety, and tolerability of longer-term exposure to pyridostigmine bromide in patients with cardiovascular disease have not been previously characterized.

Heart failure is a global public health challenge due to its increasing worldwide disease prevalence, high associated health care costs, and high risk of hospitalization and death despite advances in treatment.11 Dysregulation of sympathovagal balance, characterized by sympathetic activation and parasympathetic deactivation, occurs early in the pathogenesis of heart failure and is independently associated with increased mortality risk.12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Pharmacologic inhibition of sympathetic activation with β-adrenergic receptor blockers reduces mortality risk in chronic heart failure patients but does not fully normalize sympathovagal balance due to persistent diminished parasympathetic activity.19, 20, 21, 22 This single-site, prospective, randomized Phase II clinical trial was undertaken to compare the pharmacodynamics, safety, and tolerability of 8 weeks of daily intermittent administration of pyridostigmine versus matching placebo in patients with chronic heart failure. The study was designed to further characterize the potential therapeutic utility of chronic pyridostigmine bromide treatment in cardiovascular disease.

Methods

Subject selection

Ambulatory adults, ages 21 to 75 years with left ventricular ejection fraction <35% and mild-to-moderate reduction in functional capacity despite guideline-recommended therapy, were eligible for the study. Key exclusion criteria included spirometry evidence of obstructive lung disease, systolic blood pressure <90 mm Hg, inability to complete >3 minutes of treadmill exercise, persistent atrial fibrillation, sick sinus syndrome, atrioventricular block on ECG, peak exercise heart rate <100 beats/min, serum creatinine >2.5 mg/dL (221 µmol/L), hemoglobin level <10 g/dL, pregnancy, and any known contraindication to cholinergic stimulation. The study protocol was carried out in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. All study procedures were performed in compliance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines and were approved by the institutional review board (initial approval May 20, 2011). Privacy rights of human subjects were observed in compliance with relevant laws, and all subjects provided written informed consent before participation in study procedures. The study was registered as a clinical trial on the ClinicalTrials.gov website (Identifier NCT01415921) before the start of enrollment. The dates of the first and last subject visits were April 20, 2012, and August 10, 2015, respectively. Enrollment was terminated in 2015 due to the scheduled end of funding.

Study design

The study was designed as a prospective randomized double-blind clinical trial to compare the effects of pyridostigmine bromide at doses used in FDA-approved indications (15, 30, and 60 mg TID as tolerated) versus matching placebo on red blood cell (RBC) acetylcholinesterase activity, physiologic measures of parasympathetic signaling at the sinoatrial node of the heart (postexercise heart rate recovery and resting heart rate variability), and symptoms of cholinergic excess and safety (Figure 1).6 After completion of screening procedures, eligible subjects underwent baseline assessment of postexercise heart rate recovery, resting heart rate variability, and assessment of safety and tolerability measures as described next. Eligible subjects received a 1-week supply of single-blind placebo TID to assess adherence to the study drug regimen and stability of symptoms. Subjects with >70% study drug adherence and stable symptoms continued study participation and were randomly assigned in a double-blind fashion to either pyridostigmine bromide or matching placebo. Primary and secondary end points were assessed after completion of the 4-week maintenance phase with the highest tolerated dose of study drug approximately 2 hours after study drug dosing. Four weeks of therapy at the target dose was selected for this Phase II study to acquire sufficient safety and tolerability data to support planning a dosing strategy for potential future Phase III clinical trials.

When an eligible subject was identified after completion of screening and single-blind placebo run-in, the randomly assigned study drug was initiated at 15 mg every 8 hours for 2 weeks (visit 4) with subsequent forced titration to 30 mg every 8 hours for 2 weeks (visit 6) and 60 mg every 8 hours for 4 weeks (visit 8) according to prespecified safety and tolerability criteria. Primary and secondary end points were assessed before randomization (visit 2) and after completion of the 4-week maintenance phase at the highest tolerated dose of study drug (Post-treatment (Tx) visit 9). The study drug was then downtitrated at 1-week intervals with safety assessment 1 week after cessation of study drug (visit 12).

Study drug blinding and administration

Pyridostigmine bromide 60-mg tablets were supplied by an FDA-approved manufacturer (Bausch Health US, LLC, Bridgewater, New Jersey) under an investigational new drug exemption reviewed and approved by FDA staff. Pyridostigmine bromide 15 mg, 30 mg, and 60 mg and matching placebo capsules were prepared by EMINENT Service Corporation (Frederick, Maryland) and dispensed by the Institutional Investigational Pharmacy. A variable-size blocked randomization allocation scheme stratified by gender was generated by an independent biostatistician. When an eligible subject was identified after completion of single-blind placebo run-in, the investigational pharmacy determined treatment allocation and dispensed the blinded study drug. The study drug was self-administered by the subject at approximately 8-hour intervals (morning dose at 6–8 AM, afternoon dose at 2–4 PM, and evening dose at 10 PM–12 AM). The study drug was initiated at 15 mg every 8 hours for 2 weeks with subsequent forced titration to 30 mg every 8 hours for 2 weeks and 60 mg every 8 hours for 4 weeks according to prespecified safety and tolerability criteria (Figure 1). After completion of primary end point assessment, the dose of study drug was decreased by sequential 50% dose reductions at weekly intervals to 15 mg every 8 hours and then discontinued. Adherence to the study drug regimen was assessed by counting of returned capsules. A protocol deviation in the study drug labeling process was identified after completion of all study procedures. The serial number on the study drug label issued to subjects by the investigational pharmacy was not altered from the serial numbers of the stock study drug containers (pyridostigmine and placebo) that were known to the study coordinator from communications with the EMINENT Service Corporation (supplier of study drug). The study coordinator did not reveal study drug assignment to subjects or any other member of the investigative team during the study. All primary and secondary efficacy end points were assessed by investigators who remained blinded to study drug assignment. After discovery of the protocol deviation, an independent audit of all subject files determined that the study drug was administered in accordance with randomized assignment and that the entries in the research database by the research coordinator matched the original source documents. The study data safety monitoring board; National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute Sponsor; and institutional review board all recommended that the study data could be analyzed and reported with disclosure of the protocol deviation.

Postexercise heart rate recovery measurement

The primary outcome variable for assessment of parasympathetic signaling at the sinoatrial node of the heart was postexercise heart rate recovery at 1 minute (HRR1) after completion of symptom-limited maximal treadmill exercise (HRR1, beats/min). The deceleration in heart rate in early postexercise recovery is mediated by parasympathetic reactivation, is responsive to cholinesterase inhibition with pyridostigmine bromide in short-term studies, and is independently associated with mortality risk in heart failure populations.16,19,23, 24, 25 β-Blockers were not discontinued before exercise testing as prior reports indicated no effect on HRR1.19,21 Exercise testing was performed on a motorized treadmill with graded increases in work rate at 3-minute intervals according to the Bruce protocol.26 Electrocardiographic data and breath-by-breath expired gas analysis (Viasys VmaxYorba Linda, California) were recorded continuously during a 1-minute pre-exercise rest period, throughout exercise, and during the first minute of recovery. Peak oxygen uptake was calculated as the highest value (median 5 of 7 breaths with a moving average filter) in the last minute of exercise as previously described.16 When symptom-limited maximal tolerated workload was achieved, the treadmill was stopped, and subjects recovered at rest in a seated position. Heart rate recovery at 1 minute was calculated as the difference between heart rates at peak exercise and at 1 minute of recovery.19,23

Resting heart rate variability measurements

Six minutes of resting heart rate electrocardiographic data were digitally recorded in a supine position with breathing synchronized to a metronome at 12 respirations per minute in a darkened, temperature-controlled room. Each recording was manually reviewed to remove ectopic beats and noise. A fast Fourier transform was used to calculate power spectrum data, which were corrected for attenuation due to windowing and sampling. The power spectrum was integrated over the following frequency intervals: very low frequency, 0.003 to <0.04 Hz; low frequency, 0.04 to <0.15 Hz; and high frequency, 0.15 to 0.40 Hz.27 Parasympathetic influences on heart rate were also measured in the time domain, including percentage of successive RR intervals (the time duration) that differ by more than 50 milliseconds (pNN50 [%]), root mean square of successive RR interval differences (milliseconds), and SD of NN (normal) intervals (milliseconds). The expiration-to-inspiration ratio was calculated during paced (metronomic) slow deep breathing (6 breaths/min) from the mean (average of 3 beats) longest RR interval (milliseconds) during expiration to the mean shortest RR interval (milliseconds) during inspiration.

Laboratory analysis

Blood was collected in heparinized tubes for measurement of RBC acetylcholinesterase levels at study visits 3 to 11. These visits were timed for the collection of trough activity approximately 8 hours after the last dose of study drug and peak activity 2 hours after administration of study drug during the visit. Cholinesterase activity in erythrocytes was measured by the radiometric method of Johnson and Russell using [3H]acetylcholine iodide (1-mM final concentration) as previously described.28, 29, 30 For each assay, a maximal activity control (using excess electric eel acetylcholinesterase) and a standard curve of activity (using graded amounts of a bovine RBC “ghost” acetylcholinesterase preparation as standard) were included to reduce interassay variation.31 Catecholamines (norepinephrine, epinephrine, and dopamine) were measured before randomization and 1 week after cessation of study drug in plasma obtained from an indwelling catheter after at least 15 minutes of supine rest in a darkened room using HPLC with electrochemical detection.32

Safety and tolerability assessments

A previously validated cholinergic side effects score was used to assess symptoms of cholinergic excess due to pyridostigmine therapy.33 The score contains 13 known side effects of pyridostigmine-induced cholinergic excess embedded at random within a panel of 45 common physical and psychological symptoms unrelated to cholinergic signaling. Each symptom was rated on a scale from 0 (did not occur) to 6 (extremely bothersome). The score is calculated as the sum of the ratings for the 13 known cholinergic side effects, with the sum of the ratings of the other 32 noncholinergic symptoms as a control for general somatic complaints. Disease-specific quality of life was assessed with the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire.34 Safety assessments conducted at each visit with study drug administration included assessment of vital signs for 2 hours, electrocardiography to assess PR, QRS, and QT intervals, spirometry for determination of the forced expiratory volume at 1 second/forced vital capacity ratio, interrogation of implanted devices (pacemakers or implantable cardiac defibrillators) for detection of atrial and ventricular arrhythmias and ventricular pacing, and safety blood monitoring, including digoxin level in patients treated with digoxin, and international normalized ratio in patients treated with warfarin.

Data management

All data were entered on electronic case report forms into a secure database created and maintained by the research informatics section at Nathan S. Kline Institute Computer and Information Sciences Facility. Data queries were addressed monthly during the conduct of the trial; the completed dataset was locked before unblinding. A data safety monitoring committee, consisting of 3 cardiologists with expertise in heart failure management and clinical trials, and a biostatistician reviewed blinded reports of study enrollment, data quality, protocol deviations, and adverse events at 6-month intervals during the study.

Data analysis

The primary end point for assessment of parasympathetic signaling at the sinoatrial node of the heart was the change in postexercise HRR1 from pretreatment baseline to 8 weeks of study drug treatment. There were no missing data for the primary end point at visit 9 for subjects who completed 4 weeks of maximally tolerated study drug. Intent-to-treat analysis was used for all primary and secondary analyses. All variables were tested for deviations from normality; variables with deviation from normal distribution were mathematically transformed. A mixed-effects model for repeated measures was used to compare serial changes in postexercise HRR1 in treatment groups over time with treatment, time, and treatment-by-time interaction as covariates. PROC MIXED in SAS (version 9.1, SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina) was used to model mean data while appropriately adjusting SE estimates for correlated residuals. The same analytic approach was used to compare serial changes in physiologic measures of sympathovagal balance (resting heart rate and heart rate variability) and cholinergic symptom score. For all comparisons, statistical significance was inferred for a 2-tailed P value <0.05. The planned sample size of 40 subjects with post-treatment data provided 87% power to detect a difference of 5 beats/min in postexercise HRR1 between groups with 2-tailed α = 0.05. A 5 beats/min difference between groups was considered clinically meaningful and achievable based on prior publications of short-term administration of pyridostigmine and the prognostic significance of HRR1 in heart failure populations.16,19

Study drug adherence and tolerability

Study drug adherence based on pill counts was ≥89% at all study visits and did not differ between treatment groups (P = 0.25). The proportion of subjects who tolerated the highest dose of study drug (60 mg TID) did not differ between groups (pyridostigmine bromide 75% vs placebo 82%, P = 0.80).

Postexercise HRR1 data are summarized in Figure 3. Postexercise HRR1 did not differ between groups before study drug administration and increased significantly from baseline after 4 weeks of maximally tolerated dose within the pyridostigmine bromide group (estimated mean change, 4.65 beats/min; 95% CI, 0.16–9.15 beats/min; P = 0.042) but did not change from baseline after 4 weeks of maximally tolerated dose within the placebo group (estimated mean change, 2.62 beats/min; 95% CI, −1.72 to 6.97 beats/min; P = 0.24). The estimated mean change from baseline in postexercise HRR1 did not differ between randomized treatment groups (2.04 beats/min; 95% CI, −8.29 to 4.23 beats/min; P = 0.53).

Laboratory analyses

Pyridostigmine bromide significantly decreased RBC acetylcholinesterase activity compared with placebo (P value for treatment by visit interaction <0.02, Figure 4). After 4 weeks of the highest tolerated dose of study drug at visit 9, the RBC acetylcholinesterase activity decreased from pretreatment baseline by 22% in the pyridostigmine group and increased by 20% in the placebo group (P < 0.001). In the pyridostigmine bromide group, the change in RBC acetylcholinesterase activity was not significantly associated with the change in heart rate recovery (P = NS). The change in plasma catecholamines from pretreatment baseline (visit 2) to 1 week after completion of study drug treatment (visit 12) did not differ between treatment groups (placebo vs pyridostigmine bromide, 445 ± 160 to 511 ± 355 pg/mL vs 625 ± 271 to 517 ± 215 pg/mL; P = NS).

Safety assessments and tolerability

The cholinergic symptoms score significantly worsened in subjects treated with pyridostigmine bromide compared with placebo (estimated mean difference after 4 weeks of maximal tolerated study drug 8.6 ± 2.1 points; P < 0.001). Noncholinergic symptoms did not differ between groups (P = 0.60). Gastrointestinal complaints were reported more often in the pyridostigmine group (Table 4). There were no significant differences between groups for heart rate and blood pressure changes after study drug, Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire quality of life scores, ECG intervals, spirometry testing, safety laboratory data, or implanted device detection of arrhythmia events or ventricular pacing requirements (data not shown).

Discussion

This single-center, prospective, randomized, double-blind study compared the effects of pyridostigmine bromide 15 to 60 mg TID versus matching placebo over 8 weeks on RBC acetylcholinesterase activity, physiologic measures of parasympathetic signaling at the sinoatrial node of the heart, and safety and tolerability measures of cholinergic excess in ambulatory subjects with chronic heart failure. The most salient findings are that pyridostigmine bromide treatment significantly decreased RBC acetylcholinesterase activity but did not alter the primary physiologic end point measure of postexercise HRR1 or the secondary physiologic end point measures of heart rate variability compared with placebo. Pyridostigmine bromide therapy was associated with increased frequency of gastrointestinal symptoms of cholinergic excess compared with placebo, but other safety assessments and rates of study drug discontinuation did not differ between the treatment groups. These findings extend previous reports of pyridostigmine bromide pharmacodynamics to a wider dose range and longer time interval and provide novel insight into the potential therapeutic utility of pyridostigmine bromide in cardiovascular disease populations.

To our knowledge, a randomized placebo-controlled study design with pyridostigmine bromide treatment duration of 8 weeks combined with the prospective collection of pharmacodynamic, safety, tolerability, and physiologic data has not been previously reported in cardiovascular disease populations or normal human subjects. The rationale for the present study design was based on preclinical data and prior clinical investigations showing that physiologic measures of parasympathetic heart rate regulation were modifiable by short-term acetylcholinesterase inhibition with pyridostigmine.7, 8, 9, 10,19,23,35, 36, 37 Accordingly, the absence of significant differences between pyridostigmine bromide and placebo on postexercise heart rate recovery and resting heart rate variability during chronic dosing over 8 weeks in the present study is an unanticipated finding. One possible explanation is that the selected pyridostigmine bromide dosing regimen was not optimal, but the degree of RBC acetylcholinesterase activity inhibition observed in our study (22% reduction from pretreatment baseline) is comparable to that reported in prior studies of normal subjects and is within the range demonstrated to be protective against the effects of organophosphorus agent exposure.37, 38, 39, 40 The increased frequency of gastrointestinal side effects in the pyridostigmine bromide group is also consistent with prior reports and suggests that RBC acetylcholinesterase inhibition served as a suitable surrogate measure to reflect excess cholinergic signaling in physiologically relevant tissues.1,2,4,5,39 The longer treatment duration in the present study may have mitigated the physiologic effects of pyridostigmine bromide due to associated downregulation or uncoupling of muscarinic receptor signaling in the cardiomyocytes of the sinoatrial node.41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46 Chronic inflammation and changes in blood–brain barrier function present in heart failure and other cardiovascular diseases may also have disrupted the effects of pyridostigmine bromide on measures of sympathovagal balance via central nervous system cholinergic signaling pathways.4,47, 48, 49 Further preclinical and clinical studies are needed to delineate the pharmacodynamic and pathobiological factors contributing to the reported study findings.

The validity of the reported findings is supported by several strengths of the study design and execution, including prospective randomized treatment assignment, high rate of study drug adherence, low drop-out rates, and multidomain assessment of acetylcholinesterase activity inhibition and parasympathetic heart rate modulation. There are also several limitations to consider in the interpretation of the study findings. Despite verification of accurate data entry by an independent audit, we cannot exclude the possibility that the partial unblinding of treatment assignment by the study coordinator may have introduced bias into the study. The study population consisted mostly of male systolic heart failure patients recruited from a single center, so our findings may not be generalizable to other heart failure subpopulations, normal human subjects, or other cardiovascular disease populations. Race and ethnic group differences in the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of pyridostigmine bromide have not been previously characterized; it is possible that differences in race distribution observed in the 2 treatment groups contributed to the findings. The small sample size, which did not achieve the target enrollment, reduced study power and limited the ability to exclude significant between-group differences in the primary and secondary end points. The duration of treatment at the target dose may not reflect safety and tolerability during longer dosing periods typical of heart failure and other chronic diseases. Blood pyridostigmine levels were not measured, so interindividual variability in pyridostigmine bromide pharmacokinetics may have impacted the measurement of acetylcholinesterase activity inhibition and physiologic measures of parasympathetic modulation of heart rate and sympathovagal balance at 2 hours after dose administration.3 There were no observed changes in catecholamine levels associated with pyridostigmine bromide treatment in our study population, but parasympathetically mediated changes in cardiac structure and function, inflammatory biomarkers, and other neurohormonal biomarkers potentially relevant to clinical outcome measures in heart failure were not assessed.50,51

Conclusions

This single-site prospective randomized clinical trial showed that pyridostigmine bromide administered at a dose range from 45 to 180 mg daily over 8 weeks was associated with significant reduction of RBC acetylcholinesterase activity and mild symptoms of cholinergic excess compared with placebo but, in contrast to previous acute dosing studies, did not change selected physiologic measures of parasympathetic control of heart rate and sympathovagal balance compared with placebo. Although the findings do not support future clinical trials with pyridostigmine bromide in heart failure patients, additional investigations to assess cholinergic signaling mechanisms and explore alternative strategies to address sympathovagal imbalance in cardiovascular disease are warranted.
