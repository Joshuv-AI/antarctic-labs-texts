Exploration of Perceptions of Deprescribing Practices Among Doctors in a Low‐ and Middle‐Income Country: A Mixed‐Methods Study
Authors: Anjan Khadka, Sammodavardhana Kaundinnyayana, Kumar Roka, Arjun Poudel, Shakti Shrestha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWith the increasing prevalence of potentially inappropriate medications in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) like Nepal, the role of deprescribing is ever important. However, evidence on how Nepalese doctors perceive deprescribing in their practice is lacking. A sequential explanatory mixed‐method study was conducted among 115 doctors at a tertiary‐level teaching hospital in central Nepal using a structured questionnaire. Quantitative data from Likert‐scale items were analysed descriptively, while thematically analysing open‐ended responses. Only 45 doctors (39.1%) had heard of deprescribing, and 77.8% reported practising it. Most doctors (80%) understood the rationale behind each prescription, and 94.3% agreed on ensuring patient comprehension of prescribed medicines. Only 52.4% routinely involved patients in deprescribing decisions, and 31.4% acknowledged prescribing ‘too many drugs’ at times. Qualitative analysis identified three deprescribing‐related themes: (1) general understanding: deprescribing viewed as a planned, systematic process to optimize treatment, minimize polypharmacy, reduce costs, and improve safety; (2) Enablers: patient factors, treatment considerations, medication issues, disease process, prescriber competencies and institutional support; (3) barriers: patient resistance, prescribing inertia, knowledge gaps, coordination issues, time constraints, cost and pharmaceutical influences. Doctors demonstrated a moderate understanding of deprescribing but faced several contextual challenges, highlighting the need for targeted education, institutional policies and deprescribing frameworks in LMICs.Keywords: deprescribing, doctors, Nepal, perception, practices

Introduction and Background

Deprescribing is a process of withdrawing inappropriate medicines under the supervision of a healthcare professional to improve outcomes [1]. Various reviews have demonstrated that expert and meticulous clinical judgement is needed for prescribing and deprescribing to weigh the benefits and harms of medications, reduce potential harms, and enhance patient outcomes [2, 3, 4, 5]. Currently, the necessity of implementing deprescribing in clinical practice is becoming widely acknowledged [6, 7, 8, 9]. Reducing or stopping medicine(s) in clinical practice presents a challenge for medical professionals. Planning and assessing future interventions that facilitate the application of deprescribing in patient care must begin with an awareness of the factors that encourage and hinder deprescribing among healthcare providers [10, 11, 12].

Various studies have explored the perceptions, challenges and facilitating factors towards deprescribing among doctors in Australia, New Zealand, Singapore and Ireland [6, 7, 8, 9]. Studies from such settings have suggested that healthcare practitioners, particularly general practitioners (GPs), were confident in deprescribing practices and also accepted associated challenges [6, 13]. GPs also identified targeted engagement to spread awareness of the risks of polypharmacy and harmful effects to encourage acceptance of deprescribing among patients and their relatives. While most evidence supporting deprescribing for practice and around the perspectives of health professionals towards deprescribing comes from developed countries [6, 8, 9, 11, 12, 13, 14], evidence from low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) is limited. This gap is particularly apparent in the WHO Southeast Asia Region, which has one of the highest prevalences of preventable medication‐related harms [14, 15, 16]. Identifying the perception, enablers and barriers to deprescribing that may be related to patients and healthcare providers and create more efficient tactics for this process in LMICs is imperative. Understanding doctors' attitudes towards deprescribing is crucial for developing interventions and educational programmes that promote evidence‐based deprescribing practices for better patient‐centred care in LMICs [14, 17, 18]. Nepal is one of the LMICs of Southeast Asia Region and no previous study has explored the perception of Nepalese doctors regarding deprescribing, including enablers and barriers of deprescribing.

Aim

This study aimed to explore the perspectives of medical doctors working in a tertiary level hospital of Nepal, focusing on their perception, enablers, barriers and practices of deprescribing.

Ethics Statement

Ethical approval was obtained from the Nepalese Army Institute of Health Sciences (NAIHS) Institutional Review Committee (IRC) (Registration No. 964) on 01 February 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the Basic and Clinical Pharmacology and Toxicology policy for clinical studies [19].

Methods and Materials

Study Design and Setting

This study employed a sequential explanatory mixed‐methods design to combine quantitative screening using Likert items and qualitative exploration using open‐ended questions. The study was conducted from June to August 2024 among doctors working at Shree Birendra Hospital (SBH), a tertiary‐level hospital in central Nepal, and a teaching hospital of NAIHS. This setting was ideal for investigating deprescribing practices among physicians because it is serviced by a diverse pool of medical specialists and physicians engaged in both patient care and academic activities. This study site is also the workplace for three of the researchers (AK, SA and KR). The institutional familiarity was crucial for a convenience sampling approach to navigate the hospital environment, identify appropriate timing for participant recruitment, and establish rapport with physicians.

Participants, Sample Size and Recruitment

Participants were included based on the following criteria: (1) provided informed consent, (2) were full‐time employees of the hospital, (3) registered with the Nepal Medical Council either as graduated medical officers or postgraduated specialists, and (4) involved in day‐to‐day prescription and patient management. Doctors who were involved only in academic activities were excluded. A sample size of 115 was determined by applying the Yamane sample size determination formula and considering a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error and doctors working at SBH fulfilling inclusion criteria were 160 [20]. Doctors from 21 departments and specialties who fulfilled the criteria and were readily available and accessible during break time and postduty or shift changes were approached for the study.

Doctors were recruited using a convenience sampling during their break periods and time of availability. Researchers (KR and AK) approached participants in common hospital areas where they were readily available, such as the cafeteria, doctors' lounge and nursing stations, typically during shift change (8 AM and 4 PM), lunch breaks (1–2 PM) and tea breaks (11 AM and 3 PM). Doctors who were willing but unavailable during the first encounter were reapproached at a time they nominated. Efforts were made to ensure balanced representation across all specialties as far as possible.

Data Collection Tool

The questionnaire included three sections. Section 1 contained the title of the study, consent form, instructions, date of birth in a year, gender (male/female) and specialty. Section 2 included two closed‐ended questions with yes/no options regarding awareness. There were also five open‐ended questions for those who were aware of the term ‘deprescribing.’ Questions were framed to assess their general understanding, enabling factors and barriers to practising deprescribing (Box 1).

Open‐ended questions.

1. Have you heard about deprescribing before?

2. Only respond to the following questions if you have responded ‘YES’ to question no. 1.

a. Where did you first hear (or read) about deprescribing?

b. What is your general understanding about deprescribing?

c. Has deprescribing ever been a part of your regular clinical practice?

d. In your opinion, what factors contribute to practice deprescribing? Please provide short insights into the specific circumstances or considerations that might impact your ability to practise deprescribing.

e. In your opinion, what factors hinder you from practising deprescribing? Please provide short insights into the specific circumstances or considerations that might impact your ability to practise deprescribing.

Section 3 had nine statements on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from strongly agree, agree, neutral, disagree and strongly disagree. Out of them, seven were positive statements and two were negative statements (Box 2).

Likert statements.

Positive statements.

1. I have a good understanding of the reasons I prescribed each of the medicines.

2. Sometimes I think I prescribe too many medicines to the patients.

3. I seldom prescribe five or more number of medicines.

4. Patients might feel burdened when five or more medicines are prescribed.

5. I like to involve patients in making decisions about the medicines.

6. Patients should be asked whether they understand the instructions for prescribed medicines or not.

7. Prescriber should change the medicine or adjust the dosage during rounds for in‐patients and follow‐up for outpatients whenever necessary.

Negative statements.

8. Prescriber should listen to the patient and stop or change medicines upon their request.

9. I would be reluctant to stop a medicine that had been prescribed for a long time.

The questions were developed via expert opinions between the researchers (AK, SS, AP and SK) and pilot tested among 12 doctors working at different hospitals of Kathmandu (National Trauma Centre—2, Kathmandu Medical College and Teaching Hospital—6, Tribhuvan University Teaching Hospital—2, KIST Medical College and Teaching Hospital—2). The questionnaire combined nine Likert‐scale items to capture overall quantitative estimates of current deprescribing practice, while the five open‐ended questions captured depth on general understanding, enablers and barriers related to deprescribing. During piloting, we reduced the Likert pool from 15 to nine items and refined prompts to minimize burden while preserving content coverage.

Data Collection Procedure

Two researchers (AK and KR) approached eligible doctors who met the inclusion criteria. Each potential participant was provided with information on the study objectives, procedures and expected duration (approximately 10 min for the self‐administered questionnaire and 5 min for a face‐to‐face interview with open‐ended questions), as well as assurances of confidentiality and voluntary participation. Written informed consent was obtained before providing participants with the self‐administered questionnaire. Researchers clarified any questions or statements as needed. All participants first completed closed‐ended yes/no questions assessing their awareness of deprescribing (quantitative screening). Those who responded ‘yes’ proceeded to five open‐ended questions (qualitative follow‐up) and rated nine statements on a five‐point Likert scale.

Data Processing and Analysis

All the completed questionnaires were subjected to quality checks for consistency, completeness and legibility prior to data processing. Each participant was assigned a unique identifier to preserve anonymity while enabling data linkage across different sections. Because of the mixed‐method nature of this study, data were analysed quantitatively and qualitatively depending on the types of data.

Quantitative Data

Demographic variables and closed‐ended (Likert) responses represented the quantitative part of data analysis. These data were numerically coded and analysed using IBM SPSS 23.0 to generate descriptive statistics. The Likert scale statements represented current deprescribing practice. Their scores were coded from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with reverse coding applied to the two negatively framed statements to ensure consistent directional interpretation. Descriptive statistics including frequencies, percentages, means and standard deviations were calculated for demographic characteristics and Likert‐scale responses, with results presented through tables and graphical representations.

Qualitative Data

Open‐ended responses represented the textual data and formed part of the qualitative analysis. These responses were independently coded by two investigators (AK, SS) using NVivo Qualitative Data Analysis Software v.1.6.1 (QSR International, USA). Coding consistency was maintained through regular discussions with the entire research team from the start of the analytical process and continuing throughout until consensus was reached on the emerging themes. A conventional content analysis with an inductive approach was applied to explore participants' attributes such as age, specialty, familiarity with the term ‘deprescribing,’ and responses regarding perceptions, practices, enablers and barriers related to deprescribing. Through iterative team discussion, three overarching themes were identified: (1) general understanding of deprescribing, (2) enablers of deprescribing, and (3) barriers to deprescribing. Representative participant quotes were selected to illustrate key findings and enhance transparency. Responses related to factors contributing to and/or hindering deprescribing practice were further summarized into concise lists.

Research Team and Reflexivity

The research team comprised investigators with diverse professional backgrounds in medicine, pharmacy and medicines management. AK, a trained male physician (MBBS, MD) and an academic pharmacologist based in Nepal; SA, a trained male physician (MBBS, MD) and an academic pharmacologist; KR, a trained male physician (MBBS, MD) and academic internist; AP, a male pharmacist (PhD) with professional experience in Australia and Nepal; and SS, a male pharmacist (PhD) and academic with professional experience in Australia, United Kingdom and Nepal. All researchers have prior training and experience in qualitative research, including studies on prescribing and medicines use. SS and AP were not affiliated with the organization where the study was conducted. The interaction between the investigators and participants was limited to the distribution of questionnaires and clarification of any questions regarding the study. Potential bias from AK, SA and KR being physicians could have shaped assumptions about prescribing practices, which were mitigated through independent coding, team‐based consensus discussion and multidisciplinary oversight. SS and AP's research includes qualitative and quantitative studies on medication practices and medication safety research. SS is a pharmacy practice academic, whose research portfolio spans around geriatric and palliative medication safety, quality use of medicines and health services. AP is a senior researcher on Australia's National Palliative Care Coordination Program, which involves conducting qualitative interviews and focus group discussions with GPs, practice nurses, consumers and other key stakeholders. He has experience undertaking several qualitative pharmacovigilance projects in Nepal, where he interviewed stakeholders and consumer groups to inform research design and outcomes. AP has also been involved in multiple projects employing mixed methods approaches, where qualitative research was a key component alongside quantitative methodologies. Three investigators (AK, SA and KR) were affiliated and had familiarity with these institutions, which was crucial for a convenience sampling approach to navigate the hospital environment, identify appropriate time affiliated, and establish rapport with physicians. All investigators had a forum discussion on the study, its methodology and implications, which is continued until the time for submitting and revising the article. To ensure methodological rigour, three investigators (AK, SS and AP) with prior experience conducting qualitative research on quality use of medicines contributed to the study design, piloting of the questionnaire and thematic analysis.

Current Deprescribing Practice

Out of 45 doctors who were aware of the term deprescribing, 35 (M 24, F 11) of them were practicing deprescribing. The responses from those 35 doctors were analysed to assess perception and practice of deprescribing (Figure 1). Doctors who were practicing deprescribing were medical officers, internists, orthopaedists, pulmonologists, an obstetrician and gynaecologist, a surgeon, an interventional radiologist, an otorhinolaryngologist, a dermatologist, a nephrologist and an ophthalmologist. The majority of doctors (80%) understood the rationale behind each prescription, and they were confident in their decisions to deprescribe if needed. However, 31.43% agreed that they prescribed too many medicines to the patients; 80% of them disagreed with routinely prescribing five or more medicines and regarded it as a burden to the patients. Although only 51.43% of them liked to involve patients in decision making about the medicines, 94.28% agreed that doctors should ask patients whether they understand the instructions for prescribed medicines or not.

Physician's perception and practice of deprescribing.

The Likert‐scale analysis revealed several insights into physicians' deprescribing attitudes and practices. The mean score (±SD) for asking patients about medication comprehension was 4.54 ± 1.45 and for adjusting medications during follow‐ups was 4.40 ± 1.38, indicating strong agreement. Theme 1: General Understanding of Deprescribing

The responses of 45 doctors regarding the understanding of deprescribing were included in the study and classified into four subthemes. They have heard of deprescribing during their clinical practice, continuing medical education seminars, colleagues, medical literature, webinars, during postgraduate residency training and books.

Subtheme 1—Deprescribing as Dose Reduction or Stopping Medicine

The participating doctors generally associated deprescribing with a planned process to discontinue or reduce the dose reduction or change of medication(s) that are inappropriate. A number of them considered deprescribing as a means to mitigate the possibility of medicine harm associated with medication(s) to the patients, including adverse drug reactions and drug–drug interactions.

[Q1] 52 y, M Dermatologist: ‘[Deprescribing is] Planned and process of reducing the dose of drugs or stopping if not having any benefits or harming or giving more side effects.’

[Q2] 48 y F, Pulmonologist: ‘Drugs which has been started initially but are associated with risks and harm to patients should be managed with care either by stopping them or withdrawing them.’

[Q2] 48 y F, Pulmonologist: ‘Drugs which has been started initially but are associated with risks and harm to patients should be managed with care either by stopping them or withdrawing them.’

[Q3] 29 y, M, Internist: ‘Reducing dose or stopping medication patient is taking if it is of more than required dose for now or not needed or is causing side effects.’

[Q3] 29 y, M, Internist: ‘Reducing dose or stopping medication patient is taking if it is of more than required dose for now or not needed or is causing side effects.’

Discussion

This study found that 39.13% of the doctors of the hospital (a tertiary‐level hospital of a lower middle‐income country) were aware of deprescribing; however, only 77.78% of them responded on contributing factors and barriers to deprescribing. This finding is comparable with a Nigerian studies by Akande‐Sholabi W et al. (80%) and Saka SA et al. (93.4%) [11, 21].

Current Practice on Deprescribing

According to the doctors, the prescriber's knowledge and skills are essential in deprescribing as patients might oppose deprescribing due to fear or confusion, and the placebo effect of medicine can serve as a barrier, although it has no therapeutic advantage [22, 23, 24]. An efficient and robust framework regarding deprescribing is essential in our settings, along with training and workshops to disseminate the concept and merits of deprescribing among doctors. The majority of doctors understand the rationale behind each prescription, and they were confident in their decisions to deprescribe if needed, which is similar to a study by Djatche L et al. (72%) and Fadare JO et al. (85%) [25, 26]. Polypharmacy is regarded as burdensome to patients; hence, involving patients in decision making for modification of their medications is preferred [26, 27]. The study revealed a concerning disconnect in how doctors approach medication management. While physicians were confident in their prescribing knowledge and believed patients should understand their medications, they were hesitant to involve patients in making medication decisions. Even though doctors recognized that use of multiple medications is burden for patients, they struggled to change their prescribing habits or reduce medication loads. Most problematically, physicians were reluctant to stop long‐term medications or listen to patients' concerns about their drugs. This resistance likely stems from fears about liability and uncertainty about what might happen when medications are discontinued, highlighting the urgent need for better training and support systems.

General Understanding of Deprescribing

The concept of deprescribing was not much familiar, and there was variation in knowledge among those who were aware of the concept. This variation could be due to different levels of confidence, knowledge or experience with deprescribing practices. Doctors considered deprescribing as a systematic and planned approach to reduce or stop the medicines that are no longer appropriate for the patient. This is consistent with a systematic review by Reeve E et al. that defined deprescribing as the process of withdrawal of an inappropriate medication under the supervision of a healthcare professional intending to manage polypharmacy [9]. The purpose behind the deprescribing is to prevent the harm to the patient. The participating doctors stressed deprescribing as a proactive strategy, rather than a reactive one, because the benefits of medications can change over time. Therefore, pharmacological treatments must be reviewed regularly to ensure better patient outcomes. The study by Saka SA et al. in Nigeria considered life expectancy, potential medicine‐related harms, medication adherence and the ability to manage medications as important factors that determine the deprescribing behaviour [21]. Beyond its role as a measure to reduce medicine‐related harms, deprescribing was also viewed as a tool to minimize economic burden. Doctors also believed that irrational prescribing and polypharmacy lead to excessive healthcare costs, and it needs to be managed, particularly in conditions where drugs have to be used for a long time. Hence, to remove unnecessary drugs and avoid health hazards and to achieve financial sustainability, physicians suggested the need for sensible prescribing practices with routine deprescribing.

Doctors stated that deprescribing is an effective approach to manage polypharmacy and it aids in reducing unnecessary medicines, thereby preventing adverse effects and the risk of drug–drug interactions. This study highlighted the importance of regular medicine overview and a patient‐centred rational approach in prescribing, ensuring only necessary and medicines are used for better clinical outcomes, supporting the findings of Akande‐Sholabi et al., Linsky A et al. and Nadarajan K et al. [11, 12, 18]. Not only the consultant doctors but also the medical undergraduates were aware of deprescribing and its impact on drug safety and improving overall patient care.

Deprescribing requires coordinated multidisciplinary collaboration, involving clearly defined roles and communication among treating physicians, pharmacists and nursing staff, alongside shared decision making with patients [28, 29]. Effective patient education and transparent communication about risks and benefits are critical to building trust and improving deprescribing uptake [27].

Enablers and Barriers Related to Deprescribing

Around 30% of doctors responded to questions about factors that contribute to and hinder deprescribing. Doctors reported that there are several interrelated factors that influence the deprescribing.

Patient‐related factors are important in deprescribing as medicines need to be tailored to the individual patient. Participating doctors stated that patients struggle to comply with the medicines if regimens are complex, occurrence of adverse effects, illiteracy, and lack of adequate information and instruction. In addition, they reported that psychological characteristics like fear of harm, dependence and anxiety about withdrawal symptoms often necessitate a management plan that includes patient counselling and gradual dose reduction or stopping medicines. The comorbidities and the increasing age of the patient were regarded as the important determinants for deprescribing as they are more prone to polypharmacy, hazardous drug reactions and increased sensitivity to adverse effects, which is consistent with previously reported studies [6, 7, 30, 31]. Furthermore, several doctors also considered patient preference, noting that some patients actively want to reduce the number of their medicines or discontinue long‐term medicines.

Physicians reported that denial of patients and their caregivers regarding disease severity contributes to deprescribing resistance. This finding is consistent with studies by Nadarajan K et al. and Zhou X et al., which similarly documented physician experiences of patient and family pushback during deprescribing attempts [18, 22]. Physicians perceive that some anxious patients deter deprescribing, thinking that the discontinuing medicine would result in relapse or worsening of disease, and some patients advocate for the continuation of medicine, believing that it is useful even when there is no clinical need for ongoing use.

Prescription and treatment‐related factors are important in the process of deprescribing as it has both contributory and hindering roles. Firstly, the duration‐based treatment regimen needs discontinuing once the duration is completed. The prescription that fits with the patient's current needs is important to be optimal. Deprescribing is also driven by changes in recommendations and guidelines issued by professional societies or associations [28, 29, 32, 33]. Secondly, the hindering factors, as mentioned by responding doctors relating to prescription and treatment, included the failure of medicines to produce anticipated therapeutic effect(s) and the non evidence‐based blanket therapy to the patients regardless of the individual need. Other reported factors were fear of negative outcomes on stopping medicine or reducing the dose, lack of defined criteria to deprescribe for diseases and uncertainty on regular follow‐up. These might lead to unwillingness for prescribers to modify or discontinue medicines and might cause conflict between clinical judgement and patient expectations. This is supported by studies suggesting continuity of care or regular follow‐up by the same physician can open them to deprescribing as they can reintroduce medicines if needed [33, 34]. Medicine‐related factors have a vital role in deprescribing as pharmacological properties and effects might demand dose decrement or withdrawal. Physicians stated that the development of new potent and efficacious medicines influences deprescribing decisions. This study also suggested that the disease‐related factors contribute to deprescribing as a new diagnosis requires change in medicine and reassessment of the current medicine regimen. Study respondents indicated that a better understanding of the pathophysiology and the dynamics of diseases necessitates alteration or modification or discontinuation of medicine to ensure ongoing safety and efficacy. This study highlights the utility of expertise and standards of doctors on the deprescribing process. Regular updates on medicines allow prescribers for informed judgements on medicines when to stop, change, or reintroduce. This is similar to the findings by Scott IA et al., Anderson K et al. and Jubraj B et al., which emphasized the incorporation of deprescribing into the daily medicine review and involving patients in the process [5, 28, 35]. Successful deprescribing depends on clear patient communication, addressing medication concerns and collaborative decision making to ensure patient trust and optimize therapeutic outcomes. Determinants of deprescribing include prolonged hospital stays, revised guidelines, poor financial status of the patient, unavailability or limited medicine availability and limited consultation time. In a study in China by Zhou X et al., doctors noted that limited time, and inadequate decision support and training made deprescribing decision making difficult [22]. This study found that the physician‐related factors create obstacles in the process of deprescribing. This includes physician inertia, which occurs when prescribers are willing to prescribe and hesitant to deprescribe, citing the fear of negative consequences, limited time for medicine evaluation and an urge to uphold the status quo, which is in congruent with findings of Ostini R et al. [32]. Another factor is inappropriate prescription due to lack of adequate knowledge about new recommendations, safety issues and deprescribing protocols among prescribers. Some doctors identified institutional hierarchy as a deprescribing barrier, noting that junior doctors are often reluctant to question or modify treatment decisions made by more senior doctors. Similarly, medicine prescribed by the physician of another specialty might impede deprescribing decisions as there will be hesitancy to modify prescriptions, fearing conflicts or misunderstandings. This finding is consistent with previous studies which reported that the junior doctors generally lack the skills to deprescribe medicines for patients and they might believe that deprescribing is solely the responsibility of senior doctors [18, 28, 35]. There is a belief that the personal benefits from pharmaceuticals in the form of financial incentives to prescribers also affect prescribing behaviour, as the medicine will be continued, resulting in overprescribing. We found that some of the doctors also reported reluctance in medicine review, which might be related to stereotype practice based on prescribers' experience rather than individualized patient evaluation. Zhou X et al. commented in their study that there was always the risk of omission bias where doctors feared the consequences of stopping medicines and further increasing polypharmacy [22]. Other factors mentioned by participating doctors were lack of interdepartmental coordination, strict hospital protocols, limited patient consultation time, and ineffective physician–patient communication, causing resistance from patients who are unable to comprehend the rationale of deprescribing, thereby hindering deprescribing. Similar findings were reported in other studies mentioning that poor communication and inadequate information transfer at care interfaces and limited access to medical records as challenges to deprescribing [28, 30]. These study also reported that the financial factors also affect deprescribing choices. In our setting, patients had access to free medicines, and it is quite possible that patients prefer to continue such medicines as they do not need to pay for them. Deprescribing such medicines might make patients feel that the medicines are stopped because of institutional policy to minimize cost rather than clinical judgement.

Strength and Limitation

This study, the first of its kind in a Nepalese context, employed mixed methods (quantitative screening and qualitative exploration). The strengths of this study are piloting prior to the main study, recruitment of a diverse specialty of doctors and the conduct of an inductive approach. A detailed description of context, methods and findings facilitates judgement of transferability and validity. Doctors from different professional backgrounds supported a more complex understanding of the phenomenon and greater reflexivity in the data analysis. However, findings should be interpreted in the context of study limitations, where the sample of doctors was small and affiliated to one institution in the country. Furthermore, doctors who were faculties of educational institutions, because of their role in medical education, may have an enhanced knowledge of deprescribing. While data saturation was reached, theoretical saturation, theory development and developing a deprescribing strategy framework were not within the scope of the study. The reliance on self‐reported responses might have led to socially desirable responses, where they might have overstated the positive attitudes towards deprescribing while underreporting actual barriers.

Similarly, doctors may provide socially acceptable or professionally expected responses rather than their true perceptions about deprescribing. Doctors might overstate their positive attitudes and underreport barriers they face. To overcome this, we used neutral, nonleading open‐ended questions and created a nonjudgmental environment by employing a medical student to collect the filled questionnaire. Further studies should explore the perspectives of doctors from other hospitals in Nepal and other LMICs to come up with recommendations to the authority for the development of deprescribing guidelines and policy in resource‐constrained settings.

Potential bias from AK, SK and KR being physicians could have shaped assumptions about prescribing practices, which were mitigated through independent coding, team‐based consensus discussion and multidisciplinary oversight.

Conclusions

This study highlighted that the doctors of a tertiary‐level hospital in central Nepal demonstrated comprehensive knowledge of deprescribing as a patient‐centred intervention for medication optimisation through deprescribing. They identified multiple complex facilitators and barriers influencing successful deprescribing implementation.
