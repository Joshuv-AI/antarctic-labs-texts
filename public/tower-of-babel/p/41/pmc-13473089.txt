Bclaf1 drives heart failure by recruiting Srsf2 to enhance Hand2 pre-mRNA splicing and pathological hypertrophy
Authors: Yang Zhang, Haiyu Gao, Ying lu, Yingzi Zhang, Meng Yang, Xiaofang Zhang, Yinfeng Zhao, Liying Yu, Yinghe Zhai, Shuang Wang, Lexin Zhao, Guohui Yang, Jialiang Li, Jiaxin Yu, Chenhong Li, Changzhu Li, Yifan Wang, Ying Yang, Yue Li, Luyang Yu, Deli Zhao, Lida Yang, Lei Jiao, Baofeng Yang, Yanjie Lu, Zhenwei Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) remains a major therapeutic challenge. B-cell lymphoma 2-associated transcription factor 1 (Bclaf1) is implicated in RNA splicing and cardiac disease, but its role in HFrEF pathogenesis is unknown. Here, we demonstrate that Bclaf1 expression is elevated in human HFrEF myocardium and in male murine pressure-overload models. Cardiac-specific Bclaf1 overexpression drives pathological hypertrophy and systolic dysfunction, whereas its genetic knockout or adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9)-mediated knockdown attenuates these phenotypes. Mechanistically, Bclaf1 interacts with the splicing factor serine/arginine-rich splicing factor 2 (Srsf2) to bind to heart and neural crest derivatives expressed 2 (Hand2) pre-mRNA and enhance its splicing efficiency, leading to increased mature Hand2 levels and maladaptive remodeling. Inhibition of either Bclaf1 or Hand2 rescues cardiac function and structure in experimental HFrEF. Our work defines a Bclaf1/Srsf2/Hand2 splicing axis as a critical driver of HFrEF and reveals a promising therapeutic target for heart failure.Subject terms: Heart failure, Cardiac hypertrophy

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) represents a terminal stage of diverse cardiovascular pathologies and remains a major global public health burden1–3. Based on left ventricular ejection fraction (EF), HF is classified into three categories: HF with reduced (HFrEF; EF ≤ 40%), mildly reduced (HFmrEF; EF 41−49%), and preserved EF (HFpEF; EF ≥ 50%)4. Clinically, HFrEF manifests two distinct features: systolic dysfunction and maladaptive remodeling - the latter characterized by pathological ventricular hypertrophy5,6. This hypertrophic phenotype involves cardiomyocyte enlargement, increased protein synthesis, reactivation of fetal genes, cytoskeletal reorganization, and progressive fibrosis, ultimately leading to HFrEF7–9. However, the molecular mechanisms driving pathological hypertrophy and remodeling in HFrEF are still not fully understood.

B-cell lymphoma 2-associated transcription factor 1 (Bclaf1) was initially identified as an apoptosis-related protein that interacts with anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family members10. It regulates diverse biological processes, including the DNA damage response, pre-mRNA splicing, autophagy, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and viral infection11–14. Bclaf1 has also been implicated in several diseases, such as osteoarthritis15, colon cancer, lung cancer, and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma16–18. Our previous work demonstrated that Bclaf1 exacerbates cardiomyocyte apoptosis during cardiac ischemia/reperfusion injury14, suggesting a role in cardiac pathology. Nevertheless, its potential contribution to HFrEF pathogenesis remains unexplored.

Pre-mRNA splicing is a crucial post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism that profoundly influences calcium signaling, action potential dynamics, and cardiac function19–22. For instance, Dead-box helicase 5 (DDX5) maintains calcium homeostasis and cardiac function by regulating alternative splicing in cardiomyocytes23 and RNA-binding motif single-stranded interacting protein 1 (RBMS1) controls cardiac fibrosis by modulating LIM domain 7 (LMO7) splicing to activate the TGF-β1 pathway24. These findings underscore the important role of RNA splicing in cardiovascular diseases.

Structurally, Bclaf1 harbors bZIP and MYB DNA-binding domains, enabling transcriptional regulation11. Moreover, the RS domain of Bclaf1 facilitates its involvement in post-transcriptional processes, including pre-mRNA splicing11. Despite this, the role of Bclaf1-mediated splicing in HFrEF development remains largely uncharacterized.

To address this knowledge gap, we aimed to elucidate the role of Bclaf1 in HFrEF pathogenesis and to decipher its underlying mechanisms related to pre-mRNA splicing. Using gain- and loss-of-function approaches via Bclaf1 transgenic overexpression and knockout models, we investigated its impact on cardiac remodeling. Our findings demonstrate that Bclaf1 upregulation drives HFrEF progression by accelerating Hand2 pre-mRNA splicing, suggesting that targeting Bclaf1 or Hand2 function could be a promising therapeutic strategy for HFrEF.

Results

Upregulation of Bclaf1 in human HFrEF and experimental HFrEF mouse models

To investigate the role of Bclaf1 in HFrEF, we detected the mRNA and protein levels of Bclaf1 in the cardiac tissue from HF patients, transverse aortic constriction (TAC) model mice, and the AngII-treated cardiomyocytes. Firstly, we assessed Bclaf1 expression in cardiac tissues from HF patients and non-failing controls (non-HF). Baseline characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. HFrEF patients exhibited significantly reduced ejection fraction and elevated left ventricular end diastolic diameter (LVED) compared to non-HF patients. Quantitative RT-PCR and western blot revealed markedly elevated Bclaf1 mRNA and protein levels in HF patient myocardium versus non-HF hearts (Fig. 1A).

We next established a pressure-overload heart failure model via transverse aortic constriction in mice. Echocardiography confirmed severe cardiac dysfunction in TAC-operated mice at 10 weeks post surgery (Fig. S1). Molecular analysis demonstrated substantial upregulation of β-myosin heavy chain (β-MHC) at both mRNA and protein levels in TAC hearts, validating successful HFrEF induction (Fig. 1B). Critically, Bclaf1 expression was significantly increased in TAC myocardium relative to sham controls at both transcriptional and translational levels (Fig. 1C).

To investigate whether Bclaf1 upregulation is cell-autonomous, neonatal mouse ventricular cardiomyocytes (NMVCs) were stimulated with angiotensin II (AngII). AngII treatment markedly induced the expression of HFrEF marker β-myosin heavy chain (β-MHC, Myh7 mRNA), confirming the establishment of pathological stress (Fig. 1D). Correspondingly, both Bclaf1 mRNA and protein levels were significantly elevated in AngII-treated NMVCs compared with vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 1E).

Together, these findings demonstrate a consistent upregulation of Bclaf1 in human failing myocardium, murine pressure-overload-induced HFrEF, and stressed cardiomyocytes, supporting a potential role of Bclaf1 in the pathogenesis of HFrEF.

Cardiac-specific Bclaf1 overexpression induces severe myocardial hypertrophy

To determine whether elevated Bclaf1 expression in failing hearts directly contributes to cardiac pathology, we generated cardiomyocyte-specific Bclaf1 transgenic mice (Bclaf1-TG; Fig. 2A). Robust overexpression of Bclaf1 protein was confirmed in cardiac tissues of Bclaf1-TG mice at postnatal 6 weeks (Fig. 2B).

Bclaf1-TG mice exhibited significantly reduced survival rate compared to wild-type (WT) littermates, with mortality onset at 11 weeks and markedly decreased survival rates by 14 weeks (Fig. 2C). Echocardiographic analysis revealed severe cardiac dysfunction in Bclaf1-TG mice, characterized by significantly reduced ejection fraction (EF%), fractional shortening (FS%) and left ventricular posterior wall (LVPW), increased left ventricular internal dimension (LVID) versus WT groups (Fig. 2D, E). Gross morphological assessment demonstrated substantial cardiac enlargement and increased heart weight-to-body weight ratios (HW/BW) in transgenic animals (Fig. 2F, G). Histologically, cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area was significantly increased in Bclaf1-TG hearts (Fig. 2H, I). Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated that interstitial fibrosis increased significantly in Bclaf1-TG hearts (Fig. 2H, I). Moreover, compared with WT controls, Bclaf1-TG hearts exhibited elevated mRNA levels of fibrosis-related markers (Col1a1, Col3a1, Tgfb1 and Acta2), and higher Collagen I and Collagen III content (Fig. S2A, B). Consistent with pathological hypertrophy, protein and mRNA levels of atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP; Nppa) and β-MHC (Myh7) were significantly upregulated in Bclaf1-TG myocardium (Fig. 2J, K).

To examine the effects of Bclaf1 at the cellular level, we transfected NMVCs with Bclaf1 overexpression plasmid (Bclaf1-OE) or an empty vector control (Bclaf1-NC). Efficient Bclaf1 overexpression was confirmed at mRNA and protein levels (Fig. S3A, B). Mirroring in vivo findings, Bclaf1-OE-transfected NMVCs showed significant upregulation of ANP (Nppa) and β-MHC (Myh7) expression (Fig. 2L, M) and increased cross-sectional area (Fig. 2N).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that forced Bclaf1 expression in cardiomyocytes drives pathological hypertrophy and cardiac dysfunction in vivo, and recapitulates hypertrophic phenotypes in vitro, establishing Bclaf1 as a direct instigator of pathogenesis.

Cardiac-specific Bclaf1 knockout attenuates TAC-induced heart failure

To further define the functional role of Bclaf1 in HFrEF, we generated cardiomyocyte-specific Bclaf1 knockout mice (Bclaf1-cKO; Fig. 3A). Compared with WT mice, Bclaf1-cKO mice showed no significant difference in survival rate within 18 weeks after birth (Fig. S4A). Efficient deletion was confirmed by significantly reduced Bclaf1 protein levels in cardiac tissue of Bclaf1-cKO mice at 6 weeks postnatal versus WT controls (Fig. 3B). To further validate the knockout efficiency, we examined Bclaf1 expression in cardiomyocytes freshly isolated from mouse hearts. The results confirmed that Bclaf1 was completely ablated in cardiomyocytes from Bclaf1-cKO mice compared with those from WT controls (Fig. S4B). Bclaf1-cKO and WT mice were then subjected to TAC or Sham surgery. Survival was significantly improved in Bclaf1-cKO mice compared with WT mice at 10 weeks after TAC surgery (Fig. 3C). Critically, Bclaf1 deficiency significantly preserved cardiac function in TAC-challenged mice, with markedly higher EF%, FS% and LVPW, decreased LVID versus TAC-operated WT controls (Fig. 3D, E). Macroscopic and hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining analyzes showed no cardiac morphological differences between Sham groups. Post-TAC, WT mice developed pronounced cardiac enlargement, whereas Bclaf1-cKO hearts exhibited attenuated hypertrophy (Fig. 3F). Bclaf1 deficiency significantly attenuated the increases in both the HW/BW and heart weight-to-tibia length (HW/TL) ratios induced by TAC at the 10-week time point, compared to WT controls (Fig. 3G, H). Histologically, Masson’s trichrome staining revealed a marked reduction in interstitial fibrosis in Bclaf1-cKO hearts compared with WT hearts following TAC surgery (Fig. 3I, J). In line with this, TAC-operated WT mice exhibited pronounced cardiac fibrosis accompanied by upregulation of fibrosis-related genes (Col1a1, Col3a1, Tgfb1 and Acta2) and increased protein levels of Collagen I and Collagen III, all of which were significantly attenuated by Bclaf1 knockout (Fig. S4C, D). Moreover, wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining indicated a decrease in cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area in Bclaf1-cKO hearts relative to WT hearts after TAC (Fig. 3I, K). Molecularly, both protein and mRNA levels of ANP (Nppa) and β-MHC (Myh7) were significantly downregulated in Bclaf1-cKO mice relative to WT mice after TAC (Fig. 3L, M).

To further define the role of Bclaf1 in cardiomyocyte pathology, we transfected NMVCs with Bclaf1-targeting siRNA (Bclaf1-Si) or non-targeting control (Bclaf1-NC). Efficient Bclaf1 knockdown was confirmed at both mRNA and protein levels versus NC-transfected cells (Fig. S5A, B). Critically, under AngII stimulation, Bclaf1-Si significantly suppressed the upregulation of hypertrophy markers β-MHC (Myh7) and ANP (Nppa) at mRNA and protein levels compared to Bclaf1-NC controls (Fig. 3N, O). Concordantly, Bclaf1 silencing attenuated AngII-induced cardiomyocyte enlargement, as quantified by reduced cross-sectional area (Fig. 3P).

Together with our in vivo data, these results demonstrate that Bclaf1 deficiency, whether genetically engineered in mice or acutely silenced in cardiomyocytes, consistently inhibits pathological hypertrophy and remodeling triggered by pressure overload or AngII.

AAV9-mediated partial Bclaf1 knockdown rescues pressure-overload-induced HFrEF

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of Bclaf1 inhibition, we administered an adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV9) vector encoding Cas9 and a Bclaf1-targeting sgRNA (AAV9-Bclaf1-KD) to achieve partial cardiac-specific knockdown in mice. Mice received intravenous tail vein injections of AAV9-Bclaf1-KD or control vector (AAV9-Bclaf1-NC) at 8 weeks post TAC/sham surgery, with endpoint analyzes at 12 weeks post TAC/sham surgery (Fig. 4A). AAV9-Bclaf1-KD efficiently reduced Bclaf1 protein expression in cardiac tissue of Sham-operated WT mice at 4 weeks post injection (Fig. 4B). Although tail-vein administration of AAV9-Bclaf1-KD did not affect the survival of Sham-operated mice during the observation period, it significantly improved survival in TAC-challenged mice by 12 weeks after surgery (Fig. 4C). Critically, this partial Bclaf1 knockdown significantly improved cardiac function in TAC-challenged mice, evidenced by increased EF%, FS%, LVPW, and reduced LVID versus TAC controls receiving AAV9-Bclaf1-NC (Fig. 4D, E). Morphologically, AAV9-Bclaf1-KD attenuated TAC-induced cardiac enlargement and normalized HW/BW and HW/TL ratios (Fig. 4F, G). Histologically, Masson’s trichrome staining revealed reduced interstitial fibrosis in TAC mice treated with AAV9-Bclaf1-KD compared with the AAV9-Bclaf1-NC group (Fig. 4H, I). Moreover, AAV9-Bclaf1-KD downregulated the mRNA levels of fibrosis-related markers (Col1a1, Col3a1, Tgfb1 and Acta2), and reduced Collagen I and Collagen III content in TAC mice relative to the AAV9-Bclaf1-NC controls (Fig. S6A, B). Furthermore, WGA staining confirmed diminished cell cross-sectional area in TAC mice after administration of AAV9-Bclaf1-KD, compared with AAV9-Bclaf1-NC group (Fig. 4H, I). Molecularly, both mRNA and protein levels of pathological hypertrophy markers ANP (Nppa) and β-MHC (Myh7) were suppressed in AAV9-Bclaf1-KD-treated TAC hearts (Fig. 4J, K).

To further clarify the effects of early Bclaf1 knockdown as a preventive intervention on cardiac function in TAC-induced HFrEF, we administered AAV9-Bclaf1-KD or the control AAV9-Bclaf1-NC via tail vein injection at 2 weeks post TAC or sham surgery. Cardiac function and tissue remodeling were assessed at 10 weeks after TAC (experimental timeline shown in Fig. S7A). As summarized in Fig. S7B–G, preventive Bclaf1 knockdown significantly improved cardiac systolic function, ventricular morphology, heart weight indices (HW/BW and HW/TL), and fibrosis in TAC mice. Consistent with the therapeutic intervention results, both mRNA and protein levels of the pathological hypertrophy markers ANP (Nppa) and β-MHC (Myh7) were suppressed in hearts treated with AAV9-Bclaf1-KD (Fig. S7H, I).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that therapeutic or preventive Bclaf1 knockdown, initiated after established pressure overload, rescues cardiac dysfunction and reverses remodeling in experimental heart failure.

Bclaf1 upregulates Hand2 expression

Our gain- and loss-of-function studies established Bclaf1 as a critical regulator of pressure overload-induced HFrEF. To elucidate its molecular mechanism, we first determined Bclaf1’s subcellular localization in cardiomyocytes. Immunofluorescence staining revealed Bclaf1 expression in both nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments of cardiomyocytes isolated from Sham and TAC mice (Fig. 5A). Critically, Bclaf1 intensity was significantly elevated in cardiomyocytes from TAC versus Sham mice, especially in the nucleus (Fig. 5B), suggesting nuclear-centric functions. We therefore performed nuclear RNA sequencing on cardiomyocytes isolated from WT and Bclaf1-TG hearts (Fig. 5C). Heatmap visualization identified numerous dysregulated transcripts (Fig. 5D), and Gene Ontology-Biological Process (GO-BP) analysis highlighted “cardiac muscle hypertrophy” and “striated muscle hypertrophy” as enriched pathways (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, we analyzed the pathway enrichment of differentially expressed genes of Bclaf1-TG vs WT, and drew an enrichment bubble graph (bubble size represents the number of genes, and color depth represents the negative logarithm of the P value. Enrichment analysis revealed that the differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in molecular functions such as DNA binding and RNA polymerase II CTD phosphatase activity (Fig. 5F). It is known that the phosphorylation dynamics of the RNA polymerase II CTD regulate the transcriptional elongation rate, which directly affects splice site selection25. We speculate that Bclaf1 may influence the process of alternative splicing. Among the most significantly altered muscle hypertrophy genes (Hand2, Fbxo32, Klf15 and Rgs2) (Fig. 5G), qRT-PCR validation confirmed that Hand2, a key transcriptional regulator of cardiomyocyte hypertrophy26, exhibited the most marked upregulation in Bclaf1-TG versus WT hearts (Fig. 5H). Consistently, Hand2 protein levels were significantly elevated in Bclaf1-TG myocardium (Fig. 5I). These data position Hand2 as a dominant downstream effector of Bclaf1 in driving pathological hypertrophy.

AAV9-mediated partial Hand2 knockdown rescues pressure-overload-induced HFrEF

It has been reported that overexpression of Hand2 in the early zebrafish embryo results in an enlarged heart27. Hand2 is an upstream transcriptional regulator of ANP28. Knockdown of Hand2 inhibited AngII-induced myocardial hypertrophy and suppressed the expression level of β-MHC29. To verify whether Hand2 knockdown rescues pressure-overload-induced HFrEF, we constructed the AAV9 vectors encoding Hand2-targeting shRNA (AAV9-shHand2) or non-targeting control (AAV9-Hand2-NC). To evaluate the efficacy of Hand2 knockdown, we first confirmed that tail-vein injection of AAV9-shHand2 significantly reduced Hand2 expression in cardiac tissue of WT mice 4 weeks after administration, compared with the AAV9-Hand2-NC control group (Fig. S8A). Subsequently, in mice subjected to TAC for 8 weeks, we administered AAV9-shHand2 or AAV9-Hand2-NC via tail vein and assessed cardiac function, ventricular morphology, and key molecular markers, 4 weeks after viral delivery. Compared with the sham group, the expression level of Hand2 in the cardiac tissue of TAC model mice was significantly increased. Notably, AAV9-shHand2 efficiently reduced Hand2 protein expression in cardiac tissue of TAC-operated mice compared with that in AAV9-Hand2-NC-injected TAC mice (Fig. S8B). The survival rate of AAV9-shHand2 mice was significantly higher than that of AAV9-Hand2-NC mice 4 weeks after TAC (Fig. S8C). Critically, this partial Hand2 knockdown significantly improved cardiac function in TAC-challenged mice, evidenced by increased EF%, FS%, LVPW, and reduced LVID versus TAC controls receiving AAV9-Hand2-NC (Fig. S8D, E). Furthermore, Hand2 knockdown markedly attenuated cardiac interstitial fibrosis and cardiac enlargement in TAC mice, accompanied by downregulation of Col1a1, Col3a1, Tgfb1 and Acta2, as well as decreased protein levels of Collagen I and Collagen III (Fig. S8F–I). WGA staining confirmed a reduction in cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area in TAC mice treated with AAV9-shHand2 compared with the AAV9-Hand2-NC group (Fig. S8G). Molecularly, both mRNA and protein levels of pathological hypertrophy markers ANP (Nppa) and β-MHC (Myh7) were suppressed in hearts from AAV9-shHand2-treated TAC mice (Fig. S8J, K). To validate these findings in vitro, NMVCs were transfected with Hand2-targeting siRNA (Hand2-Si) or a non-targeting control siRNA (Hand2-NC) to examine the effect of Hand2 knockdown on AngII-induced hypertrophy. Efficient Hand2 protein knockdown was confirmed by western blot (Fig. S8L). Importantly, silencing Hand2 abolished the AngII-induced upregulation of hypertrophy markers ANP (Nppa) and β-MHC (Myh7) at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. S8M, N). Collectively, these data demonstrate that therapeutic Hand2 knockdown rescues cardiac dysfunction and reverses remodeling in experimental heart failure.

Genetic knockdown of Hand2 rescues Bclaf1-driven pathological hypertrophy and HFrEF

To establish whether Hand2 mediates Bclaf1-driven HFrEF, we administered AAV9-shHand2 or AAV9-Hand2-NC via tail vein injection to 8-week-old Bclaf1-TG mice. Cardiac function, morphology, and molecular markers were assessed 8 weeks post injection (Fig. 6A). In WT mice, tail-vein injection of AAV9-shHand2 did not influence survival compared with AAV9-Hand2-NC-injected controls. In Bclaf1-TG mice, however, AAV9-shHand2 injection significantly reduced mortality relative to the AAV9-Hand2-NC group by 11 weeks of age (Fig. 6B). Crucially, Hand2 knockdown significantly improved systolic function in Bclaf1-TG mice, evidenced by elevated EF%, FS%, LVPW and decreased LVID (Fig. 6C, D). Morphologically, HE staining revealed attenuated cardiac enlargement and normalized HW/BW ratios in AAV9-shHand2-treated Bclaf1-TG mice (Fig. 6E, F). WGA staining confirmed diminished cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area following Hand2 silencing (Fig. 6E, G). Furthermore, Hand2 knockdown via tail-vein injection of AAV9-shHand2 markedly ameliorated fibrosis in Bclaf1-TG mice compared with the AAV9-Hand2-NC injected group (Figs. 6E, H and S9). Molecularly, the levels of hypertrophic genes, including ANP (Nppa) and β-MHC (Myh7), were notably reduced in Bclaf1-TG mice after Hand2 silencing (Fig. 6I, J).

To definitively establish that Hand2 mediates Bclaf1’s pathological effects in cardiomyocytes, we transfected NMVCs with Hand2-Si or Hand2-NC. Critically, silencing Hand2 completely abrogated the upregulation of hypertrophy markers ANP (Nppa) and β-MHC (Myh7) at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. S10A, B) induced by Bclaf1 overexpression. Concordantly, Hand2 knockdown attenuated the increase in cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area driven by Bclaf1 (Fig. S10C, D). Together with our in vivo data (Fig. 6), these findings demonstrate that the Bclaf1-Hand2 axis is a central mechanistic pathway driving pathological hypertrophy and heart failure.

Bclaf1 drives heart failure by enhancing Hand2 pre-mRNA splicing via Exon2-TGA binding

The above results indicate that Bclaf1 regulates heart failure through Hand2. Therefore, we sought to elucidate the molecular mechanism by which Bclaf1 regulates Hand2. It has been reported that Bclaf1 is involved in the splicing of mRNA precursors30. We interrogated the Gene Ontology database (GO:0043484, “regulation of RNA splicing”) to identify 64 annotated genes involved in splicing regulation. Using Pearson correlation analysis of normalized transcriptome data from WT and Bclaf1-TG samples, we assessed the co-expression relationships between Hand2 and these splicing regulators (significance threshold: p < 0.05). Strikingly, the Bclaf1-TG group exhibited twice as many significantly correlated splicing regulators with Hand2 compared to WT controls (Fig. 7A). This enhanced co-expression network suggested potential alternative splicing regulation of Hand2 in the presence of Bclaf1 overexpression. To test whether Bclaf1 modulates Hand2 expression through pre-mRNA splicing, we detected the levels of Hand2 total-mRNA, pre-mRNA and mature-mRNA. Compared to WT mice, there was a significant increase in the level of Hand2 total mRNA and Hand2 mature mRNA (Figs. 7B, 5H). In contrast, the expression of Hand2 pre-mRNA was remarkably decreased compared to WT mice (Fig. 7C). By using CLIP-qPCR, we demonstrated that Bclaf1 had a strong binding affinity to the TGA downstream region of exon2 in Hand2 precursor mRNA (Fig. 7D, E). To functionally validate splicing regulation, we constructed a splicing reporter mimicking endogenous Hand2 architecture (Fig. 7F). Bclaf1 knockdown significantly inhibited Hand2 pre-mRNA-to-mature mRNA conversion (Fig. 7G). Next, we studied the effect of Bclaf1 on the splicing regulation of Hand2 pre-mRNA in vitro. Overexpression of Bclaf1 increased the level of Hand2 total mRNA and Hand2 mature mRNA, and decreased the Hand2 pre-mRNA level in NMVCs (Fig. 7H). The results from the agarose gel showed that overexpression of Bclaf1 increased the level of Hand2 mature mRNA and decreased the level of pre-mRNA of Hand2 in NMVCs (Fig. 7I). Furthermore, the addition of Pladienolide (PLAB), a splicing inhibitor, to NMVCs significantly reduced the levels of Hand2 total mRNA and mature mRNA in Bclaf1-overexpressing cardiomyocytes and increased the levels of Hand2 pre-mRNA (Fig. 7J). Additionally, we measured the total mRNA, mature mRNA, and pre-mRNA levels of Hand2 in cardiomyocytes acutely isolated from WT and Bclaf1-TG mice. Compared with WT cardiomyocytes, those from Bclaf1-TG mice showed elevated total and mature Hand2 mRNA levels but reduced Hand2 pre-mRNA levels (Fig. S11A). Conversely, cardiomyocytes from TAC+Bclaf1-cKO mice exhibited decreased total and mature Hand2 mRNA, alongside an increase in Hand2 pre-mRNA compared with TAC + WT mice (Fig. S11B). Next, we examined Hand2 maturation in heart tissues from TAC model mice and in AngII‑treated primary cardiomyocytes. Compared with the Sham group, TAC mice showed significantly elevated levels of both mature mRNA and total mRNA of Hand2 in cardiac tissue, along with a marked reduction in Hand2 pre‑mRNA (Figure S12A). Similarly, AngII‑treated cardiomyocytes exhibited a significant increase in mature and total Hand2 mRNA and a decrease in Hand2‑pre‑mRNA relative to the control group (Fig. S12B). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Bclaf1, acting as a splicing regulator, binds specifically to TGA downstream region of exon 2 in the Hand2 pre-mRNA and enhances its splicing maturation efficiency, thereby increasing the production of functional Hand2 mature mRNA and ultimately elevating Hand2 protein levels.

Bclaf1 recruits Srsf2 to regulate Hand2 pre-mRNA splicing

To elucidate how Bclaf1 modulates Hand2 splicing, we performed mass spectrometry to identify Bclaf1-interacting proteins in cardiomyocytes (Fig. 8A). Among 819 putative binders, 40 were RNA splicing-associated factors (Fig. 8B). Cross-referencing with the RNA Binding Protein Database (http://rbpdb.ccbr.utoronto.ca/) identified 12 proteins predicted to bind both Bclaf1 and Hand2 pre-mRNA (Fig. 8B). Enrichment analysis prioritized serine/arginine-rich splicing factor 2 (Srsf2)—previously reported to colocalize with Bclaf1 in nuclei31—as a key candidate (Fig. 8C). Co-immunoprecipitation confirmed direct Bclaf1-Srsf2 interaction in mouse ventricular cardiomyocytes (Fig. 8D). To investigate whether Srsf2 mediates the effect of Bclaf1 on Hand2 splicing, we transfected NMVCs with siRNAs targeting Srsf2 and validated the knockdown efficiency of these siRNAs by qRT-PCR (Fig. S13). Among the tested siRNAs, siRNA-1 (Srsf2-si) exhibited the highest silencing efficiency for Srsf2 (Fig. S13) and was therefore selected for subsequent experiments. While Bclaf1 overexpression increased total and mature Hand2 mRNA and decreased pre-mRNA, concurrent Srsf2 knockdown abolished these effects (Fig. 8E–G). Critically, Srsf2 deficiency abrogated Bclaf1-induced Hand2 protein upregulation (Fig. 8H). These data establish Srsf2 as a core effector through which Bclaf1 drives Hand2 pre-mRNA splicing in cardiomyocytes.

Discussion

This study establishes Bclaf1 as a critical driver of heart failure through Srsf2-mediated enhancement of Hand2 pre-mRNA splicing. We demonstrate that Bclaf1 upregulation in human failing hearts, pressure-overloaded murine hearts, and stressed cardiomyocytes directly induces pathological hypertrophy and cardiac dysfunction. Bclaf1 recruits Srsf2 to promote Hand2 pre-mRNA splicing, elevating mature Hand2 levels and triggering maladaptive remodeling and therefore inducing heart failure. Therapeutic Bclaf1 suppression (genetic knockout or AAV9-mediated knockdown) rescues cardiac function and structure in experimental HFrEF (Fig. 9).

Created in BioRender. Zhang, Y. (2026) https://BioRender.com/zx4kdpw.

While Bclaf1 was initially characterized as a transcriptional regulator of apoptosis10,32, our prior work revealed its pro-death role in ischemia/reperfusion injury via p53 hyperactivation14. Here, we identify a splicing-dependent function in HFrEF pathogenesis. Cardiac-specific Bclaf1 overexpression sufficed to induce hypertrophy and HFrEF, while its knockout or knockdown conferred robust protection against pressure overload. This positions Bclaf1 as a central node in cardiac stress responses. Our previous study focused on acute injury and cell death in the context of cardiac ischemia/reperfusion injury14. In contrast, the current study investigates its role in chronic maladaptive remodeling and dysfunction driven by sustained pressure overload. The finding here is the identification of a splicing-dependent mechanism in cardiomyocytes that underlies progressive ventricular dilation and systolic dysfunction. Combining with our previous work14, we identified Bclaf1’s dual roles in cardiac pathology.

The functional integrity of RNA splicing is essential for normal development, and dysregulation of this process is increasingly implicated in the pathogenesis of human disease32,33. Notably, aberrant RNA splicing represents a hallmark across nearly all tumor types34, and can compromise the function of tumor suppressor genes, thereby contributing to the upregulation of oncogenes35. Recently, the splicing dysregulation of CamkIIδ in SRSF1 knockout heart tissue leads to a disorder of Ca2+ processing and a serious excitation-contraction coupling defect, thereby aggravating dilated cardiomyopathy36. SRp38 serves as an essential regulator of cardiac-specific alternative splicing, where its targeted deletion in mice disrupts triadin pre-mRNA processing, impairs cardiomyocyte Ca2+ handling, and leads to severe embryonic cardiac defects19. RNA-binding motif protein 20 (RBM20), a key cardiac splicing regulator, governs the alternative splicing of essential genes including titin and CamkIIδ22. Dysregulation of this splicing function is a central pathogenic mechanism underlying RBM20-associated dilated cardiomyopathy22. Furthermore, RNA-binding fox-1 homolog 1 (RBFox1)-mediated Mef2 splicing aggravates myocardial hypertrophy and leads to the upregulation of pathological genes20. Therefore, splicing factors are critical in the development of heart diseases. The most significant functional domain of Bclaf1 is the serine/arginine (RS)-rich domain, which is typically involved in precursor mRNA splicing11. Our RNA sequencing and qRT-PCR data showed that Bclaf1 promoted the splicing of Hand2 pre-mRNA. Moreover, the nuclear translocation of Bclaf1 under stress fully supports its splicing function.

Pre-mRNA splicing is accomplished by the complex regulation of spliceosome37. The SR-rich family of pre-mRNA splicing factors constitutes a fundamental component of the spliceosome38. Srsf2 is one of the SR family splicing proteins and is involved in many mRNA-modulating processes, such as stability, gene transcription, genomic stability, and gene translation39–42. Moreover, Srsf2 regulates expression of cancer-associated splicing variants in hepatocellular carcinoma development43. Similarly, deletion of Srsf2 changes the alternative splicing of CD45, resulting in a T cell development defect44. Here, we found that Bclaf1 interacts with Srsf2 to regulate the splicing of Hand2 in the cardiomyocytes. Silencing of Srsf2 inhibited the splicing maturation of Hand2, emphasizing the pivotal role of Srsf2 in the Bclaf1-mediated TAC hearts.

Hand2 is a member of the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factor family and plays an integral role in normal cardiac development of chickens and mice45. Deletion of Hand2 leads to impaired cardiac development and increased apoptosis46. Postnatally, Hand2 deficiency contributes to electrical instability, manifesting as atrial arrhythmogenesis and inconsistent PR interval prolongation47. A recent study has shown that knockdown of Hand2 can effectively inhibit Ang II-induced myocardial hypertrophy29. Conditional gene-targeted Hand2 knockout mice displayed a significant resistance to pressure-overload-induced cardiac dysfunction and induction of cardiac hypertrophy gene program26. In the present study, transcriptomic analysis in Bclaf1-TG mice revealed Hand2 as a dysregulated downstream target. Mechanistically, Bclaf1 overexpression drove Hand2 upregulation, and Hand2 knockdown conversely ameliorated cardiac hypertrophy and dysfunction induced by TAC or Bclaf1. Consequently, Hand2 emerges as both a pivotal mediator of Bclaf1’s pathogenic action and a potential therapeutic target in HFrEF.

Finally, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of our study. While our findings elucidate a key mechanism by which Bclaf1 drives HFrEF, we did not assess its potential role in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). Determining whether this pathway is also operative in HFpEF represents an important avenue for future investigation.

Our work delineates a Bclaf1/Srsf2/Hand2 splicing axis as a fundamental mechanism driving pathological hypertrophy and HFrEF. Inhibiting this pathway may offer therapeutic opportunities for heart failure management.

Methods

Animal housing and maintenance

All animal protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Harbin Medical University (approval number HMUIRB3020621). Adult C57BL/6 male mice at 8 weeks of age were housed in a controlled environment with a 12-h light/dark cycle, maintained at a temperature of 23 ± 3 °C, and humidity within the range of 30–70%. The mouse facility followed rigorous animal welfare guidelines, adhering to the principles outlined in the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” published by the US National Institutes of Health (publication No. 85-23; revised, 1996). In animal studies, treatment/genotype groups were blinded during the experimental procedures. Mice that died from TAC surgery or transgenic operation were included in the analysis of survival rate but excluded from subsequent analyzes.

Human cardiac samples

Non-HF samples (diagnosed as cerebral hemorrhage or traffic accident) were collected from the tissue bank of the Heilongjiang Academy of Medical Sciences (Harbin, China), and HF samples from patients diagnosed as dilated cardiomyopathy were collected from the Department of Cardiology, the First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (Harbin, China). Demographic characteristics of the human subjects from whom the heart tissues were used are summarized in Table S1. The use of human cardiac tissues for the present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Harbin Medical University (HMUIRB3020621), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participant sex was determined based on self-report. Participants were not compensated for the use of discarded tissue samples or clinical data. Our study protocols complied with the guidelines that govern the use of human tissues outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The tissue samples used in this study were destroyed after the analysis.

Generation of cardiac-specific Bclaf1 transgenic and cardiac-specific Bclaf1 knockout mice

Cardiac-specific Bclaf1 transgenic mice were created using the CRISPR/Cas9 system by Cyagen Biosciences Inc (Guangzhou, China)14. Briefly, the sequence of Bclaf1 was cloned into the murine α-MHC promoter expression vector, and the obtained DNA fragment containing Bclaf1 driven by α-MHC promoter was microinjected into fertilized eggs. The injected eggs were then implanted bilaterally into the oviducts of pseudopregnant mice, with 10 to 15 eggs per side. Transgenic mice were identified by conventional PCR of genomic DNA from mouse tissue, and the relevant primer pair used for verifying the transgenic mice was: forward 5′-AGAGCCATAGGCTACGGTGTA-3′, reverse 5′-TCTGTACCCATAAGGTCGTCTC-3′.

Bclaf1 conditional knockout mice were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 system by Cyagen Biosciences Inc (Guangzhou, China). Briefly, two single-guide RNAs (sgRNA1 and sgRNA2) targeting Bclaf1 exons 5 and 6 were designed. The donor vector, encompassing exon 4, was flanked by two loxP sites, while the two homology arms served as a template. To verify the floxed allele in mice, genomic PCR was performed with the following primers: forward 5′-GTGAATTCTTCCTGACTTGGGAAA-3′ and reverse 5′-GCTTTAGAGGACAAATGACTTCTGG-3′. Bclaf1flox/flox mice were crossed with cardiac α-MHC-Cre transgenic mice to generate mice with cardiac-specific Bclaf1 gene knockout. Cre genotyping was performed using Cre-specific primers: forward: 5′- GAACGCACTGATTTCGACCA −3′ and reverse 5′- GCTAACCAGCGTTTTCGTTC −3′. In subsequent experiments, wild-type (WT) littermates served as controls, and all successfully generated cardiac-specific Bclaf1 transgenic or knockout mice (both male and female) were used at 8 weeks of age.

Construction and administration of Bclaf1 and Hand2 knockdown virus

We employed an AAV9-based CRISPR/Cas9 system (Cyagen Biosciences, Guangzhou, China) to generate a Bclaf1 knockdown vector targeting the mouse Bclaf1 gene (Gene ID: 72567). Vector construction was performed according to previous reports48 with little modification. Two single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were designed to target distinct exonic regions of the Bclaf1 locus—sgRNA#1 (5′-GTCCTAGACGAGGTCGGTCA-3′) and sgRNA#2 (5′-TGGCAGGGCTTCTTCGTGAT-3′)—using established CRISPR design principles to ensure high on-target activity. A synthetic DNA fragment containing both sgRNA sequences and flanked by Bbs I restriction sites was generated and co-digested with the pAAV[gRNA]-mCherry-U6>Shuttle backbone vector using Bbs I. The digested fragment was then ligated into the linearized shuttle plasmid, yielding the recombinant construct pAAV[2gRNA]-mCherry-U6>mBclaf1-gRNA. This bicistronic vector enables concurrent expression of the two sgRNAs under the U6 promoter and mCherry under a separate promoter, facilitating monitoring of transduction efficiency. Virus administration: 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice received a single intravenous injection (tail vein) of 1 × 1011 GC of either AAV9-Bclaf1-NC (negative control, containing a non-targeting sgRNA) or AAV9-Bclaf1-KO (targeting Bclaf1). At 4 weeks post injection, cardiac tissues were collected for Western blot analysis to evaluate Bclaf1 knockout efficiency.

The AAV9 vectors for Hand2 knockdown were constructed and supplied by Cyagen Biotechnology Company. The experimental procedure involved cloning specific RNA interference sequences into an AAV9 expression plasmid49. Two constructs were generated: one expressing an shRNA targeting Hand2, and a control expressing a non-targeting scrambled shRNA. These plasmids were used to package and purify the corresponding recombinant adeno-associated viruses. The final viral preparations included the Hand2-knockdown virus (designated as pAAV[shRNA]-CMV>mCherry-U6 > (Hand2 shRNA)) and the scrambled shRNA control virus. Virus administration: Male C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old) were injected via the tail vein with 1 × 1011 GC of either AAV9-Hand2-NC (control) or AAV9-shHand2 (targeting Hand2). Cardiac tissues were harvested 4 weeks post injection to assess Hand2 knockdown at the protein level.

Echocardiography

Echocardiographic parameters were acquired using the Vevo2100 imaging system (VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada). The mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and placed on a temperature-controlled platform maintained at 37 °C. M-mode echocardiography was utilized to measure left ventricular parameters, including ejection fraction (EF%), fractional shortening (FS%), left ventricular anterior wall (LVPW) and left ventricular internal dimension (LVID). The reported data represent the average of three consecutive measurements.

Mouse models of heart failure by transverse aortic constriction

Male mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and placed in a supine position on a heating pad to maintain body temperature. The surgical site was sterilized with 70% ethanol, and a midline incision was made in the neck to expose the tracheal area. The skin and underlying muscles were carefully dissected to reveal the trachea and surrounding structures. The thymus, which partially covers the aortic arch, was gently retracted or partially removed with fine forceps to expose the transverse aorta without damaging adjacent vessels or nerves. For aortic constriction, a 6-0 silk suture was passed underneath the transverse aorta between the origin of the brachiocephalic trunk and the left common carotid artery. A 27-gauge blunt needle was placed parallel to the aorta, and the suture was tied snugly around both the aorta and the needle. The needle was then promptly removed, leaving a calibrated stenosis with a consistent lumen diameter. Successful constriction was confirmed by visual inspection of the pulsatile aorta distal to the ligature and by a transient increase in the pulse pressure gradient. For the sham operation group, mice underwent an identical surgical procedure including anesthesia, neck dissection, and thymus manipulation, but the suture was passed underneath the aorta without ligation. Following surgery, the incision was closed with 6-0 sutures, and mice were placed on a warm recovery pad until fully conscious.

Histological analysis

Heart tissues from mice were excised and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at room temperature. Following fixation, tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%, 85%, 95%, and 100% ethanol), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. The embedded tissues were sectioned into 5 μm-thick slices using a rotary microtome and mounted onto glass slides. Prior to staining, sections were deparaffinized in xylene for 10 min, then rehydrated through a descending series of graded alcohols (100%, 95%, 85%, and 70% ethanol) and rinsed in distilled water.

For HE staining, the sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, rinsed in running water, differentiated in 1% acid alcohol, rinsed again in running water, and then counterstained with eosin for 2 min to evaluate general tissue morphology and cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area.

For Masson’s trichrome staining, the sections were stained using the Masson’s Trichrome Stain Kit (G1340, Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, sections were incubated with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin (Solution A) for 5 min, differentiated in 1% acid alcohol, and rinsed in running tap water for 10 min. After staining with Ponceau S (Solution B) for 5–10 min and brief rinsing, sections were differentiated with phosphomolybdic acid (Solution C) until collagen fibers were decolorized, followed directly by aniline blue (Solution D) for 5 min. Sections were briefly differentiated in 1% acetic acid (Solution E), dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. Collagen fibers appeared blue, cytoplasm and muscle fibers red, and nuclei dark brown to black.

For Wheat Germ Agglutinin (WGA) staining, the sections were incubated with fluorescein-conjugated WGA (W11261, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark to label cell membranes. After three washes with PBS(PB180327, Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd, Wuhan, China), sections were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL; C0065, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 5 min to visualize nuclei. Following a final PBS wash, sections were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium (P0126-5ml, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and coverslipped. All Images were captured using a Leica microscope (Wetzlar, Germany).

Neonatal mouse cardiomyocyte culture and treatment

To isolate primary neonatal mouse ventricular cardiomyocytes (NMVCs), hearts were harvested from neonatal mice aged 1–3 days. Hearts were collected, rinsed in a mixed solution of PBS (50 mL) and Penicillin/Streptomycin Solution (1% penicillin/streptomycin, 500 μL) (C0222, Beyotime, China), and subjected to 0.25% trypsin (C0201, Beyotime, China) overnight. Subsequently, the hearts were treated with type II collagenase (17101-015, Gibco, USA) and then centrifuged (1500 × g, 5 min). The cell suspensions were resuspended in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (11965092, DMEM Hyclone, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FCSST-01033, Oricell, USA), 100 μ/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. Cultures were maintained in an incubator (5% CO2, 95% humidified air, 37 °C). After 1.5 h of pre-plating, the supernatant containing non-adherent NMVCs was collected and replated to obtain pure cardiomyocyte cultures. The NMVCs were then treated with Ang II (A9525, Sigma, USA) at a concentration of 1 μM for 48 h to induce cardiomyocyte hypertrophy.

Cell transfection with plasmids or siRNA

The Bclaf1 overexpression plasmid was constructed and procured from Cyagen Biosciences Inc (Guangzhou, China). The Bclaf1 coding sequence was amplified using specific primers: 5′-CCACCAAGTCCCACATACCC-3′ and 5′-GGTGGCTGGTAGTTCTTAGGTG-3′. The amplified fragment was cloned into the CV702 vector to generate the pCV702-CMV-Bclaf1-3xFLAG-SV40-Puromycin plasmid. For plasmid transfection, cells were seeded in appropriate culture vessels. Transfections were performed using the Lipofectamine 2000 Kit (11668-019, Invitrogen, Grand Island, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 2 μg plasmid and 5 μL Lipofectamine 2000 were each diluted separately in 100 μL Opti-MEM (31985070, Gibco, USA) and then mixed well and allowed to stand for 5 min before being added to the cells. Cells were incubated with the liposome-DNA complexes for 48 h before harvest.

For siRNA-mediated knockdown, the following siRNAs (all from GenePharma, Shanghai, China) were used: siRNA targeting Hand2 (sense: 5′-UGAAAGAGGAGAAGAGGAATT-3′, antisense: 5′- UUCCUCUUCUCCUCUUUCATT-3′). siRNA targeting Srsf2 (sense:5′-ACCCAAGUCUCCAGAAGAATT-3′, antisense:5′- UUCUUCUGGAGACUUGGGUTT-3′). siRNA targeting Bclaf1 (sense:5′-GAAGGACCCAAGUACAAGUTT-3′, antisense:5′- ACUUGUACUUGGGUCCUUCTT −3′). A scrambled siRNA was used as a negative control for each experiment.

siRNA transfection was performed using X-treme gene siRNA transfection reagent (4476093001, Roche,Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

In brief, 6 μL siRNA and 5 μL X-treme gene siRNA transfection reagent were each mixed separately in 100 μL Opti-MEM (31985070, Gibco, USA) and then mixed well and allowed to stand for 5 min before being added to the cells. Cells were incubated with the X-treme-siRNA complexes for 48 h before being harvested for experiments.

Immunofluorescent staining

Cells were fixed using 4% formaldehyde (BL539A, Biosharp, China) for 15 min and then permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. After blocking with 10% normal goat serum (AR0009, BOSTER, China) at 37 °C for 1 h, the coverslips were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-Bclaf1 (1:500, AB10546, Millipore, USA) and anti-ɑ-actinin (1:500, A7811, Sigma, USA), diluted in PBS containing 1% BSA. After three washes with PBS, the cells were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark: Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:500, SA00013-2, Proteintech, USA) to detect Bclaf1, and Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:500, A-11004, Invitrogen, USA) to detect α-actinin. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL; C0065, Solarbio, China) for 5 min at room temperature. Following a final wash, coverslips were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium. Immunofluorescent images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

Co-immunoprecipitation was performed using the Protein A/G Magnetic Beads system (MCE, HY-K0202) according to the manufacturers’ specifications. Heart tissue was diluted with immunoprecipitation lysing reagent (P0013, Beyotme China), and approximately 1 mg of protein sample was used per immunoprecipitation. Protein A/G magnetic beads were conjugated with rabbit anti-Bclaf1 antibody (1:200, A300-608A, Thermo Fisher, USA) or anti-Srsf2 antibody (1:250, 20371-1-AP, ABclonal, China) and then incubated with cardiac tissue lysate overnight at 4 °C. The beads were separated using a magnetic separator, and the precipitates were eluted in a loading buffer and boiled at 100 °C for 10 min. The samples were subsequently subjected to western blot analysis to explore protein interactions.

Isolation of adult cardiomyocytes

Heart tissue from mouse was collected and placed into Ca2+- containing Tyrode’s solution. Excess connective tissues were carefully removed using micro-scissors and tweezers. A ligature was secured around the aorta, and a tube was inserted for perfusion with digestive enzymes. The heart was fixed on a Langendorff perfusion device. The heart was first perfused with Ca2+-free Tyrode’s solution, followed by digestion using a mixed solution containing collagenase (17101-015, Gibco, USA) and bovine serum albumin (BS114, Biosharp, China). After digestion, the tissue was transferred to calcium-containing Tyrode’s solution and gently separated using micro-scissors. After a brief settling period at room temperature, the larger tissue portions and upper liquid were discarded. The cardiomyocytes were then transferred to a new centrifuge tube for further experimentation.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic RNA extraction

Cytoplasmic and nuclear RNA were isolated and purified from heart tissue samples using Norgen’s Cytoplasmic & Nuclear RNA Purification Kit (NGB-21000, Canada) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, heart tissue was cut into small pieces and placed in a microcentrifuge tube. Subsequently, Lysis Buffer J was added to the tubes, and the tissue was homogenized until fully dissolved. After centrifugation, the supernatant contained the cytoplasmic RNA, while the pellet contained the nuclear RNA. For nuclear RNA purification, Buffer SK and ethanol were added to the nuclear pellet, and the mixture was transferred to a spin column and centrifuged to bind RNA. The column was washed twice with Wash Solution A. Purified nuclear RNA was eluted by adding Elution Buffer E and centrifuging for 2 min. All RNA samples were stored at −80 °C for long-term use.

Western blot

Total proteins were extracted from cardiac tissue or cultured NMVCs. The concentration of total protein was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0011, Beyotime, China). Protein separation was carried out using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) with 40–100 μg of protein loaded per well. The separated proteins were then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was sealed using a quick-sealing solution kit (L00276, GeneScript, China). The primary antibodies including anti-Bclaf1 (1:2000, AB181240, Abcam, USA), anti-Hand2 (1:800, ab200040, Abcam, USA), anti-Srsf2 (1:500, 20371-1-AP, Proteintech, USA), anti-ANP (1:1000, 27426-1-AP, Proteintech, USA), anti-β-MHC (1:1000, M8421, Sigma, USA), and anti-β-actin (1:200, A00702, Gene Script, China) were applied overnight at 4 °C. Following incubation with mouse or rabbit polyclonal secondary antibodies (1:10,000, 926-32210, LI-COR, USA) for 1 h at room temperature, the membrane was processed for analysis using the Odyssey infrared scanning system.

Quantitative real-time RT-PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from heart tissues or cells using TRIZOL (Invitrogen, 15596018CN, Carlsbad, USA). The RNA samples were reverse transcribed to cDNA using a Reverse Transcription kit (Toyobo, FSQ-101, Japan). SYBR Green Master (Roche, Switzerland) was used for real-time quantitative PCR to detect RNA levels of Bclaf1, Myh7, Nppa, Hand2, Fbxo32, Klf15 and Rgs2, Hand2 Total mRNA, Hand2 Mature mRNA, Hand2 Pre mRNA, Col1a1, Col3a1, Acta2, Tgfb1, Srsf2. The internal control was Actb. The primers were synthesized by Invitrogen and listed in Table S2.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Mouse collagen type I and III (Collagen I, Collagen III) levels were quantified using commercial sandwich ELISA kits (SEA571Mu, SEA176Mu, Cloud-Clone Corp, China). All procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, 100 µL of standard or sample was added to the pre-coated wells and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. After washing, 100 µL of biotinylated detection antibody was added, and the plate was incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Following a second wash, 100 µL of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated streptavidin was added and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. After a final wash, 90 µL of TMB substrate was added, and the plate was incubated for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark. The reaction was stopped by adding 50 µL of stop solution, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Mass spectrometry

Mass spectrometry analysis was performed at Beijing Novogene Co., Ltd. To investigate the role of Bclaf1 in cardiomyocytes, proteins were extracted from NMVCs and separated by SDS-PAGE, followed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining. The stained protein band was cut; the volume of the cut gel is 0.5–1 mm3. The cut gel was destained by 50% acetonitrile (ACN) in 50 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) and dehydrated upon washing with 100% ACN till the gel turned white. Proteins in the gel were treated with 50 μL 10 mM DTT for 40 min at 56 °C and subsequently alkylated with 10 μL 50 mM IAM for 30 min in the dark. The gel was then washed by destained buffer and treated with ACN as above. Next, 10–20 μL 10 ng/μL trypsin was added to the gel and incubated for 30 min on ice, then made up to 100 μL with 100 mM TEAB; proteins were digested overnight at 37 °C. After centrifugation at low speed, the supernatant was collected and remaining peptides were extracted in 100 μL of 0.1% formic acid (FA). Combined the two supernatant and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at room temperature. The supernatant was slowly loaded onto the C18 desalting column, washed with 1 mL of washing solution (0.1% formic acid, 4% acetonitrile) 3 times, then eluted twice by 0.4 mL of elution buffer (0.1% formic acid, 75% acetonitrile). The eluents were combined and lyophilized. Preparation of mobile phase A (100% water, 0.1% formic acid) and B solution (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid). The lyophilized powder was dissolved in 10 μL of solution A, centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and 1 μg of sample was injected into an in-house C18 Nano-Trap column (2 cm × 75 μm, 3 μm). Peptides were separated in an in-house analytical column (15 cm × 150 μm, 1.9 μm), using a linear gradient elution.

For analysis of peptides, the separated peptides were analyzed by Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher), with an ion source of Nanospray Flex™(ESI), spray voltage of 2.3 kV and ion transport capillary temperature of 320 °C. Full scan range from m/z 350 to 1500 with a resolution of 60,000 (at m/z 200), an automatic gain control (AGC) target value was 3 × 106 and a maximum ion injection time of 20 ms. The top 40 precursors of the highest abundant in the full scan were selected and fragmented by higher energy collisional dissociation (HCD) and analyzed in MS/MS, where resolution was 15,000 (at m/z 200), the automatic gain control (AGC) target value was 1 × 105, the maximum ion injection time was 45 ms, a normalized collision energy was set as 27%, an intensity threshold was 2.2 × 104, and the dynamic exclusion parameter was 20 s. The raw data of MS detection was named“.raw”. MS/MS spectra were searched against the PRIDE database with the following parameters: Proteome Discoverer 2.2 (PD 2.2, Thermo). The search parameters are set as follows: mass tolerance for precursor ion was 10 ppm and mass tolerance for product ion was 0.02 Da. Carbamidomethyl was specified in PD 2.2 as a fixed modification. Oxidation of methionine (M) and acetylation of the N-terminus were specified in PD 2.2 as variable modifications. A maximum of 2 missed cleavage sites were allowed. The identified protein contains at least 1 unique peptide with FDR no more than 1.0%. Proteins containing similar peptides that could not be distinguished by MS/MS analysis were identified as a same protein group.

Crosslinking immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative PCR (CLIP-qPCR)

The CLIP-qPCR experiment was performed using a CLIP-qPCR Kit (BES3014-1, BersinBio, China). NMVCs cultured in 10-cm dishes were washed three times with ice-cold PBS and covered with 1 mL of ice-cold PBS. The dishes were placed on ice and subjected to UV crosslinking using a UV crosslinker (SCIENTZ03-II, Scientz, China) at 254 nm with a total dose of 600 mJ/cm² (400 mJ/cm² followed by 200 mJ/cm²) to covalently stabilize RNA–protein interactions. Cells were harvested and subjected to nuclear extraction using nuclear isolation buffer (500 μL). The cross-linked nuclear lysates were treated with DNase I to remove genomic DNA and partially digested with RNase I to fragment RNA while preserving protein-bound fragments. Two percent of the fragmented lysate was saved as the input control.

For immunoprecipitation, protein A/G magnetic beads (HY-K0202, MedChemExpress, USA) were pre-washed and incubated with anti-Bclaf1 antibody (1:200, A300-608A, Thermo Fisher, USA) antibody to form antibody–bead complexes. The fragmented lysates were incubated with the antibody-bead complexes at 4 °C overnight. Beads were then separated using a magnetic rack and washed multiple times with wash buffer.

RNA-protein complexes were decrosslinked by incubation with proteinase K at 60 °C, and RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, followed by reverse transcription to generate cDNA. The relative RNA enrichment was expressed as % input, calculated using the formula: %Input=2(CT,input−CT,CLIP)×2%. The primers were synthesized by Invitrogen and listed in Table S3.

Construction of the Hand2 splicing reporter vector

To construct the Hand2 alternative splicing reporter, a genomic fragment containing the intron between exons 1 and 2 of mouse Hand2, along with flanking exonic sequences, was amplified by PCR using the primers pcD3-mus-gHand2-U (5′-CTTGGTACCGAGCTCGGATCCAGCTGTACATGGAGA-3′) and pcD3-mus-gHand2-L (5′-TGATGGATATCTGCAGAATTCCGCAGTGGTTTGAATA-3′). The amplicon was cloned into the pcDNA3.1(+) vector downstream of the CMV promoter, utilizing BamHI and EcoRI restriction sites engineered into the primers.

The resulting splicing reporter plasmid was transfected into NMVCs. Total RNA was isolated at 48 h post transfection, and reverse transcription was performed using a gene-specific primer (SR-RT-L: 5′-ATTAGGAAAGGACAGTGGGAGTGG-3′) complementary to the pcDNA3.1 vector sequence immediately downstream of Hand2 exon 2, enabling cDNA synthesis from both unspliced (pre-mRNA) and spliced mRNA transcripts.

Semi-quantitative RT-PCR was then carried out using a single primer pair (SR-U: 5′-CACGAGGAGAACCCCTACTT-3′; SR-L: 5′-CTCACTGCTCTCCTCTTCT-3′) that anneals to Hand2 exon 1 and exon 2, respectively. This reaction yields a 1865-bp product corresponding to the unspliced pre-mRNA (containing the intron) and a 565-bp product representing the spliced mRNA. Following agarose gel electrophoresis, band intensities were quantified by densitometry, and splicing efficiency was calculated as the ratio of spliced to unspliced transcript levels.

RNA sequencing

After anesthesia, the heart tissues of mice were collected and the nuclear RNA was extracted to perform RNA sequencing analysis (Beijing Novogene Co. Ltd). RNA per sample (1 μg) was used as input material for sequencing; previously, mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads, PCR products were purified using AMPure XP system, and library quality was assessed with the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. After the library preparations were sequenced with an Illumina Novaseq platform to produce 150 bp paired-end reads. Then reference genome and gene model annotation files were downloaded from genome website directly. The index of the reference genome was built using Hisat2 v2.0.5 and paired-end clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using Hisat2 v2.0.5. FeatureCounts v1.5.0-p3 was used to count the number of reads mapped to each gene. Differential expression analysis of two conditions/groups (two biological replicates per condition) was operated by using the DESeq2 R package (1.20.0). Genes with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 judged by DESeq2 were considered as differentially expression. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was implemented by the clusterProfiler R package. GO terms with corrected P-value less than 0.05 were considered significantly enriched by differentially expressed genes.

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism (version 10) by using a Student’s t-test (unpaired two-tailed), one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post-hoc analysis, log-rank test. All statistical analysis. A value of P < 0.05 was considered as statistical difference.
