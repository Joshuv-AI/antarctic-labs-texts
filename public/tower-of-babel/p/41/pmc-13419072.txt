Central nervous system lymphoma and neurolymphomatosis in equids
Authors: Monica Aleman, Maria I Calero, Kevin D Woolard, Kelly O’Connell, Emily Berryhill, William Vernau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLymphoma affecting the central and peripheral nervous systems in equids is rarely reported.Hypothesis/ObjectivesTo describe the clinical presentation, neuroanatomical localization, laboratory and pathologic features, type of lymphoma, and outcome to further current knowledge.AnimalsFourteen equids (13 horses, 1 mule).MethodsRetrospective study. Medical records from a veterinary teaching hospital were reviewed (1993-2025). The inclusion criteria consisted of a definitive diagnosis of lymphoma of the nervous system. Data collected included signalment, neurologic findings, laboratory results, imaging, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, immunophenotyping, and necropsy examination. Descriptive statistics were used.ResultsNervous system lymphoma, either primary or concurrent with multicentric lymphoma, accounted for 11.6% of lymphoma cases. Multifocal neurologic disease was the most common presentation. Common hematologic findings included leukocytosis with neutrophilia, normal plasma fibrinogen concentration, high neutrophil-to-lymphocyte and red cell distribution width-to-platelet ratios, and hypocalcemia. Cytology of the CSF provided a definitive diagnosis in 56% of sampled cases. Prognosis was poor with a median survival time of 4.5 days from diagnosis. All lymphomas were of B-cell origin, including diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, small lymphocytic lymphoma, and T-cell-rich large B-cell lymphoma.Conclusions and clinical importanceNervous system lymphoma in equids might be more common than previously reported. Primary nervous system small lymphocytic B-cell lymphoma in a horse and multifocal nervous system lymphoma in a mule have not been reported previously. Analysis of CSF is useful in the antemortem diagnosis of central nervous system lymphoma. Neurologic examination, CSF analysis, and necropsy should be routinely performed in cases of multicentric lymphoma to rule out neurologic involvement.Keywords: autonomous, behavior, pain, vagal tone, welfare

Introduction

Lymphoma is a malignant neoplasm arising from the clonal proliferation of B cells, T cells, and natural killer cells within lymph nodes and extranodal tissues.1–3 Lymphoma has been classified based on its location, morphology, degree of maturation, and immunophenotype.3,4 Lymphoma is the most common neoplasm of the hematopoietic system and one of the most common malignant neoplasms in horses, representing approximately 14% of malignant neoplasms.2,5 The diversity in localization, clinical signs, and presentation contributes to the diagnostic challenge. Based on anatomical location in horses, lymphoma has been classified as multicentric, mucocutaneous, alimentary or intestinal, thoracic (mediastinal, thymic), and angiotropic.5–7 However, other locations include nasal, oral, pelvis, ocular, and bladder.6,8–10 Lymphoma has been reported in various breeds of horses with an age range from 2 to 30 years old.11,12 Although sex and breed predisposition has not been identified, the Arabian breed was overrepresented in horses with intestinal lymphoma in one study.11 Several studies have investigated phenotyping of lymphoma, with the largest study identifying T-cell-rich large B-cell lymphoma (TCRLBCL) as the most common subtype, representing 43% of the cases.3

Primary and secondary nervous system lymphoma account for less than 4%, and 5%-12%, respectively, of all lymphomas in dogs, and 3% in cats.13,14 Involvement of the central nervous system (CNS) most commonly occurs as part of the multicentric form.13,14 Neurolymphomatosis is a rare form of lymphoma that affects the peripheral nervous system and infiltrates cranial nerves, plexuses, spinal nerves, or spinal nerve roots.15 This form has been rarely documented in humans, dogs, and cats.13–16 Lymphoma of the CNS affecting the brain and spinal cord has been reported sporadically in horses.17–21 A single report describes involvement of the first lumbar vertebra resulting in compressive myelopathy.22 Neurolymphomatosis has been reported rarely in equids.10,23–26 Our study compiles 14 cases of lymphoma involving the central and peripheral nervous systems. The objective was to describe the clinical presentation, neuroanatomical localization, laboratory findings, and classification of lymphoma based on cell lineage, outcome, and necropsy findings with the goal of expanding current knowledge.

Materials and methods

Retrospective study. The electronic medical database of equids presented to the William R. Pritchard Veterinary Medical Teaching Hospital at the University of California Davis from January 1993 to April 2025 was searched using the keywords: lymphoma, lymphosarcoma, neurolymphomatosis, clonality, and immunophenotype. The inclusion criteria consisted of cases with a definitive diagnosis of lymphoma involving the nervous system. An Institutional Animal Care and Use protocol was not necessary.

Neuroanatomical localization

Localization of disease included the brain (forebrain, brainstem, and cerebellum), spinal cord (spinal cord segments), or peripheral nervous system (cranial nerve ganglia and nerves and spinal nerve roots and nerves). Multifocal was designated if more than one location was affected. General proprioceptive ataxia and proprioceptive deficits were scored according to a modified grading system evaluated at different gaits and maneuvers.27

Clinical laboratory data

Clinical laboratory data consisted of CBC, biochemistry panel, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, and other body fluid analysis when available. Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), red blood cell distribution width (RDW), and RDW-to-platelet ratio (RPR) have been used to predict the outcome of various diseases.28–31 Currently, no published values for RPR are available in adult horses.31 Therefore, archived CBC data from 40 healthy adult horses (7-22 years of age) were retrieved for calculation of the RPR, NLR, MLR, and PLR reference intervals (RIs). These horses were Quarter Horse, Thoroughbred, and Warmblood breeds of both sexes. The RPR was calculated based on the published formula: RDW/platelet count × 103.32

Classification of lymphoma

Based on location, lymphoma was classified as: multicentric, cutaneous, gastrointestinal, mediastinal, splenic, hepatic, nodal, ocular/orbital, nasal, oral, heart, and nervous (central nervous system, neurolymphomatosis).3 Multicentric was defined by the presence of lymphoma in 2 or more organ systems.3 Lymphomas were immunophenotyped using the World Health Organization classification system.33

Classification of nervous system lymphoma

Nervous system lymphoma was classified as central if involving the brain, spinal cord, or both; and neurolymphomatosis if involving the peripheral nervous system. The location of lymphoma included parenchymal vs extraparenchymal. For the brain, extraparenchymal included extradural, meningeal, and intradural extraparenchymal; and parenchymal (within the brain). For the spinal cord, classifications were extradural, meningeal, intradural extramedullary, and intramedullary.

Immunophenotyping and clonality

Immunophenotype was determined either immunocytochemically or immunohistochemically using antibodies against B-cell markers CD79a (HM57, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) and CD20 (RB-9013, Lab Vision Corp, Fremont, CA, USA) and a T-cell marker CD3 (CD3-12, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), as described previously. Molecular clonality PCR for antigen receptor gene rearrangement to confirm lymphoma and lymphoma type was performed when necessary utilizing tumor-extracted genomic DNA and equine-specific primers, and resolved using capillary electrophoresis, as described previously.34,35 Amplified lymphocyte antigen receptor genes included immunoglobulin heavy chain (IgH) Framework 2, IgH Framework 3, κ-deleting element (KDEi) for B-cells, and T-cell receptor γ for T-cells.36

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics included mean + SD for parametric data and median with range for nonparametric data. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Results

Equids

One-hundred and twenty-one equids had a definitive diagnosis of lymphoma (119 horses, 1 donkey, 1 mule). Lymphoma involving the nervous system consisted of 11.6% (n = 14: 13 horses, 1 mule). The mean age at presentation was 17.6 + 5.5 years old with the youngest 9 and oldest 27 years of age. The cohort included 4 mares and 10 geldings (9 horses, 1 mule) of various breeds: Quarter Horse (n = 5), Arabian (n = 2), Thoroughbred (n = 1), Warmblood (n = 1), Morgan (n = 1), Tennessee Walker (n = 1), Appaloosa (n = 1), and Clydesdale (n = 1).

History and presenting complaint

The main complaints at presentation included abnormal gait (n = 12/14), weight loss (n = 4/14), and nasal discharge (2/14). One horse with multifocal disease was dead on arrival. Onset of clinical signs before presentation ranged from 1 to 146 days (median, 9 days). Nine horses received unspecified anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial drugs for an unknown duration, and none received corticosteroids before referral.

Physical examination

Upon admission, weight loss was recorded in 7 horses (body condition score, 3-4 of 9). Tachycardia (51.6 ± 8.2 beats/minute), tachypnea (22.4 ± 8.1 breaths/minute), and normal rectal temperature (100 ± 1.3°F) were found. Four horses had lung disease based on thoracic auscultation (eg, wheezes, crackles, or absent sounds), 2 had bilateral mucopurulent nasal discharge, and 1 had a stridor when excited.

Neurologic examination

The examination was performed by board-certified internists and 1 neurologist in 12 horses and found to be abnormal. The abnormalities included general proprioceptive ataxia (n = 8), spinal nerve deficits (n = 5; nerves forming the brachial plexus, femoral, and sciatic), cranial nerve deficits (n = 3), and cauda equina deficits (n = 1; Figure 1). Four horses had grade 3 and 4 ataxia in the thoracic and pelvic limbs, respectively. Three horses had grade 3 and 2 ataxia in the thoracic and pelvic limbs, respectively, and 1 horse was graded 4 in all limbs. One horse arrived recumbent. Apparent pain was manifested as aversion to palpation of a limb, with nerve root signature posture noted in 2 horses. The neuroanatomical localization was multifocal in 10 horses and single location in 2 equids (spinal cord = 1, spinal nerves = 1). Multifocal localization consisted of a combination of brain and spinal cord (n = 3), spinal cord and spinal nerves (n = 4), and one each: brain, spinal cord, and cranial nerves; brain and spinal nerves; and spinal cord and cauda equina. Neurologic examination was not performed in 2 equids.

Neurologic examination. (A) Seventeen-year-old Morgan gelding with multifocal central and peripheral nervous system lymphoma. Note diffuse muscle atrophy, abnormal posture, and facial expression of pain from multifocal nerve root involvement. (B) Twenty-year-old Quarter Horse gelding with multicentric and multifocal central and peripheral nervous system lymphoma. Note marked quadriceps muscle atrophy resulting from lymphoma in the femoral nerve.

Multifocal brain disease included the forebrain and cerebellum (n = 3), brainstem and cerebellum (n = 1), and diffuse (n = 1). Within the spinal cord, multifocal signs involved concurrent C1-C5 (upper motor neuron signs to thoracic limbs, trunk) and L3-S2 (lower motor neuron signs to pelvic limbs) in 2 horses, and a combination of asymmetric upper and lower motor neuron signs and ataxia in 1 horse, and 1 horse with spinal cord and cauda equina deficits. The remaining 3 horses with spinal cord disease had a combination of signs localized to C6-T2 (lower motor neuron signs of thoracic limbs) and T3-L3 (upper motor neuron signs of pelvic limbs, abnormal cutaneous trunci reflex, sensation). Four of the 8 horses with spinal cord disease also had brain disease.

Clinical laboratory data

CBC and biochemistry

Blood tests at presentation consisted of a CBC (n = 13) and serum biochemistry panel (n = 11). Three equids had anemia (Hct 16.7%, 19.9%, and 29.8%; RI, 30%-46%) and 10 had Hct within the RI (32.1%-46%). Leukocytosis (n = 8/13; WBC, 12 780-22 800 cells/μL; RI, 5000-11 600 cells/μL) with neutrophilia (n = 6/13; 6833-17 328/μL; RI, 2600-6800/μL) and normal plasma fibrinogen concentration were noted in all but one instance (n = 12/13; 100-400 mg/dL; 1/13, 600 mg/dL; RI, 100-400 mg/dL). Lymphocyte counts were within the RI in 5 horses (1788-5606 cells/μL; RI, 1600-5800 cells/μL), showing lymphopenia in 4 horses (858-1518/μL) and 1 mule (470/μL), and lymphocytosis in 3 horses (6515-9416/μL) were identified. The mean NLR was increased in the disease group (3.8 ± 4.5) compared with the control group (2 ± 0.5), representing 43% of the cases: 4 equids with multicentric lymphoma (2.9-16.1) and 2 horses (7.7 and 8) with primary nervous lymphoma. The monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio was above the RI in equids with multicentric lymphoma (0.2 ± 0.2; RI, 0.1 ± 0.04) but within the RI in horses with primary nervous system lymphoma. The most notable finding on the CBC was a high RPR (mean, 0.44 ± 1; control group, 0.118 ± 0.02).

Hyperproteinemia was present in 3 of 7 equids with multicentric lymphoma (8.1-11 g/dL; RI, 5.3-7.5 g/dL) and in 1 of 4 with primary nervous lymphoma (8.8 g/dL). Hyperglobulinemia was present in 4 of 7 equids with multicentric lymphoma (4.6-9.4 g/dL; RI, 2.2-4.3 g/dL) and in 1 of 3 with primary nervous lymphoma (6.2 g/dL). Hypoalbuminemia was present in 5 of 7 equids (1.6-2.6 g/dL; RI, 2.7-3.4 g/dL) with multicentric lymphoma and in 1 of 3 with primary nervous lymphoma (2.6 g/dL). Hypocalcemia was a common finding in equids with multicentric (n = 7/8; 10.2-11.9 mg/dL; RI, 12.1-13.7 mg/dL) and primary nervous lymphoma (n = 2/3; 10.4-10.9 mg/dL).

Cerebrospinal fluid

Cerebrospinal fluid was collected from the atlanto-occipital space (n = 1) and lumbosacral space (n = 8) and found to be abnormal: lymphocytic pleocytosis (n = 3 with one suggestive of lymphoma), neutrophilic pleocytosis (n = 1), and lymphoma (n = 5). Pleocytosis consisting of intermediate to large lymphocytes with moderate amounts of medium blue cytoplasm, and nuclei that varied from round to ovoid to lobulated or cerebriform with finely stippled chromatin, and sometimes a perinuclear clear zone, was seen in horses with lymphoma (Figure 2). The total nucleated cell count ranged from 28 to 1550/μL (RI, 0-5/μL) and total protein concentration from 55 to 370 mg/dL (RI, < 80 mg/dL), with small lymphocytes varying from 3% to 96% and large mononuclear cells from 4% to 97%.

Lumbosacral spinal fluid from a horse with nervous system DLBCL. The CSF had a total nucleated cell count of 870 cells/μL and protein concentration of 118 mg/dL. There is a relatively monomorphic population of large lymphocytes with round to indented nuclei approximately 2-3 red blood cells in diameter, finely stippled chromatin with 1-4 prominent nucleoli and a continuous rim of mid blue (darker) cytoplasm. Other samples and body fluids

Four horses had additional samples collected: abdominal fluid (n = 2), pleural fluid (n = 1), and mandibular lymph node aspirate (n = 1). Two body fluid samples from 2 horses with multicentric lymphoma were supportive or diagnostic for lymphoma (abdominal and pleural fluid, one each) based on cytologic assessment. The pleural fluid had high protein concentration (4.3 g/dL; RI, 1-2.5 g/dL) and total nucleated cell count (12 840/μL; RI, < 2500/μL). Cytology showed mixed cellularity with a predominant heterogenous population of small mature lymphocytes, large lymphocytes, and expanded intermediate-sized lymphocytes with nuclei 2 times the diameter of RBCs, high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratios, round to indented to cleaved or cerebriform nuclei with clumped chromatin and inapparent nucleoli, and blebbing of the cytoplasm, consistent with lymphoma. Cytology of abdominal fluid was highly suggestive of lymphoma. Most cells were intermediate or large lymphocytes, along with a few small lymphocytes.

Imaging

Radiographs and ultrasonography were performed in 10 of 14 equids with ultrasonographic abnormalities consisting of irregular echogenicity or a mass-like appearance in multiple organs in cases of multicentric lymphoma. Lung disease was confirmed in 4 horses with concurrent pneumonia and pleuritis (n = 1). Upper airway endoscopic examination identified laryngeal hemiplegia in the horse with stridor.

Electrodiagnostic testing

Electromyography (EMG) was performed in 5 horses and identified abnormal insertional (prolonged) and spontaneous activities consisting of fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves. Abnormal findings were found in the quadriceps muscle innervated by the femoral nerve (n = 2) and the biceps femoris, semimembranosus, and semitendinosus muscles innervated by the sciatic nerve (n = 2). Multiple muscles of the thoracic limb innervated by the brachial plexus were abnormal in one horse with concurrent sciatic nerve involvement. A fifth horse had an abnormal EMG in the paraspinal muscles along the T4-T12 spinal cord segments.

Antemortem tissue biopsy

Biopsies were performed in 8 horses: lymph nodes (n = 3), spleen (n = 2), and skeletal muscle (n = 4). Lymphoma was diagnosed in the lymph nodes and spleen in 5 horses with multicentric lymphoma. Skeletal muscle had neurogenic muscle atrophy (n = 4) and lymphocytic infiltration (n = 2).

Treatment

Nine horses were treated with corticosteroids after the onset of ataxia (dexamethasone, 7; prednisolone, 2). Treatment duration ranged from 1 to 42 days. One horse was treated with azathioprine for 4 days, and with L-asparaginase and vincristine for 2 treatments (7 days apart).

Outcome

Thirteen of 14 equids (93%) were euthanized (n = 12) or arrived dead (n = 1) at the hospital, and 1 horse was discharged to be euthanized at home because of the severity of rapidly progressive disease with poor quality of life. Survival time from onset of signs to euthanasia (n = 12) or death (n = 1) ranged from 4 days to 6 months (median, 30 days). Survival time from diagnosis of lymphoma to euthanasia or death ranged from 1 to 30 days (median, 4.5 days).

Necropsy

Thirteen of 14 equids had necropsy performed, which confirmed 9 equids (8 horses, 1 mule) with multicentric and nervous system lymphoma and 5 horses with a neurologic form (4 exclusively, 1 primarily neurologic). One 11-year-old Warmblood gelding with exclusive CNS lymphoma and neurolymphomatosis also had melanomas compressing several nerve roots. The 1 horse with no necropsy evaluation was historically healthy but developed an acute onset of rapidly progressive severe multifocal brainstem disease (starting with dysphagia). The locations of multicentric lymphoma included intestinal (duodenum, jejunum, cecum, dorsal and ventral colon, and mesentery; n = 9), cardiac (pericardium, myocardium, and heart valves; n = 7), multiple lymph nodes (n = 6), thoracic (lungs, pleura, and mediastinal; n = 5), kidney (n = 4), skeletal muscle (n = 4), spleen (n = 3), diaphragm (n = 2), stomach (n = 2), liver (n = 1), peripancreatic (n = 1), preputial sheath (n = 1), and bone marrow (n = 1).

Necropsy examination: nervous system

All horses had a multifocal distribution of lymphoma within the nervous system: CNS and peripheral nervous system (PNS) = 8, CNS = 3, and PNS = 2. The location consisted of the brain (n = 7), spinal cord (n = 9), PNS (n = 11), and meninges (n = 10) (Figure 3). In the brain, the location included the forebrain (n = 2), brainstem (n = 3), cerebellum (n = 4), and diffuse (n = 2). In the spinal cord, lymphoma was located within spinal cord segments C1-C5 (n = 3), C6-T2 (n = 2), T3-L3 (n = 4), L4-S2 (n = 2), and sacral-caudal (n = 1). The peripheral nervous system included cranial nerves (n = 3), the brachial plexus (n = 2), and spinal nerve roots/nerves (n = 9). Cranial nerves involved included the trigeminal (n = 1), facial (n = 3), glossopharyngeal (n = 1), branches of the vagus (n = 1), and hypoglossal (n = 1). The spinal nerve roots involved the cervical (n = 1), thoracic (n = 2), and lumbar (n = 1) spinal cord segments. Affected spinal nerves included the sciatic (n = 3) and femoral (n = 2). The location of lymphoma with regard to being parenchymal vs extraparenchymal was not specified for all horses.

Immunophenotyping, clonality, and classification

All 14 lymphomas were immunophenotyped as B-cell. The majority of the neoplastic lymphocytes in the cases of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) had moderate to marked diffuse cytoplasmic and membrane staining with both CD79a and CD20 antibodies (Figure 4). In the cases of TCRLBCL, 10%-30% of the lymphocytes in the lesions were large lymphocytes that had moderate to marked diffuse cytoplasmic and membrane staining with both CD79a and CD20 antibodies. The single small lymphocytic lymphoma had moderate to strong cytoplasmic staining with both CD79a and CD20 antibodies. Thirteen of the 14 nervous system lymphomas were classified according to the WHO classification scheme (1 horse did not have antemortem histopathology or a necropsy examination but was diagnosed and classified from CSF). The B-cell lymphoma types included DLBCL, TCRLBCL, and small lymphocytic lymphoma (B-cell small lymphocytic lymphoma [B-SLL]). The multicentric lymphomas were classified as DLBCL in 6 horses, and as TCRLBCL in 2. The exclusive or primary neurologic lymphomas were classified as DLBCL in 2 horses, TCRLBCL in 2, and B-SLL in 1 horse. Molecular clonality PCR was performed in 5 instances: 2 cases of TCRLBCL, 2 cases of DLBCL, and on the CSF of 1 case where histopathology was not performed. All had clonal B-cell rearrangements and polyclonal T-cell gene rearrangements, confirming lymphoma of the B-cell type, which was concordant with the immunophenotyping in all instances.

Discussion

In our study, the prevalence of nervous system lymphoma was higher than previously described, occurring concurrently with multicentric lymphoma (64%) or as primary or exclusive nervous system lymphoma (36%). Many different neurologic deficits were manifested, but they frequently involved gait abnormalities. Leukocytosis with neutrophilia, an NLR above the RI, and a normal plasma fibrinogen concentration were common findings. The RPR was above the RI in 54% of the cases. Hyperproteinemia caused by hyperglobulinemia was common in cases with multicentric lymphoma but not in primary nervous system lymphoma. Mild hypocalcemia was common, most often attributable to concurrent hypoalbuminemia. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis was diagnostic for lymphoma in 56% of the cases in which CSF was collected. All lymphomas reported here were of B-cell origin. B-cell small lymphocytic lymphoma in the nervous system of horses and multicentric and nervous system lymphoma in a mule have not been reported previously. The prognosis for all cases was poor.

Although lymphoma is a common neoplasm in horses, involvement of the nervous system is seldom reported,3,5,6,37 which in our study accounted for 11.6% of cases, notably higher than previously reported (2%-3%).3,6 Additionally, a case series and 10 single cases with nervous lymphoma have been reported.10,17,18,20,23–25,38–41 Overall, reports of lymphoma in donkeys and mules are scarce (3 single case reports each) with descriptions limited to multicentric, cutaneous, enteric, splenic, and pulmonary lymphoma but not involving the nervous system.35,42–46 Two large retrospective necropsy studies in donkeys (125 and 1444 donkeys, each) identified one geriatric donkey with suspected but not confirmed lymphoma of a peripheral lymph node.47,48 In our study, we identified a mule with multicentric DLBCL involving the brain and meninges.

Although Quarter Horses were the most common breed (43%) with lymphoma of the nervous system, an overrepresentation was not observed when compared to our hospital population. Among reports of lymphoma involving the nervous system, 22% are Quarter Horses.10,20,24 However, the Quarter Horse is the most popular breed in the United States, where the reports were derived. The mean age of horses with nervous forms of lymphoma in our study was higher than that of the overall horse population diagnosed with all forms of lymphoma (17.6 vs 10.7-16.2 years of age).3,6 The reported mean age of donkeys and mules with all forms of lymphoma was 22 years with the youngest being 5 and the oldest 35 years old.42,46–48 The mule in our study was 25 years old. Although more males than females were affected in our study, similar to a study reporting all forms of lymphoma,6 a sex predilection could not be determined.

The neurologic abnormalities in these equids were commonly multifocal with predominant gait alterations. Our finding of 2 equids with multicentric lymphoma involving the nervous system and apparent lack of neurologic deficits raises the possibility that other cases might be missed without a comprehensive neurologic and necropsy examinations. Therefore, a neurologic examination is indicated in equids with suspected multicentric lymphoma. Advanced imaging of the nervous system in our study was limited. Electromyography was useful as a diagnostic aid to identify abnormalities supporting a neurogenic process in muscles innervated by the brachial plexus, femoral, sciatic, and paraspinal nerves of the thoracolumbar area.

Common clinical laboratory findings in all forms of lymphoma in horses include anemia, hyperfibrinogenemia, hyperglobulinemia, hypoalbuminemia, and thrombocytopenia.49 However, a more recent study reported a higher number of horses with normoglobulinemia (63%) in contrast to hypoglobulinemia (20%) and hyperglobulinemia (17%).6 Leukocytosis with neutrophilia, high NLR, and normal plasma fibrinogen concentration were common laboratory findings in the equids in our study. Hyperproteinemia caused by hyperglobulinemia was present in equids with concurrent multicentric and nervous system lymphoma but not in horses with exclusive nervous system lymphoma. Similarly, 2 of 3 horses with lymphoma involving the nervous system were normoglobulinemic in another study.6 These findings suggest that when lymphoma remains localized to the nervous system, a lower overall tissue burden, and lower systemic inflammatory and immune response result in lower circulating globulin concentrations. Previous reports of multicentric lymphoma more commonly presenting with hyperglobulinemia support this hypothesis.3,6,49 Similar to other reports, hypocalcemia was a consistent abnormality in all equids in our study.49 This finding is likely a consequence of concurrent hypoalbuminemia, which was also common in our cases.

A novel aspect of our study was the inclusion of hematologic ratios that have been previously investigated as markers of disease severity and inflammatory status, as well as prognostic indicators in humans and in small animals but rarely in horses.31,50,51 These included NLR, MLR, PLR, RDW, and RPR, some of which have been investigated in various forms of lymphoma in humans.52 Equids with all neurologic forms of lymphoma combined had MLR and RDW above the reference ratio for healthy horses but when separated by group, 4 equids with multicentric lymphoma had an MLR above the reference ratio and 2 horses with primary lymphoma of the nervous system had an RDW above the reference ratio for healthy horses. In our study, NLR and RPR were commonly increased which might indicate severe disease.

Cytology of the CSF was diagnostic for lymphoma in 56% of the cases (6 horses with concurrent multicentric lymphoma and 3 with primary CNS lymphoma), of which 80% had meningeal involvement. This finding was similar to that reported in nervous system lymphoma in dogs with meningeal involvement (78%) in which CSF analysis was diagnostic in 68% of the cases.13 This finding further emphasizes the importance of pursuing this diagnostic modality when CNS lymphoma is suspected, or in cases with multicentric lymphoma with concurrent neurologic abnormalities not attributable to other disease processes. In contrast, the sensitivity of CSF cytology in detecting CNS lymphoma in humans ranges from 2% to 32%.53

Similar to other species, nervous system lymphoma in equids can present concurrently with multicentric lymphoma, as primary CNS lymphoma, or as neurolymphomatosis.13,14 In our study, concurrent with multicentric lymphoma was the predominant presentation (64%), which reflects the most common form of lymphoma reported by others in equids (41% and 52% of all lymphomas).3,6 Of 9 equids with multicentric lymphoma, 8 had CNS involvement and neurolymphomatosis, and 1 had neurolymphomatosis alone.3 In small animals, primary CNS lymphoma is uncommon in dogs (4%) and rare in cats (<3%).13,14 In our study, primary nervous system lymphoma accounted for 36% of the cases. Of these primary cases, 3 had CNS lymphoma and 2 had CNS lymphoma and neurolymphomatosis. Peripheral nervous system lymphoma is rare in dogs and mostly secondary, whereas in cats the primary type is more frequent.13,14 All horses with neurolymphomatosis reported in the literature, except for one involving the optic nerve and chiasma, have had concurrent multicentric lymphoma.10,23–26,39,41 Here, we reported 11 horses (79%) with neurolymphomatosis of which 8 had concurrent multicentric and CNS lymphoma, 1 had multicentric lymphoma, and 2 had CNS lymphoma. In further contrast to the veterinary literature reporting lower numbers of cases of lymphoma in horses involving the brain and meninges, we identified 10 equids with meningeal (77%) and 9 (69%) with brain involvement.20 Although rare, other neoplasia affecting the nervous system can occur concurrently, as in the one horse in our study (CNS lymphoma and neurolymphomatosis) also having a melanoma compressing multiple nerve roots.

Lymphoma is classified based on immunophenotyping into B- or T-cell lymphoma.3 B-cell lymphoma is the most commonly reported type in equids, accounting for 56% of cases.3,6 Among others, B-cell lymphoma can be further subdivided into DLBCL and TCRLBCL, with the latter being most frequently reported in horses (36%-43% of all lymphomas).3,6 Peripheral T-cell lymphoma not otherwise specified is the second most common type, comprising 22% of cases in a previous report.3 In our study, all 14 equids were classified as having B-cell lymphoma: 61% DLBCL, 31% TCRLBCL, and 8% small lymphocytic. This finding is similar to other published cases of nervous system lymphoma, where lymphomas were also mostly of B-cell origin.10,20,23–26,39–41

In a study of nervous system lymphoma in dogs, specific lymphoma subtypes had characteristic neuroanatomical locations with the majority being B-cell (57%) vs T-cell (43%) and predominant subtypes being peripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL) and DLBCL.13 Infiltration of the meninges, perivascular, and periventricular compartments were characteristic of DLBCL, whereas PTCL was most often seen in neurolymphomatosis.13 Because of the low number of cases in our study, we did not identify a predilection site within the CNS. To our knowledge, B-cell small lymphocytic lymphoma in the nervous system (CNS, meninges) of a horse has not been reported previously. One report describes a 16-year-old Irish Draft mare with generalized lymphadenomegaly diagnosed with leukemic small cell lymphoma, but it was confirmed to be T-cell in origin.54

Hematopoietic system disease accounted for 4.5% of the causes of death in a necropsy retrospective study of 480 horses.37 Lymphoma comprised 55% of these cases.37 Equids in our study were euthanized or died within a median time of 30 and 4.5 days from first observed neurologic signs and diagnosis, respectively. This time frame and outcome were similar to other reports of nervous system lymphoma.3,6,10

A limitation of our study was the small sample size. Because of the retrospective nature of the study, nervous system lymphoma could be underrepresented because neurologic and necropsy histologic examination of the nervous system were not performed in every case of confirmed lymphoma in horses. Furthermore, immunophenotyping and molecular clonality PCR were not consistently performed.

In conclusion, lymphoma involving the nervous system in equids might be more prevalent than previously described. Although the prevalence still appears to be relatively low, a neurologic examination is warranted in equids with multicentric lymphoma, especially in cases with concurrent neurologic deficits and gait abnormalities. Lymphoma of the nervous system appears to be rare in equids other than horses. Leukocytosis with neutrophilia, normal plasma fibrinogen concentration, increased RPR, and hypocalcemia are common features in nervous system lymphoma. Hyperproteinemia caused by hyperglobulinemia occurred in cases with nervous system involvement as part of multicentric lymphoma, but not in primary nervous system lymphoma. Analysis of CSF is useful for the investigation of nervous system lymphoma. Immunophenotyping and molecular clonality PCR are indicated to confirm lymphoma in some instances (especially TCRLBCL) and classify the subtype. In agreement with previous reports, all cases in our study were determined to be B-cell in origin, with short survival times and poor prognosis.
