Sustainable Extraction of Collagen from Tannery Raw Trimmings Using Ginger Peel-Derived Zingibain Enzyme: Process Optimization with Box-Behnken Design
Authors: Md Faiaz Hossain, Mysha Maliha, Taslim Ur Rashid, Mohammad Kamruzzaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The traditional chemical-based extraction methods of collagen pose significant environmental and health risks. This study presents a sustainable alternative by employing zingibaina plant-based proteolytic enzyme derived from ginger peelfor the hydrolysis of tannery waste, minimizing resource consumption and environmental impact. To maximize the output of the extraction process, Box-Behnken Design (BBD) under the Response Surface Methodology was employed. The design analysis revealed that the optimal hydrolysis using crude zingibain enzyme achieved a yield of approximately 92% at a temperature of 57.83 °C, neutral pH, and a reaction time of 5.36 h. The process demonstrated a cost-to-benefit ratio of 1.44, indicating its economic feasibility for large-scale implementation. The analysis of variance from the BBD yielded an R 2 value of 99.83%, confirming the reliability and accuracy of the process optimization. The structural integrity of the extracted collagen was validated through multiple characterization techniques, including Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), elemental analysis, heavy metal analysis, and X-ray diffraction. The enzymatically extracted collagen, being edible, nontoxic, and free from harmful chemicals, holds significant potential for applications in the food industry and aligns well with the global shift toward clean label products.

Introduction

One of the major export-oriented industries in Bangladesh is the leather industry. Currently, it is the third highest export earning sector in Bangladesh. In a tannery, only a small portion of raw hides or skins are converted into useable finished leather, and the rest of them are discharged as solid, liquid, and airborne wastes causing environmental pollution. Among the wastes, around 800 kg of solid waste is produced from 1000 kg of raw hide or skin. In Bangladesh, around 232 tons of solid waste is produced per day. Before the tanning process is started, the raw hides or skins are trimmed to give a suitable shape. These raw trimmings are discharged as solid waste. Around 50 kg of raw trimmings is produced from 1 ton of raw hides or skins.

These raw trimmings are often discarded untreated in open areas within the tannery estates, leading to an obnoxious odor and significant environmental pollution. Currently, landfilling is considered the only feasible method for managing this large volume of solid waste. However, even under controlled landfill conditions, the process generates approximately 624 kg of CO2 per ton of waste. In the absence of proper management, emissions can increase by 10 to 20 times. The released CO2 contributes to global warming by trapping heat and raising the Earth’s surface temperature. Notably, raw trimmings contain about 21% collagen, making them a valuable source for collagen extraction. The extracted collagen, owing to its biocompatibility, biodegradability, and nontoxicity, holds promise for use in food packaging, biomedical applications, cosmetics industries, etc.

Collagen extraction from raw trimmings can be achieved through both acid and alkali treatments. Acid hydrolysis involves the use of organic or inorganic acids to disrupt collagen bonds, facilitating the release of fibrils. Alternatively, alkaline hydrolysis is typically carried out using aqueous sodium hydroxide or potassium hydroxide. However, the unethical, unsafe, and unplanned disposal of these acids and alkalis at higher concentrations is very concerning for the environment and biodiversity. Enzymatic hydrolysis has become a viable option in the search for sustainable collagen recovery technologies, overcoming many of the drawbacks of conventional acid- and alkali-based hydrolysis. Enzymes offer enhanced reaction specificity, selectively cleaving targeted bonds within the collagen precursor or surrounding matrix. Moreover, compared to chemical treatments, enzymatic processes provide greater control over the degree of hydrolysis, require lower energy input, generate less waste, are less corrosive to equipment, and result in lower salt concentrations in the final hydrolysate. These enzymes can be sourced from plants (e.g., papain, ficin, or bromelain) or animals (e.g., pepsin, trypsin) or microorganisms.

Ginger is a well-known plant-based source of the proteolytic enzyme zingibain, which has demonstrated the ability to hydrolyze native collagen. Zingibain has previously been employed in the extraction of gelatin from bovine skin. The inedible outer peel of ginger is typically removed and discarded as household waste. However, these peels are a rich and underutilized source of zingibain enzyme. Hence, this study has developed a low cost and sustainable way of extracting zingibain enzyme from ginger peels with the help of ultrasonication. Ultrasonication effectively enhances the extraction efficiency of enzyme through improved mass transfer and structural disruption, and therefore it was applied in the study to improve the collagen extraction efficiency. This kind of ecofriendly zingibain enzyme extraction method has not been reported in the past.

The current study explores the potential of utilizing zingibain extracted from ginger peel for the extraction of collagen from bovine hide trimmings. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, enzymatic extraction of collagen from leather waste has been done with papain enzyme, bromelain enzyme, and ficin enzyme, but no work has been reported which involves the utilization of zingibain enzyme. Therefore, this study will contribute to the advancement of environmentally responsible leather waste management. Furthermore, to optimize the extraction process, the Box-Behnken Design (BBD) was employed, which takes into account the key conditional parameters of the extraction process. A thorough cost benefit analysis was also done to provide information about the profitability of the project in the long term. Thus, this work can serve as an effective and efficient medium for producing a high-value product while offering an economically viable and sustainable approach for managing solid waste generated by the tanning industry.

Materials and Methods

Materials

A local tannery (Reliance Tannery Limited, Hemayetpur, Savar, Dhaka-1340, Bangladesh) provided the raw trimmings, which were further processed in lab. Ginger peels were collected from local hotels in Dhaka. Acetic acid, ethanol, methanol, sulfuric acid, sodium acetate, sodium hydroxide, hydrochloric acid, perchloric acid, nitric acid, n-propanol, sodium chloride, Coomassie brilliant blue dye, and Folin’s-Ciocalteu’s phenol reagent were bought from the renowned manufacturer Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). Chemicals required for the protein analysis and enzyme assay were primarily sourced from India. These include casein from Research-Lab Fine Chem Industries (Mumbai, India), l-tyrosine from Qualikems Fine Chemicals Pvt. Ltd. (Vadodara, India) as well as Tris buffer AR, gelatin and bovine albumin serum from Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd. (Maharashtra, India).

Methods

Delimed Powder Preparation from Skin Trimmings

After bringing the samples from tannery, they were immediately soaked in detergent containing water and thoroughly cleaned to remove dirt, blood, and salt. Trimmings then underwent a series of pretreatment stepsliming, unhairing, fleshing, and delimingto remove the fat, hair, and unwanted protein substances. Delimed trimmings were cut into smaller irregular shapes and oven-dried at 60 °C overnight. Those dehydrated pieces were afterward converted into smooth powder with the help of a mill grinder. The powder is labeled as delimed powder (DP) and is further used for collagen extraction.

Zingibain Enzyme Extraction from Ginger Peel

Fresh ginger was collected, and the peels were separated and thoroughly washed with distilled water. The cleaned peel was then dehydrated at 60 °C overnight. Then, the dehydrated peels were powdered using a grinder. Subsequently, it was mixed with deionized water to make 1%, 3%, and 5% (w/v) dispersions and ultrasonicated using an ultrasonic machine (40 kHz, 120 W) for 2 h at 60 °C. Finally, the dispersion was centrifuged and filtered to obtain an enzyme extract (Figure S1, Appendix A).

Zingibain Enzyme Identification

Two separate solutions were prepared in individual conical flasks. The first consisted of 1.5% (w/v) agar powder and 0.5% (w/v) sodium chloride dissolved in distilled water, while the second contained 1% (w/v) powdered skimmed milk in distilled water. Both solutions, along with Petri dishes, were sterilized at 121 °C for 30 min using an autoclave (DAC-45, Human Lab Inc., Korea). Later, the prepared solutions were mixed and poured into the sterile Petri dishes. After solidification of the media, a well (9 mm diameter) was created at the center of the media using a sterile cork borer. Then, 20 μL of crude enzyme was dispersed into the well and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.

For the qualitative analysis of the enzyme by the agar plate method, a 50 mM Tris buffer solution was prepared. Into this buffer, 2% (w/v) agar powder along with 1% (w/v) gelatin were added and the mixture was sterilized. The sterilized mixture was then poured into a Petri dish. Once the medium was solidified, a well of 9 mm diameter was created with a sterile cork borer and 100 μL of crude enzyme was poured into the well. Then, it was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the surface of the incubated media was flooded with 0.1% Coomassie brilliant blue and again incubated for 3 h. Finally, the agar surface was washed with a destaining solution containing methanol, acetic acid, and water in the 4:1:5 ratio.

Protease Activity of Zingibain Enzyme

Two test tubes, each containing 5 mL of casein solution, were prepared. Then, 1 mL of enzyme was poured into one test tube, and the other served as blank. Both test tubes were incubated for 20 min, after which 5 mL of 10% (w/v) TCA was included in each of them. At this stage, 1 mL of enzyme was added to the blank. Later, both the tubes were cooled for 20 min and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 20 min. From each test tube, 2 mL of the supernatant was transferred to two new test tubes. Then, 5 mL of 0.4 M Na2CO3 and 1 mL of Folin’s solution were added into the test tubes. These mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in a water bath placed in a dark place. The prepared mixtures were centrifuged for a couple of minutes, and absorbance of both solutions was measured at 660 nm. Finally, a standard curve of tyrosine was prepared and the protease activity of the zingibain enzyme was calculated using eq : 1 activity=μmoleoftyrosineequivalentsreleased×totalassayvolumetimeofassay×enzymevolume×volumeusedinUVspectrometerunits/mL

Quantification of Protein in Zingibain Enzyme

Bradford assay was used to determine the amount of protein present in zingibain enzyme. The reagent for the Brandford assay was created by mixing several chemicals together. At first, a primary dye solution was prepared by mixing Coomassie brilliant blue G-250 dye (50 mg) with 95% ethanol (v/v) (25 mL). Afterward, 85% (v/v) (50 mL) of phosphoric acid was carefully included in the solution. The solution was continuously stirred to ensure complete dissolution and homogenization. According to the protocol, the volume of the reagent would be 500 mL, and hence, distilled water was included to increase the volume. Afterward, the freshly prepared solution was strained to remove insoluble particles and stored in a dark environment for maintaining stability.

For conducting the assay, 100 μL of freshly prepared zingibain enzyme along with 5 mL of Bradford reagent was taken, and then these two were cautiously stirred for ensuring complete mixing. Finally, a small amount of the mixture was taken in a cuvette, and the absorbance of the prepared mixture was calculated at 595 nm wavelength. The readings collected from the test were compared with those of bovine albumin serum to quantify the amount of protein present in zingibain enzyme.

Zingibain Enzyme-Assisted Extraction of Collagen

The DP prepared from raw trimmings was mixed with enzyme solution at different ratios, and with the help of a magnetic stirrer and elevated temperature, the enzymatic hydrolysis process was conducted. Then, the mixture was centrifuged at 3000 × g for 20 min. Afterward, the supernatant was dried at 65 °C in vacuum condition using a freeze-dryer (Qingdao Carebios Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Qingdao, China) for 4 days to obtain collagen. Optimization of Collagen Extraction Using Box-Behnken Design

The ginger peel powder (1–5% w/v), delimed pelt powder (3–5% w/v), temperature (40–80 °C), and time (4–6 h) were identified through lab trials as key factors influencing extraction yield. These variables were incorporated into a BBD, a part of Response Surface Methodology (RSM), to optimize the extraction process (Table ). BBD was used to assess the effects of four independent variablesginger peel powder (A), time (B), temperature (C), and delimed pelt powder (D) on the response variable, yield % (Y).

Independent Variables for Optimization of the Hydrolysis Process by BBD

To evaluate the optimum formulation for the highest yield (%), four independent variables and their combinations were used with the help of the BBD. All the self-reliant variables were run at three levels, and the individual coded values were −1, 0, and 1. The BBD was used with a view to investigating the upshot of all the individual independent variables and their in-between interaction on the response. To show the response that was the function of the self-reliant variables, a second-order polynomial equation (eq ) was applied. 2 Y=p0+p1A+p2B+p3C+p4D+p11A2+p22B2+p33C2+p44D2+p12AB+p13AC+p14AD+p23BC+p24BD+p34CD

The coefficients present in the equation are linear coefficients (p1, p2, p3, and p4), quadratic coefficients (p11, p22, p33, and p44), and interaction coefficients (p12, p13, p14, p23, p24, and p34), respectively. In order to optimize the operation conditions, 4 independent variables and 30 experimental runs (each run was repeated three times and the mean response was reported) including 6 center points were used, and the analysis was conducted using Design Expert software.

The predicted value was tested through actual laboratory trials and subsequent triplicates. Control experiments without the zingibain enzyme under identical conditions were conducted to confirm that collagen release is enzymatic and not due to thermal solubilization.

Characterization of the Hydrolysate Solution

Degree of Hydrolysis

For the evaluation of the degree of hydrolysis (DH) of collagen hydrolysate, trichloroacetic acid (TCA) was dissolved in distilled water to make a 20% (w/v) solution of TCA. After that, 500 μL of the TCA solution was taken in a test tube. Subsequently, the hydrolysate solution (500 μL) was added it and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the mixture was centrifuged for 10 min at 3000 × g. Due to centrifugation, the insoluble part of the hydrolysate got settled and separated from the mixture. The supernatant labeled as 10% TCA soluble fraction was then collected and tested for the protein content using Bradford assay. Lastly, comparing the protein contents in hydrolysate solution and the supernatant, the DH was ascertained using eq : 3 degreeofhydrolysis=amountofproteinpresentinthe10%TCAsolutiontotalproteinpresentinthehydrolysatesolution×100%

Extent of Hydrolysis

For this analysis, DP was weighed before starting the hydrolysis process. After the completion of the hydrolysis process, the residue was collected by centrifuging and filtering the hydrolysate mixture. Then, the collected residue was dried for 24 h and again weighed. Finally, the extent of hydrolysis of the sample was determined using eq : extentofhydrolysis=initialweightoftheDP‐residualweightinitialweightofDP×100% 4

Characterization of the Collagen

Solubility of Collagen

Solubility of the collagen was evaluated at various pH levels and NaCl concentrations based on a previously described method with slight modifications. Specific amount of collagen was dispersed in 0.5 M acetic acid solution to prepare stock solutions having 3 and 6 mg/mL. For the solubility test, 10 falcon tubes were each filled with 8 mL of 3 mg/mL collagen solution, and the pH of the solutions were changed maintaining a range of 1–10. Citrate buffer (for acidic conditions) and Tris–HCl buffer (for neutral/alkali conditions) were used in the study to maintain constant pH and ionic strength. Afterward, the final volume of the solution was brought to 10 mL using distilled water. Then, the samples were rigorously shaken at 150 rpm for 30 min, followed by refrigeration at 4 °C for 30 min. After cooling, the samples were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 40 min. Finally, the amount of protein present in the supernatant was measured, and the relative solubility of the collagen at pH 1–10 was quantified following eq . 5 relativesolubility=amountofproteinpresentatadefinedpHmaximumproteincontentobtained×100%

For the solubility test at different NaCl concentrations, 7 falcon tubes were taken, and 5 mL of 6 mg/mL collagen solution was taken in 7 falcon tubes. After that, NaCl was added to make the final NaCl concentrations of 0%, 1%, 2%, 3%, 4%, 5%, and 6% (w/v). Later, the samples were roughly shaken with the help of a shaking incubator at 150 rpm for 30 min. Then, the samples were kept in refrigerator at 4 °C for 30 min and later centrifuged at 3000 × g for 40 min. Finally, the amount of protein present in the filtrates was calculated, and the relative solubility of the collagen at different NaCl concentrations was determined using eq : 6 relativesolubility=proteinpresentinthesolutionatspecificNaClconcentrationmaximumproteincontentobtained×100%

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) of Collagen

The collagen sample was analyzed by a Fourier transform infrared spectrophotometer (Bruker Alpha 11, Platinum-ATR) employing the attenuated total reflectance (ATR) technique. For analysis, the collagen test strips were cut into square shapes (10 mm × 10 mm) and placed directly onto the crystal surface of the equipment. The characteristic groups forming the secondary structure of collagen were recorded in the transmittance mode over the range of 4000–600 cm–1, using 24 scans at a resolution of 4 cm–1.

Thermal Analysis of Collagen

The thermal stability of collagen was evaluated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). The collagen samples were dried to constant weight before analysis. A thermogravimetric analyzer (PerkinElmer Thermogravimetric Analyzer, TGA 8000) was used for analyzing the thermal degradation properties of the extracted collagen with a 40 mL/min flow rate within a nitrogen-purging environment. For the analysis, 2–3 mg of the collagen sample was heated from 50 to 700 °C at a rate of 20 °C/min and the changes in weight as a function of temperature were recorded.

For DSC analysis, the sample was scanned from 20 to 300 °C using a differential scanning calorimeter (Biobase Biometch Co. Ltd., Shandong, China). The preweighted sample was placed in an aluminum pan. The pan was heated at a steady rate of 10 °C/min in a nitrogen environment, using an empty aluminum pan as a reference probe. Denaturation temperatures were measured at the midpoint of the transition peak, while onset peak temperatures were measured at the start of the process.

XRD Analysis of Collagen

Molecular level amorphousness of collagen was assessed through an X-ray diffractometer (RIGAKU Ultima IV X-ray Diffractometer, Japan) which used Cu Kα as a radiation source. For the test, collagen was converted into a (2 cm × 2 cm) square film, and subsequent scans over a range of 5° to 90°, with a scanning rate of 3° per minute, were conducted. During the scan, the operating voltage was 8.04 V, and the λ was 1.5406 Å.

Quantification of Hydroxyproline

The hydroxyproline content of collagen was quantified using a previous method with slight modifications. To prepare the chloramine-T reagent, 1.27 g of chloramine-T was dissolved in 25 mL of 50% n-propanol and diluted to 100 mL with acetate-citrate buffer (pH 6.5). For the assay, 0.5 mL of collagen solution was mixed with 4.5 mL of chloramine-T reagent in a test tube and incubated at room temperature for 25 min. Subsequently, freshly prepared Ehrlich’s reagent (2 g of p-dimethylaminobenzaldehyde in perchloric acid and n-propanol (3:13)) was added, and the mixture was incubated in a water bath at 65 °C for 20 min. Finally, the absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 550 nm and compared to a standard curve of hydroxyproline to quantify the hydroxyproline content in the extracted collagen sample.

Elemental Analysis of Collagen

Elemental composition of collagen was determined using the elemental analyzer (VarioMicro V1.6.1. GmbH, Germany) with a tin boat sample pan. Combustion and reduction temperatures were set at 1150 and 850 °C, respectively. Helium and oxygen were used as carrier gases at flow rates of 200 mL/min and 14 mL/min, respectively. Each sample was analyzed for over approximately 800 s using a thermal conductivity detector.

Heavy Metal Analysis of Collagen

Heavy metal content (Cr, Cd, Pb) in collagen was measured using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer, PinAAcle 900H). 1 g of collagen powder was incinerated at 600 °C in a muffle furnace. The resulting ash was digested with a mixture of nitric acid (5 mL), sulfuric acid (3.5 mL), and perchloric acid (11.5 mL) on a hot plate for 2 h in a fume hood. After cooling, the mixture was diluted to 100 mL with distilled water, filtered, and analyzed using flame AAS. Standard solutions of Cr, Cd, and Pb were used to generate the calibration curve.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of Zingibain Enzyme

The primary objective of this study was to harness zingibain enzyme in its crude form, as this approach aligns with the broader goal of environmental protection, particularly when integrated with green solvent-based collagen extraction processes. Although purified enzyme preparations offer greater stability and enhanced potency, the purification of zingibain demands extensive chemical use and temperature-sensitive drying procedures, factors that collectively escalate the environmental burden and contradict the sustainability principles that underpin this work. Therefore, this study prioritizes the use of the crude enzyme form to achieve optimal outcomes while ensuring that waste-derived renewable resources serve as primary raw materials.

When employing the enzyme in its crude form, it becomes essential to first verify its proteolytic functionalityspecifically, its capacity to cleave peptide bonds. To this end, the presence and activity of zingibain enzyme were confirmed by assessing the hydrolysis zones it produced on both skimmed milk agar and gelatin-agar media, providing clear evidence of its protein-degrading capability. Among different dispersions, 1% dispersion of the enzyme produced a barely visible zone in skimmed milk media (Figure a), indicating insufficient capability to break down the peptide bonds present in the skimmed milk.

Clear zones, that indicate the proteolytic activity of the zingibain enzyme, which is capable of breaking peptide bonds of protein, appeared in zingibain identification by zone analysis for (a) 1% dispersion, (b) 3% dispersion, (c) 5% dispersion. Qualitative detection of zingibain by the agar plate method (d) before applying Coomassie brilliant blue dye and (e) after applying the Coomassie brilliant blue dye and destaining solution.

In contrast, the 3% dispersion showed higher enzymatic activity and more active sites than the 1% dispersion sample, resulting in the breakdown of more peptide bonds and the formation of a larger (19 mm diameter) and clearer zone (Figure b). However, the 5% dispersion produced a slightly smaller zone (18 mm) than the 3% dispersion sample. This reduction is likely due to the collision of the particles, difficulty in achieving a homogenized solution at higher ginger peel powder concentration, and enzyme–enzyme interactions, which collectively reduced its ability to hydrolyze peptide bonds, producing a smaller and more translucent zone (Figure c).

Since 3% zingibain enzyme exhibited the largest zone in skimmed milk media, it was further utilized in agar-gelatin plate analysis to qualitatively confirm enzyme’s significant capability in breaking down peptide bonds. The result of the analysis revealed that the enzyme formed a transparent zone (16 mm diameter) around the well (Figure e). This zone resulted from the hydrolysis of the peptide bonds present in the gelatin. Prior to staining, the zone was barely visible (Figure d). Upon application of Coomassie Brilliant Blue dye, the dye bonded with the intact peptide bonds in the gelatin but failed to bind in the area where the enzyme had broken these bonds. Consequently, during destaining, the dye was removed from this region, revealing a clear, transparent zone.

Proteolytic activity is a specific indicator of measuring the strength of the enzyme. Purified enzymes have higher proteolytic activity, and only a small amount of enzyme is required to hydrolyze the protein. Crude enzymes, on the other hand, have lower proteolytic activity, and a bulk amount of the liquid is required for better hydrolysis. Although plant-based enzymes have lower proteolytic activity in their crude form, they have higher specificity for structural proteins like collagen, which ensures precise biological function without unwanted side reactions during hydrolysis.

The protease activities of 1%, 3%, and 5% zingibain enzyme dispersions were 0.43 ± 0.05 U/mL, 1.57 ± 0.03 U/mL, and 1.01 ± 0.03 U/mL, respectively (Table S1, Appendix B). Enzyme typically exhibits the highest activity at an optimal concentration, where the maximum number of active sites is available for substrate binding and catalysis. In this study, the 3% enzyme dispersion showed the highest activity (1.57 ± 0.03 U/mL). Interestingly, although during the preparation of 5% enzyme dispersion, more ginger peel powder was utilized, its activity was lower compared to 3% dispersion (1.01 ± 0.03 U/mL). This phenomenon might have occurred due to improper homogenization of the substrate during ultrasonication in the enzyme extraction stage, substrate–substrate collision, and enzyme–enzyme repulsion during subsequent processing. The 3% enzyme dispersion had a protein content of 0.54 ± 0.02 mg/mL and a specific activity of 2.91 ± 0.06 U/mL. A similar type of plant-based proteolytic enzyme, papain, has been reported to exhibit a protease activity of 0.57 ± 0.04 U/mL, protein content of 0.52 ± 0.01 mg/mL, and specific activity of 1.08 ± 0.008 U/mL. The papain enzyme could extract approximately 91% collagen from pretreated raw trimmings. Since 3% dispersion of zingibain enzyme has higher activity than papain, it can be an effective alternative to extract collagen from leather waste.

Optimization of the Extraction Process Using Box-Behnken Design (BBD)

The Box-Behnken design (BBD) is an efficient Response surface methodology that evaluates the influence of the independent variables and optimizes the process parameters accurately. BBD was employed to optimize the collagen extraction process because of its efficiency in estimating second-order polynomial models. This design is particularly suited for enzyme-mediated extraction processes, where extraction parameters such as enzyme concentration, pH, temperature, and time interact in a complex, nonlinear manner. The resulting quadratic regression model identified both individual and interactive effects of the process variables on the collagen yield, and the optimal extraction conditions were subsequently determined through response surface analysis.

Model adequacy was confirmed through ANOVA, R 2, and lack-of-fit assessments, collectively validating the reliability and predictive capability of the optimized model. Compared to other experimental designs such as the central composite design, three-level full factorial design, and Doehlert design, BBD is more economical and efficient. It requires fewer experimental runs, avoids extreme factor-level combinations, accommodates extreme treatment conditions in subsequent iterations, is less time-consuming, rotatable, and well-suited for exploring experimental boundaries. Graphical analysis was subsequently conducted to evaluate the accuracy of the model equation and compare the process output.

Analysis of Variance

The ANOVA results (Table S2, Appendix B) confirmed the adequacy of the regression model for yield (%) prediction, as indicated by a high coefficient of determination (R 2) and adjusted R 2. The significance of the quadratic polynomial coefficients was assessed using F- and P-values. A high F-value and low P-value indicated that the corresponding terms had a significant effect on the response. The variables influencing the yield (%) in descending order of significance were: the quadratic effect of ginger peel powder (w/v %), the quadratic effect of temperature, the linear effect of ginger peel powder (w/v %), the interaction between temperature and DP (w/v %), the linear effect of DP (w/v %), the quadratic effect of time, the quadratic effect of DP (w/v %), the interaction between ginger peel powder (w/v %) and temperature, the interaction between ginger peel powder (w/v %) and time, the interaction between time and temperature, and the linear effect of time (all at p ≤ 0.001). Additionally, the linear effect of temperature and the interaction between time and DP (w/v %) were also significant (p ≤ 0.05). The interaction between ginger peel powder (w/v %) and DP (w/v %) showed no influence on yield (%) of the hydrolysis process and was therefore excluded from the final model, which retained only significant terms.

The model demonstrated a good fit, as indicated by the nonsignificant lack-of-fit-test (P > 0.05) for yield (%). The high coefficient of determination (R 2 = 0.9983) confirmed that the quadratic model effectively captured the variation in the experimental data. Additionally, the adjusted R 2 value (0.9970) showed strong agreement between the predicted and observed values for the yield (%), validating the model’s reliability. One Factor Plots of Yield

One-factor plots display the mean response for each individual factor, offering a visual representation of how each variable influences the yield. As shown in (Figure b–e), none of the plots exhibit a flat horizontal trend, indicating that all the examined factors significantly affect the response. Notably, the temperature and ginger peel powder concentration (w/v %) show a steeper slope compared to the other two variables, suggesting a greater impact on the yield (%). The general trend observed across these plots is an increase in yield (%) with rising levels of each component, up to a certain point beyond which the yield begins to decline. A similar trend was observed in the perturbation plot (Figure S3, Appendix A), which consolidates the individual effects of all the variables into a single graph, reaffirming the significance of each factor.

Contour Plot of Yield

Contour plots provide a two-dimensional surface visualization, where the z-axis (yield %) is represented through lines of constant value (contours). These plots effectively illustrate the relationship between the yield and the operating conditions. The maximum yield (%) is shown by the red areas of contour plots. The shape and curvature of the contour lines indicate a stronger relationship among ginger peel powder concentration (w/v %), time, temperature, and DP concentrations (w/v %). As seen in (Figure a–e), the yield increases with a rise in these variables until it reaches an optimal level, after which it starts to decrease. Compared to conventional interaction plots, contour plots offer a clearer and more intuitive visualization of the interaction effects between variable pairs (Figure S4, Appendix A).

Surface Plot (3D) of Yield

A 3D surface plot is used to visualize the relationship among two independent variables expressed (plotted on the x- and y-axes) and a continuous response variable (represented on the z-axis). The peaks and valleys of the surface plot, which represent maxima and minima of the response, are created by the combinations of these variables on the x- and y-axes. In this extraction process, the yield (%) increased with both the concentration of ginger peel powder (w/v %) and extraction time. However, beyond a certain point, the further increase in these variables led to a decline in the yield (%) (Figure a). This outcome can be attributed to the fact that a 3% enzyme dispersion exhibited higher protease activity compared with both 1% and 5% dispersions in this study. The combined effects of temperature and ginger peel powder concentrations are illustrated in Figure b.

In both cases, yield (%) initially increased but declined after exceeding optimal conditions. At higher concentrations of DP, molecular collisions and subsequent settling in the reactor likely contributed to the observed decrease in yield.

Generally, enzymatic hydrolysis is governed by substrate accessibility, active-site interactions, and environmental factors such as pH and temperature. In line with this, the action of zingibain enzyme in the present study can be interpreted as a controlled and selective peptide-bond cleavage process influenced by multiple factors, thereby strengthening the mechanistic understanding of collagen hydrolysis. The contour and 3D surface plots effectively demonstrate the interaction effects of the independent variables on yield (%), offering a more detailed representation compared to the cube plot, which visualizes the combined effect of three variables on yield using a three-dimensional cube (Figure S5, Appendix A).

Optimized Factors

The extraction process provided maximum yield (%) when the enzyme was prepared with 3.41% (w/v) ginger peel powder, and 5.36% (w/v) DP was hydrolyzed in this enzyme dispersion at 57.83 °C for 5.36 h (Table ). Under these optimized conditions, the predicted highest yield (%) was 92.98%, whereas the experimentally obtained yield was (92 ± 0.1) %, calculated based on the weight of DP. The percentage error between the predicted and actual yield was 1.07%. This minimal deviation indicates good reliability and accuracy of the model. Control experiments conducted without the zingibain enzyme under identical conditions (57.83 °C, 5.36 h, and 5.36% DP) resulted in a yield of (32 ± 0.45) %. This comparatively low yield confirms that enzymatic hydrolysis is significantly more efficient than thermal hydrolysis in terms of collagen recovery. Therefore, the use of enzymes as an extraction medium is considerably more effective for recovering collagen from waste materials.

Analysis of the Hydrolysate Solution

The degree of hydrolysis (DH) is calculated by comparing the amount and size of peptide bonds remaining after the completion of the hydrolysis process to the ones initially present in the hydrolysate solution. This value reflects the average peptide size remaining in the hydrolyzed protein. In this study, zingibain enzyme was used to hydrolyze the DP over varying durations: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 h. The supernatant from each hydrolysate was collected to evaluate the DH, which increased from (49.43 ± 0.95) % to (73.55 ± 1.09) % (Figure a). The increase in DH with time indicates that hydrolyzing the DP resulted in the extensive breakdown of the original protein fragment into smaller peptides.

In this study, the extent of hydrolysis achieved was (98.68 ± 0.19) %, which indicates that majority of the raw material fed into the extraction process was efficiently utilized and broken down throughout the extraction process. In a similar investigation, collagen extracted using papain enzyme demonstrated degree of hydrolysis of 65.24% and extent of hydrolysis of (99.45 ± 0.31) %, respectively. Similar to papain enzyme, zingibain enzyme is very efficient in cleaving peptide bonds and utilizing the hydrolysate powder. Since zingibain is a cysteine protease enzyme, it follows the Cys-His-Asn catalytic triad, where cysteine performs the nucleophilic attack on the peptide bond, histidine acts as a general base, and oxyanion hole contributed by glutamine-19 stabilizes the transition state. Due to this catalytic triad, the DP is efficiently hydrolyzed within the specific time, and collagen is released. Then, the α and β chains of collagen break down due to the continued hydrolysis, and as a result, smaller protein fragments are produced, which is confirmed by the increasing DH content with time.

Characterization of Collagen

Solubility of Collagen

The impact of pH on the solubility of the collagen extracted from DP is depicted in Figure b. Collagen exhibited high solubility in the acidic range (pH 1–4), exhibiting the highest solubility at pH 2 (96.25%). At this value, the collagen molecules carry the highest residual net charge, leading to strong electrostatic repulsion between chains and thereby enhancing solubility. Between pH 4 to 7, solubility decreased significantly from 87.08% to 22.5% due to the progressive reduction in net charge in the collagen molecules. The lowest solubility was observed at pH 7. However, as the pH increased from 7 to 10, solubility gradually showed an upward trend, likely due to an increase in the net negative charge on the molecules, which enhanced repulsive forces between collagen chains.

The effects of NaCl concentrations on collagen solubility are depicted in Figure c. Collagen was highly soluble up to a NaCl concentration of 3% (w/v), with maximum solubility observed at 2% (w/v) concentration, reaching 86.67%. At 4% (w/v) concentration, solubility declined to 78%, followed by a continued decrease at 5% and 6% (w/v) concentrations. At higher salt levels, the presence of salt ions induced a ‘salting out’ effect, enhancing hydrophobic interactions between protein chains. This promoted aggregation and precipitation of the collagen, ultimately reducing its solubility.

Spectral Characterization of Collagen

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy is commonly employed to confirm the presence of the triple helical structure in pure collagen as it provides insights into the protein’s secondary structure. The FTIR spectra of the extracted collagen are shown in Figure a. To assess the triple helical structure of collagen, spectral peaks of amide bands are analyzed. The absorption characteristic peak of the amide A band is typically observed in the range of 3250–3340 cm–1, which is associated with N–H stretching vibration. In the collagen extracted from trimming waste, the amide A band appeared at approximately 3283 cm–1, indicating active involvement of N–H groups in hydrogen bonding. Additionally, the amide B band, corresponding to asymmetrical stretching of CH2, was detected at a wavenumber of 2945 cm–1. The amide I band is the most critical indicator for analyzing the secondary structure of proteins. It typically appears in the 1600–1700 cm–1 range and is primarily associated with the CO stretching vibration of the peptide backbone. Amide I band was found at 1626 cm–1 region in collagen obtained through enzymatic hydrolysis. The amide II band, found at 1523 cm–1, arises from a combination of C–N stretching and N–H bending vibrations and typically appears within the 1500–1600 cm–1 range. The amide III band, located between 1220–1320 cm–1, is attributed to N–H deformation and C–N stretching; in this case, it was recorded at 1236 cm–1. The maximum absorbance of the amide III (at 1235 cm–1) was compared with that of the peak at 1450 cm–1 to assess potential denaturation during extraction. The resulting (A1235/A1450) ratio of 1.09 confirms that the collagen retained its native triple-helical structure following enzymatic hydrolysis.

Thermogravimetric Analysis of Collagen

The thermogravimetric analysis of collagen revealed a characteristic multistage degradation profile that reflects its complex hierarchical structure (Figure b). Initially, moisture evaporates as the sample is heated. At 135 °C, the first major weight loss occurs, where collagen loses approximately 9.05% of its mass. The second significant degradation stage is observed at 333 °C (T max), with a further mass loss of about 42.86%. This stage is associated with the denaturation of the core polypeptide chains of collagen due to elevated temperature, resulting in the collapse of collagen’s structural integrity caused by intermolecular disruption. Complete degradation is observed at around 620 °C, where the sample loses its remaining mass and demonstrates the purity of the isolated collagen. These findings align with previous research indicating that collagen from raw trimmings begins degrading near 132 °C and continues until about 700 °C and validates both the integrity of the developed extraction process and the authenticity of the material. Overall, the thermal stability profile observed in this study confirms that the collagen possesses physicochemical characteristics consistent with high-quality, structurally intact collagen, making it a promising candidate for applications in different fields where thermal behavior and structural reliability are critical performance parameters.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry of Collagen

The DSC thermogram of collagen revealed two distinct endothermic transitions. The first peak, observed at 61.53 °C, corresponds to the denaturation temperature (T d), representing the breakage of hydrogen bonds and release of weakly bound water. The result is very close to a previous study which found the denaturation temperature of 67.62 °C for a collagen sample obtained from bovine hide. The second endothermic peak was detected at 220.18 °C which indicated the melting temperature (T td) of collagen (Figure c). This transition reflects the transformation of collagen from its ordered triple-helix structure to a disordered, randomly coiled conformation.

XRD Analysis of Collagen

XRD is an effective technique for assessing atomic or molecular regularity in the structure of the collagen. Typically, collagen exhibits a characteristic broad peak within the 2θ range of 15–25°. In the present study, the extracted collagen displays a broad peak centered at 21.65° (Figure d), with a full width at half-maximum (fwhm) of 0.40°. Additionally, a sharp peak observed around 9° corresponds to the diameter of collagen’s secondary structure, while the broader peak near 20° reflects the intermolecular spacing between amino acid chains within the collagen helix. Proteins typically show a broad peak at about 20°, indicating that they are amorphous. A similar study found the broad peak at 21° for collagen extracted from tannery raw trimmings using papain enzyme. The slight variations in the 2θ value of the current study can be attributed to differences in enzyme used and hydrolysis conditions. The crystallite size of the extracted collagen was also determined from XRD data using the Scherrer eq (eq S1, Appendix C), yielding a value of 21.12 nm. This result falls within the typical range of 20–25 nm reported for mineralized collagen in previous studies, indicating good agreement with established findings.

Quantification of Hydroxyproline

Hydroxyproline is one of the three major amino acids that are present in collagen. The collagen in raw hide or skin generally contains around 11.3% to 15% of hydroxyproline. The extracted collagen contains (11.49 ± 0.2)% hydroxyproline which was measured by conducting the hydroxyproline analysis technique. Some hydroxyproline content might be degraded during the extraction of collagen using zingibain enzyme due to the application of elevated temperatures.

Elemental Analysis of Collagen

Nitrogen is present in considerable proportions in amino acids, which are the main chemical building blocks of proteins. The extracted collagen contains 15.01% nitrogen, whereas the DP contains 15.62% nitrogen. The carbon, hydrogen, and sulfur contents in the collagen are 43.72%, 7.37%, and 0.27%, respectively, while the DP contains 42.44% carbon, 7.83% hydrogen, and 0.30% sulfur (Table S3, Appendix B). The DP and collagen have carbon-to-nitrogen (C/N) ratios of 2.72 and 2.91, respectively. Organic matter such as collagen generally possesses a C/N ratio of 1–15. The C/N ratio is an indicator of how quickly a matter would release nitrogen and how quickly biodegradation would take place. The biodegradation of the collagen will take place more quickly than that of the DP since the collagen has a higher C/N ratio. The carbon-to-hydrogen (C/H) ratios are 5.93 for collagen and 5.42 for the DP, indicating slight differences in the elemental composition that may influence the thermal and biochemical properties.

Heavy Metal Analysis of Collagen

The presence of heavy metals in the collagen was analyzed using AAS. The concentrations of cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), and chromium (Cr) present in the extracted collagen were 0.511 ± 0.002 ppm, 0.694 ± 0.028 ppm, and 0.115 ± 0.009 ppm, respectively. These values fall within the permissible limits for food-grade materials: 0.05–2 ppm for Cd, 0.01–3 ppm for Pb, and 0.5–1.3 ppm for Cr, respectively (Table. S4, Appendix B). Notable, the zingibain enzyme used for collagen extraction contained no detectable levels of these heavy metals. In contrast, the DPthe source material for the collagencontained higher levels of these metals: 0.761 ± 0.004 ppm of Cd, 0.817 ± 0.011 ppm Pb, and 0.121 ± 0.001 ppm of Cr. The trace amounts of heavy metals found in the extracted collagen likely originated from this raw material. The presence of these metals in the DP may be attributed to environmental exposure, the animal’s diet, or chemical treatments applied during the pretreatment of tannery raw trimmings.

Conclusions

This study successfully demonstrated the effectiveness and broader impact of zingibain enzyme-assisted hydrolysis method for the extraction of collagen from unprocessed trimmings discarded by tanneries. The hydrolysis process was optimized using the BBD, tool that identified the optimal conditions to maximize collagen yield. The results confirmed that using zingibain, a ginger peel-derived enzyme, significantly improves the recovery of value-added products from waste in reduced costapproximately 6.96 USD per gram of collagen. Additionally, employing a green solvent, such as an enzyme, in the hydrolysis process lowers the overall carbon footprint and solves the problem of increased carbon emissions associated with traditional disposal methods such as landfilling. Future studies can focus on implementing sensors for the real-time tracking of the emissions generated from the extraction process. This way, we will be able to develop a quantifiable system for monitoring the actual environmental impact of the extraction process. Moreover, the application of plant-based enzymes promotes a sustainable, chemical-free alternative for collagen extraction for application in the food industry, supporting the use of renewable resources and contributing to environmentally responsible manufacturing practices. This green extraction method aligns with several UN sustainable development goals (SDGs), including responsible production and consumption, and climate action. Overall, enzymatic hydrolysis offers an efficient, eco-friendly solution for leather waste management, contributing to a sustainable and safer future for the next generations.
