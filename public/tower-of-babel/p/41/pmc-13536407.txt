Nasal polyp with ossification in the right maxillary sinus: Differential diagnosis of radiopaque lesions in maxillary sinus region
Authors: Ssu-Li Li, Shan-Ju Chou, Yang-Che Wu, Chun-Yu Ho
Published: 2026

Nasal polyps are soft, painless, noncancerous, teardropshaped growths that form on the lining of the nasal passages or sinuses as a result of chronic sinonasal inflammation. They are commonly associated with allergies, asthma, recurrent infections, and chronic sinusitis, and may cause symptoms such as nasal congestion, stuffiness, and a reduced sense of smell. Nasal polyp ossification is a manifestation of osseous metaplasia, which is defined as the formation of normal bone tissue within soft tissue. Although nasal polyps are common lesions, the occurrence of osseous metaplasia within them is rare, with only a few cases reported in the English-language literature. The exact pathophysiology remains unclear; however, current evidence suggests that overexpression of bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) and transforming grhowowth factor β-1 (TGF-β1) in nasal polyp mucosa may induce osteogenic differentiation of stromal cells, leading to ectopic bone formation.1,2

A 40-year-old male was referred to the oral and maxillofacial surgery department for lesion evaluation due to a chance discovery of a radiopaque mass over right maxillary area. The previous panoramic radiograph showed a radiopaque lesion over the periapical area of the right maxillary first molar (Fig. 1 A), and the compound odontoma was tentatively suspected. The cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) was then taken and revealed that the lesion was located in the right maxillary sinus with sinusitis, rather than within the maxilla bone. The mass was estimated to be 1.4 × 1.2 × 1.1 cm in size (Fig. 1 B). Considering the patient’s clinical signs of sinusitis and location of the lesion, we excluded the possibility of lesions from dental origin. Also, bone tumors were excluded according to the fact that they seldom occur in head and neck regions, especially paranasal sinus, though cases of paranasal sinus osteoma and ossifying fibromas were reviewed.3,4 Therefore, the secondary changes (e.g. osseous metaplasia) of inflammatory polyps was suspected.

Consultation of the otolaryngologists was made for the feasibility of treatment by functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS). However, the Caldwell-Luc operation was suggested. The total excision with the Caldwell-Luc approach was performed and the specimen was sent to pathologists for confirmation (Fig. 1C1-6). The diagnosis of nasal polyp with ossification, accompanied by acute and chronic inflammation of the adjacent respiratory mucosa was made (Fig. 1D1-5). Post-surgery follow-up showed satisfactory treatment outcome, and the wound healed well (Fig. 1E1-3). The signs and symptoms of sinusitis have subsided.

Similar cases had been reported.2,5 These two cases both underwent endoscopic sinus surgery and were confirmed as polyps with osseous metaplasia. In our case, the endoscopic surgery was not feasible due to the large size of the ossified portion of the lesion. Therefore, the patient underwent the Caldwell-Luc operation, and the outcome was desirable. When radiopaque lesions were identified over the maxillary region by panoramic radiography, taking CBCT is reliable to obtain accurate location of the lesion. When radiopaque lesions were noted inside the maxillary sinus, we need to take nasal polyp with ossification into consideration during the differential diagnosis.
