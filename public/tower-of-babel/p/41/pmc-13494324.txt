Osteochondral Lesions of the Talus Treated With Particulated Juvenile Cartilage Allograft in the Active Duty Military Population
Authors: Colin Mizuo, Liliya Parkman, Tarina Ayazi, Haoning Hu, Ashley Adams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Osteochondral defects (OCDs) of the talus frequently affect young, active individuals and carry a risk of chronic pain and joint degeneration. Particulated juvenile cartilage allograft (PJCA), harnesses the proliferative capacity of juvenile chondrocytes to restore hyaline cartilage without donor-site morbidity. This study evaluates the structural, clinical, and functional outcomes of PJCA implantation in active-duty military patients.Methods:We retrospectively reviewed 10 service members (10 male, mean age 28.4 years) who underwent arthroscopic PJCA implantation for symptomatic talar OCDs. Outcomes included defect dimensions and volume on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), Magnetic Resonance Observation of Cartilage Repair Tissue (MOCART) scores, visual analog scale (VAS) for pain, and Foot and Ankle Ability Measure (FAAM) subscales. Statistical analyses employed Wilcoxon signed-rank and Friedman tests (SPSS 28.0), with significance set at P <.05.Results:Median follow-up was 15.0 months (range 13.5-18.2). Lesion volume decreased from 0.77 to 0.02 cm³ (P = .027). Median MOCART scores improved from 25 to 75 (P = .027). Pain scores fell from 6.0 to 1.0 (P < .001). FAAM daily living scores increased from 50% to 87.5% (P < .001), and sports scores from 15% to 85% (P < .001). Nine of 10 patients returned to full duty (8/9 after a single limited-duty period, 1 required 2 limited-duty periods), and 1 required medical separation. No complications were observed.Conclusion:In this high-demand cohort, PJCA implantation was associated with radiographic evidence of defect fill, pain relief, functional restoration, and return to activity without donor-site morbidity. Despite the study’s retrospective design and moderate follow-up, these findings support PJCA as a promising option for talar OCD repair. Prospective, randomized trials with extended follow-up are warranted to confirm durability and efficacy against established techniques.Level of Evidence:Level IV, retrospective case series.

Introduction

Osteochondral defects (OCDs) of the talus are injuries involving both the articular cartilage and the underlying subchondral bone, typically resulting from trauma such as ankle sprains or fractures.1-3 These lesions are frequently observed in young, physically active individuals, especially those involved in high-impact or repetitive ankle-loading activities, and can lead to persistent pain and progressive cartilage degeneration if not adequately treated.1-4 The prevalence of OCDs is particularly significant among military personnel because of their physically strenuous duties and the high incidence of ankle injuries in this demographic. 3 Additionally, OCDs of the talus pose a complex clinical challenge because of the unique vascular anatomy and biomechanical environment of the ankle joint.2,4-11

The foundational work by Berndt and Harty introduced a classification system for talar OCDs, delineating 4 stages of transchondral fractures based on radiographic features. 12 With advancements in diagnostic modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), and arthroscopy, the detection and staging of these lesions have become more precise, allowing for improved clinical decision making for initial or revisional OCD repair.11,13-18 Lesions typically arise in the lateral and medial aspects of the talar dome, areas characterized by limited blood supply. The inherently poor vascularity of the talus further impedes spontaneous healing.2,4 Furthermore, the ankle’s unique biomechanical environment, which subjects the talus to forces as high as 5 times the body weight during normal gait, further complicates attempts for durable cartilage repair. 5

Traditional surgical options include bone marrow stimulation (BMS) methods such as microfracture, antegrade or retrograde drilling, and osteochondral autograft transplantation.19-23 BMS, although commonly used for small lesions, 20 results in fibrocartilage formation, which is mechanically inferior to hyaline cartilage and susceptible to degradation over time. Lesions larger than 15 mm have been associated with higher failure rates, nearing 50%. 20 Additionally, Shimozono and colleagues 24 demonstrated that BMS surgery led to progressive subchondral cystic development. Osteochondral autografts, which offer the advantage of hyaline cartilage restoration, are constrained by limited graft availability, donor site morbidity, and technical difficulties, particularly in the confined space of the ankle joint.21-23 Autologous chondrocyte implantation, another restorative strategy, has shown variable outcomes in talar lesions and necessitates multiple stages and open surgical exposure, adding to patient morbidity and prolonged recovery.25-29

In response to the limitations of traditional techniques, interest has grown in biologic repair methods aimed at regenerating hyaline cartilage using minimally invasive procedures. One such technique involves particulated juvenile cartilage allograft (PJCA) implantation, which uses minced articular cartilage from donors younger than 12 years of age. 15 These grafts retain viable juvenile chondrocytes embedded within their native extracellular matrix, facilitating cellular proliferation, robust matrix synthesis, and enhanced integration within the host tissue.30-32 Compared with adult chondrocytes, juvenile cells demonstrate superior anabolic activity, leading to improved histologic outcomes in both animal and human studies.31,32 Preclinical work, using equine and ovine models, has validated the biologic potential of minced cartilage repair and laid the groundwork for clinical adaptation in human joints.13,14,30 Clinical studies in the knee further supported its safety, feasibility, and positive integration with host cartilage.17-19

Preliminary data from PJCA applications in the ankle have shown promising clinical and radiographic outcomes; however, research within the active-duty military population is still limited.6,32-38 The aim of this study is to contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting PJCA as an effective treatment option for talar OCDs, and to expand its applicability to active-duty service members (ADSMs).

Materials and Methods

Patient Selection

Our study is a retrospective case series consisting of ADSMs 18 years or older who underwent arthroscopic or open repair of an OCD with application of PJCA. All patients failed at least 3 months of conservative therapy, such as immobilization in a short leg cast or controlled ankle mobilization (CAM) boot, PRICE (protection, rest, ice, compression, elevation) therapy, steroid or platelet-rich plasma injection, ankle brace, and/or physical or sports rehabilitation. Exclusion criteria included neuromuscular disorders, significant deformity of the ankle joint, the presence of moderate to severe ankle arthritis on preoperative imaging, defined as grade 3 or greater according to the Kellgren-Lawrence scale, 39 and incomplete functional outcome scores.

The local institutional review board (IRB) determined this study to be IRB exempt. All procedures were performed by a single surgeon between September 2020 and October 2021. No funding was received for this study.

Radiographic and Clinical Evaluations

Clinical evaluation involved circumferential palpation to the ankle and stressed range of motion of the ankle joint. In the presence of reported ankle instability, the anterolateral drawer (ALD) test and talar tilt (TT) tests were performed under live fluoroscopy. The ALD test was performed by internally rotating the foot and exerting an anterior force on the foot relative to the tibia with the foot in 15° to 20° of plantarflexion.

Preoperative radiographic assessments included anteroposterior, mortise, and lateral weight-bearing views of the ankle to rule out malalignment and significant arthritis. Preoperative MRIs were performed to assess the involvement of the OCD, subchondral bone, and ancillary injuries. Patients were scheduled for postoperative MRIs at 6 and 12 months to evaluate the progression of cartilage integration and maturation and resolution of subchondral marrow edema and cysts if applicable. Preoperative and postoperative MRI slices were carefully matched to ensure accurate interval comparison and to confirm that equivalent imaging cuts were evaluated.

Primary Outcome Measures

Pain and functionality were measured preoperatively, 6 months postoperatively, and at 12 months postoperatively using the visual analog score (VAS) and the Foot and Ankle Ability Measure (FAAM) score. The FAAM is a 29-item questionnaire with subscales of daily living (21 items) and sports (8 items), where higher scores represent higher levels of functionality. 40

Return to work status of ADSMs was measured by limited duty (LIMDU) periods or medical board status (MEDBOARD). LIMDU is a period during which an ADSM who cannot complete a fitness test, defined by the completion of a timed 1.5-mile run, push-ups, sit-ups, and other military specific tasks, because of health or injury reasons is given 6 months to seek medical treatment, undergo surgery, and to partake in a physical rehabilitation program as needed. If an ADSM has exhausted 2 LIMDU periods, or has an especially poor prognosis, they are assigned to a MEDBOARD, where a committee evaluates whether they will be medically discharged.

ADSMs must demonstrate the ability to meet their branch-specific physical fitness standards before returning to duty. These standards typically include minimum requirements for exercises such as push-ups and/or pull-ups, forearm planks, and timed runs. The minimum acceptable performance is determined by both biological sex and age category, with older service members held to less stringent requirements compared to their younger counterparts.41,42

Surgical Technique

All procedures were performed with the patient under general or monitored anesthesia care with inflation of a thigh tourniquet. Positioning of the patient varied between supine and lateral position with the assistance of an inflatable bean bag.

Ankle arthroscopy was performed initially in all cases with standard medial and lateral ankle portals. After the ankle was debrided of all synovitis and hypertrophic impingement, attention was addressed to the location of the OCD.

The loosely adhered cartilage was removed with an arthroscopic shaver and different curette instruments (Figure 1). An ankle distractor was used only when necessary. During curettage of the defect, special attention was directed toward establishing vertical walls along the periphery to allow for successful integration of the cartilage graft. Creating a round shape and avoiding irregular borders allows for easy delivery and filling of the graft into the defect. A medial malleolar osteotomy was performed for any OCDs that were to posterior to access with arthroscopy.

The arthroscopic view shows of a full-thickness cartilage lesion.

Once the loosely adhered cartilage was adequately removed, the integrity of the subchondral bone was assessed with a probe instrument (Figure 2). If the subchondral bone was intact, a subchondral abrasion was performed using an arthroscopic burr. In the presence of subchondral incompetence due to cystic development, cancellous bone graft was taken from the calcaneal body using a graft harvester through a small lateral incision. The cyst was evacuated with arthroscopic debridement and suction.

The arthroscopic view shows the probe diving into the subchondral bone, which correlates well with the presence of a subchondral cyst observed on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Once the defect was adequately prepped, the fluid from the ankle joint was suctioned out. Multiple pledgets soaked in epinephrine were placed through the medial and lateral ankle portal incisions to neutralize any small bleeders while the PJCA (DeNovo NT Natural Tissue Graft, Zimmer Biomet) was manually loaded into the distal end of a 2.7-mm cannula on the back table.

The pledgets were removed and the 2.7-mm scope was reinserted into the appropriate ankle portal to confirm a dry surgical field. When necessary, the previously harvested cancellous bone was advanced into the subchondral defect through a 2.7-mm cannula (Figure 3). The PJCA was then gently transplanted through the arthroscopic cannula and positioned lightly with a freer elevator to cover the defect uniformly, making an effort to contain the graft only within the defect (Figure 4). A fibrin glue sealant was applied over the cartilage graft for additional fixation (Figure 5). Final arthroscopic visualizing was performed to confirm the cartilage graft did not delaminate before the portal incisions were reapproximated.

Cancellous bone graft is advanced into the defect site using a 2.7-mm cannula.

The particulated juvenile cartilage allograft is advanced into the defect directly over the subchondral bone or cancellous bone graft through a separate 2.7-mm cannula.

A fibrin sealant is carefully applied over the particulated juvenile cartilage allograft to secure into place.

Postoperative Care

All patients were placed in either a posterior or AO splint and instructed to remain nonweightbearing with the assistance of crutches. Postoperative week 1, the splint was removed. Patients were issued a CAM boot but continued to remain nonweightbearing. Postoperative week 4, patients were allowed to start partial weightbearing with gradual transition to weightbearing as tolerated. An accelerated rehabilitation protocol was initiated at postoperative week 4, initially emphasizing nonweightbearing range of motion exercises, and subsequently progressing to strengthening and proprioceptive training through postoperative week 12. At week 12, patients were allowed to transition to a lace-up ankle brace in normal shoes if tolerated, and low impact activity (stationary bike or elliptical machine) was allowed. At month 6, light jogging and agility drills were allowed with gradual increase in duration and intensity over the next several weeks.

Statistical Methods

Data were analyzed using JASP (version 0.97.1; JASP Team). Numerical variables were reported in medians and first and third quartiles (Q1 and Q3), whereas categorical variables were reported in counts and frequencies. The differences in MRI measurements from preprocedure to last follow-up were analyzed with the Wilcoxon signed rank test, and differences in patient-reported outcomes over time were analyzed with Friedman test. P values less than .05 were considered significant. All analyses were exploratory and the reported P values should be interpreted accordingly. No power analysis was performed because of the known small sample of talar OCD patients.

Results

All patients were male, with a median age of 30 years (Table 1). OCDs were predominately on the left ankle (8/10). The defects were mainly located on the lateral, central talar shoulder (6/10), followed by the medial, central talar shoulder (3/10), and 1 was on both the medial-central and medial-posterior talus. One patient had a prior left arthroscopic os trigonum excision. No patient had prior BMS. All patients had ancillary ankle arthroscopy and most also had cancellous bone graft harvests (7/10). The median length of time since the most recent preoperative MRI was 199 days (Table 1).

Discussion

This preliminary study suggests that PJCA may be a safe and effective treatment for talar OCDs in ADSMs. Patients demonstrated significant improvements in lesion morphology on MRI, with a median reduction in volume from 0.77 to 0.02 cm³, accompanied by meaningful improvements in validated clinical scores and return-to-duty outcomes. These findings support the growing body of evidence that PJCA may be a viable biologic alternative to traditional treatment approaches for talar OCDs.

Our results are consistent with previous studies evaluating PJCA implantation. In a retrospective case series, Dekker et al 34 reported a 60% clinical success rate in patients treated with PJCA. Larger preoperative MRI lesion area, intraoperative defect size, and male sex were identified as predictors of failure. The study did not include follow-up MRI or MOCART score data, underscoring the need for future imaging-based outcome analyses.

Pain relief and functional recovery in our cohort also reflect outcomes reported in larger multicenter studies. Coetzee et al 36 evaluated 23 ankles treated with PJCA and reported that 78% achieved good to excellent outcomes with favorable results in moderate-sized lesions (10-15 mm). These findings confirm the utility of PJCA in patients who have failed prior BMS procedures or present with larger lesion sizes not amenable to microfracture.

Improvements across FAAM subscales in our series paralleled substantial reductions in pain, with median VAS scores dropping from 6.0 to 1.0. Favorable outcomes following PJCA implantation have also been supported by early technical reports. Hatic and Berlet provided one of the earliest published descriptions of PJCA grafting for talar lesions, outlining surgical technique, biological rationale, and potential benefits of this off-the-shelf, single-stage cartilage restoration approach. 43

Nine of 10 patients in our series successfully returned to full duty, with most resuming unrestricted activity after a single LIMDU period. One patient medically separated through the MEDBOARD process. This high return-to-duty rate is particularly significant in a military setting where physical demands exceed those of the general population. These results are in line with outcomes described by Coetzee et al, 36 who reported favorable functional recovery and work capacity following PJCA procedures in young, active individuals.

Our series reported no complications related to the surgical procedure. This low complication rate is consistent with the literature and reflects several advantages of PJCA implantation. Unlike osteochondral autograft transplantation or autologous chondrocyte implantation, PJCA avoids donor-site morbidity and the need for a second-stage procedure. These factors are well documented by Brittberg et al 28 in 1994 and Bartlett et al 29 in 2005.

Despite these encouraging outcomes, our study has limitations. The small sample size and retrospective nature of the analysis introduce the potential for selection bias. Without a control group, it is not possible to directly compare outcomes to those of microfracture, osteochondral autograft, or other cartilage repair techniques. We acknowledge that not all study participants were able to undergo postoperative MRI at the designated 6- and 12-month intervals. The demands of mandatory military courses and nonphysical training assignments posed challenges to standardizing follow-up at these specific time points. Nevertheless, all patients included in our study completed their final follow-up visits and outcome assessments, ensuring comprehensive evaluation of clinical results. Moreover, although short-term imaging and patient-reported outcomes were favorable, longer-term durability remains unknown. Concerns about the mechanical longevity of fibrocartilage formed after microfracture are well established, 20 but the chondrogenic potential of juvenile cartilage, as demonstrated in preclinical models and human cell studies, may mitigate these issues by promoting the formation of hyaline cartilage. 31

Currently, BMS remains the most widely used first-line surgical treatment for OCDs of the talus and has consistently demonstrated reliable short- to mid-term clinical success, with good to excellent outcomes reported in approximately 70% to 90% of appropriately selected patients and significant improvements in pain and function. 44 However, a consistent theme across the literature is the progressive loss of durability over time. Midterm studies demonstrate a high prevalence of repair tissue surface damage on MRI (up to 76%), 44 whereas long-term data show declining clinical scores, reduced return to preinjury activity levels, and radiographic progression of degenerative changes in approximately one-third of patients. 45

Even in patients who maintain sports participation, functional outcomes deteriorate when compared to early postoperative results, underscoring the time-dependent limitations of this technique. 46 In a 2021 Level II prospective follow-up study of 60 patients from a prior randomized controlled trial, Lambers and colleagues 46 evaluated sports activity and clinical outcomes at a mean 6.4 years after arthroscopic BMS for OCDs of the talus. The cohort demonstrated 90% sports participation at final follow-up, with 53% returning to their pre-injury level. 46 The investigators concluded that although sports participation remains high at long-term follow-up, patients and clinicians should be counseled regarding progressive clinical decline over time, likely attributable to fibrocartilage degradation and osteoarthritis progression. 46

In response, cartilage resurfacing and restorative procedures, including cell-based therapies, osteochondral transplantation, and scaffold-augmented techniques, have gained increasing interest, with early and midterm studies demonstrating promising improvements in pain, function, and structural cartilage restoration, although high-quality comparative data remain limited and long-term durability is still being defined.

Future research should focus on randomized controlled trials comparing PJCA to other cartilage restoration techniques. Extended follow-up with standardized imaging and histologic assessments would be valuable in determining the long-term integrity of the repair. In military and athletic populations, studies should also address return to duty/sport timelines, cost-effectiveness, and durability under physical stress.

Conclusion

In conclusion, preliminary associations suggest PJCA appears to have been a safe and effective option for the treatment of talar osteochondral defects in our high-demand population. In our cohort, the approach was associated with measurable radiographic improvement, significant symptom relief, functional gains, and a high rate of return to duty without complications. These results support continued clinical use and further investigation of this technique as a cartilage restoration solution in the ankle.
