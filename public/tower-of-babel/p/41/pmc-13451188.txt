Clinical outcomes of the new generation VISUMAX 800 platform in small incision lenticule extraction for Myopia in Chinese patients
Authors: Hui Zhang, Yuchen Mei, Quan Liu, Yawen Hao, Xuanyu Yang, Nian Guan, Xiaoli Li, Wentian Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis retrospective multicentre study assessed early refractive outcomes and safety of small incision lenticule extraction (SMILE) in Chinese myopic eyes and characterised astigmatic accuracy and higher-order aberration (HOA) changes. Adults who underwent SMILE using new generation VISUMAX 800 with CentraLign centration and OcuLign iris registration participated. The participants comprised 319 patients (605 eyes) (mean age 20.6 ± 4.1 years; mean preoperative spherical equivalent [SE], − 3.88 ± 1.50 dioptres [D]). At 6 months, the mean uncorrected distance visual acuity was − 0.10 ± 0.05 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution and mean manifest refractive SE was + 0.25 ± 0.33 D; 83.0% and 100.0% of eyes were within ± 0.50 D and ± 1.00 D of target SE, respectively. Residual cylinder was ≤ 0.50 D in 97.5% and ≤ 1.00 D in 100.0%. The Alpins analysis suggested mild undercorrection (correction index, 0.94 ± 0.23; difference vector, 0.21 ± 0.18 D). HOA root mean square increased (0.42 ± 0.03 to 0.57 ± 0.10 μm; P < 0.01), whereas spherical aberration was unchanged (P = 0.55). Decentration–coma analyses showed no significant associations between signed or total decentration parameters and corresponding coma changes after false discovery rate adjustment. Complications were infrequent. Introduction

The field of refractive surgery has advanced rapidly over the past decade. Flapless lenticule-based procedures, such as small incision lenticule extraction (SMILE) and keratorefractive lenticule extraction, are now widely used in clinical practice because of their well-established efficacy and safety1,2. Since its introduction on the first-generation VISUMAX 500 platform, SMILE has become an important surgical option for the correction of myopia and myopic astigmatism. Despite the important role of the first-generation VISUMAX 500 platform in supporting the global adoption of SMILE, several intraoperative drawbacks have remained, including a relatively longer laser scanning time, susceptibility to suction loss, absence of active eye tracking, and lack of integrated cyclotorsion compensation. These factors may affect centration accuracy and postoperative optical quality3,4. These issues are especially relevant for astigmatic correction5. Prior comparative studies suggest that without static cyclotorsion compensation and automated centration guidance, SMILE depends on additional alignment methods, such as cross-hair-assisted positioning, to reduce axis errors; residual astigmatism may be lower than after femtosecond laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis (FS-LASIK) or transepithelial photorefractive keratectomy, whereas axis correction accuracy appears to be similar across procedures6. These findings indicate that rotational alignment and centration control are relevant considerations for astigmatic correction, although whether integrated platform-level guidance translates into superior clinical outcomes requires comparative evidence.

The new generation VISUMAX 800 (Carl Zeiss AG, Jena, Germany) introduced in 2021, maintains the optical path and lenticule design while increasing the repetition rate to 2 MHz and adding centration support during docking (CentraLign) and intraoperative pattern rotation for cyclotorsion compensation (OcuLign); These updates have been described within the SMILE Pro software framework; the 2-MHz pulse rate markedly shortens laser delivery, which may reduce suction-loss risk related to small patient movements during suction and improve the overall workflow efficiency7. CentraLign and OcuLign provide real-time image-guided support for centration and astigmatic axis alignment, which is intended to support workflow consistency and intraoperative alignment8–10. However, favourable outcomes in a single-arm cohort cannot establish whether these platform features provide measurable advantages over earlier systems or alternative refractive procedures. The VISUMAX 800 received US Food and Drug Administration approval in 2024, which supports its wider use in routine clinical practice11.

Clinical evidence supporting the new-generation platforms is accumulating. Retrospective single-centre studies have reported the refractive and visual outcomes of VISUMAX 800 for correcting compound myopic astigmatism that suggest favourable refractive accuracy and safety, with potential advantages in intraoperative workflow efficiency and alignment control12–14. A multicentre post-market clinical follow-up study demonstrated that keratorefractive lenticule extraction performed with VISUMAX 800 achieves good refractive accuracy and visual recovery in routine clinical populations while adhering to international ethical standards and International Organization for Standardization-compliant clinical research requirements8. Large-scale retrospective studies with Asian populations have also reported its excellent safety, efficacy, and predictability, highlighting the importance of obtaining real-world evidence from regions with a high prevalence of myopia and myopic astigmatism15–18.

Comparative reports have generally suggested that VISUMAX 800 and VISUMAX 500 achieve broadly comparable short-term refractive and visual outcomes, while the newer platform may shorten lenticule creation time and provide more image-guided intraoperative support3,9–12,19–21. However, the extent to which these workflow and alignment features translate into clinically meaningful differences in visual quality remains to be established in controlled comparative studies. A recent early case-series comparison from a single centre in the United States of America likewise reported broadly similar early visual and safety outcomes among the two platforms, and a more concentrated distribution of astigmatic axis error in the VISUMAX 800 group, suggesting improved alignment precision11. However, most published reports about the VISUMAX 800 have been limited by short follow-up periods, small sample sizes, and single-centre designs; the systematic multicentre evaluations, particularly those for aberration changes and detailed astigmatic vector outcomes, are scarce. The specific incremental contribution of the present study is its integrated assessment of early VISUMAX 800 SMILE outcomes using standard refractive surgery reporting, Alpins vector analysis, and Zernike-based higher-order aberration profiling in a consecutive multicentre Chinese real-world cohort. This multidimensional approach allows visual recovery, refractive predictability, astigmatic vector accuracy, and optical quality changes to be interpreted within the same dataset. Given the large myopic population in China, often accompanied by complex astigmatic patterns, real-world evidence from consecutive cases may provide useful descriptive benchmarks across clinical settings.

To address these gaps, we conducted a retrospective multicentre study with consecutive cases from three ophthalmic centres in China to evaluate the early clinical outcomes and safety of SMILE Pro performed with VISUMAX 800, quantify astigmatic correction precision, and assess higher-order aberration changes.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This retrospective, observational, multicentre study consecutively enrolled patients who underwent SMILE using the VISUMAX 800 femtosecond laser system with the SMILE Pro software (version 1.2.4; Carl Zeiss Meditec) at three ophthalmic centres in China between May and August 2025.

This study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committees of Nanchang Bright Eye Hospital (Approval No. 202512-03), Guangzhou Bright Eye Hospital (Approval No. 2026KYPJ001), and Wuhan Bright Eye Hospital (Approval No. 2025-18). For this retrospective analysis, ethics approval was obtained before data extraction and statistical analysis of the de-identified clinical dataset. Written informed consent for surgery and authorisation for the use of de-identified clinical data were obtained as part of routine clinical care.

Participants

The inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years; preoperative manifest sphere, expressed in minus-cylinder notation, with an absolute value ≤ 8.00 dioptres (D); preoperative manifest cylinder with an absolute value ≤ 3.50 D; preoperative corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) of 20/25 or better; regular corneal topography; and an anticipated residual stromal bed thickness ≥ 280 μm. The sphere criterion referred to the preoperative manifest sphere and not to spherical equivalent or laser-programmed treatment sphere.

The exclusion criteria were prior ocular surgery; keratoconus, forme fruste keratoconus, or subclinical keratoconus; active ocular inflammation; severe dry eye disease; autoimmune disease; pregnancy or lactation; and other ocular comorbidities that could affect visual outcomes, such as glaucoma, cataract, or retinal disease. Only patients with available 6-month postoperative follow-up data were included. Patients without complete 6-month follow-up or with missing key outcome data were excluded during the screening stage.

Preoperative assessment and surgical planning

All the patients underwent a standardised preoperative assessment including manifest and cycloplegic refraction, uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA) and CDVA measured using standardised visual acuity charts, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, dilated fundus examination, corneal topography and pachymetry, optical biometry, intraocular pressure measurements, and photopic and mesopic pupil diameter measurements. Across centres, refraction and corneal imaging were performed according to harmonised clinical protocols. Measurements were obtained by trained technicians, and scans with poor fixation, motion artefacts, or low acquisition quality were repeated or excluded from analysis. The operating surgeon verified the refractive status and confirmed the final surgical plan prior to treatment.

Centration was primarily referenced to the corneal vertex. A high-resolution preoperative iris image was acquired and imported into the surgical system using OcuLign for intraoperative iris registration and cyclotorsion compensation. Angle kappa-related parameters were recorded and reported as X- and Y-axes Cartesian offsets (millimetres) provided by the device.

Surgical procedure

All procedures were performed under topical anaesthesia using the VISUMAX 800 femtosecond laser system by experienced refractive surgeons at each participating centre, all of whom had prior clinical experience with the VISUMAX 800 platform before participating in this study. The lenticule, cap, and incision geometry followed previously published protocols; all centres adopted a harmonised parameter range as part of a standardised surgical setting.

A standardised single-incision technique was used across the centres. A ~ 2.0-mm incision was created at the cap margin and positioned superonasally as the primary access site. Docking was performed using a small size contact glass in all cases.

Centration and cyclotorsion compensation

Centration assistance (CentraLign) was enabled during docking. Based on the pre-imported corneal vertex reference, the system displayed the vector offset between the corneal vertex and intended treatment centre in real time; docking centration was adjusted according to the device-guided display before suction activation. Subsequently, the OcuLign guidance screen was activated and iris registration was used to identify and compensate for cyclotorsion, thereby aligning the treatment axis with the preoperative plan.

Laser settings

The laser cutting sequence followed the standard SMILE workflow: the cap interface was created first, followed by the lenticule interface, and then the lenticule side cut and incision were completed. The laser energy, spot spacing, and track spacing were preset using standardised settings applied across the centres to ensure a clear interface and facilitate lenticule dissection. Cap thickness was adjusted within 100–120 μm according to corneal thickness and the expected residual stromal bed. The optical zone diameter was chosen based on pupil size and residual stromal bed requirements. The minimum lenticule thickness was determined according to established protocols and prior studies.

Lenticule dissection and extraction

Lenticule dissection and extraction were performed using a double-ended dissector (MR-G134T-1). The anterior and posterior interfaces under the cap were sequentially separated and the lenticule was completely removed. At the end of the procedure, fluorescein staining under cobalt-blue light was used to check the interface integrity and identify any residual lenticule fragments.

Surgical time metrics

To reduce the differences in timing criteria across centres, surgical time measures were obtained from system-recorded surgical videos using predefined start and end points. The lenticule creation time was defined as the time from suction activation to the end of laser scanning. The lenticule extraction time was defined as the time from the start of lenticule dissection to complete lenticule removal. The same timing definitions were used in all the centres.

Postoperative regimen and follow-up

All the patients were advised to wear an eye shield at night for 1 week after surgery. Standard eye drops included levofloxacin 0.5%, a steroid (fluorometholone or a tobramycin-dexamethasone combination, depending on routine practice), and sodium hyaluronate, which was used 4 times daily for 1 week.

Follow-up visits were scheduled at 1, 3 and 6 months. The UDVA and CDVA, manifest refraction, and corneal topography were measured at each visit. This analysis included only eyes with complete 6-month follow-up data for the primary visual and refractive outcomes; therefore, no imputation was performed for missing 6-month outcomes.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes

The primary outcomes were efficacy (percentage of eyes that achieved UDVA ≥ 20/20 and ≥ 20/25), predictability (percentage of eyes with final manifest refractive spherical equivalent [MRSE] within ± 0.50 D and ± 1.00 D of the intended target), and safety, assessed by postoperative CDVA and the distribution of CDVA line changes from baseline to the final follow-up visit.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes were stability (change in MRSE from 1 to 6 months), residual refractive cylinder, and astigmatic vector parameters based on the Alpins method (target induced astigmatism [TIA], surgically induced astigmatism [SIA], difference vector [DV], angle of error [AE], correction index [CI], and index of success [IoS]). Eyes were categorised as undercorrection (CI < 0.9), on target correction (CI 0.9–1.1), or overcorrection (CI > 1.1) to describe the correction bias distribution.

Intraoperative and postoperative adverse events were documented in routine clinical records at each visit and were retrospectively extracted for this study using predefined categories, including suction loss, difficult dissection, retained lenticule fragments, cap tear, corneal haze, dry eye symptoms, diffuse lamellar keratitis, and epithelial-related adverse events22. Lenticule-related intraoperative events included difficult dissection, incomplete lenticule separation, or retained lenticule fragments documented by the operating surgeon. Moderate-to-severe dry eye was defined according to the treating clinician’s documentation in the postoperative medical record.

Anterior corneal higher-order aberrations (HOAs) were calculated as Zernike root-mean-square (RMS) (µm) from the Pentacam HR system (software version 1.21r43; Oculus, Wetzlar, Germany) using a 6.0-mm pupil diameter; they are reported as mean ± standard deviation. HOA RMS, spherical aberration, horizontal coma (Coma-x), vertical coma (Coma-y), trefoil, and RMS coma were analysed. RMS coma was calculated as √(Coma-x² + Coma-y²). Because horizontal coma is affected by mirror symmetry between right and left eyes, the magnitude of horizontal coma was analysed using the absolute value of Coma-x for group-level preoperative-to-postoperative comparisons. For the decentration–coma analysis, coma changes were calculated as 6-month postoperative values minus preoperative values. Component-wise analyses were performed using signed X-axis decentration versus change in horizontal coma and signed Y-axis decentration versus change in vertical coma. Total decentration was calculated as √(Dec-X² + Dec-Y²), and its association with the change in RMS coma was also assessed. To further assess the potential influence of inter-eye correlation on the exploratory decentration–coma analyses, one-eye-per-patient sensitivity analyses were performed using both a right-eye-preferred approach and a random-one-eye approach. For the random-one-eye analysis, one eye was randomly selected from each patient using a fixed random seed to ensure reproducibility.

Sample size and data handling

The sample size was determined by the availability of consecutive eligible cases during the study period, and no a priori sample size calculation was performed. Because this was a retrospective descriptive cohort, precision was evaluated using 95% confidence intervals around key estimates, including refractive predictability, residual cylinder thresholds, CDVA line loss, adverse event rates, and higher-order aberration changes. Non-significant association analyses, including decentration–coma correlation analyses, were interpreted as absence of a detectable association within this dataset rather than evidence of no biological relationship.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median and interquartile range, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Visual and refractive outcomes were primarily reported at the eye level according to standard refractive surgery reporting.

Because both eyes from some patients were included, inferential analyses involving higher-order aberration changes were performed using cluster-adjusted models with patient identification as the clustering variable. Cluster-adjusted 95% confidence intervals were reported where appropriate. P values for higher-order aberration comparisons were adjusted using the Holm–Bonferroni method. For decentration–coma analyses, Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated, and P values were adjusted using the false discovery rate method to account for multiple correlation tests. Effect estimates were reported as mean changes for paired continuous outcomes and correlation coefficients for association analyses, together with 95% confidence intervals or adjusted P values where appropriate. Centre-level distributions and selected outcomes were summarised descriptively in Supplementary Table 2. Formal between-centre comparisons were not performed because the study was not designed or powered to evaluate centre-level differences. All tests were two-sided, and significance was set at P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY; USA) and standard refractive surgery graphs were generated using VISULYZE (Carl Zeiss Meditec).

Results

Study population and preoperative baseline characteristics

The 319 consecutive patients (605 eyes) underwent SMILE Pro using the VISUMAX 800 platform at the three participating centres. All included eyes had completed 6-month postoperative follow-up data for the primary visual and refractive outcomes. The mean age was 20.6 ± 4.1 years, and 62.1% of patients were males. Preoperatively, the mean results were as follows: spherical equivalent (SE) − 3.88 ± 1.50 D, cylinder magnitude (absolute value) 1.01 ± 0.69 D, central corneal thickness 561.4 ± 26.1 μm, flat keratometry 41.78 ± 2.27 D, steep keratometry 43.35 ± 1.44 D, and scotopic pupil diameter 6.81 ± 0.64 mm. Angle kappa-related offsets were recorded as device-provided Cartesian coordinates (mm): X, − 0.008 ± 0.08 mm (range − 0.30 to 0.24 mm) and Y, − 0.032 ± 0.08 mm (range − 0.29 to 0.23 mm). Astigmatic outcomes and Alpins vector analysis

Astigmatic outcomes and Alpins vector analyses are summarised in Figs. 1G–I and 2, and Table 4. Among the 605 treated eyes, 571 eyes had non-zero preoperative manifest astigmatism, 569 eyes had planned astigmatic correction with TIA > 0 D and were included in the Alpins vector analysis, and AE was calculable in 567 eyes. At 6 months, 97.5% of the eyes had a residual refractive cylinder ≤ 0.50 D and all eyes were within ≤ 1.00 D. The mean results were TIA 1.07 ± 0.67 D, SIA 1.00 ± 0.65 D, DV 0.21 ± 0.18 D, ME −0.06 ± 0.18 D, CI of 0.94 ± 0.23, and IoS of 0.26 ± 0.31, indicating mild undercorrection overall. The double-angle plots showed predominantly with-the-rule preoperative astigmatism and a similar directional distribution for SIA, whereas the postoperative vectors were markedly reduced and clustered near the origin. The mean preoperative, SIA, and postoperative vectors were 0.87 D at 180.0°, 0.81 D at 0.2°, and 0.06 D at 176.6°, respectively. The arithmetic mean AE was 0.2 ± 12.5°, with an absolute mean AE of 6.08 ± 10.88°; 67.0% and 92.8% of eyes were within ± 5° and ± 15° of the intended axis, respectively. Based on the CI, 37.1% of eyes were undercorrected, 48.7% were on target, and 14.2% were overcorrected. Cluster-adjusted confidence intervals and one-eye-per-patient sensitivity analyses for categorical Alpins outcomes are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Aberration changes

Compared with preoperative values, HOA RMS increased at 6 months from 0.42 ± 0.03 μm to 0.57 ± 0.10 μm, with a mean change of 0.15 μm. The magnitude of horizontal coma, expressed as absolute Coma-x, increased from 0.13 ± 0.07 μm to 0.16 ± 0.10 μm, and RMS coma increased from 0.17 ± 0.09 μm to 0.27 ± 0.15 μm. Coma-y and trefoil also increased significantly, whereas spherical aberration showed no significant postoperative change. Detailed cluster-adjusted 95% confidence intervals and Holm-adjusted P values are provided in Table 5. The decentration–coma correlation analysis is shown in Fig. 3. After adjustment for multiple testing, no significant association was observed between signed X-axis decentration and change in horizontal coma, signed Y-axis decentration and change in vertical coma, or total decentration and change in RMS coma. Although the Spearman correlation between signed X-axis decentration and horizontal coma change reached nominal significance before correction, it did not remain significant after false discovery rate adjustment (ρ = −0.081, P = 0.047, q = 0.141). One-eye-per-patient sensitivity analyses using both the right-eye-preferred and random-one-eye approaches yielded consistent results, with no FDR-significant associations observed for the decentration–coma correlations (Supplementary Table 3).

Surgical safety

Safety was evaluated using CDVA line changes and perioperative adverse events. At 6 months, no eye lost ≥ 2 lines of CDVA. Seventy eyes (70/605, 11.6%) lost 1 line of CDVA, whereas 401 eyes (401/605, 66.3%) showed no change, 113 eyes (113/605, 18.7%) gained 1 line, and 21 eyes (21/605, 3.5%) gained ≥ 2 lines (Fig. 1C).

No suction loss or cap tear occurred intraoperatively. Lenticule-related intraoperative events occurred in 3 eyes (3/605, 0.5%), including cases documented as difficult dissection or incomplete lenticule-related manipulation. These events were managed intraoperatively without conversion to another surgical procedure or vision-threatening sequelae. Postoperatively, moderate-to-severe dry eye was documented in 10 eyes (10/605, 1.7%) and was managed with postoperative medical treatment. No diffuse lamellar keratitis, corneal haze, epithelial ingrowth, or infection was observed during the 6-month follow-up. Overall, no vision-threatening adverse event was recorded.

Discussion

In this multicentre, real-world cohort study of consecutive cases, we characterised the early clinical outcomes of SMILE Pro performed using the VISUMAX 800 platform in Chinese patients with myopia and myopic astigmatism. Beyond confirming favourable early refractive outcomes, this study adds to the existing VISUMAX 800 literature by combining standard refractive surgery graphs, Alpins vector metrics, and Zernike-based HOA analysis in a consecutive multicentre Chinese real-world cohort. Overall, visual recovery was rapid and refractive outcomes remained stable during early follow-up. UDVA was maintained at better than 0 logMAR across postoperative visits, and MRSE changed minimally from 1 to 6 months, consistent with an early stabilisation trajectory in routine clinical practice. Standard refractive surgery graphs further contextualised these findings across the core domains of efficacy, safety, and predictability: at 6 months, most eyes achieved UDVA comparable to or better than preoperative CDVA with no eye losing ≥ 2 lines of CDVA, and attempted versus achieved SE demonstrated close agreement, the majority of eyes were within ± 0.50 D and all eyes were within ± 1.00 D (Fig. 1A–F). These outcomes were observed in a multicentre real-world setting, supporting the overall robustness of the early findings in routine clinical practice. For astigmatic correction, standard astigmatism plots and Alpins vector analysis showed a low residual cylinder and a relatively narrow spread of the axis error. At 6 months, almost all the eyes had a residual refractive cylinder ≤ 0.50 D and within ≤ 1.00 D, with > 90% of eyes having an AE within ± 15° (Fig. 1G–I). Vector measures suggested mild undercorrection (CI slightly < 1 and a small negative ME), whereas DV remained low (Table 4). Clinically, these patterns suggest that further improvement may be better achieved by adjusting the magnitude component of astigmatic correction (nomogram tuning) rather than using empiric overcorrection to compensate for axis-related losses; however, this idea should be confirmed in prospective studies. Total HOAs and some asymmetric components changed at 6 months, whereas spherical aberration did not change, and the overall size of these changes was small (Table 5). These results, along with the favourable UDVA outcomes, indicate that early aberration changes after SMILE Pro with VISUMAX 800 may be acceptable in the short term. However, whether these statistical differences translate into patient-noticed changes in visual quality, especially in low-contrast or night-driving settings, needs to be evaluated with functional tests such as contrast sensitivity and validated symptom questionnaires, ideally in comparative studies or with longer follow-ups. Overall, our data support the favourable early efficacy and predictability of SMILE Pro using VISUMAX 800 in a multicentre real-world setting and point to opportunities for further nomogram improvement, mainly focused on magnitude calibration.

A notable finding in our cohort was the postoperative increase in coma-related aberrations despite image-guided centration support. However, the decentration–coma analyses showed no significant associations after adjustment for multiple testing between signed X-axis decentration and horizontal coma change, signed Y-axis decentration and vertical coma change, or total decentration and RMS coma change. Decentration has been considered a potential contributor to coma induction after corneal refractive surgery, but our findings suggest that, within the observed range of intraoperative decentration, decentration was not a major detectable determinant of postoperative coma changes. In the present cohort, the absence of a detectable linear association suggests that postoperative coma changes did not scale with either signed component-wise decentration or total decentration magnitude. These findings do not exclude centration-related effects altogether, although they raise the possibility that other factors may contribute more prominently to postoperative coma-related changes after SMILE Pro using VISUMAX 800. One plausible explanation is incision-related corneal biomechanical remodelling, whereby asymmetric stromal stress redistribution associated with a superior incision could influence the postoperative corneal shape and coma components. Alternatively, interindividual differences in preoperative corneal morphology and healing responses may also play a role. Future studies with longer follow-up periods, objective measurements of the optical zone position, and functional visual quality endpoints are warranted to clarify the relative contributions of centration, incision geometry, and biomechanical remodelling to postoperative coma changes in highly myopic and astigmatic eyes.

Our findings are broadly consistent with emerging early clinical evidence for the VISUMAX 800 platform. Reinstein et al.12 reported favourable 3-month refractive accuracy and a high percentage of eyes achieving 20/20 or better UDVA after SMILE for compound myopic astigmatism with early postoperative stability toward the intended refractive target. In a large real-world series of 1,500 eyes, Ganesh et al.13 similarly demonstrated excellent 3-month outcomes with mean postoperative SEs close to emmetropia and UDVA approximately − 0.05 logMAR, thus supporting reliable early efficacy and safety in routine practice. A prospective multicentre post-market clinical follow-up study by Sekundo et al.8 reported maintained visual performance and predictable astigmatic correction at 6 months, which provides longer-term follow-up results for the early recovery and short-term stability profile observed in the present cohort. Comparative studies of the VISUMAX 500 and VISUMAX 800 have reported similar short-term visual and refractive results, and suggest that the newer platform may shorten the procedure time and improve centration-related measures, helping alignment accuracy and workflow efficiency23. These findings, together with the current results, suggest that the VISUMAX 800 may support efficient workflow and alignment consistency in routine practice. However, because the present study did not include a contemporaneous VISUMAX 500 control group, platform-related advantages cannot be directly inferred from our data.

Compared with earlier reports that were often limited by smaller cohorts, nonconsecutive enrolment, or mixed populations, the consecutive-case design and larger sample size in our study may have lowered the risk of selection bias and allowed a clearer description of early visual recovery and refractive stabilisation in everyday clinical settings. Notably, we did not rely on a single outcome; instead, we implemented a combined assessment covering visual function (UDVA), refractive accuracy (MRSE and standard refractive surgery graphs), astigmatic vector outcomes (Alpins parameters), and optical quality (HOA changes). This multidimensional approach allowed for interpretation of astigmatic correction beyond simple residual cylinder proportions toward quantitative, vector-based metrics that are more comparable across studies and populations, thereby providing clearer references for future cross-platform and multicentre evidence synthesis.

This study had several limitations. First, this was a retrospective multicentre study that focused on the early postoperative period follow-up; thus, long-term refractive drift, biomechanical remodelling and late-onset complications could not be fully evaluated. Although no eye lost ≥ 2 lines of CDVA, 1-line CDVA loss was observed in a subset of eyes. Because of the retrospective design, detailed causal attribution for minor CDVA line changes, such as tear-film instability, ocular surface symptoms, or measurement variability, could not be fully determined. Second, we did not include contemporaneous paired comparisons with the VISUMAX 500 or a use a prospective randomised design; therefore, inferences regarding platform-related advantages rely largely on indirect comparisons with prior literature and mechanistic plausibility. Accordingly, the novelty of the present work should be interpreted as a multidimensional descriptive characterisation of early VISUMAX 800 outcomes in a Chinese multicentre real-world cohort, rather than evidence of comparative superiority over earlier platforms. Third, because both eyes from some patients were included, inter-eye correlation remained an important analytical consideration. We therefore used cluster-adjusted analyses for inferential outcomes where appropriate and provided one-eye-per-patient sensitivity analyses for categorical Alpins outcomes and exploratory decentration–coma correlations; nevertheless, eye-level descriptive percentages should still be interpreted with this design feature in mind. Fourth, despite standardised data collection procedures and the descriptive centre-level outcomes provided in Supplementary Table 2, subtle inter-site variability in surgical techniques, refraction workflows, and postoperative management is difficult to eliminate in retrospective multicentre studies. Moreover, the study was not designed or powered to formally assess centre- or surgeon-level effects, which may have influenced sensitive outcomes such as astigmatic axis-related metrics. Fifth, we did not systematically incorporate patient-reported and functional visual quality endpoints (contrast sensitivity, Quality of Vision questionnaire, and night-vision symptoms such as glare and halos), which limit inferences regarding subjective visual quality benefits. Finally, the absence of significant associations in secondary analyses should not be interpreted as evidence of no clinically meaningful relationship.

Considering these study limitations, future research should investigate three complementary directions: stronger study designs, longer follow-up periods, and more granular personalisation. First, multicentre prospective comparative studies of the VISUMAX 800 and VISUMAX 500 are needed. When randomised study designs are not feasible, rigorous real-world comparative frameworks (propensity score matching with pre-specified stratification) can be used to test whether the newer platform offers measurable gains in centration consistency, astigmatic axis error control, and patient-relevant visual quality under more homogeneous baseline conditions. Second, extending the follow-up beyond 6 months and incorporating a fuller set of structure-function endpoints is important. The inclusion of epithelial thickness remodelling, objective measures of optical zone position and centration (corneal vertex–based offsets or metrics referenced to the corneal reflex axis), and validated patient-reported outcomes that capture low-light visual quality (night-vision symptoms and Quality of Vision questionnaire) would be beneficial. Such data would help distinguish early workflow-related advantages from potential medium- to long-term visual quality benefits. Third and final, as multicentre datasets continue to expand, developing and externally validating a nomogram tailored to Chinese populations may have substantial clinical value. Focus should be placed on more complex cases, such as moderate-to-high astigmatism, larger angle kappa offsets, and larger scotopic pupils, to improve individualised parameter recommendations and potentially enhance vector accuracy and patient satisfaction.

Conclusion

This multicentre, real-world cohort study presented comprehensive descriptions of the early outcomes for SMILE Pro performed using the VISUMAX 800 platform. Early visual and refractive results were good, and astigmatic correction showed a low residual cylinder with vector metrics indicating mild undercorrection. The platform workflow and alignment support may contribute to intraoperative consistency, although comparative studies are needed to determine whether these features translate into superior astigmatic or visual outcomes. Longer follow-up and well-designed comparative studies including earlier platforms are warranted to determine whether these early advantages lead to lasting improvements in visual quality and personalised astigmatism management across different patient populations.
