A randomized control trial to assess the effectiveness of a brief educational intervention on colonoscopy preparation and experience in patients with inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Sumanpreet Kaur, Neha Jindal, Amandeep Kaur, Abhirup Chatterjee, Usha Dutta, Vishal Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColonoscopy is important for diagnosis, assessment and dysplasia screening in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). A good bowel preparation is required for complete and adequate assessment.Trial designRandomised, assessor blinded parallel arm trial with 1:1 allocation.MethodsPatients with IBD undergoing colonoscopy were randomised to control group (standard preparation with written instructions) or experimental group (additional brief video educational intervention). Outcome assessors and colonoscopist were masked to the allocation. Primary outcome was adequacy of bowel preparation (Boston bowel preparation score of ≥ 6).ResultsOf 130 patients randomised, 107 (57% males, mean age 38.4 years, 84% with ulcerative colitis) were analysed. The groups were similar for baseline characters (age, gender, rural residence, educational status, duration of illness, IBD therapies, and endoscopic activity). Adequate bowel preparation was present similar numbers in experimental group compared to control group (43.1% versus 35%) (p = 0.43). The groups were similar for perfect preparation (7.8% versus 8.9%) (p = 0.84), worsening of disease activity (3.9% versus 3.7%) (p = 1.00), time to cecal intubation (240, 191 s versus 268, 228 s) (p = 0.43), tolerance (45.1% versus 30.4%)(p = 0.12), and willingness to undergo a repeat colonoscopy (54.9% versus 55.4%)(p = 0.97).ConclusionA brief educational video intervention did not improve bowel preparation in patients with IBD undergoing colonoscopy.Trial registrationCTRI/2023/09/057436 on 11 September 2023.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04242-x.

Introduction

Colonoscopy has an important role in the diagnosis of inflammatory bowel disease, estimation of disease severity, monitoring response to therapy, surveillance for neoplasia, and making important treatment decisions [1–3]. Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), a bowel disease of uncertain cause, results in ulcers or narrowing of the digestive tract [1]. Over last few decades, colonoscopy has undergone major advances in the form of image-enhanced endoscopy (like chromoendoscopy, narrow-band imaging, confocal laser endomicroscopy etc.) and has taken a leap with the incorporation of artificial intelligence (AI) with it which has now been approved by FDA [4]. However, the true benefit of these advances is dependent on adequate visualization of the colonic mucosa during the procedure [5–7].

Adequate bowel preparation is a prerequisite for successful colonoscopy. Bowel preparation is essential to ensure good visualization so that a safe and high-quality colonoscopy can be achieved. Poor bowel preparation may contribute to decreased polyp detection and removal, thus leading to missed or delayed diagnosis and treatment [8]. In patients with poor bowel preparation, the procedure might be abandoned in view of an increased risk of complications. Inadequate bowel preparation is also one of the most unfavorable predictors for cecal intubation failure and unsatisfactory patient experience [9]. However, bowel preparation is a complex and has been found to be inadequate in approximately 5% to 60% of colonoscopy examinations [9, 10]. Several factors might influence the quality of bowel preparation, including the patient’s age, purgative and protocol used, colonoscopy timing, and patient’s compliance with the bowel preparation instructions [6]. Patients have a central role in the bowel preparation and compliance with instructions predicts an optimal preparation [11].

Lack of adequate preparation in patients with IBD may have serious consequences including need for repeat procedures, economic costs, risk of missing dysplasia/malignancy and inappropriate estimation of disease extent or activity [12, 13]. Further, although various bowel preparations seem to perform similarly in respect to the bowel cleansing, the role of educational intervention(s) directed towards patients have not been evaluated [14]. Previous studies have evaluated, outside the setting of IBD, the role of educational interventions in improving the bowel preparation [15, 16]. Therefore, we studied if addition of a brief video based educational intervention could improve bowel preparation in patients with IBD.

Methods

Setting

This randomized trial was conducted at a large tertiary care center in North India. The trial was registered prospectively before first enrollment in the clinical trials registry of India vide CTRI/2023/09/057436. Approval from institutional ethical committee was obtained vide letter number INT/IEC/2023/SPL-848 prior to starting and all patients provided written informed consent before inclusion. The manuscript has been reported consistent with the CONSORT guidance for randomized trials and CONSORT checklist has been provided in the Supplementary Appendix.

Study design

A parallel randomized controlled trial with 1: 1 allocation was performed. All patients with inflammatory bowel disease planned for colonoscopy for standard indications attending gastroenterology OPD were eligible for inclusion. All patients received standard instructions for colonoscopy preparation along with written instructions in the language of their preference (Hindi, Punjabi or English). The enrolment was started in September 2023 and the last recruitment was done in August 2024.

Participants

We included adult patients with inflammatory bowel disease (age between 18–80 years) who were undergoing colonoscopy for mapping of disease extent or dysplasia screening were included in the study. We excluded participants with active disease precluding colonoscopy, acute obstruction or history suggestive of intestinal obstruction (likely stricturing disease), severe systemic illness (symptomatic heart failure, CTP -C cirrhosis, chronic kidney disease with GFR < 30), not willing for participation, pregnant or lactating females, psychiatric comorbidity (depression, anxiety disorder etc.) or with previous abdominal surgery. The study was conducted in gastroenterology department, PGIMER, Chandigarh from September 2023 - August 2024.

Interventions

Standard care arm

The preparation for colonoscopy was done as per the standard European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) guidelines [17]. We suggested a low fibre diet for two days preceding the start of bowel preparation. A standard split dose preparation with polyethylene glycol (PEG) was used. A PEG sachet of 137.15 g was dissolved in 2 L of water. On the day before the procedure the patient was advised to take 2 L over a three-hour period in the evening from 5–8 PM. On the day of the procedure, another 2 L of preparation was administered in the morning from 4–7 AM. The procedure was performed between 9 AM to 12:00 PM on the day of appointment. The procedure was performed under conscious sedation and air insufflation. Injection midazolam 2 mg was given to the patients before the procedure. All patients were provided verbal instructions and also a written detailed information sheet regarding the procedure in the language of patient’s preference (Hindi, Punjabi or English, Supplementary Appendix). Briefly, in case of intolerance to the preparation the patient was instructed to wait for half an hour and restart the preparation. In case the patient had vomiting, mouth dissolving tablet ondansetron (4 mg) was given with an instruction to start the preparation in another half an hour. In case the vomiting recurred, the patient was instructed to stop the preparation and report on the provided contact number of the investigator. All procedures were done by faculty member with at least > 2000 previous procedures.

Intervention/experimental arm

Apart from the standard guidelines and preparation (as for the Standard of Care Arm), a brief video was shown to the patient on the laptop by one of the investigators who performed the randomization. In addition to the patient, the accompanying attendant also sat through this video presentation which was shown in the language of their choice (Hindi/Punjabi/English). In addition to the video being shown at the time of randomization, video was also provided through WhatsApp or a link to YouTube. The patient was allowed to ask the questions after the video. Briefly the video provides brief information about – What is IBD?, Why is colonoscopy needed in IBD?, Why bowel preparation is needed for colonoscopy?, What are the consequences of poor preparation?, How to prepare for a colonoscopy, including diet and PEG preparations?, How to troubleshoot common problems?, and how to assess that the preparation is adequate? The videos have been provided in the Supplementary Video file.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was an adequate bowel preparation as assessed using Boston bowel preparation score (Score of 6 or more) [18]. Each part of the bowel (right colon, transverse colon, and left colon) was scored from 0–3 as per the BBPS, and a total score was calculated by adding individual scores.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes included 1) number of participants with a perfect preparation (BBPS score of 9), worsening disease activity at two weeks (SCCAI increase by 5 or Harvey Bradshaw index increase by 2) [19, 20], any adverse effects of bowel preparation (pain, vomiting, bloating, nausea or any other symptoms), tolerance of bowel preparation on a Likert scale of 1–5, satisfaction with bowel preparation on a Likert scale, completeness of the preparation, tolerance of procedure on a Likert scale, willingness to undergo procedure again if required on Likert scale, assessment of anxiety level (GAD −7) before procedure and the time to cecal intubation.

Sample size

Usually in the ongoing practice of the center prior to the trial, adequate bowel preparation was seen in around 50% of patients (non-IBD) and we expected 25% additional patients to have a good bowel preparation after our intervention. With a power of 80% and an alpha error of 5%, we needed to enroll 118 patients (Kelsey method). Allowing for 10% attrition after randomization (from randomization to colonoscopy), we planned to randomize 130 patients.

Randomization, masking and allocation concealment

The randomization sequence was generated using a computer-based program, with variable block size. It was generated by a person not associated with the study. The randomization sequence was used to make sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes that were placed under lock and key. Once a patient was considered eligible for inclusion by the clinician(s) the patients would be referred to another investigator. This study personnel would then open a single envelope, as per the sequence number, and the intervention was administered by the same individual. This study personnel involved in opening the envelope, collecting baseline data, and administering the intervention had no further role in the study. A separate study personnel oversaw the procedure and assessment of patients after the procedure and at two weeks. The endoscopist and the second investigator were not aware of allocation and were involved in assessing outcomes, the patient was aware of the allocation. However, during the process of consent the patients were made aware of an educational intervention but not the nature of the intervention (standard verbal and written instruction or additional video intervention).

Data analysis

The analysis was performed using modified intention to treat and per protocol approach with the IBM SPSS 20 software. The categorical data was compared using Chi-squared test or Fischer exact test, as appropriate. The Likert values were converted into categorical data and were analyzed accordingly. Only the score on Likert scale with value of 4 or 5 (agree/strongly agree) were used in assessing a particular parameter while neutral responses (3) and negative responses (1,2) were excluded.

Results

Study population

We screened 155 patients, 25 patients were excluded due to various reasons including presence of stricturing disease (9), past abdominal surgery (6), < 18 years (4), severe systemic disease (3) and those who gave negative consent for enrollment in study (3). A total of 130 patients were randomized, 65 patients in either of the arms. The mean age of all enrolled participants was 38.8 ± 12.37 years and more than half i.e. 76 (57.6%) participants were male. Of these 130 patients, 19 patients (12 in the experimental group and 7 control arm) did not attend the colonoscopy at the time of the appointment. These 19 patients were not significantly different from the patients who attended the colonoscopy on the basis of demographic variables.

Of the 111 patients who attended, four patients (2 in each arm) did not follow the protocol for preparation and took less than half of the bowel preparation. Therefore 111 patients were included in the mITT analysis (53 in experimental and 58 in control group) and 107 in the PPA (51 in experimental and 56 in control group). Clinical outcomes

In respect to the primary outcome (adequate bowel preparation i.e. BBPS ≥ 6), 22 (43%) in the experimental arm as compared to 20 (35%) had an adequate bowel preparation per the per-protocol analysis (p = 0.43). The mean BBPS score in the experimental group was 6 ± 1.8 versus 5.9 ± 1.8 in the control group (P = 0.83). Segment wise BBPS scores were also similar between the two groups (Supplementary Table 1). As per the mITT analysis, the adequate bowel preparation was similar in both the groups (41.5% versus 34.4%, p = 0.581). The perfect bowel preparation rates were low in both the groups (4, 7.8% versus 5, 8.9%, p = 0.84). During this same period colonoscopy was also performed in patients not included in the trial and only 8 of 32 (25%) patients had adequate bowel preparation. These patients were not included in the trial as they received appointment of colonoscopy outside of the outpatient’s department (direct referrals or appointment after hospital discharge).

Very few participants had worsening of disease: 2(3.92%) patients each in the experimental and control group reported disease worsening at 14 days (p = 1.00). A numerically higher number of participants in experimental group (23, 45.1%)) tolerated the procedure well as compared to control group (17, 30.4%), (p = 0.12). Almost equal participants in both the groups had satisfaction with the adequacy of bowel preparation i.e. 35(68.6%) in experiment group versus 38(67.9%) in control group (p = 0.93). The median time to cecal intubation was 240 (191) seconds in the experimental group versus 268(228) seconds in the control group (p value = 0.43) which was not significantly different. The median anxiety score was 3(6) in experimental group versus 2.5(5) in control group (p = 0.29) which was not significant and both the groups had mild anxiety level. Thirteen (25.5%) participants in experimental group tolerated the procedure well as compared to 10 (17.9%) in control group (p = 0.34). The willingness to undergo a repeat colonoscopy was similar between the experimental group (28, 54.9%) and control group (31, 55.4%) (p = 0.97). Discussion

While optimal bowel preparation is key for a proper endoscopic evaluation of disease activity and inflammatory bowel disease, previous evidence suggests that the bowel preparation in IBD may remain inadequate. Inadequate bowel preparation in IBD has been associated with post colonoscopy colorectal cancer in IBD [21]. In addition, the lack of tolerability and the perceived burden of colonoscopy may be higher in the setting of IBD [22, 23].

Several studies have evaluated the role of educational interventions administered by physicians and nurses after the regular instructions in improving the patient’s comprehension and compliance, ultimately improving the quality of bowel preparation, but not in the setting of IBD. In a previous study randomized patients to either a colonoscopy video or a Gastroesophageal reflux related video, those who watched colonoscopy video had a better bowel preparation [24]. In another trial where patients were randomized to a video intervention versus a written instructions group, the bowel preparation was better in the video intervention arm [25]. Similarly, the use of a mobile application improved the bowel preparation in a randomized trial [26]. In contrast, a video about diet before colonoscopy did not improve the bowel preparation [27]. A systematic review of enhanced colonoscopy instructions (booklets, telephone calls, educational videos, and mobile applications) suggested that these may help improve colonoscopy preparation [28]. However, none of these studies focused on IBD patients. IBD is a unique group because these patients are likely to undergo colonoscopy multiple times as part of disease assessment or dysplasia screening. Therefore, we hypothesized that the use of an educational video intervention aimed at educating the participants about the importance of colonoscopy and adequate bowel preparation could help in improving bowel preparation, and patients’ perceptions about the procedure. However, the results of the present trial failed to demonstrate any improvement in bowel preparation as also the experience of colonoscopy in patients with IBD. Our findings suggest that the provision of written educational material in the language of preference of the participants may also have improved the performance from the baseline (non-randomized group).

As explained earlier, the possible reason for a lack of apparent benefit of video intervention could be related to the fact that even the control group was receiving enhanced instructions in the form written instructions in the language of their choice and a contact number for any preparation related issues. This is beyond the usual verbal instructions provided routinely in our setting. The much lower adequacy of bowel preparation in the non-trial patients attests to this being the primary reason behind lack of apparent benefit of video intervention.

The study had some limitations including a low sample size. Although we calculated a priori the sample size, the bowel preparation in control group was less than expected and dropouts more than expected possibly underpowering our study. Further the expected improvement in the intervention arm during calculation of sample size was possibly more optimistic than eventually proven. However, there was no visible trend of benefit of video intervention in any of the outcomes tested. These findings though may not be generalizable as the trial was conducted in a single centre in patient population dominated by ulcerative colitis with low biological use. This trial, therefore, should not be used as conclusive evidence against using video educational intervention especially in situations where detailed written instructions are not provided. The strengths of the study include the focus on IBD as a group and masking of outcome assessors. The study also did not address certain other issues which could play a role in eventual success of bowel preparation including role of caregivers and their education, and additional educational initiatives to address misconceptions and false myths about colonoscopy. Patient satisfaction was also not analyzed in detail but previous data suggest that use of high volume preparation and sedation impact satisfaction adversely [29]. Further, the quality of information and the emotional and practical support received before colonoscopy may positively impact the overall patient experience.

In conclusion, the addition of a brief education video to detailed written instructions about bowel preparation in patients of IBD did not show an improvement in adequacy of the bowel preparation.
