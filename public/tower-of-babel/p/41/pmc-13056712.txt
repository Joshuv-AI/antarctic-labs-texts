PLM-effector: unleashing the potential of protein language models for bacterial secreted protein prediction
Authors: Dandan Zheng, Lihong Chen, Guansong Pang, Jian Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacterial secreted proteins, particularly effectors delivered by specialized secretion systems, are key mediators of virulence and host–pathogen interactions. However, accurate computational identification remains challenging, as many existing methods rely heavily on sequence similarity or handcrafted features, and often focus on a single secretion system. Recent studies have reported that some bacterial effectors may be associated with more than one secretion system, highlighting the complexity of secretion system annotation and motivating the development of system-aware computational prediction approaches. Here, we present PLM-Effector, a hybrid deep learning framework that integrates modern protein language models (PLMs) with multiple neural architectures via a two-layer ensemble stacking strategy. By extracting complementary features from N- and C-terminal regions, PLM-Effector enables secretion-type-aware prediction across five major bacterial secretion systems (T1SS–T4SS and T6SS), with each system modeled independently. Systematic benchmarking shows that embeddings from protein-specific PLMs (ESM-1b, ESM2_t33, ProtT5) are more discriminative than those from general-purpose language models (e.g. BERT, BioBERT). Leveraging these representations, PLM-Effector achieves superior performance on an independent test set, with macro F1-scores of 0.9848, 0.8649, 0.9899, 0.9620, and 0.9728 for secreted proteins of T1SS–T4SS and T6SS, respectively, outperforming existing tools and homology-based baselines. Implemented as an accessible web server (http://www.mgc.ac.cn/PLM-Effector/) with source code and datasets available (https://github.com/zhengdd0422/PLM-Effector/), PLM-Effector provides a reproducible and user-friendly platform for both small-scale and genome-wide secreted protein discovery, facilitating advances in the study of bacterial pathogenesis.

Introduction

Protein secretion is a fundamental biological process in prokaryotic cells, enabling the transport of proteins from the cytoplasm to other cellular compartments, the extracellular environment, or directly into target cells, including both other bacteria and eukaryotic cells, across cellular membranes [1]. To date, nine types of secretion systems (type I to type IX, T1SS to T9SS) have been identified in bacteria, each with distinct structural components, substrate specificities, and functional roles [2, 3]. While some systems are broadly conserved across bacterial species and secrete diverse substrates, others are restricted to specific lineages or dedicated to transporting only a limited set of proteins [1]. Among these, T1SS to T6SS are predominantly found in Gram-negative bacteria. T1SS and T2SS primarily facilitate secretion into the extracellular milieu, whereas T3SS, T4SS, and T6SS enable direct translocation of effector proteins into host cells [4]. These injection systems and their associated effectors (T3SEs, T4SEs, T6SEs) have been extensively investigated for their critical roles in the virulence of various human pathogens, with T6SS also participating in interbacterial competition [5–7]. T5SS, known as the autotransporter system, employs a β-barrel domain to either display proteins on the bacterial surface or release them extracellularly [8]. T7SS was first characterized in Mycobacterium and has since been linked to virulence in several other Gram-positive bacteria [9]. Less common systems include T8SS, involved in Curli fiber formation and biofilm development [10], and T9SS, which is specific to the Bacteroidetes phylum and supports surface adhesion and gliding motility [11].

Effector proteins employ diverse molecular strategies to subvert host cellular processes. For example, Bartonella Beps suppress innate immune signaling through T4SS-mediated delivery [12], Shigella IpgD disrupts T cell migration by depleting membrane PIP2 [13], and Yersinia Yop effectors interfere with cytoskeletal dynamics, block phagocytosis, and trigger host cell apoptosis [14]. This functional versatility underscores the importance of accurately identifying effector proteins. With the growing volume of bacterial genomic data, computational approaches have become essential tools for effector discovery [15]. Public resources such as VFDB [16], SecReT4 [17], SecReT6 [18], and BastionHub [19] support in silico identification by providing systematically curated information on secretion systems and their associated effectors.

Traditional experimental methods for effector identification, including mutagenesis, secretion assays, and mass spectrometry-based proteomics, are informative but often laborious, system-specific, and low-throughput. Meanwhile, sequence similarity-based computational tools frequently fail to detect evolutionarily distant effectors. To overcome these limitations, numerous machine learning (ML) models have been developed for binary classification of specific effector types. Examples include SIEVE [20], EffectiveT3 [21], BEAN2.0 [22], Bastion3 [6], EP3 [23], T3Sepp [24], Effectidor [25], iT3SE-PX [26] and TSE-ARF [27] for T3SEs; and T4EffPred [28], T4SEpre [29], S4TE [30], Bastion4 [5] and T4SEpp [31] for T4SEs; and Bastion6 [32] for T6SEs. More recently, deep learning (DL) approaches based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent neural networks (RNNs), and attention mechanisms have further advanced effector prediction. For instance, T1SEstacker [33] used a stacked model with amino acid composition (AAC) to predict the repeats-in-toxin (RTX) type of T1SEs. ACNNT3 [34] integrated CNN and attention layers with position-specific scoring matrix (PSSM) and one-hot encoding of the N-terminal 100 residues for T3SE prediction. In parallel, protein language models (PLMs) have emerged as powerful tools. PLM-T3SE combined ProtT5 embeddings and PSSM features for T3SE classification [35]. T4SEfinder [36] and T4Seeker [37] adopted TAPE and ESM-1b, respectively, with the latter integrating multiple PLMs, including ESM-1b [38], ProtBert [39], and ProtT5 [40] for T4SE prediction.

Despite these advances, many predictors rooted in traditional ML paradigms rely heavily on handcrafted features derived from predefined biological assumptions, thereby limiting their flexibility and robustness when confronted with atypical or hard-to-classify effector proteins. A major source of misclassification is the difficulty in distinguishing T3SS effectors from flagellar proteins, which share a common evolutionary origin and homologous secretion machineries, leading to overlapping sequence and structural features that confound feature-based classifiers [41, 42]. By contrast, recent DL-based approaches have partially reduced reliance on explicit feature engineering; however, in practice, most available tools are still designed for a single secretion system and frequently lack actively maintained web interfaces, thereby constraining their broader usability. DeepSecE addressed some of these issues by framing effector prediction as a multi-class classification task, fine-tuning the ESM-1b model to distinguish five secretion types (T1SEs–T4SEs, T6SEs) from non-secreted proteins, and providing a user-friendly web platform [43]. Nevertheless, emerging evidence indicates that certain effector proteins can be secreted via multiple systems, suggesting that these categories are not mutually exclusive. For example, the T3SS effectors YopP from Yersinia enterocolitica (Y. enterocolitica) and AvrA from Salmonella typhimurium (S. typhimurium) were also translocated via the Bartonella T4SS, reflecting evolutionary adaptability and cross-system secretion capability [44]. This phenomenon challenges the rigid multi-class categorization used by tools like DeepSecE, which presuppose strict orthogonality among secretion systems. Similarly, T4Seeker treats only T4SEs as positives while grouping T3SEs and T6SEs as negatives, potentially overlooking dual-system effectors. These limitations underscore the need for a more adaptable and systematic prediction framework. Meanwhile, Contrastive-learning of Language Embedding and Biological Features (CLEF) performs independent predictions for multiple secretion systems, including T1SS–T4SS and T6SS, but does not provide a user-friendly web interface [45].

In this work, we introduce PLM-Effector (shown in Fig. 1), a hybrid DL framework designed to overcome the limitations of existing approaches. First, we systematically benchmark a suite of cutting-edge PLMs to identify optimal sequence representations for secretion prediction, moving beyond handcrafted features. Building on established biological evidence, PLM-Effector specifically emphasizes the terminal protein regions, where secretion-related or functional motifs—such as N-terminal signal peptides of T3SEs and C-terminal motifs of T1SE and T4SE substrates—are frequently located [1, 34, 43, 46]. At the same time, accumulating evidence suggests that secretion-related determinants for certain effector classes, such as T6SEs, may extend beyond strictly terminal regions, as T6SS effectors do not possess simple linear secretion signals and are instead delivered via physical association with core structural components of the secretion apparatus [47]. To reflect this biological diversity, we assess terminal representations extracted under different contextual scopes and additionally incorporate structure-informed models to capture non-terminal and global features. Second, we introduce a novel two-layer ensemble stacking architecture that leverages both diverse neural networks and terminal sequence features to enable accurate prediction of secreted proteins across five major secretion systems. Third, we rigorously evaluate the model’s performance in biologically relevant scenarios, including the prediction of effectors with low sequence similarity and the accommodation of potential cross-system secretion events. Finally, to ensure practical utility, we deploy PLM-Effector as an open-access, user-friendly web server, providing a reliable resource for the broader research community.

Overview of PLM-Effector workflow. Protein sequences are preprocessed, and features are separately extracted from their N-terminal and C-terminal regions. These features, including embeddings from multiple pre-trained PLMs, one-hot encoded sequences, and structural representations, are used to train various deep learning architectures. For each secretion type, the optimal feature-model combination for N- and C-terminal regions is identified. The terminal-specific predictions are then integrated via a two-layer ensemble stacking strategy with XGBoost to generate robust, secretion-type-aware secreted protein predictions.

Materials and methods

Data collection and processing

To construct a comprehensive dataset, we collected experimentally validated secreted proteins from Gram-negative bacteria using the recently published framework DeepSecE and augmented it with entries from specialized resources, including BastionHub, SecReT4, SecReT6, and VFDB, to incorporate the most recently updated records. All datasets were downloaded in October 2024 to capture the most recent entries. To ensure data quality, we performed deduplication across overlapping sources, removed sequences containing non-standard amino acids, and filtered out highly homologous sequences using Cluster Database at High Identity with Tolerance (CD-HIT) [48] at a 90% sequence identity threshold. The resulting curated positive set comprised 182 T1SEs, 80 T2SEs, 571 T3SEs, 585 T4SEs, and 327 T6SEs. As negative samples, we used 1727 non-secreted proteins from previous studies [5, 6, 23, 32, 36], consisting of curated non-secreted entries from UniProt and BLASTP-identified homologs from Escherichia coli genomes based on E-values and sequence identity. One protein originally included as a negative was identified as a T4SE through comparison with the positive set and was removed, yielding a final negative set of 1726 proteins.

An independent test set was constructed following the composition used in DeepSecE, containing 19 T1SEs, 10 T2SEs, 30 T3SEs, 30 T4SEs, 20 T6SEs, and 150 non-secreted proteins. Of these, the T3SEs, T4SEs, and T6SEs were sourced directly from the test sets of Bastion3, CNN-T4SE, and Bastion6, respectively, while the T1SEs and T2SEs were randomly sampled from BastionHub. To ensure independence, all training sequences sharing >90% identity with any test sequence were removed using CD-HIT. The remaining 162 T1SEs, 70 T2SEs, 541 T3SEs, 555 T4SEs, 307 T6SEs, and 1576 non-secreted proteins were used for five-fold cross-validation. In addition, CD-HIT with sequence identity thresholds of 60% and 40% was further applied to evaluate the model’s predictive performance on remote homologs. A complete summary of dataset statistics under different redundancy thresholds is provided in Supplementary Table S1. To characterize the composition of the negative dataset, subcellular localization of non-secreted proteins was predicted using DeepLocPro [49]. Cytoplasmic proteins accounted for 59.5% in the training set and 52.0% in the independent test set, with the remaining proteins predicted in non-cytoplasmic compartments, including cytoplasmic membrane, outer membrane, periplasm, and extracellular space (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Table S2). SignalP 6.0 [50] analysis further indicated that 13.3% of training and 20.0% of independent test negative proteins contained predicted signal peptides, whereas the majority lacked detectable secretion signals (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Table S2). These results demonstrate that the negative dataset is not dominated by easily distinguishable cytoplasmic proteins, but instead comprises a heterogeneous set of challenging non-secreted proteins, reducing the risk that model performance is driven by trivial localization cues.

Pre-trained language models

Pre-trained language models have become powerful tools in computational biology, capable of extracting meaningful representations from amino acid sequences to support downstream tasks such as secretion prediction [43]. In this work, we leverage six pre-trained language models, including BERT [51], ProtBERT, BioBERT [52], ESM-1b, ESM2_t33 [53], and ProtT5, to generate frozen embeddings without fine-tuning on our training data. Specifically, BERT is a transformer-based model originally developed for natural language processing. Its base architecture includes 12 transformer layers with 12 self-attention heads each, totaling 110 million parameters. It was pre-trained on large-scale text corpora, including BookCorpus and English Wikipedia, using a masked language modeling (MLM) objective. BioBERT is a variant of BERT, further pre-trained on biomedical text from PubMed abstracts and PMC full-text articles, providing a foundation for understanding biological contexts. ProtBERT is another BERT-derived model specifically pre-trained on UniRef100, a comprehensive protein sequence database containing over 217 million entries. While the architecture is similar to BERT, it is optimized for protein-specific tasks such as residue-level prediction and sequence infilling. ESM-1b, introduced by Meta AI, is a large-scale PLM pre-trained on UniRef50 using an MLM objective. It employs a 33-layer transformer architecture with roughly 650 million parameters, enabling superior capture of evolutionary and structural information from protein sequences. ESM-2 represents an evolution of ESM-1b, trained on expanded datasets and incorporating optimized transformer layers and training techniques. It is available in multiple sizes, ranging from 650 million to over 1 billion parameters, offering improved capacity for modeling complex protein relationships and functional properties. In this study, we use the 650-million-parameter version of ESM2_t33, which balances representational power with computational efficiency. ProtT5 is built upon the T5-3B architecture and adopts a text-to-text transfer learning framework. Pre-trained on UniRef50 with a denoising objective, it formulates all tasks as sequence-to-sequence problems, providing flexibility across both generative and discriminative applications. With 3 billion parameters, ProtT5 effectively captures intricate, high-level protein features.

We sequentially employed these six pre-trained models as protein sequence encoders to generate embeddings. Prior to tokenization, input sequences were truncated to a maximum of 512 tokens for BERT, BioBERT, ProtBERT, ProtT5, and to 1024 tokens for ESM-1b and ESM2_t33, in accordance with the typical context limits of each model. The resulting embedding dimensions are 768 for BERT, BioBERT, and ProtBERT; 1024 for ProtT5; and 1280 for ESM-1b and ESM2_t33. Protein graph construction

Protein graphs were constructed using ESMFold (esmfold_v1) [53], with residue contacts defined by Cα distances <10 Å forming the adjacency matrix. Node features were encoded using either pre-trained language model embeddings or one-hot encodings, evaluated across multiple sequence lengths (full or terminal 100 residues) and structural contexts. Detailed procedures are available in the Supplementary Methods.

DL classification models

To classify protein sequences based on their primary structure, we employed frozen embeddings derived from pre-trained language models. These contextualized residue-level representations served as input features for four DL classifiers: CNN, a CNN enhanced with an attention mechanism (CNN-Attention), a multilayer perceptron (MLP), and a self-attention-based model (Attention). Each model was trained independently as a binary classifier to distinguish secreted from non-secreted proteins across each secretion system type. We also implemented a one-hot encoding representation combined with a CNN model to assess its effectiveness in protein classification. To evaluate the impact of sequence length, multiple input lengths were tested during model training. For structure-based classification, we constructed protein graphs and applied graph convolutional networks (GCNs) to iteratively aggregate features from adjacent residues. This allowed the model to capture both local and global topological patterns. A global pooling operation was applied after graph convolution to generate graph-level embeddings, which were then passed through a fully connected layer for binary classification. All models were trained using the Adam optimizer, except for the GCN, for which we used AdamW due to its improved handling of weight decay in large parameter spaces. Hyperparameters including layer depth, hidden dimensions, kernel size, dropout rate, and learning rate were extensively tuned via grid search. The optimal configurations for all models are summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

Traditional ML classifiers

To evaluate the discriminative capacity of embeddings from pre-trained language models, we applied four traditional classifiers, including logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), and extreme gradient boosting (XGB), to embeddings generated by six pre-trained models. All classifiers were implemented with default hyperparameters and were evaluated separately for every secretion system to distinguish secreted from non-secreted proteins. Additionally, XGBoost served as the meta-classifier in our two-layer stacking architecture, with optimized hyperparameters provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Stacking ensemble construction

Ensemble stacking [54], a widely recognized strategy for improving generalization and stabilizing predictions, was adopted to develop PLM-Effector, integrating top-performing DL classifiers driven by embeddings from superior pre-trained language models. Protein representations were derived from both the N- and C-terminal regions to capture complementary localization signals relevant to secretion. PLM-Effector follows a two-stage workflow. In the first stage, out-of-fold (OOF) predictions were generated for each base model via five-fold cross-validation on the training set. Class probability outputs from all folds were aggregated into a complete OOF set per model, forming robust meta-features that reduce overfitting. These OOF predictions from all top model-feature combinations were concatenated into a unified meta-feature matrix. In the second stage, an XGBoost meta-classifier was trained on this matrix to learn optimal combination weights across base learners. During inference, each input sequence is processed by all base models, with predictions averaged per model. The resulting outputs are assembled into a feature matrix and fed into the pre-trained XGBoost meta-learner to generate the final secretion type prediction.

Performance assessment

We trained all models using five-fold cross-validation, where the training data were split into training and validation subsets at a 4:1 ratio, and evaluated final performance on a held-out independent test set. Reported results represent the average performance across the five folds. Results and discussion

Comparative analysis of pre-trained embeddings on N-terminal regions using model-specific maximum length

To evaluate the discriminative power of protein embeddings, we first analyzed N-terminal representations from six pre-trained language models. t-SNE visualization (Supplementary Fig. S2) revealed clear differences in feature separation: general language embeddings from BERT and BioBERT showed substantial overlap between secreted and non-secreted classes, whereas protein-specific embeddings (ESM-1b, ESM2_t33, ProtBert, ProtT5) formed distinct clusters, suggesting more biologically meaningful representations.

Five-fold cross-validation (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Table S4) based on N-terminal embeddings showed that embedding type had a stronger effect on classification performance than the underlying DL architecture. This effect was particularly evident for T2SEs–T6SEs, where BERT and BioBERT performed markedly worse, while protein-specific embeddings consistently achieved superior results across evaluation metrics. On the independent test set (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Table S5), ESM-1b, ESM2_t33, and ProtT5 further demonstrated robust generalization across all secretion types. The F1-score river plots (Fig. 2C and 2D) reinforced these findings, showing that although different architectures yielded minor performance variations on the same embedding, their overall trajectories were highly consistent, with ESM-1b, ESM2_t33, and ProtT5 emerging as the most stable and effective representations. ProtBert performed strongly in cross-validation and retained high test accuracy on T1SEs and T4SEs but exhibited a marked decline in T2SEs, T3SEs, and T6SEs across all architectures. This suggests that ProtBert embeddings may capture secretion mechanism-specific features rather than broadly generalizable patterns. The best macro F1-scores on the independent test for each secretion type were achieved by specific embedding-architecture combinations: ProtBert with Attention for T1SEs (0.9791), ESM-1b with Attention for T2SEs (0.8276), ESM2_t33 with MLP for T3SEs (0.9631), and CNN with ProtBert or ESM2_t33 for T4SEs (0.9673) and T6SEs (0.9345). To further assess the utility of N-terminal embeddings, we applied traditional ML algorithms to the same features. Across all secretion types, models trained on protein-specific embeddings consistently outperformed those using general language embeddings (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7), confirming that protein-focused pre-trained models yield more reliable and generalizable predictions.

Generalization analysis on the independent test set showed that F1-scores for T1SEs, T3SEs, and T6SEs were largely consistent with cross-validation results, whereas T2SEs decreased by ~8%, reflecting their classification difficulty. Interestingly, T4SEs showed a ~5% improvement, raising potential concerns about data leakage. To rule this out, we applied a stricter CD-HIT filtering protocol at 0.6 and 0.4 identity thresholds to reduce redundancy both within the training set and against the independent test set (Supplementary Table S8). Re-evaluation using the best-performing embedding-architecture combinations revealed minimal performance decreases at the 0.6 threshold, with cross-validation macro F1-score reduced by <1.3% and independent test by <1.5%, confirming that the model captures generalizable discriminative features beyond simple sequence similarity. Under the more stringent 0.4 threshold, cross-validation and independent test macro F1-score showed modest decreases, with the largest drops observed for T2SE (4.1% decrease in cross-validation) and T1SE (5.8% decrease in the independent test), primarily due to their relatively smaller training sets (Supplementary Table S1), while performance for the other classes remained stable. Overall, the independent test macro F1 scores at the 0.4 threshold were T1SE 0.9210, T2SE 0.8134, T3SE 0.9542, T4SE 0.9395, and T6SE 0.9110, demonstrating that the N-terminal models retain strong generalization ability even under strict redundancy reduction.

Comparative analysis of pre-trained embeddings on C-terminal regions using model-specific maximum length

The C-terminal regions are known to harbor essential secretion signals or localization motifs, particularly in T1SEs and T4SEs [1]. To preserve this critical C-terminal information in long sequences and avoid impairing classification performance, we specifically extracted features from the C-terminal regions of each sample. Five-fold cross-validation showed that, across all DL architectures, C-terminal features derived from ESM embeddings (ESM-1b and ESM2_t33) performed nearly identically to their N-terminal counterparts (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table S9). Consistent patterns were observed on the independent test set (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table S10), mirroring the N-terminal trends (Supplementary Table S5): protein-specific embeddings from ESM and ProtT5 consistently outperformed general-purpose BERT variants across all secretion types. Focusing on macro F1-scores, C-terminal features achieved performance comparable to the N-terminal embeddings, with best results of 0.9848 for T1SEs (CNN-Attention+ESM-1b), 0.8292 for T2SEs (CNN + ESM-1b), 0.9579 for T3SEs (Attention+ESM2_t33), 0.9546 for T4SEs (CNN-Attention+ESM2_t33), and 0.9324 for T6SEs (CNN-Attention+ ProtT5). These values closely matched those obtained with N-terminal embeddings (0.9791, 0.8276, 0.9631, 0.9673, and 0.9345), indicating that both terminal regions capture comparable discriminative information for secretion-type classification. Besides, we also evaluated stricter CD-HIT thresholds on C-terminal segments (Supplementary Table S8). Consistent with N-terminal results, model generalization decreased only slightly under these stricter thresholds. Even at the 0.4 threshold, independent test macro F1-score remained high (T1SE: 0.9765, T2SE: 0.7773, T3SE: 0.9155, T4SE: 0.9241, T6SE: 0.9154), indicating that the models retain strong predictive power for novel effectors and are not solely reliant on memorizing sequences with high identity.

Comparative analysis of model-specific maximum-length and 100-residue terminal features

To investigate why N- and C-terminal features showed comparable performance, we first examined sequence-length distributions. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S3, most protein sequences were shorter than 1000 amino acids, leading to substantial overlap between N- and C-terminal regions in many cases, particularly for models with maximum input lengths around 1022 residues (e.g. ESM). To evaluate whether shorter terminal fragments retain sufficient discriminative power, we extracted features from the first and last 100 residues of each sequence. For each DL architecture, only the single best-performing embedding identified in Supplementary Tables S5 and S10 was used to generate these 100-residue terminal features. Five-fold cross-validation (Supplementary Table S11) showed that both N- and C-terminal 100-residue features maintained robust predictive capability. Compared to maximum-length features (Supplementary Tables S4 and S9), only minor precision reductions were observed for a subset of models and secretion types, such as ESM-1b with MLP on T6SEs (Fig. 4A and 4B). Independent test results (Supplementary Table S12) revealed similar mild declines when compared with maximum-length features (Supplementary Tables S5 and S10) for N-terminal features in T1SEs, T2SEs and T6SEs, whereas T3SEs and T4SEs remained stable (Fig. 4C). By contrast, C-terminal 100-residue features exhibited more pronounced degradation, particularly in Attention architectures, for instance, ESM-1b on T1SEs showed up to a 26.8% precision decrease (Fig. 4D). These findings suggest that longer sequence context preserves additional informative signals, and C-terminal-derived features are more sensitive to truncation. Despite these declines, the best N-terminal 100-residue embeddings still outperformed their C-terminal counterparts, with average macro F1-scores of 0.9676, 0.8162, 0.9540, 0.9691, and 0.9212 for T1SEs, T2SEs, T3SEs, T4SEs, and T6SEs, respectively. Although broadly comparable to the maximum-length embeddings (N-terminal: 0.9791, 0.8276, 0.9631, 0.9673, 0.9345; C-terminal: 0.9848, 0.8292, 0.9579, 0.9546, 0.9324), the slightly higher and more stable performance of the maximum-length features supports their use in the final stacking ensemble model.

Graph neural network models for protein secretion classification incorporating structural features

To evaluate whether structural information could enhance secretion classification, we trained residue-level GCN models integrating contact maps with sequence-derived embeddings (Supplementary Methods). Graphs were constructed using top-performing embeddings (Supplementary Tables S5 and S10): ProtBert (T1SEs, T4SEs), ESM-1b (T2SEs) and ESM2_t33 (T3SEs, T6SEs) for N-terminal regions; ESM-1b (T1SEs, T2SEs), ESM2_t33 (T3SEs, T4SEs) and ProtT5 (T6SEs) for C-terminal regions. In five-fold cross-validation (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Table S13), full-pdb_maxlen graphs showed minimal differences (<1.4%) between N- and C-terminal features, and full-pdb_100 and terminal-pdb_100 graphs performed comparably (<2.3% difference). Independent testing showed slightly higher variation (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Table S14). Performance varied by secretion type: T1SEs achieved the highest macro F1-score using C-terminal full-pdb_maxlen (0.9816), T2SEs using N-terminal terminal-pdb_100 (0.7885), T3SEs with N-terminal full-pdb_maxlen (0.9536), T4SEs with N-terminal full-pdb_100 (0.9600), and T6SEs with N-terminal full-pdb_maxlen (0.9454). Residue contact maps were constructed using a Cα–Cα distance cutoff of 10 Å. To evaluate the sensitivity of GCN models to this parameter, we also tested cutoffs of 8 Å and 12 Å (Supplementary Fig. S4). Across all secretion systems, five-fold cross-validation showed only marginal differences among the three thresholds, indicating that model fitting on the training set is largely insensitive to the distance cutoff. In contrast, clearer differences appeared on the independent test set. For T6SE, the 10 Å threshold consistently achieved the highest MCC, ~11.2% higher than at 8 Å and about 8.8% higher than at 12 Å. A similar pattern was observed for T1SE, where MCC at 10 Å exceeded that at 8 Å by 4.2%. For the remaining secretion systems, differences between these cutoffs were minor (generally <2.9%), with no consistent advantage for either extreme. In contrast, models using one-hot encodings performed substantially worse (Supplementary Tables S13 and S14), with 8%–17% lower macro F1-score and 12%–34% lower MCC on independent testing, highlighting the advantage of PLM embeddings for structure-informed classification.

However, incorporating contact map-based structural information into GCNs did not yield substantial improvement over using maximum-length PLM embeddings in standard DL models (N-terminal: 0.9791, 0.8276, 0.9631, 0.9673, 0.9345; C-terminal: 0.9848, 0.8292, 0.9579, 0.9546, 0.9324). This limited gain likely reflects several factors. First, secreted effectors are highly structurally heterogeneous, encompassing adhesins, toxins, and enzymes [16]. Second, many effectors contain intrinsically disordered or flexible regions that remain unfolded prior to interacting with host targets [55]. Such regions are difficult to model accurately with predicted static structures (e.g. ESMFold), meaning the resulting contact maps may not reflect the functional conformations of effectors. In contrast, PLMs can capture sequence patterns associated with structural tendencies and functional motifs, providing effective embeddings even for disordered regions [53]. Together, these observations explain why structural graphs provided little additional benefit and support the use of PLM-based stacking ensembles as the primary predictive framework.

CNN models trained on one-hot encoding sequences

To evaluate whether DL models could compensate for the absence of pre-trained embeddings, we trained CNN classifiers directly on one-hot encoded sequences, exploring multiple length thresholds at both N- and C-terminal regions. In five-fold cross-validation (Supplementary Table S15 and Fig. S5A), T1SEs consistently achieved over 90% across all metrics, regardless of terminal region. In contrast, other secretion types generally showed sensitivities below 80%, with only minor variations across different sequence lengths. Independent test set (Supplementary Table S16 and Supplementary Fig. S5B) further reduced performance for both N- and C-terminal inputs, with most macro F1-scores falling below 90%. These results indicate that, even after optimizing sequence length, CNNs trained on one-hot encoded sequences are substantially less effective than models using pre-trained embeddings for bacterial secreted protein classification. Consequently, we did not adopt the one-hot CNN approach for our final predictive model.

PLM-Effector via a stacking ensemble of terminal PLM embeddings

To enhance prediction stability and generalization, we developed PLM-Effector, a two-layer stacking ensemble integrating the best-performing classifiers and embeddings from both N- and C-terminal regions (Fig. 1). For each secretion type, six to eight top-performing combinations of features and DL models were selected to construct PLM-Effector (Supplementary Table S17). OOF probability estimates served as input features for an XGBoost meta-classifier, with class-specific thresholds optimized on the training data (0.8 for T1SEs, 0.7 for T2SEs and T3SEs, 0.6 for T4SEs, and 0.5 for T6SEs). With these configurations, PLM-Effector achieved high macro F1-scores of 0.9914, 0.9847, 0.9693, 0.9343, and 0.9821 for each secretion type on the training dataset. On the independent test set, it performed competitively with the best single models from individual folds (Table 1). For T1SEs, it matched the top N- and C-terminal models (macro F1-score of 0.9848). For T2SEs, T3SEs, and T6SEs, it outperformed individual models, achieving macro F1-scores of 0.8649, 0.9899, and 0.9728, respectively, reflecting robust predictive accuracy with balanced sensitivity and specificity. Although a slight decrease was observed for T4SEs (−2.8%) compared with the best single model, PLM-Effector delivers more stable performance across secretion types by aggregating models to reduce variance, maintain low bias, and leverage complementary strengths while avoiding overfitting from single-fold selection [54].

To facilitate broad application, PLM-Effector has been implemented as an online prediction platform (http://www.mgc.ac.cn/PLM-Effector/), providing a user-friendly interface for effector prediction. Runtime benchmarking (Supplementary Fig. S6) showed that processing 200 sequences requires ~12–17 minutes. Comparison with other existing state-of-the-art methods

To evaluate generalization and performance, PLM-Effector was benchmarked against representative state-of-the-art tools employing diverse strategies: BastionX (Bastion3, Bastion4, and Bastion6) relies on hand-crafted features, T3SEpp and T4SEpp integrate homology-based similarity search, and T4SEfinder leverages pre-trained language models. Other earlier methods solely on hand-crafted features were excluded, as they have been systematically benchmarked by these tools. Other recent approaches, including T1SEstacker, EP3, DeepT3, PLM-T3SE, and ACNNT3, were not considered due to the lack of publicly accessible web servers or downloadable software.

As shown in Table 2 PLM-Effector consistently outperformed all evaluated methods across secretion systems in terms of macro F1-score and MCC, reflecting balanced predictive performance between sensitivity and specificity. For DeepSecE, all predictions not assigned to a specific type were treated as negatives. This approach may underestimate its performance, as it is inherently a multi-class model, potentially explaining its poor results on T1SEs and T6SEs. These results demonstrate that PLM-Effector accurately predicts multiple secretion types and offers broad applicability for effector protein identification, without relying on type-specific hand-crafted features.

Comparison with homology-based baseline predictor

To establish a performance baseline, we implemented a homology-based predictor using BLASTP, as provided by the Blast+ software [56], by searching each positive sequence in the independent test set against the positive sequences in the training set. A sequence was assigned a positive label if its top hit (i.e. lowest E-value) in the training set had an E-value <0.05 (Supplementary Table S18). This homology-based baseline was then evaluated on the same independent test set and compared with PLM-Effector (Supplementary Table S19). PLM-Effector consistently outperformed the homology-based method across all secretion systems, achieving accuracies of 100.0% versus 84.2% for T1SEs, 90.0% versus 70.0% for T2SEs, 96.7% versus 46.7% for T3SEs, and 100.0% versus 66.7% for T4SEs, while both methods reached 100.0% accuracy for T6SEs. When a more stringent BLAST E-value threshold of 1 × 10−5 was applied, accuracies decreased for T1SEs (78.9%), T2SEs (50.0%), and T4SE (63.3%), whereas the results for T3SEs and T6SEs remained unchanged. This decline is expected, as stricter E-value cutoffs reduce sensitivity to remote homologs, thereby limiting the coverage of homology-based approaches. This substantial performance gap observed for T1SEs–T4SEs underscores PLM-Effector’s ability to capture discriminative features that support the identification of distantly homologous effectors, a scenario in which conventional homology-based approaches show limited sensitivity.

Case study

To evaluate the predictive scalability and robustness of PLM-Effector, we conducted a case study using recently verified effectors from T3SS, T4SS, and T6SS. The effectors included: TccP4, a novel T3SE from Escherichia albertii 1551–2 that recruits F-actin during attaching and effacing lesion formation [57]; NpeA, a Brucella abortus T4SE that targets neural Wiskott–Aldrich syndrome protein (N-WASP) to promote intracellular niche formation [58]; TseR, a trans-kingdom T6SE from Yersinia pseudotuberculosis that targets both bacterial and eukaryotic cells to enhance virulence [59]; and TseVs, a Vibrio-specific T6SE that disrupts bacterial bioenergetics to reshape microbial competition [60]. None of these effectors were included in our training dataset, and all exhibit low sequence similarity to proteins in the training set (Supplementary Fig. S7A). PLM-Effector successfully identified TccP4 as a T3SE, and TseR and TseVs as T6SEs, with ensemble scores of 0.9992, 0.9992, and 0.9964, respectively (Supplementary Table S20). However, PLM-Effector failed to classify NpeA as a T4SE. To investigate this case, we conducted an attention weight analysis using the final layer of the underlying ESM2-t33 model (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Neither the average attention heatmap nor the per-residue attention profile showed pronounced enrichment within the reported N-WASP-binding short linear motif (residues 125–133). Quantitative comparisons across specific regions indicated that the motif region received lower mean attention than the terminal regions and the global sequence average. These observations suggest, based on current attention analyses, that the misclassification of NpeA may be associated with the model not specifically attending to this short functional motif. Notably, such short linear motif-driven interactions represent a relatively rare subclass of effectors, whereas the majority of known secretion signals span broader sequence contexts that are effectively captured by PLM-Effector. Future model development could incorporate explicit modeling of short linear motifs to improve predictions for this subclass of effectors.

Besides, emerging evidence suggests that effector secretion systems are not mutually exclusive, as exemplified by the translocation of T3SS effectors YopP (Y. enterocolitica) and AvrA (S. typhimurium) through the heterologous Bartonella T4SS. When evaluated using the T4SE-specific PLM-Effector classifier, both YopP and AvrA were predicted as positive (Supplementary Table S21), despite their well-established classification as T3SS effectors and their low sequence similarity to known T4SEs in the training set (Supplementary Table S18). However, we emphasize that, within our binary and independently trained classification framework, such predictions should not be interpreted as direct evidence of dual secretion or mechanistic cross-system signaling. Rather, these results indicate that certain effectors may share sequence-derived features that are recognized by multiple system-specific classifiers. Experimental validation would be required to determine whether such features correspond to bona fide secretion signals for multiple systems.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have developed PLM-Effector, a robust stacking framework that leverages PLMs and ensemble learning to accurately predict bacterial secreted proteins across five major systems. Our comprehensive benchmarking demonstrates that embeddings from models like ESM-1b, ESM2_t33, and ProtT5 effectively capture discriminative features, with terminal regions providing critical signals for classification. Interestingly, incorporating structural information via graph neural networks provided limited gains, underscoring the capacity of modern PLMs to infer functional determinants directly from sequence. PLM-Effector achieves robust performance, outperforming existing methods while providing a flexible prediction framework that avoids enforcing strict mutual exclusivity among secretion system categories and allowing users to explore potential overlaps that warrant further experimental investigation. Due to Graphics Processing Unit (GPU) memory constraints, we relied on frozen embeddings in this study, but future work will explore fine-tuning or lightweight adaptation strategies to further enhance performance. As a publicly accessible web server and open-source code, PLM-Effector lowers the barrier for high-throughput effector discovery. Future work will focus on incorporating additional secretion systems and integrating functional motif analysis to further decipher the mechanisms of bacterial virulence.
