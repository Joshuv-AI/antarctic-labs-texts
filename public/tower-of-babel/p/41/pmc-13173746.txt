NSDHL as a regulator of lipid peroxidation and ER stress–mediated apoptosis in ovarian cancer
Authors: Xu Shanshan, Ran Jinshi, Cai Wanlin, Liu Yuwan, Jin Hanye, Yang Shizhou, Fei Weidong, Zheng Huimei, Xi Yongmei, Lu Weiguo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Elevated expression of NAD(P)-dependent steroid dehydrogenase-like (NSDHL), a key enzyme in cholesterol biosynthesis, is associated with ovarian cancer progression and poor prognosis. However, the mechanisms underlying its tumor-promoting effects remain unclear. Here, we show that high NSDHL expression correlates with adverse clinical outcomes, particularly in aggressive subtypes such as high-grade serous ovarian cancer. NSDHL knockdown in cultured ovarian cancer cells reduced viability, triggered lipid peroxidation, and disrupted cholesterol and redox homeostasis, accompanied by elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS). Quantitative proteomic profiling revealed broad alterations in lipid metabolism, including suppression of fatty acid oxidation and PPARγ signaling, with upregulation of ACSL4 and downregulation of ACSL3. This shift toward polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) enrichment enhanced susceptibility to oxidative stress but did not induce ferroptosis, owing to reduced intracellular iron levels. Notably, this paradoxical increase in lipid peroxidation despite iron deficiency contrasts with the classical ferroptosis model, in which iron is required to initiate and propagate lipid peroxidation, suggesting that NSDHL depletion uncouples oxidative membrane damage from canonical ferroptotic execution. Instead, apoptosis was mediated by lipid peroxidation–induced endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, as confirmed by upregulation of ER stress markers and activation of ER-specific caspases. Importantly, NSDHL depletion suppressed tumor growth and promoted ER stress–mediated apoptosis in ovarian cancer xenografts. These findings identify NSDHL as a key regulator of lipid metabolism and oxidative stress in ovarian cancer and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00777-5.

Introduction

Altered lipid metabolism is increasingly recognized not merely as a bystander but as a driving force in tumor progression, metastasis, and resistance to therapy. A hallmark of cancer cells is the accumulation of intracellular cholesterol and enrichment of cholesterol-rich lipid rafts, which distinguish them from their non-tumorigenic counterparts [1]. This cholesterol remodeling is not incidental. It regulates membrane fluidity and integrity, orchestrates oncogenic signaling, and shapes interactions within the tumor microenvironment, all of which collectively enhance malignant growth and dissemination [2]. High cholesterol levels have been implicated in promoting cancer cell proliferation and metastasis by activating lipid raft-associated signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK [1]. Importantly, clinical and experimental evidence indicates that statin-mediated inhibition of cholesterol biosynthesis exerts anti-tumor effects [3]. Cholesterol-metabolizing enzymes may represent key vulnerabilities, yet their roles in ovarian cancer are poorly understood, warranting further study for therapeutic development.

Among these enzymes, NAD(P)-dependent steroid dehydrogenase-like (NSDHL) has emerged as a particularly intriguing candidate. NSDHL is a key component of cholesterol synthesis, catalyzing the NAD+-dependent oxidative decarboxylation of 4α-carboxysterols [4]. NSDHL localizes to the ER membrane and lipid droplets, where it contributes to cholesterol production and lipid storage [5]. Mutations in NSDHL cause congenital hemidysplasia with ichthyosiform erythroderma and limb defects (CHILD syndrome) [6–9]. Recent studies have identified NSDHL among six cholesterol biosynthesis enzymes markedly upregulated in aggressive cancers, including breast and lung [10], implicating it in tumor progression. Knockdown of NSDHL reduces intracellular cholesterol and inhibits proliferation in skin [11], pancreatic [12], and breast cancers [10, 13, 14], suggesting its broader role in tumor survival. However, the functional significance and mechanisms of NSDHL in ovarian cancer remain unclear.

Ovarian cancer is the most lethal gynecological malignancy with poor prognosis, high recurrence, and limited therapeutic options. Although most patients initially respond to cytoreductive surgery plus platinum-based chemotherapy, resistance frequently develops, resulting in relapse and a five-year survival rate of ~ 30% [15]. Ovarian cancer is highly heterogeneous, comprising multiple histological subtypes such as high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), clear cell, endometrioid, mucinous, and low-grade serous ovarian carcinomas [16]. Among these, HGSOC is the most aggressive and associated with particularly poor outcomes [17]. Elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying ovarian cancer progression is thus essential to identify effective therapeutic targets.

Cancer cells undergo extensive metabolic reprogramming to sustain proliferation under stress, altering lipid metabolism, glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, and glutaminolysis, and redox homeostasis [18]. This reprogramming increases reactive oxygen species (ROS), mainly from mitochondria and the ER [19]. Excessive ROS accumulation induces lipid peroxidation, primary targeting polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) because of their susceptibility to oxidative attack [20]. The ratio of PUFAs to monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) is central to oxidative stress sensitivity [21, 22]. ACSL3 and ACSL4, two long-chain acyl-CoA synthetases, differ in fatty acid specificity, thereby influencing MUFA-CoA versus PUFA-CoA incorporation into membranes [23, 24]. PUFA-rich ER phospholipids are highly vulnerable to oxidative damage. To counter this, cancer cells adaptively remodel their membranes by enriching MUFAs over PUFAs, reducing susceptibility to lipid peroxidation-induced cell death (lipotoxicity) [25]. Disruption of lipid homeostasis impairs cholesterol transporters and signaling cascades, exacerbating oxidative stress and driving tumor progression.

Under stressors such as lipid peroxidation, glucose deprivation, calcium dysregulation, of glycosylation inhibition, or free radical exposure, unfolded proteins accumulate in the ER, triggering ER stress. The unfolded protein response (UPR) is then activated to restore protein folding, primarily via ER chaperones such as GRP78 (BiP) [26, 27]. While transient ER stress supports survival, prolonged stress induces apoptosis [28]. ER stress-mediated apoptosis involves caspase-12 (CASP12) activation at the ER membrane, which cleaves and activates pro-caspase-9 (PRO-CASP9), ultimately activating caspase-3 (CASP3) and driving apoptosis independently of the mitochondrial pathway [27, 29].

In this study, we show that NSDHL expression is markedly elevated in aggressive ovarian cancer subtypes and correlates with poorer clinical outcomes. NSDHL knockdown in HGSOC cells disrupted lipid homeostasis, increased lipid peroxidation and altered MUFA - PUFA metabolism through dysregulation of ACSL3 and ACSL4. Lipid peroxidation further triggered ER stress-induced apoptosis. Importantly, NSDHL inhibition suppressed ovarian tumor growth in xenograft mouse models. Together, these findings identify NSDHL as a promising therapeutic target in ovarian cancer, particularly in subtypes with dysregulated lipid metabolism and heightened oxidative stress.

Results

NSDHL overexpression correlates with poor prognosis in ovarian cancer

Our previous study showed that NSDHL expression was significantly elevated in ovarian cancer spheroids (3AO spheroids) compared with their parental 3AO cell line (Fig. 1A) [30]. To assess NSDHL expression in clinical specimens, we analyzed data from the Oncomine database, which revealed significant upregulation of NSDHL across 29 cancer types, including ovarian cancer (supplementary Fig. 1A). We next examined its clinical relevance using the Kaplan-Meier Plotter, which demonstrated that high NSDHL mRNA levels were significantly associated with shorter progression-free survival (PFS) in ovarian cancer patients (Fig. 1B, supplementary Fig. 1B). Analysis of the Welsh Ovarian Statistics dataset consistently confirmed higher NSDHL mRNA expression in ovarian cancer tissues compared with normal ovarian tissues (P < 0.001, Fig. 1C). Western blotting further validated increased protein expression in ovarian cancer tissues relative to normal ovarian epithelium (Fig. 1D).

To explore the relationship between NSDHL expression and clinicopathological features, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) on tumor samples from 130 ovarian cancer patients. NSDHL staining was markedly stronger in high-stage tumors compared with low-stage cases (Fig. 1E). Stratified analysis revealed that high NSDHL expression was significantly correlated with poor PFS (P = 0.014) and overall survival (OS, P = 0.005) (Fig. 1F and G). High NSDHL expression was detected in 57.95% (51/88) of high-grade tumors versus 40.47% (17/42) of low-grade tumors (P = 0.047), and was also associated with elevated CA125 levels (P = 0.018). Although NSDHL expression appeared higher in advanced-stage (III/IV) compared to early-stage (I/II) ovarian cancer patients, this difference was not statistically significant (median IHC score: 54.5 vs. 45.2, p = 0.240) (Table 1). These findings indicated elevated NSDHL expression to be associated with poor clinical outcomes in ovarian cancer.

The relationship between NSDHL expression and clinicopathological parameters in ovarian cancer

To further investigate the molecular basis of NSDHL function, we compared its expression in normal ovarian cells and multiple ovarian cancer cell lines (A2780, SK-OV-3 and OVCAR-5). NSDHL expression was markedly increased in HGSOC cell lines relative to normal ovarian cells, with SK-OV-3 and OVCAR-5 exhibiting the highest levels (Fig. 1H and I). These two cell lines were therefore selected for subsequent mechanistic studies.

NSDHL knockdown induces ROS accumulation and iron-independent lipid peroxidation

To investigate the functional consequences of NSDHL knockdown in ovarian cancer cells, we employed small interfering RNAs (siRNA-1, siRNA-2) targeting NSDHL in SK-OV-3 and OVCAR-5 cells. Knockdown significantly reduced cell viability of both lines (Fig. 2A and B). Concomitantly, intracellular ROS levels were markedly increased in NSDHL-deficient cells (Fig. 2C). To test whether oxidative stress mediated this cytotoxicity, we treated NSDHL-knockdown cells (NSDHL-KD) with the lipid peroxidation inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1). Fer-1 treatment restored viability, indicating that lipid peroxidation contributed substantially to cell death following NSDHL knockdown (Fig. 2D).

To examine the source of ROS accumulation, we assessed lipid peroxidation using a C11-BODIPY assay. NSDHL-KD cells exhibited significantly higher lipid peroxidation than controls (Fig. 2E and F). As lipid peroxidation is typically initiated by molecular oxygen and amplified by Fe²⁺, a hallmark of ferroptosis [31], we initially hypothesized that NSDHL knockdown might trigger ferroptosis. However, deferoxamine (DFO) failed to restore viability following erastin treatment in either siNC control cells or NSDHL-depleted cells (Fig. 2G). Thus, under our experimental conditions, erastin-induced cell death in SK-OV-3 and OVCAR-5 cells cannot be interpreted as canonical iron-dependent ferroptosis. In this context, erastin was used as a ferroptosis-related system Xc⁻ (the cystine/glutamate antiporter) inhibitor and a ferroptosis-related perturbation rather than as definitive evidence of canonical ferroptotic death. Intracellular iron quantification consistently revealed a significant reduction in both ferrous (Fe²⁺) and total iron levels in NSDHL-KD cells relative to controls (Fig. 2H), indicating that the enhanced lipid peroxidation observed after NSDHL depletion occurs in an iron-restricted state rather than through classical ferroptotic execution.

To further determine whether iron availability regulates ferroptotic competence, we generated IREB2-overexpressing SK-OV-3 and OVCAR-5 cells to enhance iron uptake. IREB2 overexpression increased sensitivity to erastin, and this effect became reversible by DFO treatment (supplementary Fig. 2), indicating that elevated intracellular iron restores DFO-sensitive ferroptotic responsiveness in these ovarian cancer models. By contrast, under baseline conditions, NSDHL-KD cells remained refractory to DFO rescue despite marked oxidative stress. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that intracellular iron availability is a key determinant of ferroptotic competence, and that reduced iron levels in NSDHL-KD cells limit canonical ferroptosis, with cell death proceeding instead through iron-restricted lipid peroxidation.

NSDHL knockdown impairs lipid metabolism and redox balance

We performed quantitative proteomic profiling using high-resolution LC-MS/MS in SK-OV-3 and NSDHL-KD counterparts to investigate the molecular consequences of NSDHL depletion. In total, 7,797 proteins were identified, of which 1,882 were differentially expressed (Log₂ fold change ≥ 1.2, p < 0.05), including 739 upregulated and 1,143 downregulated proteins (Fig. 3A).

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of the upregulated proteins revealed significant enrichment of mRNA surveillance, nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, and ubiquitin mediated proteolysis pathways (Fig. 3B). These findings suggest activation of proteostasis and mRNA quality control mechanisms, reflecting translational stress in NSDHL-KD cells.

In contrast, KEGG pathway analysis of the downregulated proteins showed significant suppression of fatty acid metabolism, steroid biosynthesis, unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, PPAR signaling, and peroxisome pathways (Fig. 3C), all of which are closely linked to lipid metabolism and oxidative function. Consistent with these findings, intracellular cholesterol levels were significantly reduced in NSDHL-KD SK-OV-3 cells (Fig. 3D). Downregulation of FAO-related pathways, including fatty acid degradation and peroxisome activity, was accompanied by reduced downstream products, such as Acyl-CoA and NADH. GO molecular function enrichment analysis further higlighted alterations in NAD+/NADH-binding proteins and oxidoreductase activity (Fig. 3E). Given that NADH serves as a key electron donor in antioxidant defense [32, 33], we measured intracellular levels and found that NADH was markedly reduced, whereas the NAD⁺/NADH ratio increased in both SK-OV-3-KD and OVCAR-5-KD cells (Fig. 3F), indicating disruption of redox balance.

In agreement with these findings, the ferroptosis pathway was downregulated in NSDHL-KD cells (Fig. 3B), likely reflecting reduced intracellular iron levels (Fig. 2H). Reduced expression of the iron transporter TFRC (Supplementary Fig. 3A-3B) provided additional evidence for the suppression of iron-dependent cell death pathways.

NSDHL knockdown modulates fatty acid metabolism and lipid peroxidation through PPARγ signaling

To further define the role of NSDHL in fatty acid metabolism, we analyzed pathways related to peroxisome function, fatty acid degradation, and PPAR signaling (Fig. 4A and D). Proteomic results revealed significant upregulation of ACSL4 following NSDHL knockdown, indicating altered fatty acid utilization. Western blotting confirmed increased ACSL4 expression and decreased ACSL3 expression in NSDHL-KD cells (Fig. 4E). As ACSL3 promotes MUFA synthesis and ACSL4 incorporates PUFAs into phospholipids [34], the combined upregulation of ACSL4 and downregulation of ACSL3 suggests a shift from MUFA toward PUFA incorporation. This imbalance is predicted to heighten susceptibility to oxidative stress, thereby increasing membrane lipid peroxidation and promoting cell death.

Since ACSL enzymes are regulated by the PPAR signaling pathway, particularly through PPARγ [35, 36], and PPARγ activation upregulates ACSL3, while downregulating ACSL4 [37–39], we then examined PPARγ nuclear localization. NSDHL knockdown significantly reduced the nuclear translocation of PPARγ in both ovarian cancer cell lines (Fig. 4F and G). These data indicate that NSDHL sustains fatty acid metabolism and lipid peroxidation homeostasis via the PPARγ signaling axis in ovarian cancer cells.

NSDHL knockdown induces ER stress–mediated apoptosis through lipid peroxidation

Impaired PPARγ signaling suggested that the lipid peroxidation and metabolic dysregulation observed after NSDHL knockdown could disrupt intracellular organelles, particularly the ER. Given that the ER membrane is highly vulnerable to oxidative damage, lipid peroxidation represents a likely trigger of ER dysfunction. Proteomic analysis revealed significant upregulation of proteins involved in ER protein processing and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis (Fig. 3C and E), both hallmarks of ER stress. We therefore hypothesized that NSDHL depletion initiates ER dysfunction and triggers ER stress-mediated cell death in ovarian cancer cells.

To determine the cell death pathway activated by NSDHL knockdown, we treated SK-OV-3 and OVCAR-5 cells with inhibitors of apoptosis (z-VAD), necroptosis (Nec-1), and pyroptosis (Ac-YVAD). Only the apoptosis inhibitor z-VAD restored viability in NSDHL-KD cells (Fig. 5A and B), indicating apoptosis to be the predominant cell death pathway. Flow cytometry-based AnnexinV assays confirmed an increase in apoptotic populations following NSDHL knockdown, from 2.69% to 4.41% in SK-OV-3 cells and from 2.26% to 6.38% OVCAR-5 cells (Fig. 5C and D). Similarly, TUNEL staining showed increased apoptosis in NSDHL-KD cells, while control cells exhibited minimal signals (supplemental information 4).

Treatment with the ER stress inhibitor 4-phenylbutyric acid (4-PBA) partially but significantly restored cell viability in NSDHL-silenced cells (Fig. 5E), supporting the involvement of ER stress in NSDHL knockdown–induced apoptosis. Although the magnitude of rescue varied across independent experiments, the effect was consistently incomplete, suggesting that ER stress contributes to, but does not fully account for, the cytotoxic effects of NSDHL depletion. Consistently, Western blot analysis showed increased expression of the ER chaperone BiP, along with increased levels of CASP12 and CASP9, in NSDHL-silenced cells (Fig. 5F, Supplementary Fig. 5). Together, these findings support the conclusion that NSDHL knockdown promotes apoptosis in ovarian cancer cells that NSDHL knockdown promotes apoptosis in ovarian cancer cells, at least in part, through lipid peroxidation- induced ER stress pathways.

NSDHL depletion limits ovarian tumor growth via apoptosis

To assess the in vivo impact of NSDHL depletion, we established a xenograft mouse model by subcutaneously injecting SK-OV-3 cells stably expressing NSDHL-targeting shRNA or scramble control into immunodeficient mice. Tumor growth was monitored for 6 weeks, during which NSDHL-knockdown markedly suppressed tumor progression. Compared with controls, NSDHL-deficient tumors displayed smaller volumes (Fig. 6A), slower tumor growth kinetics (Fig. 6B), and significantly lower tumor weights at the endpoint (Fig. 6C). TUNEL staining revealed increased apoptotic signals in tumors derived from NSDHL-deficient cells (Fig. 6D), confirming that NSDHL knockdown promotes apoptosis in vivo.

(B) Tumor growth curves over a 6-week period showing significantly slower tumor progression in the NSDHL-knockdown group (P < 0.05). (C) Tumor weights measured at the study endpoint, revealing significantly reduced tumor mass in NSDHL-knockdown mice (P < 0.01). (D) TUNEL staining of tumor sections showing increased apoptotic cell death in NSDHL-knockdown tumors compared to controls. (E-G) Immunohistochemical staining of ER stress markers in xenograft tumor tissues: (E) BiP (GRP78), (F) CHOP, and (G) ATF4, all three markers exhibited markedly increased staining intensity in NSDHL-knockdown tumors, indicating activation of ER stress signaling. Scale bars: 1 mm

We next examined whether apoptosis in vivo was associated with ER stress. Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated robust upregulation of canonical ER stress markers, including BiP, ATF4, and CHOP, in NSDHL-knockdown tumors compared with controls (Fig. 6E and G), consistent with sustained ER stress activation. Taken together, the coordinated induction of BiP, ATF4, and CHOP in xenografts, along with our in vitro findings of CASP12 and CASP9 activation, provides strong evidence that NSDHL knockdown suppresses ovarian tumor growth by triggering ER stress–mediated apoptosis. These findings confirmed the critical role of the ER stress axis in mediating the tumor-suppressive effects of NSDHL depletion in vivo.

Discussion

Understanding the metabolic adaptations of cancer cells is crucial for identifying potential therapeutic targets. Among these adaptations, dysregulated lipid metabolism is a well-established hallmark of cancer progression [22, 40, 41]. Aberrant cholesterol biosynthesis and fatty acid metabolism have been implicated in tumor growth and therapy resistance, thus marking enzymes such as NSDHL as critical determinants for cancer cell survival [2, 3, 13, 42]. NSDHL has been implicated primarily in breast cancer, where it promotes proliferation, migration, metastasis, and stem-like properties, at least in part through regulation of TGF-β signaling [13, 14]. In pancreatic cancer, disruption of NSDHL-dependent cholesterol biosynthesis has been associated with epithelial–mesenchymal transition and basal-like reprogramming through activation of TGF-β signaling [12]. In contrast, our data support a distinct mechanism in ovarian cancer, in which NSDHL depletion induces lipid peroxidation despite iron depletion, thereby triggering ER stress-associated apoptosis rather than canonical ferroptotic cell death. Collectively, our findings indicate that NSDHL promotes ovarian cancer cell survival and tumor growth by maintaining cellular redox homeostasis. Loss of NSDHL enhances lipid peroxidation, disrupts ER function, and ultimately induces apoptosis.

Previous studies have shown that ACSL enzymes play crucial roles in fatty acid metabolism, with ACSL3 promoting MUFA synthesis and ACSL4 facilitating PUFAs incorporation into membrane phospholipids [34]. This balance between MUFA and PUFA seems critical for determining susceptibility to oxidative stress, where PPARγ signaling regulates these enzymes, upregulating ACSL3 while suppressing ACSL4 [35–39], thereby maintaining lipid metabolic homeostasis. In the present study, NSDHL knockdown induced marked lipid peroxidation accompanied by dysregulation of these key enzymes, characterized by ACSL4 upregulation and ACSL3 downregulation. These changes may shift the MUFA/PUFA balance toward PUFA enrichment, thereby increasing the susceptibility of NSDHL-KD cells to oxidative stress. Downregulation of PPARγ may further contribute to decreased ACSL3 and increased ACSL4 expression, reinforcing lipid peroxidation and promoting apoptosis. Together, these findings suggest an important interplay between PPARγ signaling and lipid metabolism in maintaining cellular homeostasis. Notably, C11-BODIPY staining measures the extent of lipid peroxidation but does not directly define phospholipid composition. Therefore, it cannot distinguish between PUFA- and MUFA-containing lipid species. In this study, the proposed altered PUFA/MUFA balance is inferred from proteomic and pathway-level changes, including ACSL4 upregulation, ACSL3 downregulation, and suppression of unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis and PPAR signaling, rather than as direct lipidomic evidence. Thus, the proposed shift in membrane lipid composition should be validated in future studies by direct lipidomic profiling, particularly by quantifying specific phospholipid species such as the PE-PUFA/PE-MUFA ratio. Despite elevated ROS and lipid peroxidation, NSDHL knockdown did not trigger ferroptosis. Instead, ferroptosis-related pathways were suppressed, as shown by downregulation of TFRC and reduced intracellular ferrous iron. Rather than ferroptosis, ER stress–mediated apoptosis emerged as the dominant cell death pathway. Interestingly, Wang et al. (2025) [43] reported that ferroptotic signaling in adipocytes reduces lipid accumulation and promotes thermogenesis by enhancing lipid peroxidation and suppressing HIF1α. By contrast, our findings reveal that NSDHL knockdown suppresses both lipid metabolism and ferroptotic signaling. This suggests a bidirectional regulatory relationship between lipid metabolism and ferroptosis. On the one hand, an altered MUFA/PUFA balance may reduce ferroptotic sensitivity, while on the other, impaired ferroptotic signaling may exacerbate lipid metabolic dysfunction. Together, these processes create a feedback loop that protects cells from ferroptosis, but renders them vulnerable to ER stress-mediated apoptosis. Thus, NSDHL links lipid regulation, iron metabolism, and redox control in ovarian cancer cells.

UPR activation has been implicated in diverse cancer processes, including cell cycle regulation, oxidative stress, autophagy, cell death, and chemotherapy resistance, particularly in ovarian cancer [27, 28, 44]. While mild ER stress allows cancer cells to adapt and survive, promoting tumor growth and resistance, persistent or severe ER stress shifts signaling toward cell death. Indeed, ER stress-related genes, such as GRP78, ATF4, and PERK, are highly expressed in epithelial ovarian cancer, suggesting chronic basal ER stress [26, 29, 45, 46]. In our study, upregulation of BiP and activation of CASP12, CASP9, and CASP 3, confirmed that apoptosis was driven by ER stress following NSDHL knockdown. BiP serves as a master regulator of the UPR by binding ER transmembrane sensors (PERK, IRE1, and ATF6) under normal conditions [47]. When stress is prolonged, CHOP promotes a switch from adaptive to pro-apoptotic signaling, facilitating CASP12 activation. CASP12 then activates CASP9 and CASP3 independently of cytochrome c, distinguishing ER stress–mediated apoptosis from mitochondrial pathways [48]. Collectively, these results indicate that NSDHL depletion forces ovarian cancer cells to cross the threshold from adaptive ER stress into pro-apoptotic signaling, thereby undermining their ability to withstand metabolic and oxidative stress.

In conclusion, our study identifies NSDHL as a key regulator of ovarian cancer cell survival and tumor growth through mechanisms involving lipid peroxidation, ER stress, and apoptosis. Targeting NSDHL may offer a dual therapeutic advantage: disrupting lipid homeostasis while redirecting oxidative stress away from ferroptosis toward ER stress-mediated apoptosis. Moreover, the broader role of NSDHL in lipid metabolism and its interplay with PPARγ signaling warrant further investigation to fully elucidate its contributions to ovarian cancer progression. This mechanistic insight highlights the ER stress axis as a critical vulnerability in ovarian cancer and and supports the development of NSDHL-targeted strategies to promote apoptosis and improve therapeutic outcomes.

Materials and methods

Compounds

Ferrostatin-1, RSL3, Erastin and Deferoxamine mesylate were purchased from MCE (New Jersey, USA; HY-100579, HY-100218 A, HY-15763, HY-B0988). Antibodies against NSDHL (#199730) was procured from Abcam. Tubulin (#66031-1-lg) from Proteintech. TFRC (#5865),, ACSL3 (#A22085),, ACSL4 (#A20414), PPARγ(A25298), Bip/GRP78 (A23453), Caspase 3 (#A25309) and Caspase 9 (#A20986) were purchased from Abclonal. Caspase-12 (ab315271) from Abcam. Anti-mouse (#31430) and anti-rabbit (#31460) secondary antibodies for western blot were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). CM-H2DCFDA (#287810) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). BODIPY 581/591C11 dye (#D3861) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA).

Cell culture and spheroid culture

All cell lines in this study were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia, USA), SK-OV-3 cells were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (#C12430500BT, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), while OVCAR-5 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium(#C22400500BT, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (#16140071, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), cultured in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2 and detached using trypsin/EDTA solution. For spheroids culture, Cells were seeded into ultra-low attachment 6-well plated (5 × 104/ well) and cultured in serum-free medium (SFM) containing Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM)/F12 (BI, Kibbutz Beit-Haemek, Israel), 10ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor and 20ng/mL epidermal growth factor (Pepro-Tech, Rocky Hill, USA),10µL/mL B27 additive (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, California, USA) and 1 mg/mL insulin (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA). Spheroids were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 7 days, as descripted previously [30]. Fresh medium was added every two days.

siRNA transfection

Small-interfering RNAs (siRNAs) for NSDHL, and non-target siRNA controls, were purchased from Genepharma (Shanghai, China) and used to transfect cells (50 nM), with Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX (#13778100, ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The target sequences were as follows: siRNA-1: 5′-CCAGCAGUUAGCGAGCCAA-3′, siRNA-2: 5′-CCAGCUCUGAAAGGUGUAA-3′. A non-targeting scrambled siRNA sequence:5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3, was used as negative control. Knockdown efficiency was tested by Western blotting.

Western blot

Cells or tissues were collected and lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation (RIPA) buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (#78440, ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA) with ultrasonication (only tissue samples). Protein concentrations loaded and separated on a 4–20% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel, followed by transferring onto a 0.22-µm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was then blocked with 5% milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with a primary antibody overnight at 4 ◦C and secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Protein detection was performed by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (#WP20005, ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA) and photographed using Image quant LAS400 mini (GE Healthcare, Munich, Germany).

Cell viability assay

siRNA transfected SK-OV-3, OVCAR-5 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (3,000 cells/well or 5,000 cells/well), followed by treatment with various agents. At the end of treatment periods, CCK reagent (#GK10001, GLPBIO) was added to each well for 1 h at 37 °C. The number of viable cells is measured using a microplate reader by the absorbance at 450 nm (Thermo VARIOSKAN FLASH, Waltham, MA, USA).

Total cellular ROS assay

Cells were incubated with CM-H2DCFDA (10µM) for 30 min at 37 °C, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of cells with CM-H2DCFDA staining was measured by a flow cytometry (BD FACSVerse, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) in combination with FlowJo X software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR, USA).

NAD + and NADP(H) measurement

The intracellular NADH/NAD + level was measured using the NADH/NAD + assay kit with WST-8 (#S0175, Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. 1 × 10^6 cells were collected via centrifugation (200 g for 5 min at 4 °C) and then removed medium. Cells were lysed by the addition of 200 µL pre-cooling NADH/NAD + lysis buffer and centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. 50–100 µL supernatant was moved into a tube followed by 30 min of incubation at 60 °C, and then performed centrifugation at 10,000 g for 5 min at 4 °C. 20 µL supernatant was moved into 96-well plates and incubated 10 min at 37 °C then followed by the addition of 10 µL color-developing solution. The ratio of NADH/NAD + was measured by detecting the absorbance of mixed liquid at 450 nm. Kit (Sigma Cat#MAK037).

Lipid peroxidation assay

Cells were seeded in 12-well plates for 24 h, followed by treatment with siRNA for 48 h. Cells in each well were then incubated with BODIPY 581/591C11 dye (2µM) for 20 min at 37 °C. After trypsinization into a cell suspension, cells were analysed based on the measurement of the fluorescence intensity using a flow cytometry (BD FACSVerse). Oxidation of C11-BODIPY dye results in a shift of the fluorescence emission peak from 590 nm to 510 nm. The date was analyzed by Flowjo software (Version 10.0).

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cells were plated onto the autoclaved coverslips in 12-well plates (1*10^4 cells/well) following indicated treatment. Then cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 ℃ for 30 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and blocked with 2% BSA at 37 ℃ for 1 h. Cells were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4℃ followed by four 15 min washes in PBST, the samples were then incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Finally, DAPI reagent (Beyotime, P0131) was employed to stain cell nuclei. Images were visualized using Olympus FLUOVIEW FV1200 laser scanning microscope.

Iron ion analysis by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS)

OVCAR-5 cells transfected with sir-NSDHL or control sir were cultured and collected 5*10^5 cells each group. Thereafter, the cells were scraped and transferred to 1.5 mL centrifugal tube. The supernatant was discarded, 0.8 mL 95% HNO3 and 0.2 mL were added. The samples were pre-digested at 130 °C for about 30 min. After pre-digestion, the reference tank was placed in position for 24 h, then the apparatus was placed in a microwave oven chamber for microwave digestion. The concentration of Fe was analyzed using ICP-MS (PerkinElmer NexION 300X, USA).

Iron assay

Intracellular ferrous iron level was determined using FerroOrange (#F374) from Dojindo, (Kyushu Island, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions. In briefly, 5*104 cells were seeded into 12-well plates and incubated with the indicated treatments. Next cells were harvested and stained in 1µM Ferro orange for 30 min at 37 °C. Finally, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of cells with FerroOrange staining was measured by a flow cytometry (BD FACSVerse).

Apoptosis assay

Cellular apoptosis in SK-OV-3 and OVCAR-5 cells was determined using Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis kit (Lianke, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. 1 × 105 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated with the indicated treatments, after 48 h, cells were harvested and incubated in a binding buffer containing annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide solution at room temperature for 15 min. Finally, cells were measured by a flow cytometry (BD FACSVerse, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA).

Tumor xenograft

The stable NSDHL-downregulated SK-OV-3 cells were established as follows: shRNAs targeting NSDHL and the negative control shRNA were cloned into the PGMLV-hU6-MCS-CMV-puromycin lentiviral expression vector and introduced into SK-OV-3 cells that had been seeded in 6-well plates for 24 h, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 72 h, the cells were selected with 5 µg/mL puromycin for 4 days. Knockdown efficiency was verified by Western blotting. The target sequence used for the NSDHL shRNA was 5′-CCAGCUCUGAAAGGUGUAATT-3′, and the non-targeting scrambled shRNA sequence was 5′-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-3′. All construct sequences were confirmed using DNA sequencing.

Six-week-old female BALB/c Nude mice were purchased from the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Beijing, China) and randomly divided into two groups (n = 5 per group). SK-OV-3 cells (1 × 107) transfected with short-hairpin RNA (shRNA) or negative control were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of nude mice. Tumor size of each mouse was measured every 7days for 42 days, and the volume was calculated according to the formula 0.5 × length × width2. The mice were sacrificed and the tumors were isolated and analyzed tumor weight, Ki67 and Tunel level. and tumor tissues were excised, fixed in 4% formalin, embedded in paraffin, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or probed with the anti-γ-H2AX (CST, 9718, RRID: AB_2,118,009) and primary anticleaved-caspase3 (CST, 9579, RRID: AB_10,897,512) antibodies. In situ apoptotic cells in tumor slides were measured by an Apoptosis Detection Kit (TUNEL, Takara, MK500). Color changes of the stained slides were photographed and analyzed with a microscope (Leica, Germany).

Proteomic analysis

OVCAR-5 cells transfected with sirNSDHL or siNC (three biological replicates in each group) were underwent proteomics analyses via liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Cells were collected and lysed using RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The pellets were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min for supernatants collection. 50 µg of protein from each sample were digested to peptides with trypsin overnight at 37 °C. The following day, the peptides separation was performed on an Agilent Zorbax Extend – C18. Peak integration and statistical analysis were performed using DeCyder differential analysis software (GE Healthcare, Munich, Germany).

Immunohistochemistry

The IHC assay was performed on 130 ovarian cancer tissue samples obtained from Women’s Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University. Anti-Nsdhl rabbit mAb was used in this assay. The staining results were evaluated based on the intensity and the percentage of positively stained cells (five microscope fields for each tissue). The intensity was scored as follows: 1 - negative, 2 - weak, 3 - moderate, 4 – strong. The percentage of positively stained cells was scored as follows: 1 − 0 ~ 25%, 2–25 ~ 50%, 3–50 ~ 75%, 4–75 ~ 100%. Multiply the two parts of score and add the scores of the five microscopic fields. The sum of 1–42 was considered low expression, and that of 43–80 was considered high expression. In addition, since 16 patients failed to re-contact, only the remaining 114 patients were used for the calculation of progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival.

The Kaplan–Meier plotter

The Kaplan-Meier plotter (http://kmplot.com/analysis/) was used to investigate the correlation between NSDHL mRNA levels and PFS in various cancer types. To explore the prognostic significance of a particular gene, the cancer samples were divided into “low” and “high” expression. Subsequently, NSDHL was put into the database to acquire PFS Kaplan-Meier plotter. Hazard ratio (HR), 95%, log rank P and confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated and presented on the webpage (http://kmplot.com/analysis/index.php?p=service).

Statistical analysis

All grouped data are presented as means ± standard error of mean (SEM) from studies performed at least in triplicate. The unpaired two-sided Student’s t test was used for the comparision between unpaired two groups. For univariate analysis, χ2 test was used. *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001; ****, p < 0.0001; n.s., non-significant.
