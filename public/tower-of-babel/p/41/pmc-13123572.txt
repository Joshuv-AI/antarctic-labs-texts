Moving beyond chest compression fraction: the need to measure high-quality chest compression fraction during cardiac arrest
Authors: S. Hari Ganesh, V.T. Amrithanand, Anas Mohammed Muthanikkatt
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Chest compression fraction (CCF) is widely accepted as a key indicator of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) quality and is associated with improved survival following cardiac arrest.1 However, there is increasing attention directed toward whether uninterrupted compressions alone adequately reflect effective CPR performance.2

In our ongoing prospective quality improvement study conducted in a high-volume tertiary care emergency department, we analyzed 154 resuscitation events managed by a trained cardiac arrest response team. The cohort primarily consisted of non-shockable rhythms (144 events, 93.5%) and included 55 out-of-hospital cardiac arrests (35.7%). During the pre-intervention phase, data collected using the LAERDAL CPRmeter 2 demonstrated a satisfactory median CCF of 83.2%.

Although this finding reflects excellent adherence to recommended targets for compression continuity, a detailed analysis of compression mechanics revealed important discrepancies in compression quality. When compression parameters were further examined, only 11% of the resuscitation events from the baseline sample met the criteria for High-Quality Chest Compression Fraction (HQ-CCF)—which we define as chest compressions delivered continuously (>60%) while simultaneously achieving guideline-recommended rate (100–120/min) and depth (5–6 cm). Specifically, the median compression rate was 121.9 compressions per minute, exceeding recommended targets, while the median compression depth was 48 mm, falling below optimal guideline recommendations (Fig. 1). Consequently, reliance solely on conventional CCF may overestimate CPR quality, particularly in experienced centers where interruptions are already minimized. A high CCF does not necessarily ensure adequate coronary or cerebral perfusion if the compression rate and depth remain suboptimal.3 We propose that cardiac arrest quality-monitoring systems evolve beyond reporting traditional CCF to include composite indicators, such as HQ-CCF, that integrate compression continuity with mechanical effectiveness.

Such refined metrics may better identify actionable gaps in CPR delivery and enable focused quality-improvement initiatives. Existing literature highlights several interventions capable of improving compression quality, including structured team role allocation, real-time audiovisual feedback devices, and targeted simulation-based training.4, 5 These approaches emphasize the optimization of compression performance rather than interruption reduction alone.

As resuscitation science advances, redefining CPR quality metrics to reflect effective rather than merely continuous compressions may represent an essential step toward improving survival after cardiac arrest. We must include HQ-CCF as a new quality metric in future guideline reporting to address the multidimensional nature of CPR assessment.
