Strategies for improving rural trauma health systems
Authors: Chavi Rehani, Joseph V Sakran, Michael Person, Andrew Bernard, Zain G Hashmi, Jeffrey Kerby, Morgan Krause, Nakul Raykar, Katie W Russell, Lacey N LaGrone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryRural Americans face persistent and widening disparities in trauma and emergency care driven by geographic isolation, limited workforce capacity, longer transport times, and fragile hospital finances. These challenges are poised to intensify under the 2025 tax and spending reconciliation law, which substantially reduces federal Medicaid and Children’s Health Insurance Program funding and is projected to increase uninsurance, placing already vulnerable rural hospitals at heightened risk of service reduction or closure. This article examines the structural pressures confronting rural trauma systems and proposes strategic, scalable solutions to strengthen resilience. Key recommendations include the development of Regional Medical Operations Coordinating Centers to enhance regional and interstate collaboration, optimize patient flow, and support disaster preparedness; targeted investments in workforce recruitment, retention, and teletrauma infrastructure; and hospital stabilization through right-sizing, repurposing underused capacity, and expanding telehealth and mobile care models. While new Rural Health Transformation Program grants may offer limited relief, sustained leadership and coordinated policy action will be required to ensure equitable access to high-quality trauma and emergency care in rural communities and to prevent geography from determining survival.Keywords: health policy, Healthcare disparities, Public Health

Approximately 14%–20% of Americans live in rural areas, which cover 97% of the US land mass.1 2 Geographic barriers and concentration of resources such as tertiary centers and specialty healthcare professionals in metropolitan areas present serious obstacles to delivering optimal healthcare in rural settings. These challenges are magnified in trauma and emergency scenarios. Rural areas tend to have fewer prevention resources; higher injury incidence and trauma-related mortality rates; and limited emergency medical service (EMS) capacity.3,5 They also have longer EMS response and transport times, especially when routing to Level I and II centers. In 2010, approximately 88% of Americans had access to a Level I or Level II trauma center within 60 minutes (figure 1).6 Despite significant progress and efforts, that number had only risen to 89% by 2019.7 Moreover, rural hospitals often operate with thinner financial margins than their urban counterparts due to factors like lower patient volume with fixed operating costs, lower commercial payer share, and a higher proportion of the population depending on Medicare. Rural hospitals in states that did not participate in the expansion of Medicaid and Children’s Health Insurance Program (CHIP) under the Affordable Care Act are especially vulnerable.8 9

The 2025 tax and spending reconciliation law is expected to deepen these disparities. Under its Medicaid and CHIP provisions, gross federal Medicaid and CHIP spending will drop by nearly $911 billion over the next decade. This includes an estimated $137 billion reduction in Medicaid funding directed to rural areas, far outstripping the $50 billion in state grants included in the package as part of the Rural Health Transformation Program (RHTP).10 The Congressional Budget Office projects that, as a result of these policies, 10 million more people will become uninsured by 2034.11 Already vulnerable rural hospitals are facing mounting threats of closure or reduced services, making it urgent that we rethink how to build resilience into rural trauma systems. While the exact needs will vary from state to state, several strategic focus areas and cross-cutting solutions are broadly relevant and offer promise for bolstering rural communities nationwide (figure 2).

Regional collaboration models

We propose establishing Regional Medical Operations Coordinating Centers (RMOCCs) that will function as hub-and-spoke networks to deepen local partnerships and foster collaboration between trauma and disaster preparedness systems, EMS, hospital systems, and public health agencies like the state Department of Health. In rural settings, where distance, low volume, and resource scarcity pose ongoing challenges, RMOCCs can knit together disparate systems by coordinating communication across the region, managing hospital capacity and patient flow to minimize potentially avoidable transfers, and facilitating transparent management of resources like teletrauma capability and blood products to ensure equitable care throughout the region.12 These RMOCCs should be integrated across neighboring states—and ultimately, nationwide as part of a National Trauma and Emergency Preparedness System—to support emergency efforts during disasters, pandemics, or large-scale crises such as combat operations.13 14 In areas currently characterized as “blood deserts,” we must create prehospital blood programs and emergency transfusion protocols to ensure that life-saving blood products are available to address hemorrhage from trauma, obstetrics, and other causes when needed. In parallel, we must create standardized regional protocols for shortages and crisis response and draft evidence-informed clinical guidance adaptable to local resources and workforce capabilities to ensure operational readiness during emergencies.

Workforce recruitment and retention

Medicaid is the second-largest source of funding for graduate medical education programs in the USA.15 Changes to student loan eligibility, loan-repayment programs, and Medicaid financing as outlined in the most recent tax law are expected to have significant destabilizing effects on the rural workforce, with impacts ranging from hindering general surgery program expansions and rural surgery tracks to discouraging low-income individuals from entering the workforce pipeline altogether. To sustain high-quality care, we must systematically build and retain the health workforce. We must fund continuing education for clinicians, whether it is by providing cash assistance for certifications and intensive training like the Rural Trauma Team Development Course or by investing in resources like easily accessible microlearning modules for just-in-time training. Not only will this improve the quality of care in rural hospitals, but it will also help keep patients in their community rather than being transferred out. We should also focus on quality improvement programs to streamline existing processes and consider expanding non-physician healthcare professional and allied health programs in rural settings to increase access to care and reduce burnout. As clinician competency and satisfaction increase, so will their desire to remain in the workforce. Investing in telemonitoring and teletrauma infrastructure will also be essential, enabling rural clinicians to remain connected to centers of excellence. The ability to consult specialists in real-time will allow low-acuity trauma patients to be cared for within the community and allow providers to get assistance as needed for the stabilization and resuscitation of high-acuity trauma patients prior to transfer. Finally, by offering paramedic training and other professional pathways to local residents, we can create sustainable systems that foster trust and anchor talent in rural communities.

Hospital stabilization and right-sizing

Financial viability is critical for rural health facilities to continue operating. Underused, high-cost spaces within existing hospitals should be repurposed into clinics, rehabilitation units, specialty services, or standalone emergency facilities. We should also invest in telehealth centers and mobile care units to reduce travel burdens for trauma patients and improve continuity of follow-up care.

When healthcare funding is threatened, rural communities are disproportionately burdened. These communities are foundational to the character and economy of the nation, yet their trauma and emergency care systems confront longstanding structural and financial headwinds that are intensifying under current policy pressures. The impending Medicaid funding cuts and rising rates of uninsurance threaten to further destabilize already fragile hospitals and emergency networks. RHTP funds are expected to provide a small measure of relief. Under RHTP, $50 billion in state grants will be deployed starting in January 2026: $25 billion allocated equally among states, and $25 billion distributed based on metrics such as rural population and facilities, uncompensated care levels, workforce strength, state-level initiatives, and progress under “Make America Healthy Again” policies.16 But these funds alone will not prevent the collapse of rural health systems nationwide. Addressing these challenges will require smart, forward-looking strategies, coordinated action, and bold new ideas. The trauma community should actively engage in the planning and implementation of the new RHTP-funded state initiatives, but we must also remain at the forefront of redesigning rural healthcare. Because when financial pressures force rural hospitals to retrench, trauma and acute care surgery are often the last critical services to survive. We cannot afford to substitute optimism for leadership. Rural hospitals are not just facilities; they are lifelines. By prioritizing regional collaboration, investing in workforce capacity, and “right-sizing” hospital infrastructure, states can forge trauma systems that are both resilient and efficient. These are not idealistic goals; they are feasible and necessary to ensure that geography does not determine survival. Sustained leadership and committed investment at every level will be vital if we are to preserve the health, well-being, and dignity of rural Americans.
