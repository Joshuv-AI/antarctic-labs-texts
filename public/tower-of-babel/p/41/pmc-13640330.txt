Clinical evaluation of the SeptiScore biomarker for the diagnosis of healthcare-associated infections in critically ill trauma patients: a multicenter derivation and validation cohort study
Authors: Marwan Bouras, Lisa Neutre, Chloé Douarec, Thomas Goronflot, Cécile Poulain, Alexandre Bourdiol, Alexiane Blanc, Charlotte Arbelot, Marc Leone, Marc G Denis, Antoine Roquilly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiagnosing healthcare-associated infections (HCAIs) in critically ill trauma patients is difficult, because trauma-related non-septic systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) closely mimics infection and conventional biomarkers lack specificity. SeptiCyte RAPID is a fully automated host-response assay that quantifies the expression of two genes and returns a SeptiScore within one hour, making it suitable for bedside use. Our primary objective was to determine an optimal SeptiScore threshold for identifying HCAIs in a derivation cohort and to evaluate it in an independent validation cohort; secondary objectives were to assess diagnostic performance and to compare SeptiScore with conventional biomarkers.MethodsThis two-center diagnostic study included a prospective derivation cohort (82 samples, 44 patients) and a retrospective validation cohort (31 samples, 31 patients), comprising adult ICU trauma patients with SIRS and hemodynamic failure. SeptiScore was measured at clinical suspicion, before microbiological confirmation. Because some patients contributed several samples, the association between SeptiScore and infection was analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with a random patient intercept, and confidence intervals for diagnostic metrics were estimated using a patient-level cluster bootstrap. Diagnostic accuracy was assessed by ROC curves, the Youden index, and negative likelihood ratios (LR −), and compared with C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, and leukocyte count.ResultsOverall, 113 samples (71 trauma-related SIRS, 42 HCAIs) were analyzed. SeptiScore was higher in HCAIs (median 8.05, IQR 6.83–8.80) than in trauma-related SIRS (5.80, 5.08–7.05), and infection status remained independently associated with SeptiScore after accounting for within-patient correlation (p < 0.001). In the derivation cohort, the area under the ROC curve was 0.79 (95% CI 0.72–0.87). A threshold of 6.7 yielded sensitivity 0.84 (0.72–0.97), specificity 0.66 (0.53–0.79), and LR − 0.24 (0.04–0.53), outperforming conventional biomarkers. In the smaller validation cohort, the pre-specified threshold showed consistent performance (AUC 0.98; 95% CI 0.94–1.00), although these estimates are imprecise given the limited number of events.ConclusionsSeptiScore showed promising performance for distinguishing HCAIs from trauma-related SIRS and outperformed conventional biomarkers. Background

Severe trauma is a major cause of death and disability worldwide [1]. Critically ill trauma patients are particularly susceptible to healthcare-associated infections (HCAIs) due to disrupted tissue barriers and impaired immune responses [2]. The occurrence of HCAIs in this population is associated with increased mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and greater healthcare costs [3]. Among these infections, hospital-acquired pneumonia is the most frequent, affecting nearly half of trauma patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) [4, 5]. Other HCAIs include bloodstream infections, surgical site infections, and urinary tract infections [6].

Following severe trauma, most patients develop a trauma-related non-septic systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), which can mimic HCAIs due to overlapping clinical and inflammatory features [7]. Diagnosing HCAIs is therefore complex and requires a combination of clinical, radiological, and microbiological evidence [8]. Biomarkers may enhance diagnostic accuracy, yet current options such as procalcitonin (PCT), C-reactive protein (CRP), and elevated leukocyte count are often unreliable due to the non-specific inflammatory response in severe trauma. Although several biomarkers have been proposed to assess sepsis in ICU patients, their use remains challenging due to low specificity, high costs, and a lack of standardized guidelines [9–11]. Recently, interest has grown in using host response signatures as diagnostic tools to complement clinical, microbiological, and radiological data [12, 13]. SeptiCyte RAPID (ImmunExpress, Seattle, USA) is an FDA 510(k)-cleared host response assay that quantifies the expression of two genes (PLAC8 and PLA2G7) involved in phospholipid metabolism, essential to the host’s response to infection [14, 15]. It assigns a “SeptiScore” from 0 to 15 within 1 h to indicate the likelihood of sepsis, using a fully automated process suitable for bedside clinical use in the ICU. While this biomarker has shown promising results in sepsis [15–18], persistent Gram-negative bacteremia [19] and COVID-19 [20], it has never been evaluated in critically ill trauma patients, a population in which non-infectious trauma-related SIRS is common and often clinically indistinguishable from infection. We hypothesized that SeptiScore would accurately discriminate HCAIs from trauma-related SIRS in critically ill trauma patients, as it reflects infection-specific gene expression pathways, unlike conventional biomarkers, which are also elevated in sterile inflammation. The primary objective of this study was to determine the optimal SeptiScore threshold for identifying HCAIs in critically ill trauma patients using a derivation cohort and to validate this threshold in an independent validation cohort. Secondary objectives were to evaluate SeptiScore diagnostic performance using this threshold and to compare its performance with that of conventional biomarkers.

Methods

Study design

This multicenter, observational clinical study was conducted across two tertiary ICUs in France from November 2022 to December 2023. We reported our results following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline [21]. Clinical data were registered without interfering with usual clinical practice. Two independent cohorts were analyzed: a prospective “derivation cohort” at a tertiary academic ICU in France and a “validation cohort” at another tertiary academic ICU in France. The derivation cohort was prospectively used to assess the diagnostic performance of the SeptiScore biomarker and to identify and establish diagnostic thresholds, while the validation cohort was used to test the optimal clinical threshold and to confirm its accuracy and reliability. The use of both cohorts is essential to ensure robust and generalizable findings, as it enables validation of thresholds in an independent patient population [22, 23].

Ethical approval

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nantes university hospital (Groupe Nantais d'Éthique dans le Domaine de la Santé, GNEDS; approval numbers GNEDS 23–89-07–072 and 25–43-04–181). All participants received written information about the study and the use of their personal medical data. Informed consent to participate was obtained through a written non-opposition procedure, and all enrolled patients agreed to the use of their data. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects.

Study population

In the derivation cohort, all adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted to the ICU for trauma were prospectively screened for eligibility and included if they met the SIRS criteria defined as two or more of the following: central body temperature > 38 °C or < 36 °C, heart rate > 90 beats/min, respiratory rate > 20 breaths/min or partial pressure of CO₂ < 32 mmHg, leukocyte count > 12 G/L and if they developed hemodynamic failure, defined as the need for fluid resuscitation and/or vasopressor therapy and if they required an expected duration of invasive mechanical ventilation of more than 72 h. No patient declined participation. Exclusion criteria included antibiotic-treated infections, imminent death, fixed dilated pupils with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 3 (considered moribund), pregnancy, or breastfeeding.

The retrospective validation cohort consisted of patients admitted to a second tertiary ICU. As in the derivation cohort, adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted to the ICU for trauma were screened for eligibility and included if they met at least two of the previously defined SIRS criteria and developed hemodynamic failure, defined as the need for fluid resuscitation and/or vasopressor therapy. The only difference from the derivation cohort concerned the expected duration of invasive mechanical ventilation greater than 72 h, which was an inclusion criterion in the prospective derivation cohort; because this criterion relies on a prospective clinical estimation made at the time of inclusion, it could not be applied in the retrospective validation cohort. The exclusion criteria were the same as those used in the derivation cohort. Treating clinicians were blinded to SeptiScore results in both cohorts.

In both cohorts, blood samples for SeptiScore measurement were obtained at the time of clinical suspicion, before the final diagnosis was established and before microbiological results were available. Samples were obtained either at the onset of a clinically suspected trauma-related SIRS episode or at the time of clinical suspicion of an HCAI during the ICU stay. In some patients, repeated sampling was performed during distinct, clinically separate trauma-related SIRS episodes (8 patients with SIRS underwent more than one sampling). Each sample was subsequently classified as trauma-related SIRS or HCAI by the attending intensivist, who was blinded to the SeptiScore result, based on clinical, microbiological, and radiological data. Trauma-related SIRS was defined by the absence of any clinical, microbiological, or radiological evidence of infection at the time of sampling and during the following 72 h. HCAI was defined by clinical suspicion of infection at sampling, subsequent microbiological confirmation, and fulfillment of ECDC criteria for a healthcare-associated infection. The 48-h ECDC threshold was used to qualify an infection as healthcare-associated, whereas the 72-h window was used to ensure that samples classified as SIRS did not correspond to an early evolving infection. We compared trauma-related SIRS and HCAIs to mirror the real-life diagnostic challenge faced in the ICU, where clinicians must distinguish sterile post-traumatic inflammation from infection at an early stage of the infectious process.

Definitions of healthcare-associated infection (HCAIs)

Each episode of HCAI was defined according to ECDC criteria [24] as occurring more than 48 h after ICU admission. Accordingly, SeptiScore samples collected within the first 48 h after ICU admission were only considered as trauma-related SIRS and did not contribute to the HCAI group. The ECDC criteria provide standardized definitions for HCAIs to ensure consistent diagnosis and reporting across healthcare settings, which include specific timelines, clinical signs, and microbiological confirmation [25]. Only the initial episode of a diagnosed HCAI was included in the analysis. Hospital-acquired pneumonia was suspected when at least two of the following signs were present: body temperature > 38 °C, leukocytosis > 12 G/L or leukopenia < 4 G/L, purulent pulmonary secretions, along with a new or worsening infiltrate on chest X-ray. The diagnosis was confirmed by quantitative culture of a respiratory tract sample, with a threshold of 104 CFU/mL for bronchoalveolar lavage or non-bronchoscopic samples, and 103 CFU/mL for protected specimen brushes. Respiratory samples were always collected before initiating new antibiotic treatment. All cases of hospital-acquired pneumonia were ventilator-associated pneumonia. Neuro-meningeal infections were suspected in the presence of fever, altered consciousness, meningeal signs, headache, seizures, or sensory-motor deficits, and confirmed by biochemical and microbiological analysis of cerebrospinal fluid. Urinary tract infection was suspected and confirmed by a urine culture showing bacteriuria ≥ 105 CFU/mL (≥ 102 CFU/mL in pyelic urine). Intra-abdominal infection was suspected with abdominal symptoms (pain, tenderness, contracture, ileus, diarrhea, evisceration, or abscess) or unexplained systemic signs (tachycardia, tachypnea, dyspnea, cognitive impairment) and confirmed by abdominal CT with contrast and intraoperative microbiological sampling. Catheter-related bloodstream infections were evaluated in febrile patients with catheters in place for more than 48 h and confirmed by a time-to-positivity difference ≥ 120 min between peripheral and central blood cultures in cases of bacteremia. All HCAI diagnoses were made by the treating clinicians, who were blinded to prior SeptiScore results.

Laboratory methods

The SeptiScore biomarker was assessed on whole blood collected in EDTA tubes. To perform this analysis, 0.24 mL of whole blood was placed in a SeptiCyte RAPID cartridge (ImmunExpress, Seattle, WA, USA) carrying out all the assay steps: extraction, sample purification, reverse transcription, amplification, detection, and quantification of two mRNA transcripts. The software on the Idylla platform (Biocartis NV, Mechelen, Belgium) provided a quantitative score, SeptiScore, ranging from 0 to 15, calculated by combining the RT-PCR Cq (quantification cycle) values measured for PLAC8 and PLA2G7 expression levels. Higher SeptiScore values suggested a greater probability of infection. Results were available within 1 h. All biochemical measurements were performed in a clinical biochemistry laboratory accredited according to ISO 15189 standards. Serum CRP and PCT were determined with immunoassays performed on cobas c503 and e801 analytical units (Roche Diagnostics, Rotkreuz, Switzerland). The limits of detection (LoD) were 0.3 mg/L and 0.02 ng/mL for CRP and PCT, respectively. CRP and PCT runs were validated by measuring two levels of quality control material prior to starting the experiment.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics of participants and infection characteristics at SeptiScore measurement time were presented as numbers and percentages for categorical data. These were compared between groups using Fisher’s exact test. Continuous data were reported as mean plus standard deviation or median with 25th and 75th percentiles, and were compared using Student’s t test, Mann–Whitney-Wilcoxon or Kruskal–Wallis tests based on their distribution. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Because several patients contributed more than one sample over time, repeated measurements from the same individual could not be treated as independent observations. To account for this clustered structure when assessing the association between SeptiScore and infection, we used linear mixed-effects models with a random intercept for each patient, fitted with the lmerTest package (R version 4.5.1) [26], using type III F-tests with Satterthwaite’s approximation for the degrees of freedom. Two complementary models were specified: one in which infection was defined at the sample level (SeptiScore ~ Infection × Timing + (1 | Patient)), and one using a patient-level infection group (SeptiScore ~ Final infection group × Timing + (1 | Patient)), where “Timing” denoted time since ICU admission and “Final infection group” distinguished patients who developed a healthcare-associated infection during their ICU stay from those who remained infection-free. As sensitivity analyses restricted to independent or paired observations, we compared, among patients contributing both a pre-infection and an infection sample, the first sample obtained before infection onset with the first sample obtained during infection, using a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test. We also compared baseline SeptiScore values between final infection groups using admission samples only, with one independent sample per patient and excluding patients already infected at admission.

In the derivation cohort, a Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve was generated to assess the diagnostic ability of the SeptiScore to discriminate HCAIs from trauma-related SIRS. Two thresholds were predefined.

ROC curves of CRP, white blood cell count and PCT were generated to compare the SeptiScore with other infection biomarkers. The 95% confidence intervals of the AUROC were computed using 1000 bootstrap iterations.

To account for repeated samples contributed by some patients, confidence intervals for the AUROC and diagnostic performance metrics were estimated using a patient-level cluster bootstrap, ensuring that all samples from a given patient were kept together within each bootstrap replicate. A linear regression model was fitted to evaluate the association between SeptiScore and respiratory failure, using the PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio. In the validation cohort, the SeptiScore ROC curve was computed, and diagnostic performance metrics were estimated using the threshold selected in the derivation cohort. No imputation was performed. Mixed-effects models and sensitivity analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.1); all other statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.10.12).

Results

A total of 113 samples were analyzed across both cohorts, including 71 trauma-related SIRS and 42 HCAI episodes.

Derivation cohort

Study population

82 samples were analyzed from 44 patients included in the first tertiary surgical ICU between November 2022 and December 2023 (Appendix 1). Of the 82 samples collected from 44 patients, 50 were obtained during trauma-related SIRS and 32 during HCAI episodes. 29 patients contributed both a trauma-related SIRS and an HCAI sample, while 13 were sampled only during trauma-related SIRS, as they did not develop any HCAI during their ICU stay. Notably, eight patients with trauma-related SIRS were sampled during distinct, clinically separate non-infectious inflammatory episodes (e.g., bleeding or reoperation). All suspected SIRS cases, as initially hypothesized by the attending physician, were ultimately confirmed. Most patients were male (79.5%), with a median age of 40 years (IQR: 28.8 to 55.2). Traumatic brain injury was the primary cause for ICU admission (59%). The 30-day mortality rate was 4.5%. Infection sites and microbiology are presented in Appendix 2. No patient sampled during trauma-related SIRS received curative antibiotic therapy; surgical antibiotic prophylaxis was permitted in cases of open or contaminated wounds (Table 1).

SeptiScore diagnostic accuracy and threshold for HCAIs

The median SeptiScore was significantly higher in patients with HCAI: 8.05, interquartile range (IQR) (6.83 to 8.80) than in trauma-related SIRS patients: 5.80, IQR (5.08 to 7.05); p < 0.001) (Fig. 1A). In a linear mixed-effects model accounting for within-patient correlation, infection status remained strongly associated with SeptiScore values (p < 0.001), whereas sampling time (p = 0.84) and the infection × time interaction (p = 0.74) were not. The random patient effect was statistically significant, confirming the presence of within-patient correlation across repeated samples. When patient group was modelled instead of sample-level infection status, the group × time interaction was significant (p < 0.001), while the main effect of group was not (p = 0.42). Among the 29 patients sampled both before and during infection, SeptiScore increased significantly after infection onset, with a median increase of 2.4 points (95% CI, 1.6 to 3.2; p < 0.001) (Fig. 1B, Appendix 3). In analyses restricted to admission samples, patients who subsequently developed HCAI did not have higher baseline SeptiScore values than those who remained infection-free; conversely, admission SeptiScore values were lower in patients who later developed HCAI (p = 0.008; Appendix 4).

The area under the curve (AUC) for SeptiScore was 0.79 (95% CI: 0.72 to 0.87) (Fig. 2A). We identified 7.2 as the optimal statistical threshold based on the Youden Index, which maximizes the sum of sensitivity and specificity. At this threshold, sensitivity was 75% (95% CI: 0.60 to 0.90) and specificity was 76% (95% CI: 0.64 to 0.88), resulting in a Youden Index of 0.51 (Appendix 5). However, considering clinical priorities and the asymmetric risk for patients, we selected an optimal clinical threshold of 6.7 to prioritize sensitivity and negative predictive value to minimize missed HCAIs. At this threshold, sensitivity was 84% (95% CI: 0.72 to 0.97), specificity 66% (95% CI: 0.53 to 0.79), and negative predictive value 87% (95% CI: 0.76 to 0.98), with a Youden Index of 0.50 (Appendix 5). This allowed us to achieve the best likelihood ratio for negative results of the test (LR⁻ = 0.24, 95% CI: 0.04 to 0.53). Diagnostic performance compared to conventional biomarkers and association with respiratory failure

We compared the diagnostic performance of SeptiScore, C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin, and leukocyte counts for the diagnosis of HCAI. The AUC values were 0.73 (95% CI: 0.65 to 0.81) for CRP, 0.57 (95% CI: 0.49 to 0.66) for procalcitonin, and 0.64 (95% CI: 0.55 to 0.72) for leukocytes. SeptiScore showed better discrimination than the other biomarkers in diagnosing HCAIs (Fig. 3). A detailed analysis of CRP diagnostic performance is provided in Appendix 6. SeptiScore and CRP were positively correlated (R2 = 44%, p < 0.0001) (Appendix 7). Given that over 90% of HCAIs were ventilator-associated pneumonias, we further assessed the relationship between SeptiScore and the severity of respiratory failure. All ARDS cases in our cohort were attributable to microbiologically confirmed infections. Linear regression analysis demonstrated a significant inverse association with PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio (p = 0.022), with SeptiScore decreasing by 0.4 units for every 100 mmHg increase in PaO₂/FiO₂ (Appendix 8).

Validation cohort

Study population

To validate the robustness of our findings, we applied the results from the derivation cohort to a validation cohort. 31 samples were analyzed from 31 patients admitted to the polyvalent ICU of AP-HM, Hôpital Nord, between September 2022 and April 2023 (Appendix 9). Of these, 21 were sampled in the presence of a trauma-related SIRS, and 10 were sampled at the time of clinical suspicion of an HCAI, subsequently confirmed microbiologically. Most patients were male (80.6%), with a median age of 49 years (IQR: 40 to 63.5). The primary causes for ICU admission were orthopedic trauma (limbs, pelvis, or spine) (54.8%), thoracic trauma (45.2%), and traumatic brain injury (TBI) (41.9%). Twenty-four patients (77%) required invasive mechanical ventilation. The median length of ICU stay was 17 days (IQR: 8 to 32.5), and the 30-day mortality rate was 19.4%. There were no significant differences between groups in terms of antibiotic prophylaxis or anti-inflammatory treatments prior to SeptiScore sampling; however, surgical procedures were more frequent in the HCAI group (Table 2). Infection sites and microbiological data are presented in Appendix 10.

Performance of the SeptiScore optimal clinical threshold in the validation cohort

Using the validation cohort, sensitivity and specificity analyses yielded an AUC of 0.98 (95% CI: 0.94 to 1.00) (Fig. 4A). For the optimal clinical threshold of 6.7, as identified in the derivation cohort, the sensitivity and specificity were 1.00 and 0.90, respectively. In this cohort, all patients with an HCAI at the time of SeptiScore measurement had values above 6.7. However, two patients with SeptiScore values above 6.7 did not develop HCAI during their ICU stay (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

In our exploratory derivation and validation study, SeptiScore showed promising diagnostic performance for identifying HCAIs in critically ill trauma patients. A threshold of ≥ 6.7 provided clinically useful discrimination between HCAIs and trauma-related SIRS, with a sensitivity-oriented profile intended to limit missed infections. SeptiScore also appeared to discriminate infection better than conventional biomarkers, including CRP, procalcitonin, and leukocyte count. In addition, higher SeptiScore values were associated with more severe respiratory failure in patients requiring invasive mechanical ventilation.

Our results align with previous studies validating SeptiCyte LAB [16–18] and SeptiCyte RAPID [15] to differentiate sepsis and non-infectious SIRS in ICU patients. Analysis of our two cohorts shows that a threshold of 6.7 was effective for discriminating these two conditions. By contrast, higher thresholds have been reported in recent surgical cohorts: one study identified an optimal threshold of 8.2 to diagnose secondary peritonitis in patients with acute mesenteric ischemia undergoing emergency surgery [28], while another reported thresholds ranging from 6.2 to 8.2 for early postoperative sepsis detection [29]. These differences likely reflect variations in baseline inflammatory status and immune activation across populations, reinforcing the need for context-specific validation of SeptiScore thresholds. SeptiCyte RAPID outperformed conventional biomarkers for distinguishing HCAI from non-infectious systemic inflammation. Indeed, CRP, PCT, and leukocyte count tend to increase during inflammation, resulting in false positives. Our findings are consistent with literature reporting an AUC for CRP between 0.7 and 0.8 to distinguish sepsis from SIRS [30, 31]. Procalcitonin showed limited discriminative performance in our cohort and performed less well than CRP. This finding is consistent with recent data in severe trauma patients showing that PCT may perform no better than CRP [32], as well as studies in medical ICU sepsis suggesting that PCT is not always reliable for distinguishing SIRS from sepsis [33]. While CRP and PCT levels rise as the infection progresses, SeptiCyte RAPID may detect HCAIs at an earlier stage by analyzing gene expression patterns. Repeated SeptiScore measurements may facilitate earlier HCAI diagnosis, potentially preceding the appearance of clinical signs. As demonstrated, gene expression measured by SeptiCyte RAPID responds early to infection, often before the onset or worsening of organ failure in sepsis [15]. Early diagnosis of HCAIs is critical for optimizing patient care and minimizing unnecessary antibiotic use. For ventilator-associated pneumonia, the infectious process develops gradually [34], typically preceding clinical manifestations by several days [35]. Notably, a previous study identified a transcriptional signature of bacterial infection two days before pneumonia onset [36], suggesting that the SeptiScore could potentially contribute to earlier HCAI detection. This early detection may allow prompt intervention, potentially improving patient outcomes.

Our study has several strengths. First, the threshold identified in the derivation cohort was tested in an independent cohort recruited at a different center, with a different case-mix. Although limited in size, this independent evaluation showed directional consistency of the pre-specified threshold across populations, providing preliminary external support for our findings. Second, in the derivation cohort, we measured the SeptiScore at ICU admission (corresponding to trauma-related SIRS) and again at the time of HCAI onset, allowing assessment of its diagnostic performance and temporal progression. Monitoring SeptiScore kinetics is essential for evaluating biomarkers, a dimension often overlooked in previous studies [19].

Our study has several limitations. First, because ventilator-associated pneumonia was predominant in our derivation cohort (> 90%), it is difficult to generalize our results to all HCAIs. This predominance may have influenced biomarker performance, as SeptiScore might not perform as well in other types of infections. However, the higher performance observed in the validation cohort, where ventilator-associated pneumonia was less common, suggests potential utility beyond these infections. Although the specificity was acceptable in our study, a few false positives were observed, highlighting the importance of integrating SeptiScore with clinical and microbiological assessment to avoid unnecessary antibiotic exposure. Overall, the performance in our study was superior to that reported in a previous study where SeptiCyte LAB distinguished between infectious and non-infectious acute respiratory failure [37]. These differences could be due to the younger age and fewer comorbidities of trauma patients. A second limitation is the small size of our validation cohort, which included only 10 HCAI events and 21 trauma-related SIRS events. This precludes precise estimation of diagnostic accuracy: although the threshold of 6.7 performed consistently (AUC 0.98), these estimates are imprecise and likely optimistic and should be regarded as showing directional consistency rather than confirming accuracy. A more adequately powered, multicenter external validation is therefore required before clinical use. The validation cohort was also retrospective, whereas the derivation cohort was prospective, which may have introduced differences in sampling and data collection. The difference in diagnostic performance between the two cohorts may also reflect differences in case-mix. The validation cohort had more diverse infection sources, including central nervous system and intra-abdominal infections, which may have produced clearer clinical phenotypes, whereas patients in the derivation cohort had longer ICU stays and prolonged mechanical ventilation, suggesting more complex or overlapping inflammatory states.

A third limitation is the lack of biomarker kinetics evaluation after starting antibiotic therapy in patients with HCAIs. Similar to procalcitonin [38, 39], this biomarker could potentially help guide antibiotic duration and de-escalation strategies. Future studies should explore the clinical utility of SeptiScore-guided approaches, including whether its use can safely reduce unnecessary antibiotic exposure, optimize infection management, or improve patient outcomes in randomized controlled settings. Fourth, we acknowledge the absence of baseline samples, which could not be obtained because trauma patients are typically admitted after the onset of systemic inflammation. Fifth, both cohorts were from French ICUs, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other healthcare settings. Finally, HCAI classification was performed by the treating intensivist, who was blinded to SeptiScore results, rather than by an independent adjudication panel, which may introduce some degree of classification bias.

Conclusion

SeptiScore showed promising performance for distinguishing healthcare-associated infections from trauma-related SIRS in critically ill trauma patients. A threshold of 6.7 provided potentially clinically useful discrimination and appeared to outperform conventional biomarkers in the derivation cohort. These findings support the potential value of host-response biomarkers in complex inflammatory settings such as severe trauma but remain preliminary. Adequately powered multicenter studies are needed to confirm diagnostic performance and to determine whether SeptiScore-guided management improves patient outcomes.
