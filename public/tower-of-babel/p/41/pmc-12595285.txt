Behavior of Dissociated Salts in Aqueous Solution: Insights From T2 Relaxation Time and Signal Intensity Using a LF‐NMR
Authors: Afroza Sultana, Ali Asghari, Christophe Cordella, Seddik Khalloufi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study explores the influence of NaCl, KCl, CaCl2, and MgCl2, salts commonly used in the food industry, on aqueous solutions using transverse (T2) relaxometry in low‐ field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF‐NMR) at a salt‐to‐water ratio ranging from 0 to 0.1079 mol/mol. The results demonstrate that T2 relaxation times are significantly affected by the chemical nature and concentration of the ions, with divalent salts exhibiting distinct behavior compared to monovalent salts. Notably, the kosmotropic (structure‐making) and chaotropic (structure‐breaking) properties of the salts were found to influence relaxation times. Mg2+ exhibited the most pronounced effect (low relaxation time) due to its high charge density and strong hydration ability, followed by Ca2+, Na+, and K+. Relaxation times decreased with increasing salt concentration for MgCl2, CaCl2, and NaCl (from 3080 ms at 0% to 504, 1671, and 2796 ms at 0.1079 mol/mol, respectively), whereas the chaotropic KCl solution had extended relaxation times (3884 ms at 0.0771 mol/mol). A novel finding of this study is the exponential decay of peak area in salt solutions, as evidenced by experimental data. The alteration in the structural arrangement of water and the synergistic movement of cations or anions in higher‐mass salt solutions are hypothesized to underlie the observed behavior, although the exact reason requires further study. Electrical conductivity (EC), water activity, and the enthalpy of solution upon salt incorporation provide further insights into the behavior of salt solutions. This research advances the understanding of how ionic solutes modulate water dynamics, affecting both relaxation time and the total peak area.Keywords: chaotrope ions, kosmotrope ions, LF‐NMR, salt solution, signal intensity

Introduction

Water is widely applied in food processing, sanitation, cleaning, and other maintenance tasks within the food industry. The dynamics and structural properties of pure water are modified when different solutes or ions are added, significantly affecting its overall properties, such as cohesiveness (Rezaei‐Ghaleh 2022). Excessive salinity or high amounts of ions can greatly impact water quality, which in turn hampers its effectiveness for specific applications. For example, Mg2+ and Ca2+ ions increase water hardness, leading to scale, film, or precipitate formation on equipment surfaces. This can cause further corrosion and reduce the effectiveness of cleaning and sanitation efforts (Bhagwat 2019; Wujie 2011).

Salts consist of cations and anions held together by ionic bonds and exist in their ionic form in aqueous solutions through the solvation process (Manciu et al. 2017). When a salt dissociates in water, the electric fields of the individual ions (cations and anions) start to rearrange the bonding with nearby water molecules, creating hydration shells around them and altering the dynamics of the bulk water (Rezaei‐Ghaleh 2022). The size of the hydration shell can vary depending on ion concentration and charge density. The innermost shell typically becomes immobilized due to the strong ionic field, with more disordered water molecules present in the outer layers of the hydration shell (Conte 2015). According to the literature, ions influence hydrogen bonding between water molecules, but the effects of different ions on water structure remain somewhat controversial (Foglia et al. 2022; Guo et al. 2020; Lee et al. 2017; Qiu et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2022). Particularly, this controversy concerns whether ions affect bulk water's hydrogen‐bonded structure beyond their hydration layers, or if observed changes are due to the properties of hydrated ions (Rezaei‐Ghaleh 2022). The binding capacity of ions with water molecules affects the mobility of water molecules. Advanced tools capable of rapidly analyzing molecular dynamics, such as NMR, provide valuable insights into the effects of ions on water structure and mobility. For example, Laage and Stirnemann (2019) studied water dynamics influenced by the added ions in both dilute and concentrated salt solutions using NMR. Rezaei‐Ghaleh (2022) utilized 1H and 17O longitudinal (T1) relaxation times to study the reorientational mobility of water molecules in saturated salt solutions. Conte (2015) investigated ions’ effect on water dynamics using a low‐field NMR (LF‐NMR) with a fast field cycling setup by analyzing T1 relaxation times. The relaxation times are the times required for the magnetization to transition from a nonequilibrium state to the equilibrium distribution of the nuclear spin (Conte and Alonzo 2013). T1 and T2 relaxation times are obtained from NMR, where T1 represents the time needed for the recovery of the T1 component of the magnetization along the z‐axis, and T2 represents the time required for the decay of the T2 component of the magnetization to zero (Conte and Alonzo 2013). Despite extensive studies on ion–water interactions, most literature has focused on T1 relaxation time or high‐field NMR technique. In contrast, LF‐NMR requires no sample preparation and conducts measurements in a simple, cost‐effective, and non‐invasive manner within a very short time (milliseconds) (Niumag Corporation 2020). In some cases, the T2 relaxation time is more preferable due to its fast analysis time, thus enabling high throughput. As far as we know, the effect of total hydrogen content in salt solutions (calculated from the mass of water in the sample, molecular mass of water, atomic mass of hydrogen, and number of hydrogen atoms in water molecule) on the total signal in 1H NMR has not yet been reported.

To address these gaps, this study focused on identifying the influence of ions in aqueous solutions using T2 relaxometry with a 0.5 T LF‐NMR. Additionally, the effects of other parameters such as conductivity, water activity, and enthalpy of salt solutions on T2 were investigated to explore their possible correlation. Two monovalent (NaCl, KCl) and two divalent chloride salts (CaCl2, MgCl2) were chosen to investigate their behavior in aqueous solution. These salts were selected due to their widespread application in the food processing industry. For example, chloride salts such as KCl, CaCl2, and MgCl2 are extensively used as replacements or substitutes for NaCl in meat products, cheese, and bread without altering the sensory qualities of these products, helping to reduce the adverse effects of excessive NaCl consumption (Ayyash et al. 2013; Bansal and Mishra 2020; Charlton et al. 2007; Vidal et al. 2021).

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Materials

Analytical‐grade crystalline salts were used for solution preparation. Specifically, NaCl and KCl were obtained from Fisher Scientific (USA); MgCl2, KNO3, KI, NaF, LiCl, and LiNO3 from Sigma‐Aldrich (China); CaCl2 from Merck KGaA (China); and Mg(NO3)2·6H2O from Ward's Science (Canada). Deionized water was used throughout all experiments.

Preparation of Salt Solution

Salt solutions were prepared by dissolving the required amount of each salt in deionized water. The salt‐to‐water molar ratios ranged from 0.0077 to 0.1079 mol/mol. These concentrations were selected to cover a range from dilute to near‐saturation conditions, enabling the observation of relaxation behavior across different regimes of ion‐water interactions. The exact quantities (g/10 g water) for each salt and concentration are presented in Table 1. After dissolution, all solutions were equilibrated at 32°C for at least 1 h to eliminate thermal fluctuations that could affect NMR measurements. In addition, various amounts of water were used specifically 2, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10 g to investigate the effect of sample mass on NMR measurements. Briefly, the range of 2–10 g was chosen to vary the number of protons (1H) and investigate the relationship between total hydrogen content and NMR signal intensity. Masses below 2 g produced weak signals, while amounts above 10 g exceeded the sample holder capacity.

T2 Relaxometry Obtained by LF‐NMR

T2 relaxation spectra were measured following the method described by Asghari et al. (2024) and Sultana et al. (2024), using a LF‐NMR (PQ001‐20‐025 V Pulsed NMR Analyzer from Niumag Corporation, China) equipped with a permanent magnet of 0.5±0.08 T and operated at a controlled temperature of 32°C. The sample holder dimensions were ϕ 25 mm × 25 mm in height. Experiments, based on initial free induction decay (FID) tests, were conducted at a frequency bandwidth of 100 Hz with specific settings for analog gain (20 dB), digital gain (3), preamplifier gain (0), and radio frequency delay time (0.250 ms). Optimal 90° (P1) and 180° (P2) pulse times were determined for each treatment. To ensure accurate results and minimize noise interference, five observations were taken from each sample at 240 s intervals, as shown in Figure 1. The Larmor resonance frequency of 1H nuclei were measured before each observation, and scanning was limited to twice per observation to prevent sample heating. The signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) consistently remained above 4000 throughout the experiments. The simultaneous iterative reconstruction technique (SIRT) was applied to the data obtained from Carr‐Purcell‐Meiboom‐Gill (CPMG) curves to convert them to T2 relaxation time (Carr and Purcell 1954; Meiboom and Gill 1958; Trampert and Leveque 1990).

Timing between the sequences of the scans in LF‐NMR (fL : finding the Larmor frequency, TW: 10000 ms waiting time).

Measurement of Electrical Conductivity (EC) of Salt Solution

EC measurements were performed using an Orion 3‐star conductivity meter (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For each measurement, 50 mL of the sample was prepared in a beaker. The conductivity meter was calibrated using a standard solution with a conductivity of 1413 µS/cm, corresponding to a total dissolved solids (TDS) value of 692 ppm as NaCl (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The conductivity probe was rinsed with distilled water and dried prior to each test. The meter was allowed to stabilize, and then the conductivity reading was recorded. Each sample was measured in triplicate. The average of the three observations was calculated and presented in this study.

Statistical Analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Linear regressions were performed to determine the relationship between T2 and salt concentration before and after critical concentration (CC). Regression analysis was performed and coefficients of determination (R2 ) were reported where applicable.

Results and discussions

Effect of Salt Concentration on the Relaxation Behavior in LF‐NMR

The relaxometry in LF‐NMR shows different behaviors of salts based on their types and concentrations, as illustrated in Figure 2. T2 of divalent salts such as CaCl2 and MgCl2 decreased rapidly as the concentration of these salts increased. The salts were added incrementally until near saturation; however, complete saturation was avoided to ensure proper dissolution of the salts in the solution. The mass of the water in the sample holder was maintained at 10 g for all salt solutions. Monovalent salts such as NaCl and KCl exhibited different trends in the relaxation distribution curve. For NaCl, T2 decreased slightly at the initial concentration up to a 0.0154 mol/mol salt‐to‐water ratio. Subsequently, it increased sharply until the concentration reached a 0.0462 mol/mol salt‐to‐water ratio, after which it began to decrease again. T2 of NaCl solution decreased from 3080 ms at the initial concentration (0 mol/mol, salt‐free solution) to 2796 ms at 0.1079 mol/mol. The most significant T2 decrease was observed for MgCl2, (from 3080 to 504 ms), followed by CaCl2 (from 3080 to 1671 ms) when increasing salt‐to‐water ratio from 0 to 0.1079 mol/mol. In contrast, KCl showed the opposite trend, with T2 increasing as the salt‐to‐water ratio increased, up to 0.0771 mol/mol. Beyond this concentration, further addition was not possible due to the saturation point of KCl. The relaxation of proton is dependent on the strength of dipolar interactions; the weaker the interaction, the longer the relaxation time, and vice versa (Conte 2015). Under dilute conditions, water molecules predominantly behave as bulk water due to the minimal electrostatic effect of ions, remaining unrestricted (Conte 2015; Rezaei‐Ghaleh 2022). Although the addition of ions (in concentrated solutions) increases viscosity and restricts water molecule, according to Jones and Dole (1929), the solution can become either more viscous or more fluid (less viscous) depending on the nature of the ions (Lo Nostro and Ninham 2012; Turton et al. 2008), leading to significant variations in relaxation times.

T2 relaxation time and total peak area obtained from the LF‐NMR spectra as a function of salt concentration.

Unlike existing literature, which primarily focused on relaxation time and rate, this study emphasizes the peak area obtained from relaxation spectra. As shown in Figure 2, the total peak area of almost all the salt solutions drastically reduced with increasing salt concentration. MgCl2 exhibited a different trend compared to the other salts; after reaching a concentration of 0.0308 mol/mol salt‐to‐water ratio, the total area remained stable or even slightly increased, rather than continuing to decrease. However, the reduction in peak area was more pronounced at higher concentrations compared to dilute conditions. Table 2 presents a clear overview of the average percentage change in the total area of the peak relative to pure water (used as reference). In this study, a single peak predominantly arose for all solutions, consistent with the findings of Rezaei‐Ghaleh (2022), who mentioned that water molecules mostly position themselves in the first layer of the hydration shell, resulting in the detection of one ion‐bound water in the NMR signal. Table 2 shows that near the saturation point, the highest reductions in peak area were approximately 58%, 49%, 40%, and 24%, in the order of KCl > NaCl > CaCl2> MgCl2. Since the anion is the same in all solutions, it is assumed that the cation has a more significant influence on the observed behavior. Rezaei‐Ghaleh (2022) also found that the type of cation affected the linewidth of peaks in saturated alkali chloride solutions and influenced water structure and dynamics as observed by 17O NMR. Several studies using LF‐NMR have indicated that the peak area is proportional to the number of available water molecules in a sample and reduction in peak area reflects changes in water component (mobile, immobilized, or bound) (Cheng et al. 2019; Li et al. 2018). Consequently, decreases in peak area and shorter T2 preliminary indicate a transition of water mobility towards bound water (Cheng et al. 2019; Li et al. 2018).

These findings on peak area can be correlated with hydration number, surface tension, ionic radius, and charge density of the electrolytes. For example, the hydration numbers of Mg2+, Ca2+, Na+, and K+ in the first and second layers of the hydration shell decrease in descending order, with the first layer containing fewer water molecules facing the electrolyte than the second layer (Yuan et al. 2018). The hydration number typically decreases with increasing solute concentration, reflecting a lack of available water molecules to be attracted by the solute, as this number represents the amount of water molecules attached to the solute (Gharsallaoui et al. 2008). In different layers of the hydration shell, the hydration numbers are as follows: in the first layer, K+, Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+ have values of 2.94, 3.85, 7.44, and 8.69, respectively; and in the second layer, these values are 22.39, 23.41, 26.69, and 28.25, respectively (Yuan et al. 2018). Moreover, the surface tension of these solutions increases in the order of KCl, NaCl, CaCl2, and MgCl2 at the same concentration, and this trend persists with increasing concentration (Yuan et al. 2018). The authors observed that surface tension aligns with the hydration ability of the cations, explaining that greater hydration ability pulls water molecules into the internal portion of the solution. Additionally, surface tension is strongly associated with ionic radius; smaller ionic radii lead to greater ionic bonding energy and increased surface tension (Leroy et al. 2010; Yuan et al. 2018). The surface tension may not have a direct relation to peak area reduction, but it depends on hydration ability and hydration energy, i.e., the bonding strength of ion–water interaction. This interaction influences the water structure and hydrogen bonding network. In this study, surface tension was considered a supportive interpretation rather than a definite measure Supplementary materials.

It is well known that divalent cations have greater hydration ability than monovalent cations. However, among cations with the same valence, those with smaller radii have a greater hydration ability than those with larger radii (Yuan et al. 2018). For example, the radii of K+, Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+ are 1.38, 1.02, 1.00, and 0.72 Å, respectively (Dean 1999), which corresponds to their hydration numbers. Ion charge density is even more directly related to hydration ability than ionic radii; as charge density increases, so does hydration ability. Cation with higher charge density enhance electrostatic forces between it and nearby water molecules, reducing water‐water interactions (Collins and Washabaugh 1985; Conte 2015; Hribar et al. 2002; Leroy et al. 2010; Rezaei‐Ghaleh 2022). These ions attract more water molecules, organizing them in a structured manner, thereby influencing the structure and dynamics of water molecules. This study finds a correlation between the peak area obtained from LF‐NMR and the behavior of cations in terms of surface tension, hydration ability, and charge density. Therefore, it is speculated that the signal corresponding to peak area in proton‐NMR is influenced by the structural behavior of ions with water molecules.

CC, Properties of Salt Solutions and Their Relationship With T2

Further studies were conducted to correlate the EC and water activity (aw) of salt solutions with relaxation time, as shown in Figure 3. Figure 3(a) clearly demonstrates that the T2 of all salt solutions can be divided into two distinct regions, with a noticeable change occurring at a specific concentration, referred to in this study as the CC of 0.0462 mol/mol. Although this CC value was determined empirically, it consistently reflects the onset of noticeable effects in T2 relaxation time. Beyond this CC, the T2 values exhibit a linear trend, with a high regression coefficient (R2 ) of 0.9 for KCl and ≥ 0.99 for the other salts. The T2 values of CaCl2 and MgCl2 show a downward trend both before and after the CC, while KCl shows a consistent upward trend. Conversely, NaCl shows an upward trend before the CC and a downward trend afterward. However, in the first region (before CC), particularly in low‐concentration solutions, NaCl has the lowest R2 (0.73), followed by KCl (0.85), CaCl2 (0.87), and MgCl2 (1.00). In dilute conditions, the availability of bulk water and the lower number of ions result in fluctuations in T2, as bulk water behavior may still dominate. Consistent with other studies, this research confirms a strong correlation between the interaction of salts and water (Conte 2015; Rezaei‐Ghaleh 2022). The behavior of salt solutions can be described by the concepts of kosmotropes and chaotropes. Kosmotrope, or structure maker, and chaotrope, or structure breaker, indicate the ion that has the ability to increase and decrease the hydrogen bonding network in the water molecule, respectively, while organizing and disorganizing the water molecule in the hydration shell (Conte 2015; Lo Nostro and Ninham 2012; Luo et al. 2020). Basically, in an aqueous‐ionic solution, water molecules can stay in the three regions, such as: (i) the closest region: rigidly aligned water molecules, which are carried together with ions if they move in the solution; (ii) the second region: poorly aligned water molecules, where the H‐bond network is broken as compared to bulk water; and (iii) the rest is bulk water (Impey et al. 1983). The ability of ions to induce or perturb the orientation of water molecules determines their kosmotropic or chaotropic nature (Galindo et al. 2023). The arrangement of water molecules in the hydration shell also affects the viscosity of the solution and, consequently, water mobility. For example, Turton et al. (2008) reported that the viscosity of MgCl2 increased exponentially with concentration due to its strong kosmotropic behavior, whereas NaCl showed only a slight increase.

Finding the critical concentration (CC) based on T2 relaxation time.

The variance between maximum and minimum relaxation times corresponding to the concentration of the salt in the solution was calculated to be approximately 17%, 20%, 89%, and 461% for NaCl, KCl, CaCl2, and MgCl2, respectively. Notably, the greater variation in relaxation times for divalent salts compared to monovalent salts aligns with the higher charge density of divalent cations, making them more kosmotropic (Conte 2015; Kanno et al. 2006; Soper and Weckström 2006). The findings of this study on KCl and CaCl2 are consistent with previous research by Conte (2015), which identified potassium as a weak chaotrope and calcium as a kosmotrope, while chloride is a weak chaotrope. As more KCl is added, its chaotropic nature becomes more pronounced, leading to weaker dipolar interactions and prolonged relaxation times. The increase in intermolecular proton‐proton distance in KCl solutions likely contributes to the longer T2 values compared to pure water (Rezaei‐Ghaleh 2022). On the other hand, CaCl2 and MgCl2 demonstrate a significant reduction in relaxation time with increasing concentration, despite the weak chaotropic nature of chloride ions. The stronger kosmotropic behavior of these ions promotes electrostatic interactions and higher polarization of atoms to be reoriented with the ions, as in O–H·Anion and O–Cation bonds (Kanno et al. 2006). Highly concentrated NaCl solutions exhibit similar trend with the aforementioned study. Moreover, study revealed that the residence time of water in the hydration shell of Mg2+ (67 µs) is much higher than Ca2+ (3.1 ns), Na+ (9.9 ps), and K+ (4.8 ps), respectively (Impey et al. 1983; Larentzos and Criscenti 2008), indicating that the strong hydration of Mg2+ followed by Ca2+, Na+, and K+, respectively (Galindo et al. 2023). This stronger bonding leads to faster relaxation rates and shorter relaxation times. However, in diluted conditions, the neutral or weakly kosmotropic nature of NaCl may not overcome the chaotropic influence of chloride ions. Therefore, it is assuming that below the concentration of 0.0462 mol/mol, NaCl behaves similarly to KCl, exhibiting weak electrostatic interactions. Beyond this concentration, stronger electrostatic interactions emerge, causing NaCl to behave more like other kosmotropes. This assumption aligns with previous studies that report NaCl as a neutral or weakly kosmotropic salt, exhibiting no or minor structuring effect (Lim and Song 2024; Onuk et al. 2025). However, the CC value may not be universal for all salts, and further investigation with additional salts is required.

According to AMC Drilling Optimization (2019), the values of aw of salt solutions decreased with increasing salt concentration (Figure 3(b)), with divalent salts showing a sharper decline than monovalent salts. Particularly, the order of decline is MgCl2> CaCl2> NaCl > KCl. The minimal decline in the aw of KCl suggests a weak interaction between KCl and water molecules, which aligns with the relaxation time data. According to the moisture sorption isotherm of Labuza (1970), the moisture content available for chemical reactions increases rapidly above an aw of approximately 0.8, and Bell and Labuza (2000) found that significant moisture adsorption occurs around an aw of 0.85. Therefore, a lower aw indicates stronger binding of ions with water molecules. Although MgCl2 and CaCl2 started to decline in aw before reaching the CC, it is noticeable that aw approaches 0.8 near the CC.

Dependence of water activity (aw) and electrical conductivity (EC) on the concentration of salt in the aqueous solutions (Source of aw: AMC Drilling Optimization (2019), conducted at 20°C for 100% purity).

Results reported by Schmidt (2020) correlated aw of macromolecular food systems (e.g., casein, pectin, corn starch, sodium alginate) with T2 values obtained from pulse‐NMR, showing that T2 was low at low aw and increased exponentially with higher aw. This correlation aligns with the observed dropping T2 trends in NaCl, MgCl2, and CaCl2 solutions at higher concentrations. In contrast, the aw of KCl did not drop much with concentration, and other factors such as the chaotropic nature of K+ and Cl− contributed to the disruption of hydrogen bonding in water‐ion interactions. While aw represents macroscopic translational mobility of water, NMR reflects molecular rotational and translational mobility (Schmidt 2020), making it challenging to establish a direct correlation between them.

In general, EC reflects the ability of a solution to conduct electricity, which depends on the dissolved salts. Figure 3(b) shows that EC initially increases with salt concentration, indicating more free ions in the solution. However, for divalent salts, EC reaches a peak near 0.05 mol/mol, close to the CC (Figure 3(a)). In contrast, the EC of monovalent salts continues to rise, with KCl showing the highest EC (361.5 mS/cm) compared to NaCl (251.4 mS/cm), suggesting that KCl solutions have more free‐moving ions carrying electric charge. Typically, EC increases with concentration, but at high concentrations, ion pairing, increased viscosity, and ion atmosphere formation can decrease EC. This calculation shows that Mg2+ (1.28 C/Å3) and Ca2+ (0.48 C/Å3) have higher values than Na+ (0.22 C/Å3) and K+ (0.09 C/Å3). Thus, divalent salts have stronger electrostatic forces and may have a greater tendency to ion pair with Cl−, reducing the number of free ions and decreasing EC at high concentrations. Conversely, the weaker electrostatic interactions in monovalent salts allow EC to continue increasing with concentration. The Debye–Huckel limiting theory explains that at higher concentrations, counterions (oppositely charged ions) create an ion atmosphere that shields the charge of ions, reducing their mobility and conductivity (Labuza and Altunakar 2020). Relative viscosity (viscosity of salt solution/viscosity of pure water) depends on ion concentration and induces the degree of water structuring (Galindo et al. 2023). The Jones‐Dole coefficient (B) is a constant calculated using conductivity, concentration, and relative viscosity, expressing the nature of ions in aqueous solutions (Galindo et al. 2023). Ions exhibit their kosmotropic and chaotropic nature when B > 0 and B < 0, respectively (Collins 2012; Marcus 2010). This coefficient has been reported for Mg2+, Ca2+, Na+, K+, and Cl− as 0.385, 0.284, 0.085, −0.009, and −0.005 dm3mol−1, respectively (Galindo et al. 2023), indicating a sequence from structure makers (Mg2+) to structure breakers (Cl+). Therefore, it is easily understandable that divalent salts (MgCl2 and CaCl2) increase viscosity at higher concentrations. Higher viscosity restricts ion and water molecule mobility, reducing EC for divalent salts (Figure 3(b)). Our study confirms that higher EC is associated with a stable or increasing T2 with minimal variance from pure water (such as KCl). In contrast, decreasing EC corresponds to greater T2 variance and lower T2 values, particularly at higher concentrations. Notably, NaCl does not fully follow this trend, likely due to the chaotropic influence of Cl− on the relatively low kosmotropic nature of Na+. Overall, B provides subtle information whether ions are structure makers or breakers and their effect on viscosity. Structure makers increase viscosity more than structure breakers, which reduces mobility and consequently influences EC and T2.

Correlation Between Enthalpy and T2 of the Ionic Aqueous Solution

When solute and solvent come together, the dissolution process begins, causing a change in the enthalpy of the solution (∆HSolution). This change of enthalpy depends on the attractive and repulsive interactions between the solvent and solute (Halpern 2024). When ∆HSolution > 0, the process is endothermic, meaning it absorbs heat from the surroundings. Conversely, when ∆HSolution < 0, the process is exothermic, releasing heat into the system. Although the value of ∆HSolution can change based on concentration and temperature, the thermodynamic nature of the process (endothermic or exothermic) remains consistent for that particular solute. Figure 4(a) and 4(b) represents the values of ∆HSolution at 25°C for various salt solutions (adapted from Halpern (2024)) and T2 relaxation time at a concentration of 0.0617 mol/mol. Although findings at a single concentration may limit broader applicability, this approach effectively served the initial goal of determining the correlation between ∆HSolution and T2. Interestingly, T2 was found to be related to whether the process was endothermic or exothermic. It was observed that in endothermic processes (∆HSolution > 0), T2 was longer (>3000 ms (corresponding T2 of pure water), whereas in exothermic processes (∆HSolution < 0), T2 values were shorter (<3000 ms). When ∆HSolution = 0, T2 was approximately 3000 ms. However, T2 did not strictly follow a chronological order corresponding to ∆HSolution values, indicating that while heat absorption or release affects the relaxation rate, it is not the sole influencing factor. Other factors, such as the chemical nature of cations and anions, their hydration, and ion‐pairing ability, also greatly impact the relaxation process. The nature of cations and anions plays a crucial role in both the thermodynamic process and relaxation rate. It has been reported that combinations of kosmotropic cations and anions (kosmo‐kosmo) or chaotropic cations and anions (chao‐chao) are responsible for endothermic processes, while kosmotropic‐chaotropic combinations (kosmo‐chao) typically result in exothermic processes (Lo Nostro and Ninham 2012). For example, NO3 −, I−, Cl−, F−, and K+ display increasing chaotropic strength, while Mg2+, Ca2+, and Li+ show increasing kosmotropic behavior. The combination of these cations and anions influences the heat absorption and release during the dissolution process, which in turn affects relaxation time. Additionally, the nature of the ion, whether simple (Cl−) or complex (NO3 −), affects the organization and strength of hydrogen bonding with water molecules in the hydration shell. Asymmetrical hydration has been reported for salts containing NO3 −, leading to hydrogen bonds of varying lengths and strengths (Conte 2015). This variation in bonding can cause a V‐shaped relaxation rate pattern for salts like ammonium and sodium nitrate, which exhibit both kosmotropic and chaotropic behavior depending on concentration (Conte 2015). In our study, although MgCl2 displayed greater negative values of ∆HSolution, its T2 was shorter than that of Mg(NO3)2. This may be due to the disorganized hydration in the presence of NO3 −. Moreover, both weak and strong hydrogen bonds can be formed with NO3 − depending on concentration (Conte 2015). Overall, Figure 4(c) summarizes the correlation between ∆HSolution and T2 for several mono‐ and divalent salt solutions. A straight line between ∆HSolution and T2 was observed with the R2 value of 0.69. The plausible reason behind this finding is that ∆HSolution is distributed among all atoms in the solution, including oxygen, cations, and anions, rather than only hydrogen atoms. Consequently, the fraction of energy transferred to hydrogen varies depending on the nature of the ions present. Since T2 depends only on hydrogen atoms, the molecular mobility and T2 vary according to the energy transferred to the hydrogen atoms. Moreover, the lattice energy of salt differs. Thus, this R2 between the ∆HSolution and T2 relaxation time of hydrogen atoms cannot be very strong. In this sense, the quantitative correlation is moderate, but the qualitative relationship clearly shows that larger positive or negative enthalpies correspond to larger changes in T2. However, since a single concentration was used in this experiment, a more detailed explanation is not possible at this time and requires further investigation.

Relationship Between Total Hydrogen Content and the Peak Area in T2 Relaxometry

Generally, it is well‐known that the signal intensity obtained from LF‐NMR, as the total area under a peak, corresponds to the total proton content of the sample. In our previous experiment (Asghari et al. 2025), we observed that the summation of signal intensities (peak area) in LF‐NMR was influenced by the quantity of hydrogens involved in hydrogen‐bonding of the sample. To investigate the reliability for salt solutions, different masses of water such as 2, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10 g was used separately in the sample holder, while keeping the concentration of salt constant (same ratio of salt and water) as previously mentioned. The total hydrogen content was calculated based on the mass of the solution and plotted on the X‐axis against the total peak area obtained from LF‐NMR on the Y‐axis, as shown in Figure 5. Interestingly, a different trend of results was observed, which deviates from the conventional theory regarding the relationship between total hydrogen content (proton) to the signal intensity as aforementioned. Two distinct factors were responsible on observed signal intensity: (i) peak area based on increasing concentration while keeping the total hydrogen content constant (indicated by the downward arrow); and (ii) the peak area based on increasing total hydrogen content while maintaining the same concentration (indicated by the left‐to‐right arrow). At a low mass of the water (2 g), the peak area for all concentrations is very close to that of pure water. As the quantity of the sample increases, the difference between pure water and the salt solution becomes more pronounced. At 10 g of water in the solution (1.1 g of hydrogen content), this difference is greatest when compared to pure water at a concentration of 35 g NaCl/100 g water (0.1079 mol/mol). It is noteworthy that a linear relationship between peak area and increasing quantity of pure water, corresponding to increasing hydrogen content, was observed, which aligns with the LF‐NMR theory for obtaining peak area. However, unlike pure water, the peak area of the salt solution displayed a non‐linear decaying curve as a function of concentration, which became more prominent with the increasing mass of salt solution.

According to the findings in Figure 5, it is clear that the amount of signal in LF‐NMR for salt solutions does not depend solely on the total proton content. Figure 7 speculates on the overall behavior of the peak area obtained in T2 relaxometry for salt solutions. In this figure, five observations are made. Figure 7(1) and 7(2) describe the signal for pure water, considering masses of water x g and 2x g, respectively, indicating that the total proton content and signal will be exactly doubled. We hypothesize that the reduction in total peak area could be due to the following two reasons by the incorporation of salts in the solution:

Alteration in the structural arrangement of water: When salts are added to water, the structural arrangement of water molecules changes as they reorient to form a new structure with a different arrangement. This alters the number of hydrogen atoms involved in hydrogen bonds. Asghari et al. (2025) reported that LF‐NMR is capable of detecting only the signal of loosely bonded hydrogen atoms, since extremely tightly bonded hydrogens in the sample (such as ice), are not in the measurable range of 0.5 T LF‐NMR due to the very short relaxation time. Therefore, as the concentration of salts increases, the changing pattern of loosely bonded hydrogen with water molecules may cause a drop in the signal (total peak area) compared to pure water (Figure 7(3), 7(4)).

Therefore, as the concentration of salts increases, the changing pattern of loosely bonded hydrogen with water molecules may cause a drop in the signal (total peak area) compared to pure water (Figure 7(3), 7(4)).

Synergic movement of cations and anions: According to the Lorentz force law, in LF‐NMR, the rotational magnetic field generated by the nuclei responsible for the signal (e.g., hydrogen) can exert a force on ions in the solution (Eroğlu et al. 2019). Based on the Ampère–Maxwell law, the movement of any electrically charged particle (such as ions) generates a parasitic magnetic field (Eroğlu et al. 2019; Griffiths 2023) that interacts with the same nuclei responsible for the signal. The magnetic field produced by the movement of ions interferes with the magnetic field of the arranged hydrogen atoms. This interference reduces the total signal after the addition of salt to the aqueous solution (Gomes et al. 2019). Furthermore, the movement of cations in a liquid occurs in synchrony to maintain the distance between them. This means that nearby cations move together in the same direction at a certain time to avoid coming too close and to maintain energy stability during this vibrational movement. A similar phenomenon occurs for anions in the solution. In brief, the ions act synergistically, disrupting the proportionality between signal intensity and sample mass. This synchrony increases the magnetic field of the ions as the mass of the solution increases, amplifies the disturbance in the uniformity of the applied magnetic field, and decreases the total signal (Figure 7(5)).

Conclusion

The T2 distribution curve from LF‐NMR of different salt solutions revealed that the relaxation time depends on the chemical nature and the ion concentration in the solution. Generally, monovalent salts behave distinctly from divalent salts. This study reports that the kosmotropic (structure‐making) and chaotropic (structure‐breaking) nature of ions affects relaxation time. The shortest T2 was observed for Mg2+, followed by Ca2+, Na+ and K+, respectively. Beyond a CC (0.0462 mol/mol), a rapid alteration in T2 was observed in all salt solutions—either upward (KCl) or downward (NaCl, MgCl2, CaCl2). The difference between the highest and lowest T2 values corresponding to salt concentration in the solution was approximately 17%, 20%, 89%, and 461% for NaCl, KCl, CaCl2, and MgCl2, respectively. Although EC and aw can generalize the behavior of salt solutions in relaxometry, they are not the only parameters that can explain the specific behavior of salt solutions. A correlation between T2 and thermodynamic processes in salt solutions at 0.0617 mol/mol was observed: endothermic processes in salt solutions require a greater relaxation time, while exothermic processes correspond to a shorter T2. However, the trend in T2 did not follow the chronological order of the ∆Hsolution values. This study also reports an interesting results regarding the reduction of peak area with the incorporation of salts in aqueous solutions. The peak area in salt solutions followed an exponential decay equation. The decay rate constant (K) for NaCl remained constant (−0.09 g/g or –30.58 mol/mol) across different water bases, whereas for KCl, MgCl2, and CaCl2, the values ranged from –22.95 to –32.58 mol/mol, –84.01 to –127.25 mol/mol, and –87.43 to –60.09 mol/mol, respectively, at 6 g and 10 g water bases. The maximum peak area decreased proportionally based on the total mass of the NaCl solution, ranging from −17.2% to −51.5% as the mass of water in the solution increased from 2 to 10 g. Although the proton content remained the same, the deviation in peak area of the salt solution compared to pure water was greatest at higher mass. In contrast, at lower quantities of solution, the peak area was nearly the same as that of pure water. A nonlinear decaying curve was observed, becoming more pronounced at higher concentrations. The interpretation of the decaying behavior of the peak area is speculated to result from alterations in the structural arrangement of water and the involvement of loosely bound hydrogen, which affect the signal within the measurable range of 0.5 T LF‐NMR. Additionally, the synchronization of similar types of ions (cation‐cation and anion‐anion) is also assumed to enhance the magnetic fields at higher masses, leading to a decrease in the overall signal intensity of NMR. Further investigations using a broader range of salt types and concentrations may help unravel the correlation between T2 and the thermodynamic processes. Moreover, simulating decay curves across a broader range of salt solutions could help validate the proposed interpretations.
