Disruption of the transsulfuration pathway by acute kidney injury causes intestinal damage
Authors: Shunshun Jin, Charith U.B. Wijerathne, Yaw L. Siow, Karmin O
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAcute kidney injury (AKI) impairs intestinal function through oxidative stress. The transsulfuration pathway is essential for sulfur amino acid metabolism and antioxidant defense through glutathione biosynthesis. This study investigated how AKI caused intestinal injury and the mechanisms involved. AKI was induced in Sprague-Dawley rats through kidney ischemia (45 min)-reperfusion (24 h). AKI decreased intestinal glutathione (antioxidant) levels and compromised intestinal barrier function. Glutathione biosynthesis in mammals requires cysteine made through the reverse transsulfuration pathway, catalyzed by cystathionine beta-synthase (CBS) and cystathionine gamma-lyase (CSE). AKI decreased the expression of these enzymes in intestinal epithelium. AKI also altered gut microbiota composition and the expression of bacterial enzymes in the forward transsulfuration pathway, which could disrupt intestinal antioxidant defense. The inhibition of this pathway in gut epithelial cells reduced glutathione levels and tight junction protein expression. These results suggest that the disruption of the transsulfuration pathway impairs antioxidant defense, leading to intestinal barrier damage and dysbiosis in AKI.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is characterized by a rapid deterioration of kidney function over a short period of time.1 AKI can occur after kidney transplantation, surgical procedures, or in critically ill patients due to ischemia-reperfusion injury.2,3,4 AKI often leads to the dysfunction of other organs, a phenomenon known as distant organ injury, which increases morbidity and mortality.5 It has been reported that the lung, heart, liver, and intestine are the most commonly affected organs in AKI.3,6 AKI can induce intestinal inflammation, microbiota dysbiosis, and leaky gut due to impaired epithelial barrier function.7 Although the systemic effects of AKI are increasingly recognized, the underlying mechanisms by which AKI disrupts gut health remain poorly understood.

The intestine serves as the largest barrier against the external environment, where nutrient absorption and metabolism take place.8,9,10 Damage to tight junction proteins can compromise the intestinal barrier and disrupt nutrient absorption, allowing endotoxins and macromolecules to penetrate the normally sterile submucosal layer and trigger immune responses.11,12,13 The increased intestinal permeability, known as “leaky gut,” is associated with oxidative stress and low-grade inflammation. Oxidative stress due to an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) and antioxidant defenses is an aggravator for many diseases, including leaky gut.14,15,16 However, the factors that weaken the barrier function in the context of AKI are not well defined. Glutathione is an important antioxidant that can be synthesized in host cells and in gut microbiota. It has a pivotal role in maintaining redox balance in the digestive tract.17 It is plausible that AKI may affect the antioxidant capacity by reducing glutathione levels, leading to oxidative stress and inflammation, which in turn can disrupt tight junction proteins and compromise the intestinal barrier function.

Glutathione is synthesized through two sequential reactions that are catalyzed by glutamate-cysteine ligase (catalytic subunit Gclc, modifier subunit Gclm) and glutathione synthetase.18 In mammalian cells, glutathione biosynthesis depends on the provision of cysteine that is de novo synthesized through the reverse transsulfuration pathway (Figure S1).19 In this pathway, cystathionine β-synthase (CBS) and cystathionine γ-lyase (CSE) metabolize homocysteine to cysteine.20,21 In parallel to the reverse transsulfuration pathway in the host, cystathionine β-lyase (CBL) and cystathionine γ-synthase (CGS) are responsible for the synthesis of homocysteine from cysteine through the forward transsulfuration pathway in bacteria (Figure S1).22 This forward transsulfuration pathway provides a substantial source of sulfur amino acids in the intestine.23,24 A recent study has estimated that the intestinal microbiota contributes to at least 25% of the intestinal methionine in mice.25 By modulating the availability of key precursors such as methionine and cysteine, the gut microbiota plays a critical role in regulating host glutathione biosynthesis and maintaining intestinal redox homeostasis.

Although renal replacement therapy has improved in recent decades, AKI still has a high mortality, especially in patients with multi-organ dysfunction.26 Increased oxidative stress and impaired intestinal function can alter nutrient absorption and metabolic homeostasis, causing malnutrition.27 Despite the recognition of oxidative stress as a mediator in AKI and distant organ injury, the interplay between AKI-induced oxidative stress and the gut microbiota remains poorly understood. This study aimed to examine the effects of AKI on intestinal oxidative stress, microbiota and the importance of the transsulfuration pathway in antioxidant defense through glutathione biosynthesis.

Results

Kidney ischemia-reperfusion injured the kidney and intestine

Kidney ischemia-reperfusion significantly elevated plasma creatinine compared to the sham group (Figure 1A), indicating impaired kidney function in the rats. Kidney ischemia-reperfusion also significantly increased the endotoxin levels in the plasma (Figure 1B), suggesting that the intestinal permeability might be compromised. The significant decrease in the expression of tight-junction proteins (occludin and ZO-1) in the intestine of rats with kidney ischemia-reperfusion could increase the intestinal permeability (Figure 1C). Furthermore, the morphological changes of the jejunum were examined by H&E staining. Kidney ischemia-reperfusion decreased the villus height to crypt depth ratio along with inflammatory foci, suggesting a change in the structure of the intestine and inflammation (Figure 1D). To further examine the pathological changes in the intestine, the expression of inflammatory cytokines in the jejunum was measured. There was a significant increase in IL-6, NLRP3 and TNF-α mRNA expression in the jejunum of rats with kidney ischemia-reperfusion injury (Figure 1E). Kidney ischemia-reperfusion increased oxidative stress in the intestine

Oxidative stress can impair intestinal integrity.28,29 To evaluate the redox status in the intestine, we measured oxidative stress biomarkers. Malondialdehyde (MDA), an end product of lipid peroxidation, was significantly elevated in the intestine of rats subjected to kidney ischemia-reperfusion (Figure 2A). The level of the antioxidant glutathione in its reduced form (GSH) was significantly decreased (Figure 2B) while the oxidized form (GSSG) was significantly elevated in the intestine of those rats (Figure 2C). Consistently, the ratio of GSH to GSSG was decreased (Figure 2D). Gut bacteria also serve as an important source of GSH in the gut.30,31 The GSH level in the digesta of rats with kidney ischemia-reperfusion injury was significantly decreased (Figure 2E) while the GSSG level was increased (Figure 2F), resulting in a lower GSH:GSSG ratio (Figure 2G). Effect of kidney ischemia-reperfusion on the reverse transsulfuration pathway and glutathione synthesizing enzymes

Next, we examined the expression of enzymes responsible for glutathione biosynthesis. The glutathione biosynthetic cascade consists of two key components: the reverse transsulfuration pathway and GSH production (Figure S1). The reverse transsulfuration pathway is facilitated by the enzymes cystathionine-β-synthase (CBS) and cystathionine-γ-lyase (CSE), which are responsible for the de novo synthesis of cysteine, a precursor for GSH (Figure S1). Kidney ischemia-reperfusion significantly decreased the mRNA and protein levels of CBS and CSE in the jejunum (Figures 3A and 3B). GSH production is catalyzed by glutamate-cysteine ligase (GCL) and glutathione synthetase (GS). There was no significant change in the expression of glutathione-synthesizing enzymes in the jejunum after kidney ischemia-reperfusion (Figure S2). To further elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the downregulation of CBS and CSE during AKI, we examined the transcriptional regulation of these enzymes. Specificity protein 1 (Sp1) is a key transcription factor that modulates the expression of CBS and CSE.32,33 A significant decrease in Sp1 DNA-binding activity was observed in the jejunum from rats subjected to kidney ischemia-reperfusion (Figure 3C). We also measured cysteine in the intestine and digesta. There was a significant decrease in cysteine concentrations in AKI rats (Figures 3D and 3E). Effect of kidney ischemia-reperfusion on gut microbiota and the forward transsulfuration pathway in bacteria

The intestinal microbiota can regulate sulfur amino acid metabolism in the host, which, in turn, affects glutathione homeostasis in the gut.31,34 We examined the functional and taxonomical profiles of the gut microbiota in the digesta. While there was a significant difference in β-diversity between the sham and ischemia-reperfusion groups (PERMANOVA p = 0.003∗) (Figure S3A), no difference in α-diversity, indicated by the Shannon index, was observed (Figure S3B). Among the major phyla that were detected in the gut microbiota, only Proteobacteria were elevated in the rats with ischemia-reperfusion injury (Figure S3C). LEfSe analysis revealed that the genera Escherichia-Shigella, Parasutterella, Alloprevotella, and Bacteroides were significantly enriched in AKI rats, while Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group and unclassified Prevotellaceae were significantly reduced (Figure 4A). Functional prediction using PICRUSt2 showed that several sulfur-related metabolic pathways, including the superpathway of sulfate assimilation and cysteine biosynthesis, sulfate reduction I (assimilatory), superpathway of heme biosynthesis from glutamate, superpathway of L-methionine biosynthesis (by sulfhydrylation), and superpathway of sulfur oxidation (Acidianus ambivalens), tended to be increased in AKI rats (Figure 4B). In contrast, the chondroitin sulfate degradation Ⅰ (bacterial) tended to be decreased in AKI rats (Figure 4B). Unlike mammals, the forward transsulfuration pathway, regulated by cystathionine-β-lyase (CBL) and cystathionine-γ-synthase (CGS), is predominantly present in bacteria (Figure 4C). The genes encoding CBL, including metC and patB/malY, were detected in the gut microbiota of these rats (Figure 4D). While the abundance of patB/malY was suppressed in the gut microbiota of rats with ischemia-reperfusion injury, the metC abundance was increased (Figure 4D). As the abundance of patB/malY was 10-fold higher than that of metC, a decreased patB/malY abundance could lead to low CBL activity during AKI (Figure 4D). Such a decrease in patB/malY might be due to a decreased supply of cystathionine from the host. No difference in the abundance of metB was observed between the sham and ischemia-reperfusion groups (Figure 4E). Furthermore, the genes encoding enzymes for glutathione production, gshA and gshB, were also detected in the gut microbiota of rats (Figure S4). There was no alteration in these genes in rats with ischemia-reperfusion injury compared with the sham controls (Figure S4). These findings suggested that kidney ischemia-reperfusion altered the gut microbiota composition and disrupted microbial gene expression in the forward transsulfuration pathway. Correlation between the glutathione levels and the expression of enzymes in the transsulfuration pathway

To explore the relationship between intestinal glutathione (GSH) content and the reverse transsulfuration pathway (in host) and the forward transsulfuration pathway (in microbiota), a correlation analysis was performed. Both jejunal and digesta GSH levels were positively correlated with the CBS and CSE protein expression in the jejunal epithelium (Figure 5A). Among these correlations, the association between jejunal CBS protein and jejunal GSH was the strongest (Figure 5A). Conversely, the jejunal GSH level was positively correlated with the abundances of patB/malY but negatively with that of metC in the gut microbiota (Figure 5B). Only the abundance of patB/malY showed a positive correlation with digesta GSH level (Figure 5C). Inhibition of the transsulfuration pathway decreased glutathione and increased inflammatory cytokines in Caco-2 cells

The above results indicated a strong association between the transsulfuration pathway and the GSH content in the intestine. To further investigate the role of the transsulfuration pathway in GSH production and cytokine expression, Caco-2 cells were incubated with aminooxyacetic acid (AOAA), a known inhibitor of CBS and CSE in the reverse transsulfuration pathway.35 The AOAA significantly decreased intracellular GSH levels (Figure 6A) and increased lipid peroxidation (Figure 6B). Moreover, the inhibition of CBS and CSE significantly increased the inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, NLRP3) mRNA expression (Figures 6C–6E). The addition of GSH was able to reverse the AOAA-induced lipid peroxidation and proinflammatory cytokine expression (Figures 6C–6E). The treatment of AOAA in Caco-2 cells decreased the expression of tight junction protein ZO-1 (Figure 6F). The addition of GSH also effectively restored the expression of tight junction proteins (Figures 6F and 6G). Discussion

Distant organ injury represents a unique challenge for the treatment of patients with AKI. Gastrointestinal complications can exacerbate AKI by promoting systemic inflammation, increasing oxidative stress, and disrupting the gut microbiota, which can further impair renal function and complicate the overall recovery process.36,37,38,39,40 However, the mechanisms by which AKI affects intestinal function remain unclear. In this study, ischemia-reperfusion caused kidney injury, impaired intestinal epithelial barrier integrity, and altered gut microbiota. AKI also elevated intestinal oxidative stress biomarkers and inflammatory cytokine expression. The levels of antioxidant glutathione were significantly decreased in the intestinal epithelium and in the digesta of AKI rats. Changes in the glutathione levels were linked to the reduced expression of intestine enzyme CBS in the reverse transsulfuration pathway and low expression of bacterial enzyme CBL in the forward transsulfuration pathway.

Both host cells and gut bacteria are capable of synthesizing glutathione.41 In mammalian cells, glutathione biosynthesis depends on the provision of cysteine that is de novo synthesized through the reverse transsulfuration pathway (Figure S1).42,43 In this pathway, CBS and CSE convert homocysteine to cysteine through an intermediate cystathionine.20,32 In the host, a decreased CBS and CSE expression could reduce the pool of cystathionine, which, in turn, might limit cysteine availability for glutathione synthesis.19,44 In the present study, ischemia-reperfusion induced AKI inhibited the expression of CBS and CSE in the intestine. To further explore the underlying mechanism responsible for CBS and CSE downregulation during AKI, we investigated the role of the transcription factor Sp1, which is known to regulate the expression of enzymes in the transsulfuration pathway.32,33 Sp1 DNA-binding activity was significantly reduced in the jejunum of AKI rats. These results suggested that AKI might suppress CBS and CSE expressions by inhibiting Sp1 activity. In accordance, cysteine levels were significantly decreased in the intestinal tissue of AKI rats, indicating impaired cysteine biosynthesis due to the suppression of the reverse transsulfuration pathway.

Cysteine is a crucial amino acid for protein synthesis (nutrient role) and energy generation (energy role) in bacteria.45 Without sufficient cysteine, bacteria in the gut may struggle to maintain optimal growth and function.46 In bacteria, the enzyme CBL that converts cystathionine to homocysteine in the forward transsulfuration pathway is encoded by the patB/malY gene (Figure S1). In the present study, AKI significantly reduced the expression of CBL, along with a marked expansion of Proteobacteria. It was reported that AKI-induced oxidative stress altered the intestinal redox environment and favored facultative anaerobes such as Proteobacteria that were more tolerant to oxidative conditions.31,34 The overgrowth of Proteobacteria could outcompete beneficial anaerobic commensals that typically express patB/malY, resulting in a shift in microbial composition.22 Since CBL is involved in the metabolism of sulfur-containing amino acids in bacteria, the alteration of its expression can lead to dysbiosis. The gut microbiota contributes substantially to host methionine levels, and at least 25% methionine in the gut originates from microbial sources.25 The microbiota also contributes to host antioxidant defense by generating reactive sulfur species (RSS), including hydrogen sulfide, cysteine persulfide, and glutathione persulfide, which account for up to 50% of RSS in the circulation.34 These microbiota-derived sulfur metabolites not only scavenge free radicals but also regulate protein function through persulfidation.34 In the present study, AKI led to significant compositional shifts in the microbiota, including an increase in Escherichia-Shigella, Parasutterella, Alloprevotella, and Bacteroides, and a reduction in Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group and unclassified Prevotellaceae. Escherichia-Shigella is frequently associated with intestinal inflammation and epithelial barrier disruption.47 Parasutterella influences bile acid metabolism and has been linked to host metabolic disturbances.48 Although Alloprevotella can produce short-chain fatty acids, its increase is often observed under inflammatory and dysbiotic conditions.49,50 While Bacteroides generally supports carbohydrate metabolism, some strains, such as enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis, can disrupt the mucosal barrier and promote inflammation.51 In contrast, AKI led to a significant decrease in beneficial taxa, including Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group and unclassified Prevotellaceae. These bacteria are linked to short-chain fatty acid production and mucosal health, and their reduction can cause a loss of beneficial metabolic function.52,53 Taken together, these microbial changes, including a shift toward an unbalanced community and disrupted sulfur metabolism, suggest that AKI impairs both the reverse and forward transsulfuration pathways, thereby disturbing the homeostasis of sulfur-containing amino acids and intermediates in the host and microbiota. This, in turn, may contribute to reduced intestinal glutathione levels and compromise redox balance.

Glutathione is a potent endogenous antioxidant that plays an important role in maintaining intestinal redox balance and protecting cellular integrity.54 It was reported that glutathione, at optimal levels, could protect distant organs such as the liver from systemic oxidative stress induced by AKI.55 The glutathione level in the gut is inextricably linked to the methionine-homocysteine-cystine metabolism through the transsulfuration pathway.18,20,31 A decreased expression of CBS and CSE could reduce the availability of cysteine for glutathione synthesis. In the present study, there was a significant correlation between decreased CBS expression in the intestine and reduced GSH levels in the intestine or in the digesta. Furthermore, the inhibition of CBS and CSE resulted in decreased GSH levels and increased lipid peroxidation in Caco2 cells. Such an inhibition also triggered an increase in the expression of inflammatory cytokines. The supplementation of glutathione not only attenuated lipid peroxidation and inflammatory cytokine expression but also enhanced tight-junction protein expression in Caco2 cells. These results further supported the notion that the downregulation of CBS and CSE expression in the intestinal epithelium could decrease glutathione levels. This, in turn, might weaken the antioxidant defense, increase oxidative stress, and inflammatory response during AKI. We also observed that the supplementation of glutathione could effectively mitigate the adverse effects caused by the disruption of the transsulfuration pathway in epithelial cells. Recent studies have suggested that the modification of gut microbiota by probiotics or antibiotics may serve as a therapeutic approach to mitigate AKI progression to CKD.56,57 Further studies are warranted to investigate how oxidative stress and gut microbiota interaction can affect AKI-induced distant organ injury, and if the restoration of redox balance can improve kidney and distant organ function.

Limitations of the study

While this study provides novel insights into the role of the transsulfuration pathway in AKI-induced intestinal oxidative stress, a few aspects warrant further investigation. Despite the significant reduction in glutathione levels in the intestinal epithelium, the expression of key glutathione synthesizing enzymes remained unchanged. This observation suggests that glutathione depletion may be primarily due to reduced cysteine availability stemming from suppressed transsulfuration pathway activity. However, the synthesis of glutathione also depends on the activity of glutathione synthesizing enzymes. Glutamate-cysteine ligase, the rate-limiting enzyme in glutathione biosynthesis, is known to be regulated through various post-translational modifications, including phosphorylation, S-glutathionylation, and nitration, which can alter its catalytic activity in response to oxidative stress.18,58 The effect of AKI on the post-translational modification of glutathione synthesizing enzymes remains to be investigated. It is also warranted to investigate whether activation of the transsulfuration pathway can restore intestinal glutathione levels, alleviate intestinal oxidative stress, and improve epithelial barrier function. Future studies using pharmacological or genetic approaches to modulate the enzymes in the transsulfuration pathway can provide new insights into new therapeutic strategies to prevent or mitigate AKI-associated gut dysfunction and systemic complications.

Conclusions

AKI can impair intestinal function through increased oxidative stress and inflammatory response. Our results, for the first time, demonstrate that the downregulation of the transsulfuration pathway is associated with low glutathione levels in the intestinal epithelium and in the digesta. This, in turn, may weaken the antioxidant defense capacity, contributing to oxidative stress in the intestine. AKI-induced changes in microbiota may further deplete glutathione and exacerbate intestinal damage. Glutathione and sulfur-containing amino acids, synthesized in the host and bacteria, work together to maintain a redox balance within the gut. The interaction between the host and microbiota is essential for the maintenance of gut health and barrier function. The disruption of redox balance leads to oxidative stress and dysbiosis, which may contribute to AKI-induced distant organ injury. The restoration of antioxidant defense may protect intestinal barrier integrity and improve AKI-induced distant organ injury.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (270-300 g, 8 weeks) were obtained from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA, USA) and acclimatized for one week prior to surgery under controlled environmental conditions (22 ± 2°C, 12 h light/dark cycle, ad libitum access to food and water). Rats were housed two per individually ventilated cage. All experimental procedures were complied with the Guide to the Care and Use of Experimental Animals by the Canadian Council on Animal Care and received approval from the University of Manitoba Animal Care Committee (24-006 AC1188).

Cell line

The Caco-2 cell line (ATCC: #HTB-37) was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Rockville, MD, USA). This human colorectal adenocarcinoma-derived cell line is male in origin and widely used to model intestinal epithelial cells.66 Caco-2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Hyclone Laboratories Inc., Logan, UT, USA). Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. The culture medium was refreshed every two days, and cells were subcultured using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 70-80% confluence. Cells between passages 15 and 25 were used in experiments. The cell line was authenticated by the supplier. All cultures used in this study were negative for mycoplasma. Reduced glutathione (GSH, ≥98%, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# G4251) was dissolved in sterile PBS and added to the culture medium at the indicated concentrations.

Method details

Ischemia-reperfusion model

A total of 12 rats were randomly assigned to either the kidney ischemia-reperfusion (IR) group or sham-surgery group (n = 6 per group), with randomization stratified by body weight. All animals were fed a rodent chow (Prolab® IsoPro® RMH 3000, LabDiet, St. Louis, MO) and all surgical procedures were performed between 8:00-12:00 AM to minimize circadian influence. Kidney ischemia was induced by applying a non-traumatic vascular clamp to the renal pedicle, blocking blood flow to the left kidney for 45 minutes. This was followed by reperfusion and removal of the right kidney.67 The sham group underwent the same surgical process without inducing ischemia. After 24 hours, all rats were euthanized, and blood was collected from the abdominal aorta for plasma preparation.68 The 24-hour time point was selected based on our previous study, which demonstrated that this time point corresponds to significant oxidative stress and inflammation in distal organ following renal ischemia-reperfusion.69 The intestine (jejunum) samples were gently flushed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline, and the jejunal tissues and the colon digesta were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C. To investigate the effects of AKI on both the host and intestinal microbiota, we selected anatomically distinct regions of the intestine for specific analyses. The jejunum was used for assessing oxidative stress, inflammation, and barrier function because it plays a critical role in nutrient absorption and is highly susceptible to ischemia-reperfusion-induced epithelial damage.70 The colon digesta was analyzed for microbial composition and function due to its higher microbial density and community stability, which offer greater accuracy and reproducibility in 16S rRNA-based sequencing analysis. This region-specific sampling strategy ensured reliable interpretation of both host and microbiota responses to AKI.

Biochemical analysis

Kidney function was evaluated by measuring plasma creatinine using the Cobas c111 analyzer (Roche, Risch-Rotkreuz, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The analyzer automatically mixed plasma samples with alkaline picrate reagent, and creatinine concentration was determined based on the resulting colorimetric reaction. Calibration was performed with Roche Calibrator for Automated Systems and internal quality control was ensured using Roche PreciControl ClinChem Multi 1 and 2.

Plasma endotoxin (lipopolysaccharides) was measured using Chromogenic Endotoxin Quant Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Cleveland, OH, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Plasma samples were diluted and heat-treated to inactivate proteases, then incubated with Limulus Amebocyte Lysate and a chromogenic substrate. Endotoxin concentrations were determined based on colorimetric detection.

Intestinal malondialdehyde (MDA) was measured using thiobarbituric acid assay following a modified Ohkawa method.71 Jejunal tissue was homogenized in 1.15% (w/v) KCl on ice and mixed with 8.1% (w/v) SDS, followed by adding 20% (v/v) acetic acid (pH adjusted to 3.5) and 0.8% (w/v) thiobarbituric acid (TBA). The reaction mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath (95°C) for 60 min, and then cooled to room temperature. The mixture was centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 532 nm. The MDA concentrations were calculated using a standard curve prepared with 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane.

Glutathione levels were quantified by spectrophotometry using a 5,5'-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB)-based recycling assay.72 Samples were deproteinized by sulfosalicylic acid at a ratio of 2:5 (SSA:sample, w/v), followed by centrifugation (10,000 × g, 10 min). An aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with sodium phosphate buffer (0.2 M, pH 8.0), DTNB (0.01 M), NADPH (40 mM), and glutathione reductase (250 U/mL). The GSH concentrations were calculated using a standard curve prepared with serial dilutions of reduced glutathione (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) standard solution. For GSSG measurement, an aliquot of the deproteinized supernatant was incubated with 2-vinylpyridine at room temperature for 45 min, followed by adding phosphate buffer, DTNB, NADPH, and glutathione reductase. The GSSG concentrations were calculated using a log-transformed standard curve prepared with serial dilutions of oxidized glutathione standard solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).

Cysteine quantification

Cysteine content in intestinal tissue and colonic digesta was measured using an amino acid analyzer (Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The freeze-dried and finely ground samples were treated with 2-octanol and oxidized with freshly prepared performic acid. The mixture was incubated at 0°C for 16 h to convert cysteine to cysteic acid.73 The residual oxidants in the mixture was quenched with sodium metabisulfite, followed by hydrolysis with 12 M hydrochloric acid at 110°C for 24 h. After cooling, the hydrolysate was neutralized with 25% sodium hydroxide and then adjusted to pH 2.2 with sodium citrate buffer. The hydrolysate was passed through a P-5 filter paper and cysteic acid was quantified.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

The intestine (jejunum) was fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, transferred to 70% ethanol, and processed for routine paraffin embedding. The embedded tissues were sectioned at 5 μm using a rotary microtome and mounted on Superfrost Plus slides. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol (100%, 95%, 70%), and rinsed in distilled water. H&E staining was performed by immersing slides in Harris hematoxylin, followed by differentiation in 1% acid alcohol and bluing in 0.2% ammonia water. Sections were counterstained with eosin Y, dehydrated, and cleared in xylene. Tissue morphology was visualized using an Olympus BX43 upright light microscope equipped with an Olympus Q-Color3 digital camera (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Villus height and crypt depth were measured from 25 well-oriented villi per sample using Image-Pro Plus 7.0 software (Media Cybernetics, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Gut microbiota analysis

Colon digesta DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Venlo, Netherlands), including a blank extraction control to monitor kit contamination. DNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), and integrity was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Barcoded primers targeting the V4 region of 16S rRNA gene were used to PCR amplify the extracted bacterial DNA using the Earth Microbiome Project standard protocol.74 Quantification of the product from these PCR amplifications was completed using PicoGreen (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). The UltraClean PCR Clean-Up Kit (MoBio, Carlsbad, CA) was used to clean and pool equal amounts of DNA from each sample. Amplicons were pooled in equimolar amounts and sequenced using the Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 250 bp paired-end reads) across three runs, with mock community and no-template controls included. The taxonomical and functional profiles of gut microbiota were analyzed by Micon Analytics (Toronto, Canada).75 The 16S rRNA amplicons were demultiplexed using QIIME2 (Version 2023) and then denoised to define Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) using DADA2.59 Sequences were rarefied to a depth of 10,000 reads per sample based on rarefaction curves. Taxonomical profiles were generated using the SILVA reference database (release 138).61 Alpha and beta diversity were analyzed using QIIME2 core-metrics-phylogenetic pipeline. Group differences in beta diversity were evaluated using PERMANOVA and visualized via non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS). Differentially abundant taxa were identified using Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) with an LDA cutoff score of 2.0.62 Functional profiles were generated by the PICRUSt2 algorithm to predict the abundance of gene families using the MetaCyc database.63,65

Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction analysis

To perform quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis, total RNA was prepared from small intestine (jejunum) and Caco-2 cells with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) followed by chloroform, isopropyl alcohol, and 75% ethanol. RNA concentrations and purity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). One microgram of total RNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA using the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Primers were designed using Primer-Blast (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast). Real-time PCR was carried out using the iQ5 real-time PCR assay system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) to analyze the mRNA expression of the target genes. The relative expression levels of the target genes were presented as values relative to the sham group rats. Western immunoblotting analysis

Protein was prepared from intestinal tissue and Caco-2 cells using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Equal amounts of protein (40-80 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE on 6%-10% gels and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL substrate (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) and imaged with a ChemiDoc™ Imaging System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). β-Actin was used as a loading control.

SP1 DNA-binding activity

The DNA-binding activity of SP1 in jejunal nuclear extracts was assessed using an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA). Nuclear proteins were extracted following a modified protocol based on hypotonic lysis and differential centrifugation. Jejunal tissue was homogenized, and nuclear proteins were prepared using the NE-PER™ Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), which separates cytoplasmic and nuclear protein fractions via sequential detergent lysis and centrifugation. EMSA was performed using the LightShift™ Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A biotin-labeled double-stranded oligonucleotide probe containing the consensus SP1-binding sequence (5’-ATTCGATCGGGGCGGGGCGAGC-3’) was synthesized (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Binding reactions were assembled with nuclear extract, binding buffer, poly(dI•dC), NP-40, MgCl2, and glycerol, incubated at room temperature, and resolved on a 6% native polyacrylamide gel. DNA-protein complexes were transferred to a nylon membrane and cross-linked by UV light prior to chemiluminescent detection.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using the R program (version 4.3.2). The distribution of each dataset was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test to assess normality, and Levene’s test for homogeneity of variances. The differences in gut microbiota patterns were evaluated by permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) using the vegan package in the R program. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed. The two-group comparison was analyzed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, Welch’s t test or Mann-Whitney U test. For multiple-group comparison, the Kruskal-Wallis test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or Welch’s ANOVA were performed, followed by post-hoc analysis using Dunn’s, Tukey’s test or Dunnett’s T3. The results were expressed as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). P < 0.05 was considered a significant difference.
