Atrial Fibrillation Already Known Versus Detected After Stroke: Same Battle?
Authors: Charles Guenancia, Yannick Béjot
Published: 2024

With the ongoing aging population, atrial fibrillation (AF) has become the leading cause of ischemic stroke (IS). Indeed, epidemiological data suggest that about 25% of ISs are of a cardioembolic origin, either in patients with a history of AF 1 or diagnosed with AF during hospitalization, 2 and up to 30% of patients with cryptogenic stroke develop AF during follow‐up detected with implantable loop recorders. 3 , 4 Although the association between AF and stroke may seem straightforward, the reality is undoubtedly more complex. For instance, the certainty of AF causation of stroke is sometimes questionable given the high prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors in patients with both AF and IS that may contribute to other sources of embolisms, especially atheroma. 5 In addition, a recent work has shown that patients with known AF (KAF), whether or not on oral anticoagulants, do not have the same clinical and imaging profile as patients diagnosed with AF detected after ischemic stroke (AFDAS). 6 In fact, some AFDASs are thought to be more related to autonomic dysfunction triggered by specific stroke sites related to the autonomic nervous system (ie neurogenic), 7 , 8 while others may be directly involved in the causal pathophysiology of IS. 9 Although persistent AF and AF burden are associated with a higher risk of thromboembolic events, IS may also occur in paroxysmal AF, and the timing of AF onset may be different. This is the concept of atrial cardiomyopathy, in which the atrial tissue itself is responsible for atrial thrombus formation due to atrial fibrosis, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction. 10 This structural atrial disease may be manifested either by its electrical symptoms (premature atrial contractions 11 and AF 12 ) or its hemodynamic consequences (heart failure with preserved or impaired left ventricular function) or directly through thromboembolic events that may precede both electrical and hemodynamic dysfunction. This theory explains the lack of temporal association between AF and IS found in several studies, 13 , 14 , 15 although other studies have found an association between AF and thromboembolic events. 16

In this setting, it is crucial to better understand the pathophysiological significance of AFDAS as well as its clinical implications and consequences. The study by Ryu et al in this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA) 17 is therefore welcome. The authors chose to specifically focus their work on patients with IS treated with mechanical thrombectomy, that is, involving a large‐vessel occlusion of the anterior circulation. Such ISs are associated with larger brain lesion volume, poorer functional outcome, and a higher mortality rate as compared with IS related to a distal vessel occlusion. Ryu et al compared 252 mechanical thrombectomy–treated patients with either KAF (n=151 [59.9%]) or AFDAS (n=101 [40.1%]) in terms of clinical, biological, imaging, and functional outcome (using the modified Rankin Scale score) 3 months after IS. As described in previous studies, patients with KAF differed from patients with AFDAS in that they had a higher prevalence of previous stroke, suggesting that some of them were diagnosed with AF after a first cerebrovascular event. Interestingly, patients in both groups did not differ significantly in terms of modified Rankin Scale at hospital discharge, but at 3‐month follow‐up, patients with AFDAS were more likely to have low functional disability, quantified by a modified Rankin Scale score between 0 and 2, compared with patients with KAF, even after adjustment for some selected confounders.

Importantly, the authors further divided patients with AFDAS into early and late AFDAS, with early being AF diagnosed on admission ECG and late being AF diagnosed by Holter ECG telemetry. This distinction is important because recent papers emphasized that AF on admission ECG is very likely to be preexisting AF that was not diagnosed before stroke due to absence of symptoms or lack of medical follow‐up, whereas AF diagnosed during hospitalization may refer to short‐lived AF and be triggered by the stroke itself (ie, neurogenic). 9 Although this subanalysis is limited by a low sample size, the authors found that patients with early AF had a clinical profile intermediate between patients with a high atrial cardiomyopathy burden (KAF) and patients with a low atrial cardiomyopathy burden (late AF). As demonstrated in previous studies, patients with KAF have the more severe clinical phenotype, with more advanced atrial cardiomyopathy (also suggested by larger left atrial diameters in patients with KAF in the present study) and a more frequent history of stroke. 6

This study adds an important piece to the puzzle of understanding AFDAS, but the reader should be aware of the study design in order not to extrapolate these results to all patients with IS. Actually, the focus on a specific mechanical thrombectomy population selects patients with large‐vessel occlusion (accounting for 18%–25% of all patients with IS), 18 that is, cardioembolic strokes with more severe consequences. The results of the study might be different if more distal IS with KAF or AFDAS, which are associated with smaller thrombus and thus less functional impairment, were included. In addition, the inclusion of early AFDAS in the main analysis may be questionable given the tendency to exclude these patients from the AFDAS concept.

Furthermore, the differences in 3‐month functional outcome observed between patients should be interpreted with regard to confounders considered in the multivariable models. For example, it has been suggested that patients with KAF have a higher prevalence of previous cognitive impairment and dementia, but also of previous silent (or not, as shown by the higher incidence of previous stroke in patients with KAF in this study) ischemic brain lesions, which may worsen stroke lesion volume and reduce brain plasticity after stroke. 19 As cognitive status and silent brain lesions before stroke were not considered, we cannot exclude their contribution to the worst functional outcomes observed in patients with KAF in this study.

Finally, another important finding should be highlighted. Despite all the efforts made in recent years to increase physicians' awareness of the risk of stroke in AF, only 49% of the patients with KAF were treated with oral anticoagulation. Given the mean age and cardiovascular background of this population, it is likely that most of the untreated patients had a clinical indication for anticoagulation, that is, a CHA2DS2‐VASc score ≥2 (or even ≥1), given that direct oral anticoagulation has been shown to improve the net clinical benefit of oral anticoagulants and thus potentially lower the score threshold at which patients would benefit from them. 20 This is consistent with previous studies reporting an underuse of anticoagulation in patients with AF. For instance, in the population‐based Dijon Stroke Registry, although the use of oral anticoagulant treatment in patients with both KAF and IS had increased between 2006 to 2009 and 2014 to 2017 (from 29.3% to 61.3%; P<0.0001), 37.5% of patients with KAF and a CHADS2 score≥2 were not treated with oral anticoagulants despite a clear indication. 1 In the vast majority of cases, the lack of prescription of anticoagulants in patients with AF before IS has been shown to be unjustified 21 and thus represented a missed opportunity to reduce the burden of stroke. In addition, beyond reducing the risk of IS occurrence, some studies suggested that before‐stroke anticoagulants in patients with KAF may be associated with a lower clinical severity. 6

In conclusion, the study of Ryu et al sheds light on the intricate relationship between AF and IS, emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of AFDAS and its clinical implications, particularly in the context of mechanical thrombectomy. While the findings offer valuable insights into functional outcomes, caution is warranted in generalizing them to all patients with IS, given the specific selection of patients and unmeasured confounding factors. Addressing these gaps in understanding and clinical practice is paramount in effectively managing the burden of AF‐related IS in the aging population.
