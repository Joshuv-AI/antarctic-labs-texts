Changes in fish and coffee intake and pain intensity in older adults: longitudinal results from the COPERNICUS study
Authors: Agnieszka Kujawska, Joanna Androsiuk, Brendon Stubbs, Virginia Boccardi, Huan-Ji Dong, Sławomir Kujawski, Paweł Zalewski, Kornelia Kędziora-Kornatowska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPain intensity frequently increases with age, impacting quality of life in older adults. Fish and coffee consumption, may influence pain trajectories, but longitudinal evidence remains limited.AimTo investigate the association between changes in fish and coffee consumption frequency and alterations in pain intensity over 2 years in older adults.DesignA longitudinal observational study assessing changes in dietary patterns and pain intensity over a 2-year follow-up.MethodsA sample of 205 healthy older adults was evaluated at baseline and after 2 years. Pain intensity was measured using the Numeric Pain Rating Scale (0–10). Coffee and fish consumption frequencies were recorded weekly and categorized as decreased, no change, or increased over the study period. Analyses were adjusted for confounders: age, sex, depression severity changes, health self-assessment, and analgesic use.ResultsAn increase in fish consumption, compared to a decrease, was associated with a 4.45-point reduction in pain intensity (95% CI: −7.06 to −1.84; P = 0.003). Increasing fish intake relative to no change corresponded to a 2-point reduction (95% CI: −3.71 to −0.29; P = 0.03). Conversely, increased coffee intake, compared to a decrease, was linked to a 6.56-point rise in pain intensity (95% CI: 3.93 to 9.19; P = 0.0001), and compared to no change, with a 2.83-point increase (95% CI: 1.48 to 4.18; P = 0.0004).ConclusionsIncreasing fish consumption was associated with changes in pain intensity in older adults, while increased coffee intake was linked to higher pain levels, however, caution is needed interpreting conclusions.

Introduction

Chronic pain is a pervasive and complex health challenge among older adults, with prevalence rates ranging from 35% to 50% in community-dwelling populations aged 60 and older, and up to 80% in institutionalized settings.1,2 This condition profoundly impacts quality of life, functional independence, and mental health, contributing to increased healthcare utilization and societal costs.3,4 The etiology of chronic pain in older adults is multifactorial, driven by age-related physiological changes such as heightened systemic inflammation, diminished endogenous pain modulation, and a higher burden of comorbidities, including osteoarthritis, neuropathic disorders, and cardiovascular disease.5 For instance, age-associated upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, exacerbates nociceptive signaling, while functional decline amplifies musculoskeletal pain.6 Psychosocial factors, including depression, anxiety, and social isolation, further modulate pain perception by altering central pain processing pathways, such as those involving the prefrontal cortex and amygdala.7,8

Beyond these biological and psychosocial determinants, emerging research underscores the critical role of lifestyle factors, particularly diet and nutrition, in influencing chronic pain trajectories. Both fish and coffee contain dietary components that have anti-inflammatory properties.9,10 Dietary patterns rich in anti-inflammatory nutrients, such as omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (e.g. eicosapentaenoic acid [EPA] and docosahexaenoic acid [DHA]) found in oily fish, have been shown to mitigate pain by suppressing inflammatory pathways and enhancing microvascular function.11,12 For example, omega-3s inhibit the production of pro-inflammatory eicosanoids and promote the synthesis of resolvins, specialized pro-resolving mediators that dampen chronic inflammation.13 Conversely, dietary components like caffeine, the primary bioactive compound in coffee, exhibit complex effects on pain perception. Caffeine’s antagonism of adenosine receptors and modulation of transient receptor potential (TRP) channels, such as TRPA1 and TRPV1, may either alleviate or exacerbate pain depending on dosage, chronicity, and individual physiological context.14,15 High caffeine intake may heighten pain sensitivity in chronic pain states by enhancing nociceptive signaling, particularly in older adults with altered pain processing.16

Despite these insights, longitudinal studies exploring how changes in dietary habits, such as fish and coffee consumption, influence pain intensity over time remain scarce, particularly in Eastern European populations where dietary patterns and healthcare systems differ from Western cohorts.17 The present study addresses this need by examining how changes in the frequency of fish and coffee consumption over a 2-year period are associated with alterations in pain intensity among older adults in Poland, offering novel insights into dietary strategies for pain management.

Materials and methods

Study group

The Cognition of Older People, Education, Recreational Activities, Nutrition, Comorbidities, and Functional Capacity Studies (COPERNICUS) constitutes the first comprehensive longitudinal investigation of the aging population in Poland. The present analysis represents a subset of this broader research initiative, with a primary focus on longitudinal changes in cognitive function. Accordingly, the sample size for the current study was determined based on the methodology outlined in a previously published protocol.18

Sample population

Initially, 407 individuals aged 60–88 years, of whom 95 were men, completed baseline assessments. Of these, 205 participants (40 men) returned for a follow-up evaluation 2 years later. In a previous publication, we detailed a comparative analysis between those who discontinued participation before the second wave and those who underwent re-examination.19

Participant recruitment was conducted through a multifaceted approach, including regional television and radio advertisements, public health lectures at the Collegium Medicum, gatherings of organizations dedicated to older adults in Bydgoszcz, and senior daycare centers. The advertisements highlighted the opportunity for individuals aged 60 and above to engage in complimentary assessments encompassing behavioral analysis, body composition, and overall health status. Individuals below the age of 60 were excluded from participation; no additional exclusion criteria were imposed. The evaluations took place over a period spanning November 2015 to February 2020. This study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the Ludwik Rydygier Memorial Collegium Medicum in Bydgoszcz, Nicolaus Copernicus University, Torun (approval number KB 340/2015). Informed written consent was obtained from all participants before inclusion. All research activities were conducted in strict accordance with pertinent guidelines and regulations, adhering to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Chronic pain measurement

A physical examination was conducted in the physician’s office. Participants were queried about the presence of chronic pain persisting for more than 3 months (yes/no).20 Furthermore, they were asked to rate the intensity of the current pain using a 0–10 Numeric Pain Rating Scale (NPRS), where 0 corresponds to “no pain” and 10 represents the “worst pain imaginable.”21 Respondents were instructed to select the number that best represented their pain level at the time of the assessment. This self-reported measure was administered both at baseline and at the 2-year follow-up to capture longitudinal changes in pain intensity.

Frequency of coffee and fish consumption

The frequency of coffee and fish intake at baseline and during the follow-up was assessed by asking participants to recall how often they had consumed coffee and fish. Responses on frequency of coffee and fish consumption were categorized and assigned numerical codes as follows: “never” was coded as 0, “once a year” as 1, “several times a year” as 2, “1–2 times a month” as 3, “once a week” as 4, “a few times a week” as 5, and “daily” as 6. Based on these codes, delta values were calculated (after 2 years minus before). Eventually, based on those numerical values indicating a change in the frequency of coffee and fish intake, three categorical groups were created: “decrease in the frequency of consumption over 2 years,” “no change,” and “increase in the frequency of consumption over 2 years.”

Covariates measurement

The Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS), consisting of 15 chosen items, was utilized as a screening tool to identify potential depressive symptoms. This brief version has demonstrated efficacy in detecting depressive symptoms among the oldest adults, including those both with and without cognitive impairments.22,23 The questionnaire delves into multiple facets of the individual’s experience, such as quality of life (e.g. “Do you feel full of energy?”), current circumstances (e.g. “Do you feel that your situation is hopeless?”), participation in everyday activities (e.g. “Have you dropped many of your activities and interests?”), emotional well-being (e.g. “Do you feel happy most of the time?”), and overall life satisfaction (e.g. “Are you basically satisfied with your life?”). Scores on the GDS-15 range from 0 to 15, with higher scores reflecting more pronounced depressive symptoms. The scale has been reported to have high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.94) and it is well applied in the Polish population.24 In addition, participants were asked to rate their present health status on a 10-point scale, where a score of 10 represents the highest attainable level of health. Also, the list of medications taken was assessed both before and after 2 years. Then, medications were grouped based on their clinical action. Eventually, a binary variable was created to denote subjects who started to take a painkiller within 2 years.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted within the R environment.25 Alpha level set at 0.05. When the normality assumption was satisfied, a parametric dependent t-test was employed; otherwise, the semi-parametric Wilcoxon test was utilized to compare test outcomes before and after 2 years. To compare changes in pain intensity between more than 2 independent groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied. The Dunn pairwise comparisons test with Holm adjustment was used as a post hoc test in the case of a statistically significant result of the Kruskal-Wallis test. A regression model designed for continuous ordinal data was employed to forecast changes in pain intensity scores across 2 years using the ordinalCont package.26 The change in pain intensity (ΔNPRS) was analyzed using a regression model for continuous ordinal outcomes (ordinalCont package). Although ΔNPRS is a difference score, its values are intrinsically linked to the bounded, discrete, and potentially non-linear nature of the original 0–10 NPRS. Linear regression assumes that the residuals are normally distributed and that the intervals between scale points are equal—assumptions that may not hold for patient-reported pain outcomes, even when differenced. Ordinal regression for continuous data relaxes these assumptions by modeling the observed change scores as manifestations of an underlying latent continuous variable, connected via a flexible, data-driven monotonic function (spline). This provides a more robust analytical framework, particularly when the distribution of change scores is skewed or contains many tied values (e.g. zero change), as is common in longitudinal pain data. The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the model coefficients were calculated using the standard normal approximation method. Specifically, for each estimated coefficient, the standard error was extracted from the model summary. The lower and upper bounds of the 95% CI were then computed by subtracting and adding, respectively, 1.96× the standard error from the coefficient estimate. Since ordinalCont does not have built-in influence tools, linear model approximation was applied to rely on its diagnostics. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the observed associations between dietary factors and pain intensity. Separate models including only fish intake (fish delta) or only coffee intake (coffee delta) were fitted to assess their independent effects on pain without potential confounding from the other dietary variable. Additionally, influential observations were identified using Cook’s distance, and models were refitted after excluding these outliers to determine whether the main results were affected by extreme data points.

Results

Sample description and confounders

A total of 205 participants, including 40 (19.5%) men and 165 women (80.5%) with a mean age of 69.67 years, were initially evaluated at baseline and subsequently reassessed after a 2-year interval. The subjects’ body mass index (BMI) was 27.54 ± 4.44 kg/m2 (mean ± SD) at baseline and 27.45 ± 4.3 kg/m2 after 2 years. Participants had a mean of 14.37 ± 3.4 years of education. Most participants (84.39%) had completed at least a full secondary education or attained a higher level of education. At follow-up, the cohort’s mean age was 69.67 years, with a 95% confidence interval of 68.85–70.50 years, and an age range spanning from 60 to 88 years. Participants self-assessed their health (median ± IQR) on 7 ± 2 both before and after 2 years. All participants had engaged in work at some point in their lives; as a result, there were no individuals classified in the unemployed subgroup. No statistically significant change in pain intensity was noted before vs. after 2 years (median 25%; 75% percentile) (3 (0; 5) vs. 3 (0; 6) after 2 years, P = 0.71).

Significant difference between those groups was noted in coffee consumption frequency (P = 0.042). Subjects who were not re-examined tended to more frequently abstain from drinking coffee (n = 34 (16.8%) vs. n = 20 (9.8%) in the re-examined group. No other significant differences were revealed.

The association between changes in the frequency of fish and coffee consumption was also evaluated after adjustment for potential confounders, including changes in self-rated health, variations in Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) scores, and alterations in painkiller medication status (a dichotomous variable indicating whether painkiller use was initiated within the past 2 years). Supplementary Table S2 presents these key covariates.

The change in pain intensity over 2 years, relative to changes in the frequency of fish and coffee consumption during the same period, was analyzed using a regression model tailored for continuous ordinal data, adjusting for relevant confounding factors. An increase in fish consumption frequency, compared to a decrease, was associated with a significant reduction in pain intensity by 4.45 points (95% CI: −7.06 to −1.84; P = 0.003) (Table 1). Similarly, increasing fish consumption relative to no change corresponded to a 2-point decrease in pain intensity (95% CI: −3.71 to −0.29; P = 0.03) (Table 1). Conversely, an increase in coffee consumption frequency, compared to a decrease, was linked to a significant increase in pain intensity by 6.56 points (95% CI: 3.93 to 9.19; P = 0.0001) (Table 1), and compared to no change, an increase in coffee consumption was associated with a 2.83-point rise in pain intensity (95% CI: 1.48 to 4.18; P = 0.0004). Discussion

The present study offers longitudinal evidence that changes in dietary habits, specifically fish and coffee consumption, are associated with alterations in chronic pain intensity among older adults in Poland. Participants who increased their frequency of fish consumption over 2 years experienced a significant reduction in pain intensity compared to those who decreased or maintained their intake, independent of multiple covariates. Conversely, those who increased coffee consumption reported elevated pain scores relative to those with decreased or unchanged intake. These findings provide novel insights into the modulatory role of dietary factors in chronic pain, a prevalent and debilitating condition in aging populations, and highlight the potential for nutritional interventions to improve pain outcomes in clinical and public health settings. While our findings suggest a longitudinal association between dietary changes and pain intensity, they cannot establish causality. The observed 4.45-point reduction in pain linked to increased fish consumption and the 6.56-point increase associated with higher coffee intake represent clinically substantial shifts on the 0–10 NPRS scale. Such large effect sizes warrant cautious interpretation, as they may be inflated by methodological factors rather than reflecting true biological impact. Several limitations must be considered. First, reverse causality is a plausible alternative explanation: individuals experiencing worsening pain may have altered their coffee or fish intake as a coping mechanism or due to reduced mobility, rather than diet driving the change in pain. Second, residual confounding is likely. Despite adjusting for key covariates, unmeasured factors (e.g. overall dietary quality, physical activity levels, sleep quality, or socioeconomic status) that influence both diet and pain perception may not be fully captured. Third, the use of self-reported data for both dietary frequency and pain intensity introduces potential for recall bias and misclassification, which can distort effect estimates. Finally, the relatively small sample sizes within some dietary change subgroups (e.g. those who decreased consumption) may lead to unstable estimates and reduced statistical power, making the large coefficients more susceptible to influence from outliers or model misspecification. Therefore, while these results are hypothesis-generating, they should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct causal effect. Future research employing randomized controlled trials or more robust quasi-experimental designs is essential to confirm these associations, elucidate underlying biological mechanisms, and determine whether targeted dietary modifications can produce clinically meaningful, long-term reductions in pain for diverse older adult populations.

Chronic pain in older adults is a dynamic condition shaped by a complex interplay of biological, psychological, and social determinants. Age-related physiological changes, including heightened systemic inflammation and impaired descending pain inhibition, amplify pain sensitivity and persistence.20 The absence of a significant overall change in pain intensity in our cohort over 2 years (median Numeric Pain Rating Scale [NPRS]: 3 at baseline vs. 3 at follow-up, P = 0.71) suggests a stable pain burden, consistent with longitudinal studies reporting persistent pain in 35–50% of older adults.3 However, individual variations in pain trajectories, as observed in our subgroup analyses, underscore the influence of modifiable lifestyle factors, such as diet, on pain modulation.

The association between increased fish consumption and decreased pain intensity is consistent with a growing body of research on the anti-inflammatory properties of omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), particularly eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), abundant in oily fish. A longitudinal study in Spain found that higher oily fish intake was associated with a lower risk of pain development and reduced pain progression over 5 years in older adults, with omega-3 PUFAs mediating these effects through anti-inflammatory pathways.11 Similarly, a randomized controlled trial demonstrated that fish oil supplementation reduced osteoarthritis-related pain and improved well-being in overweight and obese older adults, potentially through enhanced microvascular function and reduced synovial inflammation.12 Although our study did not directly investigate underlying biological mechanisms, the observed 4.45-point lower in pain intensity associated with increased fish consumption, compared to decreased intake, aligns with the hypothesis that dietary omega-3 PUFAs may play a role in modulating nociceptive signaling. Omega-3 PUFAs are known to inhibit the biosynthesis of pro-inflammatory eicosanoids and to facilitate the production of resolvins, a class of specialized pro-resolving lipid mediators that contribute to the active resolution of inflammation. These anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving properties of omega-3 PUFAs provide a plausible mechanistic framework for interpreting our findings.13

Conversely, the association between increased coffee consumption and heightened pain intensity, a 6.56-point higher compared to decreased intake, suggests a potential, though untested, role of caffeine in exacerbating pain perception. Caffeine, the principal psychoactive component of coffee, has complex and dose-dependent effects on nociception. It is hypothesized to influence pain signaling through antagonism of adenosine A2A receptors and modulation of transient receptor potential (TRP) channels, including TRPA1 and TRPV1. While these mechanisms may offer a theoretical basis for the observed association, they were not directly assessed in our study.14 While low doses of caffeine may enhance analgesia in acute pain models,16 chronic high intake, as likely reflected in our participants with increased consumption, may heighten pain sensitivity by sensitizing nociceptive neurons. A cross-sectional analysis of the 2009–2010 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) corroborated this, linking higher caffeine intake to increased chronic musculoskeletal pain in adults.15 Additionally, our prior COPERNICUS study found that frequent coffee consumption is associated with elevated systolic and mean blood pressure over 2 years, suggesting a broader pro-inflammatory or sympathetic activation effect that may exacerbate pain.19 These findings diverge from studies reporting caffeine’s analgesic effects in acute pain, emphasizing the need to differentiate between acute and chronic pain contexts in older populations.

Comparative analyses further contextualize our results. A systematic review of nutritional factors in chronic pain found that diets rich in anti-inflammatory foods, including fish, were associated with reduced pain intensity in conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis and fibromyalgia, though evidence in general older populations is limited.27 Caffeine’s role in chronic pain, however, is less studied longitudinally, with most research focusing on cross-sectional associations or acute pain models.16 The longitudinal design of our study strengthens evidence by establishing temporal relationships between dietary changes and pain outcomes, addressing a critical gap in the literature and providing a foundation for future interventional studies.

The findings have substantial implications for clinical practice and public health, particularly in managing chronic pain among older adults. Increasing fish consumption, especially of oily fish rich in omega-3 PUFAs, could serve as a practical and cost-effective dietary strategy to reduce pain intensity. Clinicians might recommend incorporating one to two servings of fish per week, consistent with international dietary guidelines, while addressing potential barriers such as cost, availability, or cultural preferences in Eastern European populations.11 Nutritional counseling could be integrated into primary care or geriatric clinics, emphasizing anti-inflammatory diets as a complement to pharmacological pain management. Conversely, the association between increased coffee consumption and heightened pain suggests that moderation of caffeine intake may be warranted for older adults with chronic pain, particularly those with musculoskeletal conditions. Clinicians should routinely assess patients’ coffee consumption habits and consider personalized recommendations, such as limiting intake to one to two cups daily, to minimize potential pro-nociceptive effects.14

These dietary recommendations are particularly relevant in Poland, where fish consumption is moderate but variable, and coffee is a cultural staple. Approximately 80% of respondents in the Poland-based study reported consuming fish less frequently than the recommended minimum of twice a week.28 The average yearly coffee intake in Poland falls between 2.2 and 3 kg per person. In comparison, Germany’s per capita coffee consumption is about 5.2 kg annually, while in France, the figure is ∼3.2 kg per person each year.29,30 Public health initiatives could promote fish as part of a pain-management strategy through community education programs, while targeted campaigns could raise awareness about the potential risks of excessive coffee intake. Given the high prevalence of chronic pain in older adults (35–50%),3 even modest reductions in pain intensity through dietary modifications could yield significant improvements in quality of life, functional independence, and healthcare cost savings.

Some limitations warrant consideration when interpreting these findings. First, the reliance on self-reported pain intensity via the NPRS, without assessing pain frequency, duration, or qualitative characteristics, limits the comprehensiveness of pain evaluation.21 Objective measures, such as quantitative sensory testing or neuroimaging, were not feasible, potentially missing subclinical nociceptive changes. In addition, types of pain were not assessed.

The study defines pain as lasting longer than 3 months. Nevertheless, it does not provide information on the underlying pain etiology (e.g. musculoskeletal or neuropathic). Different types of pain may be influenced differently by various dietary factors, which should be considered. Second, the method for assessing fish and coffee intake relied on self-reported frequency data. No validated dietary frequency tools were employed in this process. This approach, while practical for large-scale or resource-limited studies, has inherent limitations. Without the use of validated instruments, the accuracy and reliability of the intake measurements may be compromised due to recall bias, where participants may not precisely remember their consumption patterns, and misclassification bias, where intake may be incorrectly categorized. These biases can lead to measurement error, potentially attenuating true associations or introducing noise into the analysis. In addition, it was not possible to analyze the method of coffee preparation in the current sample. However, previous reports indicate that in Poland, 45% of coffee consumers use ground coffee, while 40% prefer instant coffee.31 According to a questionnaire completed by 398 respondents in Poland, 48% drink espresso prepared under pressure using a machine, and 42% prefer coffee made by pouring hot water over coffee grounds in a glass or cup without filtering.31 Based on these findings, it can be reasonably assumed that the majority of coffee consumed by participants in the current sample was prepared using one of these two methods. Third, adjustments were made for factors such as overall dietary quality, total caloric intake, or other pertinent dietary components (e.g. omega-3 fatty acids, caffeine dosage, intake of fruits, vegetables, processed foods), which could potentially confound the association between the frequency of fish and coffee consumption and pain intensity. Fourth, unmeasured confounders, such as physical activity, other dietary components (e.g. antioxidants, processed foods), or socioeconomic factors, may have influenced the observed associations. Further studies should focus on the effects of compounds, as caffeine or omega-3 fatty acids, as it was not assessed in the current study. Fifth, the study’s homogeneous sample (predominantly female, Polish, and urban) restricts generalizability, as sex, racial, or cultural differences in pain perception and dietary habits were not explored. In addition, as it has been described previously, a substantial dropout rate was noted in the COPERNICUS study,18 which can limit the generalizability of the current findings. In addition, participants who were not re-examined at follow-up were more likely to abstain from drinking coffee compared to those who remained in the study. Finally, the recruitment strategy via mass media likely introduced selection bias, favoring motivated or healthier participants, and contributed to a 50% dropout rate, potentially skewing results. Therefore, future studies should use comprehensive dietary assessment tools, including dose-response assessment, as it was not applied in the current study.

Collectively, this study demonstrates that increased fish consumption is associated with reduced pain intensity, while increased coffee consumption is linked to heightened pain scores in older adults over 2 years. These findings highlight the potential of dietary interventions, particularly omega-3-rich fish, to mitigate chronic pain, while suggesting caution regarding excessive coffee intake. By addressing identified limitations through rigorous future research, the field can advance toward personalized nutritional strategies for pain management in aging populations.
