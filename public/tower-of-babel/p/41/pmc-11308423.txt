Evaluation of oxygen administration in cesarean section under spinal anesthesia via lung ultrasound and the oxygen reserve index
Authors: Manolya Alkan Canıtez, Hilal Ayoğlu, Rahşan Dilek Okyay, Keziban Bollucuoğlu, Çağdaş Baytar, Gökhan Çeviker, Gamze Küçükosman, Bengü Gülhan İncegül, Özcan Pişkin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRespiratory functions may be impaired in cesarean section (C/S) delivery performed under spinal anesthesia (SA) and oxygen supplementation may be required. Therefore, we conducted a randomized controlled study aimed to evaluate the effects of different oxygen administrations in pregnant women on the lungs during C/S under SA using ultrasound and oxygen reserve index (ORI).MethodsWe conducted a randomized, controlled, single-center study from May 1, 2021, to March 31, 2022. A total of 90 patients scheduled for C/S under SA were randomly divided into 3 groups. Following the SA, patients in group 0 were treated with room air, in Group 3 were administered 3 L/min O₂ with a nasal cannula (NC), in Group 6 were administered 6 L/min O₂ with a simple face mask. In addition to routine monitoring, ORI values were measured. Lung aeration was evaluated through the modified lung ultrasound score (LUS) before the procedure (T0), at minute 0 (T1), 20 (T2), and hour 6 (T3) after the procedure, and ∆LUS values were recorded.ResultsAfter SA, the ORI values of Group 3 were higher than Group 0 at all times (p < 0.05), while the intraoperative 1st minute and the 10th, 25th and 40th minutes after delivery (p = 0.001, p = 0.027, p = 0.001, p = 0.019) was higher than Group 6. When the LUS values of each group were compared with the T0 values a decrease was observed in Group 3 and Group 6 (p < 0.001, p = 0.016). While ∆LUS values were always higher in Group 3 than in Group 0, they were higher only in T1 and T2 in Group 6.ConclusionWe determined that it would be appropriate to prefer 3 L/min supplemental oxygen therapy with NC in C/S to be performed under SA.Keywords: Atelectasis, Caesarean section, Lung ultrasound, Oxygen, Oxygen reserve index

Introduction

In cesarean section (C/S) performed with spinal anesthesia (SA), changes occur during pregnancy, and a neuraxial block reaching the fourth thoracic dermatome in the supine position may cause a decrease in lung function and the development of atelectasis, as well as hypotension [1–3]. These conditions may cause maternal hypoxia and fetal acidemia. [3]. Supplemental oxygen (O2) treatment is frequently administered to prevent the negative effects of regional blocks and to protect the O2 reserve in unforeseen situations [4]. There are no guidelines on the techniques of applying supplemental O2 treatment, and there are various practices. Nevertheless, hyperoxia, which may develop depending on the amount of O2 administered, may also cause absorption atelectasis [5].

According to the Cochrane Library, no convincing evidence has been found that administering additional oxygen to healthy pregnant women during elective C/S surgery under regional anesthesia is harmful or beneficial for the mother and fetus. It was also stated that maternal and newborn blood gas values and free radical marker levels are significantly greater when extra oxygen is given, and these results should be interpreted with caution due to the low quality of the evidence [6].

Although pulmonary atelectasis, which may develop due to oxygen given to normally pregnant women, does not cause a clinically significant problem and can be easily monitored with simple clinical parameters, this condition may cause distress and respiratory problems in pregnant women with limited lung reserve. Therefore, determining what supplemental oxygen therapy should involve is still important.

Lung ultrasound (USG) has been found to be effective at diagnosing perioperative atelectasis in pregnant women [7]. The oxygen reserve index (ORI) is used to determine hyperoxia and hypoxia [8].

Therefore, we conducted this randomized, controlled single-center trial to evaluate the effects of two different supplemental O2 treatment methods on the lungs of pregnant women who underwent C/S surgery under SA via USG and ORI, and the secondary purpose was to investigate the effects on newborns.

Materials and methods

This study was carried out between May 2021 and March 2022 at Zonguldak Bülent Ecevit University Hospital after obtaining the written consent of the patients with the permission of the faculty ethics committee and registered at clinical studies (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05509764). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant Human Ethics rules and regulations. After obtaining written informed consent from all participants the study included 90 patients between 18 and 47 years of age who were in risk groups II-III according to the American Society of Anesthesiology (ASA) and scheduled for C/S under SA. Patients with alcohol and drug addiction, severe anemia, liver and kidney disease, history of allergy, sepsis, bacterial infection, a body mass index (BMI) > 45, eclampsia, or SpO2 < 94% were excluded from the study. All patients were divided into 3 equal groups by the closed envelope method (Group 0, Group 3 and Group 6).

Hemoglobin (Hb) and hematocrit (Htc) levels; smoking history; comorbid diseases; gravidity; and gestational age were recorded during the preoperative evaluation.

Hemodynamic monitoring included ECG, heart rate (HR), noninvasive mean arterial blood pressure (MAP) and peripheric oxygen saturation (SpO2) data. The ORI sensor (RD rainbow Lite SET™, Masimo Corp., Irvine, CA, USA) was placed on the 4th finger on the upper extremity, and it was wrapped with black opaque material. An ORI sensor was connected to the oximeter device (Radical-7, Masimo Corp., Irvine, CA, USA), and the ORI, perfusion index (PI), and pleth variability index (PVI) were monitored. Probes showing noninvasive cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO2) (O3® sensor, Masimo Corp., Irvine, CA, USA) were placed in the right and left frontal regions and connected to an oximeter device (Root, Masimo Corp., Irvine, CA, USA). The parameters of the patients were recorded before SA and immediately after SA administration, from the 1st min to the 11th min at 2-min intervals and until the 45th min at 5-min intervals after delivery. In cases where the rSO2 values fell below 20% of the initial value, fluid support was planned to increase, and ephedrine was administered. Normovolemia was maintained by ensuring appropriate PVI ranges.

Lung USG was performed by an assistant who had received training on this subject, and the diagnosis of atelectasis was previously evaluated via USG in 50 patients. Measurements were performed with an Esaote USG My lab device and a 6–18 MHz linear probe. According to the lung USG protocol, the right and left hemithorax were divided into 6 quadrants with anterior and posterior axillary lines as the longitudinal anterior, lateral, and posterior zones. The quadrants were divided into superior and inferior compartments with an imaginary line passing through the nipple. Modified lung USG (LUS) values were recorded by scanning a total of 12 regions before surgery (T0), at the end of the procedure (T1), at the 20th min of follow-up at the postoperative recovery unit (T2), and at the 6th postoperative hour (T3) [9].

According to the LUS scoring system, each region was scored between 0 and 3 according to the degree of atelectasis [9].

∆LUS values were obtained by subtracting total LUS values at all measurement times from the total LUS values at T0.

An intravenous (IV) line was established, and 0.9% saline was started at a rate of 10 ml/h. Bupivacaine (9–11 mg) and fentanyl (10 µg) were used for spinal anesthesia.

Following the SA, patients in Group 3 (n = 30) were administered 3 L/min O₂ (FiO2:32%) via a nasal cannula (NC), and patients in Group 6 (n = 30) were administered 6 L/min O₂ (FiO2:45%) via a simple face mask (FM); these patients were also treated in the recovery unit for up to 20 min. Group 0 (n = 30), which was considered the control group, was treated only with room air. The motor block and maximum sensory block levels were evaluated with the Bromage scale and pinprick test.

A 20% decrease in systolic blood pressure compared to baseline was defined as hypotension, and ephedrine was administered, increasing the volume of saline infusion. Atropine was administered if the heart rate was < 50 beats/min.

Desaturation was defined as a SpO2 decrease < 94% for more than 30 s after the probe was checked. In this case, supplemental O2 therapy was planned to increase until the SpO2 exceeded 94%, and these patients were excluded from the study.

Metoclopramide or ondansetron was planned for patients with complaints of nausea and vomiting. Fentanyl 25–50 µg IV was planned to be administered when additional analgesia was required after delivery.

Umbilical cord blood gas samples were obtained immediately after delivery. The Apgar scores of the newborns were recorded at 1 and 5 min by an independent researcher.

Postoperative pain was evaluated and recorded with the Verbal Analog Scale (VAS) (0 to 10 unbearable pain).

The sensory block of the patients was recorded at postoperative min 0. Tramadol (1 mg/kg) and paracetamol (1 g IV) were administered when the VAS score was > 3 in the first 6 h after surgery.

The complications and the amounts of drug and fluid administered were recorded.

Statistical method

The approximate sample size was calculated before the study using G*Powers software (type 1 error = 0.05, type 2 error = 0.01, correlation = 0.43). According to the results of the sample size analysis performed by Monastesse et al. [9], the minimum number of individuals needed was 30 individuals per group, for a total of 90 individuals. The data collected within the scope of the study were transferred to and evaluated by SPSS for Windows 19.0 package software. Descriptive variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) for quantitative data and as the median and minimum-maximum (min–max) and as the frequency and percentage for qualitative data. The conformity of the quantitative values to the normal distribution in the groups was evaluated with the Shapiro‒Wilk test, and it was determined that the groups did not comply with the normal distribution. For comparisons of multiple groups, the Kruskal‒Wallis test was used for independent groups, and the Freidman test was used for dependent groups. The Mann‒Whitney U test was used to compare two independent groups. The relationship between variables was evaluated by Spearman correlation analysis. The chi-square test was used to compare qualitative data between groups. The results of the analysis were evaluated with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Results

A total of 90 patients were included in the study, and no patients were excluded from the study (Fig. 1). The demographic characteristics, ASA risk classes, history of smoking, preoperative Hb-Htc values, gravidity and gestational age, and comorbid diseases were similar among the three groups. The levels of SA administered, the maximal sensory blockade, the Bromage scale score and the amount of bupivacaine used were similar among the groups (Table 1).

No difference was found between the MAP values of the groups. The HR were similar after SA, except for at minute 1 (Group 0: 104.0 ± 20.2; Group 3: 91.7 ± 15.5; Group 6: 93.0 ± 22.5; p = 0.033) and 3 (Group 0: 106.9 ± 22.6; Group 3: 94.7 ± 20.8; Group 6: 93.3 ± 18.0; p = 0.022).

The SpO2 values of the groups were similar before SA, at intraoperative minute 11, and at minute 45 after delivery; however, differences were observed between the groups at other times (p < 0.05). The SpO2 values at intraoperative minute 1 were significantly greater in Group 3 than in Group 0 and Group 6 (Group 0: 98.8 ± 0.8; Group 3: 99.4 ± 0.7; Group 6: 98.9 ± 1.0; p = 0.007). Similarly, intraoperative SpO2 values at minute 20 were significantly greater in Group 3 than in Group 0 and Group 6 (Group 0: 99.0 ± 0.9, Group 3: 99.8 ± 0.5, Group 6: 99.3 ± 1.0, p = 0.001). While the SpO2 values of Group 6 were found to be greater than those of Group 0 (p < 0.05), except for the measurements performed at minutes 1, 20, 30, and 40, the SpO2 values of Group 3 were found to be greater than those of Group 0 at each measurement (p < 0.05).

The PVI and PI values and the rSO2 values of the groups were similar at all the time points. No decrease of ≥ 20% was observed in the rSO2 during any period or for any patient.

Differences were observed between the ORIs of the groups at all time points (p < 0.05), except for the baseline ORIs of the groups before SA. While the ORIs of Group 3 were greater than those of Group 6 at intraoperative minute 1 (p = 0.001) and at 10 (p = 0.027), 25 (p = 0.001), and 40 (p = 0.019) minutes after delivery, the ORIs were similar in both groups at other time points (Table 3).

Discussion

This is the first study in which the effect of supplementary O2 treatment on LUS and ORI in C/S performed under SA has been evaluated. Supplementation of 3 L/min O2 with NC and 6 L/ min O2 with FM increased ORIs, leading to a significant decrease in LUS values compared to those at baseline. A decrease in the ΔLUS was observed until postoperative 6th h in patients who underwent 3 L/min O2 compared to patients who were exposed to room air; however, this change was observed until postoperative 20 min in patients who underwent 6 L/min O2.

It was observed that supplementary O2 at a 3 L/min flow rate led to a greater increase in ORI values than did the flow rate of supplementary O2 at 6 L/min. Maternal hemodynamics and rSO2 values were not affected by oxygen support treatments. Although fetal pH was found to be greater in the 6 L/min group, pH values were within normal limits in all groups. The Apgar scores were also similar in all the groups.

Oxygen reserves decrease in pregnant women due to increased oxygen consumption and decreased lung volume, which paves the way for airway closure [10–15].

Spinal anesthesia minimally affects spirometry in nonpregnant women via diaphragmatic compensation [16, 17]. Nevertheless, the use of SA and the involvement of the T4 dermatome impair lung functions, causing the development of atelectasis and hypoxia [2]. In our study, the median maximal sensory block was at the T4 level in all groups.

Lung USG has proven to be a useful method due to the ease of bedside administration for the early diagnosis of postoperative atelectasis [18]. The need for postoperative ventilation support is greater in patients with LUS ≥ 10 than in patients with LUS < 10 [18]. In our study, the total LUS was never ≥ 10.

Absorption atelectasis increases even more at high O2 concentrations, depending on the duration of administration [19–21]. Room air contains 79% nitrogen, and nitrogen is slowly absorbed into the blood during spontaneous breathing, thereby helping maintain alveolar patency. When we increase the inspiratory O2 concentration and reduce the nitrogen concentration in the alveolus, which normally creates pressure to prevent atelectasis, the alveolus collapses when oxygen is absorbed into the circulation because there is no more gas available to keep the alveolus open in the environment. In other words, treatment administered to correct hypoxemia can also cause absorption atelectasis and therefore an increase in hypoxemia [20, 21]. In studies comparing 30% and 100% FiO2 administration during general anesthesia induction, atelectasis areas in computed tomography sections were shown to increase in patients ventilated with 100% FiO2 [21].

Under normal conditions, hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV) develops in atelectatic areas as a homeostatic response, which attempts to optimize O2 uptake by transporting blood to better-oxygenated alveoli [22, 23]. It has been reported that 30% FiO₂ protects against HPV [23, 24].

In our study, we used O2 concentrations corresponding to 32% and 45%. Since there was no deep hypoxia in Group 0, which increased pulmonary artery pressure by suppressing the beneficial effects of HPV and restricting perfusion to lung units that remained intact, higher SpO2 and ORI values could be expected compared to those in Group 3 and Group 6, in which the LUS was recovered after delivery. However, the ΔLUS, SpO2, and ORI values were much better in our patients who received 3 L/min oxygen than in those in the room air group. Since the SpO2 and ORI values were greater in Group 3 than in Group 6 even 20 min after delivery, it was concluded that the HPV was better preserved in Group 3 and caused an increase in pressure in atelectatic alveoli with less nitrogen excretion from alveoli; it was also concluded that the LUS was improved by opening atelectatic areas and causing an increase in ORI values.

In patients receiving supplementary oxygen therapy, the partial arterial oxygen pressure (PaO2) may vary significantly from normoxia to extreme hyperoxia [25]. When O2 is combined with pulse oximetry, monitoring ORIs can prevent unexpected hypoxia by providing early warning before desaturation develops and can also prevent unnecessary hyperoxia by facilitating O2 titration [26]. Since ORI is correlated with PaO2, adjusting inspiratory O2 concentrations during preoxygenation has been reported to be useful in C/S in terms of patient safety [27]. In our study, we found a positive correlation between ORI and SpO2, similar to the findings of other studies in the literature. We believe that ORI follow-up could guide the management of supplementary O2 therapy in C/S under SA according to previous data.

It has been demonstrated in previous studies that 35–40% of FiO2 supplementary treatments administered in the C/S under regional anesthesia do not cause any increase in fetal oxygenation [28, 29]. In our study, the umbilical cord pH was greater in patients who were administered 6 L of FM; however, the pH was within normal limits in all groups, and this did not affect the clinical parameters. Additionally, the Apgar scores were similar in all the groups. It has been reported that FiO2 concentrations of 35% and above are associated with increased maternal and fetal free radical activity in patients undergoing C/S with regional anesthesia [30, 31]. Early detection of hyperoxia with ORI may guide the avoidance of unnecessarily high FiO2 concentrations during these surgeries.

Iatrogenic fluid volume loading is one of the factors causing the development of perioperative pulmonary edema [32]. In patients who underwent C/S surgery under SA, the PVI and PI were reported to guide fluid management [33, 34]. In our study, the PVI, PI, MAP, and amount of fluid administered to the groups were similar.

In a study, measurements with near-infrared spectroscopy after SA demonstrated a significant decrease in maternal cerebral blood oxygenation, which has been associated with a decrease in arterial pressure [35]. In this study, 3 L/min oxygen was supplemented in one group, and the other group was exposed to room air. Deoxyhemoglobin levels were found to be greater in patients treated with room air with a greater decrease in the cerebral tissue oxygen index. In our study, the cerebral rSO2 values of the groups were similar. The rSO2 values did not decrease by more than 20% compared to the baseline values in any of our patients. While the median maximal block level in the room air group was determined in the T3 dermatome in the study of Hirose et al. [35], we believe that the use of PVI values while ensuring fluid replacement and maximal block level reaching T4 in each group in our study was effective in maintaining cerebral rSO2 values.

In our study, parameters other than the administration of additional intraoperative analgesia that may affect the development of atelectasis were similar in all groups. In our study, 25–50 µg fentanyl was used in patients in Group 3 and Group 6, and no respiratory problems were encountered. Therefore, no comments could be made on the effect of opioid use on atelectasis.

Limitations of the study

During preoxygenation, the expired oxygen fraction (FEO2) increases faster in pregnant women than in nonpregnant women of the same age [36]. Rapid nitrogen washout with an increased respiratory rate will reduce the end-expiratory intra-alveolar nitrogen concentration, increasing the susceptibility of pregnant women to atelectasis. It may be more appropriate to manage oxygen support by measuring FEO₂ to prevent absorption atelectasis after the functional residual capacity is full. However, the lack of FEO₂ measurements in our study can be considered a limitation.

The second limitation of our study is that it was performed in patients with no known lung pathology. The experiments conducted in this study may yield different results for pregnant women with different lung pathologies, especially where HPV may be impaired.

Conclusion

Considering the LUS and ORI values in C/S performed under SA, we think that a 3 L min-1 O2 supplement with NC should be preferred. The use of lung USG and ORI together will guide the management of supplementary O2 therapy, which is intended to be administered in C/S performed under SA.
