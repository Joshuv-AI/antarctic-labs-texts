Phytohormone and transcriptome analyses unravel the molecular mechanisms of heterophylly in Pinellia ternata
Authors: Jianbang Zhou, Yulin Zou, Fenghua Zheng, Yuhao Yan, Lili Yin, Min Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPinellia ternata (Thunb.) Breit. exhibits significant leaf shape variation, which is closely associated with its growth, development, and medicinal quality. However, the physiological and molecular mechanisms underlying heterophylly remain poorly understood. In this study, we systematically investigated the relationship between leaf shape variation and phytohormone regulation as well as gene expression.MethodsFour distinct leaf types of P. ternata (peony-leaf type, peach-leaf type, bamboo-leaf type, and willow-leaf type) were used as materials. Leaf traits were measured, and the contents of auxin (IAA), cytokinin (CTK), and gibberellin (GA₃) were quantified using ELISA. Transcriptome sequencing was performed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among the leaf types, followed by GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. Candidate genes related to hormone synthesis and signal transduction were validated by RT-qPCR.ResultsSignificant differences were observed in leaf length, leaf width, and length-to-width ratio among the four leaf types, with the willow-leaf type having the longest leaves and the peony-leaf type the widest leaves. IAA content showed a strong positive correlation with leaf length and length-to-width ratio, and a strong negative correlation with leaf width, leaf base angle, and leaf tip angle, suggesting that IAA may be a key hormone influencing leaf shape formation. Transcriptome analysis identified a large number of DEGs, with the willow-leaf type showing the most distinct gene expression profile. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses indicated that the DEGs were mainly involved in pathways such as secondary metabolite biosynthesis and starch and sucrose metabolism. Several candidate genes, including PtIAA13, PtGID2, PtARF19, and PtGA3ox1, were screened and validated.ConclusionsLeaf shape variation in P. ternata is regulated by endogenous hormones, especially IAA, and is closely associated with the expression of related genes. The peony-leaf type exhibited the most stable gene expression pattern. This study provides a theoretical basis for understanding the physiological and molecular mechanisms underlying leaf shape formation in P. ternata and offers references for variety breeding and quality control of this medicinal plant.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08927-7.Keywords: Pinellia ternata, Heterophylly, Plant hormones, Transcriptome, Differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Introduction

Pinellia ternata (Thunb.) Breit., a member of the Araceae family, is documented in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2025 edition) for its dried tuber, which is used as a dampness-drying and phlegm-resolving medicinal agent. It possesses the functions of downbearing counterflow to stop vomiting, reducing swelling, and dissipating nodules [1]. P. ternata exhibits strong adaptive growth capacity and is distributed across multiple provinces in China, including Sichuan, Hubei, Guizhou, Gansu, and Shanxi [2]. Notably, P. ternata from different producing areas shows inconsistent characteristics in terms of growth, chemical composition, and stress resistance, with leaf shape variation being particularly prominent. According to preliminary surveys [3], based on the length-to-width ratio of the main leaf blade, four primary leaf types can be classified: the peony-leaf type (approximately 2.7:1), the peach-leaf type (approximately 4.2:1), the bamboo-leaf type (approximately 7:1), and the willow-leaf type (approximately 12.3:1). Geographically, the bamboo-leaf and willow-leaf types are commonly found in regions such as Shanxi and Hebei, whereas the peony-leaf and peach-leaf types are mainly distributed in Sichuan, Guizhou, and Hubei. Chemical composition analysis reveals differences in quality among the different leaf types. For instance, the total alkaloid and total organic acid contents in the bamboo-leaf type are higher than those in the other three leaf types. This indicates that leaf type is not only an important morphological classification index but may also be closely related to complex traits such as growth and development, metabolic product synthesis, and environmental adaptability in P. ternata. Since different leaf types of P. ternata exhibit differences in chemical composition, leaf type may serve as a convenient morphological indicator for field screening of high-quality germplasm. Therefore, elucidating the regulatory mechanism of leaf shape variation is of great significance for establishing a leaf type-based quality evaluation system for medicinal materials and guiding standardized cultivation of P. ternata.

Leaf shape traits are typical quantitative traits, regulated by both environmental and genetic factors. In crop genetic improvement, significant progress has been made in research on leaf-shape-related traits. In important crops such as rice [4, 5] and soybean [6], genes influencing leaf shape have been shown to be directly or indirectly associated with phytohormones including auxin (IAA), cytokinin (CTK), and gibberellin (GA3). Domestic and international studies on these three phytohormones have also demonstrated that key genes involved in hormone synthesis, transport, and signal transduction pathways can influence plant leaf morphology [7–10]. Building on this foundation, this study focuses on investigating the phytohormones that influence the different leaf types of P. ternata. By systematically analyzing the differences in phytohormones among different leaf types and combining RNA-Seq technology for transcriptome sequencing analysis, differentially expressed genes associated with the various leaf types of P. ternata were screened. The findings are expected to provide new insights into the physiological and molecular mechanisms underlying heterophylly formation in P. ternata and lay a foundation for subsequent breeding of superior cultivars.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

This study used four distinct and stably inherited leaf-type variants of Pinellia ternata as experimental materials. Based on leaf morphology, they were categorized into the Peony-leaf type (S), Peach-leaf type (T), Bamboo-leaf type (Z), and Willow-leaf type (L). Voucher specimens of the four leaf types used in this study have been deposited at the Herbarium of Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (CDCM) under voucher numbers CDCM‑S2023001, CDCM‑T2023001, CDCM‑Z2023001, and CDCM‑L2023001, respectively. The plant materials were formally identified by Professor Min Li of the Department of Chinese Medicine Identification, Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, as P. ternata (Thunb.) Breit. of the Araceae family. The four leaf-type variants assessed in this study are preserved in the Chengdu Shuangliu Germplasm Resource Bank.

The tubers (seed rhizomes) were originally collected from traditional cultivation areas in Xinjiang County, Yuncheng City, Shanxi Province, China, and subsequently transplanted into the experimental field owned and managed by our institution. The collection of plant materials complied with institutional research guidelines. All samples were obtained from research plots under the management authority of our institution. As the species involved is neither endangered nor protected, no specific field collection permits were required.

A total of 2,104 P. ternata plants were used for leaf type classification and identification, derived from previous field surveys, with sufficient sample size for each leaf type satisfying the normality assumption. From these, plants with typical morphology and uniform appearance were selected for subsequent experiments: four leaf types (peony-leaf, peach-leaf, bamboo-leaf, and willow-leaf) were chosen, with three plants per group and three groups per leaf type, resulting in a total of 36 plants. During the experiment, plants were cultivated under standard conditions in 32-cell seedling trays. At the vigorous growth stage, when leaves were approaching maturity and their morphology was fully established, the main leaves were selected for trait measurement. These leaves were then rinsed with purified water, immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 30 min, and stored at -80 °C for subsequent phytohormone assays and transcriptome analysis.

Measurement of leaf traits in different leaf types

During the vigorous growth stage of P. ternata, the main leaf blade was selected for measurement. A steel ruler (graduation: 1 mm) was used to measure the length from the leaf tip to the junction of the leaf base and petiole, recorded as the main leaf length, and the maximum width perpendicular to the midrib, recorded as the main leaf width. Measurements were recorded to the nearest 1 mm. A protractor (graduation: 1°) was used to measure the leaf tip and leaf base angles. For the leaf tip angle, the center point of the protractor was aligned with the leaf apex, and the 0° baseline was set parallel to the leaf midvein; the angle formed by the two leaf margins was read and recorded as the leaf tip angle (averaged from both sides). For the leaf base angle, the center point of the protractor was placed at the junction of the leaf base and petiole, with the 0° baseline aligned along the midvein direction at the leaf base; the angle formed by the intersection of the two leaf margins and the petiole margin was read and recorded as the leaf base angle. Measurements were recorded to the nearest 1°.

Quantification of phytohormones in different leaf types

The contents of auxin (IAA), cytokinin (CTK), and gibberellin (GA₃) were measured using a competitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) [11]. Sample extraction and measurement were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The kits used were the Plant IAA ELISA Kit (catalog number: RXJ1401005PL), Plant CTK ELISA Kit (catalog number: RXJ1401590PL), and Plant GA₃ ELISA Kit (catalog number: RXJ1401607PL), all from Quanzhou Ruixin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Quanzhou, China). The detection wavelength was 450 nm. Each sample was analyzed with three biological replicates, and each replicate was measured three times; results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 9).

IAA and CTK were selected because their involvement in regulating leaf morphogenesis has been widely documented. GA₃ was chosen because it is an endogenous active phytohormone naturally present in plants [12], plays a key role in leaf area, leaf thickness, and leaf margin formation [13, 14], and is the most widely reported gibberellin form in leaf morphogenesis studies.

Transcriptome analysis of different leaf types

RNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted separately from the leaf tissues of the four P. ternata leaf types [15]. The concentration, integrity, and purity of the total RNA were assessed. Following purification and reverse transcription, cDNA libraries were constructed. The fragment size distribution of the final libraries was checked. RNA purification, reverse transcription, library construction, and sequencing were performed by MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd. in Wuhan.

Bioinformatics analysis

After filtering the raw sequencing data, Trinity was used for de novo assembly of clean reads into transcripts [16]. Corset was then employed to cluster and remove redundancy from the assembled transcripts to obtain Unigene sequences. The clean reads were then mapped back to the Unigene sequences to calculate gene expression levels. Subsequently, the differentially expressed genes were aligned, annotated, and subjected to enrichment analysis against multiple databases, including KEGG, NR, Swiss-Prot, GO, COG/KOG, and Trembl. DESeq2 was used for differential expression analysis among the four P ternata leaf types [17], and the Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied for P-value adjustment. Genes with an adjusted P-value (padj) < 0.05 and |log₂(fold change)| ≥ 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Subsequent filtering based on P-value and log₂(fold change) was performed on the MetWare Cloud platform. KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted using a hypergeometric test at the pathway level, while GO enrichment analysis was performed based on GO terms.

RT-qPCR validation

RT-qPCR validation was performed on the four leaf type samples. Selected key genes associated with leaf traits were validated, with three technical replicates for each gene across the different leaf types. The RNA samples used were the same as those extracted for sequencing. The common housekeeping gene Pt18S was used as the internal reference [18]. Primers for the candidate key genes and the reference gene were designed using Primer 3 (primer sequences are listed in Table 1). PCR reactions were carried out in a 20 µL volume using the following program: 95 °C for 5 min; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method [19]. The steps of reverse transcription of RNA into cDNA and the RT-qPCR validation were performed by Personalbio Technology Co., Ltd. in Shanghai.

Results

Leaf traits and principal component analysis of different leaf types of P. ternata

The phenotypic characteristics of the four leaf types are shown in Fig. 1, and detailed measurement data for each trait are presented in Table 2. The willow-leaf type had the longest leaves, while the peony-leaf type had the widest leaves; both differed significantly from the other leaf types in leaf length and leaf width (P < 0.05). The leaf length-to-width ratio was highest in the willow-leaf type, with no significant difference between the peony-leaf and peach-leaf types. Both leaf base angle and leaf tip angle were largest in the peony-leaf type, while no significant differences were detected between the bamboo-leaf and willow-leaf types.

The hormone contents and their coefficients of variation across the different leaf types indicate that the variability in IAA content (as reflected by the CV) remained relatively consistent, while its concentration showed an increasing trend as the leaf shape became more slender. The CTK content appeared relatively unstable in the Peach-leaf type but more stable in the Willow-leaf type, displaying an initial decrease followed by an increase as leaves became narrower. Correlation analysis between leaf traits and phytohormones in different P. ternata leaf types

Regarding the correlation analysis (Fig. 3), IAA showed a strong positive correlation with leaf length and length-to-width ratio (P ≤ 0.001), and a strong negative correlation with leaf width, leaf base angle, and leaf tip angle (P ≤ 0.001). CTK was significantly positively correlated with leaf length (P ≤ 0.05) and highly significantly positively correlated with the leaf length-to-width ratio (P ≤ 0.01). GA3 exhibited a significantly positive correlation with leaf length (P ≤ 0.05) and a strong negative correlation with the leaf tip angle (P ≤ 0.001).

Correlation between phytohormone content and leaf traits

Among the three hormones, the strong correlation between IAA content and leaf traits suggests that IAA may be closely involved in leaf shape formation. Since the leaf length-to-width ratio is a calculated parameter, it can be inferred that CTK showed significant correlation primarily with leaf length, albeit with a relatively lower correlation strength. GA3, being significantly correlated with leaf length and the leaf tip angle, may also be associated with leaf shape formation to some extent. The Peony-leaf type exhibited the lowest IAA content among the four leaf types, indicating it may be less influenced by IAA, which could contribute to its more stable morphological traits compared to the other types.

Transcriptome sequencing data assembly and quality assessment

Transcriptome sequencing was performed on main leaf blade samples from the different P. ternata leaf types, resulting in the construction of 12 cDNA libraries. The sequencing results are summarized in Table 4. A total of 87.23 Gb of clean data were obtained. The clean data for each sample exceeded 5 Gb, with Q30 base percentages ranging from 94.44% to 95.40%. The average GC content across samples was between 54.48% and 58.15%. The average length of the assembled transcripts was 909 bp. These metrics indicate that the transcriptome data were of high quality and suitable for subsequent analysis.

PCA analysis of genes from different leaf types based on gene expression levels

PCA was performed on the gene expression levels of all genes across different leaf types of P. ternata. The PCA scatter plot (Fig. 4) showed that the four leaf types could be divided into three groups, with the peony-leaf type forming a separate group, which was consistent with the PCA results based on leaf morphological traits (Fig. 2). Moreover, the peony-leaf type exhibited the smallest within‑group dispersion and the most concentrated sample points, indicating that its gene expression profile differs from those of the other leaf types and is the most stable among them.

Pairwise comparisons of gene expression levels between the different P. ternata leaf types (Table 5) yielded six comparison groups: SP vs. LP, TP vs. LP, ZP vs. LP, SP vs. TP, SP vs. ZP, and TP vs. ZP. The results showed that the SP vs. LP comparison contained the highest number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Fig. 5), while the TP vs. ZP comparison contained the fewest. Genes differentially regulating phytohormones in different P. ternata leaf types

The biosynthesis of IAA is primarily regulated by tryptophan aminotransferase (TAA1) and flavin monooxygenase (YUCCA). Among the six comparison groups, TAA1 was only significantly up-regulated in two groups. In contrast, YUCCA was significantly down-regulated in three groups, and its overall expression levels across the four leaf types followed the order S > Z > T > L. The key enzyme involved in IAA transport is AUX1. No significant difference in AUX1 expression was found between the TP and SP groups, and the overall expression pattern among leaf types was Z > T ≈ S > L. Genes related to IAA signal transduction, including TIR1, Aux/IAA, ARF, GH3, and SAUR, were mostly differentially expressed across comparisons of different leaf types (Table 6). The expression levels of TIR1, Aux/IAA, and ARF were lowest in the LP group, while GH3 expression was highest in LP.

Validation by RT-qPCR analysis

To validate the reliability of the RNA-seq data, ten key genes with relatively high expression, selected based on their correlations with leaf traits and phytohormone contents, were chosen for subsequent RT-qPCR validation. The experimental results (Fig. 9) showed that the relative expression levels of the candidate key genes corresponded to their expression in the transcriptome, and the regression analysis reached statistical significance (P < 0.05), confirming the reliability of the transcriptome sequencing data.

Correlation between RNA-seq (FPKM) and RT-qPCR validation

Genes including PtIAA13, PtARF19, PtSAUR19, PtORR24, PtGA3ox1, PtGID2, PtGAI1, and PtSCL32 may be involved in the regulation of leaf traits (Fig. 10). The expression levels of PtIAA13 and PtGID2 in the transcriptome results (Fig. 11) were significantly positively correlated with IAA and GA3 contents but negatively correlated with CTK content, suggesting that these two genes may be involved in the synthesis or transport of these phytohormones. The expression levels of the other four genes, PtARF19, PtSAUR19, PtGA3ox1, and PtSCL32, were generally higher in the Peony-leaf, Peach-leaf, and Bamboo-leaf types compared to the Willow-leaf type. With the exception of PtSAUR19, their expression levels showed significant correlations with leaf traits, indicating their potential roles in regulating leaf morphology.

Discussion

This study reveals significant differences in phytohormone content among the four leaf types of P. ternata. Existing research in plants such as rice and Arabidopsis has shown that overexpression of genes related to IAA biosynthesis often results in smaller and narrower leaves, accompanied by increased IAA levels. This indicates a direct correlation between narrow leaf traits and elevated IAA concentrations [4, 20–22]. CTK primarily regulates cell division and differentiation and can interact synergistically with IAA to modulate plant growth and development [23, 24]. GA promotes cell elongation and division [25]. In rice, GA can inhibit cell proliferation at the leaf margins, and a reduction in its synthesis can lead to wider leaves. These findings collectively suggest that IAA, CTK, and GA all play crucial roles in determining leaf morphology. Correlation analysis between hormone content and leaf traits (Fig. 11) demonstrated that IAA is strongly correlated with leaf length, leaf width, length-to-width ratio, leaf base angle, and leaf tip angle. GA3 also showed significant correlations with leaf length and leaf tip angle. It can therefore be inferred that IAA significantly influences leaf shape formation in P. ternata, while GA3 also contributes to some extent. It should be noted that the ELISA method is inferior to liquid chromatography‑mass spectrometry (LC‑MS/MS) in terms of specificity. However, in this study, the comparability of the data was ensured by setting up multiple replicates, using commercial kits and strictly following the manufacturers’ instructions. Moreover, the consistency between hormone content and gene expression trends supports the reliability of the measurements.

Within the pathways of hormone biosynthesis, transport, and signal transduction, the expression of several key genes is closely linked to both hormone content and leaf traits. YUCCA is a key gene in IAA biosynthesis; its overexpression can lead to excessive IAA synthesis, subsequently causing alterations in leaf shape [21, 26]. Aux/IAA genes are pivotal in IAA signal transduction, interacting with IAA response factors (ARFs) [8]. When IAA concentration increases, receptors such as TIR1 bind IAA, leading to the degradation of Aux/IAA proteins. This degradation activates ARFs, which then initiate the expression of downstream IAA-responsive genes [27]. GH3 acts downstream of ARFs and modulates the concentration of free IAA within cells by catalyzing the conjugation of IAA to amino acids, forming IAA-amino acid conjugates [28, 29].

CTK is primarily synthesized in root tips; therefore, differential expression of CTK biosynthesis-related genes in leaf tissues may be limited. The observed differences in leaf CTK content in this study are more likely attributable to variations in root-to-shoot transport or differences in tissue sensitivity to CTK (e.g., changes in receptor and signal transduction gene expression). CTK signal transduction-related genes [30] (such as the CRE1 homolog PtOHK4 and the B-ARR homolog PtORR24) are involved in regulating leaf development. Among these, PtORR24 expression was higher in the willow-leaf type, whereas PtOHK4 expression was higher in the peony-leaf type but PtORR24 expression was lower. These results suggest that the responsiveness of leaf tissues to CTK signaling may vary among leaf types, thereby influencing cell division and leaf morphology.

The GA family comprises numerous members, and GA3 synthesis is typically described within the broader context of GA family biosynthesis. GA biosynthesis can be divided into three stages [31]. Among these, only the final-stage enzyme, GA3ox, showed correlation with leaf traits. GA3ox is responsible for converting GA9 to GA4, thereby completing the synthesis of biologically active gibberellins, with GA3 being subsequently derived through conversions within the GA family [10]. Regarding signal transduction, GID2 and DELLA showed positive correlations with leaf length and GA3 content, respectively. The F-box protein encoded by GID2 is part of a complex that mediates DELLA degradation. By promoting the degradation of DELLA proteins, it alleviates their repression of GA-responsive genes, thereby promoting plant growth and development [31].

Further analysis of other differentially expressed candidate genes revealed that the expression level of PtIAA13 in the willow-leaf type was much higher than that in the other three leaf types. Aux/IAA proteins typically act as negative regulators, binding to ARFs and repressing downstream gene expression at low IAA concentrations, while being degraded to relieve this repression at high IAA concentrations [32]. However, the willow-leaf type simultaneously exhibited the highest IAA content and the highest PtIAA13 expression level. This seemingly contradictory observation suggests that PtIAA13 may have atypical regulatory characteristics. Previous studies have shown that the expression of some Aux/IAA genes can be induced by IAA, forming a feedback regulatory loop [8]. Accordingly, we hypothesize that the high expression of PtIAA13 in the willow-leaf type may be a response to the high IAA content, and that the highly expressed PtIAA13 protein may selectively inhibit downstream genes that promote leaf width development through specific interactions with certain ARF transcription factors, thereby coordinately promoting longitudinal leaf elongation.

PtARF19 showed the highest expression level in the peony-leaf type and the lowest in the willow-leaf type. ARF proteins are direct executors of the auxin signaling pathway and are typically maintained in an inhibited state by binding with Aux/IAA proteins. When IAA levels increase, Aux/IAA proteins are degraded, releasing ARFs to activate downstream elongation-related genes [33]. In the peony-leaf type, PtARF19 was highly expressed but the IAA content was the lowest, suggesting that PtARF19 may primarily bind with Aux/IAA proteins, thereby inhibiting cell elongation. In contrast, PtARF19 expression was extremely low in the willow-leaf type, which may relieve the transcriptional repression of certain elongation-inhibiting genes, allowing longitudinal leaf expansion.

The expression level of PtGA3ox1 was negatively correlated with GA₃ content: in the peony-leaf type, PtGA3ox1 expression was the highest but GA₃ content was the lowest, whereas in the willow-leaf type, PtGA3ox1 expression was extremely low but GA₃ content was relatively high. This observation suggests that high GA₃ levels may suppress PtGA3ox1 expression, while low GA₃ levels relieve this suppression. Such a negative feedback mechanism has been reported in wheat and tobacco [10, 34]. Therefore, the low expression of PtGA3ox1 in the willow-leaf type is likely a consequence rather than a cause of the high GA₃ content, reflecting differential regulation of GA homeostasis among different leaf types.

PtSCL32 showed the highest expression level in the peony-leaf type and was barely expressed in the willow-leaf type, which is completely opposite to the trends of GA₃ content and PtGID2 expression. DELLA proteins are negative regulators of GA signaling, inhibiting cell elongation by repressing transcription factor activity, whereas GA signaling relieves this inhibition by mediating DELLA degradation through GID2 [31, 35, 36]. The extremely low expression of PtSCL32 in the willow-leaf type alleviates the restriction on cell elongation, thereby shaping the extremely narrow and long leaf morphology.

The willow-leaf type had the highest IAA content, the highest CTK content, and a moderately high GA₃ content. However, the expression level of YUCCA, a key IAA biosynthesis gene, was significantly lower than in the other leaf types, while the expression of GID2, a positive regulator of GA signaling, was significantly elevated, and the DELLA family member PtSCL32 was barely expressed. In addition, the auxin response factor PtARF19 was expressed at very low levels in the willow-leaf type, the gibberellin biosynthetic enzyme PtGA3ox1 was also expressed at low levels, and the Aux/IAA family member PtIAA13 was expressed at much higher levels than in the other leaf types. These molecular features collectively point to a regulatory network characterized by enhanced auxin transport, attenuated gibberellin feedback inhibition, and accelerated DELLA degradation. This observation suggests that IAA accumulation in the willow-leaf type may rely mainly on transport rather than biosynthesis, as asymmetric IAA distribution (driven by polar transport) plays a decisive role in leaf lobing and leaflet formation [37]. The willow-leaf type also had the highest CTK content; CTK promotes cell division and maintains meristematic activity [38], which may ensure an adequate supply of cells during extreme leaf elongation. The enhancement of GA signaling favors cell elongation [31]. The combination of high IAA content, high CTK content, and enhanced GA signaling in the willow-leaf type, along with longitudinal leaf elongation, may represent a morphological adaptation strategy to specific habitats (e.g., high light intensity or drought). Previous studies have shown that narrow, elongated leaves help reduce the thickness of the leaf boundary layer and improve water use efficiency [39]; thus, the willow-leaf type may be a xerophytic ecotype formed under long‑term selection.

In the peony-leaf type, the Aux/IAA family member PtIAA13 was expressed at extremely low levels, PtARF19 was highly expressed but IAA content was the lowest, PtGA3ox1 was highly expressed but GA₃ content was the lowest, and the high expression of PtSCL32 was consistent with DELLA protein accumulation. These patterns collectively reflect the molecular characteristics of the peony-leaf type: repressed auxin signaling, feedback upregulation of gibberellin biosynthesis, and enhanced DELLA‑mediated inhibition of elongation. Furthermore, the expression levels of IAA‑related genes (YUCCA, Aux/IAA, ARF) showed inverse relationships, suggesting that the signaling pathways are in an inactive state. The coexistence of high PtGA3ox1 expression and low GA₃ content further supports the presence of feedback regulation. The low levels and low fluctuations of hormone contents may be the intrinsic reasons why the peony-leaf type exhibits the highest stability in both morphology and gene expression. IAA, as a key hormone regulating leaf morphology, typically induces phenotypic plasticity when its concentration fluctuates [37]. In the peony-leaf type, IAA is maintained at a low level, which may reduce developmental instability caused by hormonal fluctuations, thereby resulting in a more uniform phenotype. From a breeding perspective, the “low hormone, high stability” characteristics of the peony-leaf type offer potential advantages for standardized cultivation and quality uniformity control of medicinal materials. Moreover, the transcriptome PCA results showed the smallest within‑group variation for the peony-leaf type, further supporting its developmental stability. This also suggests that the peony-leaf type may represent a “basal” ecotype with a conservative response to environmental changes, which may explain its wide and stable distribution in its native regions (e.g., Sichuan, Guizhou) [3].

IAA showed strong correlations with leaf length, leaf width, length-to-width ratio, leaf base angle, and leaf tip angle, whereas GA₃ was only correlated with leaf length and leaf tip angle. This difference may be attributed to the distinct roles of the two hormones at different stages of leaf development. Leaf development can be divided into three main stages: primordium initiation, cell division, and cell expansion [40]. IAA establishes a concentration gradient in the leaf primordium through polar transport, directly regulating leaflet initiation and leaf margin morphology, and its effects span the entire developmental process, thus having a broader impact [37]. In contrast, GA primarily acts during the cell expansion stage, regulating cell elongation by integrating environmental signals through DELLA proteins [41], and has a lesser effect on leaf width and base angle. Consequently, the correlation of GA₃ is limited to longitudinal growth-related traits (leaf length and leaf tip angle). This spatiotemporal specificity of hormone action may be the intrinsic reason for the differential influence ranges of IAA and GA₃ in leaf shape regulation. Furthermore, the hormone contents measured in this study represent only those at the leaf mature stage and cannot reflect dynamic changes during early development. Future studies could investigate the temporal regulation of hormones by sampling at different developmental stages.

It should be noted that this study is primarily based on correlation analysis and transcriptome sequencing, and the conclusions drawn are correlative in nature. Future experiments such as gene knockout, overexpression, or exogenous hormone treatment are needed to further validate the functions of the candidate genes and the causal roles of the hormones.

Conclusion

Through systematic analysis of four leaf types of P. ternata, this study clarified the relationships among leaf phenotypes, phytohormone contents, and gene expression. The results confirmed significant differences in endogenous hormone contents among different leaf types, with IAA content showing a strong positive correlation with leaf length and length-to-width ratio, and a strong negative correlation with leaf width, leaf base angle, and leaf tip angle. Transcriptome analysis further revealed that the peony-leaf type exhibited the most stable gene expression pattern. Several key genes significantly associated with hormone contents and leaf traits were identified in phytohormone biosynthesis and signal transduction pathways. Experimental validation indicated that PtIAA13 and PtGID2 may be involved in the metabolic regulation of IAA and GA, respectively, while PtARF19, PtGA3ox1, and PtSCL32 may participate in the regulatory network governing leaf morphogenesis. This study provides key genetic resources and a theoretical basis for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying leaf shape differentiation in P. ternata, and lays an important foundation for future breeding for leaf shape stability and cultivation management. It should be noted that the conclusions of this study are based on correlation analysis, and the causal relationships need to be further verified by functional validation experiments (such as gene knockout, overexpression, or exogenous hormone treatment).
