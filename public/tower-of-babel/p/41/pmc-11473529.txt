Diagnosis of fungal diseases in quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Willd) in Burkina Faso
Authors: Louis NEBIE, Abdalla DAO, Tobdem Gaston DABIRE, Schémaéza BONZI, Jacob SANOU, Irénée SOMDA
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuinoa is an Andean pseudo-cereal crop that is mainly cultivated for its energy and protein-rich, gluten-free seeds. Quinoa is currently being popularized in Burkina Faso, but manifestations of fungal diseases have been observed on the plants. The aim of this study was to establish a causal relationship between the observed symptoms and the associated fungal pathogens. Samples of symptomatic organs were collected from the three agro-ecological zones and examined for fungal pathogen species via isolation on media. A total of six (6) diseases and twenty-nine (29) fungal species were described and identified in the collected organs. The diseases were present in the following proportions: (i) panicle browning followed by complete plant drying (45.36%), (ii) brown crown rot (41.20%), (iii) brown stalk rot (27.31%), (iv) damping-off (22.68%), (v) chlorosis with necrotic brown leaf spots (19.33%), and (vi) plant wilting (13.88%). The essential epidemiological factors of these pathologies are discussed.Keywords: Quinoa, Diagnosis, Diseases, Fungal, Burkina FasoSubject terms: Developmental biology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Wild) is an herbaceous plant of the Chenopodiaceae family that is marginally cultivated in some countries for its seeds, which are used as human food. According to the most recent statistics, quinoa is grown on just 173,242 ha worldwide1. Originating in the Andes region of Bolivia and Peru, this crop comprises more than three thousand (3000) varieties or ecotypes that adapt well to different tropical and temperate agro-ecological conditions2–4. Quinoa requires very little water and can produce good yields even with annual rainfall between 100 and 200 mm5. In view of their increasingly high nutritional value, quinoa seeds are now gaining importance in the world food market and can make a substantial contribution to the food and nutritional security of populations, particularly those in the poorest regions6–8. Indeed, quinoa is one of the few food plants to contain eight (8) essential amino acids, trace elements and vitamins9–11. For these reasons, quinoa can be considered a promising crop today, especially in arid rural areas where malnutrition is severe. However, little is known about this crop in Burkina Faso, where it is an exceptional crop. The country’s agricultural research institutions therefore began a process of popularizing and promoting quinoa through a study of the plant’s adaptability to the country’s different agroecological zones12. However, during field experiments, the plants showed signs of attack by microorganisms through a panoply of symptoms, the causes of which are unknown12. In fact, research into the parasitic problems of quinoa in Burkina Faso is scarce. Quinoa plants are susceptible to many diseases that can lead to considerable crop losses and are caused mainly by fungal pathogens13–15. All the organs of plants can be attacked at all stages of development, and plants are most susceptible to telluric pathogens16,17. It is therefore imperative to carry out a precise diagnosis of the pathogens responsible for the symptoms observed to define appropriate control methods to improve this crop in Burkina Faso. The present study is part of this consideration. The main objective was to establish a causal relationship between the symptoms observed and the associated fungal pathogenic species.

Description of symptoms

Six primary symptom categories were identified across the three agro-ecological zones. These included damping-off, brown crown rot, brown stalk rot, plant wilting, grey mold, and chlorosis with necrotic brown leaf spots (Fig. 1). Damping-off is manifested by seed rot, followed by severe browning of the root and hypocotyl, and death of the seedlings. This damage is observed approximately 10 days after sowing (Fig. 1A). Collar rot is characterised first by wet patches on the upper part of the collar, then by the foliage becoming chlorotic, and finally by the stalk bending downward and tending to break easily (Fig. 1B). This symptom manifests when the plant reaches the 12-true-leaf stage until flowering. Brown stalk rot begins with the appearance of necrotic spots on the stalk. Over time, the stalk assumes a strangulated shape, and the plant collapses (Fig. 1C). This occurs at the same stages on the plant as crown rot. Leaf chlorosis accompanied by necrotic spots appears when the plants were full vegetation. The disease persists on the plant until physiological maturity (Fig. 1D). The disease manifested as gray mold on the quinoa plant as soon as the panicle inflection occurred, or as the panicle entered flowering. At this stage, the disease was most visible when the panicle took on a flaccid, drooping shape. The plant began to dry, and within one week of attack, the main and secondary panicles had completely dried (Fig. 1E). The disease spread rapidly, with all plants in the field being affected within 10 days. Wilt was observed on several plants in various locations, manifesting as a loss of plant rigidity, with curling of the upper apical bud (Fig. 1F).

Morphological characterization of the fungi

The number of parasite species found on each symptom varied. Rhizoctonia solani, Fusarium oxysporum, Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, and Ascochyta rabiei were the pathogens identified in samples of damping-off castings (Fig. 3a, a’; b, b’; c, c’ and j, j’). In the case of brown stalk rot, the fungi responsible are Phoma exigua, Botrytis cinerea, Colletotrichum dematium, Macrophomina phaseolina, Curvularia lunata and Curvularia pallescence (Fig. 3h, h’; g, g’; i summer i’ and p, p’).The pathogens associated with brown crown rot include Rhizoctonia solani, Fusarium solani, Fusarium oxysporum, Fusarium moniliforme, and Sclerotium sclerotiorum (Fig. 3b, b’; c, c’; j, j’; r, r’ and s, s’). On leaves exhibiting chlorosis and necrotic lesions, the following pathogens were isolated M. phaseolina, Melanospora zamiae, Botryodiplodia theobromae, Cercospora sesami, Phoma sorghina, P. lingam, and Nigrospora oryzae (Fig. 3a, a’; d, d’; e, e’; i, i’; k, k’ and q, q’). In samples exhibiting gray mold, Fusarium oxysporum and Botrytis cinerea were predominantly identified, with the occasional presence of Phoma sorghina (Fig. 3b, b’; d, d’ and p, p’). Wilting of the plants was attributed to species of the Fusarium genus, with the involvement of other terrestrial species such as R. solani, S. sclerotiorum, and Ascochyta rabiei (Fig. 3a, a’; c, c’ and j, j’).

From the Sahelian zone, the least-watered area of the country, to the moderately-watered North Sudanian zone, seven additional fungi were observed. In addition to the shift from the northern Sudanese zone to the southern Sudanese zone, four additional fungal species have been identified as diseases of quinoa. These include Fusarium oxysporum, Fusarium solani, Curvularia lunata, Macrophomina phaseolina, Phoma lingam, Phoma sorghina, and Rhizoctonia solani. These species were present in the different agro-ecological zones of Burkina Faso.

Koch’s postulates

Koch's postulate was employed to demonstrate a causal relationship between the observed symptoms and the identified fungi. This confirmed the significant involvement of the various fungal species isolated from infected organs, exhibiting a wide range of virulence levels. Figure 4 illustrates the diverse representative symptoms induced by the fungi inoculated on healthy plants. The most common fungal genera that produced necrotic spots with marked chlorosis on the leaves were Phoma, Botrytis, Curvularia, Colletotrichum and Macrophomina (Fig. 4b–d). For telluric fungi, the genera Fusarium, Rhizoctonia, Sclerotinia, and Ascochyta were identified as the primary causes of post-alkergen damping-off (Fig. 4e, e’). Additionally, the plants exhibited dwarfing compared to the control (Fig. 4f, f’).

Discussion

The introduction of quinoa cultivation in developing countries presents an opportunity to combat poverty and malnutrition. This realization prompted the research community in Burkina Faso to initiate public relations efforts surrounding quinoa in the country. However, the observed sickly facies in demonstration plots has been identified as a potential source of demotivation and reduced adoption of this crop by the farmers. Addressing these issues effectively could serve as a pivotal lever for relaunching the quinoa extension process in Burkina Faso. The accurate diagnosis of these facies is the first step towards defining an appropriate control strategy against these diseases, which is essential for the successful relaunch of quinoa extension in Burkina Faso. A total of 22 localities were explored in the country in search of fungal parasite problems in quinoa. Four localities were in the Sahelian zone, eight in the northern Sudanian zone, and ten in the southern Sudanian zone. The choice of locality was guided by environmental conditions (temperature and relative humidity), which are essential epidemiological factors for fungal diseases. These parameters vary from one agro-ecological zone to another. They factors can contribute to the diversification, appearance, and symptomness of pathogens on the crops and their position according to the agro-ecological division of the country. The locality visited provide a representative sample of the national territory. With regard to organ collection, the size of the samples from the southern Sudanese zone represented approximately half of the total number of samples collected. This could be explained by climatic conditions, notably temperature and humidity, which are favorable for the development and symptoms of parasitic fungi on the crops in this part of the country. In fact, it receives the most rainfall, with an average temperature of 25 °C and a relative humidity close to 80% during the rainy season. Additionally, this region of the country has the best vegetation cover and a denser hydrographic regime than the rest of the country18,19. These risk factors are thought to be at the root of the attacks observed. Through field trips to prospect, describe symptoms, and collect samples, he observed the diverse range of symptoms that developed on quinoa crops in Burkina Faso. The idea of extending the diagnosis to the entire territory proved to be a valuable approach, as it revealed a considerable degree of variability in the symptomatic manifestations observed on the plant. The symptoms manifested at various stages of the crop’s development. Damping-off was observed at the seedling stage. At the full vegetation stage, the signs included crown rot, brown stalk rot, and plant wilting. At the start of fruiting, leaf chlorosis with necrotic spots and gray mold were frequently observed on plants. These symptoms occur a first for the crop as a whole in Burkina Faso. The presence of these symptoms suggests that this crop is also a victim of pest attacks in the tropical regions of Sub-Saharan Africa, particularly in Burkina Faso. These symptoms have also been observed on Burkina Faso’s main production crops, such as maize, sorghum, cotton, and millet, which are affected by damping-off20. Stalk and crown rots have been reported extensively on Solanaceae crops throughout the country21,22. Leaf chlorosis with necrotic spots is a prevalent phenomenon observed on a vast majority of crops. While chlorosis may occasionally be attributed to nitrogen deficiency, which can complicate the diagnosis of the disease in the field, it is evident that chlorosis is primarily caused by pathogens. Depending on the crop, these pathogens may be caused by one or more different species of fungus, resulting in co-infection. Among the crops most severely affected by this phenomenon is quinoa, with gray mold being a particularly destructive agent. Indeed, once the disease appears in a field, there is a high probability that all the plants in the plot will be attacked within 2 weeks. Consequently, it is currently the most virulent disease affecting quinoa crops in Burkina Faso. Symptoms were observed in all agro-ecological zones. A statistical analysis of the samples revealed a significant effect of the agro-ecological zone on symptom frequency and distribution. In the southern Sudanian and Sahelian zones, gray mold was significantly more frequent, with 55.55% and 30.55% of samples from these zones, respectively. In contrast, plant wilting was the least frequent, with 8.33% of samples from each of these zones. Similarly, the distribution of diseases in the study localities is notable in the southern Sudanian zone. As we progressd toward the northern Sudanian and Sahelian zones, we observed a decrease in the number of symptoms per locality. This sporadic distribution of symptoms could be explained by the country's diverse agro-ecological conditions. Another study hypothesis suggests that the high prevalence of disease in humid zones can be attributed to the presence of optimal conditions, including humidity (80%) and soil temperature (24 °C), which are conducive to fungal growth and plant infection14,23. The inventory of fungal paraclimate zones associated with plant samples revealed a high mycofloristic richness of endophytic crop fungi. A total of 29 fungal species were identified as being associated with the typical symptoms of parasitic attack. Of these, 22 were found in the southern Sudanian zone, 18 in the northern Sudanian zone, and 9 in the Sahelian zone. Among the identified fungal species, seven (7) were consistently found in samples from different agro-ecological zones : Curvularia lunata, Fusarium oxysporum, F. solani, M. zamiae, Phoma lingam, P. sorghina, and Rhizoctonia solani. Our findings align with those of numerous other researchers. In the Atacama Desert in Chile, 11 fungal genera were identified on quinoa roots, with Penicillium, Phoma, and Fusarium being the most prevalent24. In a separate study conducted in Iran, notable frequencies of Alternaria, Bipolaris, Colletotrichum; Curvularia, Cladosporum, Macrophomina, Ulocladium, Aspergilus, Acremonium, Embellisia and Penicillium was observed in 192 samples, with Fusarium exhibiting a total dominance25. In China, quinoa roots are colonized by several fungal species, with Penicillium sp. being the most prevalent26. The six main symptoms were identified through diagnosis of the plants. Damping-off blight is caused by Fusarium oxysporum, Fusarium moliniforme, Rhizoctonia solani, Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, and Ascochyta rabiei. Brown crown rot is caused by Rhizoctonia solani, Fusarium solani, and Fusarium moniliforme. Phoma exigua, Botrytis cinerea, Colletotrichum dematium, and Macrophomina phaseolina have been identified as the causal agents of brown stalk rot. Chlorosis with necrotic leaf spots is a consequence of co-infection with Macrophomina phaseolina, Melanospora zamiae, Botryodiplodia theobromae, Cercospora sesami, Phoma sorghina, Phoma lingam, and Sclerotinia sclerotiorum. Gray mold, caused by Fusarium oxysporum and Botrytis cinerea, is another notable condition. Wilting of plants may be caused by Fusarium solani, Fusarium culmorum, Fusarium oxysporum, Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, and Rhizoctonia solani. There is literature on the symptoms associated with fungal species has identified R. solani and Sclerotium sp. as well as Fusarium sp. as the causal agents of quinoa damping-off. These findings are supported by the following references16,27–30. Phoma exigua and Fusarium spp. have been demonstrated to cause brown stem rot13,16,. Fusarium sp., Ascochyta sp., Alternaria spp., and Pythium spp. have been identified as the causal agents of brown stalk rot and wilt22,26,27,31,38. M. phaseolina, Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, and Ascochyta hyalospora have been documented to induce chlorosis accompanied by the formation of leaf spots in various studies32–37. F. oxysporum, Melanospora zamiae and Botrytis cinerea have been found to be responsible for gray mold26,37. In terms of the pathogenic power of telluric species, F. oxysporum may have a very strong pathogenic effect on crops as its action is important for seedling vigor and phenology. Compared with the control plants, plants infected with F. oxysporum showed a delay in flowering. The dwarfing effect induced by this fungus could lead to a very significant reduction in yield compared that to the control. It is reported that the Fusarium genus is one of the main agents responsible for wilting of various crops in South America; it is considered a phytopathogenic complex that, under favorable conditions, causes disease and devastating economic losses, affecting 60–100% of production16,38–40. It should also be noted that downy mildew, caused by Peronospora sp., is an endemic disease of quinoa reported in the main production areas and in countries where it was newly introduced14,40. According to the same authors, the pathogen of downy mildew is seed-borne and then spread by wind and rain. However, this pathogen was not detected in the organs studied. This may be explained by the absence of the pathogen in our study area, or by the fact that the quinoa seeds introduced in Burkina Faso by the FAO in 2015 did not contain the diseased germs. In general, when a crop is newly introduced to a country or region, there are often no or only minor diseases on the plants in the first few years of production, and these disease increase as the crop is grows in the area. There are three reasons for this. First, fungal pathogens have been introduced into new countries or regions and have gradually adapted and evolved from the plant material. Second, the same parasitic fungi were present in the new region, but were not given special attention due to the low level of symptoms. Once these pathogens encounter the appropriate host, diseases can spread on a massive scale. Additionally, some similar diseases are transmitted to introduced crops. Our study of Burkina Faso revealed that the crop was heavily attacked by parasitic fungi beginning in the first year of production in the wet season. Furthermore, analyses identified fungi that attack crops grown in the countries. This evidence demonstrates that the crop's fungal pathogens are present in Burkina Faso. Consequently, the introduction of quinoa in Burkina Faso has not been accompanied by the emergence of novel fungal pathogens. To this end, the efficacy of the recommended control techniques for these agents on crops could also be evaluated on quinoa in order to identify an appropriate control method. The study of the phytoparasitic potential of fungi associated with quinoa cultivation has enabled us to assess the virulence of the different species tested. The results revealed that the most prevalent foliar symptoms observed in infected plants were yellowing with brown spots, drying, and necrosis. These symptoms were observed in plants infected with the majority of the fungi tested. This evidence suggests that these plant parasites can be involved in the manifestation of several diseases of the aerial organs of the plant, including the leaves.

Conclusion

The study was initiated with the objective of diagnosing the various fungal diseases of quinoa throughout Burkina Faso. Consequently, six distinct fungal diseases of quinoa were identified, along with the specific pathogens responsible. Damping-off blight (i) is caused by the following pathogens: Fusarium oxysporum, Fusarium moliniforme, Rhizoctonia solani, Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, and Ascochyta rabiei. Brown crown rot is caused by the fungi Rhizoctonia solani, Fusarium solani, and Fusarium moniliforme. Brown stalk rot is caused by Phoma exigua, Botrytis cinerea, Colletotrichum dematium, and Macrophomina phaseolina. Chlorosis with necrotic leaf spots is caused by a number of fungal pathogens, including M. phaseolina, Melanospora zamiae, Botryodiplodia theobromae, Cercospora sesami, Phoma sorghina, and P. lingam. Gray mold is caused by Fusarium oxysporum, Melanospora zamiae, and Botrytis cinerea. Wilting of plants induced by Fusarium solani, Fusarium culmorum, Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, and Rhizoctonia solani was observed in all three (3) of the country’s agro-ecological zones. Among the aforementioned diseases, gray mold was particularly common and severe in its impact on crops. Given the significance of quinoa in the global effort to combat malnutrition and in Burkina Faso in particular, integrated strategies to combat these diseases are also essential to protect the plant and ensure its successful dissemination in regions where parasitic pressures are prevalent. Fusarium species produce a mycotoxin belonging to the trichothecene family.

Materials and methods

The sampling locations and methods of specimen collection were determined based on the country’s agro-ecological zones. In the northern region, the Sahelian zone is distinguished by an annual rainfall of 600 mm or less. The rainy season lasts for a mere 3 months or so. The amplitude of temperature fluctuations, both on a daily and annual basis, is considerable, resulting in low levels of relative humidity. In the center, a northern Sudanian zone distinguished by an annual rainfall range between 600 and 900 mm. This is the country’s largest agro-ecological zone. The rainy season lasts for approximately 4–5 months. The average monthly air temperature is between 28 and 29.5 °C. The average air humidity is less than 32.92%18,41. Finally, the South Sudanian zone, which occupies the southern part of the country, is distinguished by an annual rainfall of 900 mm or more. The region is distinguished by relatively low temperature amplitudes, with a range of 20–30 °C. The average monthly air temperature is above 25 °C, with an average humidity of 52.92%42. A total of 22 localities were surveyed in the three aforementioned zones for the purpose of sample collection (Fig. 5). The seeds of the SIMABA and FENI varieties were utilized in the field tests. These varieties were developed in INERA and registered in the national catalog of plant species and varieties of Burkina Faso. The reference numbers are SCHV563 for SIMABA and SCHV564 for FENI43. At each site, samples were collected separately according to the typical symptoms visible on the organs. In instances where symptoms exhibited similar characteristics within a given site, a single sample was collected. Samples were placed in envelopes or plastic bags bearing the collection number, date, name, and geographical coordinates of the site. Subsequently, the samples were transported and stored at a temperature of 10 °C in the laboratory for the purpose of conducting a study on the inventory and rate of infection by the various species.

Identification of fungal species form specific symptoms

To identify the full range of fungal paralocalities associated with quinoa cultivation in Burkina Faso, species isolation was carried out on all 138 samples collected. From each sample, five (5) fragments were removed, disinfected in ethanol (70%) or sodium hypochlorite (1%), rinsed with sterile distilled water and dried under a laminar flow hood. Following disinfection, the fragments were placed in Petri dishes containing potato dextrose agar (PDA) culture medium. Five additional fragments from each sample were also taken and placed directly into the Petri dishes without disinfection, in to recover any fungi that might have been eliminated following disinfection. The inoculated dishes were incubated in a chamber maintained under an alternating cycle of near-UV light and darkness (12/12 h) at a temperature ranging from 22 to 25 °C for 7 days. The fungal colonies grown in Petri dishes were transferred to new dishes and incubated again under the same conditions. Following purification, the colonies were identified according to the morphological criteria proposed in various reports on the identification and characterization of phytopathogenic fungi21.

Pathogenicity testing

In order to ascertain whether a causal relationship exists between fungal species and the symptoms observed, the most frequently identified fungal species were inoculated onto healthy quinoa plants.

Inoculum preparation

A conidial suspension of varying titres, as per the nature of the fungal species and pertinent literature data, was prepared. The isolates were subsequently transferred to Petri dishes containing PDA media and incubated for 7 days. Five (5) ml of sterile distilled water supplemented with Tween 20 was to each Petri dish. The mycelium and conidia were then carefully scraped off with an angled Pasteur pipette. The resulting suspension was then filtered and shaken. The conidial concentration was estimated using a Fuchs–Rosenthal counting cell and adjusted to the desired concentration per ml.

Inoculation procedure

In accordance with the nature of the fungal species obtained from the isolates, two distinct inoculations procedures were employed. Substrate inoculation was employed for fungal species known to be telluric, while leaf inoculation was used for aerial fungal species. Inocula derived from telluric fungal species were employed to infest a steam-sterilised substrate at a rate of 20 ml at a concentration of 106 per 4 kg of substrate. Seeds were disinfected with bleach and subsequently sown on the infested substrates in pots, with the non-infested control. Twenty-one day-old seedlings were produced in aseptic conditions on sterile substrate. For the inoculation of the aerial parts, a microwounds was created using carborundum to facilitate the penetration of the fungal conidia into the leaves. Inoculations were conducted on three leaves at disparate locations on the plant with a volume of 20 µl of inoculum. For the control group, plants were inoculated in the same way with sterile distilled water.

Data analysis

The dataset was organized using EXCEL 2016 spreadsheet software. The averages obtained were compared by analysis of variance using the Student–Newman–Keuls multiple comparison test at the 5% threshold, which was performed with IBM SPSS version 22 software. QGIS version 3.10 software was used to construct a geographical distribution map of fungal diseases.
