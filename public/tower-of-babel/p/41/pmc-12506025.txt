Comprehensive analysis of ammonia-induced cell death and GLS1 in gastric adenocarcinoma: implications for prognosis and therapeutic strategies
Authors: Bing Du, Jiantao Dong, Qingmin Gao, Zhaoqiang Yin, Junping Yang, Shaowei Ma, Chunyi Song, Huan Ren, Zixin Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastric cancer, primarily manifested as gastric adenocarcinoma (STAD), remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide. Ammonia-induced cell death (AID), a newly discovered form of cell death, has gained attention due to its unique mechanisms, including lysosomal alkalinization and mitochondrial dysfunction. However, the role of AID in STAD, particularly its impact on immune regulation and patient prognosis, remains unclear.MethodsThis study integrated bulk RNA-seq and single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data. The Seurat package was used for single-cell gene expression analysis, clustering different cell types, and AID scoring. CellChat software analyzed the ligand-receptor interactions between immune cell subtypes. Differential expression analysis (DEGs) and GO/KEGG enrichment identified key genes associated with AID. We constructed a prognostic risk model based on these findings. Immune cell infiltration was analyzed using the “GSVA” and “xCell” packages. In vitro experiments were conducted on HGC-27 and MKN45 gastric cancer cell lines, where GLS1 was knocked down using siRNA to assess the effects on cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and lysosomal function. Additionally, drug sensitivity tests were used to evaluate the impact of GLS1 overexpression on resistance to various anticancer drugs.ResultsSingle-cell clustering analysis revealed that T cells had significantly higher ammonia-induced cell death (AID) scores, leading to the classification of cells into high-AID and low-AID groups. Gene expression analysis and pathway enrichment showed significant enrichment in lysosomal and mitochondrial pathways, consistent with known AID mechanisms. A prognostic risk model based on five key genes (C1QA, MARCKSL1, GLS1, N4BP2L2, and CD68) effectively classified patients into high-risk and low-risk groups, with the high-risk group showing stronger immune cell infiltration, including CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages. Among the model genes, GLS1 was identified as the most significant prognostic factor, the strongest risk factor, and the most central gene in the interaction network. In vitro experiments showed that GLS1 inhibition led to increased ammonia levels, elevated lysosomal pH, and reduced lysosomal function, thereby enhancing AID expression. Furthermore, GLS1 overexpression significantly promoted cell proliferation, migration, and tumor growth. Finally, gastric cancer cells with GLS1 overexpression exhibited resistance to multiple anticancer drugs, highlighting the potential value of GLS1 as a therapeutic target.ConclusionThe AID model is a promising biomarker for accurately determining survival and predicting the effectiveness of immunotherapy in STAD patients. GLS1 plays a crucial role in driving tumor proliferation and migration and may act as a potential tumor biomarker of STAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-03982-w.Keywords: Ammonia-induced cell death, Gastric adenocarcinoma, GLS1, Prognostic model

Introduction

Stomach cancer scientifically referred to as stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) poses a significant global health challenge due to its high incidence and complex progression, impacting diverse populations worldwide [1–3]. Despite advanced medical technology and new therapeutics, the diagnosis and treatment of this cancer, especially in its advanced stages, still remains challenging [4–6]. Its complexity depends not only on its biological diversity but also on its tumour microenvironment (TME) and the various reactions occurring therein [7, 8].

Ammonia is one of the most important toxic metabolic byproducts in the gastrointestinal tract. It can cause gastrointestinal damage and related diseases through various endogenous or exogenous stimuli [9, 10]. Recent studies have shown that rapidly proliferating T cells release ammonia in the mitochondria through glutamine metabolism, which is then transported to lysosomes and stored. When ammonia accumulates excessively, it causes an increase in lysosomal pH, halting ammonia storage and triggering ammonia backflow into the mitochondria, leading to mitochondrial damage and cell death. This form of cell death is characterized by lysosomal alkalinization, mitochondrial swelling, and impaired autophagic flux. Inhibition of glutamine catabolism or blocking lysosomal alkalinization can prevent ammonia-induced T cell death and enhance T cell-based anti-tumor immunotherapy. These findings reveal a novel form of cell death distinct from previously known mechanisms, termed ammonia-induced death (AID) [11]. Therefore, AID may represent a potential new therapeutic target for gastric cancer, and research on potential biomarkers associated with AID is of significant clinical relevance for gastric cancer patients.

In this study, we explored the expression and distribution of AID at the single-cell level. Through analysis, we successfully identified five key genes (C1QA, MARCKSL1, GLS1, N4BP2L2, and CD68) and constructed an AID model based on these genes. Furthermore, GLS1 was ultimately identified as the most important prognostic risk gene and was confirmed to promote the proliferation, invasion, and growth of STAD. All results indicate that GLS1 holds significant potential as a valuable predictive biomarker and therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

The data and clinical annotations used in this analysis were taken from the public Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. In addition, bulk RNA-seq data of 320 adenocarcinoma patients from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-STAD group were also used to gain a comprehensive understanding of STAD. In addition, RNA-seq data of 433 other adenocarcinoma patients were obtained from the GSE84437 source, and RNA-seq data of 5 single-cells sequencing (scRNA-seq) under the GES167297 group were also included in this research to investigate cell-level variations more deeply. In addition, we compiled 39 AID-associated genes from the relevant literature (Supplementary Table 1).

scRNA-seq analysis

At the beginning of the analysis, we subjected 5 STAD scRNA-seq samples in GES167297 to single cell analysis, which yielded clusters of distinct cell types. We also used the AddModuleScore in the “Seurat” package to identify AID markers.

We followed rigorous quality control procedures to enhance the quality of scRNA-seq data. In this process, cells containing more than 25% of microcontroller-genes were removed from the data to keep the answers leading globally. Next, the ScaleData function based on variance stabilizing transformation (VST) was used to normalize the top 2000 highly variable genes across all samples. The RunPCA function was applied to 20 dimensions to limit the dimension of the existing data and clarify key features. Next, cells were divided into different clusters using the “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” functions, and a resolution of 0.5 was set to increase accuracy. Finally, uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was used to represent the high-dimensional cell data in a two-dimensional form, which groups together cells with similar expression patterns and displays cells with different patterns separately. This technique provided a clear and understandable depiction of cellular diversity in scRNA-seq data.

Cell–cell interaction analysis

To understand the process of communication between immune cell subtypes, we analysed single-cell gene expression matrices based on ligand-receptor interactions using CellChat software. This software not only modelled the communication possibilities but also identified the major communication pathways, which helped to provide a deeper understanding of the complex dialogue between subtypes of immune cells.

Differential analysis

We used Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function to analyze differences between AID-high and AID-low groups. Genes with adjusted p < 0.05 and LogFC > 0.585 showed significant enrichment across a range of cellular pathways and functions.

Functional enrichment analysis

We used “clusterProfiler” in R to identify significant gene expression changes between AID-high and AID-low groups, analyzing pathways through gene ontology (GO) and kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) with an FDR < 0.05.

Prognostic risk model

Using the “TinyArray” package, we performed Cox survival analysis on differentially expressed gene (DEGs), which found 145 significant genes with a p-value of < 0.05. Previous research has highlighted the role of machine learning in identifying genes that are important in medical and biological applications. Using lasso regression, we narrowed the selection from 17 initial candidates to five key genes C1QA, MARCKSL1, GLS1, N4BP2L2 and CD68 to create a prognostic model. These genes contributed to the risk score formula in the data from the GSE84437 hort: C1QA*0.310—MARCKSL1*0.203 + GLS1*0.575—N4BP2L2* (−0.430) + CD68*0.113. Based on this average risk score, patients were divided into low and high risk groups, from which significant prognostic differences emerged. The model was validated on the GSE84437 cohort of 433 STAD patients, confirming strong prognostic differences between the two groups.

Immune infiltration analysis

We used the “GSVA” R package to assess the presence and comparative amounts of 23 types of immune cells in different tumour samples. Additionally, the “xCell” R package was used to rule out the interference of 64 immune and stromal cells. To effectively display the results, visualization techniques such as heat maps, scatter plots, and box plots were used.

Cell culture and transfection

Researchers validated findings using HGC-27 and MKN45 human gastric cancer cell lines from the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Cultured in DMEM and RPMI-1640 respectively, each medium was chosen to match the specific metabolic requirements of these cell types, ensuring accurate observations for gastric cancer studies. 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin mixture were added to all culture media to provide nutrients and protect against infection. The cells were kept in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C containing 5% CO2, and their growth was monitored daily, with passage every 24 h. Next, transfection experiments of a commercial siRNA were performed in both cell lines to knock down GLS1 expression, including a reference siRNA for negative control. Cells were detached by trypsin, washed with PBS, and centrifuged and resuspended in fresh medium. After determining the cell concentration, they were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 10^4 cells per well. A mixture of siRNA and Lipofectamine 3000 was prepared and allowed to stand at room temperature for 15 min, after which the mixture was slowly added into the wells. Four hours after transfection, the medium was changed, and further experiments were performed after 48 h. Thus, this study looked at the effect of transfection of cancer cell lines and reduction of GLS1 expression, which may prove useful in cancer treatment research.

CCK-8 assay

Following 48 h transfection, cells were trypsinized (KeyGEN, China), resuspended, and seeded at 5000 cells per well in 96-well plates with triplicates for accuracy. Once adhered, each well received 200 μL of CCK-8 reagent mixed with culture medium as per protocol, shielded from light. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured post 1.5 h and at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h for a time-course assessment.

EDU assay

After 2 days (48 h) transfection, the cells were plated in a 96-well plate where each well contained 1 × 104 cells. After all the cells adhered to the well, 100 μM 2X EdU solution was added to each well and incubated for 2 h. After incubation, the cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 100 μL universal fixative for 15 min. Subsequently, the cells were washed again and permeabilized using 0.3% Triton X for 15 min. After the additional washing step of this procedure, the cells were stained with fluorescent dye for 30 min. Hoechst 33,342 was added to each well to specifically stain the nuclei. This entire procedure was repeated three times to obtain conclusive results.

Transwell assay

To maintain sterility, 45 μL of 1:7 diluted matrigel was introduced to the transwell chamber and left for 36 h. After that, 700 μL of complete enrichment media was introduced to the 24-well plate. After 48 h of transfection, trypsin digested the cells and suspended them in FBS-free media. To count, 20,000 cells were placed in each compartment with 170 μL media and cultured for 24 h. After stabilizing with paraformaldehyde for 30 min in PBS, the non-invasive cells were taken from the moist cloth. After 0.1% crystal violet coloring, the cells were imaged under a microscope. The technique was repeated for accuracy. This study examined cell aggression.

Wound healing assay

48 h after transfection, a 200 μL pipette tip made precise vertical scratches in each well, replaced with each use to avoid contamination. The culture medium was removed, and wells rinsed twice with PBS. Each well received 2 mL of serum-free base medium to set the 0-h mark for wound healing. After 24 h, wound closure was measured through imaging, with attention to precision to enhance cell culture research reliability.

Ammonia measurement

An Ammonia Assay Kit (Abcam) was employed to measure ammonia levels. In brief, cells were harvested and lysed on ice, with an equal number (around 1 × 106) of cells from various groups used for ammonia extraction. Furthermore, one-tenth of the cell lysate was used to assess the protein concentration. The ammonia content in the whole-cell lysate or subcellular organelles was measured following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Lysosomal pH measurement

The pH of the lysosome was determined using the ratiometric lysosomal pH indicator LysoSensor Yellow/Blue DND-160. Briefly, cells were exposed to 10 μM LysoSensor Yellow/Blue DND-160 in pre-warmed medium for 5 min at 37 °C. After two washes with PBS, the labeled cells were incubated for 5 min at 37 °C with 10 μM valinomycin and 10 μM nigericin in Living Cell Imaging Solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Fluorescence intensity was recorded at an excitation wavelength of 360 nm and emission wavelengths of 440 nm and 550 nm. A standard pH curve was generated using the Intracellular pH Calibration Buffer Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Enzyme activity measurement

The activities of cathepsin B and D in cell lysates were assessed using kits ab65300 and ab65302 (Abcam). The activities of α-mannosidase and β-galactosidase were measured using the substrates 4-nitrophenyl-α-d-mannopyranoside (sc-220980; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and 4-nitrophenyl-β-d-galactopyranoside (sc-220969; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), as previously outlined [10.1038/s41556-024-01503-x IF: 19.1 Q1 B1]. The assays were conducted with 5 mM substrate in enzymatic assay buffer (50 mM sodium citrate (pH = 4.5), 0.1% Triton X-100, and 0.4% bovine serum albumin) and 200 µg cell lysate in a final volume of 200 µl for 16 h at 37 °C. Reactions were terminated by adding 1 ml of 0.4 M glycine (pH = 10.4), and the absorbance of released p-nitrophenol was measured at 405 nm.

Immunoblotting

Protein from whole cells or subcellular organelles was extracted and denatured by boiling at 95 °C in 2 × working loading sample buffer (1 M Tris–HCl, 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate, glycerol and 10% bromophenol blue). Lysates were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. Membranes were blocked for 1 h with TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.5% Tween-20) containing 5% non-fat milk, then washed four times with TBST and incubated overnight with the indicated primary antibodies at 4 °C. Primary antibodies against the following proteins were used for western blotting: RHCG (1:1,000; sc-100287; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), LAMP1 (1:1,000; 46,843; Cell Signaling Technology), RHCG (1:1,000; sc-398816; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), GLS1 (1:1,000; 29,519–1-AP; Proteintech) and β-actin (1:1,000; 3700; Cell Signaling Technology). SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for detection according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence of paraffin sections, after dewaxed with xylene, ethanol, dH2O and heat-induced antigen retrieval, the slides were rinsed with PBS and then permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 twice for 10 min each. The tissues were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at RT and incubated with anti-human GLS1 Polyclonal antibody (Proteintech Cat No. 29519–1-AP), rabbit anti-human RHCG antibody (Proteintech11949-1-AP) overnight at RT. After washing, the slides were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-goat anti-rabbit antibodies (Abcam, ab150077), Alexa Fluor 647-goat anti-rabbit antibodies (Abcam, ab150079) for 60 min and counterstained with DAPI (Sigma). Finally, the tissues were observed by microscopy (Olympus IX71) and processed by cellSens imaging software.

Cloning of GLS1 and DNA plasmids constructs

RT-PCR was used to isolate full-length cDNAs of GLS1 encoding mouse mRNA prepared from whole HGC-27 cells, and then the cDNAs were subcloned into the pCS2-MT vector using endonuclease sites NotI and ApaI. The sequences of the siRNAs (Genepharma, China) used in this study are as follows:

GLS1 siRNA-1, 5’ -AGCUCAGUCUUUAGUUAUCTT-3’ (antisense);

siRNA-2, 5’-UACUUCGUUGUGAGGCUAUTT-3’ (antisense).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the R language. The process involved using Cox regression to analyze data related to survival time, including both univariable and multivariable analysis. Specifically, R packages called “survival” and “survivor” were used for this. A p-value < 0.05 was chosen as the threshold standard to measure the statistical relevance of the results, indicating that the findings were considered more significant in prediction when this threshold was crossed.

Analysis of genetic mutations between high risk groups and low-risk groups

Mutation analysis revealed distinct mutation patterns between the low-risk and high-risk groups. Single nucleotide variation (SNV) analysis showed that C > T transitions were the most common mutation type in both groups, followed by T > C transversions (Fig. 4A, upper panels). A detailed examination of the mutation landscape indicated that TP53 and MUC16 had the highest mutation frequencies in both groups; however, TTN mutations were predominant in the high-risk group, while frame-shift insertions were more frequent in the low-risk group (Fig. 4A, lower panels). Correlation analysis of the five hub genes (C1QA, MARCKSL1, GLS1, N4BP2L2, and CD68) revealed a significant positive correlation between C1QA and CD68, while MARCKSL1 and N4BP2L2 showed a negative correlation, suggesting potential cooperative or opposing roles in tumor progression (Fig. 4B). Chromosomal mapping further illustrated the genomic locations of the five hub genes, highlighting their distinct distribution across different chromosomes (Fig. 4C). These findings provide new insights into the genetic differences between risk groups and the potential molecular mechanisms underlying tumor heterogeneity and progression.

Differential immune infiltration analysis between high or low-risk groups

Immune infiltration analysis revealed significant differences in the TME between high-risk and low-risk groups. The high-risk group showed higher levels of immune cell infiltration, including B cells, CD8 + T cells, dendritic cells, and natural killer cells (Fig. 5A), suggesting a more active TME in these patients. Further correlation analysis demonstrated strong interactions among immune cells, particularly the significant synergistic effects between B cells and dendritic cells (Fig. 5B), highlighting the importance of the complex dynamic balance of immune cells in the tumor environment. Moreover, the risk score positively correlated with the infiltration levels of multiple immune cell types (Fig. 5C), indicating that the immune microenvironment in high-risk patients may play a dual role in tumor progression and anti-tumor immune responses. These findings underscore the critical role of immune cell infiltration in stratifying gastric cancer patients and provide new insights for optimizing immunotherapeutic strategies.

Identification of prognostic risk genes

To further identify the core genes of the AID model, we performed univariate Cox regression analysis to assess the prognostic significance of the five genes in the AID model. A forest plot was used to visually display the relationship between each gene and the prognosis of STAD patients (Fig. 7A). The results show that GLS1 is the most significant prognostic factor and the strongest risk factor, as it has the highest hazard ratio (1.78, p-value < 0.001), indicating a strong association with poor prognosis. On the other hand, N4BP2L2 is the most significant protective factor, as it has a hazard ratio of 0.65 (with a p-value of 0.008), suggesting a protective role in the context of prognosis. In contrast, MARCKSL1 and CD68 did not show statistically significant associations with prognosis in this cohort. The overall model demonstrated strong prognostic value with a global p-value of < 0.001 and a concordance index of 0.69 (Fig. 7A). To evaluate the stability of the expression of key molecules, we assessed the expression of five specific genes in the high-risk and low-risk groups (Fig. 7B). To further identify the key regulatory genes of AID, we analyzed the correlations between the five risk genes of AID and observed significant synergistic effects, with GLS1 showing the most central interaction position (Fig. 7C). In the GSE84437 database, a general correlation was detected among these prognostic risk genes. Given our identification of GLS1 as the most potent prognostic factor (Fig. 7A), we subsequently concentrated our research on assessing the impact of GLS1 on STAD prognosis. In survival analysis, the group with high GLS1 expression showed a poorer prognosis (Fig. 7D).

Additionally, the differences in immune cell infiltration between high-risk and low-risk groups were explored using EPIC immune cell infiltration assessment methods from the TIMER 2.0 website. In the STAD samples, the expression of GLS1 mRNA was negatively correlated with the infiltration of CD8 + T cells and CD4 + T cells, and positively correlated with the infiltration of Treg cells (Fig. 7E). The purity index suggested that GLS1 expression increases with the proportion of tumor cells in tumor samples within the TME (Fig. 7E). This indicates that high GLS1 expression in tumor cells may suppress immune cell infiltration through specific mechanisms, thereby reducing the presence of non-tumor cells within the TME.

Ammonia uptake into the lysosomes is primarily mediated by ammonia transporters, including RHAG, RHBG, and RHCG [12, 13]. To further investigate the relationship between GLS1 and AID relationship in clinical samples, we performed Immunofluorescence staining on a tissue from STAD patients. The results showed that patients with GLS1 expression had lower levels of RHCG, which indicated GLS1 contributes to negative regulation of AID (Fig. 7F). Taken together, these observations suggest that GLS1 is a key regulatory gene of AID in STAD, warranting further investigation. GLS1 is a prominent member of the glutaminase superfamily, a gene that encodes glutaminase, playing a crucial role in various cancers such as breast cancer, lung cancer, liver cancer, and glioblastoma [14, 15]. However, its involvement in STAD has been relatively underreported. Thus, it emerged as the focal point of this study aiming to capture a full and nuanced understanding.

Knockdown of GLS1 promoted AID in HGC-27 and MKN45 cells

To further explore the relationship between GLS1 and AID expression, we measured the intracellular ammonia concentrations in HGC-27 and MKN45 cells following GLS1 silencing using siRNA. The results revealed that GLS1 inhibition led to an increase in ammonia levels within the mitochondria (Fig. 8A). Previous research demonstrated a gradual rise in ammonium concentrations within the lysosomes. Correspondingly, the lysosomal pH gradually shifted from 4.5 to 6.2. We also measured the lysosomal pH in both HGC-27 and MKN45 cells. The lysosomal pH was elevated following GLS1 inhibition (Fig. 8B). Alternations in pH could lead to lysosomal dysfunction. The enzymatic activities of cathepsin B, cathepsin D, β-galactosidase, and α-mannosidase were significantly reduced in HGC-27 and MKN45 cells after GLS1 inhibition (Fig. 8C, D). Additionally, ammonia uptake into the lysosomes is primarily mediated by the rhesus family glycoproteins (RHAG, RHBG, and RHCG), which act as ammonia transporters [12, 13]. We observed an increase in the expression of RHBG and RHCG following GLS1 inhibition (Fig. 8E). These findings suggest that the inhibition of GLS1 markedly enhances AID expression.

The oncogenic role of GLS1 in gastric cancer progression

Our study on GLS1 in gastric cancer involved suppressing its expression with siRNA in two cell lines and RT-PCR and Westernblot verified the GLS1 expression was inhibited (Fig. S1A-C). Then we revealed its key roles in cell proliferation, invasion, and migration. Knockdown of GLS1 significantly inhibited the proliferation, invasion, and migration abilities of gastric cancer cells. In the CCK-8 assay, compared with the negative control group (si-NC), the proliferation capacity of HGC-27 and MKN45 cells in the GLS1 knockdown group (si-GLS1) was significantly reduced (p < 0.001), and this inhibitory effect became more pronounced over time (24, 48, 72, and 96 h) (Fig. 9A). Additionally, the EdU assay further confirmed the suppressive effect of GLS1 knockdown on cell proliferation, as the proportion of EdU-positive cells in the si-GLS1 group was significantly lower than that in the control group (p < 0.001), indicating that GLS1 knockdown markedly decreased DNA synthesis in gastric cancer cells (Fig. 9B). Regarding invasion ability, the Transwell assay revealed that GLS1 knockdown significantly reduced the number of HGC-27 and MKN45 cells penetrating the Matrigel-coated membranes (p < 0.001), suggesting that GLS1 is a key driver of invasion in gastric cancer cells (Fig. 9C). Moreover, the wound healing assay demonstrated a significant reduction in migration capacity following GLS1 knockdown (p < 0.001), with the wound closure rate in the si-GLS1 group being markedly lower than that of the si-NC group at 24 h (Fig. 9D).

GLS1 overexpression confers resistance to multiple anticancer agents but sensitizes cells to mitochondrial inhibition in HGC-27 cells

To investigate the impact of GLS1 overexpression on drug sensitivity, HGC-27 gastric cancer cells with enforced GLS1 expression were treated with a panel of six compounds: XAV939 (a tankyrase inhibitor) (Fig. 10A), TAF1 inhibitor (TAF1_5496_1732) (Fig. 10B), GNE-317 (a brain-penetrant PI3K inhibitor) (Fig. 10C), AZ960 (a JAK2 inhibitor) (Fig. 10D), CDK9 inhibitor (CDK9_5038_1709) (Fig. 10E), and Dihydrorotenone (a mitochondrial complex I inhibitor) (Fig. 10F). The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for each drug was calculated and is presented in Supplementary Table 2. Notably, GLS1-overexpressing cells exhibited significantly increased survival upon exposure to five of the six tested compounds (XAV939, TAF1 inhibitor, GNE-317, AZ960, and CDK9 inhibitor), suggesting a broad spectrum of drug resistance. However, intriguingly, GLS1 overexpression sensitized cells to Dihydrorotenone, a mitochondrial complex I inhibitor, evidenced by a lower IC50 value compared to control cells (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

AID is a unique form of cell death that is morphologically distinct from apoptosis, ferroptosis, and pyroptosis. It is primarily driven by lysosomal and mitochondrial dysfunction triggered by ammonia accumulation [11]. Recent studies suggest that AID plays a critical role in anti-tumor immunity by regulating T-cell activity within the TME. Notably, AID-related pathways have been shown to influence T-cell differentiation, metabolic reprogramming, and immune persistence, making them potential targets for immunotherapeutic interventions [11]. However, the role of AID in gastric cancer remains underexplored, particularly regarding its association with tumor progression, immune cell infiltration, and patient prognosis. In this study, we utilized single-cell RNA sequencing and multi-dimensional data analysis to uncover the expression patterns of AID-related genes in STAD and their complex interplay with the TME. Our findings not only validate the pivotal role of AID in STAD but also identify prognostic molecular markers associated with AID, offering valuable insights for clinical translation.

In this study, we identified DEGs from single-cell sequencing data of high and low AID expression groups and performed GO and KEGG analyses to elucidate their critical roles in the AID process. GO analysis revealed that the DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways related to mitochondrial and lysosomal structure and function, aligning closely with the molecular mechanisms of AID, which involves lysosomal alkalinization and mitochondrial damage. KEGG analysis further linked these genes to key metabolic pathways, such as the urea cycle and glutaminolysis. This enrichment pattern is highly consistent with the core mechanisms of AID: when lysosomal buffering capacity is disrupted by excess ammonia, mitochondrial damage and metabolic dysregulation rapidly trigger cell death. Our findings provide new evidence for the mechanisms underlying AID and underscore its central role in tumor metabolic regulation.

The function and activation of infiltrating immune cells in the TME are influenced by various factors, such as signaling molecules and metabolites [16–19]. More importantly, AID is not merely an intracellular metabolic event but may also alter overall tumor progression by modulating immune cell activity within the TME. Previous studies have shown that inhibiting glutaminolysis during certain early stages of T effector cell activation can enhance anti-tumor immunity. This effect is likely due to reduced glutaminolysis hindering the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle in rapidly proliferating T effector cells, thereby decreasing ATP and ROS production and promoting the differentiation of early T cells into memory CD8 + T cells [11, 20]. Additionally, transient GLS1 inhibition or lysosomal acidification may induce changes in chromatin accessibility and preserve epigenetic features, potentially supporting the long-term persistence of CD8 + T cells in the tumor [11]. Our immune infiltration analysis revealed significant immune cell infiltration, including CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages, in high-risk patients. This phenomenon likely reflects the complex mechanisms by which tumor cells reprogram the TME through metabolic pathways. On the one hand, immune cell infiltration in high-risk groups may represent a compensatory immune response aimed at suppressing tumor progression. On the other hand, the function of these immune cells in the TME may be impaired by metabolic dysregulation and lysosomal alkalinization induced by AID, leading to an ineffective anti-tumor immune response.

Understanding the TME may aid in developing novel STAD treatments or increasing the efficacy of immunotherapy. TME immune status analysis has gained significant attention as a critical immunotherapy component [21, 22]. Moreover, among the 5 AID genes, GLS1 was identified as a crucial prognostic indicator for STAD patients, despite receiving limited attention in the STAD so far. Recent research has demonstrated that GLS1 regulates glutamine metabolism in T cells, driving ammonia production that ultimately triggers lysosomal alkalinization and mitochondrial damage, leading to the death of effector CD8 + T cells [11]. This metabolic disruption not only impairs the anti-tumor function of T cells but also facilitates immune evasion within the tumor microenvironment. In this study, GLS1, as the most significant gene within the AID signature, is significantly negatively correlated with the infiltration of CD8 + T cells and CD4 + T cells, and positively correlated with the infiltration of Treg cells, as observed in both EPIC immune cell infiltration analysis. This suggests that GLS1 may serve as a marker of immune infiltration and could indicate the prognosis for immunotherapy of STAD patients.

To further investigate the relationship between GLS1 and AID in clinical samples, we performed immunofluorescence staining on tissue specimens from STAD patients. The results indicated that GLS1 contributes to the negative regulation of AID. Moreover, we verified that inhibition of GLS1 in HGC-27 and MKN45 accelerate AID in vitro, accompanied with lysosomal PH elevated and dysfunction. And overexpressed GLS1 cells also resist to various anti-cancer drugs. These findings suggest that GLS1 overexpression may reprogram cellular metabolism in a way that supports survival under pharmacological stress. GLS1 encodes glutaminase, a key enzyme in glutamine metabolism, which contributes to replenishing the TCA cycle and sustaining ATP production under stress conditions. The observed resistance may be attributed to enhanced metabolic flexibility, reduced ROS accumulation, or evasion of apoptosis pathways. Importantly, the drugs tested span diverse targets, indicating that GLS1-mediated resistance is not target-specific but may instead reflect a fundamental shift in metabolic state or cell survival mechanisms. This highlights GLS1 as a potential therapeutic vulnerability in drug-resistant gastric cancer.

Our study further underscores the role of GLS1 as a critical oncogenic driver in STAD. We demonstrated that GLS1 promotes malignant behaviors including proliferation, migration, and tumor growth. Knockdown of GLS1 significantly impaired cell viability, colony formation, and migratory capacity in HGC-27 and MKN45 cells, highlighting its essential role in sustaining STAD progression and metastatic potential. Beyond these tumor-promoting effects, GLS1 also acts as a key metabolic switch in ammonia-induced cell death (AID) by catalyzing glutamine hydrolysis to generate ammonia, leading to lysosomal alkalinization and mitochondrial dysfunction. Notably, although GLS1 overexpression conferred resistance to multiple targeted agents, it sensitized cells to dihydrorotenone, a mitochondrial complex I inhibitor. From a mechanistic perspective, glutamine enters the cytoplasm via amino acid transporters ASCT2 and SN2, where it is hydrolyzed by GLS1 to glutamate. This reaction not only substrates the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle but also facilitates reductive carboxylation—a metabolic adaptation characteristic of cancer cells that maintains citrate pool homeostasis and supports fatty acid biosynthesis under glucose-deficient conditions [23, 24]. Consequently, GLS1-driven glutaminolysis enhances mitochondrial bioenergetic and anabolic fluxes, resulting in increased cellular dependence on oxidative phosphorylation [25]. This metabolic rewiring provides a compelling explanation for the hypersensitivity of GLS1-overexpressing cells to dihydrorotenone: impairment of electron transport chain activity triggers a profound bioenergetic crisis in these cells. Our findings position GLS1 at the nexus of tumor growth and metabolic reprogramming, underscoring its dual utility as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target. Future investigations should focus on leveraging this metabolic vulnerability through combinatorial targeting of GLS1 and mitochondrial function to advance precision therapy in STAD.

Conclusion

In summary, our integrative analysis established a useful AID model, which accurately predicts survival in STAD, reflects the immunotherapy efficacy, and impacts clinical outcome evaluation. Importantly, we verified the characteristics and roles of the best predictive risk gene GLS1 of model, which could be a potential biomarker and therapeutic target in improving STAD precision treatment.
