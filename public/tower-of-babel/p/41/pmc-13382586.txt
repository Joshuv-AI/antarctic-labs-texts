Multi-omics profiling reveals gut microbiome signatures associated with cognitive decline in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Zhixin Zhao, Feiyan Zhao, Mengdi Zhang, Jiaqi Sun, Xiaoyan Wang, Jing lou, Ruizhi She, Lai-Yu Kwok, Zhihong Sun, Weizhong Huangfu, Bilige Menghe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is increasingly being linked to gut microbial dysbiosis via the gut-brain axis. We applied integrated metagenomics and metabolomics to characterize gut microecology in 28 patients with AD and 33 controls. Metagenomic analysis revealed distinct microbial community structures, with increased abundance of Akkermansia massiliensis, Alistipes onderdonkii, and Barnesiella intestinihominis in AD. Phageome analysis revealed increased richness and altered composition, with more Podoviridae and fewer Microviridae. Functional profiling identified shifts in microbial metabolic pathways involving tryptophan and short-chain fatty acid metabolism. Untargeted metabolomics revealed elevated fecal spermidine, taurocholate, and glycerophosphocholine levels in patients with AD. A random forest model combining metabolites, gut metabolic modules, and bacteriophages achieved good within-cohort classification (AUC = 0.83) but lacked external validation due to unavailable matched fecal metabolomic data. Overall, these findings link AD to coordinated disruptions across bacterial, viral, and metabolic gut layers, highlighting the need for external validation and mechanistic studies.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a prevalent neurodegenerative disorder affecting millions worldwide, with its incidence rising in parallel with the aging global population.1 Clinically, AD is characterized by progressive memory loss, cognitive decline, and behavioral disturbances in its early stages. As the disease advances, patients develop language impairments, disorientation, mood fluctuations, and eventually lose the ability to perform basic self-care tasks, leading to a profound deterioration in their quality of life.2

The pathogenesis of AD is multifactorial and incompletely understood. The classical amyloid cascade hypothesis posits that the accumulation of amyloid-β (Aβ) peptides into neurotoxic aggregates is a central driver of neurodegeneration.3 Concurrently, hyperphosphorylation of the microtubule-associated protein tau disrupts neuronal cytoskeletal integrity, leading to synaptic dysfunction and neuronal loss, which are hallmark pathological features of AD.4 In addition to these well-established mechanisms, growing evidence implicates neuroinflammation,5 oxidative stress,6 and gut microbial dysbiosis7 as significant contributors to disease onset and progression. Despite extensive research and the identification of these key pathological processes, current therapeutic strategies remain largely symptomatic and fail to halt or reverse disease progression, highlighting the urgent need for a deeper understanding of AD etiology.

The human gut microbiota is a complex and dynamic ecosystem composed of over 100 trillion symbiotic microorganisms residing in the gastrointestinal tract. It plays a crucial role in maintaining immune homeostasis, facilitating nutrient digestion and absorption, and regulating central nervous system function.8 Accumulating evidence supports a link between gut microbiome dysbiosis and the development of AD, suggesting that alterations in microbial composition may contribute to neurodegeneration.9 Individuals with AD exhibit reduced microbial diversity and distinct shifts in bacterial abundance, including increased levels of Bacteroides and decreased abundance of beneficial genera such as Bifidobacterium and Faecalibacterium.10 Notably, the relative abundance of Bacteroides is positively correlated with cerebrospinal fluid levels of YKL-40, a biomarker associated with neuroinflammation and AD severity.11 Furthermore, gut microbiota alterations influence the production of key microbial metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), amino acids, and bile acids, which are critical for maintaining intestinal integrity and supporting cognitive health. Patients with AD consistently display perturbed gut metabolite profiles, particularly, reduced concentrations of SCFAs.12 SCFAs, such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate, support neuronal health by enhancing the glutamate-glutamine cycle and mitigating oxidative stress.13 Consequently, diminished SCFA levels may compromise neuroprotection and accelerate neurodegenerative processes. While these findings underscore the potential role of gut microbiome-metabolite interactions in AD, the precise nature of microbial and metabolic alterations in AD and their functional implications remain incompletely characterized.

The microbiota-gut-brain axis represents a bidirectional communication network integrating the central nervous system, enteric nervous system, immune system, and gut microbiota via neural, endocrine, and immunological pathways.14 The vagus nerve serves as the primary conduit for gut-to-brain signaling, transmitting information from the gastrointestinal tract to the central nervous system. Gut microbes and their metabolites can modulate afferent neural activity by stimulating enteroendocrine and enteric neurons, thereby influencing neurotransmitter synthesis, neuroimmune responses, and synaptic plasticity. These interactions ultimately affect cognitive performance and memory function, positioning the gut microbiota as a potential modulator of neurodegenerative processes.15 Despite the growing recognition of this axis in AD pathophysiology, the mechanistic links between gut dysbiosis, changes in microbial metabolite levels, and neurodegeneration remain poorly defined.

Given the expanding body of evidence implicating the gut microbiome in AD and advances in multi-omics technologies, we conducted a cross-sectional study comparing individuals with AD with cognitively healthy controls. Utilizing an integrated dual-omics approach, we aimed to comprehensively characterize alterations in the gut microbiome composition and metabolite profiles in AD and assess the association of microbial and metabolic signatures with AD status and cognitive performance. Rather than inferring causality, our goal was to define disease-associated gut ecological signatures and provide a resource for future validation and mechanistic investigation. Because of the cross-sectional design, the study was not intended to determine whether gut dysbiosis contributes to AD progression or whether AD-associated clinical changes secondarily reshape the gut ecosystem.

Results

Participant characteristics and baseline demographics

Of the 70 individuals initially assessed for eligibility, nine were excluded (four due to failure to meet the inclusion criteria and five who declined to participate), resulting in a final cohort of 61 participants. This included 28 individuals diagnosed with AD (AD group) and 33 cognitively normal controls (CON group; Figure S1). The two groups were well-balanced with respect to key demographic and anthropometric characteristics (Tables 1 and S1). The mean age was 70.11 ± 7.80 years in the AD group and 68.52 ± 6.84 years in the CON group. The male-to-female ratio was 12:16 in the AD group and 16:17 in the CON group. The mean body mass index was 22.91 ± 2.14 kg/m2 in the AD group and 23.54 ± 2.50 kg/m2 in the CON group. Regarding educational attainment, the distribution across categories (illiterate, elementary, secondary, or higher) was 12:12:4 in the AD group and 10:16:7 in the CON group. No statistically significant differences were observed between the groups in age, sex, body mass index, or education level (all p > 0.05). Cognitive and functional impairment in AD

Cognitive function was evaluated in both the AD group and cognitively normal CON group using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), two validated tools for assessing cognitive impairment (Table 2). The AD group exhibited markedly reduced scores, with a mean MMSE score of 9.79 ± 1.95 and a mean MoCA score of 6.96 ± 2.43, consistent with severe cognitive decline. In contrast, all participants in the CON group achieved the maximum possible score of 30 in both assessments, indicating preserved global cognitive function. Statistical analysis revealed significantly lower MMSE and MoCA scores in the AD group than in the CON group(p < 0.0001 for both), confirming substantial cognitive deficits in the patient cohort. Further domain-specific analysis revealed that AD patients performed significantly worse than the CON group across all assessed cognitive domains, including visuospatial ability, executive function, attention, abstract reasoning, short-term memory, and orientation (p < 0.0001 for all comparisons; Table 2). At the species level, comparative analysis identified 126 bacterial taxa with significantly different abundances between the AD and CON groups (p < 0.05; Table S2). In this process, we identified 28 bacterial taxa that were potentially associated with AD. Among these, a selected panel of species was significantly enriched in the AD group: Akkermansia massiliensis, Akkermansia muciniphila, Alistipes onderdonkii, Alistipes ihumii, Barnesiella intestinihominis, Desulfovibrio fairfieldensis, Dysosmobacter welbionis, Eggerthella lenta, and Ruminococcus bromii. Conversely, a selected panel of species was significantly depleted in patients with AD, including Acidaminococcus hominis, Agathobacter rectalis, Anaerostipes amylophilus, Bacteroides ovatus, Bacteroides xylanisolvens, Phocaeicola dorei, Fusicatenibacter saccharivorans, Streptococcus salivarius, and Veillonella parvula (p < 0.05; Figure 1B).

Notably, members of the genus Bifidobacterium exhibited divergent abundance pattern. Bifidobacterium bifidum, B. pseudocatenulatum, B. dentium, and B. longum were enriched in the AD group, whereas B. animalis and B. adolescentis were reduced (Figure 1C). This genus-level heterogeneity underscores the importance of species- or strain-level resolution in microbiota studies, as different members of the same genus may play distinct roles in health and diseases.

Collectively, these findings reveal a significant restructuring of the gut microbial community in AD, characterized by specific taxonomic shifts that reflect a state of dysbiosis despite the preservation of overall microbial diversity.

While the number of nodes (i.e., SGBs) was comparable between groups, overall network connectivity was markedly reduced in the AD group compared with the CON group (Figure 2A). Specifically, the AD group exhibited fewer correlations across all strength categories: 22,635 strong (vs. 30,875 in CON), 6,287 medium (vs. 9,849), and 814 weak (vs. 1,971), indicating a substantial loss of interactive complexity within the gut microbial community in AD.

Notably, correlations involving Bifidobacterium species showed distinct patterns (Figure 2A). The AD group had fewer weak correlations between Bifidobacterium spp. and other SGBs (299 vs. 664 in the CON group) but displayed a relative increase in strong correlations (7 vs. 3). Among these, Bifidobacterium animalis emerged as a central node in both networks, maintaining numerous significant correlations with diverse SGBs across taxonomic groups (Figure 2B). This suggests that B. animalis may play a key ecological role in shaping the gut microbial community structure, even in the context of AD-associated dysbiosis.

These findings demonstrate that AD is associated with a significant reorganization of the gut microbiota interaction network, characterized by reduced overall connectivity and altered interaction dynamics, particularly within the Bifidobacterium genus.

Alterations in gut microbial metabolic functional potential in AD

Genome-centric metabolic reconstruction was performed to compare the functional profiles of the gut microbiomes between the AD and CON groups. Using 377 SGBs and annotations from the MetaCyc and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases, 51 gut metabolic modules (GMMs) with significantly different abundances were identified (p < 0.05; Table S3). These differentially represented modules were classified into eight functional categories: amino acid metabolism, saccharide metabolism, SCFA metabolism, energy metabolism, nitrogen metabolism, steroid hormone metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, and lipid metabolism.

Several metabolic pathways were significantly enriched in the AD group. Notably, modules associated with glutamate degradation, methionine degradation, butyrate production, acetate production, the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, and succinate consumption were all more abundant in patients with AD (p < 0.05; Figure 2C). In contrast, pathways involved in tryptophan biosynthesis, arabinoxylan degradation, fructan degradation, and nitric oxide degradation were significantly depleted in the AD group (p < 0.05; Figure 2C), suggesting a reduced capacity for dietary fiber fermentation and neuroprotective metabolite synthesis.

These functionally altered pathways were encoded by taxonomically diverse SGBs. For example, GMMs involved in glutamate degradation have been detected in Acidaminococcus hominis, Akkermansia massiliensis, and Akkermansia muciniphila. Methionine degradation was associated with Alistipes onderdonkii, Alistipes ihumii, and Ruminococcus bromii, whereas butyrate production was associated with Anaerostipes amylophilus, Odoribacter splanchnicus, and Vescimonas coprocola (Figure 2C). Importantly, many SGBs showing differential abundance between groups were also key contributors to these altered metabolic functions, indicating a direct link between taxonomic shifts and functional dysbiosis.

Collectively, these findings reveal a significant reorganization of the gut microbiome’s metabolic potential in AD, characterized by shifts in amino acid, saccharide, and SCFA-related pathways. These microbial metabolic pathways may be linked to host physiology, particularly neurological and cognitive functions, through the microbiota-gut-brain axis, although their mechanistic contribution to AD cannot be determined from the present cross-sectional data.

Procrustes analysis revealed a strong concordance between the overall bacterial microbiota and phage community profiles (r = 0.826, p = 0.001; Figure 3C), thus indicating a tightly coupled relationship between gut bacteria and their infecting viruses and suggesting that phage dynamics are closely linked to bacterial community structure in both health and disease.

Alpha diversity analysis showed that viral richness, as estimated by the Chao1 index, was significantly higher in the AD group compared with the CON group (p < 0.05), whereas the Shannon-Wiener diversity index did not differ significantly between groups (Figure 3D). This suggests an expansion of low-abundance or rare viral taxa in AD without a corresponding increase in evenness.

Beta diversity analysis using non-metric multidimensional scaling (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity) revealed a clear separation of phage community composition between the AD and CON groups. This structural divergence was statistically significant (analysis of similarities [ANOSIM], R = 0.142, p = 0.001; stress = 0.180; Figure 3E), indicating disease-associated remodeling of the gut virome. At the taxonomic level, the AD group exhibited a significantly higher relative abundance of bacteriophages belonging to the family Podoviridae and a lower abundance of Microviridae compared with the CON group (p < 0.05; Figure 3F), suggesting selective expansion or depletion of specific phage lineages in AD.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that AD is associated with significant alterations in the gut phageome, including increased viral richness, distinct community structures, and shifts in dominant phage families.

Distinct fecal metabolomic profiles in AD

Among these, several metabolites were markedly elevated in the AD group, including spermidine, citrulline, umbelliferone, 4,5-dihydroxyphthalate, phosphohydroxypyruvic acid, L-dopachrome, scopoletin glycerophosphocholine, 3-indoxyl sulfate, ellagic acid, leukotriene A4, taurocholate, and S-adenosylhomocysteine (all p < 0.05 vs. the CON group; Figure 4C). Notably, many of these compounds are associated with oxidative stress, inflammation, microbial bile acid metabolism, or microbial amino acid metabolism.

These findings reveal a profound reorganization of the fecal metabolome in AD, characterized by the accumulation of pro-inflammatory and stress-related metabolites and depletion of key bile acids. This altered metabolic landscape may reflect AD-associated shifts in microbial metabolism and provides biological context for potential microbiota-gut-brain interactions, although causal effects on neuroinflammation or neuronal dysfunction were not tested in this study.

A total of 181 significant correlations were identified between the cognitive scores and relative abundance of bacterial species. Notably, higher abundances of Alistipes ihumii, Alistipes onderdonkii, Akkermansia massiliensis, and Desulfovibrio fairfieldensis were inversely correlated with MMSE scores, indicating that increased levels of these taxa are associated with greater cognitive impairment.

Similarly, 119 significant correlations were observed between cognitive indices and fecal metabolite levels. Elevated levels of spermidine were negatively correlated with both MMSE and MoCA scores, while 3-indoxyl sulfate exhibited strong negative correlations with all three cognitive measures (MMSE, MoCA, and ADL). Additionally, higher levels of glycerophosphocholine were correlated with lower ADL scores, suggesting a link between these metabolites and impaired functional independence.

At the phageome level, 20 significant correlations were detected between bacteriophage abundance and cognitive performance. Specifically, the relative abundance of Microviridae showed positive correlations with both MMSE and MoCA scores, indicating that higher levels of this phage family are associated with better cognitive function. In contrast, Podoviridae abundance was inversely correlated with MMSE and MoCA scores, suggesting a potentially detrimental association with cognitive status.

Finally, 160 significant correlations were identified between cognitive scores and GMM abundance. Pathways involved in tryptophan biosynthesis were positively correlated with MoCA and ADL scores, implying that enhanced microbial capacity for tryptophan production may support better cognitive and functional outcomes. Conversely, the abundance of pathways for butyrate production and glutamate degradation were negatively correlated with MoCA and ADL scores, suggesting that elevated levels of these microbial pathways may be linked to worse cognitive status.

Collectively, these multi-domain correlation analyses revealed extensive associations between cognitive and functional performance and key components of the gut microecosystem. These findings support the existence of a functional microbiota-gut-brain axis in AD.

Gut microecological features show within-cohort classification performance and partial external corroboration

To explore whether gut microecological components could distinguish patients with AD from cognitively normal controls within the discovery cohort, random forest classifiers were trained using various combinations of microbial and metabolic features. Models were built using 10-fold cross-validation and assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), a standard metric for classification performance. The highest within-cohort classification performance was observed for the model integrating fecal metabolites, GMMs, and bacteriophage abundance, which yielded an average AUC of 0.833 (Figure 5B). This performance was higher than that of models based on other feature combinations: the classifier combining SGBs, metabolites, and GMMs attained an AUC of 0.767, while the model combining SGBs, metabolites, and bacteriophages achieved an AUC of 0.750. Surprisingly, the inclusion of all four data types (SGBs, metabolites, GMMs, and bacteriophages) resulted in a slightly lower AUC of 0.733, potentially due to increased feature noise or redundancy, highlighting the importance of feature selection in multi-omics classification.

The analysis of the variable importance in the top-performing model identified several gut microecological features that contributed most strongly to within-cohort classification (Figure 5C). Key discriminators included specific bacterial species (Alistipes ihumii, Alistipes indistinctus, and Ruminococcus intestinalis), metabolites (5-deoxystrigol, phosphatidylcholine (22:5/18:4), and S-(1,2-dichlorovinyl) glutathione), and GMMs (tryptophan synthesis and lactate consumption), which were among the top contributors for distinguishing patients with AD from controls.

To explore whether selected metagenome-derived signals observed in the discovery cohort were reproducible in an independent dataset, we analyzed an external shotgun metagenomic cohort from NGDC BioProject PRJCA022804. Because this external cohort did not include matched fecal metabolomic data, it could not be used to externally validate the integrated multi-omics classifier based on metabolites, GMMs, and bacteriophage features. Therefore, the external analysis was restricted to selected metagenome-derived phage and KO-level functional signals.

In the external cohort, the overall phage community structure did not show significant separation between AD and cognitively normal controls based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity (PERMANOVA, p = 0.32; Figure S2A). Podoviridae showed a concordant directional shift, with higher relative abundance in the AD group than in the CON group (Figure S2B); however, given the non-significant overall phage community separation, this result should be interpreted as limited directional corroboration rather than robust external validation of the phageome findings.

We further reconstructed sample-level KO-resolved functional profiles and tested predefined disease-associated metabolic themes derived from the discovery cohort. KO-derived pathway scores showed concordant directional patterns for selected pathways, particularly, lower tryptophan biosynthesis and arabinoxylan degradation scores in AD and higher methionine degradation scores in AD (Figure S3). These results provide partial functional support for selected predefined pathways, but they do not constitute broad external validation of all functional findings.

Together, these findings suggest that gut-derived multi-omics features, particularly metabolites, functional pathways, and bacteriophage components, may improve within-cohort discrimination between AD and control samples. However, because the external cohort lacked matched fecal metabolomic data, this integrated model could not be externally validated and should be considered exploratory.

Discussion

By integrating metagenomic and metabolomic profiling, we identified broad differences in the gut ecosystems of patients with AD, including bacterial and viral community shifts, altered fecal metabolite profiles, and changes in microbial metabolic pathways, many of which were associated with cognitive performance. We also observed that combinations of gut-derived multi-omics features could distinguish AD and control samples within the discovery cohort. These findings expand the descriptive landscape of gut ecological alterations in AD, but they do not establish causality. In particular, the cross-sectional design cannot determine whether gut dysbiosis contributes to AD progression or whether AD-associated clinical changes secondarily reshape the gut ecosystem. Therefore, the observed microbial, phage, functional, and metabolic alterations should be interpreted as AD-associated signatures rather than causal drivers of disease.

Previous studies suggest that the gut microbiota may influence brain health through multiple bidirectional pathways, including immune modulation, neural signaling via the vagus nerve, and metabolic production of neuroactive compounds.16,17 Although these mechanisms provide biological plausibility for the associations observed in our study, they were not directly tested here. Dysbiosis can compromise intestinal barrier integrity, leading to increased translocation of microbial products, such as lipopolysaccharides, into the systemic circulation. These metabolites can activate toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), which is expressed on the surface of immune cells. Activation of TLR4 triggers the downstream myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88) signaling pathway, resulting in the activation of NF-κB signaling, thereby inducing the transcription and subsequent release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, as well as chemokines such as MCP-1.18,19,20 These pro-inflammatory cytokines can traverse the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and gain access to the central nervous system, where they activate resident microglia and elicit chronic neuroinflammation accompanied by the generation of neurotoxic mediators, such as reactive oxygen species and reactive nitrogen species. The sustained presence of these neurotoxic agents can directly impair neuronal integrity, disrupt synaptic function, and attenuate neural plasticity, processes that collectively contribute to cognitive deficits and represent a key driving mechanism of neurodegeneration in AD.21,22 Consistent with these mechanisms, our results demonstrate a significant restructuring of the gut microbial community in AD patients. These findings are consistent with the involvement of the microbiota-gut-brain axis in AD-associated clinical status. However, they do not allow us to determine whether gut dysbiosis precedes AD progression or instead reflects downstream consequences of AD-related clinical and physiological changes. Therefore, the observed microbial perturbations should be interpreted as disease-associated ecological alterations rather than evidence of early causal or modifiable events in the AD continuum. This issue is particularly relevant because the AD group in our study exhibited severe cognitive and functional impairment, as reflected by markedly reduced MMSE and MoCA scores. Advanced AD status may influence the gut microbiome and fecal metabolome through multiple downstream pathways, including reduced physical activity, altered dietary intake, impaired gut motility, constipation, medication exposure, sleep disturbance, and other lifestyle or physiological changes. Although we attempted to reduce major confounding effects through participant selection and exclusion criteria, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Thus, the microbiome and metabolomic differences observed here may reflect both AD-associated biological processes and secondary consequences of advanced disease status. Furthermore, our results showed no significant difference in alpha diversity between patients with AD and healthy controls. This finding is not uncommon in the current literature. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of gut microbiota in AD spectrum populations indicate that differences in alpha diversity between patients with AD and controls remain inconsistent across studies, with not all reporting significant reductions.23 Analyses covering AD, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and subjective cognitive decline also show that altered alpha diversity is not a universal feature of AD.24 The gut microbiota is widely recognized as a highly complex ecosystem with notable resilience. Even under pathological conditions, microbial communities can sustain overall alpha diversity through self-regulatory capacities. Although alpha diversity did not show any significant changes, we observed more significant changes in the beta diversity and in the more detailed aspects of species composition and function. These observations further underscore the important role of the gut microbiota in AD and its link to cognitive function.

Notably, we identified a marked enrichment of Akkermansia and Alistipes spp. in patients with AD. It is important to acknowledge that numerous studies have established Akkermansia spp. as a beneficial microbe, and its mucin-degrading capacity under physiological conditions is known to support gut barrier homeostasis and exert anti-inflammatory effects.25,26 However, the role of Akkermansia spp. in the gut is increasingly being recognized as complex and context-dependent in nature.27 In the present AD cohort, the higher abundance of Akkermansia spp. may reflect disruption of the normal mucolytic balance in the gut. Based on previous experimental and clinical studies, excessive mucin degradation under certain pathological contexts has been proposed to weaken the mucus barrier and increase gut permeability, thereby facilitating systemic exposure to pro-inflammatory mediators, such as lipopolysaccharide, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha.28 These molecules may reach the brain via a compromised BBB or through vagal afferents, thus promoting neuroinflammation and neuronal injury.29,30 Elevated lipopolysaccharide levels have been detected in the brains of patients with AD and correlate with cognitive deficits,31 suggesting that Akkermansia overgrowth may exacerbate this pathological cascade. This apparent divergence from the conventionally beneficial portrayal of Akkermansia spp. highlights its dual potential in different physiological and pathological contexts. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the specific effects of Akkermansia spp. on the pathogenesis of AD is necessary in the future. Moreover, our results are consistent with those of Muhammad et al.,32 who found that poor cognitive ability is associated with an increase in Alistipes in the gut. The increased abundance of Alistipes has been implicated in AD pathogenesis through mechanisms involving neuroinflammation and neurotransmitter dysregulation and is associated with depressive symptoms.33,34

In contrast, members of the genus Bifidobacterium exhibited divergent abundance patterns: while B. animalis and B. adolescentis were depleted in patients with AD, other species such as B. longum and B. bifidum were enriched. This species-level heterogeneity underscores the functional diversity within the genus and highlights the importance of strain-specific analyses. Preclinical studies support a protective role for certain Bifidobacterium species; for example, supplementation with Bifidobacterium breve improved cognitive performance in an AD mouse model,35 while treatment with B. bifidum and B. longum reduced amyloid burden and upregulated brain-derived neurotrophic factor in AD mice, thereby enhancing synaptic plasticity and attenuating cognitive and memory impairments.36 Supplementation with B. breve significantly improved memory scores in AD patients,37 and B. bifidum and B. longum can effectively treat neuronal damage caused by oxidative stress by modifying the intestinal barrier, increasing SCFA production, and reducing neuroinflammation.38 These findings suggest that targeted restoration of beneficial Bifidobacterium species may help rebalance the gut ecosystem and mitigate AD pathology.

In the realm of gut phages, our study revealed that Microviridae was significantly reduced, whereas Podoviridae was markedly enriched in AD populations. This aligns with a growing body of research highlighting phages acting as either predators or symbionts of bacteria as key mediators of cognitive health and AD pathology given their ability to dynamically regulate the structure and metabolic functions of the bacterial community.39,40 James et al. similarly found that Microviridae is enriched in individuals with cognitive impairment and further demonstrated that interactions among phages, bacteria, and metabolites form unique patterns closely tied to aging and cognitive dysfunction in older adults.41 Critically, these phage-bacterial metabolite interactions can alter bacterial metabolic pathways, potentially impacting the health, including elevating the risks of cognitive decline and dementia. A contrasting pattern emerged from Mayneris-Perxachs, who observed lower levels of Siphoviridae but higher levels of Microviridae in cognitively impaired mice. Zhang et al. reported that in the cognitive impairment group, the beneficial phage family linked to neurogenesis and associative learning, such as Siphoviridae, was reduced, whereas the lytic phage family that disrupts beneficial bacteria, such as Myoviridae, was increased.42 This indicates that a reduction in beneficial phages weakens the bacterial community’s support for neural function, while an increase in lytic phages promotes cognitive decline by disrupting bacterial balance and driving inflammation and metabolic disorders. Together, these findings suggest that gut phage families may be linked to cognitive status through their associations with bacterial community structure and microbial metabolism. However, the external cohort provided only limited directional corroboration for Podoviridae and did not show significant overall phage community separation; therefore, the phage-related findings should be interpreted cautiously and require further validation. These observations highlight phages as an underexplored component of the gut ecosystem that warrants further mechanistic and longitudinal investigation in AD.

Our metabolomic analysis revealed profound alterations in the fecal metabolite profile of AD patients, including elevated levels of spermidine, L-dopachrome, glycerophosphocholine, taurocholate, and 3-indoxyl sulfate. At the functional level, microbial pathway analysis revealed dysregulation in tryptophan and kynurenine metabolism, as well as in SCFA production pathways. Currently, research on gut metabolism in the regulation of cognitive function has primarily focused on core metabolites such as SCFAs, bile acids, tryptophan metabolites, and polyamines. SCFAs are products of dietary fiber fermentation by beneficial gut bacteria, and their central role in cognitive regulation is to maintain intestinal barrier integrity and suppress neuroinflammation. For example, Clostridium butyricum metabolizes to produce SCFAs such as acetic and butyric acids. SCFAs can upregulate the expression of tight junction proteins such as occludin and claudin in intestinal epithelial cells, thereby altering intestinal permeability. At the same time, it affects the excessive phosphorylation of tau through the JNK/CDK5/GSK-3β pathway and influences neuronal integrity through Bax/Bcl-2 and inflammatory cytokines, thereby affecting cognitive function.43 Bile acids (BAs) contribute to cognitive regulation through their effects on lipid metabolism and neural signaling. Secondary bile acids can bind to specific bile acid receptors in the central nervous system, such as the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and G-protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5). Activation of these receptors promotes the secretion of neurotrophic factors and inhibits excessive microglial activation by increasing the level of cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby mediating anti-inflammatory effects and conferring neuroprotection.44 Additionally, BAs can stimulate intestinal L cells and pancreatic α cells to secrete hormones such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and peptide YY (PYY), thereby exerting effects on the brain. These hormones communicate with brain signaling via the circulatory system or vagal nerve transmission.45 Tryptophan-derived metabolites, such as indole and its derivatives, can modulate astrocyte activity and neuroinflammation via the aryl hydrocarbon receptor pathway and have been shown to promote hippocampal neurogenesis in animal models.46 Pan et al. demonstrated that indole-3-acetic acid (IPA) and other tryptophan metabolites can cross the BBB and activate free fatty acid receptor 3 (FFAR3) in brain endothelial cells and reduce paracellular permeability and restore tight junction protein expression, thereby slowing the decline in cognitive function.47 In summary, these studies collectively demonstrate that gut-microbiota-mediated metabolites regulate cognitive function through pathways including intestinal barrier maintenance, suppression of neuroinflammation, facilitation of neurotransmitter synthesis, and activation of autophagy. These metabolic and functional shifts provide biological context for the observed associations between gut metabolic features and AD-related cognitive status, and they support further mechanistic studies of microbial metabolism in AD.

Importantly, we observed significant correlations between gut microecological features and cognitive function. Multiple bacterial taxa, metabolites, bacteriophages, and microbial metabolic pathways were associated with MMSE, MoCA, and ADL scores. For instance, Alistipes species and 3-indoxyl sulfate showed strong negative correlations with cognitive performance, while Microviridae abundance and tryptophan biosynthesis were positively correlated. These associations suggest that specific components of the gut ecosystem are linked to cognitive and functional status, although their mechanistic roles were not directly tested. Moreover, the random forest model combining metabolites, GMMs, and bacteriophage features showed the highest within-cohort classification performance (AUC = 0.83) among the tested feature combinations. This finding suggests that integrating functional and ecological layers may improve exploratory discrimination between AD and CON groups within the discovery cohort. However, because this integrated model was not externally validated in an independent cohort with matched metagenomic, phageomic, and metabolomic data, the identified discriminators should be regarded as candidate features for future validation rather than established biomarkers. The newly added external KO-level analysis further refines this interpretation. Rather than providing broad pathway-level confirmation across all discovery-cohort findings, the external metagenomic cohort offered partial functional support for selected metabolic themes, particularly tryptophan biosynthesis and arabinoxylan degradation. This pattern suggests that some functionally relevant microbiome alterations may be more reproducible across cohorts than others, whereas several pathway-level signals may remain cohort-sensitive or context-dependent. Importantly, these external KO-level results still do not establish causality. However, they move the study beyond a purely within-cohort correlative description by testing biologically prioritized functional hypotheses in an independent dataset. In that sense, the external analysis provides partial functional corroboration for selected microbiome-associated pathways while maintaining an appropriately cautious interpretation regarding mechanism and causality. More broadly, the partial replication of tryptophan-related functional changes is notable because discovery-cohort analyses linked tryptophan biosynthesis to better cognitive and functional outcomes, and existing experimental literature supports a role for tryptophan-derived microbial metabolites in gut-brain communication. These observations do not demonstrate that reduced microbial tryptophan biosynthesis is a causal driver of AD, but they do strengthen the rationale for future mechanistic studies focused on this pathway. Overall, these results provide exploratory evidence that gut-derived multi-omics features are associated with AD status and cognitive impairment. However, because the integrated metabolite-GMM-phage classifier was not externally validated and the external KO-level analysis provided only partial functional corroboration of selected predefined pathways, multi-omics gut profiling should currently be viewed as a hypothesis-generating framework for future biomarker studies rather than a clinically validated diagnostic approach.

This study has several strengths. First, it employs a dual-omics approach that integrates deep metagenomic sequencing with untargeted metabolomics, thus enabling comprehensive assessment of both microbial composition and function. Second, the inclusion of phageome analysis adds a dimension to the gut ecosystem evaluation by revealing virus-host dynamics that may influence bacterial community stability. Third, the use of multi-domain correlation and machine learning models allows for the integration of complex biological data into clinically interpretable insights. Finally, rigorous clinical phenotyping, including standardized cognitive and functional assessments, ensures a reliable link between microbial features and host outcomes.

In conclusion, this study systematically characterized gut microecological alterations in AD using an integrated metagenomic and metabolomic approach. AD patients exhibited distinct compositional and functional changes in the gut microbiome, including elevated abundance of Akkermansia massiliensis, Alistipes onderdonkii, and Barnesiella intestinihominis, alongside significant shifts in fecal metabolites such as spermidine, 3-indoxyl sulfate, taurocholate, and glycerophosphocholine. These microbial and metabolic perturbations were closely associated with cognitive performance and showed exploratory within-cohort classification value when combined with phage and pathway data. Together, these findings support an association between AD and a distinct gut microbiome-metabolome-phage profile. However, given the cross-sectional design, the lack of full external validation of the integrated multi-omics model, and the partial nature of the external metagenomic corroboration, further longitudinal, multi-center, and mechanistic studies are needed to clarify generalizability and causality.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, due to the memory impairments and cognitive difficulties experienced by patients with AD, it was not possible to obtain precise and detailed dietary records. Although individuals with special dietary patterns were excluded during recruitment and the AD and CON groups were matched for key demographic variables, residual dietary confounding cannot be fully excluded. Second, the sample size was relatively small, and all participants in the discovery cohort were recruited from a single center in northern China. This design helped reduce heterogeneity in clinical assessment, sample collection, and regional background, but it limits the generalizability of the findings to broader populations with different ethnic, dietary, and geographical characteristics. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The present study cannot determine whether gut dysbiosis contributes to AD progression or whether AD-associated clinical changes secondarily reshape the gut ecosystem. This limitation is particularly relevant because patients in the AD group exhibited severe cognitive and functional impairment. Advanced AD status may influence gut microbial and metabolomic profiles through reduced physical activity, altered dietary intake, impaired gut motility, constipation, sleep disturbance, medication exposure, and other lifestyle or physiological changes. Although participants receiving antibiotics within previous two weeks and on AD-specific symptomatic medications such as cholinesterase inhibitors and NMDA receptor antagonists were excluded, we cannot fully exclude the influence of other medications commonly used in elderly individuals, unrecorded over-the-counter drugs, constipation-related treatments, sleep medications, or supportive therapies. Therefore, residual medication-related and disease-severity-related confounding remains possible. Fourth, the external metagenomic cohort provided only partial corroboration of selected metagenome-derived signals. The overall phage community structure was not significantly different between AD and control groups in the external cohort, and the observed Podoviridae pattern should, therefore, be interpreted as directional support rather than robust phageome validation. The KO-level external analysis was restricted to predefined metabolic themes and showed concordant directions only for selected pathways, including tryptophan biosynthesis, arabinoxylan degradation, and methionine degradation. Thus, it should be considered partial functional support rather than broad pathway-level validation. In addition, the external cohort consisted entirely of female participants, whereas the discovery cohort included both male and female participants; therefore, the external analysis cannot establish broad generalizability across sexes. Fifth, because the external cohort lacked matched fecal metabolomic data, the integrated metabolite-GMM-phage random forest model could not be externally validated. Accordingly, the model should be regarded as an exploratory within-cohort classification model rather than a clinically validated diagnostic tool. Finally, owing to clinical constraints, blood biomarkers such as plasma Aβ42, p-tau181, and p-tau217, as well as neuroimaging data such as amyloid-PET scans, were not available. Although metagenomic binning enabled species-level resolution, strain-level functional validation and in vitro or in vivo studies are required to confirm the mechanistic roles of the identified taxa, bacteriophages, pathways, and metabolites.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study was grouped according to the inclusion criteria. The sample size was developed through a comprehensive review of the literature and aligned methodologies employed in comparable clinical and microbiome studies.

Of the 70 individuals initially assessed for eligibility, nine were excluded (four due to failure to meet the inclusion criteria and five who declined to participate), resulting in a final cohort of 61 participants. This included 28 individuals diagnosed with AD (AD group) and 33 cognitively normal controls (CON group; Figure S1).

The two groups were well-balanced with respect to key demographic and anthropometric characteristics (Tables 1 and S1). The mean age was 70.11 ± 7.80 years in the AD group and 68.52 ± 6.84 years in the CON group. The male-to-female ratio was 12:16 in the AD group and 16:17 in the CON group. The mean body mass index was 22.91 ± 2.14 kg/m2 in the AD group and 23.54 ± 2.50 kg/m2 in the CON group. Regarding educational attainment, the distribution across categories (illiterate, elementary, secondary, or higher) was 12:12:4 in the AD group and 10:16:7 in the CON group. No statistically significant differences were observed between the groups in age, sex, body mass index, or education level (all p > 0.05). These results indicate that the AD and CON groups were closely matched at baseline, minimizing potential confounding effects of demographic variables in subsequent analyses.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice Guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legally authorized representatives prior to enrollment. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Inner Mongolia Medical University, China (Approval No. KY 2021012). To ensure transparency and accountability, the trial was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry (http://www. chictr.org.cn) on November 4, 2024, with registration number ChiCTR2400091801. All research activities adhered to applicable institutional, national, and international regulations governing human subject research.

Method details

Trial design and participant recruitment

This cross-sectional study was a component of a larger clinical trial investigating the potential of probiotic adjuvant therapy in AD and its underlying mechanisms. The present analysis focused specifically on characterizing gut microbiome composition and metabolite profiles in individuals with AD compared to cognitively healthy controls, with the aim of identifying microbial signatures associated with AD progression and informing the design of future probiotic intervention trials.

The study protocol was developed through a comprehensive review of the literature and aligned methodologies employed in comparable clinical and microbiome studies.65,66,67 To ensure adequate statistical power and consistency with previously published cohort sizes in similar investigations, it was planned to enroll a total of 70 participants: 35 individuals diagnosed with AD and 35 controls age-matched cognitively normal controls.

Participants in the AD group were required to be between 60 and 85 years of age, of either sex, and meet clinical criteria for a diagnosis of AD. Additional eligibility criteria included the ability to complete all required cognitive assessments and no prior treatment with cholinesterase inhibitors, N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor antagonists, or other medications used for the symptomatic management of AD. Exclusion criteria for AD patients included a history of substance abuse, drug dependence, or alcoholism; diagnosed metabolic disorders such as hypertension, type I or type II diabetes, or obesity (body mass index ≥30); significant gastrointestinal diseases (such as inflammatory bowel disease, intestinal obstruction, colon or abdominal tumors, intestinal tuberculosis); or a history of major gastrointestinal surgery (such as gastrectomy, bowel resection). Additional exclusion criteria included special dietary patterns, such as vegetarian diets, Mediterranean diets, or obvious high-fat or high-sugar tendencies; physician-prescribed dietary or exercise regimens; antibiotic therapy within two weeks prior to the study; or unwillingness or inability to comply with study procedures.

Cognitively normal controls were recruited to match the AD group in age (60-85 years) and sex distribution. Inclusion required documented normal cognitive function, as assessed by standardized neuropsychological testing, and provision of written informed consent by the participant or legal guardian. Exclusion criteria for controls were identical to those for the AD group including contraindications related to substance use, metabolic conditions, gastrointestinal pathology or surgery, recent antibiotic use, and non-compliance with study requirements.

Clinical assessments and biospecimen collection

Participants’ cognitive function was evaluated using a comprehensive battery of standardized neuropsychological and functional assessments. The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) was used as the primary measure of global cognitive status and severity of cognitive impairment. To account for educational disparities, MMSE cut-off scores for cognitive impairment were stratified by education level: ≤17 for individuals with no formal education, ≤20 for those with ≤6 years of education (elementary level), and ≤24 for those with more than 6 years of education (secondary or higher).49,50

Secondary cognitive and functional outcomes were assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), which evaluates multiple cognitive domains including visuospatial abilities, executive function, attention, abstract thinking, short-term memory, and orientation. Functional independence was measured using the Activities of Daily Living scale (ADL), while health-related quality of life was assessed with the Quality of Life in Alzheimer’s Disease scale (QOL-AD). These instruments provided a multidimensional evaluation of cognitive, functional, and psychosocial status in both AD patients and cognitively normal controls.

In parallel, fecal metagenome and metabolomic profiles were analyzed as exploratory biological outcomes. Fecal samples were collected from all participants (n = 61) using Longseegen Stool Storage Kits (Guangdong Longsee Biomedical Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China), which preserve nucleic acids and metabolites at ambient temperature before freezing. Upon collection, each sample was immediately labeled, placed on dry ice, and transported to the laboratory within 2 h. Samples were then stored at -80°C until subsequent metagenomic and metabolomic analyses.

Metagenomic sequencing, quality control, genome binning, and functional annotation

Fecal metagenomic DNA was extracted using the Magnetic Soil and Stool DNA Kit (DP712; TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The integrity of the extracted DNA was assessed using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and purity and concentration were measured using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer and the Qubit® dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen Corporation, Waltham, MA, USA), respectively. Only samples meeting stringent quality criteria (A260/A280 ratio 1.8–2.0, A260/A230 > 1.8, and sufficient yield) were used for library preparation (Nextera DNA Flex Library Prep Kit, Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Paired-end libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), generating a total of 405.74 Gb of raw sequencing data, with an average of 6.65 ± 0.47 Gb per sample (range: 5.88-9.06 Gb). Raw reads underwent rigorous quality control using KneadData (v.12.0) in conjunction with Bowtie2 (v.2.4.4) to trim adapters, remove low-quality bases, and filter out host-derived sequences. This processing yielded 398.69 Gb of high-quality, non-host metagenomic data (mean: 6.54 ± 0.48 Gb per sample; range 5.80-8.95 Gb), which was used for downstream analyses (Table S5).

High-quality reads from each sample were subjected to de novo assembly using MEGAHIT (v1.2.9) with default parameters optimized for metagenomic datasets. Assembled contigs exceeding 2,000 bp in length were binned into metagenome-assembled genomes using MetaBAT2 (v2.15). The completeness and contamination of each metagenome-assembled genome were evaluated using CheckM2 (v1.2.2). Only high-quality MAGs, defined as those with ≥80% estimated completeness and ≤5% contamination, were retained for further analysis.

To reduce redundancy and cluster metagenome-assembled genomes at the species level, dRep (v3.0.1) was employed with a 95% average nucleotide identity threshold (parameters: -pa 0.95, -sa 0.95), yielding SGBs. Taxonomic classification of SGBs was performed using Kraken2 (v2.1.3) against the National Center for Biotechnology Information non-redundant nucleotide database with default settings. Gene abundance for each SGB was quantified as reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM). Open reading frames within SGBs were predicted using Prodigal in metagenomic mode, and functional annotation was conducted by aligning predicted protein sequences against the KEGG database. KEGG ortholog assignments were then mapped to higher-level pathways and GMMs to infer the potential functional roles of the gut microbiota in AD pathophysiology.

Phageome profiling and viral abundance estimation

To characterize the gut viral community (phageome), assembled metagenomic contigs were screened for putative viral sequences using VIBRANT (v1.2.1) with default parameters, which employs a combination of gene-based machine learning and viral hallmark gene detection to identify viral genomes. The quality and completeness of identified viral contigs were subsequently assessed using CheckV (v1.0.1). Only viral contigs exceeding 5,000 bp in length were retained for downstream clustering to enhance taxonomic and functional reliability. These high-quality contigs were clustered into vOTUs using CD-HIT (v4.8.1) at a 95% nucleotide identity threshold and 80% minimum alignment coverage.

The relative abundance of each vOTU was quantified using CoverM (v0.7.0) with the following parameters: -min-read-percent-identity 0.95, -min-read-aligned-percent 0.5, -proper-pairs-only, and -exclude-supplementary.

Untargeted fecal metabolomics analysis

Untargeted metabolomic profiling of fecal samples was performed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry. Prior to extraction, frozen fecal samples were lyophilized and homogenized. Each sample (approximately 20 mg) was extracted with 600 μL of pre-cooled methanol solution containing 2-chlorophenylalanine (internal standard). Homogenization was performed for 1 min using a high-speed bead-beater system, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were filtered through 0.22 μm ultrafiltration membranes prior to remove particulate matter and ensure compatibility with the liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry system.

Chromatographic separation was achieved using an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) maintained at 25 °C with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of (A) 25 mm ammonium acetate and 25 mm ammonia in water, and (B) acetonitrile. The following gradient was used: 0–2 min, 95% B; 2–10 min, 95% to 65% B; 10–11 min, 65% to 40% B; 11–12 min, 40% B; 12–12.1 min, 40% to 95% B; 12.1–15 min, 95% B for re-equilibration.

The eluted compounds were analyzed using a Q-TOF mass spectrometer operating in both positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes with MSˆE data acquisition mode. The MS parameters were set as follows: capillary voltage, 2.5 kV (positive) and 2.0 kV (negative); cone voltage, 40 V; source temperature, 120 °C; desolvation temperature, 500 °C; desolvation gas flow, 1000 L/h; cone gas flow, 50 L/h. The scan range was from m/z 50 to 1200. Data were acquired in full-scan mode with high resolution. Notably, due to the presence of high-concentration impurities in one sample, which caused severe ion suppression effects and resultant failure in mass spectrometric detection, the data quality did not meet the analytical standards. To ensure the reliability of the metabolomics analysis, only high-quality data from the per-protocol (PP) set were included in the subsequent statistical analysis.

Collected raw data were converted to the open mzML format using ProteoWizard and processed for peak detection, retention time alignment, and signal integration using the XCMS package in R. Metabolite identification was performed by matching accurate mass, retention time, and fragmentation patterns against public and commercial databases, including the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB; http://www.hmdb.ca/), METLIN Metabolite and Chemical Entity Database (http://metlin.scripps.edu/), Massbank High-Quality Spectral Database (http://www.massbank.jp), and KEGG Database (http://www.kegg.com/).

Random forest model construction

The random forest model was employed to assess feature importance and evaluate classification performance, with input features comprising SGBs, metabolites, vOTUs, and GMMs. For model construction, a stratified split strategy of 7:3 was adopted for the training set and validation set, ensuring both sufficiency of training data and proactive monitoring of overfitting risk via an independent validation set. To further evaluate model robustness, 10-fold stratified cross-validation was introduced, minimizing random deviation from single data partitions and stabilizing performance indicators by averaging results across iterative splits. The model performance was evaluated using the area under the ROC curve (AUC). In performance visualization, the false-positive rate (FPR), true-positive rate (TPR), and corresponding thresholds under varying decision thresholds were precisely calculated using the sklearn.metrics.roc_curve function. To quantify uncertainty in generalization ability, Bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) was applied to generate multiple ROC curve samples, and the 95% confidence interval of AUC was derived from its distribution characteristics, providing rigorous statistical support for the significance of model performance. Feature importance was determined using the Mean Decrease Accuracy, and the top 10 key SGBs, metabolites, and GMMs were selected based on their feature importance rankings (Figures 5B and 5C).

Independent external metagenomic corroboration cohort

An independent external metagenomic cohort was included to explore the reproducibility of selected metagenome-derived signals identified in the discovery cohort. The external shotgun metagenomic data were obtained from the National Genomics Data Center (NGDC) BioProject PRJCA022804.68 For the present study, we extracted the subset of samples corresponding to 39 participants, including 19 patients with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and 20 cognitively normal controls (CON), for whom group information was available and whose metagenomic data could be processed through our downstream corroboration workflow (Table S6). Because this external dataset contained shotgun metagenomic sequencing data but did not include matched fecal metabolomic measurements, it was used for partial external corroboration of selected metagenome-derived functional and phage-associated signals, rather than for full validation of the original integrated multi-omics classifier. All participants in the external cohort were female; therefore, this cohort provided sex-limited external corroboration and could not be used to infer broad generalizability across sexes. The external cohort was processed using the same bioinformatic framework as the discovery cohort whenever possible. Briefly, high-quality metagenomic reads were assembled de novo using MEGAHIT, and contigs longer than 2,000 bp were binned into metagenome-assembled genomes using MetaBAT2. Genome completeness and contamination were evaluated using CheckM2, and high-quality MAGs were dereplicated at 95% average nucleotide identity using dRep to generate species-level genome bins (SGBs). Taxonomic annotation of SGBs was performed using Kraken2. Open reading frames predicted from SGBs were functionally annotated against the KEGG database, and KEGG ortholog assignments were used for external functional corroboration. In parallel, assembled contigs were screened for viral sequences using VIBRANT, assessed with CheckV, clustered into viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs) using CD-HIT, and quantified with CoverM to estimate phage abundance. For external corroboration, emphasis was placed on the direction and reproducibility of predefined features identified in the discovery cohort, rather than on de novo feature discovery.

For KO-level external functional corroboration, KOfamScan annotation outputs from the external metagenomic cohort were reconstructed into sample-level KO-resolved functional profiles by aggregating KO counts across all retained bins within each sample. Presence-absence profiles were generated in parallel by recording whether a given KO was detected in at least one bin from the same sample. To avoid de novo pathway discovery in the external cohort, predefined disease-associated metabolic themes were assembled a priori from KO annotations based on the functional directions identified in the discovery cohort, including tryptophan biosynthesis, glutamate degradation, butyrate production, acetate production, methionine degradation, succinate consumption, arabinoxylan degradation, and fructan degradation.

For each predefined theme, a KO-derived pathway score was calculated at the sample level after abundance normalization. Briefly, KO counts were converted to relative abundances, a pseudocount was added, and centered log-ratio transformation was applied. Theme scores were then computed as the mean transformed abundance of KOs assigned to the corresponding predefined functional theme.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise specified (Tables 1, 2, and 3; S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, and S6; AD group = 28, CON group = 33). Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (v4.2.1) with the following packages: vegan for ecological diversity and multivariate analyses, ggpubr and dplyr for data manipulation and visualization, and additional tools as appropriate. Alpha diversity of the gut microbiome was assessed using the Shannon-Wiener index and Chao1 richness estimator (Figures 1A and 3D). Beta diversity was analyzed using principal coordinates analysis based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity to visualize intergroup differences in microbial community structure. The significance of group separation was tested using analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) or permutational multivariate analysis of variance (Adonis) with 999 permutations (Figure 1A). The stacked graph is used to display the quality characteristics of the vOTUs (Figures 3A and 3B). Non-metric multidimensional scaling, also based on Bray-Curtis distances, was applied to assess structural variation in the phageome, with stress values used to evaluate model fit (Figures 3C, 3E, and S2). Differences in the relative abundance of bacterial species, GMMs, and vOTUs between AD patients (n = 28) and cognitively normal controls (n = 33) were evaluated using the Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test, with a statistical significance threshold of p < 0.05 (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001; Figures 1B, 1C, 2C, 3F, S2, and S3).

For metabolomic data, principal coordinates analysis based on Bray-Curtis distances was used to visualize overall metabolite differences between groups. Differential metabolites were identified using a multi-criteria approach. Partial least squares discriminant analysis was used to maximize the separation between groups, and model validity was assessed via permutation testing (999 permutations) to avoid overfitting (Figures 4A and 4B). Metabolites were considered significantly altered if they met the following criteria: (1) variable importance in projection score >1, (2) fold change >2 (or <0.5) between groups (AD = 28, CON = 32), and (3) p < 0.05 from Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001; Figure 4C). Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was calculated to evaluate associations between various parameters. P-values were adjusted for multiple corrections using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. The correlation networks were visualized using Cytoscape (Figures 2A, 2B, and 5A). Data visualization was performed using R and Adobe Illustrator. The statistical details of all the trial results can be found in the figures, tables and legends.
