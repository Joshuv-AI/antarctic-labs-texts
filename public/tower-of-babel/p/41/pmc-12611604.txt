Nutrition management of children with acute necrotizing pancreatitis following asparaginase: A case series
Authors: Jodie Greear, Lauren Steele, Hope D. Swanson, Nickhill Bhakta, Hiroto Inaba, Ching‐Hon Pui, Karen Smith, Seth E. Karol
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAsparaginase is a critical component of modern therapy for acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). Its use has improved cure rates for both children and adults with this disease. However, asparaginase therapy can be complicated by significant toxicities, including acute pancreatitis (AP). Such pancreatitis can be severe and result in life‐threatening or fatal complications. Here, we describe five cases of severe asparaginase‐induced pancreatitis in children receiving therapy for ALL. Despite maximal supportive care, these patients experienced severe symptoms and were unable to tolerate enteral nutrition (EN) initially after presentation. This case series highlights clinical features associated with intolerance to EN in this population. Providers should be mindful of these features when considering EN trials in patients with severe asparaginase‐associated AP. Some patients may require total parenteral nutrition to adequately support nutrition and recovery in severe asparaginase‐associated AP.

INTRODUCTION

Asparaginase is an enzymatic chemotherapeutic agent used in the treatment of acute lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma (ALL). 1 , 2 , 3 It has significantly improved the cure rate of childhood ALL as part of combination chemotherapy alongside daunorubicin, vincristine, and dexamethasone. Since its incorporation into standard ALL therapy, one well‐documented toxicity is acute pancreatitis (AP), occurring in up to 18% of patients. 2 , 4 , 5 While most cases are mild and spontaneously resolve, severe cases can lead to critical illness, acute necrotizing pancreatitis, long‐term endocrine or exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (EPI), and occasionally, death. 6 , 7 , 8

The development of acute necrotizing pancreatitis increases the risk of malnutrition, morbidity, and mortality. 5 , 9 The risk of nutritional deterioration and complications with oral or enteral feeding also rises with the severity and duration of AP. 5 , 8 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 AP can trigger a highly catabolic and hypermetabolic state, making timely initiation of nutrition crucial. Although numerous studies demonstrate the benefits of early enteral nutrition (EN) for patients with AP, early enteral feeding may be infeasible in cases with acute necrotizing pancreatitis due to factors including hemodynamic instability, severe abdominal distention, ischemic bowel injury, or intractable emesis. 8 , 10 , 12 , 13 As a result, some cohorts report the need for total parenteral nutrition (TPN) in 40% or more of cases. 5 , 14 Optimal strategies for ensuring adequate nutrition in severe cases remain unclear. The aim of this study is to review cases with severe AP, identify clinical characteristics present at the time of intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and identify factors associated with needing TPN.

METHODS

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of five patients diagnosed with asparaginase‐induced acute necrotizing pancreatitis who were selected due to their need for early initiation of TPN. All patients were admitted to the ICU of St. Jude Children's Research Hospital between June 2019 and January 2022.

Ethics statement

This retrospective study was approved by the St. Jude Institutional Review Board.

RESULTS

Clinical characteristics for the five patients are detailed in Table 1. All patients were diagnosed with ALL and received pegylated asparaginase (Oncaspar®) as part of their planned cancer treatment. Two patients had T‐cell ALL, and three had B‐cell ALL. The average age at ICU admission was 13 years (range: 8–17 years). All patients were white, four were male, and one was female. All patients were treated according to the standard risk therapy arm of the total therapy studies. 11 Three patients developed AP during induction, while two developed it following asparaginase administration during post‐induction therapy. Notably, all patients were initially diagnosed with AP upon ICU admission based on pain characteristics and serum pancreatic enzymes (and imaging in Patient I), and required intensive care due to the severity of their illness. As their clinical course progressed, each was subsequently diagnosed with acute necrotizing pancreatitis. 8

The median ICU stay was 56 days (range: 9–92). All exhibited hemodynamic instability and required respiratory support at the time of ICU admission, including three patients who required intubation. All patients had significant edema during the first 3 days of ICU admission. Imaging performed during the first week of admission revealed signs of bowel wall injury or altered bowel function (ileus) in all patients. Patients I, IV, and V were suspected of having peripancreatic fluid collections upon admission, but the diagnosis was delayed in Patients I and V due to limited visualization of the pancreas on initial ultrasonographic imaging. More comprehensive imaging was deferred due to clinical instability, making transfer from the ICU to radiology infeasible. Abdominal pain was not specifically tracked, as this is a common symptom of AP and not necessarily a contraindication to initiating EN.

We reviewed AP‐related laboratory data from the first week of ICU admission. 15 Abnormal hepatic aminotransferases and bilirubin were observed in four patients, and acute kidney injury developed in three patients. In all five cases, amylase, lipase, C‐reactive protein, serum glucose levels, and triglycerides were elevated with significant interpatient variability. In this population with severe AP, there was no statistical association between the degree of laboratory abnormalities and the time between ICU admission and initiation of EN or duration of ICU stay (Bonferroni p > 0.05 for both).

Each patient's nutrition plan and barriers to the safe initiation (or advancement) of oral diet, EN, and TPN were discussed during daily interdisciplinary patient care rounds. TPN was initiated if patients were not expected to meet more than 50% of their nutrition requirements in the first week. Estimated nutrition requirements were calculated by registered dietitians using American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition guidelines with stress factors used when clinically appropriate (e.g., wounds). The Metabolic Infusion Support Services (MISS) Team considered each patient's clinical condition and nutrition status (based on a full nutrition assessment) when dosing calories, protein, fluid, and micronutrients. 16

Patient V initiated TPN later than the other evaluated patients. Once stable, he refused nasogastric tube (NGT) placement but initially appeared to tolerate diet advancement to regular with pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT). The Gastroenterology team was consulted to help manage worsening nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea, but ultimately, bowel rest with gastric decompression and TPN was required in Week 2.

Difficulties delivering full EN are detailed in Table 2. Early EN was not possible for all patients at the time of admission due to bowel injury, ileus, or hemodynamic instability. The median time from ICU admission to the initiation of TPN was 3 days, while the median time to the initiation of EN was 13 days. Patient III initiated TPN on admission due to poor pre‐existing nutritional reserve and significant abdominal distention on ICU admission. EN was initiated in all cases with an elemental or peptide‐based formula due to pre‐existing gastrointestinal symptoms or concern that documented bowel injuries would impair tolerance of a standard formula. All patients' feeding tubes were fitted with pancrelipase‐containing cartridges due to documented EPI with low fecal elastase in 96% of measures (mean 5.4 measures/patient over 6 months following onset of AP). Tube feeds were started at a trophic rate and advanced gradually based on tolerance, defined as the absence of significant increases in nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, or abdominal pain. Both patients started on a peptide‐based enteral formula were switched to an elemental formula due to poor tolerance. Surgery was consulted in all five cases; gastroenterology was consulted in all cases except Patient II.

On average, it took 2.4 attempts for patients to tolerate feeds and progress to feeding beyond 50% of their total estimated nutrition requirements. Only three of the five patients fully advanced to their goal rate after 8–32 days. Patient IV never tolerated advancement of EN due to profound loss of intestinal function resulting from non‐occlusive mesenteric ischemia. Patient V tolerated slow advancement of his EN via NGT after multiple failed attempts to place a nasojejunal tube (NJT), but removed his NGT and refused replacement after 70 days. His nutrition status was maintained with an oral diet with PERT, fortified with supplemental nutrition drinks and protein modules. Other patients transitioned to oral nutrition as tolerated following tolerance of EN. However, patients continued to require PERT due to prolonged EPI after a mean of 6 months of follow‐up.

DISCUSSION

This case series describes our experience with five patients who developed acute necrotizing pancreatitis after exposure to asparaginase, who required TPN for adequate nutrition due to the severity of their illness. While trial data and expert guidance prefer EN in patients with mild AP due to improved gut integrity, motility, and avoidance of risks associated with TPN, as demonstrated in the literature, it may not be feasible in the most severe cases, such as those shown here. Early TPN administration prevented nutritional deficiency while clinical status evolves, an issue highlighted by Patient V, who initially appeared to be a candidate for early EN but who ultimately required gut rest. Critical illness and associated fluid and electrolyte shifts seen in this population are also a potential barrier to early EN or TPN. To our knowledge, this is the first study detailing the challenge of providing EN to patients with severe asparaginase‐induced acute necrotizing pancreatitis.

In all five cases, patients presented to the ICU with hemodynamic instability and respiratory distress or failure. All five patients demonstrated symptoms of significant abdominal inflammation and endocrine pancreatic insufficiency within the first 7 days. Four patients also experienced acute kidney and/or liver injuries. Although the severity of illness varied, this group was critically ill, with end‐organ dysfunction requiring ICU support, and frequently progressed to multiorgan dysfunction, including gastrointestinal impairment. ICU admission, required by all patients in our cohort, was observed in only approximately 10% of patients in a large multigroup study, 9 underscoring the severity of their illness. In our own retrospective data, ICU support was required by 21% of patients with a mean ICU stay of 5 days. 14 This suggests that the severity of AP and thus the depth of nutritional support required by this population may be an example of the most severe nonfatal courses of AP. While our patients did have elevated body mass index, this is common in children with ALL experiencing AP and thus cannot be used to differentiate severe cases such as these from other children experiencing AP during ALL therapy.

While early initiation of EN remains the preferred route of nutrition support for most patients with asparaginase‐induced AP, this study emphasizes that such a strategy is often not feasible during the early phases of illness in patients with severe AP. 12 , 16 , 17 , 18 Efforts to initiate EN within 72 h in these patients were unsuccessful due to the severity of illness. Furthermore, the impact of delaying TPN could be compounded by the challenges of providing parenteral TPN to critically ill patients with organ dysfunction. 19 Table 2 emphasizes these challenges as no patient met their full caloric requirement and only three patients met their full protein requirements by Day 14. Line access, procedures, fluid overload, liver and kidney injuries, hyperglycemia, acid–base imbalances, lipid shortages, and electrolyte derangements reduced the MISS Team's ability to quickly meet the patient's nutrition requirements once initiated. 19 In such cases, delaying the initiation of TPN could lead to nutritional deterioration. Published clinical guidelines highlight the importance of providing adequate nutrition support (meeting two‐thirds of energy requirements and full protein requirements via EN or TPN within the first week of admission), given the known association between adequate nutrition and improved outcomes in critically ill children. 20 Once stable, patients should be individually evaluated with a focus on meeting established criteria to tolerate oral diet or EN can be initiated and advanced to provide two thirds of the energy requirements within the first week. Based on existing published clinical guidelines, 20 if achieving this goal is unlikely, TPN should be initiated and continued until EN can be safely advanced. Our patients also highlight the challenges of early TPN initiation: clinical instability associated with their AP, resulting in fluid and electrolyte shifts precluding early TPN initiation in Patients I, II, and IV, while Patient V had delayed initiation of TPN while attempting EN.

Although this single‐center study is further limited by its small sample size and lack of a control group, it highlights the challenges of nutrition in severe asparaginase‐induced AP. Critical illness precluded comprehensive radiographic assessment of disease severity in some patients, including those in this study, precluding the use of imaging features to identify patients most at risk of requiring TPN. Assessment of EPI in this population may also have been limited by the presence of diarrheal stools, particularly early in the course of illness, and difficulties interpreting fecal elastase in these conditions. Serial measures in our patients demonstrated persistent EPI, suggesting a prolonged deficit, although stool quality data are not available for all samples. Prolonged gastroenterological follow‐up for such patients is indicated. Additional research is needed to further define which children diagnosed with asparaginase‐induced AP are most likely to fail EN.

CONCLUSION

Children with acute necrotizing pancreatitis following asparaginase may require TPN to prevent nutritional deficiency. Due to the lack of specific predictors of EN failure, each patient should be assessed individually. Further prospective multicenter studies are needed to assess factors predisposing to early EN failure in this group of patients.
