Impact of New Orthopaedic Surgery Residency on Hip Fracture Time to Fixation at Rural Level 1 Trauma Center
Authors: Justin MacDonald, William Wardell, Oliver Sogard, Joseph Yee, Carter Whittemore, Jeffrey Lutton, Robert Behm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Hip fractures are among the most common fractures treated in orthopaedic surgery. An estimated 18% of women and 6% of men are affected globally, accounting for approximately 14% of all fractures in individuals aged 50 years or older in the United States1,2. Treatment of hip fractures accounts for approximately 72% of the total national direct medical costs associated with osteoporosis-related fractures including inpatient, outpatient, and long-term care components1. Hip fractures are estimated to contribute up to $16 billion in annual healthcare costs in the United States by 20403. Contributing factors costs include patient comorbidities, fixation methods, complications, delayed surgery, and hospital length of stay (LOS). Current American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) guidelines provide a moderate strength recommendation that hip fractures undergo fixation within 24 to 48 hours of admission4. The recommendation is based on evidence demonstrating that surgical delay increases the risk of complications—including pneumonia, venous thromboembolism, and myocardial infarction—and is associated with increased short-term mortality.5

The demand for orthopaedic surgeons to care for hip fractures continues to grow. Orthopaedic surgery is estimated to have one of the largest surgical subspecialty shortages with an estimated deficit of 5,050 surgeons6. This shortfall is driven by aging orthopaedic workforce approaching retirement, federal caps on residency training positions, and rising procedural demand associated with population aging6. The supply of orthopaedic surgeons is primarily dictated by residency training positions. According to National Resident Matching Program data, 916 postgraduate year 1 (PGY1) orthopaedic surgery positions from across 218 programs are currently available, an increase from 203 programs and 849 positions in 20207. Unfortunately, increased training positions have not kept pace with projected demand.

Implementing a new residency program presents challenges; healthcare systems and attending surgeons may question the effect residents have on orthopaedic standards of care. Nevertheless, the continued training of competent future orthopaedic surgeons is vital to meeting the increasing need for hip fracture care. Previous studies evaluating resident involvement in hip fracture care have reported increased surgical times, LOS, and cost8-11. Dutcher12 compared surgical time, LOS, readmission rate, and direct and indirect costs of hip fracture care before and after implementation of an orthopaedic surgery residency, finding similar total operative time with higher indirect costs but shorter LOS and lower direct costs with resident involvement. To our knowledge, no study has evaluated orthopaedic resident effect on time to fracture fixation.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the impact of residents on the time from hip fracture consultation to operative fixation before and after the initiation of an orthopaedic surgery residency program at a rural trauma community hospital.

Methods

After obtaining institutional review board approval, retrospective data were obtained from institutional electronic medical records (EMR) on all patients from July 2019 to June 2023 who underwent operative hip fracture fixation. The orthopaedic residency inaugural class began in July 2021 consisting of PGY-1 and PGY-2 residents. Data collected from July 2019 to June 2021 represented the preresidency period. Data collected from July 2021 to June 2023 represented the period of orthopaedic resident involvement. During the preresidency period, orthopaedic hip fracture consultations were initially evaluated by orthopaedic advanced practice providers, with attending orthopaedic surgeons providing supervision and determining operative management. Following implementation of the orthopaedic residency program, orthopaedic residents (PGY-1 through PGY-4) assumed primary responsibility consultations and participation in fracture fixation under the direct supervision of the attending. Postoperative protocols remained unchanged and consistent between preresidency and postresidency time periods. The preresidency period overlapped with COVID-19–related operational disruptions, which may have influenced workflow metrics but were not independently controlled for in this retrospective design.

Fracture patterns were classified into either intracapsular or extracapsular. Surgical treatment was performed at a single institution by or under the supervision of 1 of 8 attending orthopaedic surgeons. Fixation methods were categorized into arthroplasty, open reduction internal fixation (ORIF), closed reduction percutaneous pinning (CRPP), or intramedullary fixation. Data were collected from EMR. Primary outcome was time from orthopaedic consultation to time the patient was brought to the operating room (OR) for definitive fixation. This outcome was selected to specifically evaluate orthopaedic workflow after notification of a hip fracture, rather than overall hospital processes preceding orthopaedic consultation. Consultation time was defined as the time orthopaedic consult order was placed in EMR system, and time in OR was defined as the time recorded the patient physically entered the OR. Secondary outcomes included LOS, 30-day hospital readmission rate, and 90-day return to emergency department. The study excluded periprosthetic fractures, isolated greater or lesser trochanter fractures, or revision surgery for malunion, nonunion, or hardware failure.

Statistical analyses were performed by a contracted third-party biostatistician. Time from orthopaedic consultation to operative fixation and hospital LOS demonstrated right-skewed distributions and were therefore summarized using median and interquartile range (IQR). Comparisons of these non-normally distributed variables between preresidency and postresidency groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney test. Categorical variables, including fixation method, readmission, and emergency department (ED) return, were compared using chi-square tests.

Time to surgery was additionally evaluated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, with “survival” defined as the probability of not yet having entered the OR following orthopaedic consultation. Differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Mean values are reported descriptively but were not used for hypothesis testing of skewed variables. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

In total, 821 hip fractures underwent fixation from July 2019 to June 2023. Of these, 434 (52.9%) were performed with an orthopaedic resident participating in every surgery. There were no significant demographic differences noted in age, gender, or fracture location. Fracture pattern distribution was similar between groups, with no significant difference in the proportion of intracapsular vs. extracapsular fractures in the preresidency and postresidency periods (49.9% vs. 48.4%, p = 0.723). Despite comparable fracture patterns, there was a significant difference in fixation methods between groups; intramedullary fixation was more frequent after initiation of residency, while ORIF, CRPP, and arthroplasty were less frequent (Table I). Table II summarizes primary and secondary outcomes. No significant difference in average mean time from orthopaedic consultation to patient in the OR was found between preresidency and postresidency groups (28.3 hours vs. 27.8 hours, p = 0.786). All reported time-to-surgery metrics reflect the interval from orthopaedic consultation order placement to entry into the OR. The median length of time from consult to surgery was 20.18 hours (IQR = [13.02, 33.69]) in the preresidency group compared with 21.49 hours [13.92, 37.15] in the postresidency group. Figure 1 displays the Kaplan-Meier plot, which does not demonstrate notable differentiation between the 2 survival curves. Neither the Mann-Whitney test nor the log-rank test was significant (p = 0.207 and 0.700, respectively). The mean LOS with residents was 6.1 days compared with 6.4 days without residents (p = 0.33). No difference was observed in 30-day readmission rates or 90-day return to ED rates between the study groups (Table II). Thirty-day readmission and 90-day return to the ED were analyzed as all-cause outcomes, inclusive of both medical and orthopedic reasons.

In our cohort, the 30-day hospital readmission rate was 11.0%, which is comparable with previously reported 30-day readmission rates of approximately 4% to 25% in hip fracture populations, with other large registry studies reporting rates around 8% to 17%13,14. By contrast, our 90-day ED return rate of 26.8% appears higher than the 12% to 20% 90-day unplanned ED visit rates reported in recent single-institution hip fracture and hip hemiarthroplasty cohorts, suggesting a relatively high burden of postdischarge acute care utilization at our institution.15,16

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 821 surgically treated hip fractures, we found no significant differences in the primary outcome of time from orthopaedic consultation to operative fixation between the preresidency and postresidency periods. The mean time to surgery was similar between groups (28.3 vs. 27.8 hours, p = 0.786), and the median time to surgery showed comparable distributions on Kaplan-Meier analysis without separation of survival curves (log-rank p = 0.700). Secondary outcomes were likewise unchanged, with no significant differences in hospital LOS (6.4 vs. 6.1 days, p = 0.33), 30-day readmission rates (12.1% vs. 9.9%, p = 0.362), or 90-day return to the ED (26.1% vs. 27.4%, p = 0.728). Demographic and fracture characteristics were well balanced between groups, although the postresidency period demonstrated a higher proportion of intramedullary nail fixation (47.9% vs. 38.2%, p = 0.011). Overall, resident involvement did not negatively impact efficiency or short-term outcomes in hip fracture management.

Importance of timely definitive fixation is recommended within 24 to 48 hours after admission as delayed intervention has been shown to increase complications such as pneumonia, deep vein thrombosis, myocardial infarction, and mortality5. AAOS recommendations highlight the importance of timely fixation for geriatric hip fractures4. Time from admission to operative fixation is a reported risk factor associated with extended hospital LOS greater than 14 days with risk significantly increasing if surgery is delayed more than 4 days after admission17. Previous studies demonstrate mixed results regarding resident involvement affecting LOS, readmission rates, and cost.8-10,12

Our institution’s orthopaedic surgery residency started in 2021 with PGY 1 and 2 residents; by the end of the study period, PGY 1 to 4 residents were participating in hip fracture consultations and fixation. While patient demographics and fracture patterns were comparable between study periods, a higher proportion of intramedullary nail fixation was observed after residency implementation. As the fracture pattern distribution (intracapsular vs. extracapsular) did not differ between study periods, the increased use of intramedullary fixation after residency initiation is unlikely to be explained by differences in injury presentation and more likely reflects evolving surgical practice patterns and fixation preferences over time as 7 of 8 attending surgeons preferred intramedullary fixation when indicated vs. sliding hip screw. There were no changes in attending surgeon staffing or institutional standards of care regarding fixation selection during the study period. As such, the increased utilization of intramedullary nail fixation represents a potential confounding factor and should be considered when interpreting the results. Attending surgeon availability remained the principal determinant of operative scheduling in both periods. This consistency in attending-driven operative access across both periods likely contributed to the similar time to surgery observed before and after resident involvement. All hip fractures in our data were fixed at a single institution by both general orthopaedists and fellowship-trained orthopaedic surgeons (trauma, adult reconstruction, shoulder/elbow, sports medicine) encompassing all levels and areas of training acting help to eliminate confounding variables. We believe this factor aids to make results generalizable as it aims to decrease factors related to subspecialty skillset and potential hip fracture surgical volume differences.

A new orthopaedic residency with new residents and faculty attendings new to teaching presents challenges navigating OR availability logistics, attending preferences, and resident learning curves. Despite the potential challenges, when residents were responsible for primary hip fracture consultations, there was no delay in appropriate operative care. The mean time to OR, LOS, and 30-day readmission rates showed slight improvement when residents were involved in hip fracture care. Daily morning disposition rounds discussing new and existing patient cases with the entire residency and faculty present, timely rounding, prompt preoperative optimization, and direct resident communication with primary teams and social services may facilitate care coordination. Importantly, attending surgeon availability and OR access remain the primary determinants of when patients with hip fractures proceed to surgery. In this context, residents serve as a central hub for coordinating orthopaedic patient care and supporting efficient workflow once an operative plan has been established, rather than independently determining operative timing.

OR availability is a critical determinant of time to surgery for hip fracture patients and is an important consideration when assessing generalizability. During the study period, our institution did not have a dedicated orthopaedic trauma OR. Hip fracture cases were accommodated through a dedicated add-on trauma OR, with additional OR access available as needed, including on weekends. This OR structure is comparable with that of many community and rural trauma centers. Although our institution subsequently transitioned to a Level I trauma center with a dedicated orthopaedic trauma OR after the study period, all data analyzed herein predate that transition. As such, the findings of this study are likely generalizable to institutions without dedicated orthopaedic trauma ORs.

Limitations of the study include the retrospective study design and data collection. The intervals between hospital admission and orthopaedic consultation, and from consultation order placement to initial in-person evaluation, could not be reliably captured and were not analyzed. This study was limited by the inability to reliably determine the etiology of readmissions or ED visits (medical vs. orthopaedic) or to link ED visits with subsequent hospital readmissions, as these data were not consistently available in the EMR. In addition, postoperative complications and reasons for readmission or ED return were not systematically captured or adjudicated. Possible challenges with teaching residents to perform hip fracture surgery could increase complications related to the resident learning curve such as implant positioning, but specific orthopaedic complications such as fixation failure, periprosthetic fracture, arthroplasty dislocation, or infection could not be reliably identified, and all readmissions and ED visits were therefore analyzed as all-cause outcomes. Long-term morbidity and mortality beyond 30-day readmission and 90-day ED return were not evaluated. Patient comorbidities contributing to operative delays were not accounted for. The preresidency period overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic, introducing system-level disruptions to OR availability, staffing, and triage that may have independently affected time-to-surgery metrics. The influence of pandemic-related confounding cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

Despite the challenges posed by starting and developing a new orthopaedic residency program, the addition of residents does not negatively impact the standard of care regarding timely hip fracture care. Although not statistically significant, there was a trend toward resident involvement improving time from hip fracture consultation to operative fixation, LOS and 30-day readmission rates.
