Emerging Therapies in Pulmonary Fibrosis
Authors: Hugh Etchingham-Coll, Ekrem Temizel, Neso Okezie-Enyioma, Nieve Martin, Punchalee Kaenmuang, Sean Coghlan, Paolo Spagnolo, Nazia Chaudhuri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterstitial lung diseases (ILD) are a heterogenous group of respiratory disorders with varying degrees of inflammation and fibrosis. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), the commonest and most debilitating type of ILD, is a chronic, progressive disease of the respiratory system characterized by fibrosis of the alveolar interstitium. Subsequent, relentless decline in lung function leads to progressive breathlessness and respiratory failure. Treatment options for IPF have remained mostly unchanged in the last decade, with the availability of two antifibrotic therapies: pirfenidone and nintedanib. Recently, the US Food and Drugs Administration (FDA) approved nerandomilast for the management of IPF and progressive pulmonary fibrosis. Nintedanib is also globally approved for the treatment of progressive non-IPF ILDs. While these therapies have been shown to reduce the decline of lung function, they do not reverse existing lung damage or fully address the complex pathophysiology of pulmonary fibrosis (PF). Accordingly, research in the field has shifted to developing new therapies with improved efficacy and minimal adverse effects that directly target the intricate pathogenesis in PF with the aim of arresting or reversing the disease. This review article will set the scene by first describing the pathogenesis and prevalence of ILDs, followed by exploring the current and emerging therapies in the field.Keywords: Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, Therapies, Interstitial lung diseases, Novel, Treatment

Key Summary Points

There has been an almost 10-year inertia on the approval of novel therapies for interstitial lung diseases (ILD) with a number of negative clinical trials.Pharmaceutical companies continue to investigate novel therapies that target the complex pathogenesis of ILD.There are a number of ongoing phase 2 trials in ILD currently enrolling patients and these are described in this review.

Introduction

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) are a heterogenous group of chronic and irreversible lung conditions that affect the respiratory system [1].

Table 1 represents the current ILD classifications [2]. ILDs cause inflammation and fibrotic scarring of the lung interstitium, leading ultimately to respiratory failure. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is the most common and aggressive idiopathic subtype, accounting for approximately a third of cases, causing 13,000–17,000 deaths per year in the USA with estimated survival being approximately 3 years, if untreated [3, 4]. Major advancements in the understanding of IPF pathogenesis have been made; however, the exact causation remains unknown [5]. In contrast, other ILD subtypes have identifiable causes, such as connective tissues diseases, termed CTD-ILD, and environmental exposures, termed hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) [6, 7]. CTD-ILD and HP are more receptive to immunosuppressive treatments, though the evidence base is limited and relies primarily on observational data or that extrapolated from studies in scleroderma-associated-ILD trials. While antifibrotic agents are globally approved for IPF and progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF) and slow the rate of decline in forced vital capacity (FVC) and improve survival [8–10], they do not alter disease trajectory and are limited by side effects. As we increase our understanding of the complex pathogenesis of PF, new and more efficacious treatment targets have emerged. This review summarizes the current and emerging therapies for ILDs that aim to improve drug efficacy and safety profile. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Pathophysiology of ILDs

Representing a broad group of diverse types and subtypes, the underlying pathophysiology of ILDs varies among them with several common mechanisms: epithelial injury, inflammation, and cell senescence. Additionally, there is a complex interplay between environmental triggers, host factors, genetics and the subsequent development of inflammation and fibrosis. Environmental stimuli include a wide variety of triggers including organic compounds such as wood dust, pollutants and infectious agents, whilst host-specific triggers include autoimmunity, drugs and gastro-oesophageal reflux. These stimuli cause repetitive injury to the alveolar epithelial cells, specifically type II cells (primarily responsible for secreting surfactant and maintaining lung compliance), leading to abnormal fibroblastic activation, proliferation and differentiation to myofibroblasts.

Mutations in genes involved in mucin production (mucin 5B, MUC5B) and genes responsible for telomere length (mainly, telomerase reverse transcriptase TERT and telomerase RNA component TERC) are implicated in impaired epithelial repair and cellular senescence [11]. Overexposure to irritants promotes persistent epithelial irritation causing profibrotic mediators such as transforming growth factor beta (TGFβ) activating multiple pathways to recruit fibroblasts that deposit type I and II collagen, which scars the alveolar parenchyma [12]. Furthermore, TGFβ drives epithelial cells to lose their intracellular adhesion and acquire mesenchymal properties termed epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT). The EMT process results in cells with myofibroblast properties with enhanced migration and resistance to apoptosis. EMT activates fibroblasts and further contributes to fibrosis with the deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) [13]. Inflammation also plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of ILDs. Epithelial and endothelial injury results in monocyte infiltration and macrophage activation which release pro-inflammatory cytokines, which further contribute to fibroblast recruitment and ECM deposition [14]. Furthermore, the activation of T and B cells leads to dysregulated immune response contributing to chronic inflammation and fibrotic remodelling [15], with resultant thickening of the alveolar–capillary membrane and impairment of gas exchange across the blood–air barrier.

Prevalence and Incidence of ILDs

Estimating the true prevalence and incidence rates of ILDs remains challenging for several reasons. Risk factors for ILDs differ globally with variation in environmental triggers, occupational exposures and genetic influences [16, 17]. Discrepancy in healthcare systems; for example, access to specialist care and imaging alongside an evolution in the diagnostic criterion for diverse ILDs further complicates the picture [16, 18]. A systematic analysis of 80 studies in 2023 revealed that the most frequent ILDs were described as rheumatoid arthritis associated ILD (RA-ILD), systemic sclerosis associated ILD (SSc-ILD) and IPF [16]. Globally the age-standardised prevalence of ILDs has been estimated to be 57.62 per 100,000 people across 204 countries [19]. Between 1990 and 2019 the global incidence of ILD increased by 118.9% while ILD deaths rose by 166.6%, highlighting the rising global disease burden [18, 19]. IPF is considered the most common ILD subtype in high-income countries, particularly within North America and Europe. A recent meta-analysis of global IPF epidemiology encompassing studies published between 1984 and 2021 reported a pooled global IPF incidence of 5.8 cases per 100,000 person-years (PY) in 23 studies, and a prevalence of 17.7 PY (20 studies) [20]. A broad review of early IPF epidemiology in the UK utilised data from a nationwide primary care database from 1991 to 2003 and reported an IPF incidence of 4.6 PY [21]. An updated UK-specific description of IPF analysed 18,914 ILD cases from 2010 to 2019 estimating an IPF incidence of 1.39–16.73 PY (narrow and broad definitions) and a prevalence of 18.12 PY.

Current Therapies for ILDs

The current therapeutic landscape for ILDs can be divided into those that predominantly target fibrosis or the inflammatory components of ILDs. However, because ILDs are highly varied in aetiology, clinical presentation and pathology, no single therapy is universally effective [22, 23]. Lung transplantation is the only definitive treatment; however, due to risks and organ availability, this is limited to a minority of individuals. ILD is now the most common indication for lung transplants internationally [24].

The antifibrotic agents pirfenidone and nintedanib have been globally approved for the treatment of IPF. Pirfenidone is a small active molecule that works by exerting antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic properties to inhibit the progression of fibrosis in various organs including the lungs, kidneys and liver [25, 26]. Pirfenidone also inhibits profibrotic mediators including TGFβ thus halting the conversion of fibroblasts to myofibroblasts and preventing collagen deposition [27–29]. Three double-blinded phase 3 randomised controlled trials (RCT) of pirfenidone in IPF have been performed. The first two trials, CAPACITY 1 and CAPACITY 2, had conflicting results [10] thus requiring the third phase III ASCEND trial. The ASCEND study recruited 555 patients with IPF and met its primary endpoint with a 45.1% relative reduction in forced vital capacity (FVC) decline over 52 weeks (− 235 mL vs − 428 mL; difference 193 mL; p < 0.001). Furthermore, the proportion of patients who had an FVC decline of greater than 10% or death was reduced by 47.9% in the pirfenidone-treated group compared to placebo [8, 30]. Nintedanib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, blocks the ATP-binding site of certain receptor tyrosine kinases, to inhibit their activity thus exerting antifibrotic effects through blocking platelet-derived (PDGF), fibroblast (FGF) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) receptors. This inhibits fibroblast propagation, migration, and differentiation, to reduce extracellular matrix deposition and fibrotic lung scarring [31, 32]. Two RCTs of nintedanib in 1066 patients with IPF, the INPULSIS trials, demonstrated that nintedanib slows the progression of IPF by reducing the rate of FVC decline by 125.3 mL/year and 93.7 mL/year in INPULSIS 1 and 2, respectively [9]. Both pirfenidone and nintedanib therapies slow lung function decline, though neither can completely halt nor reverse disease progression fully. Furthermore, in the real-world setting, treatment is often hampered by tolerability. A multicentre long-term observational study showed suboptimal patient persistence to the current treatments [33], which may be due to their low perceived benefits compared with the side effects including diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, photosensitivity, and skin rashes [34]. Nintedanib is also globally approved for non-IPF PPF. The randomised, placebo-controlled INBUILD study demonstrated that nintedanib significantly slowed the rate of FVC decline over 52 weeks compared with placebo in patients with progressive fibrosing ILDs (PF-ILD). US and European regulatory bodies have approved nintedanib for adults with chronic fibrosing ILDs with a progressive phenotype based on these data. The drug’s adverse-event profile is well described as the most common toxicity is diarrhoea; however, overall tolerability in INBUILD and real-world series has been acceptable for most patients [35].

The role of anti-inflammatory agents in ILD largely stems from studies in SSc-ILD. SSc-ILDs are largely driven by systemic autoimmune dysregulation and inflammatory pathways. In SSc, fibroblasts undergo phenotypic changes characterized by high expression of serine peptidase inhibitor, Kunitz type 2 (SPINT2) and microfibril-associated protein 5 (MFAP5), referred to as the SPINT2hi and MFAP5hi fibroblast subtypes, with profibrotic properties causing fibrosis and scarring [36]. As this subtype is driven by immune cells and inflammation, antifibrotic therapies alone cannot mitigate disease, explaining the need for immunosuppressive drugs such as mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) and cyclophosphamide (CYC) [37]. The scleroderma lung studies (SLS1, SLS2) were two RCTs that evaluated the role of CYC and MMF in SSc-ILD. SLS1 was a RCT of oral CYC versus placebo in 158 patients with SSc-ILD. CYC is a cytotoxic immunosuppressant that reduces inflammation by suppressing lymphocyte activation [38]. The study demonstrated a modest improvement in FVC of 2.53% (p < 0.03) with CYC compared to placebo [39] and improved breathlessness as measured by the transitional dyspnoea index (+ 1.4 ± 0.23 in the CYC group and worsened by − 1.5 ± 0.43 in the placebo group; p < 0.001). Furthermore, there were some notable improvements in quality of life in SSc-ILD participants [40]. Although successful, CYC was also associated with adverse side effects of leukopenia and neutropenia and its efficacy waned after 12 months [39]. Considering this, MMF was introduced as an alternative, working to inhibit the inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase enzyme to reduce guanosine nucleotide synthesis in order limit inflammatory pathway cells and exert anti-fibrotic effects [38]. In SLS2, a head-to-head non-inferiority RCT of MMF versus CYC was performed in 142 patients with SSc-ILD. SLS2 compared the efficacy and safety of 2 years of treatment with MMF versus 1 year of CYC. MMF showed comparable efficacy to CYC in stabilizing or improving lung function (65% on CYC and 72% on MMF), although MMF had significantly fewer adverse events and better tolerability [37].

Another agent used for the treatment of CTD-ILD is the B-cell-depleting anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody rituximab (RTX) [41]. A meta-analysis of 40 studies [42] totalling 1052 patients with CTD-ILD reported that RTX improved FVC by 7.1% (95% confidence interval (CI) 4.58–9.62; p < 0.01) and diffusion capacity of lungs for carbon monoxide (DLCO) by 5.26% (95% CI 2.86–7.65; p < 0.01). The majority of these studies, however, were observational and retrospective (n = 28), with only six RCTs reviewed, thus introducing considerable bias in lung function timing and assessments. Studies highlight an increased risk of pulmonary infections, allergic reactions and death due to B cell depletion with RTX therapy [41, 43, 44]. RTX has also been explored in patients with CTD-ILD who progress despite maximal therapies. The RECITAL RCT directly evaluated the efficacy of RTX compared to CYC in this group of patients. RECITAL recruited a heterogenous group of 116 patients with SSc-ILD, idiopathic inflammatory myositis (IIM) and mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD) randomised to RTX versus CYC over 48 weeks. There was no superiority of RTX over CYC as both agents achieved similar improvements in FVC and quality of life [45]. While RTX was associated with fewer adverse events, CYC was more efficacious for more severe and progressive CTD-ILD types [45]. A systematic review and meta-analysis comparing RTX to CYC in CTD-ILD comprising two RCTs and four retrospective observational studies reported similar results [46].

A more recent advance in the treatment of SSc-ILD is the US FDA approval of tocilizumab, an interleukin-6 receptor antagonist [47]. Although the phase II trial, faSScinate, in early diffuse systemic sclerosis, did not meet its primary outcome of improved skin score, it demonstrated a preservation of lung function as measured by a stabilisation of FVC (a secondary outcome) [48]. Subsequently, the phase 3 RCT FocuSSced recruited 210 patients with early SSc-ILD, again demonstrating no difference in the primary endpoint of skin score. However, FVC stabilized over 48 weeks compared to placebo (− 0.4% vs − 4.6%; p = 0.0002). In a subgroup analysis, the cohort of patients whose FVC stabilized with tocilizumab had early ILD and raised inflammatory markers, thus identifying a clear target population [49]. Tocilizumab has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as therapy for SSc-ILD.

Lung transplantation, where appropriate, remains the only intervention demonstrated to improve long-term survival and quality of life. IPF is the predominant indication; however, non-IPF-ILDs contribute significantly. Post-transplant median survival is 5.2 years for IPF and 6.7 years for non-IPF ILD [24]. Limited donor availability coupled with stringent contraindications means transplantation is limited to a minority of eligible individuals.

Emerging Therapies for ILDs

Emerging therapeutic strategies for pulmonary fibrosis are increasingly focused on agents with distinct and complementary mechanisms of action that target the complex pathogenesis of fibrotic lung disease. Table 2 represents a list of emerging therapies for ILD. The pathogenesis involves a complex interplay between epithelial injury, dysregulated repair processes, immune cell activation, fibroblast proliferation, and excessive extracellular matrix deposition [50, 51]. Figure 1 represents the pathogenic framework of IPF and related therapeutic targets. While current approved therapies have demonstrated efficacy in slowing disease progression, they primarily target downstream fibrotic pathways, leaving substantial unmet medical needs. Over the past decade, several pivotal trials have failed to come to fruition and have been disappointingly negative either in efficacy or safety and tolerability [52–56].

(Top left) Recurrent alveolar and distal airway epithelial injury leads to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, epithelial senescence, and impaired regeneration of type II alveolar epithelial cells (AEC2). These processes are targeted by ER stress modulators, mitochondrial protectants, senolytics, microbiome–host response modulators, and early extracellular matrix (ECM) modulators. (Top right) Aberrant epithelial repair and epithelial–mesenchymal crosstalk are characterized by activation of profibrotic developmental pathways (TGFβ, Wnt/β-catenin, Notch, Sonic Hedgehog), unfolded protein response (UPR), and galectin-3 signalling, promoting fibroblast activation and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT). (Bottom right) The fibroblast and myofibroblast niche represents the effector compartment of fibrosis, sustained by growth factor signalling (TGFβ, PDGF, FGF), intracellular kinase activation (SRC, JAK), integrin-mediated mechanosensing, metabolic reprogramming, and resistance to apoptosis, ultimately driving myofibroblast persistence and ECM production. (Bottom left) Progressive ECM deposition and mechanobiology result in collagen accumulation, cross-linking (e.g. LOXL2-mediated), tissue stiffening, and mechano-transduction through Rho-ROCK and YAP/TAZ pathways, creating a self-amplifying profibrotic loop. Circular arrows emphasize the feed-forward and self-sustaining nature of IPF, where epithelial injury, aberrant repair, fibroblast activation, and matrix stiffening perpetuate each other and represent complementary therapeutic targets

Examples include GLPG1205, a selective antagonist of the G protein-coupled receptor 84 (GPR84), which is involved in inflammatory and fibrotic processes. Preclinical studies demonstrated antifibrotic effects in various models of fibrosis. The PINTA study (NCT03725852) [56] was a phase 2, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled proof-of-concept trial in which patients with IPF received GLPG1205 (100 mg once daily) or placebo, either as monotherapy or in combination with standard antifibrotic treatment (pirfenidone or nintedanib). The primary endpoint—change in FVC after 26 weeks—was not met. Treatment with GLPG1205 was associated with a higher rate of adverse events, particularly when combined with nintedanib. The most common side effects included gastrointestinal symptoms such as diarrhoea, nausea, and vomiting, as well as elevated liver enzymes, fatigue, loss of appetite, and worsening dyspnoea. These adverse events led to an increased rate of treatment discontinuations, especially among patients receiving background nintedanib therapy.

The phase 2b/3 BEACON-IPF trial (NCT06097260) evaluated the long-term efficacy and safety of bexotegrast (PLN-74809), an orally administered, once-daily selective dual integrin antagonist (αvβ6 and αvβ1) that inhibits the activation of TGFβ, thereby blocking profibrotic signalling pathways. In the INTEGRIS-IPF (NCT04396756) [57] phase 2a, multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled proof-of-concept trial in patients with IPF, bexotegrast at doses of 80 mg and 320 mg over 12 weeks demonstrated a significant reduction in FVC decline compared with placebo (+ 136 mL and + 140 mL, respectively); conversely, bexotegrast at doses of 40 mg and 160 mg was not superior to placebo. However, the treatment duration was too short to draw conclusions about long-term efficacy or clinical relevance. Adverse events were mostly mild, primarily diarrhoea, and no drug-related serious adverse events or safety concerns were observed. The study thus confirmed the proof of concept for bexotegrast as a potential therapy in IPF. However, the phase 2b/3 BEACON IPF study was prematurely terminated in March 2025 after an independent data safety monitoring board analysis revealed an increase in IPF-related adverse events—including disease progression, acute exacerbations, respiratory hospitalizations, and mortality—in the bexotegrast arms. Despite short-term improvements in FVC (e.g. + 72 mL with 160 mg after 12 weeks), the overall risk–benefit profile was considered unfavourable, and Pliant Therapeutics decided to discontinue the development of bexotegrast for IPF.

Recombinant human pentraxin-2 (zinpentraxin alfa) and pamrevlumab showed early phase II signals of reduced FVC decline, but neither demonstrated efficacy in subsequent phase III trials, with no significant differences in lung function or clinical outcomes compared with placebo [54, 58–60].

Galectin-3 plays a key role in macrophage activation, fibroblast proliferation, and extracellular matrix deposition, contributing to the development and progression of pulmonary fibrosis [61]. TD139 (also known as GB0139) is a potent and highly selective small-molecule inhibitor of galectin-3, specifically designed for inhaled administration, achieving high local concentrations in the alveolar compartment with minimal systemic exposure [52]. Preclinical studies and an early phase 1b proof-of-concept trial demonstrated that TD139 markedly reduced galectin-3 expression in alveolar macrophages and decreased serum biomarkers of collagen turnover, supporting its antifibrotic potential [52].

The GALACTIC-1 trial (NCT03832946), a phase 2b randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled study, evaluated the safety and efficacy inhaled GB0139 in 172 patients with IPF, including those receiving background therapy with nintedanib or pirfenidone. Participants received once-daily GB0139 or placebo for 52 weeks. The primary endpoint was annual rate in decline in FVC. The trial demonstrated clear target engagement, with significant reductions in galectin-3 expression on alveolar macrophages and favourable modulation of several fibrosis-associated biomarkers, including YKL-40, CCL18, and PRO-C3. However, the primary endpoint was not met, as GB0139 did not significantly attenuate the rate of decline in FVC compared with placebo. The safety profile was acceptable, with predominantly mild respiratory adverse events and no major safety signals. Although GB0139 showed promising biological activity, the absence of a clinical efficacy signal led to discontinuation of its development for IPF.

These failures highlight challenges and pitfalls in trial design, including smaller sample size in early phase 2 studies leading to an overestimate of size effect and efficacy in relation to FVC with failure to translate short-term benefits into longer-term positive outcomes. Furthermore, while concurrent use of antifibrotics may reduce the efficacy window of novel therapies, conversely excluding these therapies reduces generalisability and may pose ethical issues as antifibrotics are standard of care [62]. Thankfully, despite these challenges, the field of drug discovery in PF has continued to evolve. Indeed, following a decade of disappointment, the FDA recently approved nerandomilast for IPF and PPF. Positive results have also been reported for inhaled treprostinil in IPF—see below [63–65]. Furthermore, novel investigational therapies aim to intervene at multiple stages of the fibrotic cascade by concurrently modulating inflammatory mediators, fibroblast activation signals, and tissue remodelling processes [66]. Through the integration of anti-inflammatory, antifibrotic, and immunomodulatory activities, these compounds seek to disrupt the self-perpetuating cycle of injury and repair that drives fibrosis development and progression. This multi-targeted therapeutic approach holds promise for achieving more comprehensive disease control, potentially stabilizing lung function, reducing disease progression, and improving long-term outcome for patients with fibrotic ILDs.

Nerandomilast

Nerandomilast (BI 1015550) is an orally administered, preferential phosphodiesterase 4B inhibitor with antifibrotic and immunomodulatory properties [67]. In the phase 3 FIBRONEER-IPF trial (NCT05321069) [65], 1177 patients with IPF received nerandomilast 9 mg or 18 mg twice daily or placebo for 52 weeks. Most patients were on background therapy with nintedanib (45%) or pirfenidone (32%). At week 52, the decline in FVC was smallest in the 18 mg group (− 115 mL) compared with − 139 mL with 9 mg and − 184 mL with placebo, confirming a significant treatment benefit. Both doses were efficacious across key subgroups. In patients without background antifibrotic therapy, the most frequent adverse event was diarrhoea, occurring in 17%, 26% and 8% of patients on 18 mg, 9 mg and placebo, respectively, while serious adverse events leading to drug discontinuation were similar across groups. Beyond lung-function preservation, nerandomilast delayed disease progression and the need for supplemental oxygen, and numerically fewer deaths occurred in both treatment groups compared with placebo. Although not powered for mortality, this trend supports a potential survival benefit.

In the phase 3 FIBRONEER-ILD trial (NCT05321082) [64], 1176 patients with PPF were randomised to receive nerandomilast 9 mg or 18 mg twice daily or placebo for 52 weeks. About 44% of participants were on background nintedanib therapy. At week 52, the mean decline in FVC was − 166 mL with placebo, compared with − 99 mL with 18 mg and − 85 mL with 9 mg, confirming a significant treatment benefit with both doses. The effect was consistent across fibrotic ILD subtypes, indicating a broad antifibrotic activity. The most frequent adverse event was diarrhoea, reported in roughly one-third of patients on nerandomilast versus one-quarter on placebo, mainly in patients on background nintedanib; however, it was not severe and remained manageable while serious adverse events occurred at similar rates across groups.

Admilparant (BMS-986278)

Lysophosphatidic acid receptor 1 (LPA1) activation promotes fibroblast recruitment and activation as well as epithelial cell apoptosis, thereby contributing to the progression of PF [68]. A previous LPA1 blockade with BMS-986020 was shown to slow the decline in FVC in patients with IPF but was discontinued due to compound-specific hepatobiliary off-target toxicity [69]. Admilparant (BMS-986278) is an orally available, selective second-generation LPA1 antagonist with an improved hepatic safety profile due to lower lipophilicity, improved water solubility and enhanced bioavailability compared to BMS-986020. In the phase 2 randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (NCT04308681) [70], 274 patients with either IPF or PPF were randomised to receive admilparant 60 mg twice daily or placebo for 26 weeks. The mean change in percent predicted FVC at week 26 was − 2.7% in the admilparant group vs − 4.8% with placebo, corresponding to a between-group difference of + 2.1% (95% CI 0.3–3.9; p = 0.021). When expressed in absolute volume, this equates to an approximate mean difference of + 90 mL in favour of admilparant. The effect was consistent across all subgroups, including those on background antifibrotic therapy (pirfenidone or nintedanib). The most common adverse event, diarrhoea, was generally mild and predominantly observed in patients receiving background antifibrotic treatment. Transient, asymptomatic post-dose decreases in blood pressure occurred in < 5% of participants and were not clinically significant. Serious treatment-related adverse events were rare (< 2%), and there were no drug-related hepatobiliary abnormalities [62]. Currently, two phase 3 trials are ongoing worldwide to further evaluate admilparant: the ALOFT-IPF (NCT06003426) study in IPF and the ALOFT-PPF (NCT06025578) study in PPF. Both are multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials designed to assess the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of admilparant over longer treatment durations.

ENV-101 (Taladegib: Hedgehog Pathway Inhibitor)

Aberrant activation of the Hedgehog (Hh) signalling pathway promotes the differentiation of fibroblasts into contractile myofibroblasts, driving tissue remodelling and fibrosis in IPF [71, 72]. Taladegib (ENV-101) is an oral small-molecule Hh inhibitor that aims to interrupt this profibrotic cascade and potentially reverse fibrotic progression. In ENV-IPF-101 (NCT04968574) [73], a phase 2a proof-of-concept trial, 41 patients with untreated IPF were randomised to taladegib 200 mg once daily or placebo for 12 weeks. Treatment was well tolerated; all adverse events were mild or moderate, with dysgeusia, muscle spasms, and alopecia, the most common, being reported in 57%, 57%, and 52% of patients, respectively. No serious adverse events or deaths occurred. Patients receiving taladegib showed a mean improvement of 1.9% in percent-predicted FVC, compared with a decline of 1.3% with placebo, corresponding to a between-group difference of 3.95% (95% CI 0.31–7.60; p = 0.035). Improvements were also observed in high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT)-based quantitative parameters, including total lung capacity (+ 257 mL) and a reduction in fibrotic lung volume (− 9% vs + 1% with placebo) [64]. Building on these findings, the phase 2b WHISTLE-PF trial (NCT06422884) is currently enrolling patients with IPF to further evaluate dose–response, efficacy, and safety over 24 weeks. This international, multicentre, double-blind study randomises participants to ENV-101 50, 100, or 200 mg once daily or placebo, with the primary endpoint being the rate of change in percent-predicted FVC from baseline to week 24. Secondary endpoints include patient-reported outcomes and quantitative HRCT-based markers of fibrosis. Together, these studies support Hedgehog pathway inhibition as a novel therapeutic strategy in IPF, offering early evidence of improved lung function and reduced radiologic fibrosis with an acceptable safety profile.

WISPer (MTX-463)

MTX-463 is an IgG1 monoclonal antibody directed against the Wingless/Integrated (WNT1) inducible signal pathway protein 1 (WISP1), a matricellular mediator, upregulated in IPF, and implicated in myofibroblast activation, extracellular matrix remodelling, and tissue stiffening. Neutralisation of WISP1 in preclinical studies reduces profibrotic signalling and collagen deposition, providing a mechanistically distinct approach compared to current antifibrotic agents, which primarily slow, rather than interrupt fibroblast-driven progression [74]. The WISPer study (NCT06967805) is an international phase 2a, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial evaluating the efficacy, safety, and pharmacokinetics of MTX-463 in adults with IPF. A total of 164 participants aged ≥ 40 years with FVC ≥ 45% and DLCO ≥ 35% predicted were enrolled. Concomitant use of approved antifibrotics (nintedanib or pirfenidone) is permitted if dosing is stable for ≥ 90 days. Participants are randomised 1:1 to MTX-463 or placebo, administered as an intravenous infusion every 4 weeks for 20 weeks, with assessments continuing to week 24 and subsequent safety follow-up. The outcome measure is change FVC from baseline through week 24. Secondary outcomes include treatment-emergent adverse events, pharmacokinetics, immunogenicity, changes in predicted FVC, and biomarkers of WISP1 activity. Early clinical experience from the completed phase 1 programme [75] demonstrated acceptable tolerability and target engagement, with no dose-limiting toxicity detected. The current trial is designed to assess whether MTX-463 can attenuate short-term decline in lung function. Targeting WISP1 represents a mechanistically novel strategy aimed at upstream signalling pathways in fibrosis. If MTX-463 demonstrates a clinically meaningful reduction in FVC decline at 24 weeks and a favourable safety profile, it would support progression to phase 3 studies and could introduce the first antibody-based myofibroblast-direct therapy for IPF.

MIST-PPF (AP01: Inhaled Pirfenidone)

Pirfenidone is an established oral antifibrotic therapy for IPF. However, systemic exposure to high oral doses often leads to adverse events such as gastrointestinal intolerance and fatigue, limiting long-term adherence [76, 77]. To overcome this, AP01 is a novel inhaled formulation of pirfenidone designed to achieve high local pulmonary concentrations with reduced systemic toxicity. The rationale for this approach is based on early clinical data [78] showing that inhaled pirfenidone achieves high lung deposition with lower systemic exposure, potentially maintaining antifibrotic efficacy while minimizing side effects. The phase 1b, randomised, open-label, dose–response trial [79] evaluated inhaled pirfenidone (AP01) in 91 patients with IPF intolerant or ineligible for oral antifibrotics. Participants received 50 mg once daily or 100 mg twice daily via nebulizer for up to 72 weeks. At weeks 24 and 48, FVC remained stable in the 100 mg BID group (− 0.4% to + 0.6% predicted), while the 50 mg once daily showed modest declines. Treatment-related adverse events were mostly mild or moderate, including cough, rash, nausea, and throat irritation, and were less frequent than with oral pirfenidone. The MIST-PPF study (NCT06329401) [80] is an ongoing phase 2b, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial evaluating two inhaled doses of AP01 versus placebo in approximately 300 participants with PPF over 52 weeks. All patients continue standard of care, and stable background nintedanib therapy is allowed. Participants are randomised 1:1:1 to receive high-dose AP01, low-dose AP01, or placebo, administered twice daily via nebulizer. The primary endpoint is the change in FVC at 52 weeks compared with placebo, while secondary endpoints include changes in quantitative HRCT fibrosis scores, patient-reported outcomes, and safety. The MIST-PPF trial will determine whether this targeted delivery can reduce FVC decline and improve tolerability in patients with fibrotic lung disease beyond IPF.

TETON-1 and TETON-2 (Inhaled Treprostinil: Prostacyclin Analogue for IPF)

Treprostinil is a prostacyclin analogue with vasodilatory, antiproliferative, and antifibrotic properties mediated through activation of the prostacyclin (IP) receptor and cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) signalling. Following its proven efficacy in pulmonary hypertension associated with interstitial lung disease (PH-ILD) [63], inhaled treprostinil has been evaluated as a potential antifibrotic therapy in IPF. The TETON clinical program comprises two large, parallel, phase 3 randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, TETON-1 (NCT04708782) and TETON-2 (NCT05255991) [81], investigating nebulized treprostinil in patients with IPF over 52 weeks. Both studies share an identical design, with participants receiving treprostinil (up-titrated to 12 breaths four times daily) or placebo, in addition to background antifibrotic therapy (nintedanib, pirfenidone, or none). TETON-2, which enrolled 597 patients across 18 countries, has been successfully completed. The study met its primary endpoint, demonstrating a significant improvement in absolute FVC versus placebo (+ 95.6 mL; p < 0.0001). Benefits were consistent across all subgroups, including patients on standard antifibrotic therapy. Several key secondary endpoints—such as percent-predicted FVC, time to first clinical worsening, DLCO, and quality-of-life (K-BILD)—also showed statistically significant improvements. Time to first acute exacerbation and overall survival both trended in favour of treprostinil, although without statistical significance. The treatment was well tolerated, with a safety profile consistent with prior treprostinil studies and no new safety signals [70]. The TETON-1 companion trial with a similar design conducted primarily in North America remains ongoing, with results expected in the first half of 2026. Together, TETON-1 and TETON-2 will provide the pivotal evidence base for regulatory submission to expand the indication of inhaled treprostinil to include IPF. Furthermore, the TETON-PPF study (NCT05943535) [82] is a 52-week, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial designed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of inhaled treprostinil in patients with progressive fibrotic ILDs other than IPF. Approximately 698 participants are being enrolled, and background use of antifibrotics such as pirfenidone or nintedanib is permitted. The primary endpoints in the change in absolute FVC at week 52. The trial is currently ongoing, with the first patient enrolled in October 2023.

ELEVATE-IPF (Deupirfenidone: Next-Generation Pirfenidone Derivative)

Deupirfenidone is a novel pirfenidone analogue optimized for improved tolerability and bioavailability while retaining antifibrotic efficacy through inhibition of TGFβ-driven fibroblast activation and collagen deposition [83]. The ELEVATE-IPF trial (NCT05321420) [84], a global phase 2b randomised, double-blind, active- and placebo-controlled study, demonstrated a statistically and clinically meaningful reduction in lung-function decline at 26 weeks with deupirfenidone 825 mg three times daily (TID) compared with placebo. In the open-label extension (Part B), presented at the European Respiratory Society (ERS) 2025 Congress, participants previously receiving placebo or standard pirfenidone were switched to deupirfenidone for an additional 26 weeks. The treatment effect on FVC remained durable, with an adjusted mean change from baseline of approximately − 33 mL after 52 weeks in the 825 mg group versus − 147 mL with the lower 550 mg dose. Deupirfenidone was well tolerated, with the most frequent adverse events being mild dyspepsia (12%), nausea (11%), and upper respiratory tract infections (17%). Serious adverse events and discontinuations were infrequent and comparable between doses. These findings suggest that deupirfenidone provides sustained antifibrotic benefit and favourable tolerability, supporting its advancement to phase 3 evaluation [84].

Buloxibutid

Buloxibutid (C21) is an oral, selective agonist of the angiotensin II type 2 receptor (AT2R), which is expressed on alveolar epithelial type II cells and plays a key role in tissue repair. By activating AT2R, buloxibutid promotes antifibrotic, vasodilatory, and anti-inflammatory effects that may help restore alveolar integrity in IPF [85]. In the open-label, international phase 2a AIR study (NCT04533022) [86, 87], treatment-naïve patients with IPF received buloxibutid 100 mg twice daily for up to 36 weeks. Of the 52 enrolled patients, 28 completed the study. Buloxibutid led to stabilization and improvement in lung function, with a mean FVC change of + 216 mL and a median change of + 63 mL from baseline at week 36 compared with an external synthetic control dataset of over 10,000 patients with IPF showing an average FVC decline of − 115 mL. Buloxibutid was well tolerated, with no serious or fatal adverse events. The most common treatment-emergent events were mild and reversible hair loss (19%), rash, cough, and reflux (each around 8%). Following these results, the phase 2b ASPIRE trial [88] was launched to further evaluate buloxibutid 50 mg and 100 mg twice daily versus placebo over 52 weeks in approximately 270 patients with IPF. The study aims to confirm the effect of the compound on FVC and assess disease progression and safety in a larger population.

Conclusion

ILDs, of which IPF is the most common and lethal, continue to represent a major global health challenge due to their progressive nature, poor prognosis, and limited therapeutic options. Current antifibrotic therapies, pirfenidone and nintedanib, have a modest clinical outlook by slowing functional decline, but they cannot cease or improve disease trajectory and come with significant treatment side effects. Increasing understanding of the multifactorial pathogenesis of pulmonary fibrosis—encompassing epithelial injury, aberrant wound repair, immune dysregulation, and fibroblast activation—has driven the development of targeted therapies addressing diverse pathogenic pathways. Emerging agents include nerandomilast, inhaled treprostinil, admilparant, and buloxibutid amongst others. These new therapies demonstrate a shift towards precision-based, mechanism-specific drug development aimed at modulating inflammatory and fibrotic processes. Although several phase II and III trials have shown promising short-term improvements in lung function and disease stabilization, many have failed to demonstrate clinically significant results with satisfactory risk–benefit profiles. Ultimately, the development of novel agents to treat IPF and PPF is promising but requires continued translational research with representative patient selection in order to achieve the ultimate goal of improving outcomes for individuals affected by these devastating conditions.
