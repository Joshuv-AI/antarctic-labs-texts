Sulforaphane enhances locomotor recovery after spinal cord injury through antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and JAK/STAT-modulating mechanisms
Authors: Zainab Hameed Saeed, Widyan Gataa Weshel, Ali Hussein Lafta, Murtadha Azeez Ameen, Zainab Hannon Abbass, Akramov Akhtam, Shamshod Axmedov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal cord injury (SCI) leads to irreversible neurological deficits primarily through secondary injury mechanisms, including oxidative stress and glial dysfunction. Sulforaphane (SFN), a naturally occurring isothiocyanate, has shown antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects in various neurological models, but its impact on Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) signaling and microglial polarization in SCI remains unclear. Adult male Wistar rats were randomly assigned to Sham, SCI, or SCI + SFN groups (n = 10/group). SCI was induced by a moderate T10 contusion, and SFN (50 mg/kg, i.p.) was administered at 10 min, 72 h, and 7 days post-injury. Locomotor recovery was assessed using the Basso–Beattie–Bresnahan (BBB) scale. At day 14, spinal cord tissue was analyzed for oxidative stress markers, including reactive oxygen species (ROS), malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and reduced glutathione (GSH), as well as JAK2/STAT3 signaling and microglial polarization. SCI significantly increased ROS and MDA levels while reducing SOD activity and GSH content. Phosphorylation of JAK2 and STAT3, along with glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), was markedly elevated. SFN treatment restored antioxidant defenses, suppressed JAK2/STAT3 activation, partially recovered suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3), and reduced pro-inflammatory responses. Moreover, SFN shifted microglial polarization from a pro-inflammatory (M1) toward a reparative (M2) phenotype and significantly improved BBB locomotor scores compared with untreated SCI rats. SFN confers neuroprotection in SCI by reducing oxidative stress, modulating JAK/STAT signaling, rebalancing microglial polarization, and improving locomotor recovery. These findings highlight SFN as a promising candidate for therapeutic development in SCI.Keywords: Antioxidant defense, Inflammation, JAK/STAT signaling, Microglial polarization, Spinal cord injury

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a devastating neurological disorder that leads to long-term disability and places a substantial burden on patients and healthcare systems worldwide. While the primary mechanical insult causes immediate tissue disruption, the progression of neurological deficits is largely driven by a cascade of secondary injury mechanisms that unfold over days to weeks (Abroumand Gholami et al., 2023, 2025a; Mao et al., 2010, 2011; Benedict et al., 2012; Babaloo et al., 2025). These include excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, astrogliosis, and apoptotic cell death, which together exacerbate neuronal and glial damage, impair axonal regeneration, and hinder functional recovery (Guo et al., 2023; Yamauchi et al., 2006). Among these, oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling are particularly prominent drivers of secondary degeneration (Ageeva et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2023; Zu et al., 2014). Despite advances in surgical and rehabilitative care, there are currently no effective pharmacological therapies capable of halting or reversing these processes (Ageeva et al., 2024).

The Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) pathway is a central mediator of neuroinflammatory and glial responses following SCI. Activation of the JAK/STAT signaling cascade in astrocytes and microglia promotes transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, reinforces reactive gliosis, and amplifies neuroinflammatory signaling within the injured spinal cord. Sustained STAT3 activation has been associated with glial scar formation, chronic inflammatory loops, and impaired neuronal survival. Dysregulated JAK/STAT signaling in glial cells may further exacerbate neuronal damage and contribute to maladaptive plasticity and pain signaling following SCI (Hao et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2024). Phosphorylation of JAK2 and STAT3 promotes the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 and upregulates glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), thereby reinforcing astrocytic reactivity and glial scarring (Abroumand Gholami et al., 2025a; Ageeva et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2023; Zu et al., 2014). Sustained JAK/STAT activation contributes to chronic inflammation and poor regenerative outcomes, while the loss of negative regulators such as suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3) further amplifies this cascade (Guo et al., 2023; Yamauchi et al., 2006). In parallel, microglia and infiltrating macrophages exhibit functional plasticity between the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and the reparative M2 phenotype (David et al., 2015; Gensel and Zhang, 2015; Fu et al., 2022). SCI is marked by a predominance of M1 cells and suppression of M2 activity, creating a hostile inflammatory milieu. Therapeutic strategies that inhibit aberrant JAK/STAT signaling and restore the balance between M1 and M2 phenotypes hold promise for mitigating secondary injury and promoting functional recovery (Guo et al., 2023).

Sulforaphane (SFN) is a naturally occurring isothiocyanate derived from cruciferous vegetables such as broccoli that has attracted growing attention due to its pleiotropic biological activities (Schepici et al., 2020; Bessetti and Litwa, 2025). SFN is a potent activator of the Nrf2/ARE pathway, leading to transcriptional induction of antioxidant and detoxifying enzymes, and has also been shown to exert anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects in models of neurological disease (Schepici et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2020; Feng et al., 2017). In experimental models of traumatic brain injury, ischemic stroke, and neurodegeneration, SFN reduces oxidative stress, suppresses cytokine production, and improves neuronal survival (Dash et al., 2009; Li et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023). Importantly, studies in SCI models provide direct evidence of these effects: SFN administration decreased spinal cord edema through aquaporin-4 regulation (Mao et al., 2011), reduced MMP-9 and TNF-α expression and vascular permeability (Mao et al., 2010), and enhanced antioxidant gene expression while improving hindlimb locomotor recovery (Benedict et al., 2012). However, despite these encouraging findings, the ability of SFN to regulate JAK/STAT signaling and microglial polarization in the injured spinal cord has not been systematically investigated.

Therefore, the present study examined the therapeutic potential of SFN in a rat model of SCI. We specifically evaluated its effects on oxidative stress markers (ROS, MDA, SOD, GSH), JAK2/STAT3 signaling and related mediators (SOCS3, GFAP, TNF-α, IL-6), microglial/macrophage activation and polarization (M1 vs. M2 phenotypes), and locomotor recovery using the BBB test. By integrating biochemical, molecular, histological, and behavioral analyses, this study provides comprehensive evidence of SFN's neuroprotective effects and supports its development as a promising therapeutic candidate for SCI.

Materials and methods

Study design and animal care

This randomized, controlled, blinded in vivo study experiment was conducted in compliance with ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. Adult male Wistar rats were allocated to three parallel groups with equal sample sizes (n = 10/group). Thirty adult male Wistar rats (8–10 weeks old, 250–300 g) were obtained from National Center for Drug Control and Research in Nasiriyah. Health status was verified upon arrival; animals were group-housed (3–4 per cage) with environmental enrichment. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of University of Dhi Qar, Nasiriyah, Iraq (Approval No. H12REA191) and conformed to the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Rats were housed (22 ± 2 °C; 12:12 h light–dark; 50–60% humidity) with ad libitum access to food and water and acclimatized for 7 days before experiments. Environmental enrichment consisted of nesting materials, cardboard tubes, and wooden chew blocks, which were rotated weekly to encourage exploratory behavior and provide psychological stimulation.

Experimental groups

A total of 30 adult male Wistar rats were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 10 per group):

Rats underwent laminectomy without SCI (Sham group), rats received a moderate contusion injury at T10 (SCI group), and Rats underwent the same SCI procedure as the SCI group and received SFN (50 mg/kg) administered i.p. (SCI + SFN group). All procedures and analyses were performed by investigators blinded to group assignments.

SCI model

Under isoflurane anesthesia (4% induction, 1.5–2% maintenance in oxygen), a midline incision exposed vertebrae T9–T10. After laminectomy at T10, a moderate contusion injury was produced using an Infinite Horizon impactor (200 kdyn; dwell time 0.5 s). The maximum force generated during each impact was recorded for every animal by the Infinite Horizon device and was used to verify consistency of injury severity between the SCI groups. Sham rats underwent laminectomy only. Body temperature was maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C on a heating pad throughout. Muscles and skin were sutured in layers; animals recovered in warmed cages. All rats received lactated Ringer's (5 mL, s.c.), enrofloxacin (10 mg/kg, s.c., once daily for 5 days), and buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg, s.c., twice daily for 3 days). Bladders were manually expressed 2–3 times/day until reflexive voiding returned. Animals that did not exhibit immediate hindlimb paralysis (BBB ≤2 at day 1) were excluded a priori.

Drug preparation and administration

Sulforaphane (LKT Laboratories, Inc., St. Paul, MN; ≥98% purity) was dissolved in corn oil (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and administered intraperitoneally at 50 mg/kg per injection at 10 min, 72 h, and 168 h (Day 7) after SCI induction. Sham and SCI control groups received matched volumes of corn oil at the same time points. The selected dosing schedule and SFN administration protocol were determined based on the results of our pilot study, which included dose-response assessments and pharmacokinetic evaluations (Figs. S1–3 of the supplementary file 1). The pilot study investigated the pharmacodynamic effects of different SFN doses (25, 50, and 75 mg/kg) and the optimal time points for administration (10 min post-injury, Day 3, and Day 7), ensuring that SFN treatment maximized neuroprotection in SCI models. Additionally, prior studies by Benedict et al. (2012) and Hong et al. (2018) provided supporting evidence for the chosen dosage and administration schedule, demonstrating that SFN at 50 mg/kg effectively provides neuroprotection in SCI models (Benedict et al., 2012; Hong et al., 2018).

Behavioral assessment

Locomotor recovery was evaluated using the Basso, Beattie, and Bresnahan (BBB) open-field locomotion score at baseline and on post-injury days 1, 7, and 14. Each rat was observed for 4 min in a circular open field; scores from the two observers were averaged. The circular open field used for locomotor assessment had a diameter of 100 cm, with a smooth, non-reflective floor made of acrylic. The walls were 50 cm high and constructed of transparent acrylic to allow unobstructed observation of the animals. (Basso et al., 1995).

Western blot analysis

At the end of the experimental period, animals were deeply anesthetized with ketamine (80 mg/kg, i.p.) and xylazine (10 mg/kg, i.p.) until complete loss of reflexes was confirmed. Euthanasia was performed using isoflurane gas in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. At 14 days post-injury, a 10-mm segment of spinal cord tissue centered on the lesion epicenter (±5 mm) was rapidly dissected on ice, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. Samples were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Sigma-Aldrich). Homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (30–50 μg per lane) were denatured in Laemmli sample buffer, separated by 8–12% SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). After blocking for 1 h at room temperature in 5% non-fat milk (for total proteins and cytokines) or 5% BSA in TBST (for phospho-proteins, when probed), membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: JAK2 (1:1000, CST), p-JAK2 (Tyr1007/1008, 1:1000, CST), STAT3 (1:1000, Proteintech), p-STAT3 (Tyr705, 1:1000, CST), SOCS3 (1:500, Proteintech), GFAP (1:1000, Abcam), TNF-α (1:1000, Abcam), IL-6 (1:1000, Abcam), and GAPDH (1:5000, CST) as a loading control. After three washes in TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit, 1:5000, Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; Thermo Fisher) and imaged with a ChemiDoc digital imaging system (Bio-Rad). Band intensities were quantified by densitometry using ImageJ software (NIH). For phosphorylation analysis, p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 levels were normalized to their respective total proteins (p-JAK2/JAK2, p-STAT3/STAT3). For other markers (SOCS3, GFAP, TNF-α, IL-6), expression was normalized to GAPDH. Results are expressed as fold-change relative to the Sham group. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Oxidative stress assays

To evaluate oxidative stress, a portion of the spinal cord segment encompassing the lesion epicenter (±5 mm) was homogenized in ice-cold PBS (pH 7.4) and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, after which the supernatant was collected for biochemical assays. Total protein concentration was determined by the BCA method to normalize all measurements. Intracellular ROS levels were assessed with the DCFH-DA assay (485/535 nm) and expressed as RFU/mg protein. Lipid peroxidation was quantified by the TBARS method, with absorbance at 532 nm and values expressed as nmol/mg protein. SOD activity was measured using a commercial kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, Cat. No. A001-3) and expressed as U/mg protein. GSH was determined with the Cayman kit (Cat. No. 703002) and expressed as nmol/mg protein.

Immunofluorescence staining

Spinal cord segments encompassing the lesion epicenter were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, and sectioned at 20-μm thickness. For immunofluorescence, tissue sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min, then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma) at room temperature for 1 h. Sections were subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD11b (1:100, Abcam), CD86 (M1 marker, 1:200, Abcam), and CD206 (M2 marker, 1:200, Abcam). After washing with PBS, appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor dyes (1:400, Abcam) were applied for 1.5 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI using an antifade mounting medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were acquired using a Zeiss fluorescence microscope under identical exposure settings. Quantitative analysis was performed by selecting four standardized regions per section, and the proportion of CD11b + CD86+ (M1) or CD11b + CD206+ (M2) cells relative to total CD11b + cells was calculated using ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

Data distribution was assessed (Shapiro–Wilk). BBB scores were analyzed with two-way repeated-measures ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post hoc tests. Rest of data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc. Results are presented as mean ± SD; exact p-values are reported. α = 0.05. Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism v 9.0.

Results

Sulforaphane attenuates oxidative stress and modulates JAK/STAT signaling and inflammatory mediators after SCI

At 14 days post-injury, SCI induced pronounced oxidative stress in spinal cord tissue (Fig. 1). One-way ANOVA revealed a significant treatment effect on ROS levels (F(2,6) = 89.09, p < 0.0001). SCI rats exhibited markedly elevated ROS compared with Sham (976.7 ± 70.3 vs. 484.0 ± 24.0 RFU/mg protein, p < 0.0001). SFN significantly reduced ROS relative to SCI (752.0 ± 31.8, p < 0.01), although levels remained higher than Sham (p < 0.01; Fig. 1A). Similarly, MDA levels differed significantly among groups (F(2,6) = 70.07, p < 0.0001). SCI significantly increased MDA compared with Sham (87.8 ± 6.2 vs. 31.4 ± 6.3 nmol/mg protein, p < 0.0001). SFN treatment markedly reduced MDA levels relative to SCI (36.2 ± 7.0, p < 0.001), restoring values to levels not significantly different from Sham (Fig. 1B).

Western blot analysis of JAK2/STAT3 signaling and related markers in spinal cord tissue at 14 days post-injury. Antioxidant defenses were significantly affected by treatment. SOD activity showed a significant group effect (F(2,6) = 33.16, p = 0.0006). SCI reduced SOD activity compared with Sham (63.0 ± 9.0 vs. 122.7 ± 9.5 U/mg protein, p < 0.001). SFN significantly restored SOD relative to SCI (119.7 ± 7.9, p < 0.01), reaching levels comparable to Sham (Fig. 1C). Likewise, GSH levels demonstrated a strong treatment effect (F(2,6) = 155.60, p < 0.0001). SCI markedly depleted GSH compared with Sham (4.6 ± 0.5 vs. 11.4 ± 0.6 nmol/mg protein, p < 0.0001). SFN significantly increased GSH relative to SCI (11.9 ± 0.6, p < 0.0001), restoring levels to values not significantly different from Sham (Fig. 1D).

Western blotting was used to assess activation of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway, astrocytic response, and inflammatory mediators (Fig. 1E). One-way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of treatment on p-JAK2/JAK2 ratios (F(2,6) = 55.37, p = 0.0001). Post hoc analysis showed that SCI significantly increased p-JAK2/JAK2 compared with Sham (p < 0.001), whereas SFN treatment significantly reduced phosphorylation relative to SCI (p < 0.001), restoring levels to values not significantly different from Sham (Fig. 1F). In contrast, total JAK2 expression did not differ among groups (F(2,6) = 0.13, p = 0.8798; Fig. 1G). Similarly, a significant treatment effect was detected for p-STAT3/STAT3 ratios (F(2,6) = 15.98, p = 0.0039). SCI significantly increased STAT3 phosphorylation compared with Sham (p < 0.01), while SFN significantly reduced p-STAT3 levels relative to SCI (p < 0.05), with values comparable to Sham controls (Fig. 1H). No significant differences were observed in total STAT3 expression (F(2,6) = 0.15, p = 0.8636; Fig. 1I).

GFAP expression differed significantly among groups (F(2,6) = 20.52, p = 0.0021). SCI increased GFAP compared with Sham (p < 0.01). Although SFN reduced GFAP relative to SCI, this decrease did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05; Fig. 1J). SOCS3 expression also showed a significant treatment effect (F(2,6) = 53.96, p = 0.0001). SCI reduced SOCS3 compared with Sham (p = 0.0001). SFN partially restored SOCS3 expression relative to SCI (p = 0.0037), although levels remained lower than Sham (p = 0.0066; Fig. 1K). For inflammatory mediators, IL-6 expression was significantly affected by treatment (F(2,6) = 58.88, p = 0.0001). SCI markedly increased IL-6 compared with Sham (p < 0.0001), and SFN significantly reduced IL-6 relative to SCI (p < 0.001), though levels remained elevated versus Sham (p < 0.01; Fig. 1L). Likewise, TNF-α expression showed a strong treatment effect (F(2,6) = 95.78, p < 0.0001). SCI significantly increased TNF-α compared with Sham (p < 0.0001), whereas SFN significantly attenuated TNF-α expression relative to SCI (p < 0.001), although levels remained higher than Sham (p < 0.05; Fig. 1M). Sulforaphane modulates microglial/macrophage activation and polarization and improves locomotor recovery

Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated a robust activation of microglia/macrophages following SCI (Fig. 2A). Quantification of CD11b + cell density revealed a significant effect of treatment (one-way ANOVA: F(2,15) = 125.7, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2B), with SCI animals showing a marked increase compared with Sham controls (p < 0.0001). SFN treatment significantly reduced CD11b + cell density compared with untreated SCI animals (p < 0.0001), although levels remained elevated relative to Sham (p < 0.0001). Analysis of microglial/macrophage polarization showed a pronounced shift toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype (CD11b + CD86+) after SCI. One-way ANOVA revealed a significant treatment effect (F(2,15) = 187.6, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2C), with SFN administration significantly attenuating M1 polarization compared with the SCI group (p < 0.0001), without complete normalization to Sham levels. Conversely, the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype (CD11b + CD206+) was significantly reduced following SCI. A significant treatment effect was detected by one-way ANOVA (F(2,15) = 115.0, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2D), with SFN treatment partially restoring M2 polarization compared with SCI animals (p < 0.0001), although values remained significantly lower than Sham (p < 0.0001).

Maximum impact force values recorded during SCI induction were compared between the SCI and SCI + SFN groups to confirm consistency of injury induction. No significant difference was observed between groups, indicating comparable injury severity prior to treatment administration (Fig. 2E). Locomotor function was evaluated using the BBB open-field test at baseline and on days 1, 7, and 14 after SCI (Fig. 2F). Two-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of group (F(3,108) = 619.8, p < 0.0001) and time (F(2,108) = 1075, p < 0.0001), as well as a significant group × time interaction (F(6,108) = 162.5, p < 0.0001), indicating differential locomotor recovery trajectories among groups over time. At baseline, all groups exhibited normal motor performance with scores near 21 and no significant differences among groups (p > 0.05). Following SCI induction, both the SCI and SCI + SFN groups showed a dramatic decline in locomotor ability on day 1 (p < 0.0001 vs. Sham). No significant difference was observed between SCI and SCI + SFN at this time point (p > 0.05). By day 7, SCI rats showed only limited spontaneous recovery, whereas SCI + SFN rats exhibited significantly higher scores compared with SCI (p < 0.0001), though both remained below Sham values (p < 0.0001). Functional improvements in the SCI + SFN group became even more pronounced by day 14, with mean scores around 14 + 2.31 compared with 7 + 2.44 in SCI rats (p < 0.0001). Despite this enhancement, locomotor function in SCI + SFN animals did not fully reach Sham levels (p < 0.0001).

Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrated that SFN confers robust neuroprotection in a rat model of SCI by targeting multiple secondary injury mechanisms. SCI triggered excessive oxidative stress, activation of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway, astrocytic reactivity, increased pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and a marked shift of microglia/macrophages toward the M1 phenotype, which together contributed to impaired locomotor recovery. Treatment with SFN significantly attenuated these pathological processes, as evidenced by reduced ROS and MDA levels, restoration of antioxidant defenses (SOD and GSH), suppression of JAK2/STAT3 phosphorylation, upregulation of SOCS3, downregulation of GFAP, IL-6, and TNF-α, and a rebalancing of microglial polarization from M1 to M2. These molecular and cellular effects were functionally relevant, since SFN-treated rats exhibited markedly improved BBB locomotor scores compared with untreated SCI animals.

Oxidative stress is widely recognized as a pivotal driver of secondary injury after SCI, where mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, and depletion of endogenous antioxidants amplify cellular vulnerability. This persistent redox imbalance not only accelerates neuronal and glial death but also worsens neuroinflammation and impairs repair mechanisms (David et al., 2015; Gensel and Zhang, 2015; Gao et al., 2025; Rad et al., 2025; Abroumand Gholami et al., 2025b). SFN has consistently been shown to fulfill these criteria across diverse models of neurotoxicity. For example, Feng et al. (2017) demonstrated that SFN prevented neurotoxicity in rats by reinforcing Nrf2/ARE signaling, thereby maintaining GSH and antioxidant enzyme activity while limiting ROS and MDA accumulation (Feng et al., 2017). Similarly, in diabetic neuropathy, SFN improved motor coordination and attenuated nerve oxidative and inflammatory markers, restoring SOD and GSH levels and reducing IL-6 and TNF-α (Moustafa et al., 2018). In traumatic brain injury models, SFN reduced oxidative damage and neuronal apoptosis through activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 axis, with parallel decreases in MDA and increases in SOD and GPx activity (Wu et al., 2020). Beyond acute trauma, reviews and experimental data further support SFN's broad neuroprotective properties in neurodegenerative disease models (Schepici et al., 2020; Guerrero-Beltrán et al., 2012). Importantly, studies directly addressing SCI confirm the relevance of these mechanisms in spinal cord pathology. Benedict et al. (2012) reported that systemic SFN treatment enhanced antioxidant gene expression, reduced inflammatory cytokine production, and promoted functional recovery in a rat contusion model (Benedict et al., 2012). These findings resonate with our data, where SFN not only reduced ROS and lipid peroxidation but also restored antioxidant defenses, suggesting that its neuroprotection relies on both free radical scavenging and transcriptional activation of endogenous detoxification systems.

Sustained phosphorylation of the JAK2→STAT3 axis is a key amplifier of post-traumatic neuroinflammation and astroglial remodeling in SCI. Persistent STAT3 activation upregulates GFAP and reinforces cytokine loops (IL-6, TNF-α), thereby consolidating a pro-inflammatory microenvironment that impedes repair (Abroumand Gholami et al., 2025a; Guo et al., 2023; Yamauchi et al., 2006; Malekpour et al., 2023; Asadi‐Pooya et al., 2023). Downregulation of the negative regulator SOCS3 further prolongs STAT3 activity and enhances astrogliosis (Ageeva et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2023; Zu et al., 2014). In line with this paradigm, our data indicate that SFN dampens JAK/STAT signaling tone (reduced p-JAK2 and p-STAT3), accompanied by lower pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and partial restoration of SOCS3. While the present study focused on phosphorylation status and key inflammatory outputs of the JAK2/STAT3 axis, receptor-level components and broader transcriptional targets were not directly examined. Future investigations incorporating upstream cytokine receptor dynamics and genome-wide STAT3 target profiling would further delineate the mechanistic landscape of SFN-mediated pathway modulation. Such modulation is consistent with evidence across diverse models. In glioblastoma cells, SFN suppressed both constitutive and IL-6–induced STAT3 and JAK2/Src phosphorylation in a dose- and time-dependent manner; inhibition of ROS reversed these effects, highlighting the tight link between oxidative stress and STAT3 signaling (Miao et al., 2017). In dendritic cells, SFN diminished STAT3 phosphorylation, thereby promoting T-cell activation (Wang et al., 2022). Likewise, in a metabolic inflammation model, SFN inhibited the JAK2/STAT3/SOCS3 axis and reduced systemic inflammatory markers (Ashmawy et al., 2022). Moreover, in tendinopathy, SFN promoted a phenotypic shift of macrophages from M1 to M2 via inhibition of JAK1/STAT1, aligning with our observation of reduced M1 and increased M2 polarization after SCI (Zhang et al., 2025). These data strengthen the notion that SFN mitigates CNS inflammation by downregulating hyperactive JAK/STAT signaling, limiting astrocytic reactivity, and suppressing cytokine overproduction, thereby generating a more permissive microenvironment for repair.

An important nuance from the literature is that SFN's effect on JAK/STAT signaling is highly context-dependent. For example, in skeletal muscle regeneration, SFN was shown to enhance JAK/STAT activity: Li et al. (2025) demonstrated that SFN upregulated the transcription of Prl2C2 through STAT3 binding to its promoter, thereby promoting myoblast proliferation and tissue repair; this effect was blocked by a JAK2 inhibitor (Li et al., 2025). At first glance, these findings appear to contrast with our SCI results, where SFN reduced JAK/STAT activation. However, this highlights that SFN is not a simple STAT inhibitor but rather a dynamic regulator: in pathological contexts where the IL-6/gp130-JAK2-STAT3 axis is excessively activated, SFN attenuates phosphorylation via reduction of ROS and pro-inflammatory cytokines and partial restoration of SOCS3. Conversely, in regenerative contexts, SFN may promote JAK/STAT activity to facilitate repair and cell proliferation. This duality underscores the normalizing rather than silencing effect of SFN on JAK/STAT signaling, a mechanism consistent with its pleiotropic, redox-sensitive mode of action (Miao et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2022; Ashmawy et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025).

Our findings show that SFN attenuates the M1-dominant response after SCI while enhancing M2 phenotypes at the lesion site, indicating that immune modulation is a central mechanism of its neuroprotective action. Evidence from multiple models supports this conclusion. In vitro, SFN diminished M1 marker expression (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, iNOS, NO, ROS) in LPS-activated macrophages by interfering with PKM2 nuclear signaling, thereby reducing HIF-1α and STAT3 phosphorylation while preserving metabolic activity (Bahiraii et al., 2022). Similarly, in inflammatory bowel disease, SFN acted as an Nrf2 agonist, downregulating IL-12, IL-6, and TNF-α while enhancing M2 polarization; inhibition of Nrf2 abrogated these effects (Yi et al., 2024). In CNS models, SFN reduced Aβ-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation and inhibited M1 polarization via ROS suppression (Yang et al., 2023). Zhang J. et al. (2023) further showed that SFN reversed M1 polarization and neuroinflammation by downregulating MAPK/NF-κB signaling, while protecting neural stem cells from necroptotic injury (Zhang et al., 2023).

SFN-treated rats in our study showed significantly improved BBB locomotor scores from day 7 onward, suggesting that its molecular and cellular effects translate into meaningful behavioral recovery. These results are in agreement with previous SCI studies showing that SFN confers functional recovery through multi-level neuroprotection. Mao et al. (2011) demonstrated that SFN reduced spinal cord edema by upregulating aquaporin-4 (AQP4), improving tissue homeostasis after SCI (Mao et al., 2011). In a complementary study, Mao et al. (2010) showed that SFN administration significantly decreased TNF-α and MMP-9 expression as well as vascular permeability, thereby mitigating early inflammatory damage (Mao et al., 2010). Furthermore, Benedict et al. (2012) reported that SFN treatment not only reduced oxidative stress and inflammatory mediators but also improved hindlimb locomotor function and enhanced serotonergic axonal growth distal to the lesion (Benedict et al., 2012). One notable difference between our study and Benedict et al. (2024) is the administration of an additional SFN dose on Day 7 in our study. While the treatment paradigms were otherwise similar, this additional dose likely contributed to accelerated recovery observed in our study. The extra dose may have provided prolonged neuroprotection, thereby enhancing functional recovery more rapidly during the first two weeks after SCI. This suggests that the timing and frequency of SFN dosing may play an important role in its neuroprotective effects, especially in the early post-injury phase. Although spontaneous recovery in untreated animals is typically slow within the first week after SCI, treated animals in our study showed early weight-supported stepping by Day 7 (BBB 9.5 ± 2.12), significantly faster than control animals (BBB 3.7 ± 1.16). This early recovery is likely due to SFN's effects in reducing oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, which are known to delay recovery in untreated animals. Previous studies with similar models, such as the T9 clip-compression and NYU impactor models (Tail et al., 2024; Ikefuama et al., 2022; Griffin et al., 2023), report minimal recovery in control animals by Day 7, which aligns with our findings for untreated rats. However, the functional separation seen at Day 7 between the SCI + SFN and SCI control groups is likely due to the treatment's effect rather than an unusually rapid recovery in controls. These observations suggest that SFN significantly accelerates recovery through its neuroprotective effects, consistent with other early-intervention strategies reported in the literature.

While the present study demonstrates significant modulation of oxidative stress, JAK/STAT signaling, and microglial polarization at 14 days post-injury, these findings represent a single subacute time point. SCI is characterized by dynamic temporal changes in redox balance, cytokine signaling, and glial activation, particularly within the first week after injury. Therefore, the current data do not fully resolve the precise onset and temporal progression of SFN-mediated pathway modulation. Although pilot pharmacodynamic analyses confirmed early activation of Nrf2-responsive genes following acute and repeated dosing, future studies incorporating detailed multi-time-point molecular assessments will be required to delineate the kinetics of JAK/STAT inhibition and immune polarization more precisely. By attenuating cytokine-driven inflammatory cascades and oxidative stress, sulforaphane may indirectly bias microglial/macrophage activation toward anti-inflammatory phenotypic tendencies, thereby limiting secondary neuroinflammation after SCI. Not all proposed inflammatory or infectious triggers demonstrate a measurable association with neurodegenerative disease risk or severity, underscoring the complexity of disease pathogenesis (Khatir et al., 2025; Moarrefzadeh et al., 2025).

Conclusion

This study provides compelling evidence that SFN mitigates secondary injury after SCI by attenuating oxidative stress, inhibiting JAK/STAT signaling, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines, and shifting microglial polarization toward a reparative M2 phenotype. These molecular and cellular effects translated into significant improvements in locomotor recovery, underscoring the therapeutic potential of SFN for spinal cord trauma. While further investigations are necessary to validate long-term efficacy and optimize translational strategies, our findings support the continued development of SFN as a multi-target therapeutic approach for SCI and related neuroinflammatory conditions.
