Postprenylation processing of phosphodiesterase is critical for robust cone photoreceptor response
Authors: Saravanan Kolandaivelu, Thamaraiselvi Saravanan, Visvanathan Ramamurthy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn mammalian photoreceptors, several phototransduction proteins undergo prenylation, a posttranslational lipid modification. These prenylated proteins are then processed by RAS-converting enzyme 1 (RCE1), which cleaves the final three amino acids. RCE1-mediated endoproteolysis is critical for maintaining rod photoreceptor structure and function; its early loss in the retina causes rapid degeneration and disrupts the trafficking of rod phosphodiesterase 6 (PDE6). However, the early and rapid degeneration has limited our understanding of RCE1’s role in cones. To address this challenge, we developed a cone-specific Rce1 knockout model. Unlike in the retina-wide knockout, cone survival was unaffected. Nonetheless, electroretinography (ERG) revealed a significant reduction in cone function. Double-flash and flicker ERG further demonstrated impaired recovery in cones lacking RCE1. Despite unchanged transcript levels, cone PDE6 protein was reduced by 90%; the residual protein, though correctly assembled, failed to associate with membranes and instead accumulated in the inner segment. In contrast, the localization and abundance of other prenylated proteins, including rhodopsin kinase and transducin, were unaffected. These findings demonstrate that RCE1-dependent postprenylation processing (PPP) is specifically required for maintaining PDE6 membrane association and levels in cones. Introduction

Photoreceptor cells are highly specialized ciliated neurons with elaborate outer segments (OS) that house proteins essential for phototransduction. A common feature of phototransduction proteins is their ability to associate with OS membranes. The membrane association of phototransduction proteins is critical for outer segment development and is thought to enhance signaling fidelity1–3. In addition, the membrane association of proteins enables the transport of proteins from their site of synthesis in the inner segment (IS) to their site of function in the OS4,5.

Prenylation, a type of lipid modification involving the addition of farnesyl or geranylgeranyl groups, enhances membrane affinity of target proteins6. Following prenylation, the final three amino acids are removed by the ER-resident protease Ras-converting enzyme 1 (RCE)7. The newly exposed isoprenylcysteine is subsequently methylated by isoprenylcysteine carboxyl methyltransferase (ICMT)7. In rod photoreceptors, several key phototransduction proteins are prenylated: the γ subunit of transducin is farnesylated8,9; the catalytic subunits of rod phosphodiesterase-6 (PDE6) are modified by farnesyl and geranylgeranyl groups10; and rhodopsin kinase (GRK1)11,12, which terminates opsin signaling, is also farnesylated13.

A retina-specific Rce1 knockout model demonstrated that RCE1-mediated endoproteolysis is essential for rod photoreceptor survival and function. In these mice, assembled rod PDE6 levels remained unchanged, but the protein was mislocalized to the IS, suggesting that the unprocessed C-terminus prevents proper trafficking to the OS14. In contrast, knockout of Icmt, the enzyme responsible for the final methylation step, did not affect PDE6 trafficking, indicating that the RCE1-specific removal of the C-terminal three amino acid residues (-AAX) is the key regulatory step in PDE6 trafficking to the OS13,15. However, studying the importance of RCE1 in cone photoreceptors proved challenging due to the early and rapid degeneration of rod photoreceptors in retina-wide knockouts, which likely induces secondary cone loss due to the bystander effect16.

Cones differ from rods in the structure and lipid modification of phototransduction proteins. Cone PDE6 catalytic core is homodimeric and is considered geranylgeranylated17. Like rods, the γ subunit of transducin, the G-protein in cones, is modified by the farnesyl group18. In murine cone photoreceptors, GRK1 is farnesylated, while in fish and primates, geranylgeranylated GRK7 is expressed19. However, the importance of postprenylation processing (PPP) of these prenylated proteins in cone photoreceptors remains largely unexplored.

We generated a cone-specific Rce1 knockout mouse model to investigate the importance of RCE1-mediated endoproteolysis in cone photoreceptors. We show that, in the absence of RCE1, the levels of cone PDE6 are reduced by 90% despite normal transcript levels and protein assembly. The residual protein fails to associate with membranes and accumulates in the IS. Other prenylated proteins, including GRK1 and cone transducin, remain unaffected. These findings reveal a unique requirement for RCE1 in regulating PDE6 levels and function in cones, highlighting a previously unrecognized mechanism essential for cone photoreceptor homeostasis.

Results

Conditional deletion of Rce1 in cone photoreceptor cells

To selectively eliminate Rce1 in cone photoreceptors, we crossed mice carrying a floxed Rce1 allele (Rce1 fl/fl) with Cone-Cre transgenic mice20,21, which express Cre recombinase under the control of human red/green pigment (HRGP) gene promoter. Cre expression in this line begins around postnatal day 10 (P10) and reaches robust expression by P3021. Through successive rounds of breeding, we generated mice with cone-specific deletion of Rce1 (Cone-Cre; Rce1fl/fl) and littermate controls (Cone-Cre; Rce1fl/wt; Rce1fl/fl and Rce1fl/+) (Fig. 1A). For simplicity, throughout this manuscript, control mice are referred to as “Rce1+/+” and experimental Cone-Cre; Rce1fl/fl mice as “Rce1−/−“.

The sections were obtained from the areas of the retina as indicated in the schematic adjacent to the images. Scale bar = 10 µM; n = 3 mice per group.

RCE1-mediated endoproteolysis is not required for cone survival

To assess the role of RCE1 in cone photoreceptors, we examined retinal morphology and structure at postnatal day 35 (P35) and P100 in cone-specific Rce1 knockout mice (Rce1−/−). Throughout this study, no differences were observed among the various littermate controls generated, including (1) Cone-Cre; Rce1 fl/+, (2) Rce1 fl/fl, and (3) Rce1 fl/+. Therefore, these genotypes were grouped and referred to as Rce1+/+ throughout this study.

Immunocytochemistry (ICC) followed by confocal microscopy of retinal sections from Rce1+/+ and Rce1−/− mice revealed no significant morphological differences at P35 (Fig. 1). Cone numbers and localization of cone markers, including the α-subunit of cone transducin (Gα2) and the cone outer segment marker peanut agglutinin (PNA), were comparable between mutants and controls. Cone survival was further assessed at P100 (Fig. 1), and normal cone numbers and proper protein localization were observed. Additionally, whole-mount retinas stained with cone-specific markers, cone arrestin (mCAR) and cone transducin (Gα2), confirmed normal cone distribution and protein localization in Rce1−/− retinas across all four quadrants (Supplementary Fig. 1).

To investigate potential ultrastructural defects in cone photoreceptor outer segment (OS), we performed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) at P120. While cone outer segments were present, no gross structural defects were observed, the disc membranes appeared disorganized and contained vesicular structures (Supplementary Fig. 2). These findings indicate that RCE1-mediated postprenylation processing is not essential for cone development, survival, but may influence cone OS disc architecture.

RCE1-mediated proteolysis is essential for the transport of cone PDE6 to the outer segment

To assess the role of RCE1-mediated proteolysis in the localization of prenylated proteins, we performed immunocytochemistry (ICC) on Rce1−/− and Rce1+/+ retinas. We evaluated the localization of cone PDE6α′, cone transducin γ′ (Gγ8), and GRK1. In littermate control retinas (Rce1+/+), cone PDE6α′ localized to the cone outer segment (COS), similar to GRK1 (Fig. 2A, top panel). In contrast, in Rce1−/− retinas, cone PDE6α′ was largely mislocalized to the cone inner segment (IS), with only a small residual amount detected in the COS (Fig. 2A, bottom panel). However, the localization of other prenylated proteins, such as GRK1 and Gγ8, remained unchanged in Rce1−/− cones compared to controls (Fig. 2B, C). Since the deletion of RetGC1, an integral membrane protein, has been shown to disrupt cone PDE6 localization22, we also examined RetGC1 localization in Rce1−/− cones. RetGC1 distribution appeared unaffected and was similar to GRK1, localizing properly to the COS (Fig. 2B). Additionally, the localization of other cone-specific proteins, including cone arrestin (mCAR), S-opsin, and M-opsin, was not altered in the absence of RCE1 (Supplementary Fig. 3). Together, these results demonstrate that RCE1-mediated proteolysis is specifically required for the proper localization of cone PDE6 to the outer segment. In contrast, the trafficking of other prenylated cone proteins, such as GRK1 and Gγ8, is unaffected, revealing a unique and essential role for RCE1 in cone PDE6 localization.

The levels of cone PDE6 are severely affected in the absence of RCE1

Building on our findings that RCE1 is specifically required for the proper localization of cone PDE6, we next examined whether RCE1 also influences its protein levels. Despite normal transcript levels, cone PDE6 protein was reduced by approximately 90% in Rce1−/− retinas compared to littermate controls (Fig. 3A-C). In contrast, the levels of cone transducin (Gα2) and the integral membrane protein RetGC1 were unchanged (Fig. 3B, C). As expected, rod PDE6 and rod transducin (Gα1) levels were also unaffected at both the transcript and protein level (Fig. 3B, C). These results indicate that postprenylation processing (PPP) via RCE1 is critical for maintaining normal levels of cone PDE6, but not for other prenylated or transmembrane proteins in cone photoreceptors.

Postprenylation processing (PPP) is essential for membrane association of PDE6

To determine whether PPP is required for membrane association of cone PDE6, we first assessed whether its reduced levels in Rce1−/− cones was due to defective assembly. We used the ROS1 monoclonal antibody to immunoprecipitate (IP) assembled PDE6 complexes. ROS1 is a monoclonal antibody, which recognizes assembled rod and cone PDE6 complexes containing both catalytic and inhibitory subunits23. Prior studies have shown that ROS1 fails to detect PDE6 in retinas lacking either subunit or in models such as Aipl1−/−, where rod and cone PDE6 fail to assemble24–26.

Using ROS1 antibody for IP, we found that cone PDE6 was efficiently pulled down in Rce1+/+ control retinas, indicating normal complex formation. In Rce1−/− retinas, where cone PDE6 is destabilized, we increased the starting material 2.5-fold for IP and observed comparable levels of assembled cone PDE6 relative to littermate controls. These findings suggest that PPP is not essential for cone PDE6 complex formation (Fig. 4A, eluted fraction, E). As expected, the rod PDE6 αβ assembly was also unaffected (Fig. 4A, eluted fraction, E).

Next, we examined whether PPP influences the membrane anchoring of cone PDE6. Isotonic extraction followed by fractionation of retinal extracts from P100 Rce1−/− and Rce1+/+ mice revealed that approximately 60% of cone PDE6 was associated with the membrane fraction. In contrast, less than 25% of cone PDE6 in Rce1−/− retinas was found in the membrane fraction, indicating a defect in membrane association (Fig. 4B and C). In contrast, the membrane association of rod PDE6, rod transducin (Gα1), cone transducin (Gα2), and the integral membrane protein RetGC1 remained unaffected (Fig. 4B). Protein band intensities were quantified using the LI-COR Odyssey infrared imager and normalized to littermate controls (Fig. 4C).

These results demonstrate that although cone PDE6 assembles normally without PPP, it fails to associate with membranes efficiently, supporting a model in which RCE1-mediated postprenylation processing is essential for proper membrane anchoring and maintenance of normal levels of cone PDE6.

RCE1 deficiency in cones results in reduced cone photoreceptor function with preserved rod function

Phosphodiesterase 6 (PDE6) is a key effector enzyme in the phototransduction cascade, and defects in PDE6 result in severely diminished or absent light-evoked responses27. Given the marked reduction and impaired membrane association of cone PDE6 in the absence of postprenylation processing (PPP), we performed full-field electroretinography (ERG) to assess cone photopic (light-adapted) responses. The a-wave of the ERG is dominated by photoreceptor responses, while the b-wave is indicative of responses from downstream neurons28. Under light-adapted conditions (30 cd·m⁻²), Rce1−/− mice exhibited a ~ 80% reduction in cone-mediated a-wave amplitude compared to Rce1+/+ littermates (Fig. 5A, B; magnified inset highlights a-wave difference). In contrast, b-wave amplitudes were largely unaffected (Fig. 5A, B), suggesting intact inner retinal signaling. As expected, rod-mediated scotopic ERG responses were normal in Rce1−/− mice (Fig. 5D).

Light-adapted flicker ERG responses, another measure of cone function, were also significantly impaired in Rce1−/− mice, with detectable responses limited to lower frequencies. In contrast, control mice maintained responses up to 30 Hz (Fig. 5C). Also, recovery following paired-flash stimulation revealed severely impaired cone recovery kinetics in Rce1−/− mice compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Together, these results demonstrate that the reduction in cone-mediated photoresponses correlates with decreased cone PDE6 levels in the outer segment and confirm that PPP is essential for supporting normal cone phototransduction and recovery dynamics.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the role of RCE1-mediated postprenylation processing (PPP) in cone photoreceptor cells using a conditional knockout mouse model. Our results demonstrate that while RCE1 is not required for cone survival or development, it is essential for proper cone photoreceptor function. Specifically, the loss of RCE1 in cones leads to a large reduction (~ 90%) in cone PDE6 protein levels despite normal transcript expression and intact photoreceptor morphology.

The reduction in cone PDE6 was accompanied by impaired light-evoked cone responses, including a significant decrease in a-wave amplitude under photopic conditions, reduced flicker response, and defective cone recovery. These findings indicate that PPP is indispensable for functional cone phototransduction, and that cone dysfunction correlates with the loss of cone PDE6 in the outer segment.

Immunolocalization and biochemical analyses revealed that cone PDE6 is mislocalized to the inner segment in Rce1−/− retinas. Despite proper assembly, as confirmed by ROS1 immunoprecipitation, cone PDE6 fails to associate with the membrane efficiently without PPP. Fractionation studies confirmed a significant shift of cone PDE6 from the membrane-bound to the soluble fraction. In contrast, other phototransduction proteins, including cone transducin (Gα2), GRK1, and RetGC1, were unaffected regarding localization and stability, highlighting a specific dependence of cone PDE6 on PPP.

The selective requirement for PPP in cone PDE6 processing is striking. We speculate that the absence of RCE1-mediated cleavage disrupts downstream methylation, thereby impairing the ability of PDE6 to interact with trafficking chaperones such as PrBP/δ. Support for this model comes from study showing that the interaction of PrBP/δ occurs only in methylated and processed prenylated C-terminus of PDE6 catalytic subunits29. In addition, PrBP/δ facilitates the transport of prenylated proteins to the outer segment and that loss of PrBP/δ selectively destabilizes cone PDE630,31. In our model, the accumulation of cone PDE6 in the inner segment and its loss from the membrane fraction suggest a failure in this trafficking step, ultimately leading to rapid protein degradation.

A related question is why the trafficking of other prenylated proteins, such as transducin and rhodopsin kinase (GRK1), appears unaffected by the absence of postprenylation processing. We speculate that dual lipidation of transducin, myristoylation of the α-subunit and farnesylation of the γ-subunit, is likely sufficient to mediate its trafficking to the outer segment (OS)32. Support for this idea comes from studies using GFP modified with a single lipid group, such as myristoylation, which promotes membrane association and facilitates the trafficking of lipid-modified GFP to the OS33. Similarly, GRK1 may reach the outer segment through its interaction with the transmembrane protein opsin34.

Our findings also demonstrate that cone function is severely impaired, as evidenced by the loss of flicker ERG responses. Previous studies have suggested that the prenylation and/or postprenylation processing of GRK1 influences its interaction with opsin, reducing its phosphorylation efficiency by up to fourfold12. In addition, loss of GRK1 in mouse cones is known to impair the recovery of cone ERG response35. It is, therefore, possible that unprocessed GRK1 in the absence of RCE1 is ineffective in mediating cone opsin shutoff, contributing to the defect observed in our studies. However, further investigation is required to test this hypothesis.

Interestingly, while rod photoreceptors show severe degeneration upon global Rce1 deletion14, cones in our conditional model survive despite significant loss of PDE6. However, we observed abnormalities in the cone OS structure, including disorganized disc membranes and presence of vesicular structures. This uncoupling of cone survival from cone PDE6 levels highlights a potential difference in stress resilience between photoreceptor subtypes and opens avenues for further investigation.

In summary, our findings establish that RCE1-mediated PPP is essential for the membrane anchoring and stability of cone PDE6, a critical component of the cone phototransduction cascade. The selective dependence of cone PDE6 on PPP, but not other prenylated cone proteins, suggests a unique vulnerability in cone PDE6 trafficking mechanisms. Understanding this pathway could inform therapeutic strategies for cone dystrophies and other inherited retinal disorders involving defects in protein trafficking influenced by protein-lipid modifications.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All experimental procedures involving animals in this study are performed according to the ARRIVE guidelines and approved and conducted per guidelines stated by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of West Virginia University and the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology. Animals were euthanized by CO2 inhalation (with a flow rate of 30–70% cage volume/min) and then by cervical dislocation as a secondary method, following the procedure consistent with the recommendation of the Panel on Euthanasia of the American Veterinary Medical Association.

Animal model

Cone-Cre; Rce1fl/fl (Rce1−/−) and control littermates, including Rce1fl/fl, Cone-Cre; Rce1fl/+, and Rce1fl/+ (Rce1+/+) mice, were genotyped by PCR using primers listed in our previous publication14. PCR conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 32 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 56 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s. Animals were maintained under a 12-hour light/dark cycle, and all electrophysiological experiments were conducted under dim red-light illumination using a Kodak No. 1 Safelight filter (transmittance > 560 nm). The antibodies and reagents used in this study are listed in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, respectively. Rce1fl/fl animals were acquired from Dr. Martin Bergo14,20 and Cone-Cre animals were a gift from Dr. Yun Le21.

RT-PCR

Retinae (2) were dissected from enucleated mouse eyes and flash-frozen in 200 µl of TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) on dry ice. Total RNA was isolated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Oligo(dT)-primed reverse transcription was performed using 2.5 µg of total RNA and SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen) to generate cDNA, as described previously 19. PCR amplification was carried out using the following conditions: 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 33 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 56 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s. Quantitative RT-PCR was performed using 200 ng of retinal cDNA, quantified by NanoDrop spectrophotometry, from Rce1−/− and Rce1+/+ littermates. Reactions were run in triplicate using the MyiQ Real-Time PCR system (Bio-Rad) and MyiQ SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad). Primer sequences used for qRT-PCR were previously published26. Gene expression was normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (gapdh) as a housekeeping control.

Western blotting

Retinae (2) were homogenized in 200 µl of ice-cold 1× PBS containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor tablets using a Microson ultrasonic cell disruptor (3 pulses, 5 s each, power setting 10). Homogenization was performed in 1.5-ml microcentrifuge tubes on ice. Protein concentration was measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ND-1000, Thermo Scientific). Equal amounts of total protein (150 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE using 4–20% Tris-HCl gradient gels (Bio-Rad) and transferred to Immobilon-FL membranes (Millipore). Membranes were probed with the indicated primary antibodies (see Supplementary Table 1), diluted 1:2000 in 1× PBST (1× PBS with 0.1% Tween-20).

Following overnight incubation at 4 °C, membranes were washed three times in 1× PBST for 10 min each. Secondary antibodies, Odyssey goat anti-rabbit Alexa 680 and Odyssey goat anti-mouse Alexa 680 (LI-COR Biosciences), were used at a 1:50,000 dilution in 1× PBST and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were then rewashed three times in 1× PBST and scanned using the Odyssey Infrared Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences). Band intensities were quantified using the Odyssey software (LI-COR Biosciences). according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Electroretinography

Mice were dark-adapted overnight before testing dark- and light-adapted ERGs were performed as previously described26. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane in 2.5% oxygen and maintained under anesthesia with 1.0% isoflurane delivered via a nose cone on a temperature-controlled heating stage set to 37 °C. Pupils were dilated using a 1:1 mixture of phenylephrine and tropicamide for 10 min. Eyes were lubricated with 1:5 diluted methylcellulose in PBS before electrode placement.

A silver wire contact electrode was positioned on the corneal surface over the methylcellulose, and a reference needle electrode was inserted subcutaneously on top of the head between the ears. Mice were placed inside LKC-UTAS 4000 Ganzfeld chamber, and light flashes of varying intensities were delivered. For light-adapted ERGs, mice were exposed to background illumination at 30 cd·m⁻² for 10 min before and during flash presentation.

Immunocytochemistry

Mouse eyes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA) at room temperature for 10 min. After removal of the cornea and lens, eyecups were prepared and further fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h at room temperature. Tissues were cryoprotected in 20% sucrose in 1× PBS overnight at 4 °C, embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, frozen on dry ice, and stored at − 80 °C. Cryosections were cut at 16 μm thickness and mounted on Superfrost Plus slides (Fisher Scientific).

Sections were washed with PBST (1× PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100) and blocked for 1 h at room temperature in a blocking buffer containing 10% normal goat serum (Invitrogen) in 1× PBS with 0.1% Triton X-100. Primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 1) were diluted 1:1000 in 1× PBS containing 5% normal goat serum and 0.05% Triton X-100 and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, sections were washed three times with PBST and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 568, LI-COR Biosciences) diluted 1:1000 in the same buffer used for primary antibody incubation. Nuclear staining was performed by adding DAPI (1:5000, Molecular Probes). Slides were mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade Reagent (Life Technologies) and coverslipped (1 mm). Confocal imaging was performed at the WVU Microscope Imaging Facility with a Zeiss LSM 510 laser scanning confocal on an LSM Axioimager upright microscope using excitation wavelengths of 405, 488, and 543 nm.

Immunoprecipitation

The assembly status of cone PDE6 was evaluated using ROS1 mouse monoclonal antibody. Retinas were collected from postnatal day 75 mice. For each immunoprecipitation, four retinas from wild-type and ten retinas from Rce1−/− were pooled. Tissues were homogenized in 500 µl of ice-cold IP buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, 20 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl₂) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor tablets, 1% Triton X-100, and 10 mM iodoacetamide. The homogenates were pre-cleared by incubating with 20 µl of ImmunoPure immobilized Protein A/G Plus beads (Thermo Scientific) for 1 h at 4 °C. Samples were then centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C (Eppendorf 5424/5424R). The supernatant was collected and subjected to immunoprecipitation using the ROS1 antibody coupled to Protein A/G beads, as described previously24. Assembled PDE6 complexes were eluted by boiling in 1× SDS loading buffer, resolved on 4–20% SDS-PAGE gels (Bio-Rad), and analyzed by western blotting using the cone PDE6 polyclonal antibody (3184P).

Fractionation of retinal extracts

To separate soluble and membrane-bound fractions of retinal proteins, isotonic extraction was performed using 1× PBS containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Two retinas from Rce1−/− or littermate controls (Rce1+/+) were homogenized by sonication in 200 µl of ice-cold isotonic buffer (1X PBS) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Pierce, Thermo Scientific). The homogenate was first centrifuged at 5000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. The resulting supernatant was transferred to a new tube and subjected to ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C using a Beckman tabletop ultracentrifuge. The high-speed supernatant (soluble fraction) and high-speed pellet (membrane fraction) were separated. The pellet was resuspended in a volume equal to that of the supernatant by sonication. Equal volumes of each fraction were resolved by SDS-PAGE (4–20% Tris-HCl gel) and transferred to PVDF membranes for western blotting. Membranes were blocked and probed with the indicated antibodies.

Ultrastructure analysis of cone photoreceptor

Retinal samples from control (Rce1+/+) and knockout (Rce1−/−) mice were prepared and imaged as previously described36–38. Enucleated eyes were fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde and 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 100 mM cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4). A 20-G needle was used to make a small corneal incision, and eyes were fixed for 30–60 min at room temperature, then placed in 7% sucrose (200 mM cacodylate, pH 7.4). After cornea and lens removal, eyecups were fixed for two more days, post-fixed in 2% osmium tetroxide and 1% uranyl acetate, dehydrated in graded ethanol, and embedded in Polybed 812. As described in our earlier study, ultrathin sections were post-stained with 3% Reynold’s lead citrate and imaged using a JEOL 1400 TEM at 80 kV36–38.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE) unless otherwise indicated. Differences between experimental groups and littermate controls were analyzed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test (online version: http://www.usablestats.com/calcs/2samplet).
