Medical Spine Sagittal MRI Dataset for Segmentation and Foraminal Stenosis detection
Authors: Osamah F. Abdulmahmod, Mugahed A. Al-antari, Hyunwook Kwon, Afnan Habib, Mukhlis Raza, Metin Kaplan, Bilal Ertuğrul, İsmail Akçin, Ertan Bütün, Yeong Hyeon Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lumbar spine disorders represent one of the most prevalent musculoskeletal conditions worldwide, particularly among the elderly population. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is the gold-standard diagnostic tool due to its superior ability to visualize soft tissues, neural structures, and degenerative changes. However, accurate interpretation of lumbar MRI scans requires specialized clinical expertise and remains time-consuming and costly. As the demand for automated diagnostic support increases, the development of robust artificial intelligence (AI) systems critically relies on large, high-quality, and precisely annotated datasets. To address this need, we introduce a new sagittal lumbar spine MRI dataset comprising 500 patients, enriched with comprehensive anatomical annotations. The dataset includes foraminal detection labels extracted by expert neurosurgeons, provided as bounding-box annotations with clinically assigned severity grades for each lumbar level on both left and right sides. In addition, it contains pixel-level segmentation masks for the vertebrae, intervertebral discs, sacrum, and posterior elements (Posterior A and Posterior B), which were generated by an AI-based model and subsequently validated and refined by expert neurosurgeons to ensure anatomical accuracy. Both the detection and segmentation annotations are further evaluated using AI models to confirm their reliability for downstream clinical and research applications. To demonstrate the dataset’s utility, we developed a complete AI-based computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) system for foraminal stenosis analysis, consisting of automated slice selection, region-of-interest localization, and severity classification. The system achieved 86% accuracy in slice selection, 90% accuracy in region of interest (ROI) localization, and 65% accuracy in severity classification, reflecting the complexity of the task and the diagnostic value of the dataset. For anatomical segmentation, the best-performing architecture, SegResNet, achieved a DSC of 97.32%, HD95 of 2.337 (mm), and a recall of 97.30%, confirming the consistency and robustness of the segmentation annotations. Overall, the proposed dataset provides a reliable, richly annotated foundation for developing and benchmarking advanced AI algorithms in lumbar spine analysis, supporting a wide range of clinical and research applications from anatomical segmentation and morphological analysis to automated foraminal stenosis assessment.

Background & Summary

The accelerating aging of the population and the ubiquity of advanced imaging technologies have exponentially increased the demand for the diagnosis and treatment of degenerative spinal diseases, particularly lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS). Epidemiological studies in the United States indicate that the frequency of lumbar fusion surgery due to degenerative conditions surged by 220% per 100,000 population between 1990 and 20011. Furthermore, by 2015, the annual hospital costs associated with these procedures exceeded $10 billion, imposing a massive socioeconomic burden. Notably, this increase in surgical intervention is most precipitous among the elderly population aged 60 and older2. Given reports that moderate stenosis is observed in 23.6–77.9% and severe stenosis in 8.4–30.4% of adults over 403, precise radiological assessment is indispensable to distinguish between normal aging processes and pathological conditions, thereby preventing unnecessary medical and surgical procedures. While LSS is clinically classified into central, lateral recess, or foraminal stenosis depending on the lesion’s location4, these types can occur independently or in combination, necessitating a comprehensive structural evaluation. Unlike X-ray or Computed Tomography (CT), which primarily capture bone structures, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) excels at providing high-resolution, non-invasive visualization of soft tissue structures, including intervertebral discs (IVD), nerves, ligaments, and spinal cord. Due to this superior soft tissue contrast, MRI is utilized as the most reliable diagnostic tool for LSS, serving as the cornerstone for evidence-based clinical guidelines in diagnosis and treatment planning5. Among the different MRI imaging planes, the sagittal view holds exceptional clinical value, particularly for evaluating foraminal stenosis. It allows for the continuous observation of not only the reduction in vertebral and IVD height but also the structures forming the superior/inferior and anterior/posterior boundaries of the neural foramen, through which nerve roots pass. Consequently, accurately grasping the structures surrounding the neural foramen on sagittal MRI plays a significant role in understanding the pathological mechanisms of stenosis and developing automated diagnostic algorithms. However, although deep learning technologies are being actively applied to medical image analysis, significant barriers remain in establishing a diagnostic system viable for clinical settings. According to a recent systematic review by6, the core factor determining the performance of deep learning models is not the algorithmic framework itself, but the quality and quantity of the training data. Nevertheless, due to strict data confidentiality requirements and the lack of high-level expertise required for precise annotation, the field of spinal imaging still lacks research resources that can be truly classified as Big Data6. In practice, most publicly available spinal MRI datasets are limited to simple segmentation centered on vertebrae and IVDs. Even though foraminal stenosis results from the complex interplay of hypertrophy and deformation of posterior elements, such as facet joints, ligaments, and pedicles, existing datasets do not include specific pixel-level information on these anatomic structures. Furthermore, the lack of integration between structural segmentation information and stenosis severity labels results in insufficient data granularity for training automated pathology reporting models.

To bridge these gaps, we introduce in this article a new sagittal MRI dataset that includes the detection of foraminal stenosis in both the left and right foramina across the five lumbar IVD levels from L1–L2 to L5–S1, along with their diagnostic severity, annotated as bounding boxes within volumetric DICOM series. In addition, we provide segmentation masks for key anatomical structures on the mid-sagittal slice, including the vertebrae, IVDs, sacrum, posterior A, posterior B, and the background representing the anterior region. Specifically, Posterior A represents the posterior anatomical compartment of the lumbar spine, including the posterior spinal canal, posterior osseous elements (e.g., spinous processes), and adjacent epidural fat structures, whereas Posterior B corresponds to the outer posterior soft-tissue/background region located posterior to the vertebral column, primarily included to preserve the global curvature and alignment of the back contour. Overall, this dataset serves as a comprehensive resource for lumbar spine analysis, supporting tasks such as segmentation, foraminal detection, and the development of AI-assisted diagnostic tools, and offers a strong benchmark for future research. Related work

Deep learning networks, which currently dominate spinal image analysis, are often perceived by external users as black-box systems that fail to transparently reveal their internal computational processes. Although technical attempts have been made to visualize internal operations, AI predictions still tend to be determined by ambiguous logic that is difficult for human observers to intuitively understand or interpret. This opacity leads directly to the issue of accountability; in the event of a misdiagnosis, it is difficult to determine whether the responsibility lies with the radiologist who made the final decision, the AI model, or the manufacturer7. Consequently, these ambiguous characteristics compel regulatory bodies to demand deeper testing and validation, acting as a severe bottleneck that increases the time and cost required for marketing approval and commercialization of new AI tools. To overcome these regulatory and liability issues and implement reliable automated diagnosis, an explainable approach based on segmentation, beyond simple classification, is essential. Since foraminal stenosis arises not from the deformation of a single structure but from the complex interaction among multiple structures, a process of precisely separating anatomical structures to quantitatively measure (measurement-based approach) the foraminal area, height, and hypertrophy of surrounding bone structures must take precedence8,9. This approach provides visual and numerical evidence for diagnosis, thereby eliminating the ambiguity of AI judgment and serving as the only viable alternative to effectively assist clinical decision-making within the actual clinical workflow. Despite the necessity for such precise structural analysis, existing large-scale datasets exhibit limitations in terms of Structural Diversity and the Coupling of pathological information required for foraminal stenosis assessment. In our recent systematic review, we comprehensively investigated the landscape of LSS MRI datasets. Representative examples include CSpineSeg10, which provides vertebral and IVD labels in cervical sagittal T2-weighted MRI, and the dataset introduced by van der Graaf et al.11 which includes annotations for vertebrae, IVDs, and the spinal canal in lumbar MRI. However, most available datasets are confined to segmentation of vertebrae and discs and lack detailed annotation of posterior elements, which are important contributors to stenosis. Although various studies have utilized sagittal views12–17, they are often limited by small sample sizes or a narrow focus on central structures such as vertebral bodies and IVDs, thereby failing to capture the complex lateral anatomy required for accurate foraminal assessment. Crucially, previous datasets and studies have primarily focused on the mid-sagittal slice or the axial view18. For instance, while Sudirman et al.19 provided pixel-wise labels for posterior elements and the thecal sac, and the extensive dataset by Tumko et al.20 recently introduced comprehensive labels including neural foramina and nerves, these contributions remain fundamentally limited by their exclusive reliance on the axial view. Similarly, several prior studies have relied primarily on axial T1- or T2-weighted MRI sequences to annotate the dural sac and spinal canal21–24. Although the axial view is well suited for measuring the cross-sectional area of the dural sac and diagnosing central stenosis, it is insufficient for visualizing the vertical compression of exiting nerve roots within the neural foramen and therefore presents limitations for the assessment of foraminal stenosis. A recent review by Seo et al.25 on spinal stenosis grading systems highlighted that the widely used Lee grading system for foraminal stenosis evaluates fat obliteration around the exiting nerve root and the morphology of nerve compression in the sagittal view26. According to this study, relying solely on axial images makes it difficult to clearly distinguish the anterior/posterior and superior/inferior boundaries of the foramen. Furthermore, as noted in18, many existing sagittal-based studies rely exclusively on the mid-sagittal slice, which is centered on the spinal canal. However, the neural foramina are located laterally on both the left and right sides of the vertebrae. Therefore, relying only on the mid-sagittal slice fails to capture the lateral regions where foraminal stenosis actually occurs. Accurate detection and severity assessment are impossible without examining the lateral sagittal slices. Nevertheless, the research community currently faces a paucity of datasets comprising paired sagittal view–based fine-grained segmentation masks and foraminal stenosis severity grade labels. Existing datasets typically provide either anatomical structure masks or patient-level diagnostic labels. While several studies offer large-scale severity scores, classification labels, or bounding box annotations for lumbar stenosis, these works are primarily limited to classification or detection tasks rather than detailed morphological segmentation27–32. This fails to offer the pathological ground truth specifying how narrow a specific level of the foramen is (e.g., Normal, Mild, Moderate, Severe) alongside structural information. This absence of data is a critical factor hindering the development of end-to-end models that learn the causal relationship between anatomical morphological changes in images and actual clinical diagnoses33. Furthermore, while research on spinal stenosis diagnosis using deep learning is advancing rapidly, with models proposed by20,27, the majority of existing studies still show limitations in terms of clinical explainability. Many models rely solely on cross-sectional information from axial images due to limited label granularity or fail to sufficiently reflect the structural context of the entire spine even when utilizing sagittal views. The proposed LSS dataset bridges this technical gap by integrating fine-grained segmentation information with lateral detection of stenosis, thereby presenting a new benchmark for the development of next-generation explainable diagnostic models.

Methodology

Data curation

The data curation process began with the collection of 500 sagittal lumbar spine MRI examinations from 500 unique patients at Fırat university hospital (FUH), Elazığ, Türkiye. Each patient contributed exactly one MRI examination to the dataset. The scans were acquired on different MRI scanners (Philips Ingenia either 1.5 T or 3 T, GE Signa HDxt 1.5 T and GE Signa Excite 1.5 T), under imaging parameters as illustrated in Table 1, ensuring consistency and reproducibility across all imaging sessions. All examinations employ routine clinical T2-weighted sagittal sequences and are required to provide complete visualization of the lumbar spine from L1–L2 through L5–S1, thereby supporting reliable assessment of foraminal stenosis and anatomical segmentation. Following acquisition, each MRI volume undergoes a rigorous quality evaluation to confirm adequate anatomical coverage, sufficient image quality, and full representation of the neural foramina. Studies exhibiting motion artifacts, missing slices, truncated fields of view, or incomplete depiction of lumbar IVD levels are excluded to preserve dataset integrity and analytical reliability. Subsequently, all DICOM files undergo comprehensive de-identification to ensure patient confidentiality and compliance with ethical guidelines. Sensitive metadata including (patient name, patient address, patient ID, patient history, ethnic group, performing physician name, etc.) are removed, while only technical imaging attributes essential for interpretation and analysis are retained. Data annotation

The dataset annotation was performed by three neurosurgeons from our consortium team, with 3, 11, and 26 years of clinical experience, at the department of neurosurgery, Fırat University Faculty of Medicine, Türkiye. The AI technical team supported the annotation process by generating the initial segmentation masks, as described below. The involvement of experienced clinicians was essential to ensure accurate anatomical interpretation, consistent labeling, and clinically meaningful annotations, thereby enhancing the reliability and usability of the dataset for medical imaging research.

Sagittal foraminal detection and classification

The foraminal annotations in the proposed dataset were manually created using rectangular bounding boxes applied to sagittal lumbar spine MRI slices. As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), each bounding box corresponds to a specific IVD level and specifies the laterality of the neural foramen left or right along with its clinically assigned stenosis grade. All annotations were produced using the graphical tool LabelImg34, which exports XML files in the PASCAL VOC format. The XML files also contain standard PASCAL VOC tags (e.g., <pose>, <truncated>, <difficult>) automatically generated by the annotation tool; these fields are retained for format compliance but are not used in the present study. For every sagittal slice, bounding boxes were placed around the visible neural foramina across IVDs levels L1–L2 through L5–S1 to accurately delineate the regions of interest used for stenosis assessment. The exported XML files contain the IVD level (L1–L2 to L5–S1), and the laterality (left or right). The stenosis grade encodes foraminal narrowing severity using right foraminal stenosis (RFS0–RFS3) and left foraminal stenosis (LFS0–LFS3) labels, where 0 corresponds to Normal, 1 to Mild, 2 to Moderate, and 3 to Severe. Spatial localization is defined by bounding-box coordinates (xmin, ymin, xmax, ymax), which specify the top-left and bottom-right corners of the annotated foraminal region in pixel units relative to the original image resolution, as shown in Fig. 2. Figure 3 illustrates the distribution of the number of IVDs annotated per patient, showing that most examinations provide visibility of three to four levels per side. Although the lumbar spine comprises five foraminal levels per side, the neurosurgeons declare that not always all levels are consistently visualized across patient MRI examinations. Sagittal MRI segmentation

The proposed dataset comprises also segmentation masks for the lumbar spine anatomical structures including vertebrae, IVDs, sacrum, and the posterior regions (Posterior A and Posterior B) on the mid-sagittal slice as shown in Fig. 1(b), where these structures are clearly visualized. The segmentation masks have been generated through a human-in-the-loop annotation workflow depicted in Fig. 4. This workflow begins with a publicly available LSS dataset35 containing sagittal T2-weighted lumbar spine MRI scans, and the mid-sagittal slice is subsequently selected from each volume, resulting in a total of 515 samples for training and evaluating using SegResNet model36. The dataset was randomly split at the patient level into 70%, 10%, and 20% for training, validation, and testing, respectively, the model demonstrates strong performance, achieving a Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of 97.58% on the testing set. Based on this model, the proposed dataset serves as an external testing dataset to generate the initial segmentation masks for the mid-sagittal slice of each patient. These predicted masks are then refined using the Napari tool37 to remove over-segmentation, fill gaps, and correct remaining inaccuracies. After refinement is completed, a morphological closing operation is applied to smooth the boundaries of each structure. In the end the segmentation masks are validated and refined by three expert neurosurgeons to ensure that each mask accurately corresponds to the correct anatomical structure. In cases where discrepancies are identified, the experts manually adjust and refine the masks to guarantee anatomical correctness. In total, 500 samples are segmented and included in the final version of the dataset as published publicly in38.Fig. 4Human-in-the-loop segmentation workflow for generating anatomical masks in the proposed dataset.

Foraminal stenosis standard

Data annotation is performed on sagittal MRI slices in which the anatomical structures forming the lumbar neural foramen are clearly visualized. These structures are schematically illustrated in Fig. 5. Since foraminal stenosis is clinically associated with neural compression, nerve root compression was considered in the grading of stenosis severity. Foraminal stenosis was classified into 4 grades: normal, mild, moderate, and severe. Grading was based on the degree of compression exerted on the perineural fat tissue (which surrounds the nerve root within the foramen) by the foraminal structures. Data Records

The dataset is publicly available at Mendeley Data repository38 under the title “LSS MRI AISSLab Dataset: Medical Spine Sagittal MRI Dataset for Segmentation and Foraminal Stenosis Detection” with DOI of 10.17632/rgb77xm3jf.4. As depicted in Fig. 7, the dataset is organized under the root directory “LSS_MRI_AISSLab_Dataset_V0.1”, which contains three primary folders: DICOM, Segmentation, and Foramina_Detection. The DICOM directory contains raw sagittal lumbar spine MRI volumes stored as sequential DICOM files (IM000000.dcm, IM000001.dcm, etc.) organized into patient-specific subfolders (0001, 0002, …, 0500). The Segmentation directory contains two subfolders: Middle_Slice, which stores the mid-sagittal slice of each patient’s DICOM series as.png images (e.g., 0001_IM000007.png), and Masks, which contain corresponding segmentation masks in.png format and annotation files in.xml format with matching filenames. The Foramina_Detection directory is organized by patient ID subfolders (0001, 0002, …, 0500) and contains sagittal slices converted to.png format (IM000000.png, etc.). Annotation files are provided in.xml format (PASCAL VOC structure) only for slices where foramina are visible. Each XML annotation file contains the following fields: (i) foraminal level (L1–L2, L2–L3, L3–L4, L4–L5, L5–S1), (ii) anatomical side (LFS: left foraminal stenosis; RFS: right foraminal stenosis), (iii) stenosis grade (e.g., RFS0, LFS1), and (iv) bounding box coordinates (xmin, ymin, xmax, ymax).Fig. 7Directory structure of the LSS MRI AISSLab dataset, showing the organization of raw sagittal DICOM volumes, middle-slice images with corresponding segmentation masks, and foraminal stenosis detection annotations stored as slice-level PNGs with XML bounding-box labels.

Technical Validation

Preprocessing

A consistent and rigorously designed preprocessing pipeline ensures the robustness and reproducibility of the end-to-end framework for lumbar foraminal stenosis analysis and segmentation. All 3D sagittal DICOM volumes are padded solely to standardize the number of slices, ensuring consistent input depth for the 3D slice selection network. This step is performed for tensor dimensional alignment and does not address variability arising from different acquisition protocols. Each slice is resized to 256 × 256 pixels and intensity-normalized using Min–Max normalization, rescaling pixel values to the range of (0,1) on a per-slice basis to ensure consistent input scaling. For both foramina detection and segmentation, comprehensive data augmentation is applied including geometric transformations (random horizontal/vertical flips, 90° rotations, translations, and scaling), photometric modifications (brightness and contrast jitter, as well as nonlinear intensity transformations such as gamma correction), and distortion-based operations such as Gaussian noise and Gaussian blur. Although sagittal DICOM images in the dataset follow consistent clinical orientation, flips and rotations were intentionally applied during training as regularization strategies to reduce reliance on fixed spatial priors and improve feature invariance. These transformations were used solely for robust enhancement and not to simulate acquisition variability. By introducing controlled variability into the training data, these augmentations reduce overfitting and enhance robustness to unseen imaging conditions.

Sagittal MRI foraminal stenosis detection

The validation of foraminal stenosis detection and classification in the proposed dataset follows the pipeline illustrated in Fig. 8. Initially, in Step 1, the 3D lumbar MRI DICOM volume is divided into two anatomical sides, left and right, which are then processed independently. Subsequently, in Step 2, each side is analyzed using an AI-based 3D CNN slice-selection network to identify the sagittal slice containing the highest number of clearly defined foraminal regions. However, because a single slice may not capture all foramina, the adjacent slices (N–1 and N + 1) are also selected so that full coverage of the lower lumbar foramina is ensured whenever they are visible in the MRI scans. In step 3, the three selected sagittal slices from each side are forwarded to an AI-based ROI detection module implemented using the YOLOv1239 framework, which localizes foraminal regions corresponding to all lumbar IVD levels from L1–L2 through L5–S1. For each slice, the detector generates bounding boxes with associated confidence scores, and thus, for each level, only the ROI with the highest confidence is retained for subsequent classification. In step 4, stenosis classification is performed using a dual-branch ensemble strategy. Specifically, the selected ROIs are processed by two parallel classifiers: the first is based on a Data-efficient Image Transformer (DeiT) architecture40, which leverages transformer-based attention mechanisms to capture long-range spatial dependencies within the foraminal region; the second is a custom-designed shallow convolutional neural network, whose hyperparameters were optimized using an MLOps-based framework, following our prior work33. where they were optimized using an MLOps-based framework. In this study, the model is used without further modification as a component within the proposed framework. The optimized hyperparameters include batch size, dropout rate, filter size, number of layers, learning rate, and weight decay are carefully selected to ensure low computational complexity and robustness for small anatomical regions. Both classifiers independently produce stenosis grade predictions across four clinical categories (Normal, Mild, Moderate, Severe), and their outputs are then combined via weighted fusion, assigning 60% weight to the DeiT classifier and 40% to the shallow CNN. This approach integrates the global reasoning ability of the transformer with the fine-grained feature sensitivity of the CNN, thereby improving prediction stability. Finally, in Step 5, the pipeline generates level-wise stenosis predictions for the entire lumbar spine on both sides, such that each side contains predictions from L1–L2 through L5–S1, which facilitates direct clinical interpretation and downstream quantitative or statistical analyses. Accordingly, to ensure reliable identification of lumbar neural foramina within 3D sagittal MRI volumes, the proposed end-to-end CAD pipeline was systematically evaluated by analyzing the performance of its three core components: slice selection, ROI detection, and stenosis classification. This evaluation was conducted both at the individual module level and within the fully integrated system to reflect real-world diagnostic behavior. As illustrated in Fig. 9, all components were quantitatively assessed using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, following the same evaluation protocol adopted in our previous studies16. This modular analysis clarifies the contribution of each stage and highlights how their combined performance determines the overall reliability of the CAD framework. The slice-selection module demonstrated the most stable and consistent performance, achieving an accuracy of 89%, precision of 90%, recall of 92%, and an F1-score of 91%. These results confirm its effectiveness in identifying the most informative sagittal slice together with its adjacent neighbors, thereby ensuring comprehensive anatomical coverage of lumbar foraminal regions for downstream processing. Similarly, the ROI-detection module achieved strong performance, with 93% accuracy, 73% precision, 93% recall, and 82% F1-score. In addition, the object detection performance measured by mean average precision (mAP) at IoU of 0.5 (mAP@0.5) reached 86%, further confirming reliable localization of foraminal regions across lumbar IVD levels. This indicates reliable localization of foraminal regions across all lumbar IVD levels while maintaining a low rate of missed detections, which is critical for preserving diagnostic accuracy in subsequent classification. In contrast, the stenosis-classification module exhibited comparatively moderate performance, achieving 72% accuracy, 78% precision, 72% recall, and a 74% F1-score. This outcome reflects the intrinsic difficulty of foraminal stenosis grading, where subtle morphological differences between severity levels, combined with the small size and low contrast of foraminal ROIs, limit the availability of discriminative visual features. Nevertheless, the consistently high performance of the upstream slice-selection and ROI-detection modules provides a strong foundation for reliable stenosis assessment. Sagittal MRI segmentation

While the proposed dataset primarily provides bounding box annotations for foraminal stenosis across multiple slices in the 3D DICOM series, it also includes high-quality segmentation masks for critical spinal anatomical structures, including the vertebrae, IVDs, sacrum, and posterior regions (Posterior A and Posterior B). These segmentation masks are provided exclusively for the mid-sagittal slice of each MRI volume. These segmentation masks precisely delineate essential anatomical structures and provide quantitative information that can be leveraged for a wide range of clinical assessments. Such assessments include the evaluation of vertebral fractures, disc degeneration or herniation, spondylosis, spinal alignment abnormalities (lordosis), bone quality evaluation, and other spinal conditions41. By enabling accurate and reproducible measurements of these structures, the dataset supports downstream analyses that facilitate automated diagnosis, treatment planning, and disease progression monitoring, as demonstrated in previous studies17,42,43. To rigorously evaluate the technical reliability and robustness of the segmentation masks, extensive experiments were conducted using six public state-of-the-art deep learning architectures: DeepLabv344, Attention U-Net45, Swin UNETR46, Swin Transformer47, SegResNet48, and TransUNet49. All models were trained and evaluated on a desktop workstation running Windows 11, equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 Ti GPU, an Intel® Core™ i7-14700K CPU (3.40 GHz), and 64 GB of RAM. All implementations were developed in Python 3.13.5 using PyTorch 2.7.1, ensuring a consistent computing environment for fair performance comparisons across all architectures. The same hardware and software configuration was used for all deep learning components in this study, including slice selection, ROI detection, and segmentation models. All segmentation experiments were conducted exclusively on the proposed dataset. As segmentation masks are available only for the mid-sagittal slice of each MRI volume, the task is formulated as a 2D segmentation problem. The dataset is split into training, validation, and test sets in a 70%, 10%, and 20% ratio, respectively, using a patient-level random split to ensure independent samples across sets, to enable robust performance assessment while minimizing potential biases in evaluation. Technical validation performance is evaluated using standard metrics such as DSC, 95th Percentile Hausdorff Distance (HD95), Average Symmetric Surface Distance (ASSD), Precision, Recall, and Accuracy as our previous work50,51. Limitation and future work direction

One inherent limitation of this study arises from the three-dimensional nature of the lumbar spine. Under normal conditions, the lumbar region exhibits physiological lordosis; however, in patients with lumbar spinal stenosis, alterations in spinal alignment are frequently observed. Kyphotic and/or scoliotic deformities, regardless of severity, can influence the orientation of sagittal imaging planes, leading to asymmetric slice representations. Such asymmetry may hinder complete visualization of the neural foramina and introduce ambiguity in accurately grading foraminal stenosis, particularly when sagittal slices do not pass symmetrically through the foraminal region. Consequently, reliable interpretation in these cases remains highly dependent on the clinical expertise of the annotating neurosurgeon. To mitigate this limitation, the dataset was curated and validated through the involvement of multiple highly experienced neurosurgeons, ensuring anatomical correctness and clinical reliability. As future work, we plan to extend the dataset and diagnostic framework by incorporating axial MRI–based segmentation and detection of lumbar spine structures, enabling complementary multi-plane analysis. Furthermore, the integration of automated report generation for lumbar spine diagnosis, combining segmentation, foraminal detection, and severity grading into a unified AI-driven reporting pipeline, represents an important direction for advancing clinical applicability.

Usage Notes

The proposed dataset consists exclusively of fully de-identified sagittal lumbar spine MRI data. Raw MRI volumes can be visualized using standard medical imaging software such as ITK-SNAP or 3D Slicer by importing the corresponding DICOM series folder. For segmentation tasks, users may load the mid-sagittal slice images and overlay the associated segmentation masks to analyze vertebrae, IVDs, sacrum, posterior elements (Posterior A and Posterior B), and background regions. For foraminal stenosis detection and classification tasks, researchers can train models using the provided bounding-box annotations following the standardized LFS/RFS grading scheme. Each annotation specifies the lumbar IVD level (L1–L2 to L5–S1), anatomical laterality (left or right), and stenosis severity grade. All annotations were generated and validated by expert neurosurgeons following established clinical criteria and are provided as finalized ground truth to ensure reproducibility, fair benchmarking, and consistent method comparison.
