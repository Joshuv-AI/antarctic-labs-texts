Inadvertent guidewire retention after radial artery cannulation: report of two cases and safety lessons for vascular access practice
Authors: Leopoldo Muniz da Silva, Rafael Souza Fava Nersessian, Saullo Queiroz Silveira, Helidea de Oliveira Lima, Glenio B Mizubuti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInadvertent guidewire retention is an uncommon but potentially serious complication most often described in the context of central venous catheterization, and reports involving arterial access are exceptionally rare. This report describes two cases of inadvertent guidewire retention following radial artery cannulation performed using the Seldinger technique for intraoperative hemodynamic monitoring.Case presentationIn both cases, arterial cannulation was indicated because of complex (and possibly prolonged) surgical procedures with a risk of clinically significant blood loss, thereby necessitating continuous beat-to-beat arterial pressure monitoring. The presumed embolization mechanism involved advancement of the catheter while the pre-attached proximal cap remained in place, thereby preventing the guidewire from exiting proximally and ultimately leading to intravascular displacement of the wire. In the first case, the event remained unrecognized until the patient developed forearm pain, reduced radial and ulnar pulses, and sensory changes to the affected upper extremity several weeks postoperatively. Imaging revealed a retained guidewire extending from the radial artery to the axillary artery, requiring surgical intervention for retrieval. In the second case, the inadvertently retained guidewire was found incidentally on a routine chest radiograph eighteen days postoperatively.ConclusionsThese cases illustrate that guidewire retention, albeit rare, can occur during arterial cannulation by the Seldinger technique and shares the same human-factor vulnerabilities long recognized in central venous access-related guidewire retention, including distraction, incomplete preparation and loss of wire control. In response, several system-level safety measures were implemented, including explicit “wire in hand” confirmation before catheter advancement, the use of wire length markers, mandatory removal of manufacturer attached catheter caps prior to insertion, optional sterile forceps control of the guidewire, multidisciplinary verification, and mandatory documentation of guidewire retrieval. These events reinforce the need for robust safety barriers across all procedures using the Seldinger technique to prevent guidewire retention and enhance procedural safety.Keywords: Case reports, Guidewires, Radial artery, Catheterization, Patient safety, Seldinger technique

Background

Intraoperative monitoring through central venous catheterization and invasive arterial pressure measurement is frequently employed depending on the perceived surgical/anesthetic risk and patient underlying comorbidities [1]. The Seldinger technique is the established method for accessing vascular and other luminal structures [2, 3]. It involves puncturing the desired vessel with a sharp needle, advancing a round-tipped guidewire through the needle’s lumen while concurrently withdrawing the needle, and finally passing a cannula over the guidewire into the target cavity or vessel [3].

Complications associated with this procedure include hematoma formation, local bleeding, infection, vascular thrombosis, air embolism, guidewire fracture, among others [4]. Complete guidewire embolization is an uncommon but clinically significant complication of vascular access. Although its true incidence remains unknown, previously published reports have primarily described guidewire embolization in the context of venous catheterization [5, 6]. Human factors such as distraction, inadequate supervision, high workload, emergency insertions, and the absence of a structured procedural checklist for central venous cannulation have been identified as key contributors to guidewire loss/retention during vascular access [7]. These same human factors are equally relevant to arterial cannulation procedures where similar workflow pressures and cognitive demands may predispose clinicians to fail to remove the guidewire during arterial catheterization using the Seldinger technique [2, 7, 8]. This report describes two cases of guidewire embolization that occurred during radial artery cannulation (RAC) performed for intraoperative hemodynamic monitoring, highlighting the clinical presentation, diagnostic assessment, subsequent management, and key lessons learned to strengthen procedural safety.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 72-year-old woman, American Society of Anesthesiologists’ Physical Status (ASA-PS) II, ex-smoker, dyslipidemic, hypertensive, anemic, and with chronic kidney insufficiency not necessitating dialysis underwent a robotic left lung upper lobectomy for adenocarcinoma. RAC was indicated given the anticipated surgical complexity, expected duration of the procedure, and the potential for clinically meaningful blood loss. Preoperative right RAC was carried out uneventfully using a 20-gauge arterial catheter kit inserted with the Seldinger technique. The patient spent the first postoperative day in the intensive care unit (ICU) where the arterial catheter was removed due to malfunction. She was discharged home on postoperative day 5.

Thirty-four days following her initial surgical procedure, the patient presented with right upper extremity pain prompting a computed tomography (CT) scan that identified a long (> 30 cm) guidewire extending from her right radial artery to the proximal axillary artery (Fig. 1). Clinical examination revealed reduced radial and ulnar pulses, satisfactory perfusion (≥ 2 s), and diminished sensation in her right hand. The guidewire was surgically removed through a combined right femoral puncture arteriography and right cubital fossa incision under general anesthesia (Fig. 1). The patient was anticoagulated postoperatively and presented a slow but progressive improvement of her symptoms over time. No additional complications were identified, and she was discharged home on postoperative day 5. No residual deficits/symptoms were observed at her six-month follow-up evaluation. Notably, a retrospective review of her imaging studies revealed that the retained guidewire remained unrecognized despite being plainly visible in four postoperative chest radiographs taken during her initial hospital admission (Fig. 2).

Case 2

A 58-year-old woman, ASA-PS II, previously treated for interatrial septum communication, and with underlying gastroesophageal reflux disease, attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder, and prior transient ischemic attack underwent robotic right lung segmentectomy. Uneventful preoperative left RAC using a 20-gauge arterial catheter kit inserted with the Seldinger technique was performed given the perceived surgical technical complexity and the potential for significant intraoperative blood loss. The patient spent the first postoperative day in the ICU and was discharged home on postoperative day five.

Eighteen days after her initial procedure, a routine chest radiograph (unavailable for inclusion in this report) incidentally identified the presence of an elongated foreign body in her left upper limb extending into her left hemithorax. The patient remained asymptomatic. She underwent endovascular foreign body removal under local infiltration and conscious sedation. Postoperatively, she remained asymptomatic and was discharged home the following day on dual antiplatelet therapy for outpatient follow up.

Institutional safety interventions implemented following the events

Following a thorough review of these events by the institutional safety committee, strengthened practices were implemented across the board to improve awareness and procedural safety. A mandatory multidisciplinary check for guidewire removal was incorporated into the institutional vascular access bundle. Additionally, at the end of every Seldinger-based procedure, the guidewire must be visually inspected (to ensure it is intact/not fractured) and its removal documented in the patient’s chart/anesthetic record. Furthermore, specific human-factor countermeasures were introduced to address the procedural vulnerabilities identified as contributing factors in these cases including the adoption of an explicit “wire-in-hand” verbal confirmation before catheter advancement, the use of wire-length markers to facilitate continuous manual control of the guidewire, and the requirement to remove manufacturer-attached catheter caps before advancing the catheter over the wire in order to prevent inadvertent wire propulsion (Table 1). Notably, the safety implemented interventions align with contemporary international recommendations for vascular access safety, which emphasize continuous guidewire control, standardized device preparation, structured pause points, and team-based verification as key strategies to prevent guidewire retention and other procedural adverse events [1, 9].

Discussion

Invasive or minimally invasive monitoring is a routine practice for anesthesiologists, intensivists, emergency physicians, and other healthcare allies (e.g., nurse practitioners and respiratory therapists). The Seldinger technique is the most used method for performing these procedures, and there are reports of guidewire embolism secondary to guidewire fracture, inattention, lack of experience, among others [10]. These are universally preventable events with potential for severe patient harm such as vascular thrombosis, tissue ischemia, arrhythmias, direct vascular injury, and even death [11].

Several reports suggest that most cases of guidewire retention/embolization remain unnoticed until after the procedure [12, 13]. One common characteristic in both cases described herein was the absence of postoperative diagnosis prior to hospital discharge, despite the foreign body in question being plainly visible in multiple imaging studies (Case 1) during the patient’s hospital admission. This diagnostic error may have resulted from the “satisfaction of search” phenomenon, which occurs when the examiner fails to continue to search for further abnormalities (of lack thereof) after identifying an initial diagnosis of interest. In these cases, chest radiographs did not evidence any significant pulmonary abnormalities, which may have satisfied the clinician(s) evaluating these imaging studies [14]. In a similar report, a guidewire was inadvertently left inside the patient following a femoral venous central catheterization using the Seldinger technique [15]. Five days later, the guidewire was incidentally discovered during the insertion of an inferior vena cava filter [15]. A subsequent retrospective review of imaging studies performed daily and reviewed by multiple radiologists, emergency physicians, and intensivists revealed that the guidewire was plainly visible in every one of them [15]. This illustrates that inadvertent guidewire retention represents a failure not only at a procedural level, but also a potential downstream system issue possibly influenced by cognitive overload, inattentional blindness, and fragmented communication [8, 16–18]. Also noteworthy is that in both cases presented herein the guidewire had migrated into the chest and, perhaps counterintuitively, in an opposite direction to the blood flow within the vessels in which they were accidentally placed. Indeed, sequential imaging studies showed unpredictable migration of the guidewire in and out of the chest.

The recommended technique for vascular cannulation requires continuous manual control of the guidewire throughout the procedure and avoidance of catheter advancement over the wire unless the proximal catheter port is fully open. In both cases, a 20-gauge arterial catheter kit was used, consisting of a 5-cm vascular catheter and a 35-cm guidewire. Importantly, the guidewire length varies across commercially available arterial catheter kits; therefore, the 35-cm length described herein reflects the guidewire/kit specifications used at the authors’ institution which may differ from other vascular access kits commonly used in clinical practice. By manufacturer default, the proximal catheter outlet is typically occluded with a plastic cap that must be manually removed prior to advancing the catheter over the guidewire to allow proximal wire control and ensure complete guidewire retrieval following catheter placement. It is hypothesized that in both cases the operators may have failed to remove the vascular catheter proximal cap prior to advancing the catheter over the guidewire, ultimately driving it intravascularly along with the catheter. Subsequent saline flushing through the catheter may have also contributed to further proximal intravascular migration of the guidewire.

Inadvertent guidewire retention may result in vascular perforation, thromboembolic events, infection, among other foreign body-related complications. Hence, prompt guidewire removal is mandatory. Indeed, the longer the wire remains in situ, the greater the likelihood of vascular wall adhesion thereby significantly increasing the complexity and risk of retrieval [12, 19]. Interventional radiology is often the preferred and least invasive modality for retrieval. With contemporary endovascular techniques, retained catheters/guidewires can be successfully removed in ≥ 90% of cases [20]. In Case 1, the delayed diagnosis contributed to a somewhat dire clinical course. The patient developed reduced radial and ulnar pulses (possibly due to partial arterial thrombosis) and a sensory deficit in the affected hand. Although her symptoms had significantly improved upon hospital discharge, full resolution had not yet occurred five days post-guidewire removal. The technical complexity of guidewire removal was also greater, requiring a combined endovascular and open surgical approach, including vascular exposure at the level of the cubital fossa, to achieve complete guidewire removal.

The findings from these two cases are consistent with the broader human factors patterns described by Mariyaselvam et al. in the analysis of England’s Never Event database [13]. In their review of 163 retained central venous guidewires, most events were not recognized at the time of insertion, and human factors such as operator error, distraction, inadequate supervision, and high workload were the dominant contributors [13]. Although their investigation focused exclusively on central venous catheterization, the underlying mechanisms are directly applicable to arterial cannulation performed with the Seldinger technique. As in central venous access, radial artery cannulation involves multiple simultaneous cognitive demands, time pressure, and the need for coordinated manipulation of guidewire, needle, and catheter. Our cases similarly highlight that reliance solely on operator memory is insufficient. Rather, a system-based approach, incorporating standardized procedural workflows, mandatory “wire-in-hand” verification before catheter advancement, and equipment design modifications, is more likely to reduce the risk of guidewire retention during both venous and arterial cannulation.

Inadvertent guidewire retention is preventable, particularly when a structured technique/workflow is employed to ensure the operator maintains continuous guidewire control. Although numerous cases of inadvertent guidewire retention following central venous catheterization have been reported, guidewire retention related to arterial cannulation has been rarely discussed in the literature, with only one case previously described [21]. Given that arterial and central venous cannulation share a similar (Seldinger) technique, it is likely that they also share similar contributing factors for complications (e.g., inadvertent guidewire retention) which underscores the need for strong individual and institutional safety barriers during vascular catheterization. Recent analyses of retained foreign object events further reinforce the need for standardized processes, team-based verification, and technological support to reduce preventable safety events across procedural settings [22, 23].

Although this report arises from the perioperative setting, the safety implications are broadly applicable across areas (e.g., intensive care, emergency medicine, other multidisciplinary environments) where vascular access and other Seldinger-based procedures are commonly used. Finally, terminology in this report follows the NAVIGATE nomenclature recommendations for vascular access devices to ensure precise and standardized device description [24].

Conclusion

Inadvertent guidewire retention during radial artery cannulation is rare but potentially harmful and may remain unrecognized. These cases demonstrate that arterial catheterization shares the same human factor vulnerabilities described in central venous access. System based safety strategies including continuous guidewire control, standardized device preparation, and multidisciplinary verification are essential to prevent guidewire retention and improve vascular access safety.
