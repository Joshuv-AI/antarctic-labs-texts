Global Burden of Fungal Skin Diseases and Future Projections: An Analysis of the 2021 Global Burden of Disease Study
Authors: Shao‐Jie Chen, Song Chen, Ye Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe global prevalence and incidence of fungal skin diseases continue to rise due to population ageing, urbanisation and environmental changes, posing a significant public health challenge. These infections are particularly severe in immunocompromised individuals, potentially leading to deep‐seated infections and life‐threatening complications. However, comprehensive data on the global burden of fungal skin diseases remain limited, especially in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) with constrained resources.MethodsUsing Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data from 1990 to 2021, this study analysed trends in incidence, prevalence and disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) across 204 countries and territories. Joinpoint regression was employed to identify temporal trend changes, while the Age‐Period‐Cohort (APC) model was used to disentangle age, period and cohort effects. Additionally, the Bayesian Age‐Period‐Cohort (BAPC) model was applied to project disease burden from 2022 to 2036.ResultsIn 2021, the global age‐standardised incidence rate (ASIR) was 21,668.4 per 100,000 population, showing a slight increase compared to 1990 (Estimated annual percentage change [EAPC] = 0.11). BAPC projections suggested a continued rise in global incidence by 2030, with females likely facing a higher burden than males.ConclusionThe burden of fungal skin diseases is closely linked to socioeconomic development, with resource‐limited regions bearing the highest risks. The projected upward trend underscores the urgent need for targeted public health interventions, particularly in strengthening prevention and management systems in low‐SDI countries. This study provides critical evidence to inform global strategies for mitigating the impact of fungal skin diseases.Keywords: age‐standardized incidence rate (ASIR), fungal skin diseases, global burden of disease (GBD)

Introduction

The global burden of dermatological diseases continues to escalate due to multiple factors including population ageing, accelerated urbanisation and environmental changes. Among these conditions, fungal skin diseases represent a prevalent category of cutaneous infections that pose significant public health challenges [1, 2, 3]. Early‐stage or mild infections typically manifest as localised pruritus, pain, erythema and swelling—symptoms that patients may tolerate and consequently neglect. However, immunocompromised individuals, elderly populations, infants and patients with immunodeficiency face substantially higher risks, where fungal infections may progress to invasive systemic involvement, increasing treatment complexity and potentially leading to life‐threatening complications.

Epidemiological evidence indicates that fungal dermatoses are influenced by multiple determinants. Warm and humid climates, ageing demographics and underdeveloped economic conditions have been identified as key contributors to disease endemicity. Furthermore, nations with predominant agricultural and livestock industries demonstrate higher disease prevalence. In resource‐limited settings, inadequate healthcare infrastructure, delayed treatment initiation and poorly regulated use of topical antifungals have collectively contributed to the emergence of drug‐resistant fungal strains. Ultimately, disease severity and progression are modulated by complex interactions between pathogen virulence factors and host immunological status [4].

While the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study provides crucial data for understanding disease impacts across global, regional and national levels, comprehensive epidemiological data on fungal skin diseases remains particularly scarce in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) [5, 6]. This knowledge gap significantly hinders the development and implementation of effective intervention strategies. The current study systematically analyses global trends in incidence, prevalence and disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) of fungal skin diseases from 1990 to 2021 using GBD data. Through application of Age‐Period‐Cohort (APC) modelling and Bayesian Age‐Period‐Cohort (BAPC) analysis, we further project future disease burden patterns across different regions. Our findings aim to provide evidence‐based support for global prevention and management strategies, with particular emphasis on informing targeted public health interventions in resource‐constrained settings.

Method

Data Sources

The present study employed data extracted from the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) [7, 8], the official data repository for the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study. All dermatological conditions were classified according to the standardised GBD diagnostic criteria and case definitions. Our analysis incorporated population‐level, cross‐sectional data from 204 countries and territories during the 1990–2021 study period. The dataset was systematically stratified by three key demographic parameters: (1) age groups (categorised using GBD standard age brackets), (2) biological sex (male/female) and (3) Socio‐demographic Index (SDI) quintiles (a composite measure of development status).

Descriptive Statistical Analysis

This study analysed the absolute changes and age‐standardised rates of incidence, DALYs and prevalence of fungal skin diseases globally from 1990 to 2021. The findings were presented using tabular data, trend line graphs, world maps (covering 204 countries and regions) and SDI stratification.

Joinpoint Regression Analysis

We employed Joinpoint regression analysis to assess annual trends in age‐standardised incidence rates (ASIR) from 1990 to 2021. Joinpoint regression is an advanced statistical method designed to identify multiple inflection points (termed ‘joinpoints’) in temporal trends, making it particularly suitable for analysing dynamic changes in disease burden over time [9]. The model utilises piecewise linear regression with least squares estimation, where optimal trend segments are determined by minimising the sum of squared residuals [10]. Analyses were performed using the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.4.0.0; National Cancer Institute, USA), a gold‐standard tool in epidemiological research that has been widely applied in public health studies to detect significant turning points in cancer and other disease trends [11].

APC Model

In this study, we employed the Poisson distribution‐based APC model to analyse temporal trends in the global incidence of fungal skin diseases. The APC model enables us to disentangle the effects of age, period and birth cohort, facilitating the identification of mortality changes within specific age groups over time and the influence of birth cohorts on these trends [12]. The fitting and analysis of the APC model were conducted using the APC Tool provided by the National Cancer Institute [13, 14] (available at: https://analysistools.cancer.gov/apc/).

BAPC Projection Analysis

We employed the BAPC model to perform Bayesian inference under the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) framework, predicting the global ASIR of fungal skin diseases from 2022 to 2036 [15]. This model integrates time‐series stationarity and future variation uncertainty, providing a 95% Uncertainty Interval (UI). Data processing and graphical visualisation were conducted in the R language environment (version 4.4.1), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Result

Epidemiological Trends of Fungal Skin Diseases: 1990–2021

In 2021, the global ASIR of fungal skin diseases was 21,668.4 per 100,000 population, showing a slight increase since 1990 (estimated annual percentage change [EAPC] = 0.11). However, stratification by SDI revealed marked disparities: high‐SDI countries exhibited a declining trend (EAPC = −0.15), whereas low‐middle and middle‐SDI countries experienced rising ASIR, with age‐standardised death rates (ASDR) following a similar pattern (Tables 1, 2). Epidemiologically, incident cases and DALYs in 2021 were predominantly concentrated in younger populations, peaking in the 5–14 age group, with males slightly more affected than females. Notably, low‐SDI countries bore the highest disease burden among those under 15 (Figures 1, 2), underscoring critical gaps in public health prevention. Longitudinal analysis (1990–2021) demonstrated pronounced SDI‐based gradients in ASIR, age‐standardised prevalence rates (ASPR) and ASDR, with low‐SDI nations consistently sustaining the highest burden (Figure 3). By 2021, these regions recorded an ASIR of 31,124.75 and ASDR of 71.80 per 100,000–1.93 and 2.55 times higher than high‐SDI counterparts (ASIR: 16,147.92; ASDR: 28.19), highlighting stark global inequities in fungal skin disease control.

Incidence, Prevalence and Burden of Fungal Skin Diseases at National and Regional Levels (1990–2021)

Fungal skin diseases demonstrate striking geographic heterogeneity, exhibiting a distinct ‘Africa > Asia > Europe/Americas’ gradient in disease burden. Sub‐Saharan Africa bears the highest global incidence, with Ethiopia recording an extraordinary ASIR of 45,535.04 per 105 population—nearly triple the global average—highlighting severe public health challenges in the region. Notably, several sub‐Saharan African nations—including Mali, Sudan, Niger and Nigeria—exhibited alarmingly high incidence rates, with ASIR consistently exceeding 40,000 cases per 100,000 population. Southeast Asian nations such as Indonesia (28,436.94), Vietnam (26,400.14) and Thailand (26,308.44) exhibit moderate‐to‐high incidence rates. In stark contrast, high‐SDI countries maintain substantially lower burdens, with China (14,263.04), the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Germany and Australia all demonstrating ASIRs below 15,000 per 105 (Figure 4). This pronounced north–south divide underscores how climate conditions, socioeconomic development and healthcare infrastructure collectively shape disease distribution patterns.

World map of ASIR age‐standardised incidence rates of fungal skin diseases, 2021.

The global burden of fungal skin diseases demonstrates pronounced disparities when stratified by SDI levels. Our analysis reveals a consistent inverse relationship between socioeconomic development and disease prevalence, with ASPR progressively declining as SDI increases. This pattern is particularly striking in low‐SDI nations—Niger, Ethiopia, Mali and Nigeria exhibit ASPRs exceeding 15,000 per 100,000 population, with Mali and Ethiopia surpassing 20,000 per 100,000, representing the highest global prevalence. These findings underscore the chronic, high‐prevalence nature of fungal dermatoses in resource‐limited settings.

A parallel trend emerges for incidence rates, which remain elevated in low‐SDI countries before plateauing in middle‐SDI regions and showing a modest uptick in high‐SDI areas. Somalia, Niger, Ethiopia and Mali bear the heaviest incidence burden (ASIR > 40,000 per 100,000), contrasting sharply with high‐SDI countries like Japan, Canada and Switzerland (ASIR < 20,000 per 100,000)– a divergence that highlights the effectiveness of advanced healthcare systems in disease control.

Mortality patterns (ASDR) mirror this socioeconomic stratification, with Somalia, Niger, Mali and Ethiopia experiencing the most severe outcomes, while high‐SDI nations including the United States, Germany, New Zealand and Sweden maintain the lightest global burden (Figure 5). These persistent gradients emphasise how health system capacity and socioeconomic determinants collectively shape the global epidemiology of fungal skin diseases.

Joinpoint Regression Analysis

Globally, the ASIR exhibited an initial increase followed by a decline and then a slight rebound. Specifically, ASIR rose from 1990 to 2001 (APC = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.15–0.17), slowed to an APC of 0.12 in subsequent years, then declined marginally from 2013 to 2019 (APC = −0.01), before rising again in 2019–2021 (APC = 0.24). Low‐SDI countries showed the most pronounced fluctuations, with a sharp increase from 1990 to 1993 (APC = 0.23) but a decline from 2016 to 2019 (APC = −0.26). Low‐middle‐SDI countries experienced a mild overall upward trend (APC ≈0.04), while middle‐SDI countries saw a sustained decline post‐2006. In contrast, High‐middle and high‐SDI nations demonstrated significant and steady reductions in ASIR since 1990, particularly high‐SDI countries, where the annual average percentage change (AAPC) was −0.16 for males and −0.10 for females, with both trends being statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Figure 6).

APC Analysis

Age Effect (Longitudinal Age Curve): Globally, disease incidence exhibits a marked increase with advancing age. Overall, incidence rates remain relatively low around age 20 but rise significantly after 60, surging sharply among individuals aged 80 and above. The age‐specific trends are similar between males and females, with incidence escalating rapidly in older age groups. However, the curve for males is slightly higher than that for females, particularly in the 85+ age group, where male incidence is significantly elevated (Figure 7).

Annual percentage change (APC) analysis of global fungal dermatosis incidence rates: (A) Overall, (B) Females, (C) Males.

Period Effect: From 1990 to 2020, the global period effect on disease burden remained relatively stable. The rate ratios (RRs) for both males and females fluctuated narrowly around 1, indicating minimal temporal variation in disease trends. Specifically, males showed a marginally elevated RR around the year 2000 before stabilising, while females exhibited a slight decline post‐2010. Although subtle period‐specific influences were observed, the overall magnitude of change was limited, suggesting that temporal factors had only a moderate impact on disease burden (Figure 7).

Cohort Effect: The cohort effect reflects shifts in disease risk across birth cohorts. Globally, early cohorts (born before 1900) had higher RRs, followed by a decline in the 1940–1960 birth cohorts. However, RRs gradually increased in later cohorts, particularly those born after 1970, peaking at approximately 1.12 for post‐2000 cohorts. This upward trend indicates rising relative risks among younger generations, signalling a potential increase in future disease burden. Both sexes exhibited similar patterns, though the rise was more pronounced in males (Figure 7).

Local Drift: Local drift illustrates age‐specific annual percentage changes in incidence. Overall, individuals aged 0–20 showed positive growth (~0.4% per year), while those aged 20–70 exhibited negative trends, with the steepest declines occurring in the 60+ population. Although male and female drift curves were similar, males demonstrated higher positive growth around age 20 and more pronounced negative declines beyond 60, suggesting greater volatility in disease burden changes for males in these age groups (Figure 7).

BAPC Projections

The BAPC model projections for global fungal skin disease incidence reveal a gradual upward trend for both males and females between 1990 and 2020, with rates increasing from approximately 20,000 to nearly 22,000 cases per 100,000 population. While the rise in incidence remained relatively stable during this period, the model predicts continued growth post‐2021. By 2030, the incidence rate is projected to exceed 24,000 per 100,000 for males and 25,000 per 100,000 for females.

Although some uncertainty exists in these projections, the overall trend clearly indicates a persistent increase in fungal skin disease incidence in the coming years, with a slightly more pronounced rise anticipated among females (Figure 8). This escalating disease burden underscores the need for strengthened public health policies focusing on prevention and control measures, particularly targeting high‐risk populations and specific age groups.

Discussion

This study conducted a comprehensive analysis of the global incidence, prevalence and temporal trends of fungal skin diseases from 1990 to 2021 using GBD data, with projections of future disease burden. The findings reveal a substantial increase in the global burden of fungal dermatoses over the past three decades, with marked disparities observed across regions, countries and by gender [16, 17, 18].

First and foremost, the global incidence and prevalence of fungal skin diseases has demonstrated a persistent upward trajectory, with this trend being particularly pronounced in regions characterised by low SDI levels.

Our findings reveal a striking escalation in disease burden across low and low‐middle‐SDI regions, where case numbers surged from 149 million in 1990 to 326 million in 2021, representing a dramatic 118% increase. This phenomenon is most acutely manifested in sub‐Saharan African nations, exemplified by low‐SDI countries such as Niger and Somalia where ASIR consistently exceed 40,000 per 100,000 population—markedly higher than global averages. This pronounced geographical disparity likely stems from multiple synergistic factors: tropical climatic conditions that foster fungal proliferation; inadequate basic sanitation infrastructure hindering infection control and limited accessibility to antifungal medications—all collectively exacerbating disease burden. Of particular concern is the dual challenge these regions face regarding scarce medical resources and underdeveloped public health systems, which substantially constrain effective disease containment efforts [19, 20].

In contrast, high‐SDI nations including Canada and Germany, while reporting considerable incidence rates (ASIR approximately 16,000–20,000 per 100,000), demonstrate relatively lower disease burden as evidenced by significantly reduced DALY metrics compared to low‐income countries. This divergence may be attributed to these countries' comprehensive healthcare systems, widespread disease screening programmes and standardised antifungal treatment protocols. Furthermore, high‐income countries generally benefit from robust public health infrastructure and health education initiatives, which collectively contribute to more effective disease management and control. Notably, our APC model analysis indicates that even in well‐resourced settings, elderly populations and specific birth cohorts exhibit elevated disease risks, underscoring the necessity for developing precisely targeted intervention strategies for these vulnerable demographic groups.

Notable gender disparities emerged as a key finding in this study. While global incidence rates were generally comparable between males and females overall, a significant divergence was observed in older populations—particularly among those aged 80 and above, where men exhibited markedly higher infection rates. This discrepancy may be attributed to age‐related immunological decline in men and/or their greater cumulative exposure to fungal infection risk factors (e.g., outdoor occupations and environmental exposures). Furthermore, longitudinal trends revealed an increasing disease burden among women in certain regions, with projections indicating that female incidence rates will rise more sharply than male rates in low‐SDI countries in the coming years.

Global disparities were also prominently reflected in the findings. For instance, high‐income North American countries like the United States and Canada exhibited higher crude incidence rates than other regions, yet demonstrated declining age‐standardised rates—suggesting the effectiveness of implemented prevention and control measures. In contrast, low‐ and middle‐income nations across Southeast Asia (e.g., Laos) and sub‐Saharan Africa (e.g., Solomon Islands) currently maintain relatively low incidence rates. However, accelerating urbanisation and evolving sanitation conditions may drive a substantial increase in disease burden across these regions in the coming years [21, 22].

Joinpoint regression analysis identified significant inflection points in the incidence and prevalence trends of fungal skin diseases. Globally, the fastest growth occurred between 1990 and 2001, followed by a plateau from 2001 to 2013. However, a notable resurgence emerged post‐2021, potentially attributable to climate change, rising antifungal resistance and fluctuating sanitation conditions. Notably, fungal infections were reported as the most prevalent cutaneous manifestations among COVID‐19 patients, suggesting a possible pandemic‐related influence [23, 24, 25]. This trend was particularly pronounced in low and low‐middle‐SDI regions, underscoring the urgent need for enhanced public health interventions in these vulnerable areas.

The APC analysis revealed a significant global increase in the incidence of fungal skin diseases with advancing age, particularly among elderly populations. The age effect clearly identified older adults as the primary bearers of disease burden, with males over 80 years showing markedly higher incidence rates than their female counterparts. Cohort effects demonstrated an elevated disease risk among younger birth cohorts compared to middle‐aged and older cohorts, suggesting a potential escalation of disease burden in future generations. While period effects exhibited temporal fluctuations, their overall impact on disease burden remained relatively modest. Local drift analysis highlighted distinct annual variation rates across age groups, with particularly pronounced risk increases observed in populations under 20 and over 80 years old. This trend may be associated with age‐related immune decline and prolonged exposure to environmental risk factors in older adults. Notably, cohort analysis identified a substantial rise in the relative risk of fungal skin diseases among individuals born after 1970, with this upward trend becoming especially prominent in post‐2000 birth cohorts. These findings suggest an impending increase in disease burden among younger generations.

BAPC modelling projects a continued rise in the global burden of fungal skin diseases, with a more pronounced increase anticipated among females. By 2030, the incidence rate is expected to exceed 24,000 cases per 100,000 population in males and 25,000 cases per 100,000 population in females. These projections indicate a persistent upward trend in the worldwide burden of fungal dermatoses, with women experiencing a more substantial escalation. This disparity may be attributed to gender‐based differences in healthcare access, exposure to infection risks and immunological responses.

While this study provides a comprehensive analysis of the global burden of fungal skin diseases, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the reliability of model estimates may have been affected by suboptimal data quality in certain low‐income countries. Additionally, heterogeneity in diagnostic criteria, reporting practices and healthcare resource availability across nations could introduce variability in the findings. Nevertheless, by leveraging GBD data, this research offers valuable evidence to support fungal dermatosis prevention and control strategies, particularly in resource‐limited settings.

In summary, our findings reveal a persistent and growing global burden of fungal skin diseases from 1990 to 2021, with particularly pronounced increases in incidence observed across LMICs. This concerning trend is projected to continue, with global incidence rates expected to rise further by 2030—a pattern that will disproportionately affect female populations. These global disparities underscore critical deficiencies in healthcare infrastructure, medical resources and capacity for early diagnosis and treatment in resource‐limited regions. Consequently, public health policies targeting these areas must prioritise both resource allocation and health system strengthening as urgent imperatives.

These regions require tailored prevention and intervention strategies that emphasise enhanced prophylactic measures and improved sanitary conditions. Simultaneously, there is a pressing need to strengthen disease surveillance systems and regulate antifungal treatment protocols to mitigate drug resistance. Projections indicate that future public health interventions should specifically address gender and regional disparities, with particular attention to enhancing health education and early screening programmes for women in low‐SDI regions.

These findings highlight the imperative for global health policies to rapidly adapt to evolving disease burden patterns, thereby addressing the potentially escalating public health challenges posed by fungal skin diseases in the coming years.

Conclusion

Based on the GBD data from 1990 to 2021, this study systematically examined the epidemiological characteristics and future trends of fungal skin diseases worldwide. The findings demonstrate a persistent global burden of fungal dermatoses, with an ASIR of 21,668.4 per 100,000 population in 2021, showing a slight increase compared to 1990 levels. Notably, substantial geographical disparities were observed, with sub‐Saharan African countries bearing the heaviest disease burden while high‐income nations maintained relatively lower incidence rates. Temporal trend analysis revealed more pronounced fluctuations in low (SDI) regions, contrasting with a consistent downward trend in high‐SDI countries. The APC model identified elevated risks among elderly populations and younger birth cohorts. Projections from the BAPC model indicate a concerning upward trajectory, with incidence rates potentially exceeding 24,000 per 100,000 by 2030. These findings underscore the urgent need for targeted prevention strategies in high‐risk regions and vulnerable populations, along with optimised allocation of healthcare resources to mitigate this growing public health challenge.
