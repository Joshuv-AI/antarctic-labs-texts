Silymarin-loaded phytosomal nanoparticles mitigate dyslipidemia, oxidative damage, and hepatic injury in high-fat diet-induced metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease in rats
Authors: Shahad W. Kattan, Amany I. Almars, Wajnat A. Tounsi, Areej Ahmed Turkistani, Ahmed M. Mohamed, Mona M. Elghareeb, Ekramy M. Elmorsy, Shaymaa H.E. Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh-fat diets (HFDs) play a central role in the onset and progression of metabolic syndrome. This study investigated the protective effects of silymarin (SM)-loaded phytosomal nanoparticles (SM-PNPs) against high-fat diet (HFD)-induced hepatic dysfunction and metabolic disorders, with emphasis on antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms.MethodsSixty rats were randomly divided into six groups: control, SM-treated (200 mg/kg body weight), SM-PNPs-treated (200 mg/kg body weight), HFD-treated, and two combination groups receiving HFD along with either SM or SM-PNPs (200 mg/kg body weight) for 5 weeks during the final phase of a 20-week HFD feeding period.ResultsSM-PNPs therapy demonstrated stronger hepatoprotective activity than unencapsulated SM. It significantly improved metabolic abnormalities, including insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and hepatopathy in HFD-fed rats. Moreover, SM-PNPs ameliorated HFD-induced alterations in fasting glucose and insulin levels and improved HOMA-IR indices, thereby restoring glucose homeostasis. Treatment also markedly reduced oxidative stress while enhancing the antioxidant defense system. RT-PCR analysis revealed significant downregulation of key inflammatory genes, including interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), along with a significant reduction in systemic inflammation, as indicated by decreased high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) levels, as well as apoptosis caspases activities. The in-silico analysis revealed significant interactions of SM with crucial proteins involved in regulating antioxidant defense and inflammatory pathways. Histopathological and ultrastructural examinations further supported these biochemical findings, showing that SM-PNPs attenuated hepatic steatosis, collagen deposition, and fibrosis.ConclusionsSM-PNPs exert a significant protective effect against HFD-induced hepatic damage and metabolic disturbances and represent a promising therapeutic strategy for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), potentially due to improved solubility, bioavailability, and sustained release in the nanoparticle formulation.Graphical Abstract The graphical abstract illustrates the protective effects of silymarin-loaded phytosomal nanoparticles (SM-PNPs) against liver and metabolic disorders induced by a high-fat diet (HFD). SM-PNPs significantly mitigate oxidative stress by enhancing the activities of glutathione peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, and catalase. They decrease the levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and CRP to inhibit inflammation and lower the levels of caspases-3, -8, and − 9 to prevent apoptosis. Additionally, SM-PNPs exhibit antifibrotic effects by decreasing TGF-β expression. These mechanisms collaborate to prevent hepatic steatosis, fibrosis, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD).

Background

Among chronic liver disease, alcoholic liver disease (ALD) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) are the most common both sharing the hallmark feature of hepatic fat accumulation [1]. ALD arises due to harmful alcohol consumption and is often complicated by psychiatric disorders, while MASLD develops independently of alcohol or drug use and spans a continuum from simple steatosis to more advanced stages such as steatohepatitis, cirrhosis, fibrosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma [2]. The global prevalence of MASLD has increased significantly in recent years, largely driven by modern lifestyle changes and influenced by factors such as environmental exposures, diet, metabolic status, and genetic factors [3]. It frequently occurs in association with obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, which are strongly linked to metabolic syndrome components [4]. Current management of the disease focus on modifying lifestyle and managing symptoms, with limited strategies aimed at the core pathophysiological pathways, including oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways [5]. Consequently, growing attention has been directed toward natural products possessing antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, aimed at alleviating hepatic oxidative stress and inflammation associated with MASLD and high-fat diet (HFD)-induced hepatic injury [6].

Flavonoids, a prominent subgroup of polyphenols, exert wide-ranging health benefits, including reduction of cardiovascular mortality [7], and favorable impacts on diabetes outcomes [8]. Silymarin (SM), a flavonoid mixture obtained from the fruits and seeds of milk thistle (Silybum marianum), is notable for its multiple biological properties, such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antiapoptosis, antiglycation, and anticancer activities, with hepatoprotection being its most recognized property [9]. Moreover, SM has been shown to alleviate HFD-induced metabolic disorders, including atherosclerosis, MASLD, insulin resistance, and obesity [10]. Despite these benefits, its therapeutic application is compromised by low oral bioavailability (23–47%) and poor water solubility (50–430 µg/mL), which limit systemic absorption and overall effectiveness [11].To improve its oral absorption and therapeutic efficacy, SM has been formulated using several strategies: solid dispersion incorporation, β-cyclodextrin complexation, and prodrug derivatization,, in addition to targeted delivery systems such as floating tablets, microspheres, nanoparticles, phytosomes, and liposomes [12]. Phytosome technology, first introduced in 1989, is based on binding water-soluble phytoconstituents such as polyphenols to phospholipids, forming lipid-compatible complexes. This structural modification improves stability and, bioavailability, as the lipid-oriented design facilitates penetration through lipid-rich biological membranes and improves systemic uptake compared to crude herbal extracts [13]. In phytosome formulations, phospholipids not only act as carriers but also provide additional hepatoprotective activity, which synergistically improves the therapeutic potential of SM. The key interaction is mediated by hydrogen bonds between phytoconstituents and the polar regions of phospholipids, imparting improved stability compared to standard liposomal systems [14]. Furthermore, the application of nanoscale drug delivery offers clear superiority over standard drug formulations, where reduced particle dimensions contributes to accelerated dissolution, improved solubility, and, in many cases, enhanced in vivo outcomes [13]. The novelty of the present study lies in developing silymarin-loaded phytosomal nanoparticles (SM-PNPs), which combine the advantages of phytosome technology with nanoscale delivery, resulting in superior bioavailability, stability, and hepatoprotective efficacy compared to other nanoformulations of SM. Therefore, the present study aimed to develop an oral SM phytosome and assess the hepatoprotective and metabolic benefits of SM-PNPs against HFD–induced fatty liver in rats, in comparison with unencapsulated SM, highlighting the enhanced therapeutic potential of the nanosized phytosomal system.

Methods

Molecular docking

Molecular docking of Silymarin with target proteins was conducted using SwissDock (https://www.swissdock.ch/), employing the EADock DSS engine. The target protein structures were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) [PDB IDs: specify here for Superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) are key antioxidant enzymes, while tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β)]. Prior to docking, all water molecules were removed, and polar hydrogens were added to the protein structures to improve docking accuracy. The chemical structure of Silymarin was obtained from PubChem (CID: specify). Docking simulations were performed using default SwissDock parameters, and the best binding poses were selected based on the lowest binding free energy (ΔG, kcal/mol). Visualization and interaction analysis were performed directly through SwissDock, including hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, to elucidate key residues involved in ligand binding. Docking validation was conducted by redocking co-crystallized ligands into their respective proteins to ensure the reliability of the docking protocol.

Preparation of SM-PNPs

Silymarin (> 98% purity) was obtained from the Arab Company for Pharmaceuticals and Medicinal Plants (MEPACO-MED), Cairo, Egypt. An appropriate amount of SM and Soy Phosphatidylcholine (SPC) were weighed in a molar ratio of 1:5 and dissolved in 20 mL of anhydrous ethanol. The mixture was stirred continuously at a temperature below 25 °C for 2 h. The solvent was then removed under vacuum, and the dried residues were transferred to a desiccator overnight. The obtained SM–SPC complex was collected and stored at room temperature. To prepare phytosomes, the SM–SPC complex was dissolved in anhydrous ethanol and loaded into a 250 mL round-bottom flask. A thin lipid film was formed using a rotary evaporator at 180 rpm under vacuum. The film was hydrated and subjected to intense vortex mixing for 2 min, followed by probe sonication for 4 min at 60% amplitude with alternating 5-second on/off pulses to reduce vesicle size to the nanoscale range. The resulting nanosized phytosomal suspension (SM-PNPs) was stored at 4 °C for at least 24 h prior to further analysis. Formulation optimization was performed by varying the concentration of the SM–SPC complex (0.2%–5%) and the SM-to-phospholipid molar ratio (1:5, 1:10, and 1:15) to obtain stable and uniform nanosized phytosomes. Drug loading (DL,%) and loading efficiency (LE,%) were determined by quantifying the free SM in the supernatant after centrifugation, calculated as follows:

LE (%) = [Total free (unencapsulated) drug] / [Total drug] × 100.

DL (%) = [Total free (unencapsulated) drug] / [Total weight of nanoparticles] × 100.

Animals and diets

Sixty adult male albino rats, averaging 184.02 ± 9.98 g in weight, were obtained from the Faculty of Medicine, Mansoura University, Egypt. Only adult male albino rats were used in the present study to avoid potential variability associated with sex hormones and their known influence on lipid metabolism, inflammatory responses, and the progression of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease. Animals were allowed a two-week acclimatization period before the experiment. They were housed in stainless steel cages under standard laboratory conditions, including a temperature of 21–25 °C, a 12-hour light/dark cycle, and relative humidity maintained at 50–60%. During the study, all rats were provided ad libitum access to commercial rodent chow (Al Wadi Co., Giza, Egypt) and water. All procedures were performed in line with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and adhered to the International Guiding Principles for Biomedical Research Involving Animals (1985).The normal diet consisted of Laboratory rodent diet 5001 (23% crude protein, 56% carbohydrate, 6% crude fiber, 8% ash, 4.5% crude fat, and 2.5% added minerals), with a caloric content of 3.36 kcal/g. The high-fat diet (HFD) was formulated from 35% beef tallow and 65% Laboratory rodent diet, yielding 5.53 kcal/g. Diets were kept at 4 °C prior to experimentation [15].

Experimental protocol

The animals were randomly allocated into six groups (n = 10 per group). Group I served as the normal control and was maintained on a standard laboratory rodent diet. Group II (SM group) received the standard diet and was orally administered silymarin at a dose of 200 mg/kg body weight during the final 5 weeks of the 20-week feeding period. Group III (SM-PNPs group) received the standard diet and was given 200 mg/kg body weight of SM-loaded phytosomal nanoparticles by oral gavage during the final 5 weeks of the 20-week feeding period. Group IV (HFD group) was fed a high-fat diet (HFD) for 20 weeks, as described by [16]. A high-fat diet (HFD)-induced rodent model was employed to mimic MASLD-like hepatic steatosis, as it reliably reproduces key metabolic and histopathological features observed in human metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease. Groups V and VI were fed an HFD for 20 weeks and co-administered either SM or SM-PNPs, respectively, at the previously mentioned dose (200 mg/kg body weight) by daily oral gavage during the last 5 weeks. The selected dose (200 mg/kg) was based on prior studies demonstrating the efficacy and safety of silymarin in experimental models of hepatic injury and metabolic dysfunction. Importantly, the same dose was applied to both free silymarin and SM-loaded phytosomal nanoparticles to ensure a direct and unbiased comparison of formulation-dependent therapeutic effects. The silymarin dose was chosen according to earlier reports demonstrating its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities [16].

Sample collection and tissue homogenate preparation

Rats were fasted overnight and humanely euthanized 24 h after the final treatment under deep anesthesia induced with ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), followed by cervical dislocation. Blood was collected from the hepatic portal vein, transferred to non-heparinized tubes, clotted at room temperature, and centrifuged (3000 × g, 10 min) to separate serum. The obtained serum was distributed into Eppendorf tubes and promptly subjected to biochemical testing. Liver specimens were carefully harvested, washed with chilled 1.15% KCl solution to remove blood remnants, and preserved at − 20 °C until further analysis. For histopathological evaluation, a representative liver section was fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. Primary hepatocytes were isolated from a portion of the liver using a two-step collagenase perfusion method, and cell viability and purity were evaluated by trypan blue exclusion (> 80% viable cells). Liver homogenates prepared in 50 mmol/L Tris–HCl buffer (pH 7.4) were centrifuged (10,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C). Supernatants were collected and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent biochemical analyses.

Body weight, liver index, and epididymal fat

The body weight of each rat was recorded at the start of the study and again at the end of the 20-week period. During sacrifice, the liver was rapidly removed, weighed, and used to determine the liver index (liver weight/body weight × 100%). Epididymal fat was also dissected, weighed, and used as an indicator of visceral adipose tissue.

Serum biochemical analysis and hepatic redox status

Serum biochemical parameters, including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), and glucose, were measured using photometric methods with commercial auto-analyzer kits, following the procedures described by Raja et al. (2007). Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels were calculated using the Friedewald formula, with all lipoproteins expressed in mg/dL. Serum insulin was quantified using a sandwich ELISA method with a Biovendor kit. Insulin resistance was assessed by calculating the HOMA-IR index with the equation: [glucose (mmol/L) × insulin (mU/L)] / 22.5. The enzymatic activities of glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) in hepatic tissues were determined using colorimetric assay kits (BioDiagnostic, Egypt) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines: GSH-Px (Cat. No. GSH-Px 2524), SOD (Cat. No. SOD 2521), and CAT (Cat. No. CA 2517). They were measured spectrophotometrically: SOD via NBT inhibition at 560 nm, CAT via H₂O₂ decomposition at 510 nm, and GSH-Px via NADPH oxidation at 340 nm. Lipid peroxidation was assessed by measuring malondialdehyde (MDA) levels using a thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) kit (Cat. No. MD 2529, BioDiagnostic, Egypt) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Hepatic levels of transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) were assessed in liver tissue homogenates using a commercially available ELISA kit following the manufacturer’s protocols.

Flow cytometry studies

The DCF H2DCFDA (Abcam, Cat No. ab113851) fluorescent assay was conducted to quantify liver ROS levels, adhering to manufacturer guidelines. The flowcytometry was conducted across four experimental groups: Control, High Fat Diet (HFD), HFD with Silymarin supplementation, and HFD/SM-PNPs. The analysis was limited to these groups as oxidative stress assessment was specifically aimed at evaluating HFD-induced alterations and the potential protective effects of Silymarin and its nanoparticle formulation. Accordingly, non-HFD-treated SM and SM-PNPs groups were not included in this assay, as oxidative stress was not anticipated in the absence of HFD-induced metabolic insults. Liver samples were meticulously excised, weighed, and homogenized in ice-cold PBS using gentle mechanical disruption to obtain tissue lysates. Then, samples were centrifuged at 10,000 x g for 10 min at 4 °C and debris were removed. Supernatants were incubated with 10 µM DCF H2DCFDA dye at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark to allow chemical oxidation of the probe by ROS present in the lysate. Following incubation, samples were diluted and analyzed using flow cytometry on a BD FACSCanto II (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA). Fluorescence emission was quantified at 525 nm following excitation at 488 nm. Data analysis was conducted using FlowJo v10 (BD Biosciences). ROS levels were quantified based on the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of DCF in the tissue homogenate. Since freshly isolated hepatocytes were not easily accessible, H2DCFDA was used in the current investigation on liver homogenates as a quick indirect technique to assess oxidative stress in various groups. H2DCFDA is commonly used in live cells for the measurement of intracellular ROS. Cellular compartmentalization is lost, even though this enables a consistent evaluation of the overall ROS burden. The updated manuscript acknowledges this methodological shortcoming.

Fluorometric assays of caspases activities

Fluorometric assay kits (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; Caspase-3: Cat. No. ab39401, Caspase-8: Cat. No. ab39700, Caspase-9: Cat. No. ab65608) were utilized to assess the activities of Caspase-3, -8, and − 9. Frozen liver samples were thawed on ice, rinsed with cold PBS, and subsequently mixed with the provided lysis buffer. Tissue lysates were maintained on ice for 10 min and subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to achieve clarity. Supernatants were collected, and the Bradford assay was employed to quantify the protein concentration. Equal amounts of proteins were combined with specific caspase substrates in the assay buffer: DEVD-AFC for caspase-3, IETD-AFC for caspase-8, and LEHD-AFC for caspase-9. Reaction mixtures were maintained at 37 °C for one hour in the absence of light. A microplate reader measured fluorescence at an excitation wavelength of 400 nm and an emission wavelength of 505 nm. Caspase activity was measured in relative fluorescence units (RFU) and compared among experimental groups.

Quantitative analysis of inflammatory gene expression

RNA was isolated from liver tissues using 1 mL QIAzol Lysis Reagent and homogenization with a Tissuelyser II. Chloroform was then added, and the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min to separate the phases. The aqueous phase was transferred to new tubes, and RNA was precipitated using isopropanol, followed by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 10 min). The resulting pellet was washed with ethanol after removing the isopropanol. Ethanol residues were cleared by centrifugation (7,500 × g, 5 min). The resulting RNA was dissolved in DNase/RNase-free water, and its concentration was measured. In the subsequent RT-PCR step, complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from total RNA using the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For the third RT-PCR stage, cDNA was mixed with iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, 172–5121) and primers targeting IL-1β (interleukin-1β), IL-6 (interleukin-6), TNF-α (tumor necrosis factor-alpha), iNOS (inducible nitric oxide synthase), and NF-κB (nuclear factor kappa) (Table 1) were designed and synthesized by Macrogen (Seoul, South Korea). Primers were dissolved in nuclease-free water to preserve their stability and prevent nucleic acid degradation. Real-time PCR amplification was carried out with the Rotor-Gene Q system (Qiagen), and relative expression was determined using the 2^–ΔΔCt method described by Livak and Schmittgen (2001).

Histological evaluation of liver

Tissues designated for histological examination were collected and immersed in 10% formalin at a 20:1 formalin-to-tissue volume ratio. After fixation for three days, the tissues were dehydrated through ascending grades of alcohol and subsequently cleared with xylene. After clearing, the tissue samples were immersed in molten paraffin for one hour to allow complete infiltration, followed by preparation of tissue blocks. Sections were cut with a rotary microtome, dehydrated through a series of alcohols, stained with eosin and hematoxylin or Masson and examined under light microscopy [17, 18]. Histological evaluation was conducted using the Nonalcoholic Steatohepatitis Activity Score (NAS) in alignment with the standards set by the NASH Clinical Research Network (CRN) [19]. Collagen deposition and fibrosis were assessed by staining supplementary sections with Masson’s Trichrome. The CRN fibrosis staging system (F0–F4) was employed for fibrosis staging [20] (Table 2). The quantitative collagen proportionate area was measured using ImageJ software.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

Liver tissues were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (0.1 M phosphate buffer) at 4 °C for 24 h, rinsed thoroughly, and post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h. The samples underwent stepwise dehydration in ascending ethanol concentrations (50%–100%), were cleared in acetone, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin Sects. (60–70 nm) were produced with an ultramicrotome, double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined using a JEOL 2100 transmission electron microscope at 160 kV.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to confirm data normality, and Levene’s test was used to assess equality of variances. Statistical evaluation was performed through one-way ANOVA (SAS, 2012; Proc ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Values are presented as mean ± SD, with statistical significance accepted at p < 0.05. Graphical illustrations were prepared in GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Multivariate analyses, including heatmap clustering and principal component analysis (PCA), were conducted via SRplot (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/en).

Results

Molecular docking

The docking analysis of silymarin with target proteins reveals significant binding affinities and interaction patterns, indicating potential therapeutic effects. TNF exhibited the highest affinity, characterized by a binding energy of -8.1 kcal/mol, two hydrogen bonds with Leu120 and Ser60, and five hydrophobic interactions involving Tyr119 and Tyr59. Silymarin exhibited strong interactions with CAT and GSH-PX, with a binding energy of -7.5 kcal/mol, characterized by six and four hydrogen bonds, respectively, along with multiple hydrophobic contacts. Binding energies for IL-1β and SOD were moderate, recorded at -5.4 and − 6.7 kcal/mol, respectively, attributed to a reduced number of hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions (Table 3; Fig. 1).

Characterization of Silymarin -loaded phytosomal nanoparticles (SM-PNPs)

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis (Fig. 2A) showed that SM-PNPs were spherical, uniform, and well-dispersed, with particle sizes ranging from 44 to 132 nm (Fig. 2B). In contrast, dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements indicated a larger hydrodynamic diameter of 187 nm, reflecting solvent-associated hydration and particle-medium interactions. The polydispersity index (PDI) was measured at 0.403 (Fig. 2C), indicating a moderate degree of polydispersity rather than uniform size distribution, as PDI values below 0.1 are considered highly monodisperse, 0.1–0.3 indicate acceptable uniformity, and values above 0.3 reflect increased heterogeneity, particularly in lipid-based and phytosomal nanocarrier systems [17]. Additionally, the zeta potential of -34 mV (Fig. 2D) confirmed the nanoparticles’ colloidal nature stability. The formulation exhibited a loading efficiency of 87.13% and a drug loading of 17.27%, confirming successful encapsulation of SM within the phytosomal nanoparticles. These favorable characteristics indicate good physical stability and suggest an improved potential for SM bioavailability, supporting their effectiveness in therapeutic applications.

Body Weight, Liver Index, and Epididymal Fat

The results of final body weight, liver index, and epididymal fat percentage are shown in Figs. 3A–C. Rats fed HFD showed a significant increase in body weight gain compared to the normal control and the groups treated with SM or SM-PNPs alone (p < 0.05). Co-treatment with either SM or SM-PNPs did not produce a significant decrease in body weight gain compared to the HFD treated group (p > 0.05). In terms of liver index and epididymal fat percentage, both were significantly elevated in HFD-fed rats compared to all control groups (p < 0.05). Treatment with either form of SM significantly decreased these parameters, with the nanoform restoring values close to those of the negative control, showing no significant difference (p > 0.05). These findings indicate SM-PNPs effectively alleviate HFD-induced hepatic and adipose changes, reflecting its improved therapeutic efficacy.

SM and SM-PNPs in HFD-induced inflammatory pathways

In this study, iNOS, a key mediator of inflammation and nitrosative stress, was significantly upregulated in the hepatic tissue of HFD-treated rats compared to controls (P < 0.05; Fig. 6A). Treatment with SM-PNPs significantly attenuated this elevation, highlighting its potential in alleviating nitrosative stress. In parallel, NF-κB, a key transcription factor regulating inflammatory cytokines, was upregulated significantly in HFD-fed rats (p < 0.05; Fig. 6B). Administration of SM and SM-PNPs reduced NF-κB expression, with the nanoform restoring it to control levels, demonstrating superior anti-inflammatory effects. Regarding proinflammatory cytokines, the expression of inflammation-related genes, including IL-1β (Fig. 6C), IL-6 (Fig. 6D), and TNF-α (Fig. 6E), was significantly upregulated in the hepatic tissue of HFD-treated rats compared with controls (P < 0.05). Co-treatment with either SM or SM-PNPs significantly reduced the expression of these genes (p < 0.05), minimizing in the HFD/SM-PNPs treated group. Regarding systemic inflammation, hsCRP levels were significantly elevated in HFD-treated rats compared to the negative control. Co-administration of SM or SM-PNPs with HFD significantly reduced hsCRP levels, with the HFD/SM-PNPs treated group showing values close to those of the negative control, exhibiting no significant difference (p < 0.05; Fig. 7A). In terms of antifibrotic effects of SM, hepatic Transforming Growth Factor Beta (TGF-β) levels were significantly elevated in HFD-treated rats compared with the controls (p < 0.05). Co-treatment with either SM or SM-PNPs significantly reduced these levels (p < 0.05), with the most pronounced reduction observed in the HFD/SM-PNPs treated group, where TGF-β values were not significantly different from those of the control groups (Fig. 7B).

SM and SM-PNPs in HFD-induced histopathological damage

Examination of liver tissue indicated that treatment with SM or SM-PNPs did not induce any histopathological alterations. The lobular structure was preserved, central veins appeared unaltered, and hepatocytes exhibited typical polygonal shapes with centrally positioned nuclei and eosinophilic cytoplasm (Figs. 9A–C). Considerable histopathological damage was showed in the HFD-fed-rats group, characterized by central vein congestion, hepatocellular vacuolar degeneration, and widespread nuclear necrosis (Fig. 9D). Treatment with either SM or SM-PNPs effectively ameliorated these lesions, preserving lobular architecture and orderly arrangement of hepatic cords (Figs. 9E–F).

Hepatic fibrosis was assessed by Masson’s trichrome staining (Fig. 10). The control group exhibited fine collagen fibers surrounding the central vein. Similarly, the SM and SM-PNPs groups showed minimal collagen deposition comparable to the control. In contrast, the HFD group demonstrated a marked increase in collagen fiber accumulation. Notably, treatment with SM and SM-PNPs in HFD-fed rats resulted in a clear reduction in collagen deposition around the central vein and between hepatocytes.

Administration of a high-fat diet (HFD) to rats resulted in notable steatohepatitis and fibrosis, evidenced by an elevated NAS of 6.8 (6.0–8.0), increased steatosis at 2.8 (2.0–3.0), heightened lobular inflammation at 2.4 (2.0–3.0), and increased hepatocyte ballooning at 1.6 (1.0–2.0) (Table 5). Additionally, there was a significant increase in collagen deposition (29.4 ± 9.8%), a Masson score of 3, and a CRN stage of F2–F3 (p < 0.001 compared to control). The co-administration of SM resulted in a partial improvement in liver histology (NAS 3.9 ± 0.9; collagen 15.6 ± 5.4%, Masson 2, F1–F2; p < 0.01). In contrast, SM-PNPs significantly enhanced both steatohepatitis and fibrosis (NAS 2.3 ± 1.0; collagen 7.1 ± 2.9%, Masson 1, F0–F1; p < 0.05), nearing normal liver architecture. The control, SM, and SM-PNPs groups demonstrated minimal histological alterations. The results indicate that SM, particularly in nanoparticle form, effectively reduces NASH and liver fibrosis associated with a high-fat diet (Table 6).

SM and SM-PNPs in HFD-induced ultrastructural alteration

Figures 11A–C illustrate that hepatocytes from the normal control and SM- or SM-PNPs -treated rats exhibited typical ultrastructure, characterized by structurally intact mitochondria, orderly rough endoplasmic reticulum, and round nuclei with clearly visible nucleoli. Hepatocytes from HFD-fed rats exhibited severe ultrastructural alterations, such as chromatin condensation, dilated rough endoplasmic reticulum, nuclear deformation, extensive vacuolation, mitochondria with disrupted cristae and cytoplasmic disintegration (Fig. 11D). Co-treatment with SM (Fig. 11E) or SM-PNPs (Fig. 11F) substantially alleviated these changes, with hepatocytes displaying minimal mitochondrial alterations, mostly intact organelles, and only slight vacuolation, demonstrating effective hepatoprotective action.

Discussion

Over the past decades, the global consumption of high-fat and energy-dense foods has increased significantly, posing a significant threat to public health. Excessive intake of dietary fats and calories plays a key role in the onset of obesity and metabolic syndrome, both of which are major risk factors for diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular diseases [21]. Studies have shown that dietary habits, particularly high-calorie diets, are strongly associated with increased hepatic fat levels [22, 23]. Metabolic dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD) is defined by steatosis of the liver confirmed by imaging or histology, after excluding secondary factors such as steatogenic drug use, alcohol abuse, or genetic conditions [2]. This study employed high-fat diets (HFD) to induce MASLD and silymarin (SM) was evaluated for its hepatoprotective potential through its reported antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antilipidemic, antiproliferative, antifibrotic effects, and mitochondrial protective action [24, 25].

In line with previously approved experimental systems, the current 20-week high-fat diet (HFD) protocol effectively created a model of diet-induced metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), which is characterized by hepatic steatosis, lobular inflammation, and early fibrotic changes. According to a wealth of evidence, C57BL/6 mice fed a high-fat diet for an extended length of time develop progressive hepatic lipid accumulation, inflammatory infiltration, and mild to moderate fibrosis, depending on the diet’s composition and duration. This closely resembles early-stage human MASLD rather than advanced cirrhosis [26]. Longer exposure to dietary fat (20–24 weeks) has been found to cause steatosis with early inflammatory and collagen deposition alterations, although more severe fibrosis typically requires longer time or additional dietary or chemical stressors [27]. The fact that HFD-based protocols consistently result in steatosis and inflammatory characteristics, with the degree of fibrosis being variable and model-dependent, is further supported by recently published systematic reviews of diet-induced MASLD models [27]. The successful induction of MASLD was validated by histological results from H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining, NAS score, and biochemical alterations seen in the current investigation. As is frequently observed in HFD-only models that more accurately reflect early MASLD than end-stage disease, the mild-to-moderate collagen deposition is consistent with early fibrotic remodeling.

The current study molecular docking of silymarin with proteins associated with inflammation and oxidative stress indicates its therapeutic potential due to favorable binding interactions. TNF exhibited the highest binding affinity at -8.1 kcal/mol, facilitated by two hydrogen bonds and five hydrophobic interactions indicating significant modulation of TNF activity. Silymarin demonstrated interactions with catalase (CAT) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-PX), exhibiting binding energies of -7.5 kcal/mol along with multiple hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts, indicating an enhancement of the antioxidant defense system. Moderate affinities for IL-1β and SOD (-5.4 and − 6.7 kcal/mol, respectively) suggest a significant interaction that may enhance anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. The docking results indicate that silymarin directly interacts with pro-inflammatory cytokines and antioxidant enzymes, thereby supporting its potential as a multi-targeted therapeutic agent. Hence, further in-vivo experiments were conducted in the study to validate the in-silico docking outcomes. Therapeutic value of SM is limited due to its poor oral bioavailability and water solubility [28]. Nanotechnology has emerged as a valuable tool to overcome these limitations, enhancing stability, safety, efficacy, and targeted delivery of therapeutics [29]. In this study, we prepared silymarin-loaded phytosomes nanoparticles (SM-PNPs) and assessed their protective effects in a high-fat diet–induced liver injury model.

In this study, 20 weeks of HFD consumption led significant increase in body weight gain, along with hyperlipidemia. Steatohepatitis was also observed, confirmed through histological and biochemical examinations. Administration of SM-PNPs for 5 weeks had no significant effect on overall body weight but significantly reduced epididymal fat. In line with this, the present results showed a significant increase in liver weight in the HFD treated rats compared with normal controls. Similar results have been observed previously, demonstrating that HFD significantly increase liver mass [30]. This finding may be explained by ectopic fat accumulation in the liver. In support of this, a previous study reported that 5 weeks of SM administration significantly reduced liver weight gain induced by 12 weeks of a HFD [31], which is generally consistent with our findings showing that SM-PNPs significantly decreased relative liver weight.

SM and SM-PNPs attenuate HFD-induced insulin resistance

The present results indicate that HFD induces insulin resistance and reproduces the metabolic profile of type 2 diabetes, such as elevated blood glucose and body weight gain [32]. Several reports have highlighted HFD as an effective approach for inducing insulin resistance [33], which is attributed to augmented hepatic glucose production, decreased peripheral tissue sensitivity to insulin, and impaired insulin secretion by the pancreas [34]. In the diabetic liver, glucose metabolism is disrupted, showing impaired glycogenesis, decreased glycolysis, and upregulated gluconeogenesis [35].

In our study, administration of SM-PNPs improved insulin sensitivity, as evidenced by improved insulin and glucose profiles as well as the calculated HOMA-IR. Previous studies have indicated that SM improves endocrine function, enhances pancreatic morphology, and stimulates antioxidant enzyme activity [36], resulting in lower circulating insulin and glucose levels. Likewise, silibinin has been reported to reduce HOMA-IR in mice subjected to a methionine–choline-deficient diet [37]. Moreover, the beneficial effects of SM on hepatic steatosis [38] may help ameliorate insulin resistance, as excessive hepatic lipid accumulation is strongly linked to reduced insulin sensitivity [39]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the hepatoprotective effects of SM and SM-PNPs may contribute to the observed improvement in insulin resistance, reflecting the tight interplay between hepatic lipid accumulation and glucose homeostasis.

SM and SM-PNPs attenuate HFD-associated steatohepatitis

Multiple reports have shown that supplementation with SM enhances lipid metabolism in animal models by reducing total cholesterol (TC), low density lipoproteins (LDL-C), very low density lipoproteins (VLDL), and liver triglyceride (TG) levels, in part by inhibiting intestinal cholesterol absorption [40]. In line with previous findings, rats received HFD exhibited dyslipidemia, characterized by increased TG, TC, and LDL-C. Treatment with SM-PNPs significantly decreased TG, and TC. Although HDL-C values showed a modest numerical increase in the HFD group compared to the control, this difference did not reach statistical significance, and no significant variation was observed among all experimental groups. Therefore, the observed changes in HDL-C should be interpreted cautiously and do not support a definitive effect of SM-PNPs on HDL modulation. Taken together, the present results indicate that SM-PNPs exert a dual hepatoprotective role by ameliorating dyslipidemia, regulating lipid metabolism, and safeguarding hepatocytes against HFD-induced hepatic damage. Consumption of a HFD enhances fatty acid synthesis in the liver and promotes the entry of free fatty acids, while impairing their β-oxidation. The resulting lipid accumulation in hepatocytes can damage the liver parenchyma and disrupt cellular function. Elevated levels of intracellular enzymes, such as transaminases (ALT and AST), in the blood are among the most sensitive biomarkers of hepatocyte injury. In this study, treatment with SM-PNPs significantly mitigated the HFD-induced increases in ALT, AST, and gamma-glutamyl transferase, what in general agreement with prior study’s results [40].

SM and SM-PNPs control HFD-induced oxidative damage and fibrosis

Oxidative stress, resulting from an imbalance toward oxidation, may serve adaptive functions under mild conditions but contributes to liver diseases such as Wilson’s disease, alcoholic liver disease, MASLD, and hepatitis C under severe conditions [41]. Rodent HFD models reproduce multiple hallmarks of liver pathology, including oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, insulin resistance, steatosis and depletion of n-3 LCPUFAs, establishing them as a useful platform for investigating pathophysiology and testing nutritional interventions [42]. Herein, HFD-treated rats exhibited elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, reflecting enhanced lipid peroxidation, along with reduced hepatic antioxidant enzyme activities. SM-PNPs administration significantly restored GSH and the activities of GSH-Px, SOD, and CAT while markedly reducing MDA concentrations compared to untreated HFD rats, proving more effective than free SM. The present results are consistent with the documented antioxidant properties of SM, which include enhancing RNA transcription, promoting polymerase activity, and protecting cell membranes from oxidative stress [43]. In patients with liver disorders, SM has been observed to improve SOD activity in both erythrocytes and lymphocytes, and to elevate circulating levels of GSH and GSH-Px [44]. SM’s antioxidant effects are largely mediated by its ability to scavenge reactive oxygen species, leading to increased intracellular glutathione, stabilization of cell membranes, inhibition of lipid peroxidation, and steroid-like modulation of nuclear expression [44].

Additionally, SM reduces collagen fiber accumulation by blocking the activation of stellate hepatocytes into myofibroblasts, reducing the risk of cirrhosis development [45]. In accordance with these mechanisms, our results revealed notable antifibrotic effects of SM, evidenced by lower levels of hepatic TGF-β as well as fibrosis levels in Masson-stained slides in SM-PNPs-treated rats relative to HFD-treated rats. The present results align with a previous study, which reported that HFD-treated rats exhibited elevated hepatic TGF-β levels, reflecting oxidative increased fibrosis risk [46]. Treatment with SM significantly restored GSH levels, while decreasing MDA and TGF-β levels, underscoring its antifibrotic and antioxidant benefits.

SM and SM-PNPs control HFD-induced inflammation

In addition to boosting antioxidant markers and mitigating hepatic oxidative damage, SM is recognized for its potent anti-inflammatory properties [47]. It achieves this primarily by decreasing iNOS expression and blocking NF-κB activation, leading to reduced pro-inflammatory signaling under different pathological contexts [48]. It further mitigates inflammation by suppressing key pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α, as well as inhibiting inflammasome activation, while simultaneously improving anti-inflammatory signaling [49]. In this study, HFD treated rats exhibited significant upregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, NF-κB, and iNOS,, contributing to hepatic inflammation. SM-PNPs treatment exerted superior anti-inflammatory benefits compared to crude SM, evidenced by marked downregulation of hepatic IL-1 β, IL-6, TNF-α, NF-κB, and iNOS gene expression.

Given that CRP is a hepatic acute-phase protein that increases during inflammation [50], and high-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) provides a reliable marker of low-grade systemic inflammation, interventions targeting inflammatory pathways can effectively lower hs-CRP levels. By downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6 and TNF-α [51], SM inhibits hepatic CRP synthesis, leading to decreased hs-CRP levels. This aligns with the current results, in which SM-PNPs administration significantly lowered CRP levels. These results are in agreement with another study, which reported that hs-CRP levels were markedly elevated in HFD-fed rats and decreased by 51.80% and 59.43% following 4 and 11 weeks of SM therapy, respectively [52].

SM and SM-PNPs control HFD-induced apoptosis

Administration of a high-fat diet (HFD) to rats markedly stimulated apoptotic pathways in hepatocytes, evidenced by a substantial rise in caspase-9 and caspase-3 activities, alongside a lesser increase in caspase-8 activities. This pattern suggests a primary activation of the intrinsic (mitochondrial) apoptotic pathway, likely triggered by oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and lipid accumulation in hepatocytes [53]. Elevated caspase-9 activity indicates that the mitochondrial outer membrane has become permeable, resulting in the release of cytochrome c [54]. This results in the activation of caspase-3 and subsequent programmed cell death. Silymarin (SM) treatment markedly diminished HFD-induced caspase activation, highlighting its anti-apoptotic and hepatoprotective effects, consistent with previous research on SM’s function in maintaining mitochondrial integrity, neutralizing reactive oxygen species, and inhibiting stress-induced apoptotic signaling [55–57]. SM-loaded phytosomal nanoparticles (SM-PNPs) demonstrated improved efficacy compared to free SM, showing a greater inhibition of caspase-9 and caspase-3 activities.

Multivariable analysis was conducted on the data sets derived from the various assays. Hierarchical clustering visually summarizes complex biochemical data, revealing patterns and relationships that may not be easily discernible through individual marker analysis. PCA decomposes multidimensional data into principal components, facilitating the identification of group differences and treatment effects in a comprehensible manner. The statistical tools employed enhance the reliability of the findings, providing substantial evidence that Silymarin, particularly in nanoparticle form, is beneficial for diet-related metabolic issues. These treatments may be effective in addressing metabolic issues associated with obesity. The histopathological observations were consistent with the study results. HFD consumption induced macrovesicular steatosis in the liver, evident as cytoplasmic lipid vacuoles of different sizes, as well as fibrosis, whereas the treatment by SM-PNPs significantly reduced these histopathological changes.

Study limitations

This study has several noteworthy aspects that require attention. Initially, although the biochemical, molecular, and histopathological findings collectively support the hepatoprotective effects of SM-PNPs, histological validation specific to apoptosis, such as TUNEL staining, was not performed. Further investigations into employing TUNEL and caspase-3 immunostaining will clarify the mechanistic basis of the anti-apoptotic properties of SM-PNPs. Secondly, ROS flowcytometry was conducted using cell lysate however using isolated primary hepatocytes or collagenase-perfused tissue can provide more accurate cell-level resolution of ROS which will be more reflective to the protective antioxidant effect of SM and SM-PNPs. Thirdly, the research employed an animal model of high-fat diet-induced hepatic injury, which, although well-established, may not fully replicate the complexities of human metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Also, only adult male albino rats were used in the present study to avoid potential variability associated with sex hormones and their known influence on lipid metabolism, inflammatory responses, and the progression of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease; however, this represents a limitation as sex-specific differences were not evaluated. Extended studies involving various durations and both male and female rats of high-fat diets and clinically applicable dosing regimens are essential to improve the accuracy of translational relevance predictions. An additional limitation of this study is the absence of fluorescence imaging data for caspase-3, caspase-8, and caspase-9, which precluded spatial visualization of apoptotic activation and may have limited a more comprehensive assessment of apoptosis pathways at the tissue level. Finally, the nanoparticle formulation lacked evaluation for pharmacokinetics, tissue distribution, and long-term safety, all of which are critical considerations for future therapeutic development. Notwithstanding these limitations, the study provides strong integrative evidence that supports the beneficial effects of SM-PNPs in alleviating metabolic and hepatic dysfunction induced by high-fat diets.

Conclusions

The present research explored the development and application of SM-loaded phytosomal nanoparticles as a potential therapy for high-fat diet-induced MASLD. Our results showed that these nanoparticles efficiently delivered SM, preventing HFD-induced lipid accumulation, restoring redox balance, alleviating metabolic dysfunction, and reducing hepatic inflammation in HFD-fed rats. The observed benefits are attributed to the nanoparticle system’s ability to improve SM stability, solubility, sustained release, and overall bioavailability. Moreover, the nanoparticles enhanced SM’s efficacy, leveraging its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, which was supported by in silico studies. Molecular docking demonstrated strong interactions between SM and proteins central to oxidative stress and inflammation, underscoring its potential as a potent hepatoprotective agent.
