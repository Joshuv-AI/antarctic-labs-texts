Popliteal plexus block in total knee arthroplasty: a single-center randomized controlled double-blinded trial
Authors: Kevin Stebler, Nadia Elia, Isabelle Zaccaria, Roxane Michelle Fournier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWhether a popliteal plexus block improves postoperative pain following total knee arthroplasty remains debated. This randomized trial tested if adding a popliteal plexus block to a continuous femoral nerve block decreases postoperative opioid requirement.MethodsWe included 66 patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty. 32 received continuous femoral nerve block and popliteal plexus block (intervention), and 34 received continuous femoral nerve block alone (control). The primary endpoint was the 12-hour postoperative morphine-equivalent consumption (mg). Secondary outcomes included opioid consumption, Visual Analog Pain Score (0–10), and sensorimotor extension of the block in postanesthesia care unit, at 12 hours, 24 hours and 48 hours postoperatively.Results66 patients with a median body mass index of 28.7 (IQR 26.3–33.8) were included in the study. In an intention-to-treat analysis, the median 12-hour morphine-equivalent consumption was lower in the intervention group (6.1 mg (0.5–14.5) vs 10 mg (5.0–17.3); one-sided Wilcoxon test (p=0.04)). The average pain intensity experienced in postanesthesia care unit was lower in the intervention group (median: 3.0 (3.0–5.0) vs 2.0 (1.0–4.0), two-sided Wilcoxon p=0.01) and fewer patients reported lateroposterior pain of the knee (11 (34.4%) vs 21 (61.8%) p=0.03). These benefits disappeared after 24 hours. The median duration of the popliteal plexus block procedure was 5.0 min (2.0–5.0).ConclusionsAdding a popliteal plexus block to a continuous femoral nerve block decreases 12-hour opioid utilization, but the effect size is small, calling into question its clinical relevance.Trial registration numberNCT04048889.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) induces severe postoperative pain that can limit early postoperative ambulation, prolong hospital stay and increase opioid requirement with unwanted side effects.1 Perioperative pain management including regional anesthetic approaches contribute to faster recovery, reduced opioid consumption and improved pain scores.2 Femoral nerve block or adductor canal block (ACB) provide analgesia only to the anterior region of the knee. After TKA, the incidence of posterior knee pain has been reported to be as high as 67%–89%.25

To provide analgesia to the posterior part of the knee, a sciatic nerve block was formerly considered but was associated with a deep motor block of the lower leg and foot and could mask surgical common peroneal nerve (CPN) injury.6 There has been increasing interest in regional analgesia techniques that could provide posterior analgesia of the knee, such as infiltration between the popliteal artery and the posterior capsule of the knee (IPACK) block,7 selective tibial nerve block (STNB),8 genicular nerve block9 or local infiltration analgesia (LIA),10 in combination with multimodal analgesia, to preserve muscle strength following TKA. Due to its simplicity, LIA is a relative common procedure but is also a blind technique which may provide insufficient analgesia.11 12 IPACK block was shown to be associated with limited pain benefits when added to a continuous ACB within a robust multimodal analgesic regimen.13 A recent systematic review showed limited evidence supporting its use as a complement to ACB after TKA.14 STNB at the popliteal crease may lead to CPN block by spread of local anesthetic (LA) to the sciatic nerve bifurcation and could mask surgically induced injury.15

Runge et al, inspired by an anatomic study on cadavers showing spread of injectate in the popliteal fossa,16 assessed the analgesic effect of a popliteal plexus block (PPB) added to a femoral triangle block (FTB) in 10 patients with significant pain after TKA and found effective pain relief without motor blockade of the foot.17 In a very recent randomized controlled trial (RCT) the same authors added the PPB to an FTB in TKA and concluded a statistically significant reduction of 24-hour opioid consumption.18

The primary aim of this trial was to examine whether adding a PPB to a continuous femoral nerve block (CFNB) for TKA could decrease postoperative opioid requirements. We hypothesized that it could decrease opioid needs by 5 mg during the first 12 hours postoperatively.

Methods

This trial was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04048889, July 22, 2019) and is reported following the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials 2010 checklist for reporting of randomized trials.

Trial design

Single-centered, parallel, randomized, double-blind controlled trial.

Participants

ASA 1–3 patients undergoing elective TKA were eligible.

We did not consider patients with allergy to LA, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), or morphine, those who had used daily opioid for more than a month before enrolment, those with glomerular filtration rate <50 mL/min, with lower limb neuropathy, undergoing revision of TKA or who had participated in another clinical study within the last 30 days.

The first participant was enrolled on August 21, 2019.

Interventions

After providing written informed consent, patients were randomly allocated, on the day of surgery, to receive a CFNB alone (control group), or a CFNB combined with a PPB (intervention group).

Preoperative procedures: all patients

After application of routine monitors in the operating theater, dorsal and plantar foot isometric muscle strength was measured with the hand-held dynamometer (MicroFET2; Hoogan Industries, West Jordan, Utah, USA). A perineural catheter (Silverstim 85 mm-30 VYGON Ecouen, France) was inserted over the femoral nerve with a 50 mm linear high-frequency ultrasound transducer (SonoSite S-nerve Fugifilm; Sonosite, Bothell, Washington, USA). All participants received a standard general anesthetic.

Intervention group

After induction of general anesthesia, and before the beginning of surgery, a PPB was performed by a second anesthesiologist with ultrasound guidance. First, the apex of the femoral triangle (or Scarpa’s triangle) where the medial border of the adductor longus muscle intersects the medial border of the sartorius muscle was identified. Then, the ultrasound was slid distally following the superficial femoral artery until the artery leaves the sartorius muscle toward the adductor hiatus to become the popliteal vessels. A 80 or 120 mm 22 G block needle (Stimuplex D plus needle, B. BRAUN, Sempach, Switzerland) was inserted in-plane with the US beam, in a lateral to medial direction (figure 1). The needle tip was advanced under direct US guidance adjacent to the femoral artery at about 1–2 o’clock between the medial vastus and the adductor magnus muscles, and 15 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% was injected, in slow 5 mL increments, with intermittent aspiration to prevent intravascular injection.17 19

Control group

Patients allocated to the control group did not undergo any additional procedure after induction of general anesthesia. To maintain the anesthesiologist in charge of the patient blinded to the allocated group, they were replaced for 30 min by the second anesthesiologist after induction of anesthesia.

Intraoperative procedure: all patients

Positive pressure ventilation was adjusted to maintain end tidal PCO2 of 4.7–5.4 kPA. Sufentanil 5–10 µg intravenous was administered as needed to treat increase in arterial pressure or heart rate >20% above preinduction values. All participants received dexamethasone 0.1 mg/kg intravenous, paracetamol 1000 mg intravenous and ketorolac 30 mg intravenous before emergence from anesthesia. Muscle relaxation was antagonized with neostigmine 50 µg/kg and glycopyrrolate 10 µg/kg at the end of the surgery if necessary.

Postoperative procedures: all patients

Participants were brought to the postanesthesia care unit (PACU), where pain was treated with morphine 2 mg intravenous/10 min as needed to reach a numeric score of pain <3. All participants received a standard multimodal analgesia of paracetamol 1000 mg/6 hours and ibuprofen 400/8 hours. 15–20 mg morphine p.o. (max.10 times/day) was available as rescue analgesia. An infusion of 0.2% ropivacaine was administered through the femoral catheter, as patient controlled perineural infusion (infusion of 5–7 mL/hour plus boluses of 10 mL with a lockout time of 60 min). Knee pain location was evaluated as posterior, anterior, medial, or lateral in PACU. Before leaving PACU, dorsal and plantar foot isometric muscle strength with the hand-held dynamometer (MicroFET2; Hoogan Industries) was assessed.

Pain intensity and location, opioid consumption, occurrence of nausea vomiting, or neurologic disorders linked to the PPB were recorded at arrival and exit from PACU, and at 12, 24 and 48 hours postoperatively.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the cumulative 12 hours intravenous morphine-equivalent consumption (mg). It was assessed by a research nurse on medical records on the day after surgery and checked by the senior author (RMF).

Secondary outcomes included the cumulative intravenous morphine-equivalent consumption in PACU, at 24 and 48 hours after surgery, the mean and maximal pain scores (Visual Analog Scale (VAS), 0–10 cm) in PACU (at arrival and discharge) at 12, 24 and 48 hours after surgery, the dorsal and plantar foot isometric muscle strength measurement (newtons) before the surgery and at PACU discharge, the duration of the PPB procedure (min), location of knee pain (anterior, posterior, lateral, or medial) and the presence of sensitive or motor block extension to the sciatic nerve in PACU.

Sample size

A reduction of the 12 hours cumulative intravenous morphine-equivalent consumption of 5 mg with PPB was deemed clinically relevant.

We expected a mean 12 hours cumulative intravenous morphine-equivalent consumption of control patients of 15 mg (SD 8) and of patients receiving PPB of 10 mg (SD 8).

To reach a power of 80% and using an alpha threshold of 5%, we needed to recruit 33 patients per group to be able to identify a decrease of 5 mg (SD 8) in the intervention vs the control group using a one-sided Student’s t-test. A one-sided test was chosen prehoc because our hypothesis was that adding a PPB would decrease opioid consumption. A potential increase in opioid consumption with PPB was not considered clinically relevant.

We expected missing data and drop-outs due to technical problems and missing or incomplete follow-up. In case of missing data on the primary endpoint, the participant was replaced by a new inclusion, to obtain the necessary number of inclusions. No imputation was made for missing data.

Randomization

A computer-generated randomization list was created (ratio of 1:1, blocks of 4).

Randomization assignments were concealed in sequentially numbered sealed opaque envelopes by a research nurse not involved in other aspects of the trial, and the randomization list was kept blinded to the rest of the research team by the biostatistician.

The biostatistician generated the random allocation sequence and kept the randomization list. Physicians and research nurses enrolled the patients and gathered their informed consent. On the day of the intervention, the senior author opened a sealed envelope, in ascending order, to allocate the patient to one group or the other.

Blinding

Participants were unaware of their group allocation. We chose not to perform a sham posterior block since it could have induced nerve damage without any expected benefit. Blinding of the anesthetists in charge of the patients was maintained by asking them to step out of the room for 30 min after induction of anesthesia, time during which another anesthetist either performed a PPB or not. The surgeons and the physicians or a nurse assessing postoperative outcomes were also kept blinded to the group allocation.

Statistical methods

The baseline characteristics of participants are described by groups. Quantitative variables are reported as means±SD for normal distributions, or medians (IQR) otherwise. Qualitative variables are reported as numbers and percentages (%). No formal comparisons between groups are made since the study was randomized.

The primary endpoint is reported as medians (IQR) in the control and intervention groups and compared by a one-sided Wilcoxon test using an intention-to-treat (ITT) and a per-protocol (PP) subset representing a subset excluding patients with major protocol deviations, as planned in the statistical analysis plan. An “as-treated” subset was subsequently added that analyses patients according to the intervention they received, instead of the intervention they had been allocated to by the randomization process, to account for possible error in the administration of the intervention. There was a prehoc decision to analyze the primary endpoint on a one-sided basis, with a threshold α=0.05 since we did not expect the patients to experience more postoperative pain in the intervention group than in the control group. All secondary endpoints are reported on a two-sided ITT basis. No imputation was made for patients lost to follow. The quantitative variables are compared using a Wilcoxon test and the qualitative variables are reported as numbers (%) and compared with χ2 test.

Analyses were performed on SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

Participant flow

A total of 495 patients were assessed for eligibility, 128 agreed to participate, and 66 were randomized. Of these, two were excluded. One patient was mistakenly included twice (for the second knee surgery) and one suffered from femoral block failure. Two additional patients were randomized to replace them leading to 34 participants in the control group and 32 in the intervention group. All 66 were included in the ITT analysis. Eight patients were excluded for the PP analysis for major protocol violations. Five (control group) had received morphine just before extubation, two (control group) had been wrongly allocated to the intervention group, and one (intervention group) had not received the prescribed postoperative NSAID (figure 2).

Recruitment

Participants were included from August 21, 2019 to May 11, 2022. The inclusion process suffered from the first and second waves of the COVID-19 pandemic due to anesthesiologists working in intensive care units and stopping of the elective surgical program at the University Hospitals of Geneva (figure 2).

Pain in the lateroposterior region of the knee affected fewer patients in the intervention group in PACU. This effect disappeared 12–48 hours postoperatively (table 3).

Harms

None of the patients presented symptoms compatible with a lesion of the sciatic nerve due to the PPB.

One patient presented a transient plantar hypoesthesia. A second patient presented with a transient CPN damage. Both adverse events occurred in patients from the control group.

Discussion

This trial suggests that a PPB, performed at the adductor canal hiatus level in TKA, could spare about one dose of oral morphine during the first 12 postoperative hours, could decrease peroperative sufentanil requirement and maximum pain score during the first 24 hours.

Our results call for certain comments. The spread of the LA in the PPB approach, reaching the popliteal plexus and the genicular branch of the posterior obturator nerve, has similarities to that obtained with the proximal IPACK described by Tran et al20 in which the transducer was applied on the medial thigh, 1–2 finger breaths above the patella, and the LA injected posterior to the femoral shaft in the interspace between the posterior capsule of the knee and the anterior wall of popliteal artery. In our PPB approach, the LA was injected between the femoral shaft and posterior wall of the popliteal artery.

Our results confirm previous findings from an observational study by Runge et al17 and those from a recently published RCT18 that compared the 24 hours intravenous oxycodone consumption in patients undergoing TKA and receiving either PPB and FTB, standalone ACB, or standalone FTB. None of the groups received LIA. They demonstrated a 24-hour saving of 4 or 6 mg intravenous of oxycodone depending on the control group (ACB or FTB). In this trial 10 mL of bupivacaine 0.5% was injected from medial to lateral, anterolaterally to the artery (about 10 o’clock). This is reminiscent of the proximal IPACK technique19 and different from the one described by Runge et al17 in 2018, that we applied. Also, we administered a larger volume of a less potent LA and provided postoperative analgesia by intravenous or oral morphine whereas intravenous oxycodone was administrated through a patient-controlled-analgesia pump in their trial. Finally, we applied CFNB instead of FTB. Although our PPB approach could have been performed together with the FTB with one needle and the same probe, we chose to place a femoral catheter to ensure excellent analgesia of the anterior region of the knee and enable us to distinguish lateral and/or posterior pain that is related to the sciatic nerve territory. Femoral block analgesia is more consistent and reliable than FTB in the absence of multimodal analgesia.

Since we aimed to assess the effectiveness of PPB that had been, at the time we designed the study, only described in a few patients, and because we were worried about patients receiving too large amounts of LA as they received some through the femoral catheter too, we chose to compare PPB and CFNB with CFNB only, together with systemic multimodal analgesia but excluding any other analgesic strategy or LIA administrated by surgeons.

As the PPB is supposed to be a motor-sparing block, we evaluated dorsal and plantar foot isometric muscle strength in both groups and were able to exclude a possible extension to the sciatic nerve. The reason for the difference from the findings of Runge et al, who reported a decrease in sensitivity in the CPN territory in 50% of patients despite receiving less 0.5% ropivacaine (10 mL vs 15 mL)17 remains unclear. The absence of sensorimotor extension after any block favors early ambulation, an advantage in the fast-track era.

The opioid savings found are modest. Interestingly, a systematic review focusing on total hip and knee arthroplasty suggested that the minimum important clinical difference was 10 mg of intravenous morphine over 24 hours,21 and a recent study focusing on opioid-related side effects in the same population reported a difference of 5 mg morphine between patients with mild vs moderate opioid-related side effects.22 Moreover, this modest effect should be considered in the context of an elderly population, requiring significant analgesics once the effect of the peripheral nerve blocks wanes, and well-known opioid side effects. PPB could, therefore, still be considered as part of a multimodal analgesic management approach. This was also suggested by Sørensen et al.18 12 hours postoperatively, the benefit from the PPB had disappeared.

Our study has limitations. First, the PPB cannot be tested. We could only observe a possible extension to the sciatic nerve in the PACU, but it is not a prerequisite for success. Second, systemic injection of LA may have contributed to multimodal analgesia. Third, eight patients were excluded from the PP analysis due to protocol violations, seven of which belonged to the control group. Five had received morphine just before extubation although this was precluded in this protocol. It remains unclear why only the patients in the control group received morphine at this time point. It may be due to the patients showing indirect signs of pain before extubation more often in this group compared with patients in the intervention group or may be a sign of blinding failure. This early administration of opioid however has probably been compensated by a decreased administration of opioid in PACU, therefore, not affecting our point estimate, as the PP analyses seem to confirm. Fourth, two patients allocated to the control group have received the intervention. The administration of the intervention to patients allocated to the control group was unexpected but was considered (post hoc) as a major protocol violation. An “as-treated” subset, representing a subset of patientsexplanation analyzed according to the intervention received, instead of the intervention allocated by the randomization process, was therefore added to the planned statistical analysis plan to consider these cases of error in the administration of the intervention. To date, we have no explanation for this misallocation which could have led to an underestimation of the impact of PPB, as the “as-treated” subset suggests. Fifth, the last patient was enrolled in 2022. Our local practice has not changed since. Finally, our investigation was not designed to estimate the duration of action of PPB. On the positive side, this trial has a good external validity with blocks were performed by trainees, residents or attending physicians, all supervised by the authors (KS and RMF).

This trial allows us to draw a research agenda. Future trials should compare PPB to IPACK and to surgical LIA. There are reasons to believe that the PPB could be easier and quicker to perform than the distal IPACK, and differences in the side effects and duration of analgesia provided by these different approaches remain unknown. The question of the outcomes to be measured in future trials remains a challenge. It is perhaps time to stop measuring opioid sparing (surrogate endpoint), and start focusing on outcomes that matter to patients, in a patient and public involvement approach. Additionally, practicing an easy plane block with few potential side effects such as PPB may facilitate junior anesthetists’ training of ultrasound-guided nerve block; this could be studied. Finally, although it took 3 years to complete this trial, mainly due to the COVID-19 pandemic, a total of 128 patients had accepted to participate suggesting that future randomized trials are feasible.

Conclusions

Despite a modest opioid saving of short duration, the PPB may be a simple technique to consider as part of a multimodal analgesic pathway. Future trials should compare PPB with distal IPACK or posterior surgical infiltration, different LA volumes, and focus on outcomes that matter to patients.
