Ginsenoside Rh2 targets SIRT1-mediated deacetylation to modulate ERα/AR balance and overcome endocrine therapy resistance in prostate cancer using 3D organoid models
Authors: Xinan Chen, Wei Luo, Yueying Ren, Zezhong Mou, Chenyang Xu, Jimeng Hu, Mengbo Hu, Haowen Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Resistance to endocrine therapy remains a major challenge in treating prostate cancer (PCa), highlighting the need for alternative therapeutic approaches. In this study, we investigated the potential of Ginsenoside Rh2 to counteract such resistance by influencing the SIRT1-dependent deacetylation pathway, thereby modulating the equilibrium between estrogen receptor α (ERα) and androgen receptor (AR). We proposed that Rh2 may suppress therapy-resistant PCa progression by adjusting ERα/AR transcriptional dynamics. Through network pharmacology analysis, key anti-PCa targets of Rh2 were identified, with Cytoscape enrichment indicating a pivotal role in AR signaling modulation. Functional validation was performed using 3D tumor organoids and human PCa cell lines (C4-2B and LNCaP) treated with Rh2 to assess cellular behaviors and receptor deacetylation status. Additionally, xenograft mouse models were employed to evaluate Rh2’s in vivo effects, based on tumor burden, serum PSA levels, and tissue histopathology. Rh2 treatment led to significant, dose- and time-dependent inhibition of PCa cell proliferation and metastatic traits, accompanied by restored ERα/AR balance through activation of SIRT1. In animal studies, Rh2 notably reduced tumor size, decreased PSA expression, and improved systemic health indicators. Collectively, our results suggest that Rh2 re-sensitizes PCa to endocrine therapy by targeting the SIRT1 pathway, positioning it as a promising phytochemical candidate for managing resistant PCa. Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) ranks among the most prevalent malignancies affecting the male population worldwide, with its incidence steadily rising across numerous geographic regions (Li et al. 2022a, b; Liu et al. 2022). Current treatment modalities include surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and hormonal (endocrine) approaches. Among these, endocrine therapy remains a frontline option due to its considerable therapeutic efficacy and comparatively low toxicity (Heydari et al. 2023; Kumar et al. 2023). However, as treatment progresses, many patients develop resistance to endocrine therapy, particularly to androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), where resistance is a significant concern (Feng et al. 2023). The emergence of resistance accelerates the progression of PCa and markedly reduces patient survival (Di Carlo et al. 2023; Mosquera et al. 2021; Talib et al. 2024). Consequently, overcoming resistance to endocrine therapy and extending patient survival has become a critical challenge in PCa research. Despite numerous studies aiming to uncover resistance mechanisms and develop new treatment strategies, current therapeutic options remain limited in addressing resistance, highlighting the urgent need for new molecular targets and treatment approaches (Cai et al. 2023; Yamada et al. 2021; Chan et al. 2022).

Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), a key member of the sirtuin family of NAD+-dependent deacetylases, plays diverse roles in both physiological and pathological settings (Xie et al. 2024). By modulating protein acetylation status, SIRT1 influences various cellular events, including metabolic homeostasis, senescence, apoptosis, and cellular stress responses. Its involvement in cancer biology is particularly complex and context-dependent (Prabhakar et al. 2024). On the one hand, SIRT1 can inhibit tumorigenesis by promoting DNA repair and antioxidant responses; on the other hand, it may also promote tumor development by enhancing cell proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis (Qin et al. 2021; DiNicolantonio et al. 2022). In PCa, SIRT1’s role is similarly significant (Wen et al. 2022; Huang et al. 2022). Studies have shown that SIRT1 regulates PCa cell growth and resistance to endocrine therapy through deacetylation of the estrogen receptor (ERα) and androgen receptor (AR). These functions suggest that SIRT1 could be an important target for overcoming resistance to endocrine therapy in PCa.

Ginsenoside Rh2 (Rh2) is a key bioactive component extracted from ginseng, known for its diverse biological effects (Wang et al. 2022). Traditionally rooted in Chinese medicine, ginseng has been employed to improve physical endurance, enhance immune responses, and delay the aging process. In recent years, Rh2 has attracted considerable attention for its notable anticancer potential. A growing number of studies have begun to elucidate the molecular mechanisms behind Rh2’s tumor-suppressive properties, particularly in the context of PCa (Li et al. 2022a, b; Li et al. 2023). Accumulating evidence suggests that Rh2 suppresses tumor progression by interfering with crucial oncogenic pathways, including PI3K/AKT signaling and AR modulation (Song et al. 2022; Peng et al. 2023). Additionally, Rh2 exhibits multi-target regulation, influencing the expression of various cancer-related genes. These findings provide a theoretical basis for considering Rh2 as a potential anticancer agent. However, its specific mechanisms in reversing endocrine therapy resistance in PCa remain unclear (Ben-Eltriki et al. 2023).

The present study focuses on delineating the molecular basis by which Rh2 restores endocrine sensitivity in resistant PCa. We hypothesize that Rh2 may inhibit tumor cell proliferation and invasion by activating the SIRT1-mediated deacetylation pathway, thereby regulating the balance of ERα and AR and ultimately reversing resistance. To test this hypothesis, we conducted a network pharmacology screening and Gene Ontology (GO)/Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis to identify the anticancer effects and potential targets of Rh2. Subsequently, we constructed a 3D tumor organoid model and performed comprehensive in vitro experiments using PCa-resistant cell lines to assess the effects of Rh2 on cell proliferation, apoptosis, and deacetylation levels. Additionally, we employed a mouse xenograft model, coupled with tumor volume measurement and Prostate-Specific Antigen (PSA) level detection, to validate the antitumor efficacy of Rh2. Our results demonstrate that Rh2 suppresses PCa progression by targeting AR signaling through multi-target mechanisms and rebalancing ERα/AR via SIRT1-mediated deacetylation. This mechanism not only blocks tumor growth and reverses endocrine resistance but also highlights Rh2’s potential as a lead compound for novel therapeutic development.

Materials and methods

Target gene screening for Rh2

The 2D and 3D structures of Ginsenoside Rh2 (PubChem CID: 119,307) were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The 2D structure file (in SDF format) was uploaded to the PharmMapper platform for inverse docking analysis. Target prediction was restricted to Homo sapiens proteins, with all other parameters set to default. This approach enabled the identification of candidate Rh2 targets and corresponding pharmacophore models.

Selection of candidate targets for Rh2-regulated PCa

Candidate genes associated with PCa were retrieved from the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/omim) databases using the keyword “Prostate cancer.” Overlapping genes from both sources were determined via Venn diagram analysis. Subsequently, potential targets of Rh2 were screened using the Sangerbox tool. Another Venn analysis was then conducted to identify the candidate targets that overlap between Rh2 and PCa-associated genes.

Functional enrichment analysis of Rh2-regulated targets in PCa

To investigate the biological relevance of Rh2-regulated targets in PCa, GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were conducted. Candidate genes were first converted to Entrez IDs using the org.Hs.eg.db package in R (v4.2.1). Enrichment analysis was then performed with clusterProfiler (v4.4.4), applying a hypergeometric test and a significance cutoff of p < 0.05. GO analysis covered biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) categories. KEGG analysis identified enriched pathways relevant to Rh2’s mechanism of action. All results were visualized using ggplot2 (v3.3.6).

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction

To explore functional associations among PCa-related targets of Rh2, PPI data were retrieved from the STRING database. The resulting network was visualized using Cytoscape (v3.7.2). To identify key functional modules, the MCODE plugin was applied with the following parameters: MCODE score > 5, degree cutoff = 2, node score cutoff = 0.2, max depth = 100, and k-score = 2. This analysis enabled the construction of a Rh2-target-disease interaction network.

Generation of sgSIRT1 knockout cells via CRISPR/Cas9

CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing was employed to generate SIRT1-knockout (sgSIRT1) C4-2B and LNCaP cells. Specific sgRNA sequences (listed in Table 1) were cloned into the Lenti-CRISPR v2 vector (HANBIO, Shanghai), which encodes Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9. Cells were transduced with the lentiviral construct and selected using 4 μg/mL puromycin (Gibco, A1113803). Clonal populations were isolated via limiting dilution. Successful SIRT1 knockout was confirmed by RT-qPCR, Western blot, and DNA sequencing. Edited cells were collected 48 h post-transfection and stored at −80 °C for downstream experiments. Cell culture

PCa cell lines C4-2B, LNCaP, DU145, and 22Rv1 (all from ATCC, USA) were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, 11,965,092) enriched with 10% FBS (Invitrogen, 10,100,147) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Invitrogen, 10,378,016). Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. Medium was refreshed every 2–3 days, and cells were subcultured at ~ 80% confluence using 0.25% trypsin/EDTA (Sigma-Aldrich, T4174).

For 3D tumor organoid formation, cells were trypsinized and resuspended in Matrigel (Corning, 354,234) at 5000 cells/μL. The suspension was filtered through a 30 μm mesh (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) to isolate larger aggregates. These were transferred to inverted plates and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min to initiate gelation. The mixture was then plated as 20 μL droplets into ultra-low attachment 96-well plates (Corning, USA), allowed to solidify for 30 min, and overlaid with warm culture medium. Organoids were maintained in culture for 7 days.

PCa cells were divided into the Ctrl (non-targeting sgRNA), Rh2 (treated with the optimal concentration of 25 μM Rh2 for 72 h), sgSIRT1 (SIRT1 knockout), sgSIRT1 + Rh2 (SIRT1 knockout treated with 25 μM Rh2 for 72 h), sgSIRT1 + SIRT1-R (SIRT1 knockout with exogenous SIRT1 added), and sgSIRT1 + SIRT1-R + Rh2 (SIRT1 knockout with exogenous SIRT1 added, treated with 25 μM Rh2 for 72 h) groups.

For Rh2 exposure studies, C4-2B and LNCaP cells were treated with a concentration gradient of Rh2 (0, 1, 5, 10, and 25 μM) for 24, 48, or 72 h, respectively. In the enzalutamide treatment experiments, C4-2B and LNCaP cells were treated with 12 μM enzalutamide for 24 h. To simulate androgen deprivation, LNCaP cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 enriched with 10% charcoal-stripped serum for 72 h before subsequent experiments. Additionally, exogenous dihydrotestosterone (DHT, 10 nM, 2 h) was added to mimic high androgen levels.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Cells cultured in 3D were fixed with 10% formalin (Sigma-Aldrich, HT501128) and post-fixed in 0.1M sodium cacodylate buffer (43,125, Sigma-Aldrich), followed by 2% osmium tetroxide (75,632, Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.5% uranyl acetate (8473, Sigma-Aldrich). Samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in acetone, and infiltrated with a 1:1 acetone:Spurr resin mixture (Polysciences, 03519–1), then embedded in pure Spurr resin and polymerized at 70 °C overnight. Ultrathin Sects. (90 nm) were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate before imaging on a FEI Tecnai Spirit Biotwin electron microscope (FEI, SPRBT001). Images were acquired using an Olympus-SIS Veleta digital camera (Olympus, V123200BU000).

Cell proliferation assay

Cell viability was tested utilizing the CCK-8 kit (C0041, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). C4-2B and LNCaP cells were treated with Rh2 at varying concentrations (0, 1, 5, 10, and 25 μM) for 24, 48, and 72 h. At each time point, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added per well, followed by a 4-h incubation. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm utilizing a microplate reader to quantify cell viability.

To compare Rh2’s antiproliferative effects across PCa cell lines, LNCaP, DU145, and 22Rv1 cells were similarly treated with Rh2 under identical conditions, and viability was evaluated as described above.

Additionally, to assess the impact of gene knockout or overexpression on cell growth, CCK-8 assays were performed in treated C4-2B and LNCaP cells.

Transwell invasion assay

C4-2B and LNCaP cells were pretreated with varying concentrations of Rh2 (0–25 μM) for 24, 48, or 72 h. To assess potential synergistic effects, four groups were tested: untreated control, enzalutamide alone (12 μM, 72 h), Rh2 alone (25 μM, 72 h), and Rh2 + enzalutamide combination (25 μM + 12 μM, 72 h). DU145 and 22Rv1 cells were also exposed to Rh2 (25 μM, 72 h) to evaluate its impact on invasion. For the invasion assay, 50 μL of Matrigel (BD Biosciences, USA) was added to the upper chamber of 8.0 μm-pore Transwell inserts (Corning, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to allow gelation. Treated cells (1 × 105 per well) were seeded into the upper chamber in serum-deprived DMEM (0.1% FBS), while the lower chamber contained DMEM with 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2, non-invading cells were removed from the upper membrane surface.

Invaded cells on the lower side of the membrane were fixed with methanol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), stained with a fluorescent dye (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and counted under an optical microscope (CKX53, Olympus, Japan). Similarly, LNCaP, DU145, and 22Rv1 cells were treated with 25 μM Rh2 for 72 h to evaluate its effects on apoptosis, using the same protocols for flow cytometry.

Flow cytometry analysis

C4-2B and LNCaP cells (ATCC, USA) were treated with Rh2 at concentrations of 0, 1, 5, 10, and 25 μM for 24, 48, or 72 h. After treatment, 1 × 105 cells per well were harvested, rinsed with cold PBS (Gibco, USA), and stained using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (BD Biosciences, USA). Cells were incubated at ambient temperature in the dark for 15 min, resuspended in 400 μL of binding buffer, and labeled with 5 μL of Annexin V. Apoptotic populations were quantified using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Early, late, and total apoptosis rates were calculated and reported as mean ± standard deviation, followed by statistical analysis.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from 3D tumor organoids derived from C4-2B and LNCaP cells cultured in Matrigel and treated with Rh2 (0, 1, 5, 10, and 25 μM; Sigma, PPD-060, USA) for 24, 48, or 72 h. Following treatment, cells were lysed using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 10,296,010, USA), and RNA was purified using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74,104, Germany). RNA concentration and purity were assessed with a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, ND-1000, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent kit (TaKaRa, RR037A, Japan), and gene expression was quantified by real-time PCR. Primer sequences are provided in Table 2. Relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH serving as the normalizer. Immunoblotting

Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min with intermittent mixing and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. Supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0012S). Equal amounts of protein (30–50 μg per lane) were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010) at 100 V for 1.5 h. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (anti-rabbit IgG, Abcam, ab6721, 1:2000) for 1 h at ambient temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (Beyotime, P0018A) and imaged with a ChemiDoc XRS + system (Bio-Rad, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to GAPDH (Abcam, ab9485, 1:5000) as the internal control. Luciferase reporter assay

Prostate cancer cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL (100 μL per well). After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing various concentrations of test compounds, followed by a 24-h incubation. At the end of treatment, cells were lysed using 1 × Glo Lysis Buffer (Promega, USA). Luciferase activity was quantified using the Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega), and luminescence was measured with a Synergy™ 4 Hybrid Microplate Reader (BioTek, USA). Relative transcriptional activity was calculated as fold change compared to untreated control groups.

Immunofluorescence staining

To confirm both the identity and biological relevance of the 3D prostate cancer spheroid model, immunofluorescence staining was employed to detect the expression and subcellular distribution of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and AR. LNCaP and C4-2B cells were cultured in 3D suspension to form spheroids, which were subsequently fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma, P6148) for 10–15 min. Fixed samples were rinsed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 5 min each. Permeabilization was carried out using 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma, T8787) for 10 min, followed by additional PBS washes.

To minimize non-specific antibody binding, the spheroids were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma, A9647) at ambient temperature for 30 to 60 min. Next, samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting PSA (Abcam, ab9537) and AR (Abcam, ab74272). Following primary incubation, the spheroids were washed three times with PBS and subsequently treated with secondary antibodies—either Alexa Fluor 488 (Abcam, ab150077) or Alexa Fluor 594 (Abcam, ab150116)—for 1 h at ambient temperature in the dark. After another series of PBS washes, nuclei were stained with DAPI (Sigma, D9542) for 5–10 min. Imaging was conducted using a fluorescence microscope, and signal intensities were quantified with ImageJ software. PSA signals were predominantly observed in the cytoplasm, whereas AR localization was primarily nuclear, thus verifying the tumor-specific characteristics of the generated 3D spheroids.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) for acetylation detection

3D spheroids were lysed on ice using lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013), incubated for 30 min with gentle mixing, and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4°C. Supernatants (1 mg total protein) were incubated overnight with anti-Flag antibody (Sigma, F1804) at 4°C. Protein A/G agarose beads (Santa Cruz, sc-2003) were added and incubated for 2 h. Beads were washed three times in lysis buffer and eluted with Laemmli buffer, then boiled at 95 °C for 5 min. Samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and Western blot. Acetylation and total levels of ERα and AR were quantified using ImageJ.

Establishment and treatment of the mouse xenograft model

Sixty male BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old, 25–30 g) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. Mice were housed under SPF conditions with 60%−65% humidity, 22–25°C temperature, and a 12-h light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water. Following a 1-week acclimatization, animal health was assessed, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the "Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals" (Czernin et al. 2020). Ethical approval was granted by the Animal Ethics Committee of Huashan Hospital, Fudan University (Approval No. 22YF1404300).

To determine the optimal Rh2 dosage, 30 mice were randomly assigned to receive 40, 80, or 120 mg/kg Rh2 (n = 10/group) for 4 weeks. Serum PSA levels were measured using an ELISA kit (Elabscience, E-EL-M0961), identifying 120 mg/kg as the most effective dose. Based on this, three experimental groups were established (n = 10 each): Model + sgNC + DMSO, Model + sgNC + Rh2, and Model + sgSIRT1 + Rh2.

LNCaP and sgSIRT1-LNCaP cells were cultured and prepared in sterile PBS at a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL (Nunes et al. 2017). After anesthesia, 100 μL of the cell suspension was injected orthotopically into the prostate of each mouse (designated as day 0). Tumors were allowed to establish for 7 days. Mice in treatment groups received intraperitoneal Rh2 (120 mg/kg) three times per week, while controls received DMSO. Tumor growth was monitored every 5 days by caliper measurements, and volume was calculated as: V = (π/6) × (length × width2). Bioluminescent imaging (CRi Maestro, CRi Inc., USA) was conducted every 5 days to track tumor progression. On day 36, mice were euthanized, and tumors were harvested, photographed, weighed, and preserved at −80°C for further analyses.

On day 36, the mice were euthanized, tumors were excised and photographed, and tumor weights were measured using an electronic balance. Tumors were then placed in cryovials and stored at −80°C for subsequent experimental analyses (Linxweiler et al. 2020; Kim et al. 2022).

Histopathological staining

Tumor tissues were fixed, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Paraffin was removed using xylene, followed by sequential dehydration in 100%, 95%, and 70% ethanol. After rehydration, sections were stained with hematoxylin (Solarbio, H8070, Beijing, China) for 5–10 min at ambient temperature, rinsed with distilled water, and counterstained with eosin (Solarbio, G1100) for 5–10 min. Slides were then dehydrated, cleared, and mounted using standard histological protocols.

Immunohistochemical staining for ERα and AR in tumor tissue

Tumor specimens were fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Slides were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, and 75%, 3 min each). Antigen retrieval was performed by heating sections in 0.01 M citrate buffer for 15–20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 30 min at ambient temperature. After blocking with goat serum for 20 min, sections were incubated with primary antibodies against ERα and AR (details in Table 3) for 1 h at ambient temperature. Following PBS washes, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam, ab6785, 1:1000) at 37 °C for 20 min, then treated with SP for 30 min. Signal development was performed using DAB (Beyotime, P0202) for 5–10 min, and the reaction was stopped by rinsing with water. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin (Beyotime, C0107), differentiated in acid alcohol, and rinsed thoroughly. Dehydration was completed through a graded ethanol series, followed by xylene clearing and mounting with neutral resin. Stained slides were examined under an upright light microscope. For quantification, five random high-power fields (HPFs) per section were analyzed, and 100 cells were counted per field to determine the percentage of ERα- and AR-positive cells.

Statistics

Quantitative data were derived from a minimum of three biologically independent replicates and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Pairwise comparisons were evaluated using unpaired t-tests. For analyses involving multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was conducted; if overall significance was detected, Tukey’s post hoc correction was applied to assess intergroup differences. In instances where the assumptions of normality or homoscedasticity were not satisfied, non-parametric alternatives—namely the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test—were employed. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 and R software. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value less than 0.05.

From the GeneCards and OMIM databases, 14,025 and 161 PCa-related genes were retrieved, respectively. Their intersection yielded 141 common genes (Fig. 1B). In parallel, 376 Rh2-associated targets were acquired via TCMSP. Venn analysis between Rh2 targets and PCa-related genes revealed 14 overlapping candidates: SRC, AR, ADH1B, ESR1, EGFR, ALDH2, NQO1, PLA2G2A, FGFR2, AKT1, NQO2, BRAF, HRAS, and PMS2 (Fig. 1C). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of these targets suggested Rh2 may modulate BP, such as prostate gland development, enzyme activity regulation, and cell migration—mechanisms closely tied to PCa progression and drug resistance (Fig. 1D).

Using Cytoscape, we constructed a target-disease-compound network involving Rh2, its candidate targets, and associated pathways (Fig. 1E). The potential targets of Rh2 include AR, SRC, EGFR, and AKT1, which play critical roles in key BP, such as cancer signaling, cell proliferation, division, and cell cycle regulation. Notably, AR plays a central role in the initiation and progression of PCa, suggesting that Rh2 may directly influence PCa development by modulating the AR signaling pathway. Additionally, Rh2 may inhibit tumor cell growth, migration, and invasion by affecting related signaling pathways involving EGFR, SRC, AKT1, and BRAF, potentially overcoming cancer drug resistance.

Construct a PPI network for candidate genes regulated by Rh2 in PCa (Fig. 1F). In this network, the AR serves as the central node, exhibiting interactions with several key genes such as EGFR, SRC, and AKT1.

Validation of PCa resistance mechanisms using a 3D tumor organoid model

Discussion

PCa is among the most prevalent malignancies in men, with endocrine therapy (including ADT and antiandrogen therapy) being a primary treatment approach (Desai et al. 2021). Despite initial responsiveness, resistance frequently develops over time, contributing to disease progression and diminished treatment efficacy (Pernigoni et al. 2021). Therefore, identifying effective strategies to overcome endocrine therapy resistance and halt or slow the progression of PCa has become a critical research focus (Berruti and Dalla 2017). In this context, Rh2, a natural compound with various biological activities, has garnered significant attention for its potential role in cancer treatment (Wang et al. 2008). This study investigates the mechanism by which Rh2 reverses endocrine therapy resistance in PCa. Using a 3D tumor organoid model, we demonstrate that Rh2 reverses this resistance by activating SIRT1, which regulates the deacetylation of ERα and AR, thereby restoring the effectiveness of endocrine therapy (Fig. 10).Fig. 10Rh2 Inhibits PCa Resistance by Regulating ERα/AR Balance Through SIRT1-Mediated Deacetylation

Rh2 Inhibits PCa Resistance by Regulating ERα/AR Balance Through SIRT1-Mediated Deacetylation

For the first time, our research reveals that Rh2 can activate SIRT1 and modulate the transcriptional activity of ERα and AR through deacetylation, contributing to its anticancer effects. SIRT1, an essential deacetylase, plays a critical role in cellular metabolism, proliferation, and apoptosis (Tong-Lin Wu et al. 2018). Our findings show that Rh2, by activating SIRT1, reduces the acetylation of ERα and AR, thereby modulating their transcriptional activity—a mechanism crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis and preventing the progression of therapy-resistant PCa. Moreover, the use of a 3D tumor organoid model provides an experimental platform that closely mimics the in vivo tumor microenvironment, enabling a more accurate assessment of the drug's efficacy.

Endocrine therapy resistance is a significant challenge in the treatment of PCa, often leading to poor patient outcomes. Estrogen receptors, mainly ERα and ERβ, are crucial regulators. ERα exerts oncogenic effects via PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways, EMT induction, and enhances cancer stemness—associated with recurrence and poor prognosis. In contrast, ERβ1 plays a tumor-suppressive role by maintaining epithelial phenotype, promoting apoptosis, and repressing AR signaling. It can also activate FOXO3a, upregulate pro-apoptotic factor PUMA, and induce cell cycle arrest via p21 and p27 (Christoforou et al. 2014). Our study proposes a novel therapeutic approach to counter endocrine resistance in PCa by activating SIRT1 through Rh2 treatment. This strategy may enhance the effectiveness of current therapies, limit resistance development, and ultimately improve patient outcomes. Furthermore, identifying SIRT1 as a central regulator provides a promising target for future precision therapies and paves the way for more individualized treatment options.

Despite the encouraging results, this study has some Limitations. Firstly, while the 3D tumor organoid model approximates aspects of the in vivo tumor environment, it does not fully recapitulate its complexity. Further validation in animal models and clinical settings is therefore essential. Secondly, the precise molecular mechanisms by which SIRT1 regulates ERα and AR deacetylation remain unclear and require deeper mechanistic exploration. Lastly, a comprehensive assessment of Rh2’s long-term toxicity and safety profile is needed to support its potential for clinical translation.

Future research should focus on several key areas. Firstly, comprehensive in vivo studies using PCa animal models are needed to validate the therapeutic efficacy and safety of Rh2. Secondly, the potential for combining Rh2 with other anticancer drugs should be explored to assess synergistic effects and develop potential combination therapies. Additionally, further investigation into the downstream signaling pathways and molecular interactions involved in SIRT1 activation and deacetylation deepens our understanding of Rh2's mechanisms of action. Lastly, translating our preclinical findings into clinical practice through rigorous clinical trials could offer new hope for patients with endocrine therapy-resistant PCa.

Our findings uncover a previously underexplored mechanism through which Rh2 restores endocrine sensitivity in PCa, as demonstrated in a 3D tumor organoid model. Specifically, Rh2 exerts its effect by activating SIRT1-mediated deacetylation, thereby regulating the balance of ERα/AR. These findings provide theoretical and experimental support for the potential application of Rh2 in PCa treatment.
