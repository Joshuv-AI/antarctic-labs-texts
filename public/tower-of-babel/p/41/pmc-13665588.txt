Community composition, abundance and activity‐density of carabids and staphylinids depend on prey abundance and adjacent habitat even in diverse agricultural landscapes
Authors: Riina Kaasik, Silva Vilumets, Kaia Treier, Uno Roosileht, John M Holland, Eve Veromann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDThe functioning of agroecosystems depends on key ecosystem processes that deliver ecosystem services, yet agricultural management has increasingly shifted towards simplified landscapes lacking essential resources for service providers. Using field‐ and landscape‐scale approaches, we assessed how habitat composition and management shape predator communities and their pest control potential. This study assessed carabid and staphylinid communities in oilseed rape and field‐bordering habitats, relating predator abundance to prey availability, landscape metrics and field management.RESULTSThe type of field boundary habitat influenced carabid and staphylinid communities even in diverse landscapes. The activity‐density of both carabids and staphylinids in fields depended on field boundary habitat type, whereas their species communities in fields were a subset of the nearby habitats' communities. The high activity‐density and diversity were positively related to the sentinel prey consumption in winter oilseed rape fields. Furthermore, the abundance of superdominant species Poecilus cupreus increased with the abundance of its potential prey, the pollen beetle, and also was associated with a higher consumption of Calliphora sp. sentinel prey. Additionally, we provide a full species list of captured carabids and staphylinids.CONCLUSIONLocal habitat characteristics, such as field‐boundary habitat type and prey availability, are key determinants of carabid and staphylinid assemblages – even in landscapes with high semi‐natural habitat coverage. Because crop field communities represent subsets of the species found in nearby semi‐natural habitats, these surrounding habitats play a pivotal role in sustaining biodiversity. This underscores the need for landscape‐level management approaches to ensure the provision of vital ecosystem services in agricultural systems. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

The global decline of invertebrate fauna and biodiversity has been closely linked to the dramatic habitat loss caused by agricultural landscape simplification, agricultural intensification and urbanization. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Although agroecosystems are intensively managed, covering 44% of habitable land globally, their functioning depends on key ecosystem services such as pollination and natural pest control, which are directly affected by biodiversity loss. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Consequently, the conservation of natural enemies has become central component of integrated pest management (IPM). 11 Pest control by beneficial organisms within agricultural landscapes is strongly influenced by the amount, quality and connectivity of green infrastructure, 12 , 13 particularly uncropped areas. 6 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Enhancing such elements can increase pest predation within fields, with high proportions of semi‐natural habitats supporting natural enemies and providing ≤50–90% pest control in associated fields thereby reducing pesticide use. 14 , 20 , 21 The presence of >20% of semi‐natural habitats around fields resulted in parasitism rates of ≤70% in conventional winter oilseed rape fields. 22

In agricultural landscapes, generalist predators are keystone contributors to natural pest control as they attack a variety of pest species and can persist despite fluctuations in individual prey populations. In arable fields, carabids (Coleoptera: Carabidae) and staphylinids (Coleoptera: Staphylinidae) are important and widespread predatory insects. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 Carabids are a numerous and diverse group of predominantly ground‐dwelling (about half of them are capable of flight) generalist predators providing natural pest control in both crop and noncrop areas. Species of agricultural relevance often overwinter in grassy linear elements, such as field margins, forest edges and sown wildflower areas, whilst others remain permanently within arable fields. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 Their abundance and species richness across fields and boundary habitats are well‐documented. 33 , 34 , 35 Staphylinids occur worldwide across most ecosystems and are predominantly facultative predators. 36 The majority of them have higher flight ability than carabids, 37 and can recolonize fields quickly after overwintering or harvest from nearby habitats, providing a mechanism for rapid spillover. 38 Landscape simplification reduces species diversity by favouring ubiquitous, generalist species, thereby homogenizing communities between crop and noncrop habitats. 23 In general, more complex landscapes tend to favour biodiversity, including beneficial arthropods, compared to simple landscapes. 14 , 39 , 40 , 41 The value of different semi‐natural habitats varies among natural enemies, with low‐mobility predators benefiting most from specific habitat types. 29 , 41 Ground‐dwelling species are particularly affected by vegetation structure; brachypterous (short‐winged, flightless) and hygrophytic (moisture‐associated) carabids benefit from high tree cover, whereas winged species prefer areas with <20% tree cover. 42 Nevertheless, greater hedgerow cover increases predator richness and supports post‐hibernation diversity of both carabid and staphylinid communities. 43 , 44

Pest levels also are affected by landscape configuration. 14 , 22 , 45 This study focused on the pollen beetle (Brassicogethes aeneus Fab.), a major pest of winter oilseed rape (Brassica napus L.). 46 Management typically relies on insecticides, although natural pest control contributes significantly, ≤25% of larvae are predated by generalist predators before pupation 47 and ≤70% parasitized by key parasitoids. 48 , 49 , 50 Together with parasitoids, predators such as carabids, staphylinids and spiders play an important role. 51 In the UK, camera‐trapping studies have demonstrated that carabids, staphylinids and predatory larvae are the main predator groups of B. aeneus larvae. 52 Previous studies also have revealed spatiotemporal links between pest abundance and predator activity, as well as species preying on pollen beetles and other oilseed rape pests. 27 , 52 Although semi‐natural habitats increase natural enemy abundance and species richness, measuring their abundance alone may not accurately reflect the level of pest control service they provide. Prey sentinel systems provide a complementary, highly sensitive approach for detecting the effects of semi‐natural habitats on predatory arthropods at both local and landscape scales. 52 , 53

Research on carabid and staphylinid beetle communities inhabiting fields and adjacent semi‐natural habitats, and their contribution to pest management, remains limited. This study addressed this gap by examining their assemblages in conventional winter oilseed rape fields and the adjacent nonarable habitats within agricultural landscapes. Specifically, this study investigated the pest control potential of carabids and staphylinids in conventional oilseed rape fields, with particular focus on how their abundance related to that of the key prey species, the pollen beetle. Additionally, it aimed to determine how various nonarable habitats within agricultural landscape influence the abundance and diversity of these generalist predators within and around the fields, and the extent to which they contribute to the natural pest control considering both surrounding landscape context and within‐field management.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study area

The study was carried out in 18 conventional winter oilseed rape fields and adjoining field‐bordering elements or crop fields over a 2‐year study period (2014 and 2015), covering two cropping seasons, in Tartu County, Estonia (Fig. 1). The study area represents an agricultural landscape where agricultural fields are interspersed with forest patches, grasslands and residential areas.

Regional map of Europe and the area in Estonia illustrating the areal distribution of 1 km radius landscape circles surrounding focal fields and their respective field‐bordering habitat types in 2024 and 2015. The centroid coordinates of the study area are 26.4689573 °E, 58.3061854 °N.

The overall selection of study areas, the classification of semi‐natural habitats and landscape mapping followed standardized protocols of the EU FP7 project QuESSA as detailed previously. 17 , 22 , 29 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56

Habitat mapping and site selection

The proportion of semi‐natural habitats around focal fields was calculated based on digital maps created during the project QuESSA, briefly, land use within a 1‐km radius around the focal fields were mapped, digitalized and divided into five main categories: semi‐natural habitats, crops, urban areas, water and nonhabitat, and the resulting GIS data were used to calculate semi‐natural habitat proportion within each landscape circle in both. 17 , 22 The proportion of semi‐natural areas within landscape circles ranged from 21% to 75% in 2014 and from 11% to 52% in 2015.

The focal field selection was based on the following criteria: (i) a conventional winter oilseed rape field positioned centrally as the focal field with a minimum size 3 ha; (ii) equal replication of herbaceous and woody linear elements (hereafter referred to as ‘boundary’) and crop fields bordering the focal field (hereafter referred to as ‘adjacent field’); (iii) a minimum distance of 1 km between any two focal fields was required during the site selection, however, the final study resulted in a minimum distance of 1800 m within the same year, and (iv) a minimum distance of 200 m between the studied boundary habitat and any other semi‐natural habitat element at the sampling location. The adjacent crop fields in 2014 were winter wheat (2 fields), spring oilseed rape, potato, and barley (2 fields); in 2015 winter wheat (2 fields), pea (2 fields), barley and oat.

Data on insecticide applications and cultivation practices on fields were obtained from the field book records provided by the farmers (Supporting Information, Table S1). The typical crop rotation in Estonia includes winter oilseed rape followed by winter wheat, then a cereal or legume crop; in mixed farming systems, rotational grasslands also are commonly included in the rotation.

Arthropod sampling

Ground‐dwelling arthropods were sampled using 100‐mL capacity pitfall traps installed at four distances (2, 25, 50 and 75 m) from the focal field edge, and in two locations within boundary habitat and adjacent field spaced 25 m apart (Fig. S1). Each trap was half‐filled with a 1:3 propylene glycol‐water solution. The traps were emptied weekly over three consecutive weeks between May and June each year, resulting in 756 pitfall trap‐days, covering the time when pollen beetle larvae are dropping on the soil for pupation. All carabids and staphylinids were counted and identified to species level based on morphological characteristics and vouched specimens in the Estonian Museum of Natural History. 57 , 58 , 59 , 60

Pest control potential was measured using sentinel prey (Calliphora sp larvae, Diptera: Calliphoridae) on two occasions in both years [26.05.2014 (24 h), 05.06.2014 (8 h) and 10.06.2015, 17.06.2015 (8 h)] at the same distances as pitfall traps with two replicates placed 5 m apart. Each sentinel item consisted of 10 larvae pinned alive to polystyrene plates (10 × 2.5 × 0.7 cm), the prey consumption was measured after 24 (one occasion) or 8 h during daylight hours in the field.

The measurement of the abundance of natural food source and potential prey, pollen beetle larvae, is described in Vilumets et al. 22 Briefly, their abundance was measured using funnel traps (31 cm diameter) closed with 50‐mL plastic vial. The traps were placed in all focal fields at three distances (2, 25 and 75 m) beneath winter oilseed rape plants to collect the dropping larvae during the same intervals as the pitfall traps. Trap contents were sieved through voile fabric, stored in labelled bags and transported to the laboratory for counting.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using R (v2022.02.3 + 492). 61 Pitfall trap data were expressed as abundance (total number of specimens), activity‐density (specimens per trap per week), species richness (number of species) and species diversity (Shannon index). Effects of adjacent habitat type, sampling year, proportion of semi‐natural habitats, tillage type (inversion/minimum) and insecticide use (yes/no) on carabid and staphylinid abundance, species richness, and species diversity were analyzed using linear models followed by ANOVA (stats package). Abundance data were log‐transformed (log(N + 1)) to improve residual normality, whereas species richness and diversity were analyzed without transformation.

The proportion of semi‐natural habitats was included only in initial field‐scale models assessing overall responses and excluded from distance‐based models focusing on local habitat effects. Linear models also were used to test the effects of habitat type, sampling year and their interaction, on species diversity in both focal fields and adjacent habitats, and to assess variation in the proportion of semi‐natural habitats among field‐bordering habitat types. Model residuals were tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

In order to assess the activity‐density of carabids and staphylinids, a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM; package glmmtmb) 62 with a negative binomial distribution and log‐link function was used. The dependent variables in the GLMMs were not transformed prior to the analyses. The first model tested the effects of adjacent habitat type, distance from adjacent habitat, sampling year, tillage type, insecticide use, and only the interaction between adjacent habitat type and the distance from the adjacent habitat on activity‐density. To study the activity‐density specifically within the adjacent habitat, a second model including adjacent habitat type, sampling year and their interaction was applied. Additionally, we used a GLMM with a negative binomial distribution and log‐link function to test the effects of field‐and‐boundary overwintering species and boundary‐overwintering species In this model, the activity‐density of field‐and‐boundary overwintering species and boundary‐overwintering species was used as the dependent variable, whereas adjacent habitat type, distance from the adjacent habitat and sampling year were included as explanatory variables. In the model we also tested the three‐way interactions between adjacent habitat type and distance; adjacent habitat type and year; and adjacent habitat type, distance and year. The effects of all factors in the GLMM were tested using a Type II analysis of deviance with Wald χ2 statistics and the model fits were evaluated using the dharma package. 63

Spearman's rank correlation was used to analyze the relationships between the activity‐density of carnivorous and omnivorous carabids, staphylinids and P. cupreus, the abundance of B. aeneus larvae and Calliphora sp. predation rates using the ‘stats” package and visualized with ggscatter (GGPUBR” 64 ). Correlations for the 10 most abundant carabid and staphylinid species were analyzed but not visualized.

A Bray–Curtis distance matrix based on species abundance was used to examine carabid and staphylinid community composition across three adjacent habitats and between sample origin focal field versus adjacent habitat). Differences in community composition among habitat types, sampling location, and year were evaluated using PERMANOVA (999 permutations). Analyses were performed in R using the vegan package. 65 NMDS ordination plots are provided in Fig. S2.

RESULTS

Landscape effects on abundance and species composition of carabids and staphylinids

Landscape mapping revealed high proportions of semi‐natural habitats within our study area, averaging 38% in 2014, and 35% in 2015 (ranges 21–76% and 11–52%). The proportion of semi‐natural habitats did not differ among field‐bordering habitat types (F 2,37 = 0.37, P = 0.70). With two exceptions in 2015 (11% and 14%), all landscape circles exceeded the ≥20% threshold considered sufficient to support pest control services, 66 indicating adequate habitat availability around the focal fields. Semi‐natural habitats consisted primarily of woody areas (25.8%), followed by herbaceous areas (6.2%), permanent grassland (4.4%) and fallow land (2.4%), with minor contributions of woody (0.4%) and herbaceous (1.3%) linear features (see Kovács et al. 17 and Vilumets et al. 22 for details).

A total of 14 879 carabids from 92 species and 9735 staphylinids from 174 species were collected across the experiment (detailed information in Tables S2–S4). Ten carabid species (Poecilus cupreus L., Harpalus rufipes DeGeer, Amara similata Gyllenhal, Pterostichus melanarius Illiger, Harpalus affinis Schrank, Anchomenus dorsalis Pontoppidan, Bembidion properans Stephens, Bembidion lampros Herbst, Loricera pilicornis Fab., Asaphidion flavipes L.) comprised 90% of all individuals, with P. cupreus dominating the assemblages (54% of all carabids). Only five species were present in all studied landscapes across both years (P. cupreus, H. rufipes, A. similata, P. melanarius, B. lampros). Likewise, 10 staphylinid species (Philonthus cognatus Stephens, Tachinus signatus Gravenhorst, Philonthus rotundicollis Menetries, Aleochara bilineata Gyllenhal, Aleochara brevipennis Gravenhorst, Atheta fungi Gravenhorst, Philonthus carbonarius Gravenhorst, Aloconota gregaria Erichson, Gyrohypnus angustatus Stephens, Anotylus rugosus F.) comprised 80% of total abundance.

Habitat type and sampling year significantly affected the abundance of carabids and staphylinids in the sampled landscapes, whereas proportion of semi‐natural habitats, pesticide use and tillage had no effect (Table 1). Carabids' diversity increased with a higher proportion of semi‐natural habitats, was lower in 2014 than in 2015 and was slightly but significantly increased by insecticide use (P = 0.04). Tillage type only affected carabid abundance (P = 0.067), which was higher in fields managed with minimum tillage compared to ploughed fields. Staphylinid diversity in landscape circles was affected by the adjacent habitat type, being significantly higher in woody linear and field combination (2.62 ± 0.07) compared to herbaceous linear and field combination (2.28 ± 0.06, P = 0.0037), and field–field combinations (2.09 ± 0.10, P < 0.0001). Staphylinid abundance varied with adjacent habitat type and year, but was unaffected by landscape composition or management variables. Species richness of carabids was influenced by sampling year and insecticide use, whereas staphylinid species richness varied only between years and showed no significant response to adjacent habitat type, landscape composition or management practices.

The activity‐density and diversity of carabids and staphylinids in focal fields and adjacent habitats

A total of 11 216 carabids and 7614 staphylinids were collected from the focal fields and 3663 carabids and 2123 staphylinids form the habitats adjacent to focal field across the study (Tables S2–S4). In the boundary elements, the abundance of carabids was the highest in the adjacent field, followed by herbaceous linear and woody linear elements.

The activity‐density of carabids in focal fields was influenced by the adjacent habitat type, year, tillage type and insecticide use throughout the study, whereas the distance from the adjacent habitat or the combined effect of the adjacent habitat type and distance, had no impact (Table 2). Activity‐density was higher when the focal field was bordered with another crop (33.77 ± 2.70 beetles trap−1 week−1) compared to those bordered by herbaceous (24.67 ± 2.67 beetles trap−1 week−1) or woody linear (19.45 ± 2.0 beetles trap−1 week−1) elements (Table 2).

Overwintering habitat information could not be obtained from the literature for 31 low‐abundance species.

The activity‐density of both field‐and‐boundary and boundary overwintering carabids in focal field was significantly affected by adjacent habitat type, distance from the field edge and sampling year (Table 3). Community composition indicates reduced diversity in focal fields

PERMANOVA analysis revealed significant differences in carabid community composition based on adjacent habitat type, sampling location (focal field versus adjacent habitat), sampling year, and the interaction of year and sampling site (Table 5). By contrast, for staphylinids, only adjacent habitat type, sampling location and sampling year significantly influenced community composition (Table 5). Sampling location (focal field or adjacent habitat) explained the largest variation in both the carabids (13.29%) and staphylinids (16.41%) community composition, whereas the adjacent habitat type accounted for ≈8% of the variation (Table 5). Overall, communities in the focal fields were more homogenous than those in boundary habitats (Fig. S2). Woody linear habitats showed some variation from the other habitats, indicating distinct carabid and staphylinid assemblages. Communities in adjacent habitats were more dispersed than those in the focal fields, reflecting higher diversity, whereas the focal field communities largely were a subset of the boundary habitat communities.

The activity‐density of carabids and staphylinids increases along with prey availability and prey consumption rate in conventional winter oilseed rape fields

The activity‐density of carnivorous and omnivorous carabids increased with the abundance of their potential prey, larval stages of the pollen beetle, as evidenced by significant positive correlation [carnivorous and omnivorous: ρ = 0.38, P < 0.0001, Fig. 4(A); carnivorous: ρ = 0.33, P < 0.0001, Fig. 4(B)]. The relationship was strongest in focal fields adjacent to another crop field, intermediate when adjacent to woody linear elements, and weakest in the fields adjacent to herbaceous linear habitat. The activity‐density of the superdominant species P. cupreus increased with the abundance of pollen beetle larvae (ρ = 0.36, P < 0.0001) which also was evident when analyzed separately by adjacent habitat types [Fig. 4(C)].

Line represents the Spearman correlation, coloured areas 95% CI.

In the first experiment in 2014 when sentinel prey were exposed for 24 h, the average prey consumption rate across sampling sites (% of larvae consumed on sentinel systems) was 98.8%; this treatment was therefore excluded from further analyses, and subsequent experiments were restricted to 8 active daylight hours. During this period, the mean prey consumption rate across sampling sites was 59,4% (± 2.51), with complete (100%) removal observed across focal fields with different boundary habitat types [Fig. 5]. Across both study years, prey consumption rate showed a moderate positive correlation with the activity‐density of carnivorous and omnivorous carabids, with similar patterns across adjacent habitat types [Fig. 5(A)]. The prey consumption rate also correlated weakly with staphylinid activity‐density [ρ = 0.26, P < 0.0001; Fig. 5(B)].

Also, relationships between activity‐density of the ten most abundant carabid and staphylinid species, pollen beetle larval abundance and prey consumption rates were analyzed. Species‐level analyses were carried out across the study. The prey consumption rate was positively correlated with the activity‐density of P. cupreus and A. bilineata, whereas weaker associations were observed for the other abundant carabid and staphylinid species (Table S6). The activity‐density of H. rufipes was moderately correlated with pollen beetle larval abundance, whereas correlations for other species were weak.

DISCUSSION

Our results demonstrate that diverse landscapes enhance the diversity of predatory insects, provides refugia, and thereby support ecosystem services such as pest control. In agricultural landscapes with high proportions of semi‐natural areas, natural pest control service in crop fields were improved. Importantly, our data indicate that semi‐natural habitats do not act as ecological traps for carabids and staphylinids by diverting them away from crops; instead, they increase species diversity and enhance pest control within oilseed rape fields. Predatory beetle activity‐density in crop fields was positively correlated with pollen beetle larval abundance, indicating that predators actively respond to prey availability and thereby contribute to pest control. The activity‐density of strictly field‐overwintering carabids could not be analyzed owing to their low abundance as most collected individuals were generalists with flexible overwintering strategies. However, previous studies have shown that semi‐natural habitats bordering agricultural areas can influence carabid assemblages through spillover effects and the use of resources from natural habitats. 67 , 87 , 88 , 89 , 90 Specifically, the diversity of surrounding areas influences the availability of ecosystem services within fields, whereas proximity to semi‐natural habitats and structurally complex agricultural landscapes enhances the stability and resilience of key ecosystem services. 14 , 91 , 92

Landscape level effects

Our results support the consensus that heterogenous landscapes promote arthropod biodiversity, 19 , 41 , 66 , 91 , 93 as reflected in the high abundance and species richness of carabids and staphylinids observed in this study. The study was conducted in a heterogeneous agricultural landscape where, with few exceptions, the proportion of semi‐natural habitats exceeded the commonly recommended threshold of 20% needed to maintain natural pest control in agroecosystems. 22 , 41 , 66 , 94 , 95 Consequently, we did not observe the strong contrasts reported from simpler landscapes with very low proportions of semi‐natural habitats. For example, increases in carnivorous carabids species richness with increasing semi‐natural habitat cover are most evident in landscapes containing <10% semi‐natural habitat. 96 Additionally, as the abundance of the main oilseed rape pests, the pollen beetle and the cabbage seed weevil (Ceutorhynchus obstrictus Marsham) remained below economic threshold in both study years, based on per‐plant assessments, indicating effective natural pest control that maintained pest populations below control thresholds. 17 , 22

Increasing semi‐natural habitat proportion positively influenced carabid diversity, but not abundance and species richness, whereas no effects were observed on diversity, abundance, or species richness of staphylinids. Elevated functional diversity is often associated with enhanced pest regulation; 14 , 40 , 41 , 44 , 45 , 97 , 98 , 99 however, within‐field practices, particularly mechanical and agrochemical disturbances, 100 , 101 , 102 can reduce both abundance and diversity of beneficial arthropods. Consistent with previous findings carabid diversity and richness were higher under reduced tillage, whereas inversion tillage reduced carabid richness and activity‐density. 100 Although insecticide use tended to reduce carabid activity‐density, these effects were not statistically significant in pairwise comparisons. By contrast, neither tillage nor insecticide use affected staphylinid assemblages. Although pesticide effects on staphylinids remain less‐studied compared to carabids, existing evidence suggest that staphylinids are generally less sensitive to insecticide applications, 101 although combined effects of insecticide use, soil type and surrounding landscape context can negatively impact staphylinid populations – effects that may not be apparent when these factors are considered separately. 101 , 102 , 103 However, laboratory studies indicate that direct treatments with broad‐spectrum insecticides are harmful to staphylinids 104 and in long‐term intensive management systems the activity‐density of carabids declines and strong species turnover may occur. 105

Over the 2‐year study, we captured approximately one‐third of the carabid and staphylinid species documented at the national level, both in and adjacent to conventional winter oilseed rape fields. An earlier study in Estonian fields conducted on only two adjacent fields reported substantially smaller species richness; six of the 10 most abundant species overlapped between studies but their relative proportions differed, and in both studies P. cupreus was the most abundant species. 106 At the same time, the high species representation rate potentially obscures the influence of semi‐natural habitat proportion on species richness. The importance of the ‘fractal perspective’ suggested that the target of >20% semi‐natural habitat coverage needs to be applied at all spatial scales. 95 Different relationships could be revealed when our results are considered at smaller or larger scales, especially for the species with low spatial dispersal ability, such as flight‐reluctant carabids, as the scale in which they operate is smaller than compared to more mobile staphylinids.

Local‐scale effects

Additionally to overall habitat availability in the landscape, natural pest control depends on habitat type and quality which might have more powerful impacts. 107 , 108 , 109 , 110 Our results indicate that habitat type is a relevant factor shaping both the community structure and abundance of carabids and staphylinids. Our previous study shows that grassy field margins are important overwintering sites for both carabids and staphylinids, 111 whereas the results presented here indicate that both field‐and‐boundary as well as boundary‐overwintering species are influenced by both the type of field boundary habitat as well as their distance from it. Higher landscape connectivity and a higher proportion of woody linear elements are reported to support both species richness and the functional diversity of carabids, 97 implanting the crucial role of woody habitats around the arable fields in supporting beetle biodiversity. The combination of grassy and woody linear elements and fields provides diverse habitats that support beetle biodiversity in agricultural landscapes. In a similar way to the proportion of semi‐natural habitats, the supporting effect of hedgerows is more evident in homogenous landscapes than in heterogenous ones. 112 Meta‐analyses has likewise shown that enrichment of agricultural agroecosystems in simple landscapes are more effective than in already diverse ones. 113 In adjacent crop fields, sustained prey availability may facilitate rapid movement between fields, resulting in higher activity‐densities of predatory arthropods in response to prey availability rather than increased population abundance. Overall, our findings underscore the crucial role of boundary habitat types in supporting the diversity of both carabids and staphylinids, even within highly diverse agricultural landscapes.

The distance effect of carabids within fields was inconsistent. Their abundance was similar at the field edge and further towards the crop centre, whereas previous studies have found that their abundance either increases 35 , 88 or decreases 114 towards the crop centre. This suggest that other factors influence their distribution more than distance at the studied scale. Staphylinid abundance was, however, affected by the distance from the field edge, generally decreasing towards the centre of the field. Ground‐dwelling natural enemies respond differently to arable‐dominated landscapes: some species exploit arable fields and maintain their populations in landscape dominated by arable fields, whereas others decline. 40

Activity‐density patterns

Carabids and, to a lesser extent, staphylinids have been recognized as important predators in both natural and agricultural ecosystems. As mainly generalist predators they are recognized as important predators for several oilseed rape pests. 27 , 52 , 115 We studied how their community was affected by the adjacent habitat type and found higher activity densities for both carabids and staphylinids on the focal fields when adjacent to another crop field. The high abundance of carabids in our study was largely driven by a small number of very abundant species: P. cupreus, H. rufipes, A. similata, Pt. melanarius, H. affinis and A. dorsalis. Dominant species contribute more substantially to ecosystem service provision 6 and thus the presence of few highly abundant carnivorous or omnivorous species can significantly enhance natural pest control in agricultural fields. The predation rate of sentinel preys and abundance of pollen beetle larvae aligned with the abundance of carnivorous and omnivorous carabids and also with dominant predatory carabid P. cupreus, indicating their role in natural pest control in winter oilseed rape. Although the abundance of carabids also was the highest when the focal field was adjacent to another crop field, Shannon index scores showed constantly lower diversity in this habitat and Bray–Curtis distance matrix data indicated that the focal field communities are a subset of the adjacent habitat communities. Nevertheless, when a crop is attacked by various pests throughout the growing season, the natural pest control is enhanced by increased functional diversity of service providers, 6 , 98 and the shift towards fewer and more abundant species may decrease the agroecosystem resilience. The dominant carabid species in our study, P. cupreus, also constitutes a substantial portion of carabid communities in oilseed rape fields across Europe. 27 , 84 , 116 , 117 Previous studies have shown it to be dominant species during the period when pollen beetle larvae drop to the ground for pupation and are easily accessible for ground‐dwelling predators. 116 Although a large proportion of carabid species occur in oilseed rape fields throughout Europe, 27 , 51 , 84 , 117 , 118 their relative abundance and species composition differ remarkably reflecting both variations in climatic conditions and species distribution. The removal of dominant species can alter the recovery of natural pest control. 99

The activity‐density of predatory and omnivorous carabids and staphylinids also was related to prey abundance and prey consumption rate, indicating their important role in natural pest control. Accordingly, pest regulation in the study area, which is likely–at least partially–driven by the diverse community of these predators. However, it should be noted that predation is thought to mainly occur during night‐time and twilight periods, and that a substantial proportion of predation may be provided by larval stages of predatory insects active during these periods. 52 In contrast, our results reflect daytime activity and many carabid species, including the dominant species P. cupreus, are also active during the day, 58 indicating that daytime predation likely still contributes substantially to overall predation. Consequently, natural pest control in our system may be higher than estimated based on daytime measurements alone.

Our study highlights the complex relationship between landscape composition, arthropod diversity and its functioning within agricultural landscape. Even in a diverse agricultural landscape with a high proportion of semi‐natural habitats, the habitat type played significant role in shaping arthropod communities. Although landscape heterogeneity and semi‐natural habitats support species diversity and are likely to enhance long‐term stability of ecosystem services, our results indicate that short‐term pest control within crops is more directly linked to prey availability and the response of dominant predatory species, particularly P. cupreus.

CONCLUSIONS

Our findings provide new insights into natural pest control in agricultural systems. Even in landscapes with high semi‐natural habitat coverage, local factors such as adjacent habitat type, pesticide use, and tillage strongly shaped carabid and staphylinid communities, indicating that field‐scale management can substantially influence ecosystem service provision. Communities in focal fields represented subsets of those in adjacent semi‐natural habitats, highlighting their role as sources of biodiversity and contributors to the stability of pest control services. Predator activity‐density, particularly of dominant species such as P. cupreus and A. bilineata, tracked pollen beetle larval abundance and prey consumption rates, underscoring the importance of abundant generalist predators in pest regulation. Overall, although landscape heterogeneity and semi‐natural habitats are essential for maintaining biodiversity and long‐term resilience, effective pest suppression within crops depends on prey availability and the responses of dominant predators, emphasizing the need to integrate landscape configuration with targeted field‐scale management.
