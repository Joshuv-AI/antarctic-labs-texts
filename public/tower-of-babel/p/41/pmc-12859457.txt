First report of Candidozyma auris (Candida auris) in a cat: A case of persistent shedding
Authors: Andrea Grassi, Sara Rigamonti, Patrizia Danesi, Emanuela Olivieri, Sofia Sgubin, Paola Prati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Candidozyma (Candida) auris is a multidrug-resistant fungal pathogen of growing concern. We report the first isolation of C. auris in a domestic cat in Italy. The yeast was repeatedly recovered from feces over six months, suggesting persistent intestinal colonization. Isolation occurred during and after immunosuppressive treatment for eosinophilic granuloma complex. Antifungal susceptibility testing revealed resistance to azoles, amphotericin B and capsofungin. This case highlights the potential role of companion animals as reservoirs of C. auris, supporting a One Health perspective in fungal surveillance.

Introduction

Climate change is contributing to the emergence of fungal infections. Rising global temperatures promote the spread and adaptation of pathogens such as Candidozyma auris (formerly Candida auris), posing an increasing threat to public health [1]. Since its first report in Japan in 2009, C. auris has drawn significant attention from the scientific community, emerging as a multidrug-resistant pathogen capable of persisting and proliferating in healthcare settings and causing severe nosocomial outbreaks [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. To date, six genetically distinct clades of C. auris have been identified, having emerged independently in different geographical regions [[7], [8], [9]]. The number of reported infections worldwide continues to rise, prompting the World Health Organization to designate C. auris as a fungal pathogen of utmost concern [10].

C. auris demonstrates resistance to various antifungal drugs, including azoles, and readily colonizes the skin and mucosal surfaces of hosts as well as inanimate surfaces. It also withstands disinfectants commonly used in healthcare environments. Notably, C. auris can survive at temperatures up to 42 °C and in hypersaline conditions, suggesting that it may have originally been an environmental fungal species inhabiting humid or coastal areas [1]. This remarkable adaptability could facilitate its persistence and proliferation in new habitats and host organisms, including terrestrial and aquatic animals. Birds, in particular, may represent a potential reservoir, given their higher body temperature compared to mammals [11].

In terms of pathogenicity, C. auris is one of the few Candida species for which human-to-human transmission has been confirmed. Rapid skin or mucosal colonization can lead to bloodstream infections, especially in immunocompromised patients. The clinical manifestations of C. auris infections vary depending on the site of colonization and range from superficial infections to life-threatening systemic diseases, the latter being associated with high hospital mortality rates [12].

To date, C. auris has not been isolated from wild animals; nevertheless, recent evidence suggests its potential presence in a variety of non-human environments. An in silico study identified the presence of C. auris ITS regions in non-human sources, including atmospheric dust, the ear canal of a dog with otitis [13], peanut fields, and the epidermis of newts [14]. More recently, C. auris has been detected in the ear canal and on the skin of stray dogs suffering from chronic skin infections and otitis, as well as in cobras used by snake charmers in Morocco [15].

Case presentation

A 6-year-old spayed female Domestic Shorthair cat (Fig. 1), living in Northern Italy with regular outdoor access, has been affected by eosinophilic granuloma complex since 2022. Clinical signs include an indolent ulcer on the tongue, alopecia of the limbs, and, occasionally, ulcers on the upper lip. During periods of clinical worsening, the cat is treated with therapeutic cycles of either cyclosporine or corticosteroids.Fig. 1Nina, a domestic shorthair cat with persistent C. auris shedding.Fig. 1

As part of a research project on the occurrence of Candida auris in animals, selective screening for the yeast was performed on fecal samples. Six fecal samples were collected starting in May 2024 and subsequently at 1, 3, 6, 9, and 15 months.

At the time of the first sampling (time 0), the cat was completing a course of immunosuppressive therapy, whereas during all subsequent fecal samplings, no pharmacological treatment was being administered.

Fungal culture and identification procedures

Fecal samples were analyzed within 4 hours of collection and plated onto Selective Auris Medium (SAM), as described by Das et al. (2021) [16], and incubated at 41.5 °C for 7 days. In parallel, 1 g of feces was inoculated into 9 mL of Salt Sabouraud Enrichment (Salt SAB) broth following the protocol by Lorenz et al. (2023) and incubated at 41.5 °C for 7 days [17]. Subsequently, 100 μL of the resulting broth culture was streaked onto SAM and incubated at 41.5 °C for an additional 7 days.

Fungal colonies grown on SAM were subcultured onto Sabouraud dextrose agar plates (Ditta) and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Gram staining was repeated, and isolates were identified using a MALDI-TOF mass spectrometer (Biotyper®, Bruker Daltonics GmbH & Co. KG., Bremen, Germany), following the manufacturer's instructions for fungal identification. Molecular confirmation was performed by PCR and sequencing of the ITS1 and ITS2 of the 26S rDNA genomic regions, as previously described [18] at the IZSVe.

Cultures were positive at time 0 both by direct plating and after enrichment in broth. At 1, 3, and 6 months, fungal growth was observed exclusively after enrichment in Salt SAB broth. At 9 and 15 months, all cultures tested negative.

The fungal colonies grown at time points 0, 1, 3, and 6 months were morphologically and microscopically consistent with Candida spp. Identification by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry classified the isolates as Candida auris, with score values ranging from 1.95 to 2.00.

Antifungal susceptibility testing

The isolate was tested with the Sensititre™ YeastOne™ (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) system to determine the lowest concentration of antifungal that inhibits yeast growth (Minimum Inhibitory Concentrations - MICs). Antifungal susceptibility testing (AFST) was carried out in duplicate at the IZSVe following the manufacturer's instructions and results were interpreted according to the CDC guidelines updated in April 2024. The fungal isolate was found to be susceptible to anidulafungin and micafungin, and resistant to fluconazole, amphotericin B, and caspofungin.

No clinical breakpoints are currently available for voriconazole and posaconazole; these agents are considered potential therapeutic options only in the presence of low MIC values. Similarly, itraconazole and 5-flucytosine are regarded as unreliable agents and are not recommended for the treatment of Candida auris. Discussion

The microbiological protocol used—originally optimized for human skin and mucosal samples—also proved effective for animal fecal specimens, demonstrating high specificity in our case by exclusively isolating Candida auris colonies.

A difference was observed between the first sample (time 0), in which Candida auris grew directly on the SAM plate, and the subsequent samples (time 1, 3, 6), in which the yeast was isolated only after selective enrichment in broth. The direct culture positivity observed, likely reflecting a higher fungal burden, may be attributable to the concurrent immunosuppressive therapy, which could have promoted or facilitated intestinal overgrowth of the yeast.

Predisposing factors identified in the cat included the periodic administration of immunosuppressive drugs (cyclosporine and corticosteroids) and the presence of a chronic granulomatous inflammatory condition. The source of exposure, however, could not be determined. The identification of C. auris in a feline host, underscores the need to investigate alternative environmental reservoirs and potential interspecies transmission pathways in order to elucidate mechanisms of acquisition and evaluate epidemiological implications.

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first report of Candida auris in a cat, and the first documented case in an animal in Italy.

The repeated isolation of the yeast from feces over a period of at least six months documents a persistent carrier state, suggesting either (i) an intestinal colonization, (ii) a transient passage if the microorganism is able to survive the gastric barrier after ingestion (e.g., from self-grooming and ingestion of yeast from the skin), or (iii) a rectal colonization, as occasionally described in humans. Further studies addressing the ability of C. auris to withstand the digestive process and its potential for rectal colonization would be valuable to clarify whether this finding represents evidence of a notable degree of fitness of the microorganism within the feline intestinal microbiota.

The identification of azole-resistant Candida auris in a domestic cat, along with its prolonged shedding over time, underscores the potential role of companion animals as reservoirs of multidrug-resistant yeasts. This finding is particularly significant given the growing concern surrounding C. auris as an emerging nosocomial pathogen, and it highlights the critical need for a One Health approach to better understand its ecology and transmission. Since the cat was clinically asymptomatic, antifungal treatment was not deemed necessary. The owner was appropriately informed about the detection of Candida auris and its potential implications for hygiene and public health. The fungal strain was deposited at the Biobank of Veterinary Resources-IZSLER (http://www.ibvr.org/) under the code FUN LO SPV 11.
