Identification of a novel EYA4 likely pathogenic variant in a Chinese family with postlingual non-syndromic hearing loss and analysis of molecular epidemiology of EYA4 variants

Background

Genetic factors contribute to approximately 60% of deafness cases, with autosomal dominant hearing loss accounting for approximately 20–25% of inherited hearing loss [1]. To date, 51 autosomal-dominant genes have been identified (http://hereditaryhearingloss.org/). Most patients present with postlingual hearing loss, and the degree of hearing loss gradually increases until severe or profound deafness. Due to its insidious onset and slow progression, early detection is difficult. Therefore, genetic screening can help in the early detection and intervention of inherited hearing loss, effectively reducing the hearing disability rate and improving patient quality of life.

The EYA4 gene was identified in 1996 as the pathogenic gene responsible for DFNA10 deafness in an American family [2]. EYA4 is located on chromosome 6q22.3-q23.2, spans 21 exons, encodes 640 amino acids starting from exon 2 [3]. To date, more than 50 EYA4 variants have been reported to cause autosomal dominant non-syndromic hearing loss (ADNSHL).

As a transcriptional coactivator, EYA4 plays a key regulatory role in the development of multiple organs, including the eye, muscle, ear, heart, lung, endocrine glands, placode, pharyngeal pouch, craniofacial skeleton, and parathyroid gland [4]. It is associated with two clinical phenotypes: (1) Autosomal dominant deafness type 10 (DFNA10), non-syndromic sensorineural hearing loss. (2) Dilated cardiomyopathy(DCM): DCM is a disease characterized by ventricular enlargement and cardiac systolic dysfunction, which leads to difficult cardiac congestion and irregular heartbeat, with patients at a risk of sudden death. The clinical phenotype in this study was DFNA10, and none of the families had any heart-related diseases.

DFNA10 hearing loss, caused by EYA4 variants, is highly heterogeneous. That is, similar variants can give rise to different clinical phenotypes and vice versa. In this study, we report a novel EYA4 variant identified in a Chinese family with postlingual hearing loss, thus adding to the variant spectrum of EYA4. Further, we summarize the research progress on DFNA10 hereditary deafness caused by EYA4 variants, providing a pathogenic variant spectrum and an analysis of phenotype-genotype correlation as well as the possible pathogenic mechanism. The current study highlights the importance of genetic testing for early intervention in patients with familial progressive sensorineural hearing loss.

Methods

Subjects

The studied family has lived in Shandong Province for four generations (Fig. 1a). This study was approved by the Committee of Medical Ethics of the Chinese People’s Liberation Army (PLA) General Hospital. Written consent was obtained from all participants aged > 18 years and from their next of kin on behalf of the minor/child participants. All experiments were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and other relevant guidelines and regulations.

The proband (Fig. 1a, IV.2), a 17-year-old boy, visited the otolaryngology outpatient department of our hospital complaining of bilateral progressive hearing loss with tinnitus in both ears since the age of 12 years. There were no other influencing factors, such as systemic organ dysplasia, infectious diseases, history of ototoxic drug use, or obvious history of noise exposure. The proband underwent systematic physical and audiological examinations including pure-tone audiometry, acoustic immittance, speech perception, distorted otoacoustic emission (DPOAE), and auditory brainstem response (ABR). Imaging studies were performed and syndromic deafness was ruled out (Fig. 2a). His father (III.3), grandfather (II.3), and great-grandmother (I.2) also had hearing loss (Fig. 1a). His father started developing hearing loss at 20 years old, his grandfather at 16 years old, and the great-grandmother at 20 years old or so.

Clinical characteristics of the proband: a normal MRI; b The audiogram shows mild to moderate hearing loss at medium-high frequency, c The acoustic immittance is expressed As; d. DPOAE is not elicited; e ABR corresponds to audiogram

We used an auxiliary examination diagnostic audiometer (Madsen Conera, Denmark), headphones with an air guide (TDD-39, Denmark), bone guide (b-71, Denmark), along with an acoustic conductivity detector (Madsen OTO Flex100, Denmark), for conducting pure-tone audiometry (PTA) and acoustic immittance tests. The instruments were calibrated according to international standards. The audiological tests were conducted in standard soundproof rooms. Hearing loss levels were graded into the following categories based on pure-tone air conduction averages: mild (26–40 dB HL), moderate (41–55 dB HL), moderately severe (56–70 dB HL), severe (71–90 dB HL), and profound (> 91 dB HL).

Genetic test

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed on DNA samples using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Shanghai, China) with quality metrics of 20X coverage > 90% and depth > 80%. DNA was quantified using a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The amplified DNA was captured using the GenCap Whole Exon Capture Kit (MyGenostics Inc., Beijing, China). Raw data were subjected to bioinformatics analysis after sequencing. Clean reads were mapped to the UCSC hg19 human reference genome. Variants were detected using several software package and multiple databases, including 1000 genomes, ESP6500, dbSNP, EXAC, Inhouse (MyGenostics), and HGMD. To further predict the function of potential pathological variants, SIFT, PolyPhen-2, MutationTaster and GERP tools were used. Whole-genome copy number variation (CNV) analysis was performed as described above. Raw image files were processed using Bcl2Fastq software for base calling and data generation, filtering out low-quality variations with a quality score ≥ 20. Clean reads were aligned to the reference human genome (hg19). The CNVkit software was employed to acquire copy number variation date. Sanger sequencing was used to confirm family co-segregation. Genomic DNA samples were collected from all available family members.

Results

Clinical evaluation

Physical examination indicated normal external auditory canal and middle ear findings in the proband. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed no abnormalities in the inner ear, internal auditory canal, or brain (Fig. 2a). Pure-tone audiometry showed that the proband had mild-to-moderate hearing loss at mid-to-high frequency, and bilateral hearing loss was symmetrical downsloping(Fig. 2b). The bilateral maximal speech discrimination rates were 72% (left ear) and 64% (right ear), respectively. The acoustic immittance was of type As (Fig. 2c). The distortion product otoacoustic emission (DPOAE) showed that the waveform was not elicited (Fig. 2d). The auditory brainstem response (ABR) showed a hearing threshold of 40 dB nHL, with normal waveform latency and I-V wave interval were normal, corresponding to audiogram (Fig. 2e).

Genetic test

The patient sample was analyzed and found to harbour a heterozygous variant of the EYA4 gene. The ACMG Genetic Variation Interpretation is detailed below: c.1745_1748del (exon19, NM_004100.5), leading to an amino acid change p.Glu582ValfsTer6, a frameshift variant. According to the Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG) guidelines, the variant was initially determined as a likely pathogenic “PVS1 + PM2_Supporting”. PVS1—This is a null variant (frameshift variant) that may lead to loss of gene function. PM2_Supporting—The frequency in the normal population database was -. This site has not been reported in the literature, and a pathogenicity analysis of this site has not been reported in the ClinVar database. As verified via Sanger sequencing, the proband’s father (III.3) and grandfather (II.3) carried a heterozygous c.1745_1748del variant in EYA4. No variants were found in the patient’s mother (III.4), uncle (III.1), or grandmother (II.4) (Fig. 1b). Sanger sequencing confirmed co-segregation of the genetic variant with the phenotype of the family.

Literature review

Thirty-three studies were included in our review [3, 5–36]. To date, 52 EYA4 variants have been identified likely pathogenic/pathogenic variants. Among these, there are 16 missense, 13 frameshift, nine nonsense, six splicing, and eight large fragment deletion variants. Most observed are the missense and frameshift variants. The majority of the 52 pathogenic variants were identified in Asian populations: 15 variants have been identified in Japan, 10 in China (including the variant reported in this study), and four in Korea. In Europe, most variants (eight) have been reported in Spain (Fig. 3, Table 1). No hotspot variant in EYA4 were found, and most of the variants did not recur. Discussion

To date, more than 50 genes and 80 loci have been identified for ADNSHL, but due to its insidious onset and slow progression, hearing loss in ADNSHL is usually challenging to detect early in clinical settings, resulting many children with mild or progressive forms of hearing loss (HL) remain undiagnosed during their critical period of speech development and school learning. Therefore, it is necessary to develop an effective method of genetic screening that ensures early diagnosis of genetic HL and enabling timely intervention. For this purpose, it is particularly important to explore the epidemiological characteristics and the correlation of genotype and phenotype of different genes. In this study, we identified a novel EYA4 variant, c.1745_1748del (p.Glu582ValfsTer6), in a Chinese family with non-syndromic ADNSHL, and performed a literature review of known EYA4 variants analyzing variant frequency, distribution characteristics, and genotype-phenotype correlations.

Unlike autosomal recessive non-syndromic HL, which most cases are caused by mutations of the GJB2 gene, ADNSHL does not have a single identifiable gene responsible for the majority of cases worldwide. According to the review of Aldè M,et al. in 2023, MYO6, TECTA, ACTG1, WFS1, POU4F3, KCNQ4 and EYA4 are some of the most common forms genes of ADNSHL, but the responsible genes differ among ethnic groups [37]. For example, in Europe, the most common forms of ADNSHL are MYO6 and TECTA, accounting for 21% and 18% of all cases, respectively [38]. KCNQ4 is the most frequent causative gene for ADNSHL in the Japanese population, whereas TECTA is the most frequent causative gene in the American population [39]. From our research of the literature review, we found most variants in EYA4 have been identified in Asian populations. There are most variant types have been identified in Japan—15 variants, following by 10 variants in China (including the variant reported in this study), and four in Korea. In Europe, eight variants have been reported in Spain.

In a large-scale genetic screening for deafness, the prevalence of EYA4-related hearing loss in ADNSHL patients was reported 0.90% (121,334 cases) in the Japanese population [7]. By screening 531 Spanish familial cases for variants, Morin et al. [6] identified eight variants of the EYA4 gene and concluded that the global prevalence of EYA4 in the DFNA10 hearing-impaired population in Spain was approximately 1.5% (8 of 531). Kim et al. [15] identified four pathogenic EYA4 variants in 87 patients with ADNSHL in South Korea. Choi et al. [22] reported a case of DFNA10 deafness caused by an EYA4 variant in 14 Korean families with ANSHL and moderate hearing loss, estimating a prevalence of 4.6–7.14% in Korea. A genetic screening was conducted on 116 cases of sensorineural hearing loss in the Han population in China, and only one variant was found, thus the authors estimated the incidence rate of EYA at 0.86% [5].

The characteristics of ADNSHL are heterogenous. In most cases, HL tends to be bilateral, postlingual, high-frequency, progressive, and variable in severity. As a kind of ADNSHL, the HL of EYA4 variants is postlingual, progressive, and bilateral symmetrical. The pattern of the HL typically limited to middle frequency initially, referred to as a "Cookie-bite" audiogram [7]. However, the HL of EYA4 also includes low frequency decline, mid-to-high frequency decline, flat type, and even an inverted U-shaped pattern as reported (Table 1). The proband in this study exhibited mild-to-moderate hearing loss characterized by mid-to-high frequency downsloping. From this study and previous reports, we can summarize that the HL pattern of EYA4 is not related to the variant type. This is consistent with the findings reported by the follow scholars. Zhang et al. [40] summarized EYA4 variants and the corresponding degree of hearing loss in 2020, suggesting that the severity of hearing loss is not related to the type or location of the variant. Kim et al. [15] suggested that there is no genotype-phenotype correlation in EYA4-related hearing loss. We believe that the genotype-phenotype correlation of EYA4 is unclear, the audiological features of EYA4 variants are highly heterogeneous.

EYA4 is located on chromosome 6q22.3-q23.2 and includes two structural functional domains. These two domains, namely eya variable region (eyaVR) and eya homologous region (eyaHR), have distinct functions. EyaVR is located at the N-terminus, acting as a transcriptional activator and threonine phosphatase. EyaHR is situated at the C-terminus and plays a role in forming co-transcription factors within the nucleus, while also housing tyrosine phosphatase activity [3]. The novel EYA4 exon 19 variants reported in this study, c.1745_1748del, similar to previously studied EYA4 variants, is expected to affect the eyaHR domain, which is essential for the continued function of the mature organ of Corti after development.

The precise molecular mechanisms underlying hearing loss caused by EYA4 gene variants also remains unclear. Many researchers have hypothesized that it is associated with the haploinsufficiency. Zhang et al. [41] conducted in vitro cellular experiments using a mouse model with the EYA4 HRR564X construct and suggested that variants in EYA4 cause the protein degradation, leading to a haploinsufficiency and causing deafness. Shinagawa et al. [7] screened 2,672 alleles and identified nine truncation variants of the EYA4 gene, further indicating that haploinsufficiency may be a mechanism for EYA4-related hearing loss. Morin et al. [6] analyzed and summarized as follows: Frameshift and nonsense variants lead to nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, ultimately causing deafness; Some missense variants are attributed to disruption of the acceptor splice site, such as c.1282-1G > A and c.1282-12 T > A; For missense variants that do not impact splicing, haploinsufficiency is also suggested as the pathogenic mechanism via reduced protein activity. Additionally, EYA4 pathogenicity may also be related to abnormal function of the Na + /K + -ATPase in inner ear sensory cells, where it is involved in hair cell development and maintenance [42].

Conclusions

In conclusion, a novel EYA4 variant, c.1745_1748del (p.Glu582ValfsTer6), was identified in a Chinese family with ADNSHL using WES and Sanger sequencing. This expands the spectrum of variants in the EYA4 gene and autosomal dominant non-syndromic hearing loss in the Chinese population, emphasizing the importance of genetic testing in patients with familial progressive sensorineural hearing loss for earlier intervention.
