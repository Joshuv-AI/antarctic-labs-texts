Development and validation of diagnostic and prognostic models for heart failure in unstable angina patients
Authors: Lingling Zhang, Li Peng, Zhican Liu, Juan Chen, Ke Peng, Mingxin Wu, Xianghong Zhou, Mingyan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop diagnostic models to predict initial heart failure (HF) hospitalisation in patients with unstable angina (UA) and prognostic models to predict rehospitalisation for recurrent or new-onset HF after discharge, aiming to enhance early identification and patient care strategies.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed data from 12 857 acute coronary syndrome patients (January 2015 to March 2023). After screening, 7092 UA patients were included and randomly divided into a training cohort (4964) and a validation cohort (2128). Logistic regression and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression identified risk factors. Diagnostic models were developed and evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curves. Using the same predictors, prognostic models predicted rehospitalisation at 1, 2 and 6 months postdischarge.ResultsThe lambda.1se criterion in LASSO regression identified three key risk factors: left ventricular diameter, N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide and ischaemic cardiomyopathy. The diagnostic model’s area under the curve (AUC) was 0.938 (95% CI 0.909 to 0.955) in the training cohort and 0.931 (95% CI 0.901 to 0.956) in the validation cohort, showing consistent and reliable performance. Prognostic models for 1, 2 and 6 months postdischarge had AUC values of 0.770, 0.794 and 0.751, respectively, supporting their utility in prognostic evaluations.ConclusionThe UA diagnostic and prognostic HF model was developed and validated. These models can quickly identify high-risk patients, enabling prompt and tailored interventions.

Introduction

As society ages and lifestyles evolve, there has been a noticeable increase in the prevalence of coronary artery disease. Among its various forms, unstable angina (UA) is a common manifestation.1 UA accounts for approximately 38% of all acute coronary syndrome (ACS) cases, according to epidemiological studies.2 Despite significant advancements in medical technology and treatment strategies, the incidence of heart failure (HF) and subsequent rehospitalisation rates among UA patients remains notably high.3 Recent data indicate that the incidence of HF in patients with UA is approximately 27.4%.3 The onset of HF not only impacts a patient’s quality of life and overall prognosis4 5 but also significantly strains healthcare resources, leading to increased costs.6 Therefore, early risk assessment of HF in UA patients is crucial for devising individualised treatment plans and making accurate prognostic evaluations.

In recent years, although predictive models concerning UA and HF have been proposed,7 a comprehensive and reliable evaluation model for assessing the risk of initial hospitalisation and subsequent rehospitalisation due to HF in UA patients is still lacking.

This study aims to bridge this gap by developing a diagnostic model to identify UA patients at risk of developing HF during hospitalisation. Using the same set of predictors, we further conducted a prognostic validation analysis to examine their association with postdischarge rehospitalisation risk at 1, 2 and 6 months.

We expect the UA diagnostic and prognostic (DP)-HF model, encompassing both diagnostic and prognostic implications, to be a valuable tool for clinicians. Designed to enhance the assessment and management of this unique patient group, this model promotes early detection, timely interventions and tailored treatment strategies.

Patients meeting exclusion criteria 6 and 7 were removed during initial screening and therefore were not included among the 12 857 patients enrolled in this study.

Among the remaining 12 857 patients, we excluded 4120 with STEMI or NSTEMI, 609 with a history of myocardial infarction, 297 with a history of HF and 739 with renal insufficiency (as detailed in figure 1). After these exclusions, a total of 7092 patients with UA were included in the final analysis. Data collection

Demographic and procedural data were extracted from hospital records and electronic databases. This included demographic details, medical history before admission, clinical and laboratory findings at admission (including vital signs, white blood cell count, haemoglobin, platelets, creatinine, lipid profile and troponin), echocardiographic parameters (such as left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and wall motion) and treatment information.

Follow-up and outcome events

Follow-up for all study participants continued until 1 October 2023. A team comprising five cardiologists and 10 nurses conducted follow-up via outpatient visits and telephone interviews to collect data on patient outcomes. Follow-up for rehospitalisation due to HF and myocardial infarction has been completed, whereas analyses of other outcome events are ongoing.

HF was defined according to the European Society of Cardiology guidelines. It was identified based on hospital discharge diagnoses coded using specific International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) (I50.0, I50.1 and I50.9) and was confirmed through a comprehensive review of clinical records. This review included identifying typical symptoms (eg, dyspnoea, fatigue), signs (eg, elevated jugular venous pressure), echocardiographic evidence of structural or functional abnormalities and elevated natriuretic peptide levels.

Ischaemic cardiomyopathy was defined as a history of significant coronary artery disease and/or prior myocardial infarction with imaging evidence of chronic ischaemia-related myocardial remodelling, such as segmental wall motion abnormalities or left ventricular dilation, regardless of the presence of HF symptoms. Only patients with a documented clinical diagnosis of HF prior to admission were excluded from the study.

Ethics and informed consent

The Ethics Committee of Xiangtan Central Hospital in Xiangtan, China, approved the study protocol (ethical approval no: 2023-02-001), aligning with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective nature of this study, which solely involved collecting clinical data without altering patient treatment regimens, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (V.4.2.0, available at http://www.R-project.org).

Baseline characteristics analysis: The compare groups package was used for automated analysis. Normally distributed continuous variables were summarised as mean±SD and non-normally distributed as median (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages (n (%)).

Identification of risk factors for initial hospitalisation due to HF: Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed, employing the glm package. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was chosen for its ability to handle high-dimensional clinical data, perform automatic variable selection and reduce multicollinearity.

The glmnet package was applied to develop models. We performed 10-fold cross-validation to identify the optimal penalty parameter (lambda). Two criteria were evaluated: lambda-min (minimum binomial deviance) and lambda-1-standard error (1se) (the most regularised value within one SE of the minimum). We selected the lambda-1se criterion to prioritise model parsimony and reduce overfitting.

To correct for the bias (shrinkage) introduced by the LASSO penalty, we used a postselection inference approach (refitting). The specific predictors identified by the LASSO model were entered into a standard, unpenalised multivariate logistic regression (using the glm function) to estimate the final unbiased coefficients, ORs and p values presented in the results. The diagnostic performance of this model was subsequently evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.

A total of 7092 UA patients were included in the analysis, among whom 163 developed in-hospital new-onset HF, constituting the event group for model development. The 1SE model retained only three predictors—left ventricular diameter (LVd), N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP)/100 and ischaemic cardiomyopathy—yielding an events-per-variable ratio of approximately 54, which far exceeds the commonly recommended threshold of 10. This ensured sufficient statistical power and minimised the risk of overfitting. The same three variables were then applied in multivariable Cox regression models using the survival::coxph function to predict HF-related rehospitalisation risk at 1, 2 and 6 months postdischarge.

Model development and evaluation: The model incorporated all selected factors. Its diagnostic performance was evaluated using ROC curves generated with the pROC package. Model calibration was assessed through the calibrate function and val.prob in the rms package. Additionally, 200 bootstrap resamples were employed for internal validation to prevent overfitting and estimate optimism-corrected performance.

Net benefit and model performance analysis: The model’s net benefit was quantified via decision curve analysis (DCA) using the dca.R package.

Comparative analysis of multiple models: The ROC curves of different models were compared using the ROCR package (a tool for evaluating and visualizing classifier performance).

Missing data were handled using a hybrid approach. Patients with missing values in key predictive variables retained in the final model (LVd, NT-proBNP/100 and ischaemic cardiomyopathy) were excluded from the regression analysis (complete-case analysis). For other variables, missing values were imputed using single imputation methods—median imputation for continuous variables and mode imputation for categorical variables.

All statistical tests were two-sided. A p value below 0.05 was deemed indicative of statistical significance. These methodologies evaluated the model’s development, discriminatory ability and calibration performance.

LASSO regression

The LASSO regression technique identified a minimal model (min) with 19 predictive variables and a 1se model with three predictive variables, as depicted in online supplemental figure 1. These models’ specific variables and Lambda values are detailed in online supplemental table 1. Comparative analysis of the min model (model A) and the 1se model (model B) revealed that in the training set, models A and B had area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.945 and 0.938, respectively (p=0.5523), while in the validation set, their AUCs were 0.923 and 0.931, respectively (p=0.6385), as shown in online supplemental figure 2. Given the negligible differences between the models and the enhanced simplicity and clinical applicability of model B, we opted for the latter. Model B, with its three factors—LVd, NT-proBNP/100 and ischaemic cardiomyopathy—was chosen to predict the risk of initial hospitalisation for HF in UA patients.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

Discussion

In this research, we developed diagnostic and prognostic models for patients with UA. The diagnostic model is designed to predict the initial hospitalisation for HF, while the prognostic model aims to predict the likelihood of HF rehospitalisation after discharge. As depicted in the central illustration, these models incorporate significant variables, including LVd, NT-proBNP levels and ischaemic cardiomyopathy (online supplemental figure 4).

ACS encompasses a spectrum of conditions, including UA and acute myocardial infarction (AMI). Previous studies have extensively investigated the prognosis of ACS.37 911 Notably, UA, while part of the ACS spectrum, presents unique predictive factors for in-hospital prognosis that differ significantly from those associated with STEMI and NSTEMI. This distinction underscores the need for dedicated research studies to address these variations.3

Unlike broader ACS models, validating these markers specifically in UA patients allows for more tailored risk stratification in a population that typically lacks the extensive myocardial damage seen in AMI but remains vulnerable to HF. In the broader field of coronary heart disease prevention, several recent studies have developed risk prediction models tailored to specific high-risk populations, such as patients with type 2 diabetes or snoring-related hypertension.1214 These works highlight the increasing attention to population-specific modelling approaches, which also support the rationale for developing dedicated models for UA patients.

Among the variables identified by our model, LVd, NT-proBNP and ischaemic cardiomyopathy are well-recognised indicators of cardiac remodelling and dysfunction. An increased LVd reflects left ventricular dilation, which is associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes and progressive myocardial remodelling.15 Elevated NT-proBNP levels indicate haemodynamic stress and neurohormonal activation and are widely used for the diagnosis and risk stratification of HF.16 Ischaemic cardiomyopathy, resulting from prior myocardial injury or chronic hypoperfusion, impairs myocardial contractility and increases susceptibility to decompensation.

From a clinical perspective, patients with elevated NT-proBNP, enlarged LVd and ischaemic aetiology should be considered at increased risk of HF hospitalisation. Management of HF in UA differs from other conditions; unlike stable HF where neurohormonal blockade is the primary focus, HF in UA is often driven by reversible ischaemia. Therefore, our findings suggest that these high-risk patients might benefit from prioritising aggressive revascularisation strategies to salvage hibernating myocardium, alongside standard guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT). However, as our study is observational, further interventional trials are needed to confirm whether guiding therapy based on these markers directly improves clinical outcomes.

Findings

Our predictive model has demonstrated a notable association between an increase in LVd and a heightened risk of HF among patients with UA. This finding is consistent with previous research, highlighting the pivotal role of LVd in evaluating HF risk in ACS patients.17 Additionally, our study emphasises the critical importance of revascularisation for restoring left ventricular function in these individuals.18 We also observed a significant link between elevated NT-proBNP levels and an increased risk of HF in UA patients, aligning with the conclusions of other studies.1921 Importantly, our research identifies ischaemic cardiomyopathy as a key predictive factor for HF risk in UA patients, corroborating prior investigations into its relationship with ACS.22 These insights further underscore the significance of ischaemic cardiomyopathy in HF prediction models.

Limitations

While our models demonstrate robust performance in our dataset, it is essential to note that they are derived from retrospective data at a single centre without external validation, potentially introducing patient selection and information biases. Additionally, there are other risk indicators for HF in UA patients not encompassed by our model, such as sex differences23 and high-sensitivity troponin levels,24 which have been implicated in increased HF risk.

LVEF, a clinically significant predictor of cardiac dysfunction, was not included in our models due to incomplete echocardiographic data and the ongoing development of a dedicated imaging-based study by our team.

Enhanced management of ACS patients has been linked to considerable reductions in the incidence of new-onset HF, mortality, as well as the rates of stroke and myocardial infarction at the 6-month mark.9 Consequently, further research involving a wider patient cohort in multicentre prospective studies is essential to ascertain the generalisability and accuracy of our models.

Finally, while we discuss management implications, our retrospective dataset precluded stratification by specific diagnostic modality (invasive coronary angiography vs coronary CT angiography) or revascularisation strategy and lacked granular details on therapeutic protocols (eg, dosage adjustments). Consequently, the potential influence of these specific procedural choices on the model’s predictions could not be quantitatively assessed. Additionally, detailed baseline medication history (eg, beta-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors) was not included due to incomplete records. Similarly, while we accounted for the presence of arrhythmias and valvular disease, we lacked aetiological quantification, such as distinguishing transient precipitants from persistent ischaemic dysfunction, 24-hour premature ventricular contraction burden or specific angiographic culprit lesion characteristics. Future prospective studies incorporating detailed Holter monitoring and quantitative coronary angiography could further refine the aetiological weighting of the model.

Furthermore, this study focused exclusively on HF outcomes; other major ACS complications such as recurrent angina, bleeding or arrhythmias were not analysed. Thus, our results reflect only one aspect of UA prognosis and should be interpreted within this specific context.

Clinical significance

Our study addresses a notable gap in the existing literature by providing predictive insights for HF risk in UA patients. Furthermore, our findings hold substantial relevance for clinical practice. The early identification of high-risk UA patients, coupled with tailored treatment and management strategies, has the potential to significantly reduce both the incidence of HF and related hospitalisation rates. Future research should explore effectively integrating these predictive models into clinical workflows and refine them to enhance accuracy and reliability across diverse patient demographics.

Compared with previous models developed for broader ACS populations, the present study uniquely focuses on UA patients and identifies a concise set of predictors with strong discriminative ability (AUC >0.93). This simplicity enhances interpretability and practical implementation in routine clinical settings.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated diagnostic and prognostic models for initial HF hospitalisation and subsequent rehospitalisation in UA patients, termed the UADP-HF models. Key predictive markers in these models include LVd, NT-proBNP levels and ischaemic cardiomyopathy. These models demonstrate potential utility for clinical practice, offering high precision in HF risk prediction. While promising, further prospective research is warranted to assess their generalisability and their ultimate impact on patient management decisions.
