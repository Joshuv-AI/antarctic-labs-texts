A rare case of Streptococcus pyogenes vertebral osteomyelitis in a young, immunocompetent male
Authors: Saran Lal Ajai Mokan Dasan, Balakrishnan Arivalagan, Pranav Singla, Yusra Shah, Raydi Mejia Landron, Angel Barrera Ventura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStreptococcus pyogenes (Group A Streptococcus, GAS) is a well-recognized cause of invasive infections. However, it’s an uncommon etiologic microorganism in adult vertebral osteomyelitis. We report a rare case of vertebral osteomyelitis, discitis, epidural abscess, and bilateral psoas abscesses caused by GAS in an otherwise healthy male. Early diagnosis via imaging, microbiological confirmation, and prompt initiation of intravenous antibiotics led to clinical improvement and prevented potentially devastating complications. This case underscores another clinical presentation of invasive GAS infection and highlights the clinical course and management of this rare, but serious condition.Keywords: Streptococcus pyogenes, Group A Streptococcus, Vertebral osteomyelitis, Immunocompetent

Introduction

Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Streptococcus, GAS) causes invasive infections such as necrotizing fasciitis, toxic shock syndrome, and bacteremia [1]. However, vertebral osteomyelitis due to GAS is unusual in the healthy adult population. This case report highlights S. pyogenes as the confirmed pathogen isolated directly from the psoas abscess in an otherwise healthy adult with multifocal spinal infection.

Case presentation

A man in his early forties with no prior co morbidities presented with worsening lower back pain of 6 weeks duration following trauma. The pain was lancinating with nocturnal exacerbations and localised to his L3 - L5 vertebrae without neurological symptoms. Two weeks later, he noticed pain in his thigh, particularly with hip extension. He denied fever and constitutional symptoms. He is an active smoker and denies intravenous drug use. On examination, he had paraspinal tenderness corresponding to the L3 - L5 regions and a positive Psoas sign. There were no lacerations or puncture wounds. His complete blood count revealed marked neutrophilic leucocytosis, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) were both elevated as in Table1. Contrast enhanced computed tomography (CECT) revealed with bilateral psoas abscesses as shown in Fig. 1. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with contrast of the lumbar vertebrae showed L3–L4 vertebral osteomyelitis, discitis, and an epidural phlegmon as seen in Fig. 2. CT guided aspiration of the psoas abscess Gram staining revealed gram positive cocci in chains and aerobic culture grew Streptococcus pyogenes. His blood and urine cultures were without growth. A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) ruled out valvular vegetations.

He was initially started on IV vancomycin and aztreonam, with eventual transition to IV ceftriaxone (2 g daily) as the isolates were penicillin and cephalosporin susceptible. The white blood cell count, and inflammatory markers decreased within two doses of antibiotics. A marked clinical response was noticed within a week of antibiotic therapy. The patient reported complete symptom resolution after 8 weeks of treatment and radiological resolution in 12 weeks.

Discussion

Streptococci are gram-positive cocci that grows in liquid media. Based on hemolysis, Lancefield classified them as alpha, beta, and gamma hemolytic streptococci. The β- hemolytic streptococci are further classified as group A, B, C, and G. Each is associated with characteristic patterns of human infection. Group A streptococci (GAS) consist of a single species, Streptococcus pyogenes. GAS primarily causes pharyngitis, otitis, skin infection (namely erysipelas, cellulitis and notable invasive infections such as necrotizing fasciitis). Of note, acute rheumatic fever, post streptococcal glomerulonephritis and toxic shock syndrome are devastating complications of GAS infections [1], [2]. Murillo, O. et al., conducted the largest descriptive study on streptococci vertebral osteomyelitis (N = 58) and reported S. pyogenes in only two of the cases [3]. The risk factors for streptococci VO are advanced age, immunocompromised state, active intravenous drug use (IVDU), individuals with indwelling devices, or recent instrumentation [4], [5]. In addition, pharyngitis and soft tissue infections cellulitis and necrotizing fasciitis are potential sources of S.pyogenes VO. This patient had no documented risk factors. Classically, VO presents a triad of localized back pain, neurological deficits, and fever, though these are noticed in only 6–8 % of cases. Fever is documented in around 45 % of cases. Back pain is localized to the infected disc space area and may radiate to the abdomen, hip, leg, groin, or perineum [5], [6]. VO with hip pain should raise suspicion for a psoas abscess, as witnessed in our case. MRI is the preferred diagnostic modality with a sensitivity of 97 % and specificity of 93 % in diagnosing VO. In a series of 253 patients with bacterial VO, 43 % had epidural or paravertebral extension, as seen in our case. We also compared our case with other streptococcal VO and recognized the lumbar vertebrae as the most common site for VO [7]. The gold standard in establishing the diagnosis of VO is an image-guided aspiration biopsy. Weihe R, et al. documented that the causative organisms are isolated in 60–70 % of biopsies, while bacteria were grown from simultaneous blood cultures in less than 40 % of cases. The sensitivity of bone and disk culture was 30.0 % and 56.0 %, respectively, with specificities of 92.8 % and 75.0 %, respectively [8]. Our patient had a concomitant psoas abscess, with an aspirate culture confirming Streptococcus pyogenes while his blood culture was negative for bacteria. Camara et al. observed that all strains of GAS were susceptible to penicillin G, amoxicillin, and cephalosporins [9]. This explains the sterile blood culture in our patients. Hence, the diagnostic yield of blood or biopsy culture is boosted by holding antimicrobials in clinically stable patients. Spinal biopsy is preferred, once the preliminary blood cultures (i.e. 48 h) show no growth. The combination of increased ESR and CRP has a sensitivity of ninety eight percent in diagnosing VO [10]. It is prudent to rule out infective endocarditis as source of VO. In this case, valvular vegetations were not observed in TTE. The reported sensitivity and specificity of transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) for IE diagnosis is excellent (87 %–100 % and 91 %–100 %, respectively) for native valves [11]. Our patient met Duke’s modified low risk criteria for IE and TEE was not warranted. Penicillin G is the preferred choice for streptococcus infections, but due to penicillin allergy, we managed the patient with ceftriaxone monotherapy. We monitored ESR and CRP on a weekly basis in conjunction with clinical assessment. There is evidence supporting oral antibiotic therapy as effective as intravenous therapy in certain selected VO cases [12], [13]. In the case of spinal cord compression, urgent neurosurgical intervention is required. There is no role of steroidal therapy in cord compression due to VO. Livorsi et al. noted an eighty-six percent cure rate with a mean antibiotic duration of eight weeks and highlighted that undrained abscess carries a significant risk of relapse, with recurrence rate of 14 % [14], [15]. Based on whole genome sequencing, GAS is boarded classified as emm and emm like strains. The emm type-5 strains of Streptococcus pyogenes were witnessed in a case of cervical osteomyelitis with quadriplegia and shock and it has a fatality rate of 30 % [16]. In our case genome sequencing not performed and the patient had a relatively favourable outcome due to expeditious imaging, targeted drainage, and timely antibiotic therapy.

Conclusion

This case illustrates a rare, yet severe, manifestation of Streptococcus pyogenes infection involving multiple spinal structures in a healthy adult. Awareness of GAS as a potential etiologic agent, in adults without classic risk factors, is essential for early diagnosis and improved prognosis. We also emphasize timely use of imaging for diagnosis and reliance on CRP and ESR for monitoring therapeutic response.
