Sugar-free chewing gum’s effect on patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis: a meta-analysis of RCTs
Authors: Shi-Ying Li, Lei-Wen Tang, Ji Li, Yu-Ping Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThirst is a prevalent adverse effect in individuals undergoing maintenance hemodialysis (MHD), frequently precipitating complications that impair well-being and quality of life. While no gold-standard therapy is currently available for thirst management in MHD patients, sugar-free chewing gum has been proposed as a low-cost behavioral intervention, though its efficacy requires further validation. This meta-analysis evaluated its impact on thirst, xerostomia, and secondary outcomes (salivary flow rate, interdialysis weight gain [IDWG]).MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines (PROSPERO: CRD42024550292), ten databases (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, Ovid, CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and CBM) were systematically searched from inception to July 7, 2025. Two investigators independently conducted literature screening, data extraction, and quality assessment. Statistical analyses were performed using RevMan 5.3. The primary outcomes were thirst intensity, measured using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and the Dialysis Thirst Inventory (DTI), as well as xerostomia, measured using the Summated Xerostomia Inventory (SXI). Secondary outcomes included the salivary flow rate and IDWG. Subgroup analysis was conducted based on the duration of intervention (< 6 weeks vs.≥6 weeks) and the type of intervention in the control group (non-intervention vs. active control).ResultsSeven studies (n = 395) were included in the analysis. Sugar-free gum significantly reduced VAS-assessed thirst (SMD = -0.7, 95% CI: -1.21 to -0.20; P = 0.006) and xerostomia (MD = -2.21, 95% CI: -2.62 to -1.79; P < 0.01), with ≥ 6-week interventions showing greater thirst reduction (SMD = -0.84) though without significant duration effect (P = 0.62). Control-type subgroup analysis revealed differential DTI effects: a significant reduction compared to non-intervention controls (MD = -2.50) but not active controls (MD = -0.19; P = 0.006 for interaction). No benefits were observed for salivary flow rate (MD = 0.10, 95% CI: -0.14 to 0.33), IDWG (MD = -0.25, 95% CI: -0.68 to 0.18), or overall DTI scores (MD = -1.57, 95% CI: -3.41 to 0.27). Initial heterogeneity (I²= 64–96%) was resolved after sensitivity analyses. Evidence certainty was very low to low (GRADE).ConclusionSugar-free chewing gum may reduce xerostomia and the intensity of thirst (measured by VAS), with a more significant effect observed in longer interventions (≥ 6 weeks), but without statistically significant duration effects. It is worth noting that its effectiveness in assessing thirst in DTI seems to depend on the type of control, showing substantial benefits compared to a non-intervention control, but not compared to an active control. Although no benefits were observed in salivary flow rate, overall DTI score, or IDWG, this intervention demonstrates potential as a practical, low-cost adjunct for symptom management. Due to substantial heterogeneity and very low to low-quality evidence (GRADE), these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further high-quality, large-scale randomized controlled trials are warranted to validate these results and optimize thirst management strategies for patients with MHD.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04430-7.

Introduction

Maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) is an established therapy for end-stage renal disease (ESRD), significantly improving patient survival [1]. However, MHD is frequently complicated by adverse effects, notably thirst—a prevalent and burdensome symptom affecting 32–81% of patients [2]. Crucially, thirst manifests as a conscious, intense drive to ingest fluids, primarily exacerbated by xerostomia (objectively reduced salivary flow or altered oral composition) [3]. This high prevalence is attributable to disease pathophysiology, dialysis regimens, and polypharmacy [4].

Unmitigated thirst drives excessive interdialytic fluid intake, directly resulting in interdialytic weight gain (IDWG). Thereby, it heightens the risk of hypertension, cardiovascular events, and cerebrovascular complications during dialysis [5, 6]. Given these sequelae, current thirst management strategies - including drug therapy, nerve electrical stimulation, and ear acupressure —yield inconsistent outcomes due to dose-limiting adverse effects, procedural invasiveness, or short-lived efficacy in randomized trials [7]. Hence, non-pharmacological alternatives, such as salivary enhancers (including sugar-free chewing gum, liquorice gargling, and lemon spray), warrant consideration [8, 9].

Among these interventions, sugar-free chewing gum merits particular attention due to its patient acceptability, low cost, and dual mechanisms: physiological stimulation through mechanoreceptor and gustatory pathway activation, which enhances salivary flow rate via parasympathetic signalling [10–12], coupled with cognitive modulation, where chewing behaviour attenuates thirst perception through neural interference [12].

Despite these theoretical advantages, clinical evidence remains contradictory. Specifically, while some trials report negligible reductions in thirst [13], others demonstrate symptomatic improvement [14, 15]. Similarly, its effects on salivary flow rate and IDWG show significant heterogeneity. Therefore, this meta-analysis aims to synthesise the current evidence on the efficacy of sugar-free chewing gum in alleviating primary symptoms (thirst and xerostomia) in MHD patients. Additionally, we evaluate its effects on secondary physiological outcomes, including salivary flow rate and interdialytic weight gain (IDWG).

Materials and methods

This study was registered in the PROSPERO database with registration number CRD42024550292 and was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis) guidelines [16]. A completed PRISMA checklist (Related File Table 1) cross-references each guideline item to the corresponding manuscript section (pages/sections Y-Z).

PICO question

Trials for inclusion met the following PICO criteria:

These four articles are among the most comprehensive accounts of the history and evolution of leukemia.

Population: patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis;

Intervention: Chewing sugar-free gum;

Comparison: routine care(including no intervention, fluid restriction using straws, and saliva substitutes);

Outcomes: Primary outcomes were (1) thirst intensity (assessed by Visual Analogue Scale [VAS] or Dialysis Thirst Inventory [DTI]) and (2) xerostomia severity (measured by Summated Xerostomia Inventory [SXI]). Secondary outcomes included (1) unstimulated salivary flow rate (mL/min) and (2) IDWG (kg).

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was undertaken across ten electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, Embase, Cochrane Library, Ovid, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), China Biomedical Literature Database (CBM), VIP Database, and Wanfang Database. The search included all records up to July 7, 2025. Our search strategy utilized a dual approach combining controlled vocabulary (Medical Subject Headings [MeSH] terms) with free-text keywords to ensure comprehensive retrieval. Search strategy on PubMed, namely (renal dialysis [MeSH Terms] OR kidney failure, chronic [MeSH Terms] OR hemodialys*[Title/Abstract] OR dialys*[Title/Abstract] OR MHD[Title/Abstract] OR heamodialys*[Title/Abstract]) AND (chewing gum [MeSH Terms] OR chewing gums [Title/Abstract] OR gum, chewing [Title/Abstract] OR gums, chewing [Title/Abstract] OR chewing gum [Title/Abstract]). The detailed search process is presented in Supplementary Material Table 1.

Eligibility criteria

Two authors (LSY and LJ) independently reviewed studies and resolved disagreements through discussion. The following inclusion criteria were used: (1) Studies included were randomized clinical trials (RCTs); (2) the study included patients with MHD; and (3) the intervention measure was chewing sugar-free gum. Exclusion criteria: (1) studies with missing critical outcome data (inability to calculate effect sizes from available statistics), or data presented in incompatible formats that precluded quantitative synthesis (only graphical data without numerical values); (2) duplicate publications; (3) book chapters, reviews, commentaries, letters to the editor, and guidelines; and (4) studies not in English or Chinese.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (LSY and LJ) independently extracted data using a standardized electronic form adapted from the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. The extracted data included study characteristics (authors, publication year, country, and sample size), participant demographics (mean age and sex distribution), intervention details (frequency and duration), and outcome measures (with specified measurement tools). Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussions, with unresolved cases adjudicated by a third reviewer (TLW).

Assessment of risk of bias

Two authors (LSY and LJ) independently assessed the quality of all trials using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (ROB 2) tool [17]. This tool looks at five key areas: (1) how participants were randomly assigned, (2) any changes from the planned treatments, (3) missing data on outcomes, (4) how outcomes were measured, and (5) how results were reported. The studies were classified as having low, some concerns, or high risk of bias. Disputes were resolved by consultation with the third author (TLW).

Strategy for data synthesis

RevMan 5.3 software was used to perform a meta-analysis. This meta-analysis focused on continuous variables. When using the same scale, the mean difference (MD) was the effect size, and the standardised mean difference (SMD) was the effect size in the other direction. Heterogeneity testing involved the I²statistic. If P ≥ 0.10 and I²< 50%, this finding suggests that the studies’ heterogeneity is acceptable, and the fixed-effects model can be used. If P < 0.10 and I²≥ 50%, the random effect model is used. We performed exploratory subgroup meta-analyses (if each subgroup included at least two studies) to assess potential sources of heterogeneity, including stratification by control group type: no intervention versus active controls (straws for fluid restriction or saliva substitutes). Additional subgroup analyses examined the intervention duration (< 6 vs.≥6 weeks) to assess potential associations between treatment length and effect sizes. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially omitting individual studies, with particular attention to excluding studies with active controls to evaluate the robustness of findings against control group heterogeneity. Cochran’s Q test was used to assess heterogeneity between subgroups. A p-value under 0.05 indicates statistical significance. Publication bias was not formally assessed using Egger’s test or funnel plots due to the limited number of included studies (n < 10 per outcome). When meta-analysis wasn’t possible due to limited studies or unavailable data, only descriptive analysis was used.

GRADE quality assessment

LSY and LJ independently assessed the certainty of evidence for thirst-related outcomes (thirst intensity and xerostomia) and secondary outcomes (salivary flow rate and IDWG) using the GRADE methodology [18]. The third author helped resolve any disagreements that arose. The GRADE rating was systematically lowered based on predefined criteria: (1) Risk of bias: downgraded by one level for ‘serious’ concerns (when > 25% of studies had high risk of bias in key domains) or two levels for ‘very serious’ concerns (> 50% high risk); (2) Imprecision: downgraded by one level when total sample sizes were < 400 participants (the calculated optimal information size for our primary outcomes) or confidence intervals included both appreciable benefit and harm; (3) Inconsistency: downgraded when I²>50% with P < 0.10 that persisted after sensitivity analyses; (4) Indirectness: considered but not applied as all studies directly addressed our PICO question. The evidence was classified as having high, moderate, low, or very low certainty.

Results

Search results and study selection

The systematic search identified 224 records. After removing 28 duplicates, we screened 196 records and excluded 113 (66 due to subject mismatch; 47 due to literature type mismatch). Following a full-text assessment of 83 articles, we excluded 76 (43 due to subject mismatch, 13 due to population criteria mismatch, eight due to duplicate team publications, five due to study type mismatch, and seven due to unavailability of full-text), leaving seven studies for final inclusion. Assessment of the risk of bias

Seven studies were evaluated using the Cochrane Randomized Controlled Trial Bias Risk Assessment Tool 2.0. Four studies [14, 19, 21, 22] were rated as high risk primarily due to: (1) unclear randomization methods (only stating ‘randomized’ without sequence generation details), (2) lack of reported allocation concealment, and (3) absence of blinding for outcome assessment - particularly critical for subjective endpoints like VAS scores. Three studies [15, 20, 23] demonstrated adequate randomization and concealment procedures. The risk of inclusion bias is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Primary outcomes

Thirst

Seven studies [14, 15, 19–23] assessed the effect of sugar-free chewing gum on thirst in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis (MHD). As two studies [19, 21] employed dual assessment scales, these data were analyzed separately.

Five studies [14, 15, 19–21] utilized the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for thirst assessment. Analysis under a random-effects model (I² = 64%, P = 0.02) revealed that sugar-free chewing gum significantly reduced thirst in MHD patients compared to control (SMD = -0.7, 95% CI: -1.21 to -0.20; P = 0.006) (Fig. 3). The exclusion of the study [19] eliminated heterogeneity (I² = 39%), but the result remained statistically significant following sensitivity analysis (SMD = -0.91, 95% CI: -1.34 to -0.47; P < 0.001). However, the GRADE assessment rated the certainty of the evidence for this outcome as very low (⊕⊖⊖⊖), primarily due to the serious risk of bias in included studies and imprecision (insufficient sample size).

Four studies [19, 21–23] utilized the Dialysis Thirst Inventory (DTI) as the measure of thirst. Meta-analysis indicated no significant reduction in DTI scores with sugar-free chewing gum versus control (MD = -1.57, 95% CI: -3.41 to 0.27; P = 0.09; 4 trials, n = 241; I² = 61%) (Fig. 4). The exclusion of the study [22] eliminated heterogeneity (I² = 0%), but the result remained statistically non-significant following sensitivity analysis. (MD = -0.28, 95% CI: -1.89 to 1.32; P = 0.73). The GRADE assessment rated the certainty of the evidence for this outcome as very low (⊕⊖⊖⊖), downgraded for a very serious risk of bias and serious imprecision.

Xerostomia

Five studies [14, 19, 21–23] evaluated xerostomia symptoms in MHD patients. Fixed-effects model meta-analysis (I² = 0%, P = 0.89) demonstrated that sugar-free chewing gum significantly alleviated xerostomia compared to control (MD = -2.21, 95% CI: -2.62 to -1.79; P < 0.001) (Fig. 5). It is important to note that the GRADE assessment rated the certainty of evidence for this outcome as very low (⊕⊖⊖⊖), due to serious concerns regarding risk of bias and imprecision.

Secondary outcomes

Salivary flow rate

Meta-analysis of four trials (n = 262) showed no significant improvement in salivary flow rate with sugar-free chewing gum versus control (MD = 0.10, 95% CI: -0.14 to 0.33; P = 0.41; I² = 94%) (Fig. 6). The exclusion of the study [22] substantially reduced heterogeneity (I² = 0%), but the result remained non-significant (MD = -0.02, 95% CI: -0.08 to 0.04; P = 0.53). The GRADE assessment rated the certainty of evidence for this outcome as low (⊕⊕⊖⊖), primarily due to concerns about risk of bias and imprecision. This low certainty means we cannot conclude there is no effect, nor can we rule out a small effect that was not detected.

IDWG

Analysis of five trials (n = 294) found no significant difference in IDWG between the intervention and control groups (MD = -0.25, 95% CI: -0.68 to 0.18; P = 0.26; I² = 71%) (Fig. 7). The exclusion of the study [22] resolved heterogeneity (I² = 0%), with no change in the non-significant outcome (MD = -0.01, 95% CI: -0.24 to 0.21; P = 0.88). The GRADE assessment rated the certainty of evidence for this outcome as very low (⊕⊖⊖⊖), primarily due to concerns about risk of bias and imprecision.

Publication bias and sensitivity analyses

Publication bias was not assessed statistically (via Egger’s test or funnel plots) due to the limited number of included studies (n < 10 per outcome), which inherently lacks power to detect asymmetry. Visual inspection of funnel plots was also deemed unreliable given the small sample size. Sensitivity analyses, involving the sequential exclusion of individual studies, supported the robustness of the primary findings. Notably, excluding the high-risk-of-bias research [22] resulted in negligible changes to the overall estimates for both salivary flow rate (original MD = 0.10, 95% CI: -0.14 to 0.33; post-exclusion MD = -0.02, 95% CI: -0.08 to 0.04) and IDWG (original MD = -0.25, 95% CI: -0.68 to 0.18; post-exclusion MD = -0.02, 95% CI: -0.27 to 0.24).

Subgroup analyses

After resolving heterogeneity through sensitivity analysis, we conducted subgroup analysis to examine potential effect modifiers. First, we analyzed VAS-assessed thirst by intervention duration (< 6 vs.≥6 weeks), based on prior evidence suggesting potential symptom improvement within 6–12 weeks [13, 24]. While the ≥ 6 weeks group showed statistically significant thirst reduction (SMD = -0.84, 95% CI: -1.45 to -0.23; P = 0.007), the non-significant subgroup interaction (P = 0.62, I² = 0%) indicated that longer durations were not superior to shorter interventions (Supplementary Figure 1). Second, we performed subgroup analyses by control group type (non-intervention vs. active intervention) for all outcomes with sufficient data. For xerostomia (SXI scores), effects were consistent across non-intervention controls (MD = -2.23, 95% CI: -2.65 to -1.81; 3 trials) and active intervention controls (MD = -1.01, 95% CI: -4.13 to 2.11; 2 trials), with no significant subgroup differences (P = 0.45) (Supplementary Figures 2). In contrast, DTI-assessed thirst scores showed significant subgroup differences (P = 0.006), with greater reduction against non-intervention controls (MD = -2.50, 95% CI: -2.88 to -2.12) than active controls (MD = -0.19, 95% CI: -1.81 to 1.44) (Supplementary Figure 3). No significant subgroup effects were found for salivary flow rate or IDWG (all P > 0.05) (Supplementary Figure 4–5).

Evidence quality (GRADE assessment)

The GRADE quality assessment for primary outcomes is summarized in Table 3. Critically, the certainty of the evidence for all outcomes assessed (including both primary and secondary) was rated as low or very low. All outcomes were downgraded in evidence quality due to methodological limitations in the original studies (randomization/blinding deficiencies) and small sample sizes. Although sensitivity analyses resolved some heterogeneity issues, they did not alter the overall conclusions.

Discussion

This meta-analysis suggests that sugar-free gum may alleviate symptoms of xerostomia and VAS-assessed thirst scores

Based on evidence of very low certainty, this meta-analysis suggests that sugar-free gum may alleviate symptoms of xerostomia in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) (MD = -2.21, 95% CI: -2.62 to -1.79, P < 0.01) and may alleviate VAS-assessed thirst scores (SMD = -0.7, 95% CI: -1.21 to -0.20; P = 0.006). These findings align with those reported by Wefer et al. [25]. The underlying pathophysiology involves renal failure impairing the excretion of metabolic waste and excess fluid, thereby elevating plasma and interstitial osmotic pressure and triggering thirst. Furthermore, reduced salivary flow rate contributes directly to xerostomia [26], with studies documenting a 20–55% reduction in salivary output in this population [27, 28]. Chewing gum may stimulate salivary secretion via both gustatory and mechanical pathways [29], which could theoretically contribute to alleviating xerostomia and thirst perception; however, our objective measures did not confirm an increased salivary flow rate. Additionally, chewing gum may also reduce patients’ thirst by distracting their attention [30]. However, the low quality of the underlying evidence necessitates extreme caution in interpreting these findings and their clinical applicability.

The subgroup analysis results showed that the long-term intervention group (≥ 6 weeks, SMD = -0.84, P = 0.007) had significant improvement in VAS-assessed thirst. This result is consistent with previous studies [13, 24], which have shown that improving thirst symptoms typically requires 6–12 weeks of continuous intervention. The mechanism behind this improvement may be related to the sustained stimulation of saliva secretion by chewing gum, thereby improving oral water content [15]. Although the short-term intervention group (< 6 weeks) did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.33), the direction of the effect was consistent with the long-term group, indicating that sugar-free chewing gum may have potential benefits in the early stages; However, larger cohort validation is required. Although the long-term intervention group (≥ 6 weeks) demonstrated statistically significant improvement in thirst symptoms (SMD = -0.84, P = 0.007), the absence of substantial subgroup differences (interaction P = 0.62) precludes conclusions about duration-dependent efficacy. This pattern suggests that while longer interventions may show numerical advantages in individual analyses, the current evidence does not establish that extended gum use provides clinically meaningful additional benefit over shorter-term use. For xerostomia (SXI), the intervention’s benefits remained consistent regardless of control type (non-intervention MD = -2.23 vs. active control MD = -1.01; P = 0.45), reinforcing the effectiveness of sugar-free chewing gum as a reliable oral moisturizer.

Null effect of sugar-free chewing gum on DTI-assessed thirst scores with significant subgroup variations

A meta-analysis of four trials evaluating the effect of sugar-free chewing gum on thirst showed no statistically significant decrease in DTI scores compared to control interventions (MD = -1.57, P = 0.09), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 61%). Sensitivity analysis excluding one outlier study [22] eliminated heterogeneity but did not alter the non-significant outcome (MD = -0.28, P = 0.73), suggesting that the overall effect of sugar-free chewing gum on thirst relief remains inconclusive. The very low certainty of evidence (GRADE: ⊕⊖⊖⊖), downgraded due to serious risk of bias and imprecision, further underscores the need for cautious interpretation. Notably, subgroup analysis revealed significant differences (P = 0.006) based on control types: a clinically meaningful reduction in DTI scores was observed when sugar-free chewing gum was compared to non-intervention controls (MD = -2.50, P < 0.01), whereas no benefit was detected against active controls (MD = -0.19, P = 0.82). This suggests that the apparent efficacy of chewing gum may be influenced by the choice of comparator. The lack of effect against active controls suggests that chewing gum may not provide superior thirst relief compared to other standard strategies. These findings highlight the importance of control selection in thirst intervention trials and the need for larger, high-quality studies to clarify the role of sugar-free chewing gum in managing dialysis-related thirst. Future research should prioritize rigorous designs, standardized controls, and patient-centered outcomes to enhance the certainty of evidence.

Sugar-free chewing gum may not improve salivary flow rate or IDWG

Our research suggests that chewing sugar-free gum does not significantly increase salivary flow rate in maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients. The subgroup analysis based on intervention characteristics also showed no significant difference (P > 0.05). This is contrary to previous research findings [31, 32]. The difference may be due to the timing of saliva measurement: research reports on measuring blood flow during dialysis [20] did not show an increase, while studies measuring saliva before and after dialysis [21] showed improvement. Therefore, physiological changes during dialysis may weaken the stimulating effect on the gums. Secondly, the baseline low saliva secretion in MHD patients [33] may make saliva flow an insensitive indicator, and the improvement after dialysis may further mask the impact of specific gingival edema [34]. In addition, differences in the composition of tree resin (xylitol, sorbitol, and mixed) in various studies may also lead to inconsistent results [35]. Future research should optimize saliva collection time, incorporate complementary indicators [36], and utilize high-quality randomized controlled trials to compare different gingival formulations. Similarly, our study found that chewing sugar-free gum did not significantly reduce IDWG (MD = -0.01, 95% CI: -0.17 ~ 0.15, P = 0.88), and the subgroup analysis based on intervention characteristics also showed no significant difference (P > 0.05), which is consistent with the findings of Jagodzinska et al. [37]. The poor chewing gum effect may be due to the multifactorial nature of IDWG, influenced by age, cultural factors, health beliefs, and adherence to liquid management protocols [38]. Thirst is just one factor [39]. Therefore, future research should explore multimodal IDWG management strategies, possibly combining chewing gum with other symptomatic treatment interventions (such as ice tablets, menthol spray), while providing structured sodium and protein diet education [40], and providing behavioral support (such as mobile app tracking, motivational interviews) [41].

Heterogeneous

There is significant heterogeneity in saliva flow and IDWG analysis (I²of 94% and 71%, respectively). Sensitivity analysis showed that excluding Said et al.‘s study [22] completely resolved this heterogeneity (I²= 0%), which we attributed to unique population characteristics: their cohort was significantly younger (average age < 50 years), and their baseline saliva flow rate was higher (0.4–0.5 vs. 0.25–0.26 ml/min in other studies). These findings suggest that the saliva response to chewing gum may be age-related, with younger patients maintaining better exocrine function and showing greater therapeutic effects. Significantly, this adjustment did not substantially alter our main conclusion regarding the effectiveness of the intervention.

Comparing alternative thirst management strategies

Besides sugar-free chewing gum, other non-pharmacological interventions can also alleviate thirst and xerostomia in hemodialysis patients. For example, lemon mouthwash utilizes the strong saliva-secreting effect of citric acid, which can significantly reduce thirst [42]. However, long-term use may raise concerns about tooth erosion [43]. Similarly, transcutaneous electrical stimulation can improve thirst and saliva flow [44], but its professional implementation is required, and its long-term efficacy has yet to be determined. Oral ice therapy can relieve thirst [45], but it increases fluid intake and may cause mucosal irritation, making it unsuitable for elderly maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients. Overall, sugar-free chewing gum has become a viable alternative due to its low cost, ease of portability, and good safety record. However, few studies have compared sugar-free chewing gum with other intervention measures. Therefore, future research can conduct comprehensive comparisons and select the most suitable thirst intervention plan for hemodialysis patients.

Limitations

This study has the following limitations. Firstly, according to the GRADE evidence grading, the intervention effect of sugar-free chewing gum on maintenance hemodialysis patients in this study showed a very low to low level of evidence, which may limit the clinical use of the evidence. The primary reasons for the analysis are as follows. The methodological flaws in the study directly led to the risk of downgrading, with insufficient description of randomization in four trials [14, 19, 21, 22] (introducing the possibility of selection bias), only three trials [15, 20, 23] reporting allocation concealment, and four trials [14, 19, 21, 22] lacking outcome evaluator blinding, which is particularly problematic for subjective endpoints such as VAS. The differences in intervention duration (1 week and 3 months) and sample size (n = 6–65 per group) led to clinical heterogeneity, resulting in a decrease in inaccuracy. Moreover, the included studies were predominantly conducted in Asia (China, Iran) and the Middle East/North Africa (MENA) region (Egypt, Turkey), which may limit the generalizability of findings to other populations due to potential variations in dietary habits, dialysis protocols, or cultural perceptions of thirst management. Secondly, the number of studies included in this study is limited, and the ability to analyze subgroups is insufficient. The subgroup analyses for DTI and SXI included only 2–3 trials per stratum, limiting their reliability. Additionally, the small sample sizes across trials (ranging from 6 to 65 participants per group) increase the risk of imprecision and may lead to overestimation of intervention effects. Due to the limited number of studies included (n < 10), we did not conduct a formal evaluation of publication bias. Therefore, strong evidence is needed to support the intervention effect of chewing sugar-free gum.

Future considerations

To address the limitations of this study, future research should make the following improvements: (1) implement and report robust randomization (computer-generated sequences through centralized systems/sealed envelopes) and allocate concealment based on CONSORT; (2) If feasible, use double-masked method and report the blinding status; And (3) utilizing standardized and validated tools to obtain objective and subjective results, including patient reported outcomes and clinical evaluations. (4) The experiment should standardize the intervention plan, use consistent evaluation tools (simultaneous VAS and DTI), develop MHD-specific scales, and measure salivary flow rate at standardized time points. (5) It is necessary to compare the intervention effects of different interventions on thirst in maintenance hemodialysis patients (such as chewing gum and lemon mouthwash) to determine the optimal thirst management strategy. Finally, research should explore multimodal intervention measures to reduce IDWG in patients.

Conclusion

Current evidence (GRADE: very low certainty) suggests that sugar-free chewing gum may alleviate VAS thirst scores and xerostomia symptoms in maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients, but its effects on DTI thirst scores, salivary flow rate, and interdialysis weight gain (IDWG) are still unclear. While some individual studies have shown benefits with longer interventions for VAS thirst scores, subgroup analyses have found no evidence that intervention duration significantly modifies treatment effects. The existing evidence has low quality, which limits its clinical application. In the future, it will be necessary to conduct large-scale, randomized controlled trials with rigorous methodology to verify the intervention effect and optimize treatment plans, while focusing on developing multimodal intervention strategies that combine chewing gum with other adjuvant therapies to comprehensively improve the clinical prognosis of MHD patients.
