Disrupting fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase by stratified nanoplatforms orchestrates metabolic-immune reprogramming and prevents post-ablation HCC relapse
Authors: Zhiwen Hong, Xiaolong Liu, Rouhan A, Zengzhen Chen, Qianjiang Wu, Jixian Fu, Xue Guan, Can Wang, Xinghua Wang, Shan Jiang, Bolong Li, Zhaobo Wang, Lei Zhang, Xianwei Meng, Yali Cui, Tengchuang Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) recurrence following microwave ablation poses a pressing clinical challenge, driven by metabolically adapted residual cells that establish an immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment. Our study identifies the post-ablation upregulation of fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase (FAH) as a crucial “metabolic checkpoint” promoting relapse. Elevated FAH expression results in increased fumarate levels within residual HCC cells, supporting tumour cell survival by enhancing energy metabolism while concurrently impairing CD8+ T cell function. Mechanistically, fumarate binds to and stabilises heat shock protein 70 (HSP70), establishing a thermal ablation induced FAH-fumarate-HSP70 axis that drives immunosuppression. To counteract this pivotal axis, we engineered a gallium-based functionalized nanoplatform. This system incorporates a lactate oxidase shell that responds to the lactate-rich tumour microenvironment, enabling the site-specific co-release of Ga3+, FAH-silencing plasmids and the glycolysis inhibitor 2-deoxy-D-glucose. Our nanoplatforms disrupt the FAH-fumarate-HSP70 axis to eradicate residual HCC, activate CD8+ T cells, and restore immunity, targeting recurrence dysregulation while integrating metabolic blockade with immunomodulation to prevent post-ablation relapse with clinical translation potential.Subject terms: Cancer therapeutic resistance, Drug delivery

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a global health challenge, with a persistently dismal five-year survival rate despite advances in therapy1–3. For early-stage HCC, thermal ablation (TA) has emerged as a key minimally invasive treatment, achieving localised tumour destruction with minimal systemic toxicity4–6. However, a critical limitation highlighted in recent clinical guidelines is the alarmingly high rate of post-TA recurrence, which exceeds 30% of patients and is primarily attributable to incomplete microwave ablation (MWA) of therapy-resistant cells and adaptive metabolic-immune reprogramming within the tumour microenvironment (TME)7,8. This recurrence stems from the survival of thermal resilient tumour cells, their subsequent metabolic adaptation, and the parallel establishment of an immunosuppressive TME9,10. There is growing evidence that residual HCC cells surviving sublethal thermal stress undergo profound metabolic remodelling, which supports their proliferation and impairs immune surveillance, thereby fostering a TME highly conducive to relapse11–13. Addressing this high recurrence rate is of utmost urgency to improve patient outcomes and transform the clinical management of early-stage HCC.

Dynamic metabolic-immune crosstalk within the TME of HCC constitutes a self-reinforcing axis that drives post-therapeutic recurrence. Residual HCC cells not only adapt autonomously to metabolic stress but also remodel their niche by promoting tolerogenic myeloid cell populations and T cell exhaustion14–16. Recent research has identified dysregulated glycolysis and fatty acid oxidation as critical “metabolic checkpoints” that enable HCC cells to reshape the TME for immune evasion17,18. Indeed, post-ablation recurrence is propelled by residual cells that are effectively “armoured” by metabolic adaptations, allowing them to evade sublethal thermal damage19. Importantly, sublethal thermal stress not only selects for pre-existing resilient clones but also actively induces oncogenic metabolic rewiring: for instance, heat-induced O-GlcNAcylation acts as a molecular switch that amplifies the Warburg effect and facilitates proliferation through epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)20. Compensatory activation of tryptophan metabolism has been shown to enhance the resistance of HCC cells to subsequent thermal insult21. Together, these findings highlight a pattern of adaptive metabolism wherein residual tumours exploit stress-responsive pathways to resist ablation and initiate relapse. Despite these insights, the specific metabolic-immune checkpoints governing recurrence after MWA are essential and warrant systematic investigation.

Herein, we analysed the GSE212604 dataset and identified a significant upregulation of fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase (FAH)22. FAH catalyses the production of fumarate, a key intermediate in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and its upregulation was implicated in HCC recurrence. RNA sequencing of HCC cells subjected to thermal stress revealed a consistent transcriptional response, with significant enrichment in glucose metabolism-related pathways. Furthermore, metabolomic analysis confirmed a notable decrease in intracellular fumarate upon FAH knockdown. Importantly, fumarate accumulation in the TME is known to impede CD8+ T cell metabolic homeostasis and activation of anti-tumour immunity23. Also, fumarate can exert pro-tumour and immunosuppressive effects by modulating CD8+ T cell metabolism and impairing T-cell receptor signalling, both as an intracellular metabolite and as a secreted organic acid24. These findings underscore the dual role of metabolic reprogramming in sustaining tumour survival and orchestrating immune evasion, positioning FAH as a potential “metabolic checkpoint” that protects residual HCC cells during TA therapy. Therefore, the FAH-fumarate-HSP70 axis constitutes a pivotal pathogenic cascade that drives tumour persistence and relapse.

Recent advances in nanotechnology offer transformative potential to overcome the limitations of current therapies for HCC recurrence. Combining metabolic checkpoint disruption with established therapies, such as immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in preclinical models25. Studies have also highlighted that stratified nanostructure, optimized through dynamic tag-mediated self-assembly, can enhance tumour penetration and drug-loading efficiency, addressing key limitations of conventional nanocarriers26–31. However, achieving precise targeting and complete eradication of residual tumour tissue at the ablation margin remains challenging, necessitating continuous optimization of nanomaterial physicochemical properties and a deeper mechanistic understanding of thermal resistance. To specifically target this axis, we designed a gallium (Ga)-based nanoplatform intended to regulate glucose metabolism, function as nanoplatform capable of modulating metabolic and immune responses. This precision-engineered nanoplatform selectively targets HCC, enforces a metabolic blockade of the TCA cycle and glycolysis and, by alleviating tumour-induced metabolic-immune suppression, acts synergistically with α-PD-1 and microwave ablation to eradicate HCC and restore systemic anti-tumour immunity. Our Ga-based nanoplatform emerges as an effective and feasible solution to counteract the challenges associated with metabolic-immune reprogramming in HCC recurrence (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Results

FAH is responsible for thermal ablation induced HCC recurrence

To systematically identify molecular drivers of HCC recurrence after MWA, we employed a multi-omics approach integrating computational biology with experimental validation. Discovery began with transcriptomic profiling of GSE212604, comparing paired HCC tissues before and after incomplete thermal ablation to identify heat ablation‑related genes. Candidate genes were further validated with RNA‑seq data from heat‑treated versus untreated HCC cells. Clinical relevance was assessed in independent patient cohorts, where FAH expression was examined for its association with post‑ablation recurrence and survival outcomes (Fig. 1a). Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed on transcriptomic data from the public dataset GSE212604 to identify recurrence-associated module eigengenes (ME). This analysis revealed module eigengene 3 (ME3) as the most significantly correlated with post-ablation tumour progression, showing distinct expression patterns between pre- and post-incomplete thermal ablation samples in radiofrequency-treated groups (Pre-iTA vs. Post-iTA; Fig. 1b). Genes within ME3 were differentially expressed between these groups (Fig. 1c). Module membership–gene significance analysis showed a strong linear relationship within ME3 (R = 0.82), indicating cohesive biological functionality among its constituent genes (Fig. 1d). Among the differentially expressed genes, FAH was markedly upregulated in the post-iTA group (Fig. 1e). In thermal stress assays, gradual temperature increases led to progressively higher FAH expression in SNU-182 and PLC/PRF/5 cells (Fig. 1f). RNA sequencing of cells subjected to 37 °C or 43 °C conditions confirmed elevated FAH transcription (Fig. 1g). Mass spectrometry imaging of clinical samples (Cohort 1) revealed lactate enrichment in recurrent HCC tissues (Fig. 1h). MRI evaluation at one month (short-term) and three months (long-term) post-ablation in patients with recurrent hepatocellular carcinoma from cohort 3 revealed that high FAH expression correlated with more severe recurrence (Fig. 1i and Supplementary Fig. 2a). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) of Cohort 3 showed elevated FAH expression in most recurrent samples, supporting its potential role in recurrence (Fig. 1j and Supplementary Fig. 2b). Enrichment analysis indicated activation of metabolic pathways, including the citric acid cycle, glycolysis, energy metabolism, mitochondrial matrix, and oxidoreductase activity, following heat treatment (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). These findings highlight that central carbon metabolism, including both glycolysis and the TCA cycle, is a core driver of the post‑ablation adaptive response, providing a rationale for co‑targeting both pathways. Western blot analysis of Cohort 4 corroborated these findings (Fig. 1k). Univariate Cox regression identified FAH-related factors as significant predictors of poor prognosis in HCC patients. Clinical data analysis indicated that high FAH expression was associated with advanced age, disease stage, and hepatitis prevalence (Supplementary Fig. 2e–g). Analyses of the TCGA, GSE14520, GSE141200 and GSE144269 cohorts consistently demonstrated worse survival in patients with high FAH expression (Fig. 1l and Supplementary Fig. 2h). We established a subcutaneous HCC relapse model to simulate post-ablation recurrence. FAHoe and FAHctrl H22 cells were used to establish subcutaneous tumours. These tumours were then subjected to off‑centre microwave ablation, in which the microwave antenna was inserted into the periphery of the tumour to achieve incomplete ablation (Fig. 1m). FAH overexpression significantly accelerated H22 tumour growth without causing significant body weight loss (Fig. 1n, o and Supplementary Fig. 2i–k). A metastatic HCC model generated via intrasplenic injection showed more severe hepatic malignancy in FAH-overexpressing groups. Tumour development and progression were monitored by bioluminescence imaging (Fig. 1p–r and Supplementary Fig. 2l). Together, these results establish FAH as a key oncogenic driver that promotes tumour progression in both primary and recurrent settings, identifying it as a thermally responsive regulator critical for HCC growth after ablation.

SP1-FAH-fumarate-HSP70 axis promotes HCC recurrence through dual glucose metabolism acceleration

To investigate the role of FAH in HCC metabolic remodelling and to develop an effective ablation strategy, we analysed TCA cycle-targeted metabolomic profiles in SNU-182 cells with FAH knockdown using the most efficient shRNA (sh3, designated as FAHsh) among three tested shRNAs, compared with control (FAHctrl). For metabolomic analysis, metabolite levels were quantified under standard culture conditions. For metabolic flux analysis, cells were cultured in glucose-free medium supplemented with [U6-13C]-glucose, and isotope enrichment in downstream metabolites was measured (Supplementary Fig. 3a and Fig. 2a). FAH knockdown reduces fumarate levels, leading to localized metabolic perturbations (Fig. 2b). In contrast to the collapse in the fumarate, malate and succinate pools revealed by 13C metabolic flux analysis following FAH knockdown, citrate levels remained unchanged, indicating that FAH deficiency exerts a confined metabolic effect at the fumarate and succinate node without inducing widespread rewiring of the TCA cycle (Fig. 2c, d). In Cohort 1, fumarate levels were significantly elevated in recurrent HCC specimens compared with primary tumours, as assessed by mass spectrometry imaging and quantified in corresponding graphs, suggesting that FAH‑mediated metabolic dysregulation with fumarate accumulation indicates HCC relapse (Fig. 2e). Using a dose-escalation fumarate oral gavage model (Fig. 2f), we observed that fumarate promoted HCC growth, as shown by bioluminescence and liver imaging, with a corresponding reduction in survival (Fig. 2g–i). Consistently, in the FAH‑knockdown setting, colony formation was markedly reduced in FAHsh cells and restored by fumarate supplementation (Supplementary Fig. 3b), whereas fumarate also rescued the in vivo tumour growth defect, as evidenced by increased liver tumour burden and elevated liver‑to‑body weight ratios (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). To explore how fumarate promotes HCC progression, PharmMapper analysis was first performed to screen potential target proteins; among the five candidates (HSP70, PAK6, coagulation factor VII, C8G, and Ran), HSP70 exhibited the highest Fit score and was selected for further molecular docking. The docking analysis then identified potential fumarate-binding residues on HSP70 (G202, T204, T14, T13, K71 and Y15; Supplementary Fig. 3e, f and Fig. 2j). Recurrent HCC tumours exhibited higher HSP70 expression than primary tumours, consistent with a metabolic adaptation resulting from FAH alteration (Fig. 2k). Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) of Cohort 2 visualised the spatial proximity of FAH and HSP70 (Supplementary Fig. 3g). Drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assays confirmed direct fumarate-HSP70 binding, showing dose-dependent protection of HSP70 against proteolytic degradation (Fig. 2l). Cellular thermal shift assays (CETSA) demonstrated thermal stabilization of HSP70 in dimethyl fumarate (DMF)-treated SNU-182 cells, indicated by a right-shifted protein melting curve, confirming target engagement (Fig. 2m, n). To further validate the functional importance of the predicted fumarate-binding residues, we generated a panel of HSP70 point mutants (Y15F, K71A, T13A, T14A, T204A and G202S) and examined their response to fumarate‑induced thermal stabilization by CETSA in SNU‑182 cells. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3h, the protective effect of fumarate on HSP70 thermal stability was preserved in the Y15F mutant, with a melting profile indistinguishable from that of the vector control. In contrast, all other mutants (K71A, T13A, T14A, T204A and G202S) lost fumarate-mediated protection, exhibiting no thermal stabilization upon fumarate treatment. These results identify K71, T13, T14, T204 and G202 as critical residues for fumarate engagement with HSP70, whereas Y15 is dispensable for this interaction. Importantly, DMF treatment restored HSP70 expression after FAH knockdown (Fig. 2o). These results establish the FAH-fumarate-HSP70 axis as a key regulator of HCC progression and recurrence. To identify transcription factors (TFs) regulating FAH expression, we integrated predictions from JASPAR and PROMO databases with chromatin immunoprecipitation qPCR (ChIP-qPCR) results of control and heat-treated SNU-182 cells. Which identified SP1 as the most significantly enriched TF at the FAH promoter (Fig. 2p). Clinically, high SP1 expression, like FAH, correlated with poor prognosis in HCC patients (Fig. 2q). mIHC (Cohort 1) and IHC (Cohort 3) showed that SP1 and FAH co-localized and exhibited positively correlated expression levels in recurrent HCC tissues, supporting their pathological association. (Fig. 2r and Supplementary Fig. 3i, j). Intriguingly, when SNU-182 cells were subjected to thermal stress, SP1 showed a parallel induction to FAH (Supplementary Fig. 3k), consistent with the pattern verified in Fig. 1f. This suggested a potential functional link under thermal stress conditions. To substantiate this hypothesis, knockdown of SP1 led to a significant reduction in FAH expression levels, directly implicating SP1 in the transcriptional regulation of FAH (Supplementary Fig. 3l). Potential SP1-binding sites within the FAH promoter were predicted using the JASPAR database (Fig. 2s). ChIP‑qPCR assays confirmed that SP1 exhibits higher binding affinity to sites 1 and 2 in the endogenous FAH promoter, with binding intensity further enhanced under heat conditions (Supplementary Fig. 3m). Luciferase reporter assays further demonstrated that mutation of site 1 abrogated SP1‑driven transcriptional activation, confirming site 1 as the functional SP1‑responsive element (Fig. 2t). Pharmacological inhibition of SP1 with mithramycin A (Mit) suppressed FAH transcriptional activity and downstream metabolic flux (Supplementary Fig. 3n). Collectively, these findings establish that SP1-driven FAH expression fuels fumarate accumulation, reprogramming the HCC microenvironment into a niche competent for recurrence.

Engineering and characterization of GADF and its dual-metabolic interference in vitro

First, the nanoplatform is encapsulated within a lactate oxidase (LOX) shell that functions as a responsive element to the acidic TME. Upon degradation in the acidic TME, LOX catalyses the oxidation of lactate to generate H2O2, which in turn disrupts the coordination bonds of the nanoplatform, thereby facilitating its dissociation and selective payload release within HCC-affected regions. Second, to enhance tumour-specific localisation, the surface is functionalised with SP94, a peptide that selectively targets HCC cells. Third, the system integrates FAH knockdown plasmids (pFAH) and the glycolysis inhibitor 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) within the Ga-based framework, structurally forming 2-DG@pFAH@Ga (GADF) (Fig. 3a). Concretely, GAD: Ga3+ + 2-DG-loaded platform; GAF: Ga3+ + pFAH-loaded platform; GADF: Ga3+ + 2-DG + pFAH combined platform. Structural characterisation revealed a relatively uniform morphology of GADF, as visualised by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 3b). The presence and spatial distribution of characteristic elements within GADF were confirmed by TEM elemental mapping and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). Specifically, Fig. 3c shows the elemental mapping of GADF nanoparticles, confirming the uniform distribution of key elements (Ga, O, N, Cl, F and S) within the nanostructure. Figure 3d presents the corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy spectrum, verifying the elemental composition of GADF. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis indicated a highly crystalline structure for GADF, with distinct peaks observed at 21.036°, 32.998°, 36.373°, 52.749° and 58.780° (Fig. 3e). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements showed an increase in hydrodynamic diameter from 352.89 nm (GAD) to 356.83 nm (GADF) (Fig. 3f). Concurrently, the zeta potential shifted from 4.52 ± 0.43 mV (GAD) to −10.34 ± 1.00 mV (GADF), which is attributed to surface functionalisation with SP94 and LOX (Fig. 3g). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy verified the presence of characteristic functional groups in GADF (Fig. 3h). Lactate-triggered degradation kinetics demonstrated that GADF began to degrade within 6 h in a 20 mM lactate solution, with complete structural collapse observed by 12 h (Fig. 3i). The quantification of Ga3+ release was performed using a UV–vis calibration curve (y = 0.04756x–0.00827, R2 = 0.99935) established for Ga(NO3)3, which exhibited a characteristic absorption peak at 300 nm (Fig. 3j, k). The Ga3+ release profiles exhibited a time-dependent increase. Notably, within 24 h, less than 25% of Ga3+ was released in PBS, whereas over 90% was released in 20 mM lactate solution (Fig. 3l). These results confirm the high lactate sensitivity of GADF and its capacity for substantial Ga3+ release, a property expected to enhance microwave sensitisation. Under microwave irradiation (2 W/mL, 5 min), GADF saline solutions exhibited concentration‑dependent temperature increases over time (ΔT = 21.1, 25.1, 27.5, and 30.4 °C at 0, 2.5, 5 and 10 mg mL−1, respectively) as recorded by continuous infrared thermal imaging (Fig. 3m). This heating behaviour was further illustrated by representative infrared thermal images captured at designated time points (Fig. 3n). Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation was significantly enhanced under microwave irradiation. Specifically, the PBS + GADF + MW group produced more ROS than the PBS + GADF group and the PBS control group (Fig. 3o). Singlet oxygen (1O2), identified as a major ROS species, effectively decomposed 9,10-anthracenediyl-bis (methylene)dimalonic acid (ABDA), as indicated by significant reductions in its characteristic fluorescence and UV–vis absorption peaks (Fig. 3p, q). Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy further confirmed the generation of 1O2 by GADF under microwave irradiation (Fig. 3r). Finally, hyperspectral imaging revealed significantly enhanced uptake of SP94/LOX-functionalised GADF by hepatocellular carcinoma cells compared with non-targeted control nanoparticles (GADF-empty), demonstrating the crucial role of SP94 in conferring targeting capability (Fig. 3s). The combination of GADF and microwave irradiation markedly elevated surface calreticulin (CRT) expression and reduced nuclear high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), confirming the amplification of immunogenic cell death (Fig. 3t).

Function validation in HCC models was performed using SNU-182 and PLC/PRF/5 cells across eight experimental groups: Control, GAD, GAF, GADF, MW, GAD + MW, GAF + MW, and GADF + MW (Supplementary Fig. 4a). The most pronounced inhibition of proliferation was observed in the GADF + MW group, where cell viability was reduced by 39.1% in SNU-182 cells and 43.2% in PLC/PRF/5 cells relative to controls (Supplementary Fig. 4b). ROS generation was significantly elevated in GADF + MW-treated cells, with DCFH-DA fluorescence intensity 27.12-fold higher in SNU-182 cells and 35.58-fold higher in PLC/PRF/5 cells compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 4c–f). pFAH-mediated inhibition of FAH expression led to a 64.27% reduction in SNU-182 cells relative to controls (Fig. 3u). Mitochondrial dysfunction was observed following GADF + MW treatment, as indicated by JC-1 staining showing 86.42% and 95.01% decreases in the red/green fluorescence ratio in SNU-182 and PLC/PRF/5 cells, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4g–j). The blockade of glucose metabolism by GADF + MW was associated with impaired cellular energy supply and reduced proliferative capacity. Colony formation was suppressed by 96.00% in SNU-182 cells and 67.00% in PLC/PRF/5 cells in the GADF + MW groups compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 5a–c). EdU assays revealed reductions in DNA replication activity of 35.58% in SNU-182 and 63.00% in PLC/PRF/5 cells, consistent with the proliferation inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 5d–f). Migration capacity was also impaired; wound area increased by 0.96-fold at 24 h and 0.62-fold at 48 h in SNU-182 cells, and by 4.63-fold at 24 h and 1.60-fold at 48 h in PLC/PRF/5 cells treated with GADF + MW compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 5g–j). Metastatic potential was significantly suppressed in the GADF + MW groups. Transwell assays showed that migration was attenuated by 51.43% in PLC/PRF/5 cells and 40.50% in HepG2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 6a–d), and invasion was reduced by 46.78% in PLC/PRF/5 cells and 46.68% in HepG2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 6e–h). The synergistic therapeutic effects of GADF + MW, which induces metabolic blockade and depletion of cellular energy, alongside ROS-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction, collectively inhibiting HCC proliferation, migration, and invasion. We further applied GADF together with microwave irradiation for combined FAH knockdown, glycolysis inhibition and HCC ablation, enabling spatial mapping and quantification of FAH and HSP70 expression patterns in treated tumours (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Notably, GADF + MW treatment significantly decreases N-cadherin expression while concurrently increasing E-cadherin expression, indicating a suppression of the EMT process (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). Following cellular internalisation, GADF concurrently suppressed FAH expression and inhibited hexokinase activity, thereby reducing fumarate accumulation. Supplementary Fig. 7e, f confirmed significantly decreased lactate concentration, fumarate levels and ATP production in GADF + MW-treated SNU-182 and PLC/PRF/5 cells, indicating inhibition of glycolysis and energy metabolism. Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) assays showed that GADF + MW treatment significantly attenuated key glycolytic parameters, including glycolytic rate and glycolytic capacity, in both cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 7g, h). Subsequent oxygen consumption rate (OCR) assays demonstrated marked suppression of basal and maximal respiratory capacity in co-treated SNU-182 and PLC/PRF/5 cells (Supplementary Fig. 7i–l). Furthermore, these interventions increased tyrosine concentrations, decreased acetoacetate levels and inhibited glucose uptake, collectively confirming potent disruption of glucose and FAH-dependent tyrosine catabolism (Supplementary Fig. 7m, n). Ultimately, GADF combined with microwave ablation effectively reversed fumarate accumulation and HSP70 upregulation while suppressing HCC metabolic reprogramming.

GADF combinatorial therapeutics against metabolic reprogramming impair HCC progression

Previous findings demonstrated a synergistic therapeutic interaction between GADF and microwave (MW) irradiation. To quantify antitumour efficacy, PLC/PRF/5 xenografts were engrafted subcutaneously into immunodeficient BALB/c nude mice. Mice received intravenous tail-vein administration of GAD, GAF or GADF nanotherapeutics (100 μL, 20 mg kg−1), followed by selective MW irradiation (5 min; 6 h post-injection). Subsequent dosing cycles (every 4 days) omitted MW exposure. MW was applied on day 0, and PET-CT imaging was performed on day 4. Experimental groups included control, GADF, MW, GAD + MW, GAF + MW and GADF + MW (Fig. 4a). Treated groups exhibited significant hyperthermic responses, with temperature increases (ΔT) of 14.6 °C (MW alone), 17.6 °C (GAD + MW), 17.6 °C (GAF + MW) and 18.0 °C (GADF + MW), confirming pronounced microwave thermal conversion efficacy and localised thermal sensitisation capability (Fig. 4b, c). Terminal tumour excision at the study endpoint (day 30) revealed marked tumour suppression in the GADF + MW group, and GADF monotherapy together with all combination groups (GAD + MW, GAF + MW, GADF + MW) showed negligible systemic toxicity, confirming their biocompatibility (Fig. 4d-f, Supplementary Fig. 8a). During the therapeutic window post-MW (after oedema resolution), 2-[18F]fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose (18F-FDG) PET–CT was used to assess glucose metabolism. Quantitative PET–CT metrics demonstrated pronounced suppression of radiotracer uptake in the GADF + MW group, evidenced by reduced maximum standardised uptake values (SUVmax) and lower relative SUVmax indices (Fig. 4g, h). Concurrent reductions in total lesion glycolysis (TLG) and metabolic tumour volume (MTV) further corroborated the metabolic suppression conferred by GADF + MW therapy (Supplementary Fig. 8b). H&E and IHC analysis revealed extensive necrosis in GADF + MW‑treated tumours, accompanied by reduced FAH and HSP70 expression, increased caspase‑3 and TUNEL positivity, and a decreased Ki‑67 proliferative index (Fig. 4i and Supplementary Fig. 8c). Furthermore, comprehensive histopathological analysis and liver and kidney function analysis confirmed minimal extrahepatic cytotoxicity and hepatotoxicity across monotherapy groups, affirming the biocompatibility of GAD, GAF and GADF nanoplatforms (Supplementary Fig. 8d, e). Mechanistically, dropout control experiments revealed that the pFAH cargo was indispensable for optimal synergy with microwave ablation, as the GA + MW and GAF0 + MW groups (empty vector controls) failed to achieve comparable tumour control (Supplementary Fig. 8f, g). We additionally used HepG2 cells to establish an orthotopic hepatic metastasis model via intrasplenic injection in BALB/c nude mice, extending the evaluation of the GADF-based therapies to a distinct HCC cell line. Tumour growth was monitored by bioluminescence imaging. On the day of tumour confirmation, mice received intravenous injections of GAD, GAF or GADF (10 mg kg−1), followed by MW. The intravenous injections were then repeated every 4 days for a total of 3 weeks. Experimental groups included control, GADF, MW, GAD + MW, GAF + MW and GADF + MW (Fig. 4j). In vivo bioluminescence imaging (IVIS) revealed potent suppression of metastatic burden in GADF + MW-treated mice compared with monotherapy controls (Fig. 4k–m). H&E staining confirmed extensive necrosis in the GADF + MW group (Fig. 4n, o). Terminal largest tumour size and liver-to-body weight ratios indicated reduced hepatic metastatic burden in GADF + MW groups, reflecting substantial therapeutic alleviation of oncological stress (Fig. 4p, q). The GADF + MW combination group showed a significant survival advantage, with 83.3% long-term survival and restoration of liver and kidney function indicators (Fig. 4r and Supplementary Fig. 8h). Collectively, these findings establish that GADF combined with MW irradiation synergistically suppresses orthotopic HCC progression while maintaining systemic biocompatibility in BALB/c nude mice, validating GADF’s capacity for profound tumour-selective metabolic reprogramming blockade.

GADF combinatorial therapy reverses the immunosuppressive tumour immune microenvironment by targeting metabolic–immune reprogramming

A central limitation in HCC immunotherapy is the limited efficacy of PD-1 blockade in immune-desert tumours, highlighting the need for combination strategies32. Thermal ablation amplifies tumour-intrinsic TCA cycle flux, a metabolic switch that is inversely correlated with the intratumoural cytotoxic CD8+ T-cell gene signature (Supplementary Fig. 9a). mIHC analysis of recurrent patient specimens revealed a profound desert-like tumour immune microenvironment (TIME), accompanied by elevated expression of FAH and HSP70 (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 9b). Importantly, fumarate serves both as an enzymatic product of FAH and as an immunosuppressive metabolite that directly impairs effector T-cell function and sustains an immunosuppressive TIME33. GADF was therefore applied as a precision therapeutic intervention to disrupt this immunosuppressive cascade through targeted FAH suppression, thereby reversing fumarate-driven immune dysfunction. The feasibility of this combination strategy depends on disrupting the FAH-fumarate-T cell suppression axis in vivo and restoring immunocompetence within the TIME. We therefore employed C57BL/6 mouse models to evaluate the ability of GADF to reverse immune desertification via FAH/fumarate modulation as assessed by systemic metabolic-immune reprogramming and functional reconstitution of T‑cell effector capacity. Contralateral implantation was performed 7 days after primary tumour inoculation. Intravenous administration of GADF (10 mg kg−1 every 4 days), anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (mAb) or IgG isotype control (10 mg kg−1) began after confirmation of distal tumour growth. MW was applied to primary tumours on day 0. Experimental groups included control, anti-PD-1, MW + anti-PD-1, GADF + MW + anti-PD-1 (Fig. 5b). In bilateral tumour-bearing mice, MW ablation of the primary tumour not only achieved local eradication but also induced regression of contralateral tumours in the GADF + MW + PD-1 group without cachexia, indicating a systemic abscopal effect (Fig. 5c–e and Supplementary Fig. 9c). Mass cytometry by time-of-flight (CyTOF) analysis of distal tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) identified eight distinct immune clusters, with significant expansion of the CD8+ T-cell compartment (Supplementary Fig. 9d). Further analysis revealed 11 distinct immune subsets, demonstrating that the GADF + MW + PD‑1 combination potently enhanced CD8+ T‑cell effector programmes through coordinated upregulation of IFN‑γ secretion, granzyme B (GZMB) production, and proliferative capacity (Ki‑67) (Fig. 5f, g). Flow cytometry confirmed the synergistic enhancement of both effector function and proliferation in CD8+ T cells (Fig. 5h, i). Concurrently, recruitment of immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs) was reduced compared with controls (Supplementary Fig. 9e). After confirming FAH ablation and T-cell reactivation in subcutaneous models, we evaluated systemic anti-metastatic immunity using a lung metastasis model established via tail-vein injection of HCC cells in C57BL/6 mice. Tumour growth was monitored by bioluminescence imaging. Following tumour engraftment, mice were treated with IgG control, anti‑PD‑1 monotherapy, GAD + anti‑PD‑1, GAF + anti‑PD‑1, or GADF + anti‑PD‑1 (GADF: 10 mg kg−1 every 4 days; anti‑PD‑1 or IgG: 10 mg kg−1) (Fig. 5j). GADF + PD-1 treatment resulted in a significant therapeutic response, extending median survival by over 50% and reducing pulmonary metastases by 0.9-fold relative to controls (Fig. 5k–p and Supplementary Fig. 9f). Complementary flow cytometry analysis revealed consistent immunophenotypic changes, indicating that GADF + PD-1 drives potent immune reconstitution within metastatic niches (Supplementary Fig. 9g, h). Together, these results demonstrate the robust combinatorial efficacy of GADF with PD-1 and MW in promoting immune activation and tumour suppression in HCC.

Synergistic GADF combinatorial therapy eradicates HCC tumours in multiple models

We further evaluated the immunomodulatory efficacy of GADF using a well-defined murine HCC model based on hydrodynamic tail vein injection (HDTVi) of transposon‑ and CRISPR-Cas9‑based oncogenic vectors (p-T3-Myc-Luc, PX330-sg-P53, CMV-SB13) into hepatocytes of 5-week-old C57BL/6 male mice, which induced spontaneous orthotopic HCC development. After tumour establishment, mice were assigned to treatment groups: control, GADF, anti‑PD‑1, and GADF + anti‑PD‑1 (Fig. 6a). The combined GADF + PD-1 regimen significantly suppressed HCC proliferation in the liver and conferred a pronounced survival advantage compared with control and monotherapy groups (Fig. 6b–e). Macroscopic and quantitative assessments of liver pathology supported these findings, showing substantial reductions in maximum tumour size and liver-to-body weight ratios in the treatment group, indicating potent tumour control (Fig. 6f–h). The GADF + PD-1 combination therapy enhanced the functional activation of tumour-infiltrating cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes isolated from orthotopic liver tumours, as evidenced by significant upregulation of IFN-γ and granzyme B (GZMB), together with increased proliferative capacity within the CD8+ T-cell subset. Concurrently, the therapy reduced intratumoural infiltration of regulatory T cells (Tregs) (Fig. 6i–k). Together, the enhanced CD8+ T‑cell effector function and proliferation, along with decreased Treg presence, indicate profound immune activation within the liver tumour microenvironment achieved by GADF + PD‑1, which acts in concert with microwave‑induced ICD and metabolic reprogramming of the FAH-fumarate axis. To address species-specific differences in tumour–stroma interactions and pharmacological responses, we established a series of patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) from a recurrent HCC patient (cohort 2), stabilised through three serial transplantations in BALB/c nude mice (Supplementary Fig. 10a). The combination of GADF with MW irradiation achieved potent tumour eradication in this clinically relevant PDX model, along with a favourable biosafety profile (Supplementary Fig. 10b–e). A retrospective Cox analysis of longitudinal clinical data from cohort 3 identified FAH and intermediate histopathology as significant high-risk factors for HCC recurrence. Furthermore, FAH emerged as a potential predictive biomarker for recurrence risk in HCC patients receiving microwave ablation therapy (Supplementary Fig. 10f and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). To clinically benchmark the efficacy of GADF combined with MW under immunocompetent conditions, we established an advanced subcutaneous HCC relapse model in C57BL/6 mice with FAHoe Hepa1–6 cells. Subcutaneous tumours were established and initially subjected to eccentric MW ablation to induce incomplete tumour destruction. After 7 days, recurrent tumours developed and were subsequently treated with complete MW ablation in combination with intravenous injections of GADF (10 mg kg−1 every 4 days), anti‑PD‑1 monoclonal antibody (10 mg kg−1) or IgG isotype control (10 mg kg−1) (Fig. 6l and Supplementary Fig. 10g). The GADF + MW + PD-1 combination exhibited significant and superior therapeutic efficacy in these models (Fig. 6m, n). Subsequent flow cytometry analysis confirmed that this combined approach effectively restored antitumour immune function, as shown by favourable shifts in key immune cell populations within the tumour microenvironment (Fig. 6o and Supplementary Fig. 10h, i).

Discussion

The high recurrence rate of HCC after microwave ablation is an urgent clinical problem to be solved4. Historically, relapse has been attributed to technical insufficiency of the ablation margin1,8. However, growing evidence indicates that sublethal thermal stress actively reshapes the biology of residual tumour cells, endowing them with enhanced proliferative, invasive and immune-evasive capacities through metabolic reprogramming34. This emerging paradigm shifts the focus from passive residual disease to an active, stress-driven adaptation process35. Our study identifies FAH as a critical metabolic node that links thermal stress to tumour recurrence and immune suppression. Clinical evidence from MRI follow-up and IHC staining demonstrated that high FAH expression correlates with poor prognosis and increased recurrence (Fig. 1i and Supplementary Fig. 2a). Based on these findings, we developed a lactate-responsive nanoplatform that disrupts the FAH-fumarate axis and synergizes with anti-PD−1 therapy, thereby improving therapeutic outcomes.

Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer, enabling tumour cells to sustain proliferation and survive under incomplete microwave ablation25,36,37. To survive heat stress, residual cancer cells must rapidly remodel their metabolic network to restore energy supply and redox homeostasis9,21. Our transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed that heat-stressed HCC cells upregulate FAH, leading to fumarate accumulation and enhanced TCA cycle flux. Notably, 13C-glucose tracing and metabolomics showed that FAH knockdown primarily affects fumarate and its immediate upstream metabolite succinate, while citrate levels remain unchanged (Fig. 2b–d). This pattern of localized perturbation is consistent with the multi-layered regulation of citrate homeostasis involving independent transporters and competing enzymes that buffer citrate levels even when adjacent nodes are disrupted38–40. Thus, FAH acts as a node-specific metabolic switch rather than a marker of broad mitochondrial dysfunction.

We further demonstrate that fumarate directly binds and stabilizes HSP70, a major pro-survival chaperone that protects tumour cells from thermal stress and promotes aggressive behaviour41. FAH overexpression accelerates orthotopic tumour growth and subcutaneous relapse, while fumarate supplementation fully rescues the growth-suppressive phenotype caused by FAH knockdown. Importantly, the immunosuppressive role of tumour-derived fumarate has been independently reported: it can succinate and inactivate ZAP70 in infiltrating CD8+ T cells, thereby dampening TCR signalling and effector functions23. Our clinic data show that recurrent tumours exhibit elevated FAH and fumarate levels, impaired CD8+ T‑cell function (Fig. 1j, Supplementary Fig. 2b, Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 9b). Disrupting the FAH-fumarate axis restores CD8+ T‑cell activation and synergizes with anti‑PD‑1 therapy (Fig. 5b–i). The combination of Ga‑MOF and microwave ablation can induce ROS and ICD; however, in the context of microwave ablation, fumarate accumulation plays a dominant, non‑substitutable role in driving immune evasion that ICD alone cannot compensate. Importantly, in our lung metastasis model without local ablation, GADF monotherapy still enhanced anti‑PD‑1 efficacy, indicating a microwave‑independent effect (Fig. 5j–p). These findings, together with the immune profiling data, support a role for fumarate in shaping an immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment. Thus, the observed synergy arises from a dual mechanism: microwave‑dependent ICD/antigen release, and GADF‑mediated disruption of the FAH-fumarate axis, which systemically relieves immunosuppression and sensitizes tumours to checkpoint blockade.

The concept of targeting tumour metabolism to overcome therapeutic resistance has gained substantial traction42. A growing body of literature has established that metabolic reprogramming not only fuels cancer cell proliferation but also actively shapes the immune landscape43,44. For instance, tumour-derived succinate has been shown to impair CD8+ T-cell function and promote regulatory T-cell differentiation45. More recently, fumarate was reported to dampen TCR signalling and antitumour immunity46. These independent observations position fumarate as a bona fide oncometabolite with immune-suppressive properties. To interrogate the functional importance of the FAH-fumarate-HSP70 axis, we performed two lines of causal validation. FAH knockdown significantly suppressed tumour growth, whereas exogenous fumarate supplementation fully rescued this phenotype, positioning fumarate as a necessary and sufficient downstream mediator of FAH‑driven pathology (Fig. 3l–o and Supplementary Fig. 3b–d). Accordingly, GAF0 failed to achieve the same level of tumour control as GAF, confirming that FAH silencing is required for optimal therapeutic synergy with microwave ablation (Supplementary Fig. 8f, g). Together, these causal validations move beyond correlative observations and firmly establish that the FAH-fumarate axis regulates HCC progression. Although the individual components of GADF act in concert to achieve therapeutic efficacy, our dropout and rescue experiments demonstrate that disrupting this axis via FAH knockdown primarily accounts for the effective suppression of tumour growth.

From a translational perspective, the use of Ga-based MOFs for microwave sensitization has been explored by several groups. Previous studies have demonstrated that Ga3+ ions can enhance the generation of reactive oxygen species under microwave irradiation and that Ga-MOFs exhibit favourable biocompatibility when properly functionalised47. More broadly, a growing number of nanoplatforms have been designed to synergise with microwave ablation by targeting tumour metabolism. For instance, Li et al. developed a Co-MOF-based nanoplatform that synchronously interferes with glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation to restrict ATP supply, thereby inhibiting sublethal tumour growth following microwave hyperthermia48. Similarly, Zhou et al. raised a Mn-based nanoplatform to reverse oxygen-related metabolic reprogramming induced tumour hypoxia and simultaneously block the PD−1/PD-L1 axis, demonstrating that metabolic reprogramming and immune modulation can be integrated to enhance cancer therapy49. In another study, a pH-responsive Ga-MOF co-loaded with baicalein was shown to trigger dual-pathway immunogenic cell death and, when combined with cryoablation, reprogram the immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment, providing a precedent for combining Ga-based MOFs with thermal ablation to enhance systemic antitumour immunity50. Meanwhile, the SP94 peptide has been well documented to home to HCC lesions via recognition of GRP78 on the tumour cell surface, a property that has been exploited by multiple nanoplatforms to achieve active tumour targeting51–54. Based on these insights, we engineered GADF that integrates three complementary actions: Ga3+-mediated microwave sensitization, pFAH-driven FAH silencing, and 2-DG-mediated glycolysis inhibition. This platform is surface-conjugated with SP94 peptide to facilitate active targeting of HCC cells and further coated with a LOX shell that confers lactate-responsive degradation, enabling controlled release of Ga3+ in the acidic tumour microenvironment. Importantly, each individual component of GADF (Ga-MOFs, 2-DG, LOX and SP94) has been previously reported to possess favourable long-term biocompatibility, and our comprehensive safety assessments (body weight, serum biomarkers and histopathology of major organs) confirmed that their combination likewise exhibited no significant toxicity.

There are several limitations in our work. While our CyTOF analysis identified that the triple combination therapy predominantly enhanced IFN‑γ+, granzyme B+ and Ki‑67+ CD8+ T‑cell populations, we also observed notable changes in other immune subsets, including PD‑1+CD8+ T cells, tumour‑associated macrophages and B cells. A detailed mechanistic investigation of these alterations falls beyond the scope of the present study, which focuses on the functional CD8+ T‑cell compartment as the primary effector arm of the antitumour response. Nevertheless, these observations provide a foundation for future studies to explore how GADF‑mediated metabolic reprogramming, in concert with microwave ablation and immune checkpoint blockade, may shape broader immune networks. Regarding the nanoplatform itself, the relatively large hydrodynamic diameter may limit passive tumour penetration, and future iterations will focus on size optimisation to enhance distribution while retaining active targeting. In addition, although the use of different sexes across models precludes formal sex‑stratified analysis, within‑model controls (same sex per experiment) ensure internal validity; we acknowledge that potential sex‑dependent effects on therapeutic response cannot be completely excluded and warrant further investigation in sex‑balanced cohorts. Similarly, despite the intrinsic gating limitations of flow cytometry-based Treg identification, we used intra‑sample negative controls and replicate experiments to minimise errors; the observed reduction in Treg recruitment was consistent across replicates and in accordance with controls. Looking forward, systematic optimisation of nanoparticle physicochemical properties, coupled with unbiased immune profiling will help to dissect the complex interplay between metabolic intervention and antitumour immunity. Such efforts may ultimately guide the design of next‑generation nanoplatforms tailored to individual patient profiles and tumour microenvironments.

In summary, this study identifies FAH as a pivotal “metabolic checkpoint” that drives post‑ablation HCC relapse by promoting fumarate accumulation, which simultaneously suppresses CD8+ T‑cell function and upregulates HSP70. To target this vulnerability, we engineered a multifunctional nanoplatform that co‑delivers FAH‑silencing plasmids and the glycolytic inhibitor 2‑DG. GADF disrupts the FAH-fumarate-HSP70 axis, enhances microwave‑induced ROS production, and reverses fumarate‑mediated CD8+ T‑cell dysfunction, thereby reprogramming the immunosuppressive microenvironment and restoring antitumour immunity. When combined with microwave ablation and anti‑PD‑1 therapy, GADF demonstrates robust efficacy across multiple preclinical models, significantly suppressing relapse with a favourable safety profile. This work establishes the FAH-fumarate axis as a metabolic–immune checkpoint and provides a mechanistic framework for integrating metabolic intervention with local ablation and immunotherapy to address a major unmet need in HCC treatment.

Ethics statement

The use of human specimens was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital. Cell culture and microwave-induced thermal assays

siRNAs and lentiviral vectors encoding target genes (shRNAs) were synthesised by Genechem (Shanghai, China) or GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The shRNA and siRNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 3. The FAHoe cell lines (Hepa1–6 and H22) were generated using lentiviral constructs (Genechem, Shanghai, China) encoding the mouse FAH gene. Each construct contained the FAH gene along with puromycin resistance and luciferase reporter genes. The core methodology of transduction and puromycin selection was consistent, but key operational parameters, including the Multiplicity of Infection (MOI) and the optimal puromycin concentration, are different. Stable cell lines were selected using puromycin for one week. FAHsh, SP1si cells were used as pooled populations, whereas FAHoe cells were subjected to single‑cell cloning to establish monoclonal lines.

HCC cell lines (SNU-182, PLC/PRF/5 and HepG2) were subjected to thermal treatment using microwave ablation system. Microwave energy was delivered at increasing power densities (0.6, 1.0, 1.4 and 2.0 W/mL), defined as output power per unit volume of cell suspensions, for 5 min. These conditions yielded peak temperatures of 37, 39, 41 and 43 °C (± 0.3 °C). A power density of 2.0 W/mL (43 °C) was selected for subsequent mechanistic experiments. After MW or any treatment with MW, cells were reseeded in complete culture medium and allowed to adhere under standard culture conditions for 4–6 h. Non‑adherent cells were then removed by gentle washing with phosphate‑buffered saline, and only the attached viable population was used for subsequent functional assays.

Mice models establishment

Subcutaneous model: A total of 3–5 × 106 PLC/PRF/5, Hepa1–6 cells or H22-luc cells were injected into the flanks of 5-week-old female nude, C57BL/6 or BALB/c mice separately. Tumours were harvested after 4–5 weeks for downstream analysis.

Relapse model: Partial MWA was applied to the tumour periphery, followed by complete MWA to the central region.

Oral gavage model: Mice received fumarate (25–75 mg kg−1) by oral gavage daily for one week.

Hepatic metastasis model55: This model was established in 6-week-old female BALB/c mice or BALB/c nude mice under tribromoethanol anaesthesia (200 mg kg−1) via intrasplenic injection of H22-luc cells or HepG2-luc cells (5 × 105 cells in 50 μL PBS), with the needle retained for 30 s to minimise reflux.

Bilateral tumour model: Male C57BL/6 mice aged 6–8 weeks received primary left-flank subcutaneous implants of Hepa1–6 cells (1 × 106 cells). Secondary contralateral tumours (2.5 × 105 cells) were implanted 72 h later. Tumour volumes and body weights were recorded every 48 h until terminal analysis at day 21.

Pulmonary metastasis model: Hepa1–6-luc cells (2 × 105 cells in 100 μL PBS) were administered via intravenous tail vein injection into C57BL/6 mice.

PDX model56: Fresh recurrent HCC specimens were transported in chilled preservation medium within 2 h of resection. Mechanically dissociated tissue fragments were suspended in a 1:1 mixture of Matrigel and DMEM, and 100 μL was subcutaneously implanted into male C-NKG mice for F0 engraftment. Serial passaging was initiated when tumour volumes reached 1500 mm3.

Myc-driven spontaneous HCC model57: Tumourigenesis was induced via hydrodynamic tail vein injection (HDTVi) of a plasmid cocktail (13 μg MYC-IRES-Luc, 10 μg pX330-sgRNA-p53 and 32 μg SB100X transposase in saline) into 5-week-old male C57BL/6 mice, administered at a volume equivalent to 10% of body weight.

Therapeutic interventions included in animal experiments: central microwave ablation and off-centre microwave ablation (eccentric MW) using microwave ablation system at the power of 18 W for 5 min; intravenous administration of nanotherapeutics (GAD, GAF or GADF at 10 mg kg−1); and intraperitoneal immunotherapy (anti-PD-1 or isotype IgG at 10 mg kg−1).

Cohorts

All HCC samples collected from Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital. Recurrent patients underwent microwave ablation therapies.

Cohort 1, assembled by 6 fresh frozen tissues from primary and recurrent HCC patients. Cohort 2, assembled by 18 fresh HCC tissues and part of them were fixed in paraformaldehyde from primary and recurrent HCC patients. Cohort 3, assembled by collecting clinical data and matched archival unstained FFPE tissue sections from 108 primary and recurrent HCC patients. Cohort 4, fresh samples derived from cohort 2 stored at −80 °C were used for western blotting.

Antibodies

Information regarding antibodies is catalogued Supplementary Dataset.

Structural and functional profiling of nanoparticles

The size distribution, zeta potential, and morphology of the nanoparticles were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and dynamic light scattering (Malvern Zetasizer Nano-ZSE). Real-time thermal dynamics during microwave irradiation were captured by forward-looking infrared (FLIR) imaging. Hyperspectral microscopy (CytoViva) was employed to delineate cellular internalization pathways, while ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectrophotometry and fluorescence spectroscopy (Duetta) quantified ROS generation and Ga3+ release kinetics, respectively.

GADF fabrication and characterisation

The synthesis of GADF nanoparticles was initiated by dissolving 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG; 20 mg), the pFAH plasmid (1 mL, 1436 ng/μL), and polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP; 50 mg) in distilled water (20 mL), followed by the addition of gallium nitrate hydrate (Ga(NO3)3·xH2O; 25.5 mg). A solution of carbenicillin disodium (Car; 63.2 mg in 10 mL) was introduced dropwise under continuous stirring for 6 h. The resultant precipitate was harvested by centrifugation, redispersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 5 mL), and subsequently functionalized via sequential conjugation with the SP94 peptide (5 mg, 3 h) and lactate oxidase (10 mg, 6 h at 4 °C), with a final centrifugation step.

First, the nanoplatform is encapsulated within a LOX shell that functions as a responsive element to the acidic TME. Upon degradation in the acidic TME, LOX catalyses the oxidation of lactate to generate H2O2, which in turn disrupts the coordination bonds of the nanoplatform, thereby facilitating its dissociation and selective payload release within HCC-affected regions. Second, to enhance tumour-specific localisation, the surface is functionalised with SP94, a peptide that selectively targets HCC cells. Third, the system integrates pFAH and 2-DG within the Ga-based framework, structurally forming GADF.

Degradation dynamics

The degradation profile of GADF (2 mg mL−1) was assessed in PBS or 20 mM lactate solution under agitation at 37 °C. Temporal morphological evolution was visualized by TEM at 3, 6, 9 and 12 h.

Ga3+ release kinetics

The release kinetics of Ga3+ were evaluated by incubating GADF suspensions in PBS or lactate solution at 37 °C under agitation. Supernatants were collected at predetermined intervals, and Ga³⁺ concentrations were determined by UV–vis spectroscopy, enabling the construction of cumulative release profiles.

Microwave sensitization profiling

Thermal performance: The photothermal conversion efficacy of GADF (0-10 mg mL−1 in saline) under microwave irradiation was evaluated by monitoring temperature in real time using FLIR imaging. Dynamic performance: The capacity for ROS generation was assessed using 2′,7′-DCFH-DA under three conditions: (1) PBS + H2O2, (2) PBS + H2O2 + GADF (2 mg mL−1), and (3) PBS + H2O2 + GADF + microwave irradiation. Fluorescence spectra of the resultant supernatants were quantified post-irradiation.

ROS verification

Singlet oxygen (1O2) generation was assessed by monitoring ABDA fluorescence decay and by ESR spectroscopy (Bruker E500) under the following conditions: (1) ABDA + PBS + H2O2, (2) ABDA + PBS + H2O2 + GADF, (3) ABDA + PBS + H2O2 + GADF + MW. TEMP-trapped ESR signals confirmed 1O2 production.

Cellular uptake mapping

Hyperspectral reference profiles for GADF-empty and SP94-conjugated GADF were acquired using the CytoViva system. HepG2 cells were incubated with 50 μg/mL of each formulation for 6 h, fixed in 4% formaldehyde, and imaged for spectral matching.

Cell proliferation and viability assay

Treated cells were plated in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere. At indicated time points, culture medium was replaced with CCK-8 solution, followed by 2 h incubation at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Varioskan Flash multimode reader (Thermo Scientific).

Intracellular ROS and mitochondrial membrane potential imaging

Treated cells on glass-bottom dishes underwent sequential processing: EdU incorporation (10 μM, 2 h), fixation in 4% PFA (15 min), and Click-iT reaction with Alexa Fluor 647; followed by live-cell staining with 10 μM DCFH-DA (30 min, 37 °C), 100 μM H₂O₂ stimulation (10 min), and 5 μg/mL JC-1 (20 min, 37 °C). Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (5 μg/mL, 10 min). Quantifications included EdU+ nuclei frequency, mean DCF intensity, JC-1 red/green ratio, and nuclear morphometrics.

RNA sequencing analysis

For transcriptomic profiling under thermal challenge, total RNA was isolated from SNU-182 cells using TRIzol, with quality control metrics affirming RIN > 7.0 and 28S:18S ratio > 1.8. PolyA-selected libraries were constructed and subjected to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina platform. Sequencing reads were aligned to the hg19 genome employing TopHat2, and gene expression was quantified using HTSeq. Differential expression was determined with edgeR (FDR < 0.05). Functional annotation was performed via DAVID (GO/KEGG), and phenotype associations were interrogated by GSEA.

IHC staining and H&E staining

Human HCC sections underwent antigen retrieval using citrate buffer (pH 6.0, 95 °C), whereas murine tissues were processed with EDTA buffer (pH 9.0, 100 °C). Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Detection was achieved with polymer-HRP secondary antibodies and kinetic-controlled DAB development (human: 110 s; murine tumours: 90 s; normal murine: 70 s), terminated in ice-water. Following haematoxylin counterstaining, whole slides were digitized (Ventana iScan HT).

Western blot analysis

Cells (1 × 105/well) were rinsed with PBS and lysed (PBS/1% Triton ×-100/1 mM PMSF; 30 min, 4 °C). Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay. Equivalent protein amounts were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk/PBST (2 h, RT), membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (4 °C, overnight), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1 h, RT). Signals were visualized using SuperSignal West Pico substrate, with Cyclophilin B as the loading control.

qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted with the TIANGEN RNA Rapid Extraction Kit. cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg RNA using the TIANGEN First-strand cDNA system. qPCR reactions utilized 0.1 μg cDNA, SYBR Green master mix (ABM), and FAH-specific primers on a real-time PCR system. FAH expression was normalized to Actin. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Multiplexed immunofluorescence staining

Multiplexed immunofluorescence utilised OPAL tyramide signal amplification on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections (4 μm) processed using a Leica Bond RX platform. Following deparaffinisation and high-pH epitope retrieval (EDTA, pH 9.0, 100 °C), sequential seven-plex staining was performed with the following antibody–opal pairs: anti-pan-cytokeratin (phospho-S39; 1:100) with Opal 520, anti-FAH (1:250) with Opal 570, anti-HSP70 (1:300) with Opal 540, anti-granzyme B (1:150) with Opal 620, anti-interferon-γ (1:100) with Opal 650, and anti-CD8 (1:200) with Opal 690. Microwave-mediated stripping (AR9 buffer, 100 °C) was applied before each staining cycle. Peroxidase inactivation (0.3% H2O2 in methanol) and autofluorescence quenching (0.1% Sudan Black B) were implemented between cycles to maintain specificity, with crosstalk validated at <3%. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Sections were imaged using a Vectra Polaris scanner.

Mass spectrometry imaging

Fresh-frozen tissues were cryosectioned at 12 μm, mounted on conductive slides, and dried under vacuum. 2,5-Dihydroxybenzoic acid (DHB) matrix (20 mg mL−1 in 70% methanol/0.2% TFA) was applied using a TM-Sprayer (30 °C, 85% relative humidity). Imaging was performed on a timsTOF fleX instrument operated in negative ion mode (10 kHz laser frequency, 50 μm spatial resolution, m/z range 50–1000). Target analytes included lactate (m/z 89.0244) and fumarate (m/z 115.0037). External calibration used red phosphorus clusters; internal validation was performed using on-tissue standards. Data were processed in SCiLS Lab 2023b with TIC normalisation and ROI analysis.

Cell clonogenic assay

Single cells were plated in 6-well plates (200-800 cells per well) and cultured for 7–14 days. Colonies were fixed with methanol and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Colonies containing >50 cells were counted. Survival fraction was calculated as (number of colonies formed/number of cells seeded) × 100%, normalised to untreated controls.

Wound healing assay

Confluent cell monolayers were linearly scratched using sterile pipette tips, washed with PBS, and maintained in serum-free medium. Wound areas were recorded at 0, 24 and 48 h using time-lapse microscopy. Absolute wound areas were quantified by image analysis. Percentage of wound area was calculated as (wound area at 24 h/initial wound area) × 100% and (wound area at 48 h/wound area at 24 h) × 100%.

Transwell assay

Serum-starved cells (5 × 104 per insert) were seeded into uncoated Transwell inserts (for migration assays) or Matrigel-coated inserts (6.25 mg mL−1; for invasion assays) with a pore size of 8 μm. The lower chamber contained medium supplemented with 20% FBS. Following incubation at 37 °C for 12 h, non-migrated cells were removed. Migrated cells on the membrane underside were fixed and stained with 0.2% crystal violet. Cells in five randomly selected fields per membrane were quantified.

In vivo imaging protocol

Tumour progression was monitored weekly using an IVIS Spectrum imaging system. Imaging commenced 5 min after intraperitoneal injection of luciferin (Solarbio; 100 mg kg−1). Hepatic bioluminescent flux was quantified with Living Image software after background subtraction. Tumours became detectable within 6–8 weeks.

Metabolic flux and metabolomics analysis

FAH knockdown SNU-182 cells and corresponding controls were inoculated in T-25 flasks containing 4.5 g/L [U-13C6]-glucose (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc.) for a 2 h metabolic flux analysis. Cells were washed twice with PBS and metabolites were extracted using ice-cold 80% methanol. Metabolic flux profiles were acquired by UPLC-QQQ-MS/MS (Agilent 1290/6460) operated in multiple reaction monitoring mode. A parallel targeted metabolomics analysis focusing on glucose metabolism employed the same extraction and LC-MS/MS quantification procedures58.

Fumarate target prediction

The three-dimensional structure of fumarate (SDF format obtained from PubChem CID 444972) was submitted to the PharmMapper Server. The following parameters were applied: 300 conformations generated, human protein targets selected, and the top 300 matches retained. HSP70 was identified as a candidate binding protein for fumarate59,60.

Molecular docking

Protein-ligand docking between HSP70 (crystallographic structure) and fumarate was performed using CB-DOCK2. Receptor preparation involved hydrogen addition and charge assignment. A grid box of dimensions 40 × 40 × 40 Å3 with 1-Å spacing was centred on the ATP-binding pocket to define the search space. Five independent docking runs were conducted to confirm pose convergence; the lowest-energy complexes were visualised using PyMOL.

Thermal shift and drug affinity reactive target stability assays

For CETSA61, fumarate-treated SNU-182 cells (0.1–100 mM, 24 h) underwent heat shock (42–56 °C, 3 min). Lysates prepared by freeze-thaw cycling were centrifuged (15,000 × g, 30 min). HSP70 thermostability in supernatants was evaluated via immunoblotting and normalised to cyclophilin B.

For DARTS62, cell lysates in M-PER/TNC buffer were incubated with fumarate gradients (0.1–100 mM, 1 h), followed by pronase digestion (1:3000, 30 min) and denaturation. HSP70 proteolytic resistance was quantified by immunoblotting using cyclophilin B as the control.

Immunofluorescence

Fixed cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against FAH, HSP70, E-cadherin and N-cadherin in PBS/1% BSA. Following PBS washes, species-matched Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies were incubated with the cells for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL, 10 min)63.

Extracellular flux analysis

Mitochondrial stress parameters were profiled by sequential injection of oligomycin (15 μM), FCCP (5 μM) and rotenone/antimycin A (5 μM). Glycolytic function was assessed by injection of glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (1.0 μM) and 2-DG (50 mM). OCR and ECAR data were acquired on a Seahorse XFe24 Analyzer, and metabolic rates were calculated using Wave Software.

Metabolic assays

Metabolite levels were determined using enzymatic methods: fumarate via enzymatic cycling (λ = 450 nm); ATP via a phosphotransferase-coupled chromogenic assay (λ = 570 nm); glucose uptake by the glucose oxidase-peroxidase reaction (λ = 505 nm); lactate generation by lactate dehydrogenase-driven NAD+ reduction (λ = 490 nm); tyrosine by tyrosinase-mediated quinone formation (λ = 475 nm); and acetoacetate by β-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase-linked NADH oxidation (λ = 340 nm). Absorbance readings were obtained in replicate using a microplate reader and normalised to total protein (BCA assay), with untreated SNU-182 cells serving as controls.

Micro-PET/CT imaging

Tumour-bearing nude mice were fasted for 8 h prior to intravenous administration of 300 μCi 18F-FDG. On day 4 after microwave ablation, mice underwent CT imaging immediately after tracer injection and PET imaging (0 h, 1 h). SUVmax, relative SUVmax, MTV and TLG were quantified for the control versus GADF + MW groups. Relative SUVmax was defined as SUVmax (1 h)/SUVmax (0 h) for each mouse.

Hepato-renal function assessment

Serum was obtained by centrifuging blood collected via cardiac puncture (3000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). AST and ALT levels were quantified using commercial reagent kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with absorbance measured spectrophotometrically at 505 nm (AST) and 510 nm (ALT). BUN concentration was determined using a urease-Berthelot method (Sigma-Aldrich kit) with measurement at 520 nm.

CyTOF immune profiling

Fresh murine tumour specimens were dissociated and stained with a 41-parameter metal-tagged antibody panel covering T-cell subsets, myeloid lineages, immune checkpoints and activation markers across four treatment groups: untreated controls, PD-1 monotherapy (10 mg kg−1 i.p.), PD-1 combined with microwave ablation (50 mg mL−1, 5 min), and triple therapy with GADF (10 mg kg−1 i.v.) + MW + PD-1. Single-cell suspensions were acquired on a Helios™ CyTOF system calibrated with EQ beads. Data were subjected to t-SNE dimensionality reduction to visualise global immune architecture and FlowSOM clustering to quantify population frequency shifts.

Flow cytometry analysis

Tumour tissues were harvested and processed into single‑cell suspensions. Two independent antibody panels were used for surface and intracellular staining.

CD8+ T‑cell functional analysis: cells were stained with APC‑Cy7 anti‑mouse CD45, FITC anti‑mouse CD3e, and R718 anti‑mouse CD8a. For intracellular cytokine detection, cells were stimulated with phorbol 12‑myristate 13‑acetate (PMA, 100 ng/mL), ionomycin (1 μg/mL), and Brefeldin A (10 μg/mL) for 4 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After surface staining, cells were fixed, permeabilised, and stained with BV650 anti‑mouse IFN‑γ, PE anti‑human/mouse Granzyme B, and BV421 anti‑mouse Ki‑67. Fluorescence minus one (FMO) controls were prepared for each intracellular marker (IFN‑γ, Granzyme B, and Ki‑67) by omitting the respective antibody while retaining all others. Positive gates for these markers were set based on the corresponding FMO controls.

CD4+ T‑cell and Treg analysis: cells were stained with Brilliant Violet 510 anti‑mouse CD45, FITC anti‑mouse CD3e, PE/Cyanine anti‑mouse CD4, APC anti‑mouse CD25, and Pacific Blue anti‑FoxP3 following a transcription factor staining protocol according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Positive gates for FoxP3 were set using CD4+CD25− cells within the same sample as the biological negative population, as these cells do not express FoxP3. Unstained cells were used to assess background fluorescence for the remaining markers.

Gating strategy: data acquisition was performed on a flow cytometer, and compensation was applied. Single cells were gated based on FSC‑A vs. FSC‑H, followed by selection of CD45+ leukocytes, from which CD3+ T cells were gated. CD8+ T cells were identified as CD3+CD8+, and CD4+ T cells as CD3+CD4+. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) were defined as CD4+CD25+FoxP3+. For functional markers, CD8+ T cells were further analysed for IFN‑γ, Granzyme B, and Ki‑67 expression. The complete gating hierarchy was: All events → Single cells → CD45+ → CD3+ → (CD4+ or CD8+) → (IFN‑γ+, Granzyme B+, Ki‑67+, or FoxP3+) (Supplementary Figs. 11, 12). Data were analysed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1).

ChIP-qPCR

SNU-182 cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde (10 min), quenched with glycine and lysed. Chromatin was sonicated to 300–500 bp fragments (Bioruptor Pico, 6 cycles of 30 s on/30 s off). SP1-bound DNA was immunoprecipitated using an anti-SP1 antibody (EPR23614-148, Abcam; 5 μg/IP) with magnetic bead capture (Pierce Magnetic ChIP Kit). Rabbit IgG and input chromatin served as controls. Precipitated DNA was analysed by qPCR, primers listed in Supplementary Table 5.

FAH Promoter mutational analysis and luciferase reporter assay

The FAH promoter region was amplified from human genomic DNA. The promoter fragment is 2000 bp in length, corresponding to human chromosome 15 (NCBI Reference Sequence: NC_000015.10). Cloning strategy: PCR primers were designed with KpnI and XhoI restriction sites at their 5′ ends. The amplified product was double-digested with KpnI and XhoI and ligated into the pGL3-Basic vector digested with the same enzymes to generate the WT reporter construct. Three putative binding sites were mutated by overlap-extension PCR, and the mutant constructs were generated using the same cloning strategy. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing.

SNU-182 cells seeded in 24-well plates were co-transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) with 200 ng of WT or mutant reporter, 50 ng of pRL-SV40 Renilla control, and 100 ng of transcription factor expression vector or 350 ng empty vector. After 48 h, dual-luciferase activities were measured using the Promega system. Firefly luciferase signals were normalised to Renilla luciferase activity. Mutant-to-WT activity ratios were calculated to identify functional regulatory elements. Each experiment was performed in triplicate wells and repeated three times.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical tests were two-sided. Information on adjustments for multiple comparisons is provided in the respective figure legends and supplementary tables, as applicable. Source data and exact P values are provided in the Source Data.
