History of Presentation

A 49-year-old woman with diffuse systemic sclerosis (SSc) presented to the emergency department with a 3-day history of progressive dyspnea, cough, lower-extremity edema, fatigue, and palpitations. On presentation, she was in respiratory distress with an oxygen saturation of 80% on room air, heart rate 105 beats/min, and blood pressure 85/70 mm Hg. Cardiovascular examination revealed a loud P2, cool extremities, and bilateral pitting edema. Pulmonary examination showed bilateral mid-lung crackles.

Past Medical History

She had received a diagnosis of limited cutaneous scleroderma, had refused immunosuppressive therapy, and had paroxysmal atrial fibrillation treated with flecainide.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnoses included scleroderma-associated myocarditis, ischemic cardiomyopathy, hypertensive heart failure related to scleroderma renal crisis, and pulmonary arterial hypertension with secondary right ventricular failure.

Management

On presentation, laboratory evaluation showed mildly elevated creatinine (75 μmol/L), transaminases (AST 92 U/L; ALT 90 U/L), markedly elevated high-sensitivity troponin (1,854-1,887 ng/L), N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (26,615 pg/mL), and lactate (2.2 mmol/L). Electrocardiography demonstrated sinus tachycardia with right bundle branch block and low QRS voltage (Figure 1). She was admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit (ICU) with Society for Cardiovascular Angiography & Interventions stage C cardiogenic shock and started on intravenous milrinone and furosemide. Pulmonary artery catheterization confirmed elevated filling pressures, moderate pulmonary hypertension, and reduced cardiac output. Left heart catheterization showed normal coronary arteries. Cardiac magnetic resonance (MRI) (Figure 3) showed diffuse mid-myocardial delayed enhancement in the basal and mid septum and epicardial delayed enhancement in the basal anterolateral wall and basal apical wall. There was subendocardial delayed enhancement in the basal and mid lateral wall. Diffuse myocardial edema was demonstrated by T1 and T2 mapping. Findings are consistent with active myocarditis as per Lake Louise criteria (2018). Rheumatology recommended cyclophosphamide and low-dose corticosteroid; the patient initially declined therapy.Figure 3Cardiac Magnetic Resonance ImagingColor-coded native T1 and T2 maps (A: motion correction T1; B: T2 map) in a mid -short-axis view demonstrate high T1 and T2 signals involving the left ventricular myocardium mainly the mid-septum, anterior and inferior wall (pointed by the arrows). She developed sustained ventricular tachycardia and was treated with amiodarone. Hemodynamic variables worsened to Society for Cardiovascular Angiography & Interventions stage D cardiogenic shock, with rising lactate, low mixed venous oxygen saturation, and oliguria. After multidisciplinary discussion, venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation with an axillary Impella CP device (Abiomed) was initiated and inhaled nitric oxide was added. Endomyocardial biopsy (Figure 4) showed patchy myocardial fibrosis and evidence of perivascular lymphocytic infiltration. Her ICU course was complicated by Serratia bacteremia, coagulopathy, and oliguric renal failure requiring continuous renal replacement therapy. Consideration for central biventricular assist device or transplantation was precluded because of bilateral cavitary lung lesions. Despite maximal therapy, she died on ICU day 54.

Discussion

We describe a case of fulminant scleroderma-associated myocarditis resulting in rapidly progressive biventricular failure and refractory cardiogenic shock. SSc is a systemic autoimmune fibrosing disease that carries a 5-year mortality rate up to 75%.1 The reported prevalence of primary heart involvement (pHI) ranges from 7% to 39%, manifesting as myocarditis, arrhythmias, or heart failure, whereas autopsy studies demonstrate subclinical myocardial involvement in up to 80% of patients.2 SSc-pHI encompasses a diverse spectrum of clinical entities, and the pathogenesis of myocardial injury in SSc is multifactorial and involves immune-mediated inflammation, microvascular dysfunction, and recurrent ischemia-–reperfusion injury due to episodic coronary microvascular spasm, a phenomenon referred to as “myocardial Raynaud's.” These mechanisms ultimately promote myocardial fibrosis, increased ventricular stiffness, and progressive systolic and diastolic dysfunction.3

Myocarditis is one of the forms of cardiac involvement in SSc and is thought to be due to skeletal myositis. Multiple extracellular factors and signaling molecules contribute to myocardial injury and fibrosis, notably transforming growth factor-β, endothelin-1, and oxidative stress driven by reactive oxygen species. Although systolic dysfunction occurs less commonly in SSc, it is frequently linked to myocardial inflammation.4 Microvascular changes are believed to be reversible in the early stages of the disease.5 However, as the disease advances and early treatment is delayed, these structural alterations can progress to an irreversible state as in our patient's case. A consensus-based definition of SSc-pHI was published by the World Scleroderma Foundation/Heart Failure Association. Patients with SSc-PHI were defined as patients fulfilling at least one of the inclusion criteria and none of the exclusion criteria (Table 1).6 Early diagnosis and detection of cardiac involvement in SSc are crucial for improving patient outcomes, as cardiac complications can rapidly progress and culminate in death.1 Clinical examinations and routine noninvasive diagnostic tools such as electrocardiography and thoracic radiography carry a low diagnostic sensitivity. Echocardiography, specifically tissue Doppler imaging, computed tomography (CT), radionuclide ventriculography, nuclear tests such as thallium-201 single-photon emission CT and positron emission tomography, and MRI, are sensitive techniques for early detection of functional and structural scleroderma-related cardiac abnormalities.7 Cardiac MRI carries significantly superior sensitivity in detecting subendocardial ischemia compared with nuclear imaging (single-photon emission CT and positron emission tomography); along with advanced parametric techniques such as T1 mapping, it provides a robust method to assess myocardial perfusion. Although cardiac CT and MRI are both valuable techniques for the assessment of pericardial and myocardial thickness and inflammation,7,8 cardiac MRI is the gold standard noninvasive tool for diagnosis and, in this case, confirmed widespread myocardial inflammation and fibrosis involving both ventricles. This pattern of diffuse myocardial involvement is consistent with prior studies demonstrating that SSc myocarditis often progresses silently before manifesting as overt heart failure or malignant arrhythmias. Detection of these cardiac MRI features is also associated with a less favorable prognosis. This case highlights several important clinical lessons. First, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion of myocardial involvement in patients with SSc presenting with dyspnea, arrhythmias, or heart failure, even in the absence of ischemic symptoms. Second, early use of cardiac magnetic resonance and prompt initiation of immunosuppressive therapy may improve outcomes by limiting irreversible myocardial injury. Third, management of fulminant SSc myocarditis requires close multidisciplinary collaboration between rheumatology, cardiology, critical care, and advanced heart failure teams. Finally, progression to refractory biventricular failure despite aggressive immunosuppression and mechanical circulatory support underscores the need for prospective studies to establish evidence-based treatment strategies in this high-risk population.

Follow-Up

No follow-up was available because the patient died during the index hospitalization.

Conclusions

SSc-Myocarditis is a rare but life-threatening manifestation that may present with rapidly progressive biventricular failure and cardiogenic shock. This case illustrates the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges posed by autoimmune myocardial inflammation complicated by multiorgan dysfunction and contraindications to immunosuppression. Although early initiation of immunosuppressive therapy and mechanical circulatory support may provide hemodynamic stabilization, advanced cases may require consideration of heart transplantation. Prompt recognition, multidisciplinary care, and early referral to advanced heart failure centers are critical to improving outcomes in this high-risk population.
