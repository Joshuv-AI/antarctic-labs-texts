APOE, Ancestry, and the Limits of Portability in Neurodegenerative Disease Genetics
Authors: Farren B.S. Briggs
Published: 2026

For decades, genetic discovery has been concentrated in populations of European ancestry, with subsequent efforts often asking whether European discoveries “replicate” in other populations. That framing has been useful, but it is increasingly insufficient as we begin to grasp the nuances of studying admixed and globally diverse populations. The central question is no longer whether known risk alleles have similar associations across populations, but how genetic risk architecture is shaped by ancestral, ethnoracial, environmental, and life-course contexts. APOE, the strongest and most widely studied genetic risk factor for late-onset Alzheimer disease, exemplifies both the promise and the complexity of this transition.

In this issue of Neurology® Genetics, 3 studies contribute to this necessary shift from different vantage points. Their combined contribution is meaningful, but so is their shared caution. Studies of genetic risk across populations must still navigate limited representation, as well as study design features that determine who is observed and how ancestry is measured. Thus, the value of these studies lies not only in the answers they provide but also in the methodological questions they pose for the field to address in future studies of diverse populations.

Lakomski et al.1 make an important contribution by pooling well-characterized, population-based cohorts with individual-level data and incident dementia ascertainment. This allowed the authors to address several limitations of the prior APOE literature, including limited population diversity, reliance on study designs in which the source populations are difficult to define, and frequent emphasis on ε4 or ε2 carrier status rather than the full range of APOE genotypes. Their findings reinforce the central role of APOE in dementia risk: ε2/ε3 was associated with lower dementia risk compared with ε3/ε3, whereas ε4/ε4 was associated with higher dementia risk among Asian, Black, and White participants, with the strongest relative association observed among White participants. By contrast, estimates for ε2/ε2 were null and imprecise and did not support clear conclusions about protection, highlighting the difficulty of studying less common APOE genotypes even in large, pooled cohorts.

At the same time, the study illustrates the challenges of APOE research across diverse populations. Differences in the magnitude of APOE associations across self-reported racial groups are difficult to interpret without distinguishing self-identified race and ethnicity, which are important social constructs, from global and local genetic ancestry, which capture key dimensions of inherited variation. This distinction is important because APOE effects may depend on its local ancestry, which could contribute to attenuated ε4 associations observed in the Black American participants.2,3 The ε2/ε2 findings also warrant caution: limited precision is expected given the genotype's rarity, but cohort eligibility and the pleiotropic biology may also contribute. Several contributing cohorts excluded participants with prevalent cardiometabolic conditions, potentially underrepresenting those with APOE-related lipid or vascular phenotypes that might have contributed to earlier morbidity or dementia onset. For example, ε2 homozygosity may have distinct remnant lipoprotein effects and it is a prominent cause of hyperlipidemia type III.4,5 It is important that even within cohorts that may have selected for healthier older adults at baseline, consistent with the older average age at dementia onset in the study population, ε4/ε4 was associated with higher dementia risk across racial groups. This reinforces the clinical relevance of APOE ε4 while underscoring that differences in effect magnitude should be interpreted through the context of genotype, ancestry, and eligibility criteria.

Labounek et al.6 extend this discussion into an American Indian population from Ojibwe and Dakota tribal nations, a population underrepresented in dementia genetics and neuroimaging research. The study's major contribution is the integration of APOE ε4 status with measures of white matter microstructure in a community-based cohort of tribal elders. The central finding was a lack of APOE ε4-associated white matter microstructural differences, which parallels findings from a previous study that observed attenuated APOE ε4 associations with cortical atrophy in American Indian populations.7 These results challenge the assumption that APOE ε4 effects observed in other populations, such as those studied by Lakomski et al.,1 can be directly generalized to American Indian communities and suggest that APOE ε4 is unlikely to be a sufficient explanation for the high burden of dementia in these populations.

A key challenge in interpreting this finding is that the study population may represent only a subset of the underlying American Indian cohort. Among participants excluded because of missing MRI data, 31% were APOE ε4 carriers, compared with 16% of those included in the analytic sample. This imbalance does not negate the observed lack of association, but it makes the null finding a bit more difficult to interpret as evidence that APOE ε4 has no neuroimaging relevance in the broader source population. As with Lakomski et al.,1 interpretation could be further informed by considering how self-identified population membership relates to broader patterns of genetic variation, including local APOE ancestry. In Labounek et al.,6 Native ancestry was operationally defined by self-reported blood quantum, a unique approach that respects tribal identity in the research setting, but one that is an incomplete proxy for genetic ancestry. Thus, Labounek et al. make an important contribution by expanding APOE neuroimaging research into an understudied population, while also underscoring that missingness, sampling variability, and the distinction between social and genetic measures of ancestry are important considerations.

Jones et al.8 bring a complementary public health framework to neurogenetics. In an era when genome-wide association studies have identified hundreds of Alzheimer disease and Parkinson disease risk loci, many with modest effect sizes and widely varying allele frequencies, it is increasingly difficult to determine which variants should be prioritized for follow-up functional or translational studies. By estimating population-specific population-attributable risk, the authors attempt to move beyond statistical significance alone and ask which variants may have the greatest relevance at the population level. This framing is valuable because variants with similar odds ratios may differ substantially in their potential population impact depending on their frequency, while rarer variants with larger effects may be biologically important but account for a smaller fraction of overall disease burden. In this sense, Jones et al. provide a useful prioritization framework that links genetic discovery to questions of population relevance.

However, population-attributable risk estimates rely on strong assumptions, including assumptions about causality, variant selection, the source and transferability of effect sizes, alignment of risk alleles across populations, and the allele-based or genotype-based definition of genetic risk used in the calculation. These issues are especially important when variants identified primarily in European ancestry discovery studies are evaluated across other populations, where effect estimates in other populations may be imprecise, nonsignificant, or unavailable. As a result, these estimates reflect portability of specific variants and not the actual relevance of the underlying loci across populations. This limitation also highlights a broader gap: the relative paucity of risk variants that have been discovered in non-European populations. Another challenge with interpretation arises when protective alleles are recoded so that the nonprotective allele is treated as the risk allele. Although this may be algebraically consistent with a population-attributable risk framework, it changes the counterfactual from removing a deleterious risk variant to assuming universal presence of a protective allele. Such estimates may therefore be difficult to interpret as the proportion of cases preventable by eliminating a harmful allele, particularly when the recoded “risk” allele is common or represents the background neutral allele rather than a deleterious variant. This concern is compounded when risk allele frequency, rather than genotype or carrier prevalence, is used as the exposure. For loci with complex allelic and genotype risk structures, such as APOE, allele-frequency estimates may incompletely capture the population relevance of the locus and may mask meaningful population differences. Population-attributable risk can therefore be a useful prioritization tool, but it should be interpreted in the context of its assumptions and not as a precise measure of preventable disease burden.

Taken together, these studies represent progress toward a more inclusive neurogenetic understanding of Alzheimer disease, Parkinson disease, and related dementias. Lakomski et al.1 show that APOE genotype remains clinically relevant across racial groups, even in population-based cohorts that may represent healthier survivors. Labounek et al.6 demonstrate that APOE ε4 associations observed in other populations should not be assumed to apply uniformly to American Indian communities. Jones et al.8 provide a useful public-health framework for prioritizing genetic variants by their potential population relevance. Each study therefore advances the field beyond a simple replication paradigm and toward a more nuanced understanding of how genetic risk may differ across populations.

The next step is to ensure that the analytic frameworks are further refined to reflect our understanding of these nuances. Future work should more consistently distinguish self-identified race and ethnicity, community identity, global genetic ancestry, and local ancestry at disease-relevant loci; report selection and missingness by genotype and ancestry where possible; model APOE and other complex loci using genotype-informed approaches; and interpret population-attributable risk estimates in light of their assumptions and counterfactuals. While APOE remains central to dementia genetics, these studies show that as genetic research expands to more diverse and historically understudied populations, the field must ask more precise questions about why and for whom genetic risk differs.
