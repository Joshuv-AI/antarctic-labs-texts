Urinary incontinence: management and quality of life from a patient’s perspective
Authors: Sharon Roman
Published: 2026

I was diagnosed with multiple sclerosis (MS) 28 years ago. Of the myriad symptoms common in patients with MS, bladder dysfunction is one of my most troublesome. Ranging from incontinence to urinary retention, my bladder dysfunction has been the cause of both embarrassment and notable health risks — the irony of neurogenic bladder. Many years ago, on learning that I was wearing Huggies Pull-Ups to bed, a clinic nurse exclaimed, “You don’t have to live like that!” I was later prescribed oxybutynin, which gave me renewed nocturnal freedom and quality of life. I was disappointed with myself that I had allowed my embarrassment about wetting the bed to prevent me from seeking help earlier, especially when treatment seemed so simple — two tablets daily. That said, the accompanying cystoscopies were more challenging because they were painful.

However, medication-induced urinary retention and increasing urinary tract infections, which can worsen symptoms of MS and trigger relapses, posed concerns, and a new specialist advised discontinuing the oxybutynin. I asked for the taper schedule. Regrettably, physician-directed sudden cessation of my long-time anticholinergic resulted in well-known sudden-onset cardiac effects and an unplanned admission to hospital. I never returned to that physician’s care (though I appreciated their decision to forgo regular cystoscopies, owing to infection risk). Afterward, the familiar urgency and incontinence went untreated, eroding my social confidence and quality of life once again.

Much later, with a long-term physician, I again raised my desire for treatment, and he told me about Botox injections. However, systemic barriers to care arose. A new specialist missed a telehealth appointment with a 4-hour window and left a voicemail hours later. The system requires repeat cystoscopies, first for workup (plus urodynamics), then again to receive the Botox injection. Given my decades-long, well-documented bladder dysfunction and a history of cystoscopies being painful, could it not all be done at once? Gate-keeping specialists’ clerks seemed uninterested in accommodating even my question, preferring the routine protocol of trying to schedule an initial appointment.

To this day, bladder dysfunction remains an embarrassing and isolating problem for me and adequate treatment is a challenge, though no longer out of embarrassment about addressing it. When I again raised the subject with a different specialist and recapped my experiences, I was shocked at their suggestion that since I had just explained I have been living with incontinence for this long I should just continue to do so. This point of view is profoundly upsetting because it fails to acknowledge the negative impact incontinence has on my life — nor does it help. Where quality of life is concerned, every effort should be made to find improvement, no matter how small.

While I am a candidate for Botox, previous experiences with cystoscopies have been traumatic, and navigating the path to being able to receive this treatment modality seems an unforgiving and insurmountable challenge, trumped only by the public humiliation of loss of bladder control.
