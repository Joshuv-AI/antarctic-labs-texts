The efficacy and effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines in Iran: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Seyede Maryam Mousavi, Sobhan Younesian, Masud Yunesian, Akbar Fotouhi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the end of the COVID-19 pandemic, there is an increasing demand for comprehensive data on vaccine effectiveness disaggregated by country. Such information will provide insights into national immunization policy differences and lay the groundwork for future pandemic readiness plans. This study evaluates the efficacy and effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines in Iran.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar up to February 1, 2025. Studies assessing COVID-19 vaccine effectiveness or efficacy in Iran without restrictions on variants or vaccine platforms were included in the systematic review. Meta-analysis using the random effects model was performed to estimate the pooled effectiveness of combined vaccination platforms against hospital admission and death outcomes.ResultsTwo randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and 11 observational studies were included in the systematic review, and five observational studies were included in the meta-analysis. Based on the meta-analysis, considering all vaccination platforms combined, the pooled effectiveness of 2-dose COVID-19 vaccines against hospital admission and death was 51.1% (21.8 to 69.5) and 55.7% (-1.5 to 80.7), respectively. Against infection, RCTs reported efficacies of 50.2% and 49.7%, while observational studies reported effectiveness ranging from 63.9% to 87.1% for different vaccines.ConclusionVaccine effectiveness varied substantially across the studies, with unique patterns related to Iran’s situation during the pandemic, including the high number of different deployed vaccines. The lack of sufficient studies and high heterogeneity between the included studies have limited the understanding of COVID-19 vaccines’ effectiveness in Iran.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11716-6.Keywords: Coronavirus, Immunization, Persian, Prevention, SARS-CoV-2

Background

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) refers to the infectious disease caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) that initially arose in late 2019 in Wuhan, China [1]. In March 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) proclaimed COVID-19 as a pandemic and announced its concern for the global spread of the disease [2]. In May 2023, the WHO pronounced the cessation of COVID-19 as a public health emergency of international concern (PHEIC) [3]. From the outset of the COVID-19 pandemic, there had been steadfast attempts to develop vaccines against the disease, which resulted in the introduction of multiple vaccines; accordingly, the BNT162b2 (Pfizer-BioNTech) and mRNA-1237 (Moderna) vaccines pioneered in obtaining emergency use authorization by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [4]. Consequently, other vaccines, including the AZD1222 (Oxford-AstraZeneca), Gam-COVID-Vac (Sputnik V), and the BBIBP-CorV (Sinopharm), were approved for emergency use, following their shown acceptable level of efficacy and safety [5–7].

Vaccines averted at least 14.4 million global deaths attributable to COVID-19 within their first year of rollout [8]. Nevertheless, countries remarkably differed regarding COVID-19 vaccination policies, coverage, national campaigns, and the composition of administered doses by the manufacturer [9]. Many low- and lower-middle-income countries faced setbacks in the early rollout of COVID-19 vaccines due to logistical, financial, and infrastructural restrictions [10]. Iran commenced vaccination campaigns in February 2021 with the strategy to prioritize healthcare workers and individuals aged over 65 years [11]. Until November 2023, Iran had vaccinated 72.8% of the total population with at least one dose and 65.4% with the primary vaccine series, nearly identical to the global estimates [12]. In Iran, the BBIBP-CorV (Sinopharm) vaccine constituted over 80% of the cumulative administered doses, followed by the AZD1222 (Oxford-AstraZeneca) vaccine [13]. As of January 2025, the total confirmed COVID-19 death number in Iran has accumulated to 146,837 [14]. Due to the specific geopolitical barriers in Iran, the country responded to the vaccination need by developing local vaccine formulations and exploiting primary health resources [15].

The overall performance of the COVID-19 vaccination program and the real-world effectiveness of the vaccines in Iran remains to be determined. Although COVID-19 no longer represents a public health threat in the country, the potential for future pandemics, whether driven by evolving SARS-CoV-2 variants or novel agents, remains imminent [16]. Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation of the efficacy and effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines in Iran is necessary, which summarizes how vaccination has contributed to COVID-19 mitigation and informs policy actions aimed at responding to future unanticipated health crises [17]. Primary studies reporting on the effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines in Iran have yielded divergent results due to their varied conduct design, time interval, and location. In this study, we conducted a systematic review of the COVID-19 vaccine effectiveness and efficacy studies in Iran; furthermore, a meta-analysis was performed to estimate the combined effectiveness of vaccines against COVID-19-related hospitalization and death.

Methods

Search strategy and databases

This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 guideline. The review protocol was not registered in any public database. The systematic search was conducted on February 1, 2025, across three databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science; additionally, two searches were performed in Google Scholar, one in English and the other in Farsi, on the same date. The search strategies in all databases are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they: (1) were peer-reviewed studies with randomized clinical trial, cohort, test-negative case-control, or case-population design (2) were conducted among the Iranian population, (3) defined COVID-19 case based on nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) including reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), (4) reported outcomes on COVID-19 infection, hospital admission, or death, (5) were published in English or Farsi. Studies were excluded if they were not designed to estimate the efficacy or effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines.

Study selection and data extraction processes

The initial search results were deduplicated using EndNote (version 21.3) software. The remaining articles were transferred to Rayyan software and screened based on predefined eligibility criteria by two independent reviewers (S.M.M. and S.Y.) for titles and abstracts. The full texts of all eligible articles were retrieved and screened by two reviewers. In cases of disagreement, the opinion of a senior reviewer (A.F.) was sought to resolve the conflict. The results of eligible articles were extracted into a Google Sheet. For each article, the following information was extracted: article title, first author names, study location, study design, study period, sample size, data sources, vaccines studied, outcomes studied, information on dominant variants in the study, inclusion and exclusion criteria, definitions of terms, and efficacy/effectiveness results along with raw data for each studied outcome, as detailed below. Given that the effect of vaccines emerges 14 days after each dose of vaccination [18], in studies that reported vaccine effect over different time periods, the first 14 days after each dose were not considered.

Outcome measures

The outcomes studied included the efficacy or effectiveness of the individual vaccine formulations or the combined vaccines in reducing COVID-19 infection, as diagnosed by RT-PCR test, hospital admission, and death, primarily within the first 40 days after a positive RT-PCR test for SARS-CoV-2. In this study, we meta-analyzed only the combined vaccine effectiveness from observational studies. The effectiveness or efficacy of individual vaccine formulations from observational or interventional studies, respectively, was not incorporated into the meta-analysis. Additionally, the number of RCTs available for different vaccines was not sufficient to perform a meaningful meta-analysis. We defined “combined” vaccine effectiveness as the reported effectiveness of all COVID-19 vaccines against outcomes of interest in the primary study, regardless of brand or formulation; thus, combined effectiveness includes both heterologous and homologous vaccination.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias in the included studies was assessed by utilizing the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies – of Interventions (ROBINS-I) [19] for observational studies. In addition, the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials version 2.0 (ROB2) was used for phase III randomized controlled trial (RCT) studies [20]. Plots were generated using the Robvis web-based tool [21]. Two authors (S.M.M. and S.Y.) independently evaluated the included studies for potential risk of bias.

Certainty of evidence

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was utilized to assess the certainty of the body of evidence for the meta-analyzed outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Using extracted raw data from the studies, the odds ratio (OR) and its standard error were calculated and log-transformed. Consequently, the inverse variance weighted meta-analysis was utilized to pool the ORs; vaccine effectiveness or efficacy was calculated as (1 - OR) * 100. Cochrane’s Q test and I2 statistic were used to estimate heterogeneity between the studies. A p-value of ≥ 0.1 and I2 ≥ 50% indicated significant heterogeneity, resulting in utilizing the random effects model with the DerSimonian and Laird approach to calculate τ2. All statistical analyses and forest plot visualizations were performed using R statistical software version 4.0.0 in RStudio version 2024.12.0.467 using the tidyverse, meta, and esc packages.

Results

Literature search and overview of the included studies

The initial database search identified 3,185 records, of which 751 were duplicates and were subsequently removed. Based on the title and abstract screening, 2391 records were excluded. The remaining 43 records, in addition to another record discovered through citation searching, underwent full-text screening, of which 31 were further excluded. Ultimately, thirteen studies were eligible for inclusion in the systematic review [22–34], and five observational studies were included in the meta-analysis [22, 24, 26, 28, 34] (Fig. 1).

Meta-analysis of the combined effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines

Infection

Only four studies reported vaccine effectiveness or efficacy against infection, three of which focused exclusively on a single vaccine formulation [23, 30, 31]. The study by Mirahmadizadeh et al. was the only study to report effectiveness against infection for multiple vaccines [22]. (Table 2) Therefore, pooling vaccine effectiveness against infection was not possible.

Hospital admission

Three studies reported an overall effectiveness against hospital admission and were included in the meta-analysis [22, 24, 26]. The heterogeneity between the studies was significant (I2 = 99.5% [95% CI: 99.2–99.6]; p < 0.0001), and the random effects model was performed. The overall vaccine effectiveness against hospital admission was 51.13% (21.77–69.47) (Fig. 2A).

legend: Forest plots indicating the meta-analysis of combined effectiveness of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines in preventing (A) Hospital admission and (B) Death

Death

Four studies provided an overall effectiveness against deaths [22, 26, 28, 34]; heterogeneity among the studies was significantly high (I2 = 99.3 [99.1–99.5]; p < 0.0001). The overall effectiveness against death, according to the random effects model, was 55.74% (−1.53–80.71) (Fig. 2B).

Risk of bias assessment

The included studies’ risk of bias assessment results are shown in Supplementary Tables 2–3 and Supplementary Figs. 1–2. All non-randomized observational studies had a moderate risk of bias, basically due to the inevitable confounding associated with their study designs. Moreover, the phase III RCTs were deemed to have a low risk of bias [30, 31].

Certainty of evidence

The GRADE assessment results for the meta-analyzed outcomes, including combined vaccine effectiveness against hospital admission and death, are provided in the Summary of Findings table in Supplementary Table 4. For both outcomes, the certainty of evidence was deemed very low.

Discussion

This systematic review evaluated the effectiveness and efficacy of COVID-19 vaccines for preventing SARS-CoV-2 infection, hospital admission, and death in Iran. The meta-analyses included only observational studies reporting the effectiveness of combined vaccines.

Due to the insufficient number of studies, it was not possible to pool the vaccine effectiveness against COVID-19 infection. Furthermore, two-dose COVID-19 vaccines achieved an effectiveness of 51% in preventing hospital admission and an effectiveness of 56% in reducing COVID-19-associated death. Regarding individual vaccines, the effectiveness and efficacy varied substantially across the included studies. Against infection, retrospective cohort studies reported higher effectiveness than the efficacy reported in RCTs for two doses. In preventing hospital admission, one RCT and two retrospective cohort studies reported similar effects, while one test-negative case–control study reported substantially lower effectiveness, all for two doses. Against death, all studies reported nearly perfect vaccine effectiveness for two doses, except for one test-negative case–control study, which reported effectiveness values ranging from 4% to more than 90% for different vaccines.

According to our findings, RCTs showed less effect on reducing infection rates than observational studies, but they demonstrated a similar effect on reducing hospital admissions. RCTs are designed to assess the efficacy of an intervention; in these studies, the intervention is precisely timed and administered to a homogenous group of individuals, with all other variables controlled and high compliance maintained. Bias is minimized, and blinding is employed to maximize internal validity. As such, RCTs are considered the gold standard for evaluating the impact of an intervention. However, while RCTs are a necessary first step in assessing the efficacy of a vaccine, they are not sufficient on their own. External validity, or the generalizability of the study to the broader population, must also be addressed [35]. Pragmatic studies are conducted on a wide range of individuals to assess the real-world impact of an intervention. Although these studies may have lower internal validity or reliability of results, it is crucial to strike a balance between internal and external validity, as neither alone is sufficient [35, 36].

Moreover, the conduction time of the included studies is a key factor contributing to the differences in their results. RCTs were conducted before the commencement of mass vaccination campaigns, whereas observational studies were performed after mass vaccination had begun. In the RCTs, the vaccine efficacy was measured against the vaccine-referenced strains; However, in observational studies, the time lapse between vaccine development and study execution might have been sufficient for new variants to emerge. Research has shown that vaccine efficacy decreases with the emergence of new COVID-19 variants [37, 38]. With the reduction in antibody neutralization against these variants, vaccine breakthrough cases increase in frequency, resulting in a decrease in vaccine effectiveness [39]. A meta-analysis of 16 European studies demonstrated that the overall vaccine effectiveness against infection with any of the original strain, Alpha, and Delta variants was 77.0% (70.4 to 82.1), which then decreased to 26.1% (21.1 to 30.8) against Omicron infection [40]. Also, it has been indicated that the effect of vaccines against SARS-CoV-2 infection diminishes with time following immunization [39–44], suggesting that the efficacy reported in the RCTs is higher compared to the effectiveness in observational studies.

Another important consideration is the issue of natural immunity in the population prior to vaccination, as previous COVID-19 infections lead to partial immunity in individuals [45, 46]. A comprehensive American study using the N3C database, which analyzed data from 10 million individuals, revealed that over one-fifth of the population had contracted SARS-CoV-2 before receiving their first dose of the COVID-19 vaccine. The infection rate for those who had not previously contracted COVID-19, regardless of vaccination status, was 20%. However, for individuals with natural immunity who had not received the vaccine, the reinfection rate dropped to 9%. The lowest reinfection rate, approximately 7%, was observed in those with hybrid immunity, acquired from both prior infection and vaccination. These findings highlight the variable vaccine effectiveness based on an individual’s immune history [47]. Because RCTs were conducted earlier than observational studies, the rate of previous natural immunity was lower among participants in RCTs compared to those in observational studies. Consequently, in observational studies, both vaccinated and unvaccinated groups possessed a higher level of natural immunity, which may have resulted in the underestimation of vaccine effectiveness in these studies.

Due to the limited and delayed access to COVID-19 vaccines in Iran, healthcare workers (owing to their high exposure to the disease) were prioritized for vaccination. Following them, high-risk groups, including the elderly and individuals with underlying health conditions (due to their higher risk of severe illness), were next in line to receive the vaccine [48]. Thus, in early studies conducted in Iran, vaccinated individuals are expected to belong to these two groups primarily. Healthcare workers and high-risk individuals are expected to demonstrate enhanced vaccine efficacy relative to the general population. However, this expectation is subject to ongoing controversy and debate. One reason for this controversy is that high-risk individual groups encompass diverse populations, such as older adults, immunocompromised individuals, and those with underlying diseases with varying immune responses and specific vulnerabilities among different subgroups [49–52]. In addition, variations in follow-up duration, intervals between vaccine doses, differing policies for testing vaccinated and unvaccinated individuals, and care-seeking behavior can all influence the estimated vaccine effectiveness [53, 54].

The performance of Iran’s vaccination program in preventing COVID-19 infections was comparable to that observed in global studies. Our study demonstrated effectiveness rates ranging from 64% to 87% for two doses of various vaccines; however, these estimates were based on only two studies. A meta-analysis of 16 European observational studies estimated vaccine effectiveness at 71% (62–77) against infection for the primary series [40]. Additionally, a rapid living systematic review and meta-analysis covering over 23 countries, most of which were observational studies, reported an effectiveness of 83% (80–86) in the 14–42 days after receiving the primary series against infection [44]. Both analyses demonstrated a decline in effectiveness over time. Conversely, our study highlights the comparatively poor performance of Iran’s vaccination regimen in reducing hospitalization and mortality, with estimated pooled effectiveness rates of 51% and 56%, respectively. Globally, the corresponding rates in the 14–42 days following completion of the primary series were 92% (88–94) and 91% (85–95), respectively [44]. Similarly, in Europe, the primary series of COVID-19 vaccines was reported to be 88% (84–91) effective in reducing hospitalization, ICU admission, or death [40].

Our results suggest that case-control studies are not appropriate to examine vaccine effectiveness, as these studies might underestimate the effectiveness. In case-control studies, the cases are derived from those seeking medical care, thus introducing a bias related to individuals’ behavior. Vaccinated individuals tend to visit healthcare centers with less severe symptoms and are identified with the disease earlier and in milder stages compared to unvaccinated individuals. This bias is exacerbated in diseases like COVID-19, which present with non-specific symptoms [55]. To mitigate the influence of care-seeking behavior, the test-negative case-control design has been proposed, where both cases and controls are hospitalized individuals, differentiated by laboratory test results such as PCR. In this design, both groups include individuals whose disease severity warranted hospitalization, with controls being those whose illness, despite similar symptoms and severity, tested negative for COVID-19. The application of this study method aids in mitigating care-seeking bias. Nevertheless, it is crucial to acknowledge that even within test-negative design studies, care-seeking behavior is not entirely neutralized, rendering these studies susceptible to this bias [55, 56]. To fully eliminate this bias, cohort and RCT studies are more appropriate, as demonstrated in this systematic review. The combined COVID-19 vaccines had an effectiveness of 51.13% (21.77–69.47) against hospital admission. Among the three studies analyzed, two [24, 26] utilized the negative test design and reported lower effectiveness compared to the third study [22], which was a retrospective cohort. Additionally, the effectiveness against death was estimated to be 55.74% (−1.53–80.71), after pooling four studies, among which the study by Heidarzadeh et al. [26] was the only study employing the test-negative design and reported the lowest effectiveness.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths and limitations that warrant discussion. In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we evaluated the impact of COVID-19 vaccines in preventing COVID-19 infection, hospital admission, and death. To our knowledge, this is the only systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating the COVID-19 vaccination system in Iran. Most systematic reviews assessing the impact of COVID-19 vaccines did not have geographical limitations. By limiting our review to Iranian studies, we enabled a focused evaluation of Iran’s vaccination performance without the confounding effects of variations in vaccine effectiveness and efficacy across different countries. Various studies employed different indices, such as risk ratio (RR), hazard ratio (HR), incidence density ratio (IDR), and OR. To avoid errors arising from conversions and the inconsistent use of these indices, we calculated the OR for all studies and ultimately pooled all ORs. Additionally, recognizing that incomplete or complete vaccination significantly affects vaccine effectiveness and efficacy, we only included data related to complete vaccination in our meta-analysis. This approach ensures that our results are not skewed by variations in the number of vaccine doses administered. Another strength of this study is the absence of restrictions regarding the type of vaccine, variant, or population. We included any study that provided information on the performance of COVID-19 vaccines in Iran and reported results by segregating these variables as much as possible.

It is noteworthy that this study also has limitations. One of the most significant limitations is that due to the small number of included studies, we could not perform meta-regression to assess the impact of time and dominant variants in the studies, and we could only qualitatively separate our results based on the study periods. Furthermore, as the certainty of evidence was very low for the meta-analyzed outcomes, there is little confidence in the effect estimate. Another important limitation of our meta-analysis is that we could not include adjusted OR or adjusted HR values in the analysis since we calculated the OR based on the raw data reported in all studies. Consequently, the effect of various factors, such as age, gender, and other variables, were not considered in our meta-analysis. Additionally, this systematic review and meta-analysis inherited the limitations of the included studies; since there was no specific program for the sequence and diversity of different vaccine platforms in Iran, evaluating the impact of different vaccine regimens was not feasible.

Conclusion

Results from different studies varied considerably, with patterns observed across both study designs and vaccine types. The meta-analysis indicated that combined COVID-19 vaccines were approximately 55% effective against death and 50% effective against hospitalization. Against infection, studies reported effectiveness ranging from 60% to 90% for different vaccines. COVID-19 vaccine efficacies were sparsely reported for individual vaccines but were approximately 100% against death, 80% against hospitalization, and 50% against infection. Our study concluded that COVID-19 vaccines performed better against more severe outcomes. However, caution is warranted when summarizing Iran’s COVID-19 vaccination program, as the limited number of real-world vaccine effectiveness studies conducted during and after vaccination campaigns prevents policymakers from being fully confident about all aspects of vaccination. This underscores the need for better preparedness for future public health emergencies.
