Donor type outcomes in setting of PTCy: more apparent than real?
Authors: Joseph A Pidala, Ravi Varadhan, Richard J Jones
Published: 2026

In a previous issue of Blood Advances, Modi et al1 presented an analysis examining the impact of donor type in the setting of posttransplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy)–based graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis. The authors conclude that when a matched unrelated donor (MUD) is available, it should be prioritized over a related haploidentical donor (haplo). In the current era, nearly every patient in need has a donor for hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT).2 Although factors including search prognosis (informed by patient race/ethnicity and haplotype frequency) determine which donors are possible,3 a relevant question for a subset of patients is the choice between a MUD and a haplo.

As prospective randomized trials of MUDs vs haplos are lacking, observational data have been leveraged to examine comparative outcomes. Earlier comparisons examined bundled approaches (PTCy/bone marrow grafts/haplo vs calcineurin inhibitor and methotrexate/peripheral blood [PB]/MUD).4 Acute and chronic GVHD were reduced with PTCy/haplo, while survival was similar. However, with increased evidence of the effectiveness of PTCy in MUDs,5 more direct comparisons became possible. A prior smaller study compared MUD and haplo PTCy–based outcomes, and demonstrated that MUDs had reduced severe acute GVHD and chronic GVHD for both reduced-intensity (RIC) and myeloablative (MAC) conditioning, and superior overall survival (OS; RIC only).6 However, a follow-up analysis including propensity score matching challenged these results.7

The current study from Modi et al aimed to resolve this controversy primarily through study of a larger patient population and statistical methods to adjust for baseline discrepancies between groups. It included 1973 patients receiving HCT from MUD and 3900 from haplo for acute leukemia or myelodysplastic syndrome from 2017 to 2021. OS, GVHD-free, and relapse-free survival (GRFS) were examined as primary outcomes. Sensitivity analyses for OS and GRFS used propensity score weights by the inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) method. Notable differences between groups included older patient age (median, 62.1 vs 58.8), younger donor age (median, 27.5 [range, 18-60.9] vs 35.7 [range, 18-74.1]), and greater use or PB (93% vs 81.5%) in MUDs. The MUD cohort was mostly non-Hispanic White (86.4%) compared to 59% for haplo.

With a median follow-up of 36.3 months, 3-year OS was 59.2% and 54.9% for MUD vs haplo, and multivariable analysis demonstrated worsened overall mortality for the haplo group (hazard ratio [HR], 1.15; P = .005). Several other factors, including donor age, were associated with increased overall mortality. The 3-year GRFS rates were 42.4% and 35.6% for MUD vs haplo, (HR, 1.19; P < .001). On sensitivity analysis using IPTW adjustment, haplo continued to have worse OS (HR, 1.10; P = .025) and GRFS (HR, 1.13; P < .001). In the analysis of secondary outcomes, haplo (vs MUD) had higher graft failure (HR, 1.67; P = .002), grade 3 to 4 acute GVHD by 6 months (HR, 1.28; P = .039), moderate/severe chronic GVHD by 24 months (HR, 1.47; P < .001), and non-relapse mortality (NRM) (HR, 1.34; P < .001). Importantly, in a subgroup with donors aged <30 years (64.9% of MUDs vs 31.2% of haplos), OS was not significantly different, with worse GRFS and NRM but less relapses in haplos. Moreover, the prior Center for International Blood and Marrow Transplant Research (CIBMTR) analysis found that MUDs were only superior in the setting of RIC conditioning, but in the current analysis improved survival was only seen in the MAC setting.

This analysis is noteworthy in the size of the study population, rigorous approach including statistical methods to address imbalance in baseline factors, and detailed coverage of post-HCT outcomes. Open questions pertain to interpretation of these results, and ultimate application to clinical care. For interpretation, there are clear imbalances in the baseline demographics of MUD vs haplo groups, reflecting expected differences under usual practices (eg, use of younger donors in MUD, patient race/ethnicity imbalance for MUD vs haplo). The IPTW propensity score adjustment minimized the differences between MUD and haplo groups, but did not completely eliminate them, with some factors showing residual non-negligible differences for OS and GRFS outcomes. As well, analysis limited to those with donors aged <30 years (a comparison that equalizes the donor age range, but limits to only 31% of the haplo cohort in this study) showed no significant difference in 3-year OS. Finally, multiple subgroup analyses failed to achieve a consistent conclusion when considering different variables and outcomes, and the limitation of survival benefit to MAC contradicts the prior CIBMTR analysis.6 Further work is needed to determine why analysis of 2 partially overlapping CIBMTR cohorts (Gooptu: 2011-2018, Modi: 2017-2021) reached discordant results.

Unfortunately, statistical approaches cannot fully overcome major imbalances between retrospective cohorts. A fundamental requirement for valid IPTW estimation is the positivity assumption, that is all patients must have a non-negligible chance of receiving either donor type. However, as seen in this cohort, many patients (particularly those of non-European ancestry) have a low probability of obtaining MUDs. The study does not report propensity score overlaps or trimming procedures to restrict inference to the region of common support. Without this, the IPTW-adjusted estimates may be susceptible to extrapolation and hence lack clinical interpretability for a major subset of patients (ie, those with non-European ancestry).

Regarding application to clinical care, by definition these registry data speak to comparative outcomes of patients who successfully reached HCT. Emerging data from the BMT CTN 1702 trial demonstrates that a search prognosis–informed donor search and selection strategy (leveraging early alternative donors—including haplo, mismatched unrelated, and umbilical cord blood—for those with low probability of MUD) can achieve similar rates of reaching HCT, OS, and post-HCT outcomes for all patients.8 Secondly, the study from Modi et al draws attention to the fact that disease relapse remains a fundamental barrier to HCT success (primary cause of death in both MUDs and haplos). Although relevant interventions to optimize cure are diverse, rapid delivery of HCT, preferably with a young donor where OS was similar between MUDs and haplos, is likely more important than whether the donor is related or unrelated, matched, or mismatched.
