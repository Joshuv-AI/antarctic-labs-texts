Astrocyte-induced dynamics of a pyramidal cell with a dendrite-connected astrocyte
Authors: Lenka Přibylová, Jan Ševčík, Anastasia Egorova, Štěpán Husa, Lucia Kajanová, Eva Kopřivová, Lucie Alexandra Mega, Veronika Eclerová
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Previous multi-parameter bifurcation analyses of the Pinsky–Rinzel neuron model have elucidated a mechanistic explanation for the complex interplay between the membrane potentials of CA3 pyramidal cells and their intracellular dendritic calcium levels. By coupling this neuron model with the Li–Rinzel-type model of astrocytic Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2+}$$\end{document} dynamics, we demonstrate how astrocytic calcium signaling dynamically modulates neuronal activity. We present a classification of potential dynamical transients, including transitions to epileptiform activity. Furthermore, we identify a bidirectional role of astrocytes where they may not only facilitate the emergence of high-frequency oscillations associated with epileptiform activity but may also contribute to their attenuation. Additionally, we propose a mechanism that prolongs the bursting duration of pyramidal cells, which may be associated with synaptic plasticity. Finally, we validate our modeling framework by replicating experimental paradigms that link astrocytic Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2+}$$\end{document} dynamics with neuronal hyperexcitability, demonstrating that astrocytes may drive neurons toward the seizure threshold. These findings enhance our understanding of integrated neural circuit dynamics, particularly the role of neuron–astrocyte interactions in modulating bursting behavior, neural signaling, and their potential contribution to both the generation and suppression of epileptiform ripples.

Introduction

Astrocytes play diverse and crucial roles in brain function, including the regulation of brain tissue homeostasis, providing neurons with essential nutrients, controlling blood flow, mediating neurovascular coupling, maintaining extracellular levels of neurotransmitters, and contributing to neuronal communication and signaling (Agulhon et al., 2008; Blackburn et al., 2009; Hansson & Rönnbäck, 2003; Haydon, 2001; Parpura et al., 1994). There is also increasing evidence that astrocyte dysfunction is associated with various brain disorders, such as epilepsy and Alzheimer’s disease (Binder & Steinhäuser, 2021; Blackburn et al., 2009; Losi et al., 2012; Maragakis & Rothstein, 2006; Seifert et al., 2010). More recently, cognitive experiments conducted by Lee et al. (2014) demonstrated that blocking astrocytic vesicular release in mice led to behavioral abnormalities and significant memory deficits. Notably, their study highlighted the relationship between gliotransmission-dependent gamma oscillations and cognitive functions and behavior.

An increasing body of evidence (Gómez-Gonzalo et al., 2010; Heuser et al., 2018; Perea & Araque, 2007; Sasaki et al., 2014; Vezzani et al., 2022) indicates that astrocytes dynamically modulate neuronal activity and contribute substantially to the complexity and emergent dynamics of neural networks. Nadkarni and Jung (2003, 2005) were the first to describe and explain a possible mechanism for the onset of epilepsy — characterized by spontaneous seizure-like spiking of neurons in the absence of external stimuli — through a physiological model incorporating neuron–astrocyte interactions. Using experimental data, they demonstrated that an increased production rate of 1,4,5-inositol trisphosphate (IP) within astrocytes can lead to neuronal hyperexcitation. Their findings were based on simulations using the Li–Rinzel-type model of astrocytic Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2+}$$\end{document} dynamics coupled to pyramidal neurons modeled by either the Hodgkin–Huxley or Pinsky–Rinzel framework, with qualitatively similar results. Subsequent studies confirmed these findings, including Ji et al. (2023), who used a Hodgkin–Huxley neuron coupled to the Li–Rinzel-type astrocyte model. Moreover, Gordleeva et al. (2021) recently developed a biologically plausible computational model of working memory, implemented through a spiking neuron network interacting with a network of astrocytes. While their model is based on synaptically coupled Izhikevich neurons (a conceptual model) and a network of Li–Rinzel-type astrocytes (a physiological model), their results demonstrated that slow–fast dynamics in interconnected networks allow for the construction of neuron–astrocyte network models capable of memory formation, including information loading, storage, and retrieval, based on burst emergence.

A defining characteristic of CA3 pyramidal neurons is their participation in theta (4–10 Hz) and gamma (30–80 Hz) oscillations, which are essential for cognitive functions such as memory encoding, retrieval, and spatial navigation (Buzsáki, 2006; Nuñez & Buño, 2021). Theta oscillations, which are prominent in the hippocampus, are thought to coordinate neuronal activity during episodic memory formation and spatial navigation (Buzsáki, 2006). Gamma oscillations, often nested within theta cycles, facilitate synaptic plasticity and information transfer across hippocampal circuits, supporting processes such as working memory and decision-making (Buzsáki & Wang, 2012). These rhythmic dynamics underscore the critical role of CA3 in organizing the neural computations necessary for cognition. In epileptiform conditions, abnormal synchronization of neuronal populations within the hippocampus can disrupt these oscillatory dynamics, leading to pathological activity associated with seizures and cognitive impairments (Bragin et al., 1999; Jefferys et al., 2012).

The Pinsky–Rinzel model (Pinsky & Rinzel, 1994) was specifically developed to capture the characteristic bursting behavior observed in hippocampal pyramidal cells, which had already been modeled by Traub et al. (1991). From a mathematical perspective, the Pinsky–Rinzel model incorporates two distinct compartments and exhibits an inherent separation of timescales, with slower dendritic and faster somatic dynamics. This naturally classifies it as a slow–fast system, providing a rich ground for the emergence of nonlinear phenomena, including chaos. These phenomena can be systematically analyzed using geometric methods for slow–fast systems (Desroches et al., 2022). In the model studied in this paper, a second slow variable emerges through interaction with astrocytic dynamics, further enriching the system’s structure. The mechanism of chaos generation in systems with more than one slow variable has been shown to arise generically when the bursting manifold approaches homoclinic trajectories and resting states (Scully et al., 2025).

In our recent study (Přibylová et al., 2025), we showed that the original Pinsky–Rinzel model of a CA3 pyramidal cell (PC) can exhibit chaotic bursting even with a single slow variable, and we systematically analyzed this behavior using bifurcation techniques. We demonstrated how the interplay between slow (intracellular dendritic calcium) and fast (membrane potential) dynamics gives rise to the random-like emergence of complex spike bursts (CSBs) — a previously unexplained phenomenon that is likely closely related to key neuronal functions such as synaptic plasticity and memory encoding. Specifically, our bifurcation analysis revealed that torus canard trajectories (Vo, 2017) emerge in the vicinity of a cusp point on the limit-cycle manifold and a Neimark–Sacker bifurcation. These canards play a crucial role in shaping the PC’s spiking regimes, including the characteristic theta–gamma coupling observed on a chaotic attractor, which features two distinct bursting patterns (with and without CSBs). Consequently, these two patterns can emerge randomly, as demonstrated in our in silico replication of the in vitro experiment described in Raus Balind et al. (2019). Other important dynamical patterns include epileptiform ripples above 100 Hz, fast ripples exceeding 250 Hz, and amplitude-modulated ripples on a 2-torus (regular bursting). Acting as a slow modulator on a secondary slow timescale, the astrocyte induces transitions between these PC regimes and can also generate transient dynamics.

From the perspective of nonlinear dynamics, regions characterized by stable limit cycles are particularly relevant for the emergence of synchronized oscillations of coupled neurons. In such regimes, the system can support in-phase as well as anti-phase (or other phase-shift) synchronization between individual neurons or their subpopulations. Such synchronization has been proposed as a plausible mechanism for the generation of high-frequency oscillations (HFOs) and very high-frequency oscillations (VHFOs), which are increasingly recognized as potential biomarkers of epileptogenic zones (Brázdil et al., 2017; Brazdil et al., 2023; Cimbalnik et al., 2018, 2020; Jacobs et al., 2012; Jiruska et al., 2017, 2013; Pail et al., 2020; Řehulka et al., 2019; Staba & Bragin, 2011; Travnicek et al., 2021; Vasickova et al., 2022; Worrell & Gotman, 2011; Zijlmans et al., 2009, 2012). Our previous work has demonstrated that weakly coupled neuronal oscillators may exhibit phase-shift synchronization in the absence of strong coupling forces, giving rise to HFOs and VHFOs in biologically plausible frequency ranges (Přibylová et al., 2024; Ševčík & Přibylová, 2025; Záthurecký et al., 2025). These findings support the hypothesis that regions in parameter space supporting limit cycles provide a dynamical substrate for epileptiform biomarkers rooted in synchronization phenomena.

Notably, coupling strength critically shapes the nature of synchronization. While strong coupling tends to promote in-phase synchronization, weak coupling allows for anti-phase or more complex phase-shift relationships (Ševčík & Přibylová, 2025; Záthurecký et al., 2025). This distinction may have important implications for understanding oscillatory signatures in epileptic networks. For instance, pathological tissue often exhibits reactive astrogliosis, which can facilitate enhanced extracellular coupling and glia-mediated modulation of neuronal activity. Under such conditions, elevated network input — potentiated by astrocytic support — may promote tight in-phase synchronization, manifesting as fast ripples during epileptic states. Conversely, in resting or low-input conditions, networks with weak coupling can support less constrained synchronization regimes, such as anti-phase or phase-shifted dynamics, which may give rise to VHFOs detectable even in the absence of seizures.

The objective of this study is to investigate neuron–astrocyte interactions as a comprehensive mechanism underlying both physiological and pathological neural dynamics. In contrast to previous studies (Ji et al., 2023; Nadkarni & Jung, 2003, 2005), we employ a more physiologically realistic approach by coupling astrocytic activity to dendrites rather than the soma. Furthermore, our methodology builds on prior rigorous bifurcation analyses (Přibylová et al., 2025) of the Pinsky–Rinzel model, which demonstrated its robustness in describing complex neuronal dynamics, including epileptiform activity. To validate the proposed framework, we perform a series of in silico replications of key experimental paradigms, including the simultaneous patch-clamp and Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2+}$$\end{document} imaging experiments of Gómez-Gonzalo et al. (2010) and the population-level Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2+}$$\end{document} measurements of Trevelyan et al. (2006). By integrating these simulations with the underlying bifurcation structure, we aim to provide mechanistic insight into how astrocytic feedback can modulate neuronal excitability and potentially drive the transition from physiological synchronization to epileptiform activity.

Methods

Model

To investigate the interactions between neurons and astrocytes, we employ a coupled neuron–astrocyte model similar to the approach by Nadkarni and Jung (2003, 2005). The neuronal component is represented by the Pinsky–Rinzel model (Atherton et al., 2016; Pinsky & Rinzel, 1994), a well-established two-compartment framework for simulating the dynamics of hippocampal PCs. The somatic compartment includes transient sodium (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{Na}$$\end{document}), delayed rectifier potassium (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{K}$$\end{document}), and leak currents, while the dendritic compartment features persistent calcium (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{Ca}$$\end{document}), calcium-activated potassium (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{KCa}$$\end{document}), after-hyperpolarization potassium (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{KAHP}$$\end{document}), and leak currents. These compartments are coupled through a coupling current, allowing the representation of neuronal behaviors such as tonic spiking and bursting. The external current to the PC is assumed to be proportional to the areas of the soma and dendrite. To facilitate rigorous bifurcation analysis using continuation software such as MatCont (Dhooge et al., 2003, 2008), we employ a slightly modified version of the Pinsky–Rinzel model, which we thoroughly analyzed in Přibylová et al. (2025). Specifically, we replaced the non-smooth Heaviside and related functions with their smooth approximations. Additionally, we refined the modeling of the calcium-activated potassium current (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{KCa}$$\end{document}) by introducing a smooth function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi (Ca_{pc})$$\end{document} that more accurately captures its dependence on intracellular calcium concentration. This approximation improves numerical stability in the continuation of periodic solutions and allows for an extended bifurcation analysis, including two-parameter continuations relevant to bursting.

The astrocytic component is modeled using the Li–Rinzel-type framework (Li & Rinzel, 1994), which captures the dynamics of intracellular calcium oscillations driven by IP-mediated calcium release from internal stores. This model accounts for the interactions between IP concentration, calcium release, and the fraction of activated IP receptors, providing a robust description of astrocytic calcium signaling. While the model captures the essential calcium-dependent astrocytic feedback, it does not include other physiological processes such as K\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{+}$$\end{document} buffering, glutamate uptake, or metabolic coupling, which may further modulate neuronal excitability and recovery dynamics.

To simulate neuron–astrocyte interactions, we deliberately couple the Li–Rinzel-type astrocyte model with the dendritic compartment of the Pinsky–Rinzel neuron model instead of the somatic compartment, in contrast to previous studies (Ji et al., 2023; Nadkarni & Jung, 2003, 2005) that have examined coupling to the soma compartment or to a single-compartment model of PCs. This approach aligns with the methodology of Pinsky and Rinzel (1994), who simplified Traub’s 19-compartmental model (Traub et al., 1991) into a two-compartmental representation by segregating ionic currents as described above: fast-spiking currents (sodium and delayed rectifier potassium) were assigned to the soma-like compartment, while calcium and calcium-modulated currents were allocated to the dendrite-like compartment. By coupling the astrocyte to the dendritic compartment, we adhere to this physiological segregation, enabling a more accurate study of neuron–astrocyte interactions. This configuration allows us to investigate the bidirectional influence of astrocytic calcium dynamics on neuronal excitability and firing patterns, as well as the effects of neuronal activity on astrocytic responses. This dendritic coupling configuration provides a more physiologically relevant model of neuron–astrocyte connections (Bohmbach et al., 2022; Matos et al., 2018).

The model Eqs. (1)–(8), along with the physiological parameters used in this study, are detailed in Appendix A and Tables 1, 2, respectively. We rely on our previous results (Přibylová et al., 2025), which identify parameter regimes associated with distinct neuronal firing patterns, including tonic spiking, various types of bursting, the onset of oscillatory epileptiform activity, and a regime of chaotic dynamics relevant to cognitive processes involving theta–gamma coupling.

Methods used for analyses

Numerical simulations and spectral analysis

Numerical simulations of the system dynamics were performed in MATLAB R2023b (The MathWorks, 2023) using the built-in solvers ode45 and, in the case of pulse inputs, ode15s. Both solvers employ variable-step Runge–Kutta methods suitable for stiff and non-stiff systems, respectively. To analyze the frequency content of model trajectories, we compute periodograms to identify dominant frequencies present in the system’s activity under different parameter regimes. Furthermore, we compute spectrograms using the plomb function, based on the Lomb–Scargle periodogram adapted for non-uniformly sampled signals. Spectrograms are calculated with a moving time window, where the window width and step size are predefined and independent.

Bifurcation analysis

We analyze bifurcations in a coupled neuron–astrocyte model using MatCont version 7p5 (Dhooge et al., 2003, 2008), a MATLAB-based toolbox for continuation and bifurcation analysis. Our approach integrates known bifurcation properties of the astrocyte subsystem (see Appendix B or Bashkirtseva and Ryashko (2022); Ryashko et al. (2022)) and the neuronal subsystem (see Přibylová et al. (2025)), complemented and interpreted through numerical simulations of the full model. In particular, we focus on codimension-1 bifurcations relevant to the system’s dynamics, including Hopf, fold, limit point of cycles, period-doubling of cycles, and Neimark–Sacker bifurcations (Guckenheimer & Holmes, 2013; Izhikevich, 2007; Kuznetsov, 1998; Wiggins, 2003). Bifurcation curves in parameter space are computed via numerical continuation, using a predictor–corrector approach based on Newton-type methods, as implemented in MatCont.

Variations in intracellular calcium levels thus provide a mechanistic link between the bifurcation structure of the model and the modulatory effects of external or glial inputs.

In regions ⑤ and ⑦, two frequency bands remain present due to the slow–fast nature of rapid membrane potential fluctuations (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_s$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_d$$\end{document}) and the slower intracellular dendritic calcium dynamics (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Ca_{pc}$$\end{document}), therefore it stays in physiological theta–gamma bands.

Bursting behavior: Defined by the presence of consecutive minima and maxima in the signal envelope. Bursting regions correspond to region ③ (regular bursting on a torus with two distinct dominant frequencies above theta–gamma bands), as well as regions ⑤ and ⑦ (exhibiting irregular bursting with chaotic attractors) in Fig. 1. The average bursting frequency decreases as the attractor approaches the vicinity of the blue Fold curve (Fold or Limit Point bifurcation curve) in regions ⑤ and ⑦. This behavior is analogous to the standard saddle-node on an invariant circle (SNIC) bifurcation (Izhikevich, 2007), where the tonic spiking period tends to infinity as the system approaches the saddle-node. However, in this case, the bursting persists, and the interburst interval progressively increases. In regions ⑤ and ⑦, two frequency bands remain present due to the slow–fast nature of rapid membrane potential fluctuations (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_s$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_d$$\end{document}) and the slower intracellular dendritic calcium dynamics (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Ca_{pc}$$\end{document}), therefore it stays in physiological theta–gamma bands.

These observations also point to the importance of understanding how astrocytes modulate neuronal dynamics, particularly through their interactions with pyramidal cell dendrites. Astrocytes can influence membrane excitability by releasing gliotransmitters or modulating extracellular ion concentrations, effectively delivering current-like input to dendritic compartments. Investigating the dynamics of this astrocyte–neuron coupling, especially in relation to dendritic ion fluxes, is therefore essential for clarifying how glial mechanisms contribute to synchronization phenomena and the emergence of pathological high-frequency oscillations.

Classification of astrocyte-induced transients

Astrocytes function as external current sources for PC by releasing calcium ions from intracellular stores, primarily the endoplasmic reticulum, through calcium-dependent signaling mechanisms. The external input current from the astrocyte to the PC is modeled as a function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{ast}$$\end{document}, which is solely determined by astrocytic calcium concentration (Nadkarni & Jung, 2005; Parpura & Haydon, 2000). Depending on whether calcium oscillates or stabilizes at an equilibrium state, we observe three distinct dynamical regimes (see Appendix, Fig. 11). Within the parameter range where only a simple equilibrium exists (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$IP < 0.31$$\end{document}), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{ast}$$\end{document} typically exhibits a transient pulse that subsequently decays to zero. In the region where a stable limit cycle is present, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{ast}$$\end{document} oscillates with a period exceeding 10 seconds. The equilibrium point at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$IP = 0.62$$\end{document} corresponds to a subcritical Hopf bifurcation, giving rise to a bistable regime where a stable equilibrium coexists with a stable limit cycle. As inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP) increases, the system remains on the oscillatory (limit-cycle) branch, and only after surpassing the limit point of cycles (LPC) at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$IP = 0.79$$\end{document} does it switch to the upper equilibrium state, characterized by an elevated level of calcium released by the astrocyte, as previously described by Nadkarni and Jung (2003, 2005).

The timescales of processes in astrocytes and PCs differ significantly. Astrocytes operate on the order of seconds, whereas PCs function on a millisecond scale. Thus, the biochemical processes in astrocytes are approximately 1000 times slower than the electrophysiological processes in PCs. To observe the influence of astrocytes in neuron–astrocyte interactions, it is necessary to monitor the associated cell’s behavior over a timescale of tens of seconds.

Astrocyte-modulated transitions between chaotic and periodic bursting regimes

Due to the level of input currents, regions ⑤ and ④ are important for applications. An important feature of region ⑤, discussed in Přibylová et al. (2025), is its division into two subregions, see Fig. 1, Figure 2. As input currents approach region ④, characterized by ripples, the chaotic attractor in region ⑤ loses its CSB activity. In a region with the CSBs, the dynamics include two burst types associated with high and low intracellular dendritic calcium levels (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Ca_{pc}$$\end{document}). CSBs are high-frequency bursts of action potentials (typically 2–6 spikes with frequencies above 100 Hz) observed in hippocampal pyramidal neurons (Frerking et al., 2005). These bursts play a significant role in synaptic transmission, enabling stronger and more reliable postsynaptic responses compared to isolated spikes. In particular, postsynaptic CSBs have been shown to facilitate the induction of long-term potentiation (LTP) at theta-frequency synaptic inputs, thus reinforcing synaptic weights and enhancing memory-related plasticity in CA1 region (Thomas et al., 1998). Within the CA3 recurrent network, CSBs may contribute to the formation of stable memory ensembles by reinforcing co-active neuronal groups through burst-driven synaptic plasticity (Schoenfeld et al., 2021). Furthermore, neuromodulatory control mechanisms, particularly cholinergic modulation, dynamically regulate CSB generation, with acetylcholine increasing dendritic plateau potentials and promoting bursting during memory encoding states (Kis et al., 2024). These findings suggest that CSBs are a critical component of associative memory formation in CA3, facilitating both network-level plasticity and neuromodulation-dependent switching between encoding and retrieval modes.

(2025), where we examine the attractor’s structure, its relation to the observed frequency bands, and the increase in frequency with higher input current in region ⑤. Theta and gamma oscillations, essential for cognitive functions such as memory encoding and synaptic integration, have been extensively studied in hippocampal circuits (Buzsáki, 2006; Buzsáki & Wang, 2012; Nuñez & Buño, 2021). Spectrograms computed for the astrocyte-triggered interval of 0–5.5 seconds highlight the coexistence of these frequency bands, emphasizing the role of astrocytic modulation in shaping chaotic neuronal dynamics in the working region of the neuron.

Astrocyte supporting pyramidal cell and prolonging burst duration

In this section, we build on the insights from Figs. 1 and 11 to configure model in Eqs. (1)–(8) with parameters from Tables 1 and 2 into a setting where the interaction between the astrocyte and the PC alters the PC’s responsiveness. In particular, we simulate a 2-second current impulse applied to the soma while exploring two different astrocyte parameter settings, leading to variations in the duration of bursting in the PC.

The main idea is to position the PC at a resting state with no external current applied, connected to an astrocyte with its natural IP equilibrium just below the lower Hopf bifurcation point, see Fig. 11. This model setting is physiological, as it easily achieves both astrocyte dynamical regimes — resting state and periodic oscillations — observed in both in vivo and in vitro experiments (Kuga et al., 2011; Ujita et al., 2016; Volterra et al., 2014). In the initial state, the dendritic activity of the PC is low and does not trigger IP activation in the astrocyte. The astrocyte remains at equilibrium with a low level of intracellular calcium, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Ca_{ast}$$\end{document}. An impulse is then introduced in the form of an input current to the soma, triggering bursting in the PC. As dendritic activity increases, glutamate is released into the synaptic cleft, activating astrocytic receptors. If the glutamate level is sufficiently high, IP production is initiated according to Eq. (6), thereby modulating astrocytic activity. In particular, the Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2+}$$\end{document} concentration in the astrocytic cytosol increases, resulting in a current to the dendrite. This current stimulates the PC into a spiking regime even after the initial impulse ends. This mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 7, where we compare two simulations: in the left column, the astrocytic response is insufficient to sustain prolonged spiking, while in the right column, astrocytic activation triggers a prolonged spiking episode.

More specifically, in the simulation presented in Fig. 7, the system is first allowed to evolve for 5 seconds under baseline conditions. Subsequently, a current pulse of amplitude \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I^s_{ext} = 2.5\,\mu \text {A}/\text {cm}^2$$\end{document} is applied to the soma for a duration of 2 seconds. After the stimulus ends at 7 seconds, it takes approximately 4 seconds for the astrocyte to return to the resting regime. This transition occurs rapidly, well before the astrocyte initiates another calcium release. Consequently, the astrocyte remains outside the oscillatory region since the equilibrium IP level drops below the Hopf bifurcation point (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$IP \approx 0.31$$\end{document}, see Fig. 11), preventing further calcium release until another sufficiently long stimulus is applied to the PC.

Thus, near the Hopf bifurcation point of the astrocyte, it can act as a mediator that prolongs the bursting behavior of the PC. Because the Hopf bifurcation is subcritical, a hysteresis effect is present, but the bistable region is extremely narrow or effectively absent. As a result, the astrocyte can prolong activity transiently but cannot sustain oscillations once the stimulus ends. If the input is too brief, the system fails to cross into the oscillatory domain and therefore cannot prolong the burst, returning rapidly to rest.

From a functional viewpoint, this subcritical but nearly monostable configuration allows transient hysteresis without persistent self-oscillation, supporting short-term prolongation of activity while ensuring eventual recovery. This behavior may represent a mechanism by which astrocytes link fast neuronal activity with slower biochemical processes underlying synaptic plasticity.

In contrast, under strong stimulation, when the intracellular IP level rises well above the first Hopf point, a second subcritical Hopf bifurcation appears, giving rise to a region of bistability and pronounced hysteresis between a stable equilibrium and a stable large-amplitude oscillation. In this overstimulated regime, the astrocyte can remain in the oscillatory state even after the stimulus is removed, exhibiting hysteresis-driven persistent activation.

Such high IP concentrations are unlikely to occur under normal physiological conditions, but they may arise in pathological or pharmacologically induced states associated with excessive neuronal discharges. Under these conditions, the astrocyte may become trapped in the upper branch of the equilibrium curve, maintaining elevated Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2+}$$\end{document} release even in the absence of further stimulation. This sustained Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2+}$$\end{document} release would generate a continuous outward ionic current and persistent gliotransmitter release, effectively providing prolonged excitatory drive to neighboring pyramidal neurons. Functionally, this behavior represents a potential transition from transient astrocytic support of neuronal synchronization to a self-sustained excitatory feedback loop capable of maintaining hypersynchronous activity. In this interpretation, the hysteretic regime corresponds to the onset of an astrocyte-driven mechanism that can promote or sustain epileptiform discharges, as analyzed in the following subsection.

Astrocytes are anatomically positioned to coordinate the activity of multiple neurons, as each astrocyte forms contacts with a large number of synapses within its domain (Giaume, 2010; Haydon, 2000; Min et al., 2012). Discussion and conclusion

In this study, we investigated the dynamics of CA3 pyramidal neurons coupled to astrocytic calcium signaling via dendritic compartments, expanding upon previous work that predominantly focused on somatic interactions (Ji et al., 2023; Nadkarni & Jung, 2003, 2005). Notably, dendritic coupling was shown to have a profound impact on neuronal excitability and bursting dynamics, highlighting its physiological relevance. By integrating known bifurcation properties of the astrocyte and neuronal subsystems, we classified astrocyte-induced neuronal transients into seven distinct regimes.

Astrocytes have emerged as efficient modulators of neuronal activity, capable of dynamically influencing neural excitability, synaptic transmission, and plasticity. Our findings support this role, particularly highlighting the astrocytic modulation of bursting behavior and theta–gamma oscillations. Such modulation is crucial for cognitive processes, including memory encoding, information processing, and decision making. The prolonged bursts mediated by astrocytic signaling could play a role in enhancing synaptic efficacy, thus facilitating synaptic plasticity and network-level learning processes.

However, astrocytic contribution does not act in isolation. The hippocampal neural circuitry involves complex interactions among multiple glial cell types, such as microglia and oligodendrocytes, each with distinct physiological roles (Henstridge et al., 2019; Salter & Stevens, 2017). Beyond glial modulation, interneurons play a critical regulatory role by providing inhibitory inputs that shape neuronal oscillations and synchrony. GABAergic interneurons specifically influence gamma oscillations, critical for cognitive functions and network stability, and modulate neuronal excitability to prevent pathological synchronization leading to epileptiform discharges (Buzsáki, 2006; Jefferys et al., 2012; Marafiga et al., 2021). Therefore, astrocytic modulation must be contextualized within a broader network perspective, recognizing the integral roles of interneurons and diverse glial cells in dynamically shaping neuronal behavior and maintaining circuit homeostasis.

Our findings indicate that both excitatory and inhibitory astrocyte-mediated mechanisms may influence neuronal excitability near the epileptic zone, potentially contributing to epileptiform discharges. Astrocytes play a dual role in these contexts: they can initiate or exacerbate epileptiform activity, yet also possess mechanisms that may attenuate or suppress seizures. Additionally, these astrocyte-mediated mechanisms may contribute to processes underlying astrogliosis (Binder & Steinhäuser, 2006; Vezzani et al., 2022).

Furthermore, we emphasize that, from a mathematical perspective, the oscillatory regime identified as region ④ is far more likely to give rise to synchronous (pathological) ripple and fast-ripple activity than regions characterized by other types of dynamics (such as bursting on a torus or chaotic oscillations), including phase-shift synchrony. This regime therefore represents a plausible substrate for locally generated field potentials in the VHFO and UFO frequency ranges, which have recently been proposed as biomarkers of the epileptogenic zone. Moreover, contemporary findings of Hao et al. (2021) have demonstrated that HFOs and VHFOs can exhibit strong phase-locking with slower oscillatory rhythms, such as theta and delta waves, in the hippocampal network during status epilepticus. In our previous work (Přibylová et al., 2025), we reported an intrinsic link between theta and gamma activity in a single pyramidal neuron, suggesting that such cross-frequency coupling may naturally emerge from the cell’s intrinsic dynamics. Extending this concept, the present model indicates that similar coupling mechanisms could occur across the neuron–astrocyte network, potentially supporting phase-shift synchronization between fast neuronal and slower astrocytic oscillations. This hypothesis warrants further investigation.

Our findings confirm and extend previous reports of astrocytic calcium bistability (Bashkirtseva & Ryashko, 2022; Ryashko et al., 2022) in the Li–Rinzel-type model, revealing its potential implications for astrocyte–neuron signaling dynamics. This bistability introduces the possibility of hysteresis effects, where a jump to an elevated level of astrocytic calcium concentration induced by excessively high levels of inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate, potentially resulting from pathological or external factors, is difficult to reverse. Such a mechanism might lead to persistent aberrant calcium signaling in astrocytes, potentially exacerbating pathological neuronal conditions such as sustained hyperexcitability or epileptiform activity (Pankratova et al., 2022). Understanding this phenomenon is critical, as it highlights a pathway by which astrocytic dysfunction could contribute to neurological disorders (Pankratova et al., 2022; Shigetomi et al., 2016).

Our in silico experiments confirm that ictal-like neuronal dynamics can emerge through neuron–astrocyte coupling, initiated either by a brief neuronal bursting, a transient excitatory input, or a short-term release from inhibition. The subsequent astrocytic feedback sustains and modulates this activity, producing a typical temporal pattern in both membrane potential and Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2+}$$\end{document} concentration that is characteristic of experimental recordings. The close correspondence between the simulated and experimentally observed dynamics suggests that this pattern is not coincidental, but rather emerges naturally from the underlying neuron–astrocyte feedback mechanism.

Despite the advances presented, our model simplifies several crucial aspects of astrocyte–neuron interactions. The Li–Rinzel-type astrocyte model, while valuable for studying calcium dynamics, does not fully incorporate critical astrocytic functions such as potassium buffering, glutamate uptake, gliotransmission, or metabolic neuron–glia coupling. While the model provides insights into astrocyte–neuron calcium dynamics, it does not encompass the full complexity of astrocytic involvement in epilepsy. Additionally, the current two-compartment model oversimplifies the dendritic structure, which in reality comprises a complex arborization capable of heterogeneous interactions with astrocytes. Furthermore, network-level interactions and feedback mechanisms involving interneurons and other glial cells are not explicitly considered, limiting the direct extrapolation of these results to in vivo contexts. Nevertheless, this simplification does not invalidate the core bifurcation-based mechanism underlying the astrocyte-induced modulation of neuronal bursting and the transition to ictal-like activity described here. Instead, they represent plausible extensions that could enhance the model’s biological realism in future work.

Future work should extend this model to encompass dendritic morphology, spatially realistic astrocytic processes, and diverse glial-neuronal interactions within larger neuronal networks. Incorporating explicit interneuronal feedback mechanisms, particularly GABAergic inhibition, would further enhance the physiological realism and predictive power of the model. Additionally, computational studies should explore the potential therapeutic modulation of astrocytic signaling pathways to mitigate epileptiform activity and promote synaptic plasticity. Experimentally validated neuron–glia network models will be crucial for elucidating the mechanistic basis of complex brain functions and identifying novel therapeutic targets for neurological diseases.
