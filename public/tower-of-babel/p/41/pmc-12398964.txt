Impact of COVID-19 and lockdown on outcomes of COPD patients in Turkey: lessons for the future
Authors: Demet Polat Yulug, Elif Sen, Aylin Ozgen Alpaydin, Sibel Nayci, Eylem Sercan Ozgur, Oya Baydar Toprak, Ahmet Melih Sahin, Elif Selcuk, Salih Yigit, Funda Elmas Uysal, Alev Gurgun, Nurdan Kokturk, Ayshan Mammadova, Yelda Varol, Ayşe Baha, Mehmet Polatli, Aybüke Yaz, Irem Serifoglu Rahatli, Ali Kocabas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic and related public health restrictions have substantially altered healthcare access and delivery, particularly for patients with chronic conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). This study aimed to evaluate the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and lockdown measures on COPD-related symptoms and hospital admissions, and to compare these outcomes with the pre-pandemic period.MethodsThis multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted between July 2021 and February 2022 across ten tertiary pulmonary outpatient clinics in Turkey. A total of 347 COPD patients were included. Data on demographics, spirometry, symptom progression, medication access, COVID-19 history, and hospital admissions were collected via structured questionnaire and medical records. Pandemic-related outcomes were compared with data from the pre-pandemic year (March 2019–March 2020). Additional multivariable regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of hospital admission and COVID-19-related hospitalization.ResultsThe mean number of hospital admissions significantly decreased during the pandemic compared to the pre-pandemic period (p < 0.001), while patient-reported respiratory symptoms increased over time. Hospital admissions were lowest during the first pandemic period, when restrictions were most intense. Regression analyses showed that lower FEV₁, advanced GOLD stage, and inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) use were independently associated with increased hospital admissions. COVID-19 was diagnosed in 21.1% of patients. Lower FEV₁, GOLD stage, and smoking were significantly associated with COVID-19-related hospitalization.ConclusionAlthough COPD-related hospital admissions decreased during the pandemic, respiratory symptoms progressively worsened. These findings reflect the complex interplay between public health measures, healthcare accessibility, and chronic disease management. Disease severity and airflow limitation were key determinants of adverse outcomes. Maintaining access to routine care and adopting personalized approaches in COPD management are essential during public health emergencies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03883-8.

Introduction

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic and the implementation of lockdown measures have significantly altered healthcare delivery and created numerous challenges [1]. The reallocation of healthcare resources has had adverse effects on the follow-up and treatment of patients with chronic diseases. At the beginning and peak of the pandemic, healthcare services for non-COVID-19 patients came to a near standstill. For example, in Belgium, healthcare services for chronic conditions decreased as resources were redirected toward the management of COVID-19 [2]. Similarly, due to lockdowns, patients with chronic diseases worldwide faced difficulties in accessing healthcare. In particular, patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), who require regular follow-up, largely avoided hospital visits during the active phase of the pandemic, except in emergencies [3–5].

COPD, a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, has been directly impacted by both the COVID-19 pandemic and the associated lockdowns. Several studies have identified COPD as an independent risk factor for hospitalization due to COVID-19 [6, 7]. In addition, disruptions in healthcare utilization among COPD patients have been widely reported, caused by factors such as fear of infection and the reallocation of healthcare services. A large population-based study in the United Kingdom found that 45% of patients with chronic respiratory disease experienced interruptions in routine monitoring and treatment [8]. Another study reported that consultations and diagnostic testing for COPD were cancelled in 90% of patients [9]. Furthermore, several studies have shown a significant reduction in emergency department visits related to COPD exacerbations during the pandemic [10–13]. Conversely, other studies have reported that COPD patients who remained confined at home experienced worsening respiratory symptoms [14]. Despite these challenges, some reports have shown improved general health status and increased medication adherence in COPD patients during the pandemic [9, 15].

While prolonged lockdowns, social distancing, and restricted healthcare access have undoubtedly influenced outcomes in COPD patients, existing findings remain inconsistent and sometimes contradictory. This multicenter study aims to evaluate the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and associated lockdown measures on COPD-related symptoms and hospital admissions, and to compare these outcomes with the pre-pandemic period.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and participants’ consent statement

The study protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Ankara University on 08.06.2021 (Approval No: I5-362-21). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and good clinical practice guidelines.

All participants were informed about the objectives and procedures of the study prior to enrollment. Participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Data privacy and confidentiality were ensured throughout the study in line with institutional data protection protocols. No personal identifiers were collected or stored.

Study design and participants

This multicenter, cross-sectional study was conducted between July 2021 and February 2022 in ten tertiary care pulmonary outpatient clinics located in different regions of Turkey. The study was designed to evaluate both retrospectively and prospectively collected data from patients with a confirmed diagnosis of COPD.

Eligible patients were ≥ 18 years of age, had a confirmed diagnosis of COPD based on GOLD 2021 criteria [16], and had no history of other chronic pulmonary diseases. Patients with overlapping respiratory diseases (e.g., asthma-COPD overlap, interstitial lung disease), or with incomplete clinical or spirometric data were excluded. We employed a consecutive sampling method and included all eligible patients who presented to the outpatient clinics of the ten participating centers during the study period. Variables, measurement and outcomes

Data were collected through structured face-to-face interviews conducted during outpatient visits, and supplemented by review of electronic medical records. The study included both retrospectively and prospectively collected variables.

Retrospective data included the number of COPD-related hospital admissions, spirometric parameters (FEV₁, FVC, FEV₁/FVC), history of COVID-19 infection and hospitalization, comorbidities, vaccination status, and medication history (e.g., inhaled corticosteroid use). Due to the risk of SARS-CoV-2 transmission during the study period, spirometry was not performed prospectively. Instead, the most recent available pre-pandemic or early-pandemic spirometric data were used.

Prospective data included sociodemographic characteristics, patient-reported outcomes regarding the course of respiratory symptoms, smoking status, healthcare-seeking behavior, and access to medications during the pandemic. Participants were asked to recall whether their respiratory symptoms worsened, remained stable, or improved during three defined pandemic periods, and to provide information on treatment adherence and barriers to healthcare access.

For temporal analysis, the pre-pandemic period was defined as March 2019 to February 2020. The pandemic period was divided into three consecutive six-month intervals: Period 1 (March–August 2020), Period 2 (September 2020–February 2021), and Period 3 (March–August 2021). These timeframes were determined based on the intensity and timing of national lockdown measures (17).

The primary outcomes were the number of COPD-related hospital admissions and self-reported worsening of respiratory symptoms during each pandemic period. COVID-19-related hospitalization was also evaluated as a secondary outcome. Independent variables included age, sex, smoking status, GOLD stage, FEV₁ percent predicted, inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) use, and COVID-19 vaccination status. These variables were selected based on prior literature and clinical relevance. ICS use and disease severity (as reflected by GOLD stage and spirometric indices) were also considered as potential confounders.

COVID-19 pandemic process in Turkey

The first case of COVID-19 was officially announced on March 11, 2020. Restrictions were very intense in the first 3 months. In June, restrictions were removed to a great extent until November when a series of new restrictions were re-imposed along with a progressive rise of cases [17].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics version 25. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the study data and included means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to evaluate the normality of continuous variables. Since not all variables met the assumption of normality, non-parametric tests were used when appropriate.

Comparisons between two independent groups were made using the Mann–Whitney U test, while differences between paired samples (i.e., pre-pandemic vs. pandemic periods) were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The Chi-square test was used to examine associations between categorical variables; Fisher’s exact test was employed when the assumptions for Chi-square were not met. A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In addition to these analyses, two separate multivariable linear regression models were constructed to identify the factors independently associated with (1) the number of hospital admissions during the pandemic period, and (2) hospitalization due to COVID-19 among COPD patients.

For both models, independent variables included age, sex, comorbidity status, GOLD classification, FEV₁ (% predicted), ICS use, home support, smoking status, and symptom burden. The presence of multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) statistics; tolerance values below 0.1 and VIFs greater than 10 were considered indicative of multicollinearity. Model adequacy was evaluated using residual diagnostics, including standardized residual plots and histograms to assess normality and homoscedasticity (Figs. 2 and 3). The adjusted R² values were used to assess the proportion of variance explained by each model.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, 184 patients (53%) reported a worsening of respiratory symptoms (Table 2). When the pandemic period was analyzed in 6-month intervals, the proportion of patients experiencing exacerbations (worsening dyspnea and/or increased cough and sputum production) showed a progressive increase (27.1%, 30.3%, and 40.6%, respectively; p < 0.001) (Table 3). Most of these patients sought medical attention: 79 (37.1%) visited a pulmonary outpatient clinic, 74 (34.7%) presented to the emergency department, and 36 (16.9%) were hospitalized due to severe exacerbation.

Compared to the pre-pandemic period, the mean number of hospital admissions was significantly lower during the pandemic (2.19 ± 3.11 vs. 1.38 ± 2.36, p < 0.0001). Only nine patients (2.6%) reported difficulties accessing their maintenance medications. A total of 76.4% of patients had prescriptions for the past 3 months. Twelve patients (5.6%) used online consultation services for COPD during the pandemic. Pulmonary function testing had been performed in the last year in 74.8% of patients (Table 2).

Table 4 presents a comparison of COPD patient characteristics according to hospital admissions before and during the pandemic. A significant decrease in hospital admissions was observed among male patients, whereas the reduction in female patients was not statistically significant. Admissions decreased significantly across all GOLD groups; however, no significant decrease was observed in patients with very severe obstruction (FEV₁ < 30%) or mild obstruction (FEV₁ >80%).

To identify factors associated with hospital admissions during the pandemic period (July 2020 – July 2021), a multivariable linear regression analysis was performed (Table 5). Independent variables included age, gender, comorbidity status, GOLD stage, FEV₁ (% predicted), ICS use, home support, and smoking status. The model accounted for 86.3% of the variance in hospital admissions (R² = 0.863). No multicollinearity was detected. GOLD stage, FEV₁, and ICS use were statistically significant predictors (p < 0.05). Specifically, higher GOLD stages and ICS use were associated with increased admissions, while higher FEV₁ values were associated with fewer admissions. Model diagnostics indicated that residuals were approximately normally distributed and homoscedastic (Figure 2).

Discussion

This multicenter study provides a comprehensive real-world evaluation of how the COVID-19 pandemic affected COPD patients in Turkey. Our findings revealed a significant decrease in hospital admissions during the pandemic compared to the pre-pandemic year, despite an observed increase in self-reported respiratory symptoms over time. This paradox suggests that the pandemic altered not only disease burden but also healthcare utilization behaviors, as patients may have avoided hospital settings due to infection concerns or encountered barriers in accessing routine care.

Our results are consistent with prior studies reporting a 27–78% decrease in COPD-related exacerbations and hospitalizations during the pandemic [18, 19]. Similar findings were observed by Sykes et al., who documented reduced hospital admissions for COPD exacerbations during lockdowns in the UK [12], and in a single-center study from Turkey reporting significantly fewer emergency visits and admissions [20]. These decreases have largely been attributed to public health measures—such as masking, social distancing, and lockdowns—which likely reduced respiratory viral transmission. In addition, improved air quality during quarantine periods may have played a role in mitigating exacerbations [21–24].

However, our study uniquely demonstrated a gradual increase in symptom burden across three pandemic periods, with the lowest rates during the initial 4-month lockdown, when restrictions were most stringent. This temporal trend suggests that short-term environmental and behavioral improvements may not have been sustained, and longer-term disruptions in care continuity might have contributed to increased symptomatology. Moreover, although hospital admissions decreased, it is unclear whether this represented true clinical improvement or unmet care needs. Prior studies have raised concerns that reduced healthcare utilization during the pandemic could lead to delayed diagnosis and treatment of chronic diseases, including COPD [8, 9].

To better understand factors contributing to hospital admissions, we conducted multivariable regression analysis. Our results showed that advanced GOLD stage, lower FEV₁, and ICS use were independently associated with increased hospital admissions. These findings align with the well-established role of airflow limitation and symptom severity in predicting exacerbation risk and healthcare use [25]. The association of ICS use with increased admissions may reflect confounding by disease severity, as ICS is more frequently prescribed in patients with a history of exacerbations.

We also explored COVID-19-related outcomes. Among our cohort, 21.1% of patients had a confirmed COVID-19 diagnosis—a higher prevalence than that reported in other studies (e.g., 11% in Montiel-López et al. [26]). This could be explained by our longer observation period. Notably, regression analysis revealed that GOLD stage, lower FEV₁, smoking, and ICS use were all associated with an increased risk of hospitalization due to COVID-19. These results are consistent with previous findings indicating that COPD severity and comorbid behaviors such as smoking increase vulnerability to severe COVID-19 outcomes [6, 7, 26].

The role of ICS in COVID-19 remains controversial [27]. In our study, ICS use was not found to be significantly associated with hospitalization due to COVID-19. While the univariate analysis initially suggested a possible association, this relationship did not remain statistically significant in the multivariable regression model. This suggests that the observed association may have been confounded by underlying disease severity or other clinical factors. Previous studies have reported mixed results, with some suggesting increased risk of poor outcomes among ICS users, while others propose potential protective effects due to anti-inflammatory or antiviral actions [28, 29]. ICS is well known to reduce exacerbation frequency in COPD patients with eosinophilic inflammation [30]; however, its use in patients with low eosinophil counts or chronic bacterial colonization has been linked to increased pneumonia risk [31]. Therefore, ICS use during the pandemic may have had dual effects—providing benefit for some patients while increasing risks in others.

Despite the challenges of the pandemic, our study showed high treatment adherence. Only 2.6% of patients reported difficulty accessing medications, and 73% had received at least two doses of COVID-19 vaccine. This is higher than adherence rates reported in some studies (e.g., Liang et al. reported 72% adherence [14]), suggesting that national policies facilitating medication access may have been effective.

Finally, while our study provides valuable insights into both the direct consequences (e.g., infection, COVID-related hospitalization) and indirect consequences (e.g., reduced healthcare access, symptom worsening) of the pandemic on COPD management, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, symptom data were self-reported and thus subject to recall bias. Second, the study only included patients presenting to outpatient clinics, which may have led to underrepresentation of more severely ill patients who avoided care. Third, long-term outcomes such as mortality were not assessed. In addition, due to the lack of systematically collected patient-reported symptom data from the pre-pandemic period, the interpretation that reduced symptom burden during the early pandemic phase was related to public health measures should be considered cautiously.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that while COPD-related hospital admissions decreased during the pandemic, patient-reported symptoms increased over time, likely reflecting both behavioral and systemic factors. The regression analyses highlighted disease severity, FEV₁, ICS use, and smoking as key predictors of hospital admissions and COVID-19-related outcomes. These results underscore the need for maintaining access to chronic disease care during public health emergencies and support a personalized approach to COPD management, especially regarding ICS use in the context of viral threats.
