A Novel Fluorescence Polarization Binding Assay for the Main Protease (Mpro) of SARS-CoV‑2
Authors: Mackenzie K. Wyllie, Rayhan G. Biswas, Jyoti Vishwakarma, Morgan A. Esler, Joseph A. Rollie, Hideki Aihara, Reuben S. Harris, Daniel A. Harki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The COVID-19 pandemic incited a global health crisis that accelerated the development of antiviral therapeutics. One successful avenue for inhibiting SARS-CoV-2 has been through targeting the main protease (Mpro; 3CLpro), a key enzyme for the viral lifecycle that cleaves at 11 sites in the viral polyprotein pp1a and pp1ab. Although potent inhibitors of Mpro have been discovered, including the FDA-approved drug Paxlovid, the potential emergence of resistant variants requires continued antiviral development efforts. The current methods to characterize binders of Mpro, such as SPR or ITC, are costly and time-consuming. To improve the speed and feasibility of Mpro inhibitor development, we developed a competitive miniaturized fluorescence polarization (FP) binding assay. We repurposed small molecules from a DNA-encoded library screen into FP probes by appending a fluorophore with various linkers. After identifying a probe that exhibited potent Mpro binding (K D ∼43 nM), we optimized buffer conditions, pH, and additives. Assay validation revealed that our competitive fluorescence polarization assay was robust, with a Z′-score of 0.79 and a signal window of 23. This assay can be used as a single-point assay for high-throughput screening or to triage small molecules by generating K i values for binding. Efforts from this work resulted in an Mpro binding assay that requires minimal protein consumption, low sample volumes, short incubation times (30 min), and operates at room temperature. In conclusion, we developed a robust FP assay that can be used to rapidly characterize Mpro-binding small molecules to support the development of new SARS-CoV-2 antivirals.

Coronaviruses are a large family of RNA viruses that are endemic to humans and typically present mild-to-moderate upper respiratory symptoms. However, the zoonotic spillover of coronaviruses has caused fatal illnesses, such as severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS), , Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS), and most recently, coronavirus disease (COVID-19). − Earlier coronavirus outbreaks have been regional, whereas COVID-19 infection resulted in a global pandemic with a fatality rate of ∼ 1%. , With three global health outbreaks caused by coronaviruses in the past two decades, the continued development of broad spectrum antivirals may prove effective in thwarting future coronavirus outbreaks. However, the current toolbox to identify novel antiviral molecules is limited. Advancement in facile in vitro assays is needed for the continued development of antiviral therapeutics.

COVID-19 is caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2, SARS2), which is an enveloped single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus. The genome of SARS2 is 30 kb and encodes 29 proteins, with a majority of its genome comprising two open reading frames, ORF1a and ORF1b. − These open reading frames are translated into two polyproteins that rely on viral proteases to process 16 different nonstructural proteins (NSPs) that are required for viral replication. SARS2 encodes two cysteine proteases that are responsible for cleaving these polyproteins into functional units; the papain-like protease (PLpro) and the main protease (Mpro, also termed 3C-like protease, 3CLpro). PLpro processes 3 (Nsps1–3) cleavage sites, whereas Mpro processes 11 (Nsps4–16) cleavage sites. , Mpro plays an essential role in processing mature Nsps that make up the large replication-transcription complex required for pathogenesis and thus has been an attractive drug target for SARS-CoV-2 inhibition. Paxlovid, an approved combination therapy consisting of nirmatrelvir, which covalently inhibits the catalytic function of Mpro, and ritonavir, a cytochrome p450 inhibitor that mitigates the metabolic clearance of nirmatrelvir, has shown remarkable success in treating SARS2 infections. − Ensitrelvir (Xocova), which was recently approved in Japan, is another Mpro inhibitor that has shown positive results in treating patients with COVID-19. , Mpro inhibition has proven to be an effective approach for COVID-19 treatment, however, the rapid evolution of SARS2 has produced clinical variants that show varied efficacy of these protease inhibitors. − Additional testing of a panel of Mpro mutants identified amino acid changes that induce various degrees of resistance to both nirmatrelvir and ensitrelvir. The amino acid changes present in that study were all identified in humans, indicating a need for the continued development of advanced viral protease inhibitors with broad spectrum activity. Additionally, the development of SARS2 Mpro inhibitors may provide chemical matter for future coronavirus outbreaks due to conservation of the Mpro protein across different coronaviruses.

There have been many reports of large high-throughput screens from academic groups to identify next generation SARS2 Mpro inhibitors. Researchers have utilized cellular based systems, , in vitro activity screens, , in silico methods, − and DNA-encoded library technology, − to survey diverse chemicals that inhibit Mpro. In all these examples, rigorous biophysical, biochemical, and medicinal chemistry efforts must be conducted to funnel the large number of compounds into validated hits. Current assays for Mpro are primarily activity-based in vitro assays that utilize peptide-based FRET quenching pairs ,,, or cellular-based activity assays that modulate fluorescence reporter expression. ,, These assays have been key in validating target engagement and the inhibitory potential of novel protease inhibitors; however, these experiments do not provide any binding affinity information. Determining the binding affinity of a molecule (dissociation constant: K D) is an important step in triaging hits to prioritize the tightest Mpro binder. Although binding affinity trends typically track with inhibition trends, only relying on inhibition data eliminates potential hits that may interact tightly with a protein of interest while not significantly affecting enzymatic activity. Such molecules may have development potential with the advent of targeted protein degraders. , Currently, there are a limited number of assays for Mpro that provide binding kinetics, and none of these assays are high-throughput, which extends the time required to triage large libraries of small molecules. The development of robust assays that provide binding data is necessary for streamlining the hit-to-lead triaging process for optimizing next generation Mpro inhibitors.

Previously, surface plasmon resonance (SPR), , biolayer interferometry (BLI), , and isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) have been used to characterize ligand binding to Mpro. SPR and BLI provide kinetic binding parameters, such as k on and k off, whereas ITC provides thermodynamic parameters of the interaction. Although these techniques are data rich, none of these approaches are high-throughput to assess a large collection of primary hits in a feasible manner. Additionally, these assays are time-consuming and expensive, and may take hours or days to acquire data, or require expensive equipment and high protein consumption. To improve the speed and feasibility of identifying and triaging Mpro inhibitors, we have developed a competitive miniature fluorescence polarization (FP) assay. The assay was optimized against the SARS2 omicron variant bearing the P132H Mpro mutation as this variant was dominant in patient populations, highly transmissible, and exhibited a propensity for therapeutic escape from vaccination due to increased mutations from prior variants. Although the original omicron variant is no longer circulating, the current dominant variants (as of Fall 2025) delineate from the omicron variant with a majority of patients possessing the P132H Mpro mutation. , Consequently, assay optimization utilized the SARS2 omicron variant of Mpro as the most clinically relevant protease. Using small molecules identified from a DNA-encoded library screen, we developed fluorescence polarization probes that bind to SARS2 Mpro in the low nanomolar range. By optimizing the Mpro-binding ligand, the linker length, and the assay conditions, we produced a robust assay (Z′-score = 0.79) that requires minimal protein consumption, low sample volumes, and short incubation times. To our knowledge, this is the first fluorescence polarization binding assay reported for Mpro, although other activity-based FP assays have been developed. Our assay can be used for high throughput screening, or to quickly characterize Mpro-binding small molecules to continue the development of SARS2 antivirals.

Results and Discussion

Rational Design of FP Probes for Mpro

When conducting FP assays, the range of resolvable inhibitor potency is limited by the affinity of the probe. Thus, high affinity probes provide the best signal window when looking to identify potent competitive inhibitors. However, small molecules that exhibit subnanomolar affinities (nirmatrelvir and ensitrelvir) are not suitable FP probes as the concentration of the tracer molecule should be less than the K D, which would require highly sensitive fluorescence detection. Consequently, an ideal FP probe exhibits high affinity for the protein of interest in a concentration range that produces a robust fluorescence signal. Typical FP probes are constructed by appending a fluorophore, with a flexible linker, to a small molecule that binds the protein of interest. ,

To develop a fluorescence polarization binding probe for SARS-CoV-2 Mpro, we selected small molecules identified from a DNA-encoded library (DEL) screen with ideal inhibition profiles (3 – 50 nM). Jimmidi et al. screened approximately 4 billion DNA-barcoded compounds and identified a novel nonpeptidic Mpro inhibitory chemotype possessing a central benzimidazole. One of the original hits, CDD1733, was optimized by modifying the N1 and C7 positions of the benzimidazole to CDD1845, which efficiently inhibits Mpro activity (K i of 3 nM). Modification of CDD1733 at the C7 position resulted in two related analogues, CDD1829 and CDD1841, which inhibit Mpro with K i’s of 46 and 571 nM, respectively (Figure D). CDD1733 and a structurally related molecule, CDD1845, were cocrystallized with Mpro (PDB: 7URB and 7UR9), which revealed solvent-exposed vectors for each compound (Figure A). Accordingly, we determined that CDD1733 was amenable to fluorophore attachment at two potential points: at the terminal N-methylamide and the ortho position of aromatic moiety at the C7 amide substituent (Figure B). Attachment of the fluorophore at the N1 position resulted in FP probe 1 (based on parent molecule CDD1829) while fluorophore attachment at the o-benzene position and removal of the trifluoromethyl group resulted in FP probe 2 (based on parent molecule CDD1841) (Figure A). Similarly, the butyramide on the benzimidazole N1 position of CDD1845 was deemed a suitable attachment point for a fluorophore (Figure C) and resulted in FP probes 3a–c (based on parent molecule CDD1845). We chose polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based linkers for conjugation, which are known for attractive properties such as water solubility, flexibility, and tunable chain length. Alexa Fluor 594 was selected as the fluorophore due to its long fluorescence lifetime, pH stability, red-shifted spectra, and successful use in other fluorescence polarization applications. ,

Initial probe development for SARS2 Mpro began by comparing the three different protein targeting ligands, which all possessed an Alexa Fluor 594 moiety connected via a PEG3 linker. With the three probes in hand, we titrated Mpro against a set concentration of probe (25 nM) in Tris buffer (pH 7.4) to generate ΔmP curves. These curves indicated that the most efficient Mpro binder was 3c, followed by 1, and lastly 2 (Figure B). Gratifyingly, these trends follow the inhibitory values determined by Jimmidi et al. (Table ). However, the binding affinity of PEGylated probe 2 in comparison to the Mpro inhibition data from parent molecule CDD1841 alone is quite different. This indicates that the attachment of the fluorophore at the ortho position may significantly hamper binding of the small molecule to Mpro. Similarly, 3c shows a small penalty in binding affinity compared to the inhibition data, indicating the fluorophore attachment point slightly perturbs binding. However, the 3c probe exhibits the best K D and is within the preferred concentration range for fluorescence detection. Thus, 3c was selected as the protein targeting ligand for further probe optimization.

The linker length between the fluorophore and the protein-binding ligand is an important factor in the development of fluorescence polarization probes. Linkers that are too flexible and too long cause local movement of the fluorophore and depolarization, also known as the “propeller effect”, resulting in a poor correlation between anisotropy and binding events. Conversely, linkers that are too short may interfere with binding of the small molecule to the protein of interest. Fluorescence polarization probe 3 was modified with linkers ranging from one to three PEG units to identify the linker length that provided the best signal window. SARS2 Mpro was then titrated against a set concentration (25 nM) of the FP probes (3a–c) to generate ΔmP binding curves (Figure C). Probe 3c was measured initially to compare to probes 1 and 2 (Figure B) and then measured again to compare the binding affinity to probes 3a and 3b (Figure C). There were slight differences in the K D of 3c; however, the binding affinities measured in both experiments are within error of each other. Overall, there was little difference in the binding affinities between 3a–3c; however, the probe with the shortest linker (3a, PEG1) exhibited the best solubility and limits the risk of inducing the propeller effect. These results indicate that the shortest linker does not significantly affect the binding between the FP probe and the protein. The binding affinity of 3a was measured using ITC to determine the accuracy of the K D measured via fluorescence polarization. Gratifyingly, 3a exhibited a similar binding affinity (K D = 59 nM) to the K D produced from fluorescence polarization (36 nM, Figure D). Thus, 3a was selected as the final FP probe for use in assay optimization against SARS-CoV-2 Mpro (Table ).

Optimization of Assay Conditions

With the overall goal of developing a facile competitive assay to characterize small molecule binders of SARS2 Mpro, we focused on optimizing buffering conditions with an emphasis on combatting false positive binders. To determine the optimal conditions, we titrated 3a in different Tris buffers used to analyze Mpro and measured the polarization stability (Figure S1). 10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 50 mM NaCl and the 20 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl buffers both showed good polarization stability for the linked fluorophore (Alexa Fluor 594). Focusing on applications for future high throughput screening efforts, the buffer consisting of 10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 50 mM NaCl was selected for reagent minimization. We then investigated how different pH values affect the binding of the tracer to Mpro. We titrated Mpro against a set concentration of 3a at five different pH values ranging from pH 6–9 and assessed binding affinity. Unsurprisingly, Mpro showed a worsened binding affinity to 3a at the extreme low and high pH values (Figure B). The pH values closer to biological relevance exhibited better binding affinities with little variation between the different pH conditions. To further narrow a suitable pH for continued assay development, we repeated the direct binding experiment at different pH values (7.0, 7.4, and 8.0), but varied the concentration of 3a (50, 25, and 10 nM), and then plotted K D as a function of tracer concentration (Figure C). Ideally, the K D of a tracer should remain constant as a function of concentration. However, if the assay conditions generate different K D values as a function of tracer concentration, data generated from experiments with these conditions may be less reliable if slight variations in tracer dilution led to significantly different binding characteristics. The lower pH conditions (7.0 and 7.4) exhibit variable binding affinities that seem to be slightly dependent on tracer concentration, with lower concentrations correlating with better binding affinity. The pH 8.0 conditions showed a relatively stable K D as a function of tracer concentration and was thus used as the buffering pH moving forward. Further optimization efforts in this work use a 3a concentration of 25 nM, as this amount displayed good fluorescence intensity and remained below the measured K D value (36 nM). Lower concentrations of fluorophore were not used due to higher error associated with a decrease in fluorescence signal.

Before considering additional buffer additives, we wanted to verify the integrity of the protein with the chosen buffering conditions. Using differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) we measured the melting temperature (T m) of SARS2 Mpro at different pH in Tris buffer (Figure D). The three pH conditions (7.0, 7.4, and 8.0) produced T m values similar to literature reports. , Lastly, we wanted to determine how common buffer additives affect the binding affinity of 3a to Mpro. With the overall goal of developing an assay that can be used as a primary screening technique, or as a tool for triage efforts, we were interested in buffer additives that can mitigate false positives. The reducing agent DTT was added to prevent nonspecific adduction by covalent inhibitors and to maintain protein stability. The chelating agent EDTA was included to mitigate metal contamination prevalent in many screening compounds. The detergent Tween-20 was added to aid in solubilizing hydrophobic small molecules. The effects of these buffering additives on K D against Mpro were assessed individually and as a combined solution (Figure E). EDTA and DTT both significantly improved the binding affinity of Mpro to 3a, whereas Tween-20 slightly improved binding compared to no additives. The buffer that included all the additives exhibited the best binding affinity to 3a. We then incorporated 0.5 mM TCEP, as opposed to DTT, due to its improved stability at higher pH and stronger reducing strength (Figure S2). These conditions [10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM TCEP, 25 nM 3a probe] were then used to characterize and validate the assay.

To assess how well the buffer composition mitigates false positives, known pan assay interference compounds (PAINs) were screened in this assay. PAINs compounds have chemical features that can produce false positive results in assays by acting as redox cyclers, covalent modifiers, metal complexes, or membrane disrupters. Four known PAINs compounds (structures found Figure S3) were tested at concentrations commonly used to screen against the main protease of SARS2 (50 μM, 20 μM and 10 μM). ,, The assay was not sensitive to NSC663284, a known redox cycler, or to curcumin, a covalent modifier and membrane disruptor, at any of the concentrations tested (Figure F). However, high concentrations of the quinone aurintricarboxylic acid (ATA) and the catechol 6-hydroxy-dl-DOPA did produce a signal that would have been flagged as a “positive hit” in a high-throughput screening scenario. Catechols and quinones are known to function as covalent modifiers and are likely interacting nonspecifically with the nucleophilic cysteine in the active site of Mpro. Competitive IC50 Development and Assay Validation

To characterize the durability of our HTS fluorescence polarization assay, we began by determining the effect of DMSO on the Z′ factor of the assay. With most small molecules being stored in DMSO solutions, and our overall goal of developing an assay to assess small molecule-Mpro binding, we wanted to ensure DMSO does not affect assay quality. We compared the background polarization of the tracer (25 nM) versus the tracer bound to Mpro at various concentrations of DMSO (0–25%) to determine the effect of DMSO concentration on Z′ factor. All the DMSO concentrations measured produced a Z′ factor well above the recommended 0.5 and ranged from 0.73–0.8 (Figure A). However, concentrations above 10% DMSO slightly reduced the assay Z′ factor when compared to 0% DMSO.

Additionally, we determined the effect of incubation time on K D for the direct binding of 3a to Mpro. Curves were generated beginning at 15 min up until 24 h. Plotting the K D against the incubation time, we observed the K D was stable from 15 min up to 3 h (Figure B). At 12 and 24 h the calculated K D worsened, presumably due to evaporation of reagents in the microplate over extended time courses. Using this information, the competitive assay was incubated for 30 min and was conducted using 5% DMSO to ensure that the competitive small molecules maintain solubility at the higher concentrations used for competing off the tracer compound.

The competitive assay is conducted after determining the K D and the concentration of protein that results in 80% of the protein being bound, also known as the SB80 value. Repeated K D measurement of 3a against Mpro resulted in a binding affinity of 45 nM and an SB80 value of 130 nM (Figure C). Thus, for the competitive assay, an unlabeled small molecule, CDD1829, was titrated against the SB80 concentration of the protein (130 nM) and 3a (25 nM). Higher concentrations of the unlabeled small molecule will compete off the tracer, producing lower anisotropy, which generates a binding curve that can be used to determine IC50, and indirectly, the K i of binding. When titrating CDD1829 in these conditions, we determined the inhibitor possessed an IC50 of 685 nM, which when using the equation in the Supporting Information (Section 2.2), produced a K i of 127 nM (Figure D). The IC50 value of this small molecule determined from the competitive FP assay was then used to characterize the Z′ factor of our assay. Over the course of 3 days, using interleaved high, medium, and low signals, we determined how robust our assay was by measuring the consistency of our maximum signal (no inhibitor), mid signal (685 nM CDD1829), and low signal (50 μM CDD1829). Analyzing three plates per day over a three-day period resulted in an assay with a Z′ factor of 0.79 and a signal window of 23 (Figure E). Both of these values are much higher than the 0.5 and 2 that is recommended by the assay guidance manual for Z′ factor and signal window, respectively. These results indicate our assay is robust and would be useful for high-throughput screening or triage of small molecules targeting the SARS2 main protease.

We then determined the binding affinity of 3a against multiple SARS2 Mpro variants and proteases from other coronaviruses. Probe 3a binds to the Washington and the omicron enzymes similarly (Figure F). However, the ΔP168/S144A and the E166V variants, both of which resist nirmatrelvir and ensitrelvir, produced reduced binding affinity to our probe. Individual point mutations of ΔP168 and S144A exhibit increased resistance to nirmatrelvir and ensitrelvir; however, the dual double mutant was tested in this work. The E166I mutation has recently been reported to exhibit stronger nirmatrelvir resistance than E166V. These results track with our data where the E166I variant produces the worst binding affinity to 3a with a K D of 2 μM. Although these Mpro mutants present varied affinities to our probe, the curves for each are resolvable and proves that 3a can be used as an FP probe for each enzyme. In assessing pan-coronavirus applicability, 3a was titrated against the main protease of SARS1, SARS2 (Washington strain), and MERS. Probe 3a produced a similar binding affinity to the main protease of SARS1 when compared to SARS2 (Figure G). However, 3a exhibited very low binding to the main protease of MERS. Although the main protease is well conserved across coronaviruses, SARS1 and SARS2 main proteases exhibit the highest homology sharing 96% sequence similarity. The main proteases of SARS2 and MERS possess similar structural features, however they only share 51% sequence identity and have distinct differences in active site residues. These structural similarities and differences between the three main proteases may explain the preference of probe 3a for SARS1/SARS2 over MERS. In assessing binding to other proteins, probe 3a exhibits minimal affinity for the other SARS2 cysteine protease, PLpro, the SARS2 spike protein RNA binding domain, or the host protein, angiotensin converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) (Figure G). These data support the use of probe 3a for future coronaviruses that possess high sequence homology to the main proteases of SARS1 or SARS2.

Finally, we wanted to determine the accuracy of our competitive binding assay. To determine the validity of our results obtained from fluorescence polarization, IC50 values were compared to the protein thermal shift measurements of five published and three unpublished molecules. Nirmatrelvir, ensitrelvir, bofutrelvir, CDD1819, CDD1829, and three unpublished Mpro inhibitors in development [Harki Lab (HLB) A, B, and C] were tested at 50 μM against the omicron variant of Mpro in differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) (Figure D). Compounds that interact tightly with the protein will stabilize the protein and produce a shift in protein melting temperature (ΔT m). The eight selected compounds produced reliable thermal shifts above the DMSO control of 52.6 °C (Figure A). Nirmatrelvir shifted the melting temperature the most at 18.6 °C, followed by bofutrelvir (17.8 °C), and then ensitrelvir and HLB A (14.2 °C). The other four compounds shifted the melting temperature from 5.3 – 10.8 °C above the DMSO reference (Figure B). These compounds were then measured in our competitive FP assay to assess the correlation to the values determined via DSF. All the compounds produce fully resolved curves that follow the trends identified in DSF experiments (Figure C). As mentioned previously, the range of resolvable inhibitor binding affinity is limited by the affinity of the selected probe. Consequently, the small molecules that bind Mpro with a higher affinity than FP probe 3a (nirmatrelvir, ensitrelvir, and bofutrelvir) all have overlapping data that is indistinguishable by fluorescence polarization. Conversely, DSF can inform on target engagement by measuring global protein stability and does not rely on the affinity of an active site directed displacement probe. These differences can be seen in the correlation plot where the tight binding molecules exhibit varied ΔT m values by DSF, but essentially identical IC50 values determined by FP (Figure E). Nonetheless, the FP assay developed here still correlates quite well to the DSF data with an R2 of 0.80. The results here indicate that this FP assay can be utilized for facile triaging of small molecule hits or as a primary screening method, but that highly optimized Mpro ligands with binding affinities superior to that of FP probe 3a cannot be accurately quantified.

Conclusions

We have developed a robust high-throughput fluorescence polarization assay to assess small molecule binding against the main protease of SARS-CoV-2. Using small molecules identified from DNA encoded library technology we synthesized fluorescence polarization probes from three different protein-targeting ligands. After consulting the X-ray crystallography structures, potential attachment points were selected to append an Alexa Fluor 594 fluorophore. These probes were then compared head-to-head for further optimization. The K D value trends generated from this experiment tracked well with historical data for these compounds and provided insight into regions where the attached fluorophore may be clashing with the protein surface. Two of our initial probes, 1 and 2, exhibited lower binding affinity than what was initially predicted based off previous inhibition data. These results indicated the appendage of the fluorophore considerably impeded binding to Mpro. Probe 3c exhibited the best binding and was selected for further optimization. Investigation into the length of the linker between the protein-targeting ligand and the fluorophore was performed; however, linker length did not significantly affect the binding affinity to Mpro. The FP probe with the shortest linker, 3a, was selected for further assay optimization due to the improved solubility and polarization stability compared to the probes with longer linkers. With an emphasis on combatting false positives, the effect of buffer additives on binding affinity was measured to identify the optimal buffer for future studies. Additionally, a pH of 8.0 was determined to be optimal for protein stability and K D. Lastly, we validated the assay and analyzed the effects of DMSO on assay window. DMSO concentrations up to 25% do not significantly affect the assay quality. Using an unlabeled small molecule, CDD1829, we determined the assay developed here is robust with a Z′ factor of 0.79 and a signal window of 23. Assessment of 3a against varying Mpro mutations exhibited worsened binding affinity; however, 3a may still prove useful in screening small molecules against these variants. Additionally, this assay may prove useful for future coronaviruses that possess high homology to the main protease of SARS1/SARS2.

In sum, we report a novel Mpro FP binding assay that requires short incubation times (30 min), low sample volume (20 μL), low protein consumption, and operates at room temperature. Previous Mpro fluorescence polarization assays rely on the activity of the protease to release the fluorophore to affect anisotropy values. This work describes the first binding fluorescence polarization assay for Mpro. This assay is a simple mix-and-read protocol that can be used for primary screening or used to generate full titration curves to quickly triage small molecule binders of Mpro. Utilization of this assay may help to accelerate the development of next generation Mpro inhibitors.

Direct Binding Fluorescence Polarization Against Mpro

In these direct binding experiments, concentrations of protein are titrated against a set concentration of probe. Prior to setting up the assay, Mpro was desalted into FP assay buffer without reducing agent (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.01% Tween-20), or into the assay buffer stated during assay optimization. The protein was then concentrated using centrifugal 10 kDa molecular weight cut off filters (Millipore, UFC801024). Protein concentration was determined by a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (ThermoScientific, 23225). Protein was then diluted in a 1:1 serial dilution across the plate in FP assay buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 50 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM TCEP, 1 mM EDTA, 0.01% Tween-20, or into buffer stated during assay optimization) producing 14 concentration points (10 μL, 384 well plate, black, low volume, Corning, 4514). An equal volume of probe (1, 2, 3a, 3b, or 3c) diluted in buffer (10 μL, 2X, 50 nM, or 2X the concentration of probe stated in the optimization) was added to every well (final concentration of tracer = 25 nM, final volume = 20 μL). The probe in buffer only was used as a sample to measure the background fluorescence polarization contribution of the probe, which ultimately is subtracted from every value. The plate was then placed on an orbital shaker (1000 rpm, 1 min) and centrifuged (1000 rpm, 1 min), before being incubated on the benchtop in the dark (room temperature, 30 min, or at the specified time in the time dependence experiment). The plate was read on a Tecan Spark plate reader with monochromator-based excitation wavelength of 560 ± 5 nm and filter-based emission wavelength of 620 ± 10 nm. G-factor was calibrated measuring the buffer only and probe only samples and determined to be 1.353. Curves were generated by subtracting the background polarization contributed from the probe only samples, then delta millipolarization (ΔmP) was plotted against Mpro concentration in μM. Each biological replicate was performed in technical triplicate with error represented as standard error of the mean (SEM) and data was analyzed using Prism v9.5.1 (GraphPad) with a nonlinear fit using the “specific binding with Hill slope” model.

Competitive Fluorescence Polarization Assay

Ten μL of a solution of 10% DMSO in FP assay buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 50 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM TCEP, 1 mM EDTA, 0.01% Tween-20) is added to every well excluding the first well (10 μL, final concentration 5% DMSO). Small molecules used in the competitive assay are then diluted in 10% DMSO in assay buffer at 2X the preferred final concentration. The small molecules were then diluted in a 1:1 serial dilution across the plate in FP assay buffer with 10% DMSO producing 14 concentration points (10 μL, 384 well plate, black, low volume, Corning, 4514, final DMSO concentration = 5%). Mpro was diluted in assay buffer to concentrations at 4X the SB80 value (concentration of protein where 80% of the tracer is bound, 520 nM for omicron variant), determined from direct binding experiments. This solution was then added to all wells, excluding the wells used for background measurements (5 μL, 384 well plate, black, low volume, Corning, 4514, final concentration = 130 nM). Probe 3a at 4X the desired concentration was then dissolved in FP assay buffer and added to every well (5 μL, 100 nM, final concentration = 25 nM). The plate was then placed on an orbital shaker (1000 rpm, 1 min) and centrifuged (1000 rpm, 1 min), before being incubated on the benchtop in the dark (room temperature, 30 min). The plate was read on a Tecan Spark plate reader using the specifications explained in the “direct binding” protocol. Data were analyzed using Prism v9.5.1 (GraphPad) with a nonlinear fit using the “[inhibitor] vs response variable slope” model. Data points and error bars are the mean and standard error of the mean of N = 3 technical replicates. Millipolarization values were normalized to the average of N = 12 technical replicates of wells containing probe only and was plotted as a function of percent bound to Mpro. This equation, as well as the equation to calculate K i from IC50, can be found in the Supporting Information (eqs S1 and S2, respectively).

Isothermal Titration Calorimetry

The experiments were performed on a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC (Malvern Panalytical, UK) at 25 °C. Untagged Mpro was diluted in ITC buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM TCEP, and 3% DMSO) to a final concentration of 10 μM. 300 μL of 10 μM Mpro was placed in a calorimetric cell, and the syringe was loaded with 70 μL of 300 μM of compound (probe 3a). The reference cell was filled with 300 μL Milli-Q water. Twenty-five injections of 1.5 μL compounds were injected into the cell at intervals of 150 s with a stirring speed of 750 rpm. Ensitrelvir (noncovalent Mpro inhibitor) used as positive control. A similar experiment was performed with 300 μM Ensitrelvir. To measure the binding affinity (K D), enthalpy (ΔH), entropy (ΔS), and stoichiometry (n), the raw ITC data was analyzed using MicroCal PEAQ-ITC analysis software, with K D fitted using nonlinear regression and error is reported as standard error of the mean (SEM).

DMSO Tolerance Testing

DMSO tolerance was measured by determining the Z′-factor under different concentrations of DMSO (0%, 2.5%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20% and 25%). The conditions for the “positive control” were Mpro at a concentration of 130 nM with 25 nM probe, while the “negative control” was 25 nM probe without any Mpro. DMSO in assay buffer at 2x the final concentration was added to every well (10 μL, 2X concentration). Five μL of 520 nM Mpro in assay buffer was added to the high concentration wells (final concentration of protein = 130 nM, 20 μL total). Five μL of assay buffer was added to the low concentration wells. Lastly, 5 μL of 100 nM 3a probe was added to every well (final concentration = 25 nM, 20 μL total). N = 24 for the positive signal and N = 24 for the negative signal, for each DMSO condition. The plate was then placed on an orbital shaker (1000 rpm, 1 min) and centrifuged (1000 rpm, 1 min), before being incubated on the benchtop in the dark (room temperature, 30 min). The plate was read on a Tecan Spark plate reader using the specifications explained in the “direct binding” protocol. Millipolarization units were plotted for each DMSO condition. Z′-factor was calculated using the equation found in the Supporting Information (eq S3).

Differential Scanning Fluorimetry (Buffer Optimization)

Proteins were buffer exchanged into assay buffer (10 mM Tris, variable pH, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.01% Tween-20). The proteins were concentrated via centrifugation with a 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) filter, and the concentration was determined via bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (ThermoFisher). Proteins were diluted with assay buffer to 10.5 μM, and adjusted to 0.52 mM TCEP, and 19 μL was added to a 96-well MicroAmp PCR plate (ThermoFisher) for a final concentration of 10 μM and 0.5 mM TCEP. 5000X Sypro Orange DMSO stock (Invitrogen) was diluted to 500X in assay buffer, and 1 μL of the dye was added to each well (final dye concentration = 25X).
