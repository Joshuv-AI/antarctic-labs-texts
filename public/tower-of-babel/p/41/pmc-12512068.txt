Defining Sex‐ and Age‐Specific Initial Stay Times for Continuous Moderate‐Intensity Work in Hot Environments Before Heat‐Mitigation Controls Are Implemented – Part A
Authors: Fergus K. O'Connor, Kristina‐Marie T. Janetos, Brodie J. Richards, Roberto C. Harris‐Mostert, Katie E. Wagar, Leonidas G. Ioannou, James J. McCormick, Robert D. Meade, Emily J. Tetzlaff, Ronald J. Sigal, W. Shane Journeay, Glen P. Kenny
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRationaleIndustry guidelines recommend work‐rest allocations to limit increases in core temperature (Tcore; > 38.0°C or > Δ1.0°C above resting) during work in the heat. However, the initial duration of permissible work before implementing these allocations is not specified (initial stay time [IST]) and individual factors such as sex and age affecting thermoregulation are not considered.ObjectivesThe objective of this study was to assess whether factors of sex and age influence IST.MethodsWe examined the permissible work duration before reaching Tcore of 38.0°C or Δ1.0°C above resting in young (18–30 years) and older (50–69 years) males and females performing moderate‐intensity (200 W/m2) simulated work at ambient conditions of 26, 29, and 32°C wet‐bulb globe temperature (WBGT).FindingsIST significantly decreased as ambient conditions intensified (p < 0.0001). Median (95% CI) durations to 38°C across groups were 59 (43–87) minutes at 26°C, 40 (34–65) minutes at 29°C, and 35 (31–46) minutes at 32°C WBGT. Likewise, median times to achieve Δ1.0°C above resting temperature significantly differed by environmental conditions (p < 0.0001): 66 (56–128) minutes at 26°C, 68 (54–80) minutes at 29°C, and 44 (41–53) minutes at 32°C WBGT. Older females were significantly more likely to experience Tcore elevations of 38.0°C (hazard ratio [95% CI]: (3.1 [1.6, 5.8], p < 0.001) and Δ1.0°C above resting (1.9 [1.1, 3.0], p = 0.007) compared to young males. In contrast, IST for young females and older males were similar to young males.ImpactOur findings underscore the impact of hotter ambient conditions on IST and identify older females as particularly susceptible during moderate‐intensity work in heat emphasizing the need for tailored occupational heat exposure guidelines.

Introduction

With climate change fueling an increase in the occurrence of hot weather, heat stress is a growing concern in the workplace as it endangers occupational health and safety [1]. Heat‐exposed workers are at an increased risk of impaired work performance, and heat‐related injuries and death [1, 2]. To reduce the health risks associated with occupational heat stress, workplaces rely upon the use of upper heat stress limits such as those provided by the American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH). These limits, which are defined by a worker's metabolic rate, the prevailing wet‐bulb globe temperature (WBGT), and protective clothing, aim to limit increases in core temperature of 1°C above an assumed pre‐job temperature of no greater than 37.5°C [3] for non heat‐acclimatized (i.e., Action Limits, AL) and heat acclimatized (Threshold Limit Values, TLV) individuals [3]. In instances where the heat load created by the combination of workload, thermal environment, and clothing worn exceed the upper heat stress limits (uncompensable heat stress), mitigation control measures such as work‐rest schedules are prescribed to limit excursions in core temperature beyond recommended limits in the ‘average’ individual [3].

While the ACGIH control measures are designed to protect most healthy, hydrated workers performing daylong work in the heat, they are largely defined on data stemming from young men [3, 4]. Yet, workplaces commonly apply these guidelines across a diverse workforce under the assumption that they provide equitable protection for all workers ‐ young to older males and females. This is an important consideration given individual characteristics such as sex and age modulate heat loss capacity [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10], which can limit heat tolerance during work in hot environments [11]; a response which is heat‐load dependent [9, 12, 13, 14]. As recently reported, irrespective of age (18–70 years), whole‐body heat loss is ~5% lower in females relative to males [12]. Further, whole‐body heat dissipation declines with increasing age in both males and females (~3%–4% per decade) with differences most evident at moderate‐to‐heavy intensity exercise [12]. Thus, relative to young adults, older adults may experience greater increases in core temperature for the same work effort, which is potentially worse in females, findings not considered in current work guidelines [15].

Recent work conducted by our laboratory explored the impact of these individual factors on tolerance to prolonged, moderate‐intensity work (metabolic rate: 200 W/m2) above and below limits for uncompensable heat stress [16, 17]. Based on the ACGIH ALs, unacclimatized workers wearing a single clothing layer (~1 Clo) should be able to work continuously for 8 h at a moderate metabolic rate (130–200 W/m2) without experiencing elevations in core temperature exceeding recommended limits (> 38.0°C [assuming a resting temperature of 37.0°C]) when the prevailing WBGT is ≤ 25°C [3]. We showed that factors of sex [17] or age [16] did not appreciably influence the validity of the ACGIH ALs, as neither sex nor age affected the proportion of individuals experiencing rises in core temperature exceeding recommended limits (> 38.0°C) when working below the ALs (16°C and 24°C WBGT). Further, no differences were observed for the work duration before core temperature exceeded 38.0°C (termed the safe initial stay time, see below) or tolerance time (i.e., work time (of the 180 min work bout) completed before volitional termination or core temperature exceeding 39.5°C) when performing moderate‐intensity work in conditions above the ALs (28°C and 32°C WBGT) [18, 19, 20]. While under the work and ambient conditions assessed, sex and age did not impact thermal strain, it is important to consider that, as noted above, sex‐ and age‐related differences in heat dissipation are heat‐load dependent [12, 13, 14]. Consequently, differences may be observed at higher work efforts or ambient conditions.

Currently, we possess limited insight on appropriate safe initial stay times before the recommended work‐rest schedule should be employed in conditions exceeding upper heat stress limits. Moreover, as evidenced in field observations [18, 19, 20] it is not feasible for many workplaces to implement work‐rest allocations at the commencement of the workshift. Nevertheless, work‐rest allocations are assumed to be protective and therefore maintain core temperature ≤ 38.0°C or Δ1°C from baseline resting irrespective of when they are implemented within the workshift (assuming work intensity and ambient conditions remain constant). Thus, information on the duration of work performed before the implementation of work‐rest allocations is particularly important for workers who, due to the nature of the work to be completed, time constraints, emergency response or other factors, are unable to implement and adhere to the recommended guidelines from the commencement of the workshift. We recently generated preliminary estimates of initial stay times for physically active, non heat‐acclimatized young‐to‐middle aged males and females (assessed at a WBGT of 28°C and 32°C) and a smaller number of young and older males (assessed at a WBGT of 26°C, 28°C, 30°C, and 32°C) [21] before work‐rest allocations are employed in environments above the ACGIH ALs upper heat stress limits (> 25°C WBGT) during moderate‐intensity work (metabolic rate: 200 W/m2) [21]. While we showed that sex and age did not influence initial stay times, there is a need to confirm these preliminary estimates for continuous, moderate‐intensity work [22]. Additionally, given that prior assessments on initial stay times for moderate‐intensity work was limited to young to middle‐aged females 18–45 years, it is important that we define initial stay times for older females who represent a growing segment of the workforce worldwide [23]. This includes extending estimates for initial stay times for heavy‐work [22] for both young and older males and females that currently do not exist (see companion paper [24]).

In the present study, we determined the initial stay times for young (18–30 years) and older (50–69 years) males and females for moderate‐intensity work (metabolic rate: 200 W/m2 [in our companion paper, we assess responses during heavy‐intensity continuous work [24]]). The older adult age range (50–69 years) was selected to represent a growing, yet underrepresented population at increased risk of heat‐related illness due to age‐related declines in thermoregulatory function [25]. Young adults (18–30 years) were included to enable direct age‐based comparisons. Indeed, an aging workforce [26] means that older workers (≥ 50 years) are increasingly required to perform physically demanding tasks [27], potentially placing this population sub‐group at heightened risk to the dangers of occupational heat exposure. Assessments were made in a range of ambient conditions exceeding the ACGIH ALs (i.e., WBGT of 26°C, 29°C and 32°C) at the start of the work shift. These environmental conditions reflect average (26°C WBGT) and peak (29°C WBGT) conditions recorded during the summer period in northern‐hemisphere continental climates [28], while 32°C WBGT represents conditions recorded during recent deadly heatwaves [21]. Employing these ambient conditions, our first aim was to confirm our preliminary estimates for young and older males at the upper and lower limits (i.e., WBGT of 26°C and 32°C) previously assessed [16], while validating the limit at a WGBT of 29°C. Similarly, we could confirm estimates for young females at a WBGT of 26°C and 32°C, while defining limits for older females for the first time at the lower and upper limits (i.e., WBGT of 26°C and 32°C). A secondary aim of our study was to provide preliminary guidance on exposure limits for when work must be extended beyond initial stay times under time constraints, such as during emergency response, or other situations where the implementation of heat‐alleviation controls in the form of rest breaks is limited or not possible.

Methods

This single‐site, laboratory‐based intervention trial was approved by the University of Ottawa Health Sciences and Science Research Ethics Board (H‐11‐22‐8660). The work was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, except for registration in a database. Written informed consent was obtained from all volunteers before participation.

Participants

Of the 80 volunteers screened for eligibility, 49 met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate. Participants were 24 healthy young adults (12 female, age: median (IQR) 23 (20–25) years, fitness (indexed by peak rate of oxygen consumption (V̇O2peak)): 44.3 (37.0–49.8) mL/kg/min and 25 healthy older adults (13 female, age: 63 (61–67) years, V̇O2peak: 32.6 (30.0–40.1) mL/kg/min) participated (Table 1). All participants were habitually active, non‐smokers and reported to have no history of cardiovascular, metabolic, or respiratory disorders. All participants were neither acclimatized nor engaged in occupations or activities where they were regularly exposed to heat stress.

Experimental Protocol

All participants completed one preliminary session, and three experimental sessions in a randomized order in environmental temperatures of 26°C, 29°C, and 32°C WBGT (equivalent to 34.1°C, 37.5°C, and 41.3°C, with 35% relative humidity, respectively). The experimental sessions were conducted on three separate days (minimum 72‐h between sessions) and were performed at the same time of day (within‐participant). Participants were instructed to refrain from exercise, alcohol, caffeine, and nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs for > 24 h, and were instructed to consume a light meal and 500 mL of water ~2 h before each session.

Preliminary Screening

During the preliminary screening visit, standing height, body mass, body surface area, body fat percentage, and V̇O2peak were determined. Upon arrival to the laboratory, body surface area was derived from measures of height (model 2391, Detecto) and body mass (IND560, Mettler Toledo Inc.) [29]. Body fat percentage was estimated using bioelectrical impedance analysis (MC‐780U, Tanita Worldwide, Arlington Heights). Thereafter, participants underwent a treadmill (Desmo Evo, Woodway) walking test to determine the speed and percent grade of the treadmill to achieve a moderate‐intensity workload (equivalent to a heat production of 200 W/m2), quantified using indirect calorimetry (MCD Medgraphics Ultima Series, MGC Diagnostics). After a period of rest, V̇O2peak was estimated, using an incremental treadmill exercise protocol to volitional fatigue [30].

Experimental Sessions

Upon arrival, participants provided a urine sample to confirm euhydration (urine specific gravity ≤ 1.025). After ~45‐min instrumentation in a temperate room (~22°C), where resting data were recorded (core (indexed by rectal temperature) and skin temperatures, heart rate, blood pressure), the participant's nude and fully clothed body weight (while wearing a T‐shirt, shorts, single‐layer cotton coveralls [100% cotton], socks and sport shoes [total insulation: ~1 Clo]) was then obtained. Thereafter, participants entered a climate‐controlled chamber with minimal airflow (< 0.2 m/s) regulated to one of three randomized environmental conditions at a WBGT of 26°C, 29°C, and 32°C (equivalent ambient air temperature of 34.1°C, 37.5°C, or 41.3°C, with all conditions performed at a fixed relative humidity (35%)). Participants rested for a 15‐min seated habituation period during which time baseline resting data were collected (core and skin temperatures, heart rate, and perceptual responses). Participants then commenced a simulated work shift (i.e., walking on a treadmill at a pre‐determined speed and grade) until their core temperature (i) reached 38°C or Δ1°C from baseline resting (whichever came last), (ii) until volitional fatigue, or (iii) 240‐min of exercise was completed. A 240‐min work period was chosen to replicate the duration of work typical in many occupations; such as the start of the work shift and post‐lunch resumption of work. Upon cessation of exercise, participants exited the climate‐controlled chamber and provided both a nude and fully clothed body weight, and a urine sample.

Measurements

Core and Skin Temperatures

Core (rectal) temperature was measured using a thermocouple temperature probe (Mon‐a‐therm General Purpose Temperature Probe; Mallinckrodt Medical Inc.) inserted at least 12 cm past the anal sphincter and was recorded every 15 s on an HP Agilent data acquisition module (model 3497A, Agilent Technologies Canada Inc.).

Skin temperature data were recorded continuously using temperature data loggers (DS1922L Thermochron, OnSolution Pty Ltd) affixed to eight body regions using double‐sided adhesives and medical tape. Mean skin temperature was calculated according to the weightings recommended in ISO 9886:2004: 7% forehead, 17.5% right scapula, 17.5% upper right chest, 7% upper right arm, 7% right forearm, 5% right hand, 19% right anterior thigh, and 20% right calf [31].

Cardiovascular Measurements

Heart rate was continuously measured throughout the experimental sessions using a Polar coded WearLink and transmitter (Polar M400 watch, Polar H7 WearLink; Polar Electro Oy). Systolic and diastolic arterial blood pressure were measured after 10‐min of seated rest during the baseline period (before entering the heat) and following the final 15 min of rest after reaching their initial stay time. Blood pressure was measured at the brachial artery in triplicate with a minute in between each measurement, via an automatic blood pressure cuff (Digital Blood Pressure Monitor UM‐211, A&D Medical). Heart rate responses were presented in absolute values as well as a percentage of the participant's estimated heart rate reserve (% HRR) calculated using the following equation: %HRR=HRt−HRblHRmax−HRbl100, where HR t is the heart rate recorded at a given time point (beats/min), HRbl is the heart rate recorded at baseline (beats/min), and HRmax is the participant's maximal heart rate taken from their maximal incremental treadmill exercise protocol during the participants preliminary screening session.

Urine Specific Gravity

Hydration status was assessed upon participant arrival to the laboratory, and at the end of exercise, by measuring urine specific gravity using a hand‐held total solid refractometer (Reichert TS 400 Total Solids Refractometer, Reichert Inc.).

Thermal Sensation

Thermal sensation was recorded at 15‐min intervals throughout exercise and at exercise termination utilizing a modified American Society of Heating, Refrigeration and Air‐Conditioning Engineers scale (0: neutral to 7: extremely hot) [32].

Rating of Perceived Exertion

Rating of perceived exertion (RPE) was recorded at 15‐min intervals throughout exercise and at cessation of exercise utilizing the Borg (6–20) scale [33].

Data Analysis

Deidentified participant data was examined for completeness and occurrence of outliers or erroneous datapoints. Subsequently, erroneous data were removed from individual data files and minute averages were calculated for all continuously measured variables (i.e., core and skin temperatures, and heart rate). In the 26°C WBGT condition there were six instances where participants completed 240‐min of simulated work without reaching a core temperature of 38°C (n = 4) or a Δ1°C above baseline resting (n = 2), these participants were included in the final analysis and treated as censored cases.

Missing Data

Due to technical difficulties, the time to 38°C was not recorded for one older male in the 26°C WBGT condition. Similarly, heart rate measurements were not recorded for one young female and one older male in the 32°C WBGT condition.

Statistical Analysis

To assess the influence of environmental conditions, sex and age on the time taken to reach a rectal temperature of 38.0°C, and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting these times were compared between groups (Young Males [reference group], Young Females, Older Males, Older Females), and conditions (26°C WBGT [reference condition], 29°C WBGT, 32°C WBGT) using Cox regression in the ClinicoPath [34] module in Jamovi (v.2.6.13). To evaluate differences in survival distributions across groups and conditions, log‐rank tests were conducted. When assessing responses across environmental conditions, identical methods to those described above were utilized accounting for repeated measures within individual using the “survival” [35] package in R [36]. To account for potential effects of physical fitness on the initial stay time, additional analysis identical to the methods described above, incorporating individual V̇O2peak as a continuous covariate was conducted. Participants who completed the 240‐min work period without reaching either 38.0°C, or Δ1.0°C above baseline resting, or withdrew from the work simulation due to volitional fatigue were included in respective Cox regression analyses as censored cases. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Secondary analysis was undertaken to assess whether heart rate (and heart rate reserve), mean skin temperature and perceptual responses (RPE, thermal sensation) at core temperatures of 38.0°C, or Δ1.0°C above baseline resting were influenced by group (Young Males [reference group], Young Females, Older Males, Older Females) or environmental conditions (26°C WBGT [reference condition], 29°C WBGT, 32°C WBGT) using linear‐mixed effects models, accounting for the individual as a random effect in the GAMLj package [37] in Jamovi.

Finally, to estimate the duration of work that could be performed before reaching core temperatures of 38.5°C, 39.0°C and 39.5°C, forecasting simulations were performed utilizing exponential triple smoothing [38]. Linear mixed effects models were utilized to assess the influence of group (Young Males [reference group], Young Females, Older Males, Older Females) on projected work duration, where environmental conditions (26°C WBGT [reference condition], 29°C WBGT, 32°C WBGT) were included as fixed effects, and the individual was included as a random intercept to account for repeated observations. The final 20‐min before reaching a rectal temperature of 38.0°C was used for forecasting analysis.

Initial Stay Time

The median (interquartile range) time to reach 38°C and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting across all groups and environmental conditions is displayed in Figure 1.

Median [interquartile range] time taken to reach rectal temperatures of 38.0°C and 1°C above baseline resting during continuous moderate intensity (200 W/m2) simulated work in ambient conditions of 26°C (A), 29°C (B) and 32°C WBGT (C).

Effect of Varying Environmental Conditions on the Time Taken to Reach a Core Temperature of 38°C, or 1°C Above Baseline Resting

Across all groups, the time taken to reach a core temperature of 38.0°C was significantly influenced by environmental conditions (log‐rank test: p = 0.002). The rate of experiencing a core temperature of 38.0°C was higher in 32°C WBGT (hazard ratio: 2.1 [95% confidence interval: 1.5, 3.0], p < 0.001), but not in the 29°C WBGT (1.4 [1.0, 1.9], p = 0.074) compared to 26°C WBGT (Figure 2), with the median (95% confidence interval) time to 38°C across groups being 59 (43, 87) min, 40 (34, 65) min and 35 (31, 46) min in the 26°C, 29°C and 32°C WBGT conditions, respectively.

Survival curves showing the proportion of participants terminating the 240‐min work bout upon reaching a core temperature of 38.0°C (A) and Δ1.0°C (B) above baseline resting during continuous moderate intensity (200 W/m2) simulated work in ambient conditions of 26°C, 29°C and 32°C WBGT.

Similarly, the time taken to reach a core temperature of Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was significantly influenced by environmental conditions (p < 0.0001). The rate of experiencing a core temperature of Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was higher in 32°C WBGT (3.6 [2.4, 5.3], p < 0.001), and the 29°C WBGT condition (1.8 [1.3, 2.5], p < 0.001) compared to 26°C WBGT, with the median (95% confidence interval) time to Δ1.0°C above baseline resting across groups being 66 (56, 128) min, 68 (54, 80) min and 44 (41, 53) min in the 26°C, 29°C and 32°C WBGT conditions, respectively.

Effect of Sex and Age on the Time Take to Reach a Core Temperature of 38°C, or Δ1°C Above Baseline Resting

Across all environmental conditions, the time taken for participants to reach 38.0°C core temperature was significantly influenced by group (p < 0.001). The likelihood of experiencing a core temperature of 38.0°C was higher in older females (3.1 [1.6, 5.8], p < 0.001), but not in young females (1.4 [0.7, 2.8], p = 0.292), or older males (1.1 [0.7, 1.9], p = 0.705) compared to young males. When accounting for physical fitness within the analysis, the time taken to reach a rectal temperature of 38.0°C was not different between young females (1.3 [0.7, 2.4], p = 0.414), older males (0.9 [0.5, 1.6], p = 0.414) compared to young males. However, the rate at which older females (2.2 [1.1, 4.2], p = 0.025) experienced elevations in core temperature of 38.0°C was greater than young males.

Similarly, across all environmental conditions, the time taken to reach a core temperature of Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was significantly influenced by group (p = 0.01). The rate of experiencing a core temperature of Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was higher in older females (2.0 [1.0, 3.9], p = 0.047), but not in young females (0.9 [0.5, 1.7], p = 0.818), or older males (1.1 [0.6, 2.1], p = 0.720) compared to young males. However, when accounting for physical fitness within the analysis, the time taken to reach a core temperature of Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was not different between young females (0.8 [0.4, 1.3], p = 0.116), older males (0.9 [0.5, 1.3], p = 0.353) and older females (1.2 [0.6, 2.6], p = 0.558) compared to young males.

When performing simulated moderate intensity work in environments of 26°C WBGT, the likelihood of experiencing a core temperature of 38°C and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was greater in older females compared to young males, but not different between young females, or older males and young males (Table 3). Similarly, in the 29°C WBGT condition, the likelihood of experiencing a core temperature of 38°C was greater in older females compared to young males, while the likelihood of experiencing a core temperature of Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was greater in older males and females compared to young males. In the 32°C WBGT condition, the likelihood of experiencing a core temperature of 38°C and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was not influenced by group.

Perceptual Responses to Simulated Work

While a main effect of condition existed such that the corresponding thermal sensation when core temperature was 38.0°C increased with higher ambient conditions, these responses were not influenced by group. Discussion

Our data provide the first detailed estimates of the duration of continuous moderate‐intensity work that can be completed by young to older males and females at the start of a work shift before exceeding industry‐guided safe work thresholds defined by increases in core temperature of 38.0°C or a Δ1.0°C above baseline resting, at which point work‐rest allocations should commence, across very warm to hot ambient conditions. Notably, we highlight the increased vulnerability of older female workers, who experience significantly reduced work durations compared to their younger male counterparts. Despite this, under the most extreme environmental conditions assessed, all groups experienced similar initial stay times, highlighting the physiological strain imposed when performing work tasks in environments of 32°C WBGT commonly experienced during extreme heat events in North America [21].

Previous work simulations studies from our laboratory have shown that the duration of continuous work that can be completed before reaching a core (rectal) temperature of Δ1.0°C above baseline resting in environmental conditions of 28°C and 32°C WBGT are not different between young (18–30 years) and older males with and without common chronic health conditions (type 2 diabetes, hypertension) [16, 39]. Similarly, no differences were observed between young‐to‐middle aged males (19–45 years) and females (18–43 years) [17]. Extending on these findings, the data in the current investigation show that the duration of work that can be completed in environmental conditions of 26°C, 29°C and 32°C WBGT are largely similar between young (21–24 years) and older (63–67 years) males, and young (20–28 years) females but was significantly reduced in older (60–65 years) females. Despite this, estimates of initial stay times in the current investigation are, in general, shorter compared to those proposed by Notley et al. [16, 17, 39]. The underlying reasons for the disparate findings remain unclear, but slight discrepancies in methodology may, in part, explain some of the incongruences. Most notably, the estimates of work intensity in the current investigation were derived during participant preliminary screening, where, following the completion of an incremental treadmill test to fatigue, and following an extended rest break (30–45 min), participants walked on a treadmill, where the speed and grade were adjusted until steady‐state metabolic heat production values (200 W/m2) were obtained for 10‐15 min. This is in contrast to the methodology used by Notley et al. [16, 17, 39], where metabolic heat production was obtained from participants in the initial 10‐min of their first experimental exposure, while wearing trial instrumentation including coveralls. Further to this, aside from examining stay time estimates within the 32°C WBGT condition of both studies, the ability to draw direct comparisons between studies is limited. Indeed, while Notley et al. [16, 17, 39] had participants undertake simulated work in ambient conditions of 28°C and 32°C WBGT, only a limited subset of individuals [30–40% of the participant cohort] completed simulated work in 26°C WBGT, and no individuals completed work when exposed to ambient conditions corresponding to 29°C WBGT. Thus, our data provide the most comprehensive estimates of initial stay times in a range of ambient conditions, inclusive of estimates for older females.

In accordance with our previous work that highlighted a reduction in the duration of work that could be completed before reaching a core temperature of 38.0°C with progressively challenging environmental conditions [16], the current data show that as ambient conditions increase, the likelihood of experiencing increases in core temperature > 38.0°C and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting significantly increases. Of interest, while the median time taken to reach a rectal temperatures to Δ1.0°C above baseline resting across groups appears similar between the 26°C and 29°C WBGT conditions (65 vs. 68 min), it is noteworthy that no participants were able to complete the full 4‐h work period in the 29°C condition. This is in contrast to the 26°C condition, where ~10% of the participant cohort were able to complete the full simulated work bout without experiencing elevations in core temperature > 38.0°C and/or Δ1.0°C above baseline resting. This notion can be further evidenced by the fact that at the ~90‐min mark of simulated work in the 29°C WBGT condition, 25% of the population cohort had not reached a rectal temperature of Δ1.0°C above baseline resting, in contrast, by the ~170 min mark the final 25% of the population cohort had not attained this rectal temperature threshold in the 26°C WBGT condition (Figure 2B). This appears to be largely a result of the increased likelihood of older males experiencing greater rates of core temperature increase compared to young males in the 29°C, but not 26°C WBGT condition (Table 3). Together, highlighting the potential susceptibility of older individuals, particularly as ambient conditions become progressively more challenging (i.e., > 26°C WBGT), confirming prior reports that age‐related differences are heat‐load dependent [9, 12, 13, 14].

Our data show that mean skin temperature, heart rate and heart rate reserve at rectal temperatures of 38.0°C and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting are largely influenced by environmental conditions. These findings are in accordance with Notley et al. [16], who showed marked increases in core temperature and heart rate following 60‐min of work as environmental conditions become progressively more challenging. While these findings are somewhat unsurprising, they together highlight the significant level of thermal strain elicited by progressively more challenging ambient conditions. Whether these findings remain consistent when assessed following multiple work to rest cycles and across daylong work shifts presents as an important area of future research.

Aging is associated with impairments in heat loss mechanisms of cutaneous vasodilation and sweating, which in turn exacerbates body heat storage and core temperature during exercise [12, 14]. Similarly, sex‐related differences in heat loss mechanisms indicate that females store more body heat for a given level of heat stress compared to their male counterparts [40, 41]. The additive effect of these interindividual factors likely underpins the significantly reduced initial stay times in older females in the current investigation. Indeed, older females largely experienced significantly reduced initial stay times compared to their young, male counterparts, an effect that was only mitigated in the 32°C WBGT condition. Of note, when accounting for physical fitness within the analysis, the rate at which older females experienced core temperature elevations of 38.0°C, but not Δ1.0°C above baseline resting, remained statistically significant. Indicating that the reduction in physical fitness, as well as other factors beyond fitness alone which characterized the older female cohort translated to significantly reduced work capacity. These findings are in support of previous mechanistic work, which shows that physical fitness is a key modulator of heat loss capacity [14] and likely demonstrate the marked impairments in the activation of heat loss responses observed in older females in both hot‐dry [42, 43] and hot‐humid conditions [43]. Further, highlighting the magnitude of physiological strain experienced by the older female cohort, despite the significantly decreased duration of continuous work that was permissible, heart rate reserve, mean skin temperature and perceptual responses to simulated work were largely comparable to those experienced by young males at the same core temperature thresholds. Altogether, these findings emphasize the rapid increase in physiological and psychometric strain experienced by this population group.

Obtaining perceptual responses to given work‐tasks have been suggested to provide an accurate estimate of physiological strain [44]. While our data show no group‐level differences in perception of effort at core temperatures of 38.0°C and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting, it is important to note that we also failed to observe an effect of the increasing environmental conditions. This finding was observed despite increases in heart rate, and heart rate reserve as ambient conditions increased, and further highlights incongruencies in how individuals perceive their actual level of physiological strain [45, 46, 47]. In contrast, however, our data show group‐level differences in thermal sensation at core temperatures of Δ1.0°C above baseline resting, indicating that older adults perceived their level of thermal stress at heightened core temperatures to be greater than their younger counterparts.

Of practical importance, the initial duration of work that was able to be completed before core temperatures reached an increase of 1.0°C above baseline resting in the current investigation far exceeded the recommended work duration before periods of rest are suggested to be implemented (Figure 1) within industry guidelines [3]. For example, when unacclimatized workers complete moderate‐intensity work, it is recommended that periods of work do not exceed 50–75% and 0–25% of the work‐shift in environmental conditions of 26°C and 29°C WBGT [3], respectively. However, our findings show that across all groups, when completing moderate intensity simulated work, the median duration taken to reach a rectal temperature of Δ1.0°C above baseline resting in environmental conditions of 26°C, 29°C and 32°C WBGT conditions was 66, 68 and 44 min, respectively. While it is not possible to draw conclusions on the possibility of individuals completing work for > 75% of the work shift even in the more extreme environmental conditions, these observations, in conjunction with findings reported by Notley et al. [16, 17, 39], highlight that current guidelines may be overprotective and limit worker productivity, at least for the initial period of work before the implementation of industry guided work‐rest schedules. Further, as noted above, under the assumption that core temperature has not exceeded recommended limits, work‐rest allocations are assumed to be protective and therefore maintain core temperature ≤ 38.0°C or no greater than a Δ1°C from baseline resting (assuming a resting core temperature of 37.0°C) irrespective of when they are implemented within the workshift (assuming work intensity and ambient conditions remain constant). As such, individuals may continue to perform work employing the recommended work‐rest allocations following the completion of the initial stay times. For example, following an initial continuous moderate‐intensity work bout at a WBGT of 26°C and 29°C, individuals may continue performing their duties while implementing the recommended work‐rest allocations of 50%‐75% and 0%‐25% respectively for the duration of the 4‐h work period. While additional work is not recommended at WBGT of 32°C, work may resume following an extended recovery in a cooled space to permit the recovery of core temperature back to pre‐job resting core temperature (i.e., < 37.5°C [3]; 75–90 min which is based on an estimated core temperature cooling rate of 0.012°C for a passive resting recovery following work in the heat) [48]. However, when possible, work should be restricted to a non‐heat stress environment while under supervision.

In many situations, initial work durations commonly exceed recommended limits [49] due to various factors such as job and time constraints related to completion of time‐sensitive tasks, emergency work, and others that limit their ability to implement heat mitigation in the form rest breaks [19, 20]. In some cases, incentive‐based contracts where individuals are remunerated on work‐production (i.e., crop‐harvesting [50], and mining [51]) can drive individuals to extend continuous work beyond recommended upper limits (i.e., > 38°C). Notably, a recent report showed that young to older adults with and without common chronic disease (type 2 diabetes, hypertension) can perform prolonged, continuous work in uncompensable heat stress (i.e., 32°C WBGT) as assessed during a 180‐min simulated work bout [16], with mean (SD) core temperatures reaching elevated values of up to 39.0 (0.4) °C. However, this can lead to fatigue [52], impaired work performance, reduced concentration, and increased risk of heat‐related illness if core temperature elevations remain unchecked and exceed these upper levels [49, 53]. Therefore, in an effort to provide guidance for individuals and workplaces in managing situations where work is extended beyond initial stay times, we provide preliminary estimates of the duration of work that can be completed before reaching core temperature thresholds of 38.5°C, 39.0°C, and 39.5°C in a range of ambient conditions (Table 5). In situations where it is not possible to implement industry‐guided work‐rest schedules following initial stay times, workforces can use these projected estimates to effectively manage workers during these work scenarios. When work is extended beyond the recommended limits (i.e., > 38°C) workers should be constantly monitored for signs that suggest a risk of illness due to heat stress. If the continuation of work leads to an estimated increase in core temperature of 38.5°C, we recommend individuals recover in a cooled space (22°C and relative humidity of 45%; equivalent WBGT of ~17°C) for ~90–105 min thereafter while continuing to hydrate. This duration of recovery is estimated to reduce core temperature back to pre‐job resting core temperature (i.e., < 37.5°C [3]), while also reducing potential fatigue‐related reductions in work ability [54, 55] before the resumption of work is recommended. As an example, for moderate‐intensity work performed at a WBGT of 26°C and 29°C, the recommended work‐rest allocation are 50–75% and 0–25% respectively, while no further work would be advised for a WBGT of 32°C. However, the amount of time available to perform additional work would be limited for both conditions. Based on a projected initial continuous work time of 118 min at 29°C WBGT for a young male (Table 5), additional work time would be limited following a 105‐min recommended recovery in a cool space, as total time (~223 min) would approach the maximum work period of 4‐h. In contrast, with a lower estimated work time of 91 min, an older male would have an additional 44 min to complete work, albeit still limited based on the recommended work‐rest allocation. Thus, while resumption of work in the heat is possible, due to the limited available time, it may not be feasible for workers to resume activities in the heat. In this situation, individuals could perform light duties continuously in a cooler location for the duration of the 4‐h work period. This would also ensure the worker is well‐rested before the resumption of work such as following a lunch break.

When extending the duration of continuous work results in an increase in core temperature of 39°C, recovery should be extended to ~105–120 min in a cooled space. A longer recovery should be considered for older workers, and individuals with a reduced level of fitness [12, 54, 56, 57]. Although no further work is recommended for any of the conditions thereafter, workers may perform light duties for the duration of the work shift while supervised in a non‐heat stress environment when possible. Additionally, workers should be encouraged to hydrate regularly. In this scenario, resumption of moderate‐ or higher‐intensity work is not recommended for the duration of the workday due to the fact that workers may experience excessive fatigue and dehydration [19, 20] following prolonged, physically demanding work, leading to impairments in thermoregulation [58], reductions in work capacity and cognitive function [49, 53] as well an elevated risk of a heat‐related injury [58]. In all cases, if work is to extend beyond recommended limits of an equivalent core temperature increase of 1°C of pre‐job resting levels (or 38.0°C; assuming pre‐job core temperature of ≤ 37.5°C [3]), individuals should be supervised and workplaces should consider implementing additional heat mitigation practices, such as wearable cooling garments, which dependent on garment type, may provide thermal relief during work bouts [2]. In all situations, it is important that workers are monitored and assessed for any signs and symptoms of heat‐related illness or other health problems, especially before departing the workplace.

While preliminary guidance for recovery following extended periods of continuous work has been provided, it is limited to recovery in a cooled space (~17°C WBGT) and does not consider the individual variations in heat loss capacity due to sex, age [14, 59] and other factors that might affect optimal recovery times [54, 56, 57]. Additionally, while work durations which are projected to induce a core temperature of 39.5°C have been provided, these should only serve to assist individuals and workplaces to identify durations of continuous work that would be unsafe and result in intolerable levels of heat strain that could place workers at a greater risk of work‐ and heat‐related injury [58, 60]. Individuals and workplaces must avoid extending work times that would increase core temperature above 39°C.

Limitations

While this study provides valuable insights into the physiological and perceptual responses to heat stress under varying environmental conditions, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study used a climate‐controlled chamber to regulate environmental conditions, which, while ensuring consistency in exposure between participants, may not fully replicate the dynamic environmental and occupational conditions experienced in real‐world settings. Factors such as wind speed, solar radiation, and varying humidity levels (which can all vary significantly across worksites) were not accounted for, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to outdoor work scenarios. Second, the continuous nature of work performed in the current investigation may not be wholly reflective of the often intermittent nature of moderate‐intensity work in the field [19, 20, 61]. Third, the study included a relatively small sample of young and older males and females, which may not be wholly representative of occupational workforces. Fourth, core temperature was measured using a rectal thermocouple probe, which, although reliable, may not capture rapid fluctuations in deep body temperature, which may influence work tolerance and motivation to take short‐intermittent breaks from work. Fifth, participants wore standardized, fully encapsulating coveralls (~1 Clo) during simulated work, and thus findings may not be translatable to industries where other personal protective equipment is mandated. Sixth, while we recommend that work‐rest allocations may be applied following initial stay times, which is supported by recent preliminary findings, the verification of initial stay times in combination with industry‐guided work‐rest allocations in field‐based settings is required. Seventh, while guidance for recovery following extended periods of continuous work has been provided, these times have not been validated. Future studies are required to verify optimal recovery times that should be introduced following an initial extended work bout to enable workers to perform their duties safely in the heat. Finally, an a priori power calculation was not undertaken. As such, the analyses may be underpowered to detect certain group differences, where age was categorized into two distinct groups: young adults (18–30 years) and older adults (50–69 years). This categorical approach was selected to align with established differences in thermoregulatory function across the lifespan and to ensure meaningful group comparisons. Thus, our findings do not reflect discrepancies in thermoregulatory capacity across the lifespan (e.g., middle‐aged adults compared to young adults). To this point, we acknowledge that our data are representative of the individuals included within our cohorts, where within‐ and between‐group variability in heat tolerance has been observed (Table 5). Our findings should be interpreted with these points in mind, considering that the information provided should be used to promote informed decision‐making, and not as a replacement for the clinical assessment of heat stress within the workplace.

Conclusion

Our data provide the first estimates of the duration of permissible moderate‐intensity continuous work at the commencement of the work shift in young and older males and females across a range of hot environmental conditions. We herein highlight the increased level of vulnerability within older female workers, particularly during the initial stages of simulated work bouts. Notably, however, while our data show significant between‐ and within‐group variability, median initial work estimates were not different between young and older males, and young females. The information provided within this manuscript can be used by those in the field to guide the implementation of continuous moderate‐intensity work periods that can be performed before the implementation of industry‐guided work‐rest allocations. The information presented in this document is intended to serve as a guidance tool to promote informed decision making and should not replace the clinical assessment or advice of an on‐site occupational health specialist. Users should always verify information with appropriate authorities and seek professional advice for specific situations.
