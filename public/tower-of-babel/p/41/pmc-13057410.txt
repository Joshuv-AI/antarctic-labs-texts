Introduction

Renin‐secreting tumors (reninomas) are rare functional neoplasms arising from juxtaglomerular cells of the afferent arteriole, capable of producing excessive renin and subsequently activating the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system. They represent a potentially curable cause of secondary hyperaldosteronism, typically presenting with severe hypertension, hypokalemia, and metabolic alkalosis. Fewer than 200 cases have been reported worldwide, with a higher prevalence in females, and most diagnoses occur in young women [1]. Although renin–angiotensin system inhibitors may offer temporary blood pressure control, nephron‐sparing surgical intervention remains the definitive and preferred therapeutic approach.

We describe a case of severe hypertension in a young woman with clinical features suggestive of secondary hypertension, in whom a reninoma could not be identified on initial diagnostic evaluation. The tumor became detectable only one year later as it increased in size, allowing for a definitive diagnosis. The patient subsequently underwent successful tumor enucleation—distinct from previously reported cases that were predominantly managed with partial nephrectomy.

Clinical History and Examination

A 27‐year‐old woman was referred for evaluation due to chest discomfort and fatigue. She had a one‐year history of hypertension, hypokalemia, and markedly elevated plasma renin levels. However, she did not exhibit any symptoms typically associated with hypokalemia, such as muscle weakness, cramps, or paralysis. An extensive workup for secondary hypertension performed one year earlier included normal cortisol and ACTH concentrations, normal 24‐h urinary catecholamines, a plasma aldosterone level of 14.4 ng/dL (within the reference range), and a markedly elevated plasma renin concentration > 500 μIU/mL. Abdominal computed tomography revealed no focal renal abnormalities, and renal artery Doppler ultrasound was unremarkable. She was managed with nifedipine LA 30 mg twice daily and potassium chloride 500 mg nine tablets per day, with regular outpatient follow‐up. There was no family history of hypertension or endocrine disease.

On admission, the blood pressure was 160/100 mmHg in the right arm and 155/90 mmHg in the left arm while supine, with a heart rate of 98 beats per minute. Her height was 155 cm, weight 52 kg, and body mass index 21.6 kg/m2. Physical examination revealed no features suggestive of endocrine disorders, including moon facies, facial plethora, supraclavicular fat pads, striae, hirsutism, vascular bruits, exophthalmos, abdominal bruits, or focal neurological deficits.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Laboratory evaluation revealed normal hemoglobin (151 g/L) and preserved renal function (serum creatinine 45 μmol/L). Arterial blood gas analysis showed no metabolic alkalosis (pH 7.433, bicarbonate 28 mmol/L). The patient had significant hypokalemia (2.5 mmol/L), and 24‐h urinary electrolytes demonstrated renal potassium wasting (127.7 mmol/24 h), as values > 15 mmol/day are indicative of renal potassium loss. Urinalysis showed proteinuria (1.5 g/L) and microscopic hematuria (150 red blood cells/μL).

Supine plasma renin concentration was markedly elevated at 3180 μIU/mL (reference range 2.8–39.9 μIU/mL), approximately 80‐fold above the upper limit of normal, accompanied by increased plasma aldosterone at 52 ng/dL (reference range 1.17–23.6 ng/dL). Biochemical evaluation for alternative endocrine etiologies, including urinary catecholamine metabolites, thyroid function tests, ACTH, and cortisol, was within normal limits.

Ophthalmoscopy, chest radiography, and electrocardiography were unremarkable. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular ejection fraction without features suggestive of coarctation of the aorta. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed no renal parenchymal abnormalities, focal masses, or asymmetry in renal size. Renal artery Doppler ultrasound was also normal.

Several conditions should be considered in the differential diagnosis of hypertension associated with elevated renin and aldosterone levels. The most common cause is renovascular hypertension due to renal artery stenosis. Other potential etiologies include renin‐producing renal tumors, such as renin‐secreting renal cell carcinoma or Wilms tumor. Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn syndrome) should also be considered; however, this condition is typically characterized by elevated aldosterone levels with suppressed renin. In our patient, renovascular hypertension was excluded by renal Doppler ultrasonography. Moreover, the markedly elevated plasma renin concentration accompanied by increased aldosterone levels was inconsistent with Conn syndrome, which typically presents with high aldosterone and low renin levels.

Abdominal magnetic resonance imaging subsequently identified a focal lesion within the mid‐pole anterior parenchyma of the left kidney measuring 12 × 9 mm. The lesion demonstrated restricted diffusion on DWI/ADC sequences but was isointense to the surrounding renal cortex on all other sequences, both before and after gadolinium administration (Figure 1). Mild narrowing of both renal arteries was noted without evidence of hemodynamically significant stenosis.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrating the renal tumor.

A follow‐up contrast‐enhanced abdominal computed tomography scan revealed a well‐circumscribed cortical nodule in the mid‐anterior renal parenchyma measuring 10 × 11.5 mm (Figure 2). The mass displayed smooth margins, poor early enhancement, and clear demarcation from the pelvicalyceal system. A branch of the anterior superior segmental artery coursed adjacent to the lesion, with no identifiable intralesional feeding vessel. The mass caused mild displacement of a nearby renal vein while maintaining a distinct interface with surrounding structures.

Computed tomography scan showing the renal tumor.

Given the clinical presentation and biochemical profile demonstrating marked hyperreninemia with secondary hyperaldosteronism, the focal mid‐pole lesion in the left renal parenchyma was suspected to represent a renin‐secreting tumor (reninoma). However, functional confirmation was required, as the renal lesion could represent another tumor type, while renin hypersecretion might arise from an extrarenal source, such as the ovary or lung. In addition, surgical treatment of renal tumors frequently involves partial or radical nephrectomy, which may compromise renal function. Therefore, renal vein sampling (RVS) was performed to confirm that the identified renal lesion was the source of renin overproduction.

RVS was performed according to the protocol described by Wolley et al. [2], with blood samples obtained from both renal veins and the inferior vena cava at baseline and 15 min after administration of oral captopril 25 mg (Figure 3). Prior to captopril stimulation, the renin concentration ratio between the left and right renal veins was 1.06 (< 1.5), which was insufficient to demonstrate lateralized renin secretion. Following captopril administration, however, the renin ratio increased markedly to 3.97 (> 1.5), indicating autonomous renin production from the left kidney and relative suppression of renin secretion from the contralateral side (Table 1).

The RVS procedure was discussed in detail with the patient, and informed consent was obtained. Antihypertensive therapy with nifedipine LA 30 mg twice daily was discontinued for at least two weeks and replaced with diltiazem 60 mg four times daily and doxazosin 2 mg once daily. Potassium chloride supplementation was increased to 500 mg, fifteen tablets per day, to correct hypokalemia. Blood pressure stabilized at approximately 130/80 mmHg, and serum potassium improved to 3.7 mmol/L. A low‐sodium diet (< 40 mmol/day) was implemented for four days prior to the procedure in accordance with recommended RVS preparation protocols.

A multidisciplinary conference involving endocrinology, urologic surgery, and radiology specialists concluded that surgical excision of the lesion was indicated. Intraoperative ultrasonography was performed to assess the feasibility of tumor removal and to delineate the margins (Figure 4). The patient subsequently underwent successful tumor enucleation with preservation of the surrounding renal parenchyma.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

In the early postoperative period, her blood pressure normalized without the need for antihypertensive medication, and her hypokalemia fully resolved without further potassium supplementation.

Gross examination of the excised specimen revealed a well‐circumscribed, darkly pigmented tumor measuring 12 × 10 mm, localized within the renal cortex. As shown in Figure 5, histologic evaluation demonstrated a subcapsular neoplasm with sharply defined borders but lacking a true capsule. The tumor was composed of sheets and trabeculae of round to polygonal cells with centrally placed, pale nuclei and eosinophilic cytoplasm, with indistinct cell borders. Immunohistochemical staining supported the diagnosis of reninoma, showing positive membranous expression of CD34 and vimentin, and negative staining for CD117, cytokeratin, PAX8, and β‐catenin.

At one month postoperatively, the patient's blood pressure was well controlled at 110/70 mmHg, and her serum potassium level had normalized to 4.5 mmol/L without any pharmacologic therapy. Plasma renin concentration measured 4.6 μIU/mL, and plasma aldosterone was 7.7 ng/dL, both within the normal reference range.

Discussion

Renin‐secreting tumors are an uncommon cause of secondary hypertension. The most frequent clinical manifestations include headache, nausea, polyuria, and nocturia; however, approximately 10% of patients may remain asymptomatic, presenting only with hypertension [3]. Our patient, a 27‐year‐old woman, presented with severe hypertension and profound hypokalemia. A diagnostic evaluation performed one year earlier had failed to identify an underlying etiology; however, markedly elevated plasma renin and aldosterone levels suggested secondary hyperaldosteronism driven by excessive renin secretion. Reninomas typically exhibit striking elevations in circulating renin and aldosterone, often exceeding tenfold the upper limit of normal. Consistent with this pattern, our patient demonstrated a plasma renin concentration > 500 μIU/mL at initial testing.

Small reninomas may be radiographically occult, as occurred in this case, where no renal lesion was detectable on imaging one year earlier. In a series of 10 patients with juxtaglomerular cell tumors, the lesion was initially missed on CT in 4 cases, and in one patient the tumor was entirely undetectable on CT [4]. On computed tomography, reninomas typically appear hypo‐ to isodense on non‐contrast imaging, with poor early enhancement and mild delayed enhancement after contrast administration—features that help distinguish them from other renal tumors [5], and which were observed in our patient. Benign renal cysts may mimic reninomas radiographically; moreover, certain renal neoplasms, such as nephroblastoma or renal cell carcinoma, can also produce renin, although their clinical biochemical profiles are generally less pronounced [6]. In our case, the lesion became detectable only after interval growth over the subsequent year.

RVS was performed following established protocols. Medications interfere with the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, including diuretics, β‐blockers, ACE inhibitors, and ARBs, were discontinued 2–4 weeks before the procedure. Agents that do not significantly affect renin levels, such as diltiazem and doxazosin, were continued. Serum potassium was corrected, and a very low‐sodium diet (< 40 mmol/day) was advised for four days prior to sampling. Supine posture was maintained overnight and on the day of the procedure, and stress was minimized [7]. Intravenous enalaprilat stimulation could not be performed because the agent was unavailable at our institution; instead, oral captopril was used as an accepted alternative [7].

Renin secretion may be stimulated during RVS using intravenous enalaprilat (0.04 mg/kg, maximum 2.5 mg) or oral captopril (25 mg), followed by sampling of renal veins and peripheral blood before and 20 min after stimulation. A lateralization ratio ≥ 1.5 provides 56% sensitivity and 94% specificity for identifying the affected kidney [3, 7]. The modest sensitivity likely reflects variable venous drainage patterns in reninomas, which may not always drain directly into the main renal vein [3]. In our case, baseline renin levels in the renal veins and peripheral blood showed no meaningful gradient, potentially due to subcapsular venous drainage or catheter displacement during sampling. After captopril stimulation, however, the renin ratio increased to 3.9 (> 1.5), enabling definitive localization of the functional tumor.

Surgical excision remains the primary treatment for reninoma, as these tumors are typically benign [1]. Depending on tumor depth, reninomas may be removed by nephron‐sparing partial nephrectomy when superficial or by radical nephrectomy when deeply situated [3]. Preoperative planning in our case initially favored left radical nephrectomy due to the lesion's proximity to the renal hilum. However, intraoperative ultrasonography provided sufficient anatomic clarity to permit successful tumor enucleation while preserving renal parenchyma.

Histopathological analysis is the gold standard for confirming reninoma, as certain malignant renal tumors may also secrete renin [8]. Definitive diagnosis was established by histopathology, and no adjuvant therapy, such as chemotherapy or radiation, was required following complete tumor excision.

Conclusion

Reninoma is a rare cause of hypertension and may remain undetectable on initial imaging studies. Repeat diagnostic evaluation is warranted when biochemical suspicion persists. In selected cases, intraoperative ultrasonography is essential for precise localization, allowing minimally invasive tumor enucleation to be performed instead of partial or radical nephrectomy.
