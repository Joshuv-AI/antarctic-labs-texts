Parvovirus B19 rebound outbreak 2024 and implications for blood‐ and plasma‐product safety
Authors: Maria R Farcet, Michael Karbiener, Claudia Aberham, Nicholas Powers, Daniel Aue, Thomas R Kreil
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSince the beginning of 2024, several European countries reported unusually high numbers of Human parvovirus B19 (B19V) infections. INTRODUCTION

Human parvovirus B19 (B19V) is a small, non‐enveloped DNA virus that is mainly transmitted via the respiratory route. It is endemic worldwide and causes mostly asymptomatic infections or fifth disease in children with usually mild illness, which is believed to result in lifelong immunity. B19V infection in pregnancy can cause miscarriage, and infection of people with immunodeficiencies or with chronic hematological disease may result in chronic infection or anemia, including life‐threatening conditions.

Since the beginning of 2024, several European countries reported unusually high numbers of B19V infections, particularly in pregnant woman and young children. 1 Since B19V infections are not routinely monitored in several European countries nor are under effective EU surveillance, there is limited availability of large datasets for analysis of this outbreak and subsequent risk evaluation.

As B19V can occur in blood plasma of entirely asymptomatic donors at particularly high titers, the plasma‐derived medicinal products industry has implemented B19V nucleic acid testing (NAT) of plasma donations to eliminate high‐titered units. 2 As millions of liters of plasma are collected to produce plasma‐derivatives, this testing can provide a large numbers basis to solidify epidemiological observations of increased B19V circulation after the COVID‐19 pandemic and to provide for additional demographic insights, both of which will be helpful for risk evaluations.

STUDY DESIGN AND METHODS

B19V nucleic acid testing of plasma donations

Plasma donations collected between June 2018 and May 2024 from mainly Central European countries (i.e., Austria, Czech Republic, Hungary; n = 9.6 million) and the United States (n = 70.7 million) were subjected to B19V NAT at minipool level of 96 or 480 donations and, if reactive, resolved to the individual donation level. Testing was done using a fully validated assay (Roche cobas® DPX) with an upper limit of quantification of 109 IU/mL. Donations were reported as “positive” when the viral load exceeded 105 IU/mL and were discarded to ensure that plasma pools will not exceed 104 IU/mL B19V DNA. 2

Graphs and statistical analysis

Graphical illustrations were done in GraphPad Prism v9.2.0 software.

RESULTS

Epidemiology of B19V from June 2018 to May 2024

The B19V incidence of Central European plasma donations was on average 0.006% from June 2018 to February 2020, that is, in the time before the COVID‐19 pandemic. After the WHO declaration of a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) in spring 2020, social distancing measures were enforced to varying degrees until the PHEIC status was lifted again in May 2023. 3 During the PHEIC period from March 2020 to April 2023, the average plasma donation B19V positivity rate was 0.002%, that is, approximately 3‐fold lower than pre‐PHEIC, with no B19V positive donations collected for 19 months, from June 2020 to January 2022. In the post‐PHEIC period from May 2023 to May 2024, average plasma donation positivity increased to 0.06%, that is, 10‐fold higher than in the pre‐PHEIC period. The incidence markedly increased from November 2023 onwards (average positivity 0.11% till May 2024) and peaked at 0.2% in April 2024, that is, 33‐fold higher than the average B19V incidence before the COVID‐19 pandemic (Figure 1).

Incidence of parvovirus B19 nucleic acid positive plasma donations collected in Central Europe by year and month, June 2018 to May 2024 (n = 9.6 million).

The average B19V positivity rate of plasma collected by plasmapheresis in the United States was similar to the incidence rate in Central Europe in the pre‐PHEIC period (0.005%) but dropped to a 10‐fold lower average of 0.0005% during the PHEIC period, with a slower and less pronounced B19V rebound to an average of 0.01% positivity in the post‐PHEIC period from May 2023 onwards. Like in Europe, an increase in B19V incidence was seen during the winter/spring season of 2023/2024, and markedly so from March 2024 onwards (0.02%) to reach 0.04% at the end of the survey period in May 2024, that is, a 4‐fold increase as compared to the pre‐PHEIC period, yet still 6‐fold lower than the situation in Europe at the same time (data not shown).

Demographic details of the current B19V rebound outbreak

More detailed information on plasma donors could only be evaluated for the United States. When the age of B19V positive plasma donors was compared for the pre‐pandemic (June 2018–May 2019) donations versus the post‐PHEIC period (June 2023 till May 2024), in cohorts of 10 years starting at 18, a disproportional 3.3‐fold increase in B19V positivity was seen for the youngest age cohort of 18–27 year‐old plasma donors, that is, pre‐PHEIC average of 0.005% versus post‐ average of 0.016%. For all other age cohorts, the B19V positivity rate increased by ~2‐fold or less (Figure 2).

Incidence of parvovirus B19 nucleic acid positive plasma donations analyzed for plasma donor age cohorts. Plasma was collected in the United States during the pre‐pandemic (June 2018 to May 2019) and in the post‐PHEIC period (June 2023 to May 2024) (n = 24.8 million).

Virological aspects of the 2024 B19V rebound outbreak

Levels beyond the 109 IU/mL B19V NAT quantification limit were determined in US plasma donations in the pre‐ as well as in the post‐PHEIC period, and extrapolated values reached levels of up to ~2.5 × 1012 IU/mL (data not shown). As only healthy persons are eligible for plasma donation, these highly infectious units were obtained from asymptomatic donors, for whom B19V infection was detected only through the in‐process B19V NAT screening prior to plasma pooling for manufacture. These levels are quite similar to those earlier reported. 4

DISCUSSION

Usually, B19V circulates at a rather low incidence, and thus few transmissions occur via transfusion of labile blood components, and none by pathogen‐reduced, industrially manufactured plasma‐derivatives since the introduction of B19V NAT. B19V NAT screening of plasma donations collected in Central Europe confirms, with a sample size of more than 9 million and resolution to the single plasma donation level, that we currently witness a “rebound outbreak” (2019 vs. 2024) of B19V in healthy adults, with an up to 33‐fold increased B19V incidence (Figure 1). Similar work was recently reported from France, where B19V NAT plasma pool testing also indicated a marked increase in B19V incidence pre‐ versus post‐COVID‐19. 5 The present study supports this data from French blood donors and provides an extension, as plasma donations from other European countries and the United States were analyzed. The B19V rebound infections are most probably due to the relatively short pre‐pandemic B19V exposure time in the young, and thus proportionally to their life span larger immunization gap, as evidenced by the disproportionally larger 3.3‐fold increase in B19V positivity in the youngest plasma donor cohort (Figure 2). The 2023/2024 B19V rebound outbreak occurred nearly 2 years after most of the social distancing measures were lifted, raising the question as to why this unusually high B19V incidence did not already occur in 2022/2023. B19V infections usually peak in spring till early summer, a pattern which was also seen by B19V NAT screening of plasma donations for 2018–2022, except in 2021 (Figure 1). In 2023 however, B19V incidence did not decline after the summer and in the autumn months, which could have driven the rebound outbreak to unprecedented magnitude. Plasma for fractionation is also routinely tested for the presence of other viruses (Hepatitis A, B, and C virus; Human immunodeficiency virus). For those, a similar increase in incidence as observed for B19V was not detected, possibly due to the different infection routes.

While plasma donations for fractionation are routinely tested for B19V by NAT, this is not generally the case for blood products for direct transfusion, nor for blood components used in the manufacture of advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs; e.g., human platelet lysate used in cell therapies). Particularly for higher risk transfusion recipients (pregnant, immunosuppressed and patients with hemopoietic diseases), however, the currently increased risk of B19V transfusion transmission, with a 33‐fold increased incidence rate in Central Europe, may suggest consideration of B19V NAT at least during this higher risk period. Alternatively, as younger plasma donors are currently disproportionately affected by B19V infections (Figure 2), advanced age blood donors who carry a higher chance of B19V antibody‐positivity 6 or selection of donors who test positive for B19V IgG antibodies for at least 6 months 7 may be prioritized for higher risk recipients of non‐inactivated blood products.

For plasma‐derivatives, all donated units are subject to B19V NAT even before the dedicated virus clearance processes during manufacture contribute further safety margins. 8 , 9 , 10 This is, however, not the case in all geographies, 11 a fact that may deserve additional consideration in the context of a rebound outbreak as currently ongoing, particularly in Europe.

Finally, in case of an unfortunate clinically relevant B19V exposure, immunoglobulins are known to contain highly potent virus neutralization capacity, 12 have previously been used to successfully treat infection 13 , 14 , 15 and therefore may—while not clinically proven—represent a therapeutic option.
