Effect of metasurface and process parameters on adhesion during transfer printing using a nanoscratch test
Authors: YeonKyeong Park, Dae-Geun Choi, Joo-Yun Jung, Jun-Hyuk Choi, Eun-chae Jeon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransfer printing of metasurface has emerged as a promising technique for realising ultra-high-resolution displays beyond the limits of conventional patterning methods. Reliable transfer requires precise control of adhesion at the interfaces between the metasurface and transfer mold/transfer substrate. Here, we quantitatively investigate adhesion variations at interfaces without or with metasurface, as well as the effects of transfer printing parameters such as applied pressure and temperature. Four paired transfer molds and transfer substrates were fabricated for simulating actual process conditions, and adhesion forces were measured independently using nanoscratch tests. We find that, compared to the specimen without metasurface, adhesion to transfer mold with metasurface is slightly reduced due to the formation of dimples, while adhesion to the transfer substrate with metasurface is significantly decreased resulting from void formation. Application of 5 bar pressure enhances adhesion at both interfaces. In contrast, increasing the temperature to 90 °C decreases adhesion at the transfer mold due to thermally induced residual stresses whereas the transfer substrate adhesion remains nearly unchanged as APS1 activation and residual stress effects counterbalance. Based on the adhesion difference between paired transfer molds and transfer substrates, the optimal conditions of transfer printing are identified as 5 bar and 90 °C. These results elucidate the mechanisms governing adhesion in transfer printing of metasurface and provide guidelines for optimising transfer printing for next-generation ultra-high-resolution displays.Keywords: Adhesion, Metasurface, Transfer printing, Nanoscratch testSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Optics and photonics

Introduction

With the advancement of image processing and data compression technologies, the production and consumption of content in the range of 4 K to 8 K resolution have increased, driving the growing demand for ultra-high-resolution displays1–3. To enhance the immersive experience of high-resolution content, it is essential to secure not only high pixel density per unit area but also a sufficiently large display size. Consequently, growing attention has been paid to display technologies capable of achieving both ultra-high resolution and large-area realization simultaneously. Among representative display types including liquid crystal displays (LCDs), quantum-dot light-emitting displays (QLEDs), and organic light-emitting diode (OLED) displays, OLEDs have attracted significant research interest due to their outstanding optical properties, such as a wide color gamut, self-emission, and high contrast ratio4–7. In conventional micro-OLED fabrication processes, patterned organic emissive layers are formed via deposition process using a fine metal mask (FMM). Since maintaining uniform hole sizes becomes increasingly difficult as the hole size decreases and a minimum rib thickness must be secured, the FMM is limited in its applicability to ultra-high-resolution displays8,9. To overcome the resolution limitations inherent in conventional processes, nanoimprinting10,11 and transfer printing12,13, which offer exceptionally high pattern resolution, have emerged as a particularly prominent alternative fabrication technique and has attracted significant attention.

Nanoimprinting is a representative microfabrication technique capable of producing high-resolution nanopatterns over large areas without the need for complex lithographic equipment14,15. However, nanoimprinting is mainly applicable to deformable materials such as polymers or a few metals16. To complement these limitations, transfer printing has been utilized together. Transfer printing is a process that transfers target material deposited on a transfer mold onto a transfer substrate, maintaining the advantages of nanoimprinting such as high patterning resolution, process simplicity, and large area scalability while enabling the transfer of metals, ceramics, or polymers without deformation17. Owing to these advantages, transfer printing has been actively studied as a fabrication technology for forming nanostructures required in micro-OLED manufacturing17,18. Transfer printing enables not only simple pattern transfer but also the precise transfer of optically functional metasurface. Metasurface can control light on a pixel scale by modulating the phase and amplitude of emitted light, which is particularly advantageous for ultra-high-resolution realization19,20. The metasurface is typically fabricated using highly reflective metals such as silver21,22, aluminum23, and gold24, and it is detached from the transfer mold and then attached to the transfer substrate during transfer printing. If its adhesion to the transfer mold is excessively strong, it may remain on the transfer mold without transferring to the substrate25, whereas its insufficient adhesion to the transfer substrate may cause delamination of the transferred metasurface, leading to degradation of its intended functionality. Therefore, the adhesion of the transfer substrate should exceed that of the transfer mold, and the adhesion difference between them must be sufficiently large to ensure stable and uniform transfer printing26. The adhesion of the transfer mold and transfer substrate changes depending on the applied temperature27 and pressure28, and the non-flat geometry of the metasurface can also cause variations in adhesion.

To accurately understand and control such complex interfacial adhesion behavior, various analytical approaches have been proposed in previous studies. These investigations have mainly relied on theoretical or numerical modeling based on fracture mechanisms and macroscopic experimental techniques to evaluate interfacial adhesion. However, fracture-mechanics-based approaches generally assume an ideal and flat surface29, making it difficult to accurately describe the interfacial behavior of non-planar structures such as metasurface. Furthermore, most of these models adopt simplified loading conditions30, and thus fail to fully account for the complex stress states and damage mechanisms that can arise during the service or post-processing conditions of transfer printed devices. Meanwhile, macroscopic experimental techniques generally have resolutions on the micrometer or millimeter scale31,32, making quantitative evaluation of interfacial adhesion in nanoscale thin films difficult33. In contrast, the nanoscratch test is minimally affected by surface morphology or specimen geometry34, allowing stable application even to samples with fine structural features and providing relatively high freedom in specimen design. In addition, the applied load can be precisely controlled35, making this technique particularly advantageous for identifying the critical load at which delamination initiates, thereby enabling quantitative comparison of adhesion force among specimens. With its sub-nanometer resolution36, the nanoscratch test can accurately measure adhesion variations at nanoscale interfaces quantitatively. This study evaluated the adhesion between the target material and the transfer molds/substrates, and elucidated the adhesion mechanisms depending on the absence or presence of metasurface and different process parameters. Nanoscratch test was employed to measure and analyse the adhesion quantitatively, and the optimal process parameters were derived based on the differences of adhesion forces of the transfer molds and the transfer substrates.

Experimental methods

A total of eight specimens were fabricated, consisting of four transfer molds (M-1 to M-4) and four transfer substrates (S-1 to S-4) without or with metasurface, and with different process parameters in transfer printing. M-1 and S-1 were designated as reference specimens without metasurface and without exposure to high-temperature or high-pressure conditions. The transfer molds were categorised as M-1 (without metasurface) and M-2 to M-4 (with metasurface). For M-1, an acrylate-based UV-curable resist (NIMS 311RM, Minuta Tech Inc., Korea) was dispensed onto a silicon wafer to form a flat resist layer. FOTS (Trichloro(1 H,1 H,2 H,2 H-perfluorooctyl)silane) was then vapor-deposited to form a release self-assembled monolayer (SAM), followed by e-beam evaporation of a 250 nm Ag layer without metasurface. For M-2 through M-4, the same resist was used, but the surface contained metasurface consisting of a 150 nm-diameter hole array with 250 nm-period, and subsequent FOTS vapor deposition and Ag e-beam evaporation were identical to those of M-1. The conditions of e-beam evaporation of M-3 and M-4 were identical; however, after the deposition, 5 bar pressure was applied on them with a Teflon film applied to prevent delamination of the Ag layer under different temperatures of 30 °C and 90 °C, respectively. Though M-1 and M-2 experienced no heat treatment and no additional applied pressure, they were stored under about 30 °C, thus, their heat treatment temperature can be assumed 30 °C.

The transfer substrates were defined as specimens onto which the Ag-deposited layer was transferred. On a single-crystal silicon wafer, (3-Trimethoxysilyl) propylethylenediamine (APS1, MRT Inc.) was coated to form an adhesive SAM with a thickness below 10 nm. For S-1, M-1 was used as the transfer mold, while for S-2 through S-4, M-2 was used, resulting in metasurface (pillar arrays) that were inverse replicas of the 150 nm diameter hole arrays of the M-2 transfer mold. Since temperature and pressure at the transfer printing are known to significantly influence adhesion, different conditions were also applied to S-2 through S-4 for comparison of adhesion. The specimens M-1 and S-1 were prepared as reference samples to analyse the variation in interfacial adhesion associated with the presence or absence of the metasurface. In contrast, M-2 through M-4 and S-2 through S-4 were designed to investigate the effects of varying process parameters of transfer printing on adhesion. During general transfer printing, the transfer mold and transfer substrate come into contact and are subjected to pressure and temperature as required. Under these conditions, the adhesion of metasurface to both the transfer mold and the transfer substrate changes depending on the applied pressure and temperature. However, since the transfer mold and transfer substrate remain contacted to each other during processing, direct measurement of adhesion is practically impossible. Therefore, in this study, to closely simulate the changes in adhesion occurring during the transfer printing, we fabricated specimens and applied the same temperature and pressure conditions as those used during processing for measuring each adhesion independently. Specifically, M-2 and S-2 simulated transfer printing under baseline conditions (1 bar and 30 °C), M-3 and S-3 simulated an increased pressure of 5 bar, and M-4 and S-4 simulated an elevated temperature of 90 °C in addition to the increased pressure.

Since the metasurface has a thickness of several hundred nm, an adhesion evaluation method with nanoscale resolution is required. However, conventional techniques such as the cross-hatch test37,38, peel test39,40, tensile adhesive test41, and dual cantilever beam test42 provide only microscale resolution, making them unsuitable for nanoscale adhesion analysis. Although recently developed in-situ TEM-based methods43 allow precise nanoscale measurements, they require complex experimental procedures and are limited in specimen geometry. Meanwhile, adhesion analysis using atomic force microscopy (AFM) enables nanoscale interfacial characterization with minimal constraints on specimen geometry44; however, the measurable force range is generally limited to under µN45–47, which is much smaller than the adhesion force in this study (The detailed values of the adhesion force are presented in the section of ‘Results and Discussion’). Therefore, nanoscratch test, which offers proper force and displacement resolution, was employed in this study for quantitative comparison of adhesion30,48. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the nanoscratch test involves applying a normal force with an indenter while moving laterally and measuring both the lateral force and depth. This approach enables not only nanoscale quantification of adhesion but also continuous observation of delamination as a function of progressively increasing force within a single test. The experiments were conducted using a nanoscratch tester (NST, Anton Paar) with force and displacement resolutions of 0.01 µN and 0.1 nm, respectively. A 90° sphero-conical diamond indenter with a tip radius of 1 μm was employed to apply a maximum normal force of 50 mN along a 1 mm scratch track, as summarised in Table 2. Each test was repeated at least three times on the different locations to ensure reproducibility.

In general, the adhesion force in a nanoscratch test is determined as follows. After completing the nanoscratch test, a panorama image of the scratch track is constructed using SEM, and the point of delamination for each track is identified on the panorama image. The (lateral) displacement corresponding to the delamination point is then measured from the panorama image, and the lateral force at this displacement is obtained from the graph of lateral force – (lateral) displacement. This lateral force is defined as the adhesion force. In this study, the adhesion force for each nanoscratch test was determined using the same procedure. The variation in adhesion among the specimens was subsequently analysed based on the adhesion force.

Analysis of the adhesion variation of the metasurface on the transfer molds

Figure 4(a) presents the representative lateral force – lateral displacement curves obtained from nanoscratch tests of the transfer molds, while Fig. 4(b) shows the delamination points observed through SEM. In general, delamination can be classified into ductile and brittle modes depending on the properties of a base material under a thin film49. In the case of the transfer molds, the base materials and a thin film were 311RM and metasurface, respectively. Since the base material was a polymer, ductile delamination was expected in the transfer molds. As shown in Fig. 4(a), all transfer molds (M-1 to M-4) exhibited a gradual increase in lateral force without abrupt drops or sudden fluctuations. This behavior is considered to reflect the progressive nature of ductile delamination. Furthermore, SEM images in Fig. 4(b) indicate that, in all four specimens, buckling developed along the scratch direction and was subsequently followed by tearing of the metasurface. Such a pattern is consistent with ductile delamination, in which cracks initiate from buckling ahead of the indenter and subsequently propagate in a gradual manner until delamination occurs. As described in the Experimental methods section, the lateral force at the delamination point was obtained from the lateral force–(lateral) displacement graph and was defined as the adhesion force. Therefore, even if the maximum lateral force in Fig. 4(a) is higher, the adhesion force may be lower if delamination occurs earlier. The averaged adhesion force of each specimen is presented in Fig. 5.

The adhesion force of M-2, which possesses metasurface, decreased by about 0.48 mN compared with M-1 (without metasurface), and the magnitude of this difference is sufficiently small and therefore negligible. As illustrated in Fig. 6, the metasurface can exert both positive and negative effects on adhesion. Specifically, filling the concave patterns of the transfer mold results in mechanical interlocking between the metasurface and the transfer mold, thereby providing a positive contribution to adhesion. In contrast, during Ag deposition, the concave patterns induce the formation of dimples on the upper surface of the metasurface. These dimples shorten the crack path length from the upper surface to the interface, thereby facilitating delamination and exerting a negative effect on adhesion. The relative magnitudes of these opposing contributions are expected to depend on the interfacial area subjected to dimple formation and mechanical interlocking. As shown in Fig. 6, the area beneath the dimple is comparable to that of the concave patterns, allowing the approximation of similar areas of dimples and interlocking. Accordingly, positive and negative effects on adhesion​ can be regarded as similar, indicating that no abrupt changes in adhesion of M-1 and M-2 are expected. Indeed, as observed in Fig. 5, the adhesion forces of the M-1 without metasurface and the M-2 with metasurface exhibit similar values, supporting the validity of this analysis.

A schematic diagram of metasurface and transfer mold showing (a) the region where dimples negatively affect adhesion and (b) the region where mechanical interlocking positively affects adhesion.

The adhesion force of M-3, subjected to pressure of 5 bar, was approximately 2.4 times greater than that of the as-deposited M-2, indicating a significant enhancement in adhesion force. In other words, applying a pressure of 5 bar after deposition substantially increased the adhesion force between the metasurface and the transfer mold. As shown in Fig. 7, this increased adhesion can be attributed not only to mechanical interlocking in the concave-patterned regions but also to enhanced contact between the metasurface and the transfer mold in the convex-patterned regions by high pressure. This trend is consistent with previous reports50–52.

Schematic diagram of the metasurface transfer mold specimens before and after pressure application.

On the other hand, despite the application of the same pressure of 5 bar, the adhesion force of M-4, with a temperature of 90 °C, decreased by approximately 60% compared with that of M-3, with temperature of 30 °C. In other words, the adhesion force exhibited a pronounced decrease as the temperature increased from 30 °C to 90 °C. According to a previous study53, the FOTS treated on the surface of the transfer mold was reported to cause negligible changes in adhesion force at temperatures up to 150 °C (423 K), from which it can be inferred that the increase in temperature from 30 °C to 90 °C would not significantly influence the adhesion force through the effect of FOTS. However, heating during the transfer printing can induce interfacial residual stress due to differential coefficients of thermal expansion (CTE) between the target material and the molds/substrates54,55. Analysis of the adhesion variation of the metasurface on the transfer substrates

Figure 8(a), Fig. 8(b) show the lateral force-lateral displacement curves obtained from nanoscratch tests of transfer substrates and the delamination or fracture points of transfer substrates observed by SEM, respectively. Since the base material of the transfer substrates is brittle single-crystal silicon, brittle delamination was anticipated. The lateral force in Fig. 8(a) showed abrupt decreases and increases at all transfer substrates. This behavior is consistent with the effect of brittle delamination, where the generation of new surfaces converts part of the applied external force into the surface energy of these newly created surfaces57. However, as shown in Fig. 8(b), while the tracks of S-1 and S-2 exhibited a sudden increase in track width, characteristic of brittle delamination, the tracks of S-3 and S-4 did not show such an increase. Instead, fracture of the single-crystal silicon substrate was observed at points where the metasurface appeared to have been delaminated. Taken together, these results indicate that brittle delamination occurred in S-1 and S-2, whereas in S-3 and S-4 the silicon substrate fractured prior to brittle delamination, resulting in the detachment of the metasurface. The adhesion force of S-1 and S-2 was determined by the same procedures described in Experimental methods sections, and presented in Fig. 9. By contrast, because S-3 and S-4 experienced brittle fracture of the silicon substrate before brittle delamination, their adhesion force was exceptionally high and thus could not be measured in this experiment. For this reason, their adhesion force was provisionally indicated as infinite in Fig. 9.

The adhesion force of S-2 decreased by approximately 85% compared with that of S-1. As illustrated in Fig. 10(a), dimples are formed on the upper surface of the metasurface at concave patterns of the transfer molds during the deposition process, and when the metasurface is transferred on the transfer substrate, voids are formed between the metasurface and the transfer substrate at the original dimple region. Consequently, as depicted by the red line in Fig. 10(b), the adhesion area between the metasurface and the transfer substrate of S-2 is reduced compared with that of S-1 leading to a substantial reduction in the adhesion force of the metasurface.

(a) Schematic images and SEM images showing dimples on the surface of transfer mold as well as voids on the transfer substrate and (b) comparison of the adhesion area of transfer substrates without and with metasurface. (The red line represents the adhesion area.)

When a pressure of 5 bar was applied, the adhesion force of S-3 increased substantially, and no delamination occurred prior to the fracture of the transfer substrate. This phenomenon can be interpreted as the result of enhanced mechanical interlocking between the metasurface and the transfer substrate with increasing applied pressure, a behavior that has also been reported in similar patterned surfaces in previous literature58. In addition, the voids illustrated in Fig. 10(b) may decrease in size under increased applied pressure, which would further contribute to the increase in adhesion force. S-4 likewise exhibited much higher adhesion force, indicating that applied pressure exerts a significant influence on the adhesion of the transfer substrate. Therefore, to improve adhesion, it is desirable to apply the highest possible pressure within the range that does not cause damage to the device during transfer printing.

In principle, a comparison of adhesion force between S-3 and S-4 is not feasible, since no delamination occurred in either case. However, previous studies59,60 showed that when adhesion is strong, the fracture of the substrate is delayed, and thus the degree of delay in fracture can be used to comparatively evaluate the relative adhesion between S-3 and S-4. According to previous literature57, single-crystal silicon fractured at a lateral force of 5.7 mN when the loading rate was set to 50 mN/mm, identical to the condition used in the present study. As shown in Fig. 11, fracture of the single-crystal silicon substrate in S-3 and S-4 occurred at approximately 8.15 mN (standard deviation: 0.10 mN) and 8.28 mN (standard deviation: 1.06 mN), respectively, both higher than the fracture force reported in the literature and occurring at similar force levels. This can be interpreted as the delayed fracture of the silicon substrate resulting from the adhesion between the metasurface and the transfer substrate. It is evident that the increase in measured fracture force relative to the reported fracture force is proportional to the adhesion force and they have similar adhesion.

Comparison of averaged adhesion forces of S-3 and S-4 with reported fracture force of single-crystal silicon57.

To elucidate the reason why the adhesion force of S-4 remained comparable to that of S-3 despite the change in process temperature, the influences of chemical and physical interfacial factors were examined. First, with respect to the influence of chemical factors, it is necessary to consider the role of APS1 surface treatment in enhancing adhesion. According to a previous study, APS1 increases adhesion by promoting the ionisation of Ag in the metasurface, whereby the resulting cations form covalent bonds with oxygen in the APS1 layer, leading to the formation of AgOx [61]. In general, as the process temperature increases, the generation of Ag cations is promoted, thereby increasing the amount of AgOx formed at the interface; consequently, the adhesion-enhancing effect of APS1 is expected to be more pronounced in S-4. However, the effect of residual stress induced at the interface must also be considered. As indicated in Eq. (1), S-3 and S-4 differ only in process temperature, with values of 5 °C and 65 °C, respectively. Accordingly, the residual stress () in S-4 is expected to be approximately 13 times greater than that in S-3. Thus, the reduction in adhesion force due to residual stress is also expected to be more significant in S-4. Taken together, both the increase in adhesion force driven by the activation of the surface treatment and the decrease in adhesion force caused by residual stress become more pronounced in S-4, offsetting each other. As a result, the adhesion force remains nearly equivalent between S-3 and S-4, as shown in Fig. 11, despite the increase in process temperature.

Optimisation of process parameters for reliable transfer printing

As previously described, the transfer mold and transfer substrate sharing the same numerical designation (2–4) were paired to simulate specific conditions of transfer printing. Since the adhesion force of the transfer substrate must exceed that of the transfer mold, a larger difference between these adhesion forces facilitates easier transfer printing. Therefore, the optimal conditions can be determined based on the difference in adhesion force between the paired transfer mold and transfer substrate. This relationship is summarized in Table 3, which lists the adhesion force for each paired condition. As shown in Table 3, the transfer mold exhibited higher adhesion force than the transfer substrate under condition 2 (1 bar and 30 °C), which means that the metasurface may not be transferred during the transfer-printing process. In contrast, under condition 3 (5 bar and 30 °C) and condition 4 (5 bar and 90 °C), the adhesion force of the transfer substrate was significantly higher, suggesting that applying a pressure of 5 bar is critical for achieving reliable transfer printing. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 11, although the adhesion forces of S-3 and S-4 are comparable, M-4 exhibits lower adhesion force than M-3, indicating that 90 °C provides more favorable conditions than 30 °C. Therefore, the condition 4 (5 bar and 90 °C) was determined as the optimal condition.

Conclusions

We employed the nanoscratch test to quantitatively analyse the adhesion force of the transfer mold and transfer substrate under different metasurface conditions (with or without metasurface) and process parameters of transfer printing. The mechanisms governing adhesion variation were elucidated, with the aim of optimising the metasurface transfer printing. The specific conclusions are as follows.

Effect of metasurface on adhesion

In the transfer mold, the adhesion of specimens with metasurface was slightly lower than that of those without metasurface, whereas in the transfer substrate it was significantly reduced. This difference can be attributed to the fact that the dimples generated by the metasurface in the transfer mold exert both positive and negative effects on adhesion, while the voids generated by the dimples in the transfer substrate impart only negative effects.

Effect of applied pressure on adhesion

In both the transfer mold and the transfer substrate, adhesion increased substantially, which can be attributed to the strengthening of mechanical interlocking between the metasurface and the transfer mold or the transfer substrate under applied pressure.

Effect of process temperature on adhesion

In the transfer mold, adhesion decreased markedly with increasing temperature, whereas in the transfer substrate it remained nearly unchanged. This outcome is attributed to the combined effects of differences in the activation of surface treatment between the transfer mold and the transfer substrate, as well as residual stresses induced by mismatches in the coefficient of thermal expansion.

Optimisation of process parameters of transfer printing with respect to adhesion

The reliability of transfer printing can be determined by the difference in adhesion forces between the transfer mold and the transfer substrate. Although adhesion degradation was caused by the formation of the metasurface, this drawback could be overcome by applying pressure and temperature, with 5 bar and 90 °C yielding the largest difference in adhesion force and thus identified as the optimal condition of transfer printing.
