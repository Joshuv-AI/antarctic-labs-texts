Molecular Pathogenesis and Therapeutic Response of Diffuse Large B Cell Lymphoma Genetic Subtypes
Authors: James D. Phelan, Julius C. Enssle, Sean R. Corcoran, George W. Wright, Louis M. Staudt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is a clinically and genetically heterogeneous disease. Molecular profiling studies in DLBCL have identified three distinct disease subtypes using gene expression profiling, whereas mutation analysis of tumors has identified at least six separate subtypes. Although each classifier predicts clinical responses to immunochemotherapy and targeted therapies, molecular profiling is not universally performed or uniformly implemented. In this review, we focus on the biology of the LymphGen algorithm defined genetic subtypes revealed by genomic, transcriptomic, and single-cell profiling. We highlight recent advances in understanding the major drivers of disease and discuss how different mutations promote common hallmarks of cancer that are vulnerable to precision medicine agents.

INTRODUCTION

The advent of molecular profiling of malignant tumors foretold a future of precision cancer treatment based on drugs targeting oncogenic mechanisms in subtypes of cancer. We are just now entering this anticipated era with advances in the diagnosis and treatment of diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL), an aggressive and common type of non-Hodgkin lymphoma. Early gene expression profiling studies of DLBCL revealed three cell-of-origin (COO) subtypes—germinal center B cell–like (GCB), activated B cell–like (ABC), and unclassified—that mirror B cell differentiation states, have distinct genetic profiles, and respond differentially to therapy (Alizadeh et al. 2000, Rosenwald et al. 2002, Shaffer et al. 2012, Wright et al. 2003). ABC tumors rely on “chronic active B cell receptor signaling” to drive prosurvival NF-κB activity (Davis et al. 2001, 2010), while GCB tumors rely on toncogenic B cell receptor (BCR) signaling to instead activate PI3 kinase (PI3K) (Phelan et al. 2018). Functional genomics revealed that chronic active BCR signaling can be targeted by the BTK inhibitor ibrutinib (PCI-32765) (Davis et al. 2010, Lenz et al. 2008, Ngo et al. 2006). Three clinical trials have tested the efficacy of ibrutinib in ABC: (a) ibrutinib monotherapy induced frequent objective responses in relapsed/refractory (R/R) ABC DLBCL (38%) but rarely in GCB (5%) (Wilson et al. 2015); (b) ibrutinib monotherapy of primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL), an often lethal form of ABC DLBCL, induced responses in most patients (94%) (Lionakis et al. 2017); and (c) ibrutinib in addition to R-CHOP chemotherapy improved survival of younger patients (age < 60) in ABC but not GCB (Younes et al. 2019). Nonetheless, genetic analysis of a handful of genes hinted at subtypes within ABC with greater or lesser sensitivity to ibrutinib (Wilson et al. 2015).

GENETIC CLASSIFICATION OF DLBCL

This review focuses on recent efforts that subdivide DLBCL COO subtypes into genetic subtypes defined by co-occurring mutations, DNA copy alterations, and translocations (Chapuy et al. 2018, Lacy et al. 2020, Schmitz et al. 2018, Wright et al. 2020). Currently, seven genetic subtypes have been delineated and named for their defining lesions: MCD (MYD88L265P/CD79B mutations), N1 (NOTCH1PEST truncation), A53 (TP53 inactivation/aneuploidy), BN2 (BCL6 translocation/NOTCH2PEST truncation), ST2 (SGK1/TET2 mutations), and EZB (EZH2 mutation/BCL2 translocation), which is subdivided further by the presence or absence of MYC translocations into EZB-DH (EZB-double hit) and EZB-nonDH, respectively (Schmitz et al. 2018, Wright et al. 2020) (Figure 1). The defining lesions of these DLBCL subtypes are shared with other B cell malignancies, which may reflect either indolent antecedents of DLBCL or phenotypically divergent cousins that share mutations (Figure 1).

Recently, >500,000 single cells from DLBCL biopsies were subjected to multiomic profiling [single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing (scATAC-seq)] to reveal that each genetic subtype has characteristic gene expression and chromatin signatures, as well as a distinctive tumor microenvironment (TME) (Wang et al. 2026) (Figure 1). From these data, distinct biological attributes and regulatory logic could be inferred in each subtype, implying that their genetic lesions drive their phenotypes. The action of a transcription factor (TF) in a subtype could be inferred by the coordinate increase or decrease in TF genomic targets by, respectively, the TF acting as an activator (+/+) or repressor (−/+). These regulatory modules—termed enhancer-driven regulons (eRegulons)—revealed DLBCL subtype–specific gene regulatory networks (Bravo Gonzalez-Blas et al. 2023). Finally, these data highlighted six overarching gene expression themes that cross genetic subtype boundaries, defining B cell identity (Pan-B), differentiation stage (GC B cell, memory B cell, plasma cell), malignant cell growth and metabolism (cell growth), and proliferation (cell cycle) (Wang et al. 2026).

Our intent is to filter recent research findings in DLBCL through the prism of the genetic subtypes and show how these subtypes predict the success or futility of targeted therapies. For each subtype, we characterize the frequent genetic features as oncogenes or tumor suppressors that affect apoptosis, BCR signaling, cell cycle, cell growth, gene expression, immune interactions, or oncogenic signaling. This synthesis highlights both the genetic and phenotypic distinctiveness of each subtype, and also many oncogenic mechanisms that are shared across multiple subtypes. MCD

With ibrutinib monotherapy, the most responsive ABC tumors were those with both MYD88L265P and CD79B mutations, now recognized as the MCD genetic subtype (Lionakis et al. 2017, Wilson et al. 2015). This observation prompted a reevaluation of the Phoenix phase 3 trial of R-CHOP ± ibrutinib based on LymphGen analysis of patient biopsies (Wilson et al. 2021). Younger (<60 years) patients with MCD DLBCL treated with ibrutinib and R-CHOP had a remarkable 100% event-free survival compared to 48% in those treated with R-CHOP alone (Wilson et al. 2021). Why did MCD tumors respond exceptionally to BTK inhibition? Arguably, the first genetic lesion in MCD pathogenesis is the formation of an autoreactive BCR by immunoglobulin VH-DH-JH rearrangement, which occurs in the B cell precursors of MCD prior to acquisition of MCD-defining genetic aberrations (Figure 2a). For example, MCD tumors frequently rearrange the IgVH4-34 segment, forming BCRs that bind N-acetyl-lactosamine on glycoproteins (Cappione et al. 2004, Young et al. 2015) (Figure 2b). Autoreactive BCRs form signaling clusters when they engage autoantigens, triggering downstream NF-κB activation. Notably, transformed B cells in a mouse model of MCD also have autoreactive BCRs (Pindzola et al. 2022). Interestingly, self-antigen-induced BCR clustering in MCD is dynamic and depends critically on N-linked glycosylation, potentially offering new therapeutic opportunities (Scheich et al. 2023). The recurrent CD79B mutations in MCD increase surface BCR expression and reduce negative regulation by LYN (Davis et al. 2010). Inactivation of the ubiquitin ligase KLHL14 in MCD reduces degradation of BCR subunits by the ERAD pathway, also increasing BCR on the cell surface (Choi et al. 2020).

NF-κB activation in MCD is mediated by a multiprotein signaling supercomplex—the My-T-BCR—named for the association of MYD88L265P, TLR9, and the BCR. My-T-BCR formation recruits the CBM (CARD11, MALT1, BCL10) adapter complex and IkB kinase (IKK) to endolysosomes, where NF-κB is activated (Phelan et al. 2018). The My-T-BCR is detectable in MCD DLBCL, PCNSL, lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma, and Waldenström Macroglobulinemia, each of which harbors MYD88L265P, suggesting that this mutant isoform may nucleate My-T-BCR formation (Phelan et al. 2018). Stimulation of both TLR9 and IgM is a uniquely potent mode of NF-κB and mTOR activation (Guo et al. 2025) that may provide sufficient oncogenic signals needed to break immune tolerance. Indeed, engineered murine models with syngeneic MYD88 and CD79B mutations and a Bcl2 transgene have noted a MYD88 supercomplex that is sensitive to BTK inhibition or TLR9 deletion (Flumann et al. 2024, Pindzola et al. 2022).

The sensitivity of MCD to BTK inhibition has recently been ascribed to chronic selective autophagy, a mechanism that modulates My-T-BCR signaling by continuously targeting MYD88L265P for autophagic degradation (Phelan et al. 2024) (Figure 2g). This ATGABC autophagy pathway involves proteins required for autophagic membrane formation and extension but not proteins required for lipidation of LC3. Rather, multiple ubiquitin-binding selective autophagy receptors recruit ubiquitinylated MYD88L265P to autophagosomes. The ATGABC autophagy pathway performs a tumor suppressor function in MCD, as evidenced by focal deletions targeting ATG13 and significant downregulation of other components of this pathway in MCD. Notably, BTK inhibition promotes the autophagic degradation of MYD88L265P and other My-T-BCR components by this pathway, plausibly contributing to the exceptional sensitivity of MCD to BTK inhibitors.

MCD was distinguished from other genetic subtypes by high expression of signatures of NF-κB, IRF4, OCT2, and JAK/STAT signaling, which stem from chronic active BCR and autocrine IL-10 signaling in this subtype (Beguelin et al. 2015, Davis et al. 2010, Yang et al. 2012) (Figure 2c). Multiple TF eRegulons were significantly enriched in MCD including TFs that drive plasmacytic differentiation such as IRF4, PRDM1, TCF4, and XBP1 (Figure 2e). However, terminal plasmacytic differentiation is blocked in MCD by genetic inactivation of PRDM1 and by amplification of SPIB, which represses PRDM1 expression. MCD is thus frozen in a state akin to an activated GC centrocyte that has been propelled toward plasmacytic differentiation by IRF4 due to NF-κB activation (Figure 2f).

MCD has high expression of MYC target genes, comparable to EZB-DH, possibly due to frequent BTG1 mutations that stabilize MYC mRNA (Mlynarczyk et al. 2023). Consequently, MCD malignant cells express the cell growth theme, due to MYC activation of protein synthesis and metabolism. MCD tumors also express the cell cycle theme highly, likely due to deletion of CDKN2A, encoding the cell cycle negative regulator p16 (Figure 2d).

The MCD TME is notable for its paucity of T cells and increased exhaustion of residual CD8+ T cells. Similarly, the MCD TME is significantly enriched in macrophages with angiogenic and inflammatory profiles (Ma et al. 2022). This reflects the many genetic lesions in MCD that promote immune evasion, evident in >75% of cases (Wright et al. 2020) (Figure 2a,g). Inactivation of one or more MHC class I (MHC-I) alleles, with a distinct bias toward HLA-B, suggests the possibility that the presentation of MCD-specific peptides prompts immune surveillance (Fangazio et al. 2021). The apparent immunogenic nature of MCD malignant cells may favor their frequent extranodal localization in immune-privileged sites such as the brain, eye, and testis, both as the primary site at diagnosis or as a secondary site in nodal MCD cases (Wright et al. 2020). The strong bias of PCNSL toward the MCD subtype underlies its extraordinary sensitivity to ibrutinib (Lionakis et al. 2017).

N1

N1 is the least prevalent of the LymphGen subtypes, representing ~5% of ABC tumors. N1 has an inferior outcome with R-CHOP, with a 5-year overall survival of only 27% (Wright et al. 2020). Remarkably, younger N1 patients in the Phoenix trial had a 100% survival rate with ibrutinib plus R-CHOP but only 50% with R-CHOP alone (Wilson et al. 2021). Genetic analysis of N1 provided clues to this exceptional ibrutinib response. N1 is defined by truncating mutations in the C-terminal PEST domain of NOTCH1 that prevent proteasomal degradation and augment ligand-dependent signaling (Gupta-Rossi et al. 2001, Oberg et al. 2001), abetted by mutations that inactivate DTX1, a ubiquitin ligase that targets NOTCH1 for degradation. All told, 50 genes are more frequently mutated in NOTCH1-mutant DLBCLs than in those with wild-type NOTCH1 (Wilson et al. 2021) (Figure 3a).

Although N1 BCRs do not often utilize autoreactive IgVH segments (Figure 3b), N1 frequently acquires mutations that inactivate BCR negative regulators (PTPN6, PRKCD, PTPRO, GRB2, LYN), thereby unleashing BCR signaling. BCR surface expression in N1 is promoted by inactivating mutations targeting KLHL6, which ubiquitinates CD79B and targets the BCR for lysosomal degradation (Corcoran et al. 2024, Meriranta et al. 2024). Additionally, N1 acquires gain-of-function mutations targeting activators of BCR-dependent NF-κB signaling (CD79A, PLCG2, PRKCB, CARD11, BCL10, and IKBKB) and loss-of-function mutations targeting TNFAIP3 (A20) and TNIP1, which negatively regulate IKK. Although these lesions target downstream components of the BCR pathway, they may serve to amplify signals emanating from the BCR.

N1 is unique among the DLBCL subtypes in frequently acquiring gain-of-function mutations in KRAS, NRAS, and BRAF, together affecting >15% of cases. This is notable given the availability of the BRAF inhibitor vemurafenib and multiple new RAS inhibitors in clinical development.

N1 is distinguished from other genetic subtypes by high expression of memory B cell signatures and low expression of GC B cell signatures (Wang et al. 2026, Wilson et al. 2021, Wright et al. 2020) (Figure 3c,d). Like memory B cells, N1 expresses a cellular quiescence signature highly as well as the TF KLF2, which promotes quiescence (Wang et al. 2026). These findings may explain, in part, the poor response of N1 to chemotherapy, which requires DNA replication to work optimally.

The likely cell of origin for N1 is an atypical memory B cell type that is self-renewing rather than terminally differentiated: Both N1 and atypical memory B cells typically express IgM, CXCR3, CD11c, CD27, and the TF TBX21 (T-Bet), upon which atypical memory B cells depend (Johnson et al. 2020, Kenderes et al. 2018, Wilson et al. 2021). Notably, N1 acquires frequent TBL1XR1 mutations, and N1 malignant cells are characterized by a repressive TBL1XR1 eRegulon (Figure 3e), suggesting that this TF acts as a tumor suppressor in this subtype. In mice, Tbl1xr1 loss-of-function mutations drive memory B cell differentiation, apparently by promoting the association of the NCOR-family corepressors (Ncor1, Ncor2/SMRT, Bcor) with Bach2 instead of Bcl6 (Venturutti et al. 2020). Of note, N1 recurrently acquires mutations targeting these corepressors, which may promote memory B cell differentiation by relieving BCL6 repression of its targets (Figure 3a). However, N1 also mutates genes associated with GC biology (ID3, KMT2D, TNFRSF14, P2RY8), suggesting that this subtype originates in the GC but acquires additional lesions that promote memory B cell differentiation.

Single-cell profiling of N1 tumors revealed a TME enriched in stromal cells and an interferon-associated macrophage subpopulation (IFN-TAM) characterized by high expression of interferon- and STAT1-regulated genes (Ma et al. 2022). Notably, the malignant N1 cells also expressed a STAT1 activator eRegulon highly, suggesting the possibility that interferon production by IFN-TAMs may drive STAT1, upregulating PD-L1 and depleting T cells (Figure 3g). Importantly, the abundant stromal cells and macrophages in the N1 TME form a supportive niche for NOTCH1-mutant malignant cells by expressing the NOTCH ligands JAG1 and DLL1, respectively.

A53

The co-occurrence of TP53 alterations and aneuploidy, as assessed by gains or losses of whole chromosomes or chromosome arms, defines the A53 subtype. A53 appears to arise by bi-allelic inactivation of TP53, since it typically acquires both a TP53 mutation and a heterozygous TP53 deletion, or a homozygous TP53 deletion. Importantly, TP53 mutations are not exclusive to the A53 subtype and cannot be used as a surrogate. As such, the response of A53 DLBCL in the Phoenix trial could not be assessed because DNA copy number data were not available (Wilson et al. 2021). By COO, A53 cases are often ABC (50–75%) but can also be GCB and unclassified (Wright et al. 2020). Many similarities between A53 and MCD emerge from genetic analysis, including recurrent MYD88L265P, CD79B, BTG1, ETV6, SETD1B, and histone H1 mutations (Figure 4a). A53 and MCD both are enriched for IgM BCRs, often utilizing IgVH4-34, suggesting a common origin from autoreactive B cells (Figure 4b). Moreover, A53 highly expresses IRF4-induced gene signatures, suggesting that A53 engages the BCR-dependent NF-κB pathway, like MCD (Figure 4c). A53 uniquely acquires amplifications of NFKBIZ. Increased gene expression of NFKBIZ has been linked to amplifications and 3′ UTR mutations frequent in ABC cases (Arthur et al. 2018). By single-cell eRegulon analysis, A53 and MCD both express eRegulons that promote plasmacytic differentiation and suppress GC B cell identity (IRF4, PRDM1, SPIB, BATF) (Wang et al. 2026) (Figure 4d,e). Similarly, A53 and MCD malignant clones had highly concordant gene expression patterns, suggesting a shared regulatory biology and B cell differentiation trajectory.

An intriguing possibility is that A53, like MCD, may assemble the My-T-BCR supercomplex, given its recurrent MYD88L265P and CD79B mutations (Figure 4f). Interestingly, ~25% of A53 tumors have focal copy number gain/amplification of CNPY3, encoding a TLR9 chaperone that is required for its endolysosomal localization. These considerations beg the question of whether BTK inhibitors would provide the same exceptional benefit in A53 as they do in MCD, which should be evaluated prospectively, using both mutations and copy number alterations to correctly identify A53 cases.

BN2

The hallmark of BN2 is co-occurrence of BCL6 translocations with NOTCH2 PEST domain truncations (Wright et al. 2020) (Figure 5a). These NOTCH2 mutations promote protein stability, but engagement by a ligand is still required to release NOTCH2ICN2 into the nucleus. Many BN2 tumors inactivate DTX1, thereby stabilizing NOTCH2, and/or SPEN, a corepressor protein that inhibits NOTCH2ICN2 target gene expression. Notably, DTX1 and SPEN mutations occur in BN2 tumors without NOTCH2 mutations, again suggesting the need for NOTCH ligand engagement, which may be provided by stromal cell JAG1 expression. Both BN2 and N1 express NOTCH target gene signatures, but BN2 has a unique gene expression signature distinguishing it from other genetic subtypes (Figure 1). In mice, Notch2 is essential for marginal zone B cell differentiation (Saito et al. 2003), and NOTCH2 mutations are also recurrent in indolent marginal zone lymphomas (MZLs), suggesting that BN2 might arise from an occult MZL in some cases (Figure 1). Interestingly, though, BN2 expresses an activator eRegulon for BHLHE41, a TF specifically expressed in self-renewing B-1a cells in the mouse peritoneum that may have a human counterpart from which chronic lymphocytic leukemia develops (Kreslavsky et al. 2017, Li et al. 2022, Shen et al. 2025) (Figure 5e). Clearly, mouse models are needed to clarify the cell of origin for BN2 (Figure 5f).

A further complexity is that BN2 can adopt all three COO phenotypes, although most cases are unclassified. This phenotypic heterogeneity revealed clinical heterogeneity as well, with ABC and GCB BN2 cases having a favorable outcome with R-CHOP relative to their COO counterparts, while unclassified BN2 cases have inferior outcomes (Wright et al. 2020). In the Phoenix trial, younger BN2 patients showed no difference in survival with or without ibrutinib (Wilson et al. 2021). Given the phenotypic and clinical heterogeneity of BN2, though, the efficacy of BTK inhibitors in BN2 remains an open question.

Indeed, there is evidence that BN2 lymphomas may rely on chronic active BCR signaling. Like MCD and A53, BN2 BCRs are enriched in IgM and IgVH4-34, suggesting autoreactivity, and express NF-κB signatures highly, consistent with BCR-dependent NF-κB pathway activation (Figure 5b,c). Moreover, BN2 tumors acquire gain-of-function mutations in CD79B, PRKCB, and BCL10 and loss-of-function TNFAIP3/TNIP1 alterations (Figure 5a). BCL10 is activated by R58Q hot-spot CARD domain mutations and by multiple mutations that truncate its C-terminal tail (Xia et al. 2022), which promote BCL10 filament formation and MALT recruitment, thereby activating NF-κB (Qiao et al. 2013). Finally, a BN2 cell line model (Riva) engages chronic active BCR signaling for survival and is highly sensitive to ibrutinib (Wright et al. 2020).

Expression profiling of the TME of BN2 revealed a rich milieu consisting of stromal cells, macrophages, and an abundance of T cell subsets including T follicular helper (TFH) cells (Wang et al. 2026). TFH cells promote formation of GCs and survival of GC B cells by providing T cell help via CD40 ligand, which triggers CD40 signaling in B cells. BN2 tumors coopt this pathway by inactivating TNFRSF14 (also known as HVEM), the ligand for BTLA. Without TNFRSF14-BTLA signaling, CD40 ligand expression on TFH cells is unchecked, thus promoting CD40 signaling within B cells (Mintz et al. 2019). In addition, TFH cells can express FAS ligand, which triggers apoptotic cell death by binding FAS, explaining why DLBCLs frequently inactivated FAS genetically (Razzaghi et al. 2021). Of note, these genetic events are more prevalent in EZB and ST2 (see below). Like other subtypes, BN2 avoids immune surveillance through the loss of MHC-I and CD58. BN2 is unique among the subtypes in recurrently inactivating CD70, which provides costimulatory signals to T cells by binding CD27 (Figure 5g).

ST2

The ST2 subtype represents approximately 10% of DLBCLs. ST2 cases are primarily GCB by COO and they have an excellent prognosis with R-CHOP treatment, perhaps due to very low BCL2 expression, as in normal GC B cells (Wright et al. 2020). Nonetheless, viability of malignant ST2 cells may be sustained by the BCR-dependent NF-κB pathway, which is altered by mutations that activate signaling (PRCKB, CARD11, RAC2), inactivate negative regulators (TNFAIP3, TNIP1, PTPN6), or increase surface BCR levels by inactivating KLHL6 and KLHL14 (Choi et al. 2020, Corcoran et al. 2024, Meriranta et al. 2024) (Figure 6a). Mutations of RAC2 are notable since it can bind and activate PLCG2, thereby bypassing BTK and fostering ibrutinib resistance (Shaffer et al. 2021). Additional inactivating mutations targeting IκBα (NFKBIA) in ST2 cause nuclear translocation of NF-κB in the absence of upstream BCR signals.

Other recurrent ST2 mutations activate PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling and MYC including those targeting the P2RY8/GNA13 chemokine receptor pathway (Muppidi et al. 2014, Nguyen et al. 2024). Mutations targeting the AKT family kinase SGK1 generate alternatively spliced, stable isoforms lacking the N-terminal degron that enable GC B cells to overcome AKT inhibition by inhibiting GSK3β (Gao et al. 2021), thereby increasing MYC protein expression. Accordingly, ST2 expresses glycolysis-related signatures (Wright et al. 2020), potentially due to MYC activity (Figure 6c).

Preclinical ST2 models are highly dependent on a toncogenic form of BCR signaling that activates PI3K but not NF-κB (Havranek et al. 2017, Phelan et al. 2018, Young et al. 2019). This antigen-independent signaling mode explains the low utilization of autoreactive IgVH segments in ST2 (Figure 6b). How might this form of BCR signaling be targeted therapeutically? The answer may lie in an old class of drugs, glucocorticoids. Recently, glucocorticoids were shown to disrupt proximal BCR signaling by inducing glucocorticoid receptor (GR) to activate or repress target genes regulating BCR signaling (Choi et al. 2024). GR directly induced expression of LAPTM5, which promotes lysosomal BCR degradation, and KLHL14, which promotes BCR degradation by the proteasome. Conversely, GR directly repressed CSK, a kinase that negatively regulates LYN and other Src-family kinases involved in proximal BCR signaling. Consequent hyperactivation of these Src-family kinases triggers their proteasomal degradation, attenuating BCR signaling. Additionally, GR reduced PI3K and mTOR signaling by activating SHIP-1 (INPP5D) and DDIT4, respectively. All told, glucocorticoids emerge as ideal inhibitors of toncogenic signaling, and their inclusion in the R-CHOP regimen may contribute to its efficacy in ST2 DLBCL.

High GC theme expression in ST2 and low IRF4 expression point to a GC B cell origin for this subtype (Figure 6d,f), which is supported by recurrent inactivation of the epigenetic modifiers TET2, KMT2D, and CREBBP. Inactivation of these epigenetic enzymes in the mouse generated GC hyperplasia and frank lymphomas (Dominguez et al. 2018, Jiang et al. 2017, Shukla et al. 2022, Vlasevska et al. 2023, Zhang et al. 2017). Inactivation of Tet2—a hallmark of ST2—expanded the mouse GC light zone while decreasing Prdm1 and Irf4 expression. Mechanistically, Tet2 loss induced DNA damage caused by G-quadruplex/R-loop formation and an increase in IgH locus translocations (Shukla et al. 2022).

ST2 has high expression of JAK-STAT target genes (Figure 6c), which is notable given its origin in the GC, where TFH cells produce IL-4 (Reinhardt et al. 2009), and IL-4 signaling by GC B cells promotes memory B cell differentiation (Shehata et al. 2024). Indeed, single-cell analysis demonstrated that ST2 expresses a STAT6 activator eRegulon highly compared with other subtypes (Figure 6e). Constitutive JAK/STAT signaling in ST2 likely stems from loss-of-function mutations targeting the phosphatases SOCS1 and DUSP2, which dephosphorylate JAK kinases and STAT3, respectively. Notably, the ST2 tumors are enriched in TFH cells, suggesting that they may rely on local IL-4 production in the TME. Given its T cell–rich milieu, it is not surprising that ST2 escapes immune surveillance by inactivating multiple immune regulators (Figure 6g).

EZB

The EZB subtype, named for EZH2 mutations and BCL2 translocations (Figure 7a), is the most prevalent of the GCB DLBCL subtypes (>60% of cases). It is further subdivided into EZB-DH and EZB-nonDH based on the presence or absence of MYC translocations, respectively (Figure 7b). EZB-DH (previously EZB-MYC+) is also known as high-grade B cell lymphoma with MYC and BCL2 rearrangements (HGBL-MYC-BCL2) in the World Health Organization classification. EZB-DH has a distinctive gene expression signature, termed double hit or dark zone (Ennishi et al. 2019a, Urata et al. 2023), revealing a GC dark zone origin coupled with expression of MYC target genes (Wright et al. 2020). Both EZB-DH and EZB-nonDH can arise by transformation from clinically apparent or, potentially, occult follicular lymphoma, which shares many genetic features with EZB. However, outcomes with R-CHOP chemotherapy are decidedly worse with EZB-DH than in most EZB-nonDH cases (Wright et al. 2020).

EZB acquires genetic changes in multiple chromatin modifying enzymes that alter histone H3 tails. These mutations profoundly alter the selection of GC B cells. KMT2D, CREBBP, and EP300 activate genes in GC B cells cooperatively through histone H3K4 methylation and H3K27 acetylation. Their inactivation singularly, and in tandem, promotes lymphomagenesis in the mouse (Meyer et al. 2019, Vlasevska et al. 2023). Gain-of-function EZH2 mutations foster H3K27 trimethylation, thereby promoting heterochromatin formation and gene repression. EZH2 is required for normal GC B cell formation and cooperates with BCL6 to oppose GC exit by silencing IRF4 and PRDM1 (Beguelin et al. 2013, 2016). Consequently, EZH2 inhibitors are a promising class of therapeutics for EZB lymphomas that derepress EZH2 targets, including MHC-I, which reignites immune surveillance (Dersh et al. 2021, Ennishi et al. 2019b, Isshiki et al. 2025). EZB also frequently inactivates ARID1A and SMARCA4, components of SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complexes. Loss of Arid1a or Smarca4 in the mouse disrupted GC dynamics by impairing binding of PU.1 and NF-κB to target genes and led to malignant transformation in conjunction with Bcl2 and Myc transgenes (Barisic et al. 2024, Deng et al. 2024).

However, despite many genetic similarities, EZB-DH and EZB-nonDH have telling genetic differences (Figure 7b). EZB-DH is characterized by mutations inactivating TP53, GNA13, DDX3X, and FOXO1, which aid and abet MYC function. MYC overexpression is inherently toxic to most cells, but TP53 inactivation mitigates this effect (Evan et al. 1992, Hermeking & Eick 1994). In normal GC B cells, loss of DDX3X cooperates with MYC to promote survival by decreasing the proteotoxic stress imposed by MYC-induced transcription and translation. Later in transformation, lymphomas from male patients upregulate DDX3Y, a DDX3X paralog, thereby restoring protein translation (Gong et al. 2021). In EZB mouse models, loss of Gna13 promotes MYC translation by activating mTORC1 in response to dietary glutamine (Nguyen et al. 2024).

By contrast, EZB-nonDH cases are enriched for genetic alterations targeting CARD11 and TNFAIP3, implicating the BCR-dependent NF-κB pathway (Figure 7b). These mutations act downstream of BTK in this pathway, and in some cases they predict insensitivity to ibrutinib. However, some CARD11 isoforms augment rather than supplant BCR signals (Lenz et al. 2008), and TNFAIP3 inactivation may likewise amplify NF-κB activation triggered by BCR signaling. EZB tumors do not utilize autoreactive IgH V genes and instead rely on antigen-independent toncogenic BCR signaling (Phelan et al. 2018) (Figure 7c,h). Taken together, these considerations suggest that BCR signaling may be a therapeutic target in some EZB-nonDH tumors. By contrast, BCR expression was shown to be largely absent in a large cohort of EZB-DH tumors (Varano et al. 2025).

An unusual form of partial BCR signaling occurs in a subset of EZB-nonDH cases in which AID-mediated somatic hypermutation has introduced acquired glycosylation sites (AGSs) into the IgH variable regions, enabling aberrant N-linked glycosylation (Chiodin et al. 2021). The glycans attached to these sites preclude any antigen engagement and instead enable binding by the lectin DC-SIGN, which is expressed in the TME. The cellular consequences of the resulting partial BCR signaling are unclear, but the presence of AGSs in EZB non-DH tumors identifies a patient population with an inferior survival with R-CHOP.

Single-cell analysis of EZB malignant cells revealed an expression and regulatory landscape inherited from their normal GC counterparts (Figure 7e,g), including activator eRegulons of MEF2B and MEF2C, which are essential for GC B cell differentiation (Brescia et al. 2018, Wang et al. 2026, Ying et al. 2013), and an activator eRegulon for IRF8, a TF that transactivates BCL6 in GC B cells (Wang et al. 2019, 2026). Conversely, genes in a repressive eRegulon of BACH2 were expressed at low levels in EZB tumors, consistent with the role of BACH2-mediated repression in maintaining the GC B cell phenotype (Muto et al. 2004, Shinnakasu et al. 2016, Wang et al. 2026) (Figure 7f,g). EZB also expressed an activator eRegulon for FOXO1, befitting the recurrent gain-of-function FOXO1 mutations in EZB-DH that promote a GC dark zone phenotype (Dominguez-Sola et al. 2015, Kabrani et al. 2018, Sander et al. 2015, Wang et al. 2026, Wright et al. 2020). Interestingly, EZB tumors downregulated a repressive eRegulon for the glucocorticoid receptor, a TF that controls BCR signaling and apoptosis and is constitutively nuclear in normal GC B cells (Bal et al. 2022, Choi et al. 2024, Okazaki et al. 2006) (Figure 7f,g).

Despite this shared regulatory biology, EZB-DH and EZB-nonDH differ significantly in their gene expression profiles, highlighting divergent biology (Wright et al. 2020). EZB-DH tumors express signatures of ribosomal biogenesis, lipid metabolism, and proliferation, all of which are promoted by MYC. In contrast, EZB-nonDH tumors have reduced expression of MYC target genes, like most GC B cells (Figure 7d).

Single-cell analysis showed that EZB-DH and EZB-nonDH diverge strikingly in their TMEs, with EZB-nonDH tumors having the highest abundance of T cells of all subtypes and EZB-DH tumors the lowest. In particular, EZB-nonDH is enriched in TFH cells, befitting a GC light zone origin (Wang et al. 2026) (Figure 7h). Genetic evidence suggests that the relationship between malignant B cells in EZB-nonDH and TFH cells is carefully orchestrated. As mentioned above, mutations in TNFRSF14 promote CD40 ligand expression by TFH and CD40 signaling in EZB-nonDH, while FAS mutations prevent TFH from inducing apoptosis using FAS ligand. Additionally, EZB tumors frequently inactivate CIITA, a transcription factor required for MHC-II expression and HLA-DMB, a component of the peptide loading complex required for antigen presentation by MHC-II, suggesting that EZB precisely tunes its interaction with TFH cells (reviewed in Mottok & Steidl 2015).

RATIONALLY DESIGNED COMBINATION THERAPY FOR DLBCL

Based on the efficacy of the BTK inhibitors in ABC patients (Wilson et al. 2015), a combination therapy regimen known as ViPOR was designed to include four drugs that target lymphoma pathways: venetoclax, prednisone, lenalidomide, and ibrutinib (Ceribelli et al. 2024, Mathews Griner et al. 2014, Yang et al. 2012). These drugs synergistically kill ABC cell line models. ViPOR also includes the anti-CD20 antibody obinutuzimab, which induces antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity. ViPOR was tested in 50 R/R DLBCL patients and given in six cycles over 18 weeks, then stopped. ViPOR proved to be safe and effective. Objective responses were observed in 54% of patients and progression-free survival (PFS) was 34% at 2 years (Melani et al. 2024). Remarkably, 5/6 MCD patients and 1/1 N1 patients achieved a complete response (CR); all were negative for minimal residual disease, as measured by circulating tumor DNA. This finding parallels the results of the Phoenix trial, in which MCD and N1 patients responded exceptionally well to R-CHOP plus ibrutinib. Surprisingly, CRs were observed in 50% (3/6) of patients with EZB-DH, resulting in a 2-year PFS of 47%. Mechanistically, this is likely due to venetoclax, which showed high activity against EZB-DH models compared with other GCB models. In EZB-DH, BCL2 inhibition may unleash MYC-driven apoptosis (Fanidi et al. 1992). Most patients in this trial were heavily pretreated, with a median of three prior lines of therapy, including 40% of patients who relapsed after CAR-T therapy. Notably, among the 15 patients who had relapsed after only one prior chemotherapy, ViPOR was even more active, inducing CRs in 80% of patients (Melani et al. 2024). These encouraging clinical results provide hope for patients with R/R DLBCL. More generally, these findings demonstrate the power of precision medicine to unravel mechanisms underlying the response and resistance of rationally designed combination regimens such as ViPOR, and they highlight the explanatory power of the DLBCL genetic subtype paradigm.

CONCLUSIONS

We have attempted to demonstrate the utility of molecular profiling to classify DLBCL into subtypes with defined pathogenesis and predictable responses to therapy. LymphGen subtypes predict response to chemotherapy, to targeted agents added to chemotherapy, and to combination regimens of targeted agents. Will LymphGen subtypes predict response to all therapies? Likely not, especially when those therapies depend on the TME. Overall, these considerations provide a compelling rationale to include comprehensive molecular profiling in all clinical trials in DLBCL going forward, which can be achieved by whole-exome sequencing and RNA sequencing.
