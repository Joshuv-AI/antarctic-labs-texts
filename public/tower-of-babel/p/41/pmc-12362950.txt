Patient-centered medical home and the quality of primary care: survey study with patients and administrators of community healthcare centers in Shanghai, China
Authors: Hua Jin, Huining Zhou, Leiyu Shi, Hanzhi Zhang, Aizhen Guo, Sen Yang, Shuoyuan Tan, Yuqing Shao, Dehua Yu, Jianwei Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the widely acknowledged importance of primary care, there remains a significant gap in its comprehensive evaluation in China, particularly from the dual perspectives of institutional performance and patient experience.ObjectiveWe utilized two internationally recognized tools, the Primary Care Assessment Tool (PCAT) for measuring primary care quality from the perspective of patients and The Patient-Centred Medical Home (PCMH, emphasizing providing comprehensive, accessible, and high-quality healthcare services, addressing the evolving needs of patients while enhancing the efficiency and effectiveness of primary care systems) for assessing an organization’s primary care achievement, to evaluate whether institutional achievement in primary care correlates with better patient experiences in community healthcare centers (CHCs) in Shanghai, China.MethodsNine CHCs were randomly selected using a multi-stage sampling method, followed by a random selection of patients. The PCMH tool was used for CHCs, while the PCAT was administered to patients. Multivariate logistic regressions were used to analyze the association between the PCMH status of the CHCs and the quality of primary care experienced by patients.FindingsA total of 1,782 patients and 9 CHC administrators completed the PCAT and PCMH tools, respectively. Generally, higher PCAT scores were associated with higher PCMH scores. Compared with patients from Level 1 PCMH CHCs, those from Level 2 and Level 3 CHCs reported significantly better experiences in ongoing care (Level 2: OR = 4.57, P < 0.001; Level 3: OR = 4.54, P < 0.001), family-centeredness (Level 2: OR = 3.39, P < 0.001), and cultural competence (Level 2: OR = 4.27, P < 0.001; Level 3: OR = 2.83, P = 0.03).ConclusionThe study validated PCMH as a robust framework for assessing primary care performance. Furthermore, it identified specific areas where CHCs can enhance the patient experience, particularly in the domains of Coordination (Information Systems), Care Coordination, and Care Transitions. These findings underscored the need for targeted interventions to strengthen these aspects of primary care delivery, ultimately improving patient outcomes and satisfaction.

Introduction

CHCs form the “bottom of the net” of the public health system and the foundation of the basic medical security system [1]. Their level and quality directly impact residents’ health and quality of life [2]. Therefore, assessing the current service quality of CHCs is crucial to target areas for improvement. With the ongoing development of community health services, various countries have refined the quality evaluation systems for these services. Generally, demand (patients) and supply (providers) are the focal points for studies assessing the quality of primary health care services, exploring the relationship between institutional primary care quality and patient experience [3, 4, 5, 6]. Starfield et al. conducted telephone interviews addressing first contact, continuity of care, comprehensiveness, and coordination, finding no differences between patients’ and providers’ perceived quality of primary health care [3]. However, other studies have found associations between patient and provider assessments of quality services in primary health care. For instance, a study in Ghana revealed a negative correlation between medical staff and patient perceptions of quality care [4]. In China, a quantitative study using the Primary Care Assessment Tool (PCAT) showed that patients rated primary health institutions higher than secondary and tertiary hospitals [5]. A study in Vietnam using PCAT demonstrated that CHC patients experienced higher quality primary care services compared to other healthcare providers in Vietnam [6]. The inconsistency in patients’ subjective perceptions of healthcare quality and the diversity of evaluation instruments have led to inconclusive results in available studies. Additionally, most studies lacked systematic quantitative evaluation of healthcare providers.

This study employed the widely recognized National Committee for Quality Assurance Patient-Centered Medical Home (NCQA-PCMH) and PCAT as quantitative assessment tools for institutional assessment (supply-side) and patient evaluation (demand-side) based on the “structure-process-outcome” quality assessment process to comprehensively evaluate CHCs in Shanghai [7, 8, 9]. Shanghai was chosen for its advanced and representative primary care system, which serves as a model for CHCs in China. Additionally, it allows for meaningful comparisons with previous studies, providing valuable insights into trends and areas for improvement in primary care quality. The supply-side and demand-side evaluation method used in this study can enrich the content of community service quality evaluation, identify early-stage problems in community health services, promote the effective use and rational allocation of available health resources, and thus enhance residents’ utilization and satisfaction with community health services.

Methods

Study setting

In this study, we employed a multi-stage sampling method from the total 244 CHCs in Shanghai, as illustrated in Fig. 1. First, all community health service centers were categorized into urban, suburban, and rural groups based on their geographic locations. Second, using computer-generated random numbers, 4 CHCs were drawn from each group, resulting in a total of 12 CHCs. We explained the purpose and significance of the research to the selected CHCs, ultimately, nine institutions agreed to participate in the study. Third, among these nine institutions, our survey subjects included one leader responsible for medical service quality in each CHC and 200–225 patients (based on the total number of contracted residents over 40 years old in each CHC). The selection criteria for patients were: (1) contracted residents aged 40 years or older; (2) had visited the CHC at least twice within six months before the study; (3) agreed to participate in the survey after being informed about it. The number of patients selected per CHC was calculated by first obtaining the value of proportion of patients who responded favorably to PCAT questions through a pilot (i.e., 85%) and then using 5% as margin of error.

Measurement

Service institution self-assessment

The NCQA-PCMH evaluation tool for service providers in this study was published on the NCQA website in the United States in 2014 [10–11]. This tool, originally based on nine dimensions and ten essential elements from 2008, was refined to encompass six dimensions and six essential elements. These dimensions include patient-centered access, team-based care, population health management, care management and support, care coordination and transitions, and performance measurement and quality improvement. The specific content of all dimensions is detailed in Table 1. Each dimension comprises various elements, with a total score of 100 points. Using predefined cut-off values (total PCMH scores), CHCs were categorized into three groups: Level 1 (35–59), Level 2 (60–84), and Level 3 (85–100) [12]. In our study, we initially employed stratified sampling to select 12 community health centers, evenly distributed across three PCMH levels (Level 1, Level 2, and Level 3), with 4 centers in each group. However, after explaining the purpose and significance of the research, 3 centers from the Level 1 group opted to withdraw. Ultimately, the participating CHCs consisted of 1 Level 1, 4 Level 2, and 4 Level 3 centers.

This tool has been widely used in both the United States and China. For instance, a cohort study in the United States revealed that up to 40% of primary healthcare institutions employed the NCQA-PCMH identification questionnaire for evaluation, with Level 3 institutions comprising 35% and Level 2 institutions 5% [13].

Service experiencer evaluation

We used the adult version of the Primary Care Assessment Tool (PCAT-AE) to evaluate the quality of primary care services provided by CHCs from the perspective of service consumers. Developed by the Johns Hopkins Primary Care Policy Center, the PCAT-AE [14] is based on the theoretical framework of primary care functions and is designed to objectively evaluate service quality from the patient experience perspective [15]. The scale has been widely used in both developed countries, such as Canada and the United States, and developing countries, including China, demonstrating high reliability and validity [16, 17, 18]. Given the existence of studies in China that have used a translated version of the PCAT-AE questionnaire, we received approval from the designer to use this questionnaire in our study.

The questionnaire includes general patient information (e.g., age, gender, marital status) and the PCAT-AE scale, which consists of 10 dimensions and 87 items. The specific content of all dimensions is detailed in Table 2. All PCAT-AE items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale: 1 = “Never,” 2 = “Occasionally,” 3 = “Sometimes,” and 4 = “Always,” with “Don’t know/Do not answer” serving as a neutral option, assigned a value of 2.5. The overall service quality of CHCs is evaluated by calculating the average score of all items [19]. The higher the PCAT score, the better the patient experience and the quality of primary care [20]. In this study, a total of 2,025 PCAT questionnaires were distributed, and 1,782 valid responses were successfully collected, yielding a response rate of 88.00%. Non-respondents were primarily associated with time constraints, lack of interest, but their proportion was minimal and unlikely to introduce significant bias into the study findings. A random sample of 193 patients was pretested with the PCAT questionnaire, which showed high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.924). Structural validity was confirmed by exploratory factor analysis with a KMO value of 0.879, a Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.001), and all factor loadings exceeding the suggested threshold of 0.40.

Data collection

Data were collected via structured questionnaires by the face to face interview in this cross-sectional study. To minimize interviewer bias, all interviewers underwent standardized training to ensure consistency in both question delivery and response interpretation. Prior to formal data collection, a pre-test was conducted, allowing interviewers to practice with actual patients under close supervision. During the initial phase of the study, all interviews were directly monitored by supervisors until interviewers demonstrated proficiency in questionnaire administration. This rigorous oversight ensured that all questionnaire items were fully completed by each participant, with no missing responses. Additionally, the two survey instruments, PCAT and PCMH, were initially developed in English and subsequently translated into Mandarin Chinese through a rigorous process of translation, back-translation process, review and reconciliation, and pilot testing to ensure accuracy and cultural relevance.

Analysis

All analyses in this study were performed using SPSS software (version 21.0, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistical analysis was employed to compile the demographic data of patients who completed the PCAT scale, including gender, age, marital status, occupation, education, family income, and health insurance status. A regression analysis was used to analyze the correlation between the PCMH level (dependent variable) and the PCAT dimension scores (independent variables). In this analysis, the dependent variable was categorized into three groups (Level 1, Level 2, and Level 3) based on the PCMH scores. The independent variables were the scores of the ten dimensions of the PCAT (e.g., First Contact, Continuity, Coordination). Control variables, including demographic indicators such as age, gender, marital status, and occupation, were also incorporated to account for potential confounding effects.

Evaluation of the status of health service quality in CHCs

Table 4 presents the total PCAT scores and the scores across 10 dimensions. The overall average PCAT score for all CHCs was 30.72 (SD = 3.27). Institution 6 had the highest score at 34.04 (SD = 2.03), while Institution 1 had the lowest score at 27.82. All dimension scores were above 2.50, indicating general patient satisfaction.

Table 5 summarizes the self-evaluation scores of CHCs across six dimensions. Overall, most CHCs were at level-2, with one CHC at level-1, four at level-2, and four at level-3. The average score across all institutions was 76.33 (SD = 13.34). The top three institutions were Institution 7 (90.00), Institution 6 (88.13), and Institution 9 (87.50). The bottom three were Institutions 2, 1, and 3, with the lowest score being 52.38.

Figure 2 illustrates the PCAT scores for each dimension relative to the CHCs’ PCMH status. Interestingly, level-2 CHCs had a lower overall PCAT score (29.76) compared to level-1 CHCs (29.82). Specifically, the PCAT scores for First contact (utilization), Comprehensiveness (available), and Comprehensiveness (provided) were lower for level-2 than for level-1. Additionally, the PCAT scores in the Coordination (Information system) dimension decreased as the PCMH level increased. Conversely, the PCAT scores in dimensions such as First contact (accessibility), Ongoing care, Coordination (Referral system), Family-centeredness, Community orientation, and Cultural competence increased with higher PCMH levels.

Correlation analysis of PCAT and PCMH scores

Figure 3 illustrate the correlation between the dimensions of the PCAT and the PCMH. Overall, there was a strong positive correlation between the total scores of PCAT and PCMH (r = 0.71, p < 0.05). Specifically, within the dimensions of PCMH, Team-based care (r = 0.81, p < 0.05) and Care management and support (r = 0.73, p < 0.05) showed high correlations with Ongoing care (PCAT). Additionally, the scores of Care coordination and care transitions were positively correlated with the scores of First contact (accessibility) (r = 0.79, p < 0.05) and Coordination (Referral system) (r = 0.67, p < 0.05).

Among the dimensions of PCAT, all dimensions except for Coordination (Referral system), Coordination (Information system), Family-centeredness, and Culturally competent were related to the performance measurement and quality improvement scores, with First contact (accessibility) showing the highest correlation (r = 0.86, p < 0.05).

Multiple logistic regression analysis of PCAT grouped by PCMH score

Using multiple logistic regression analysis, CHCs were categorized into three groups (Level-1, Level-2, and Level-3) based on their PCMH scores. With Level-1 as the control group, we controlled for demographic characteristics and service utilization information of patients to analyze the influence of PCAT dimensions on community grouping (Table 6). Compared to patients in Level-1 CHCs, an increase in the PCAT scores for Ongoing care (OR: 4.57, 95% CI: 2.44–7.55, P < 0.001), Family-centeredness (OR: 3.39, 95% CI: 1.76–6.52, P < 0.001), and Culturally competent (OR: 4.27, 95% CI: 2.28-8.00, P < 0.001) was associated with a higher likelihood of being in a Level-2.

CHC (P < 0.05).

For Level-3 CHCs, higher scores in Ongoing care (OR: 4.54, 95% CI: 1.05–7.04, P < 0.001) and Culturally competent (OR: 2.83, 95% CI: 1.41–5.68, P = 0.03) were linked to a higher PCMH level compared to Level-1. Conversely, increases in the scores for First contact (utilization) (OR: 0.12, 95% CI: 0.06–0.22), First contact (accessibility) (OR: 0.21, 95% CI: 0.08–0.52), Comprehensiveness (available) (OR: 0.32, 95% CI: 0.17–0.57), and Community orientation (OR: 0.04, 95% CI: 0.01–0.11) were associated with a lower probability of being in a Level-2 CHC compared to Level-1.

Similarly, in Level-3 CHCs, higher scores in First contact (utilization) (OR: 0.21, 95% CI: 0.10–0.43), Comprehensiveness (available) (OR: 0.18, 95% CI: 0.08–0.39), and Community orientation (OR: 0.03, 95% CI: 0.01–0.09) were associated with a lower likelihood of being in a Level-3 CHC compared to Level-1.

Discussion

In this study, we employed two scales, PCAT and PCMH, to assess the quality of primary care provided by healthcare facilities from both patient (demand-side) and manager (supply-side) perspectives. Overall, service users rated community healthcare centers (CHCs) positively, though improvements are needed compared to developed countries like the United States [21]. China’s primary healthcare system is still developing, contrasting with the maturity of systems in developed countries. Comparing PCAT scores from 578 survey respondents in Shanghai in 2013 to those in 2019 (30.73 vs. 23.96), significant improvements were noted, particularly in first contact (utilization) (2019: 3.53 vs. 2016: 2.74) and comprehensiveness (provision) (2019: 3.26 vs. 2016: 2.71) [22]. This suggests recent policies like the “1 + 1 + 1” private family doctor contracting program have positively influenced Shanghai’s primary care, prompting increased future government investment [23].

From the provider’s perspective, only four facilities scored above 85 on the PCMH scale, indicating level 3 quality. Areas needing improvement included care coordination, transitions, performance measures, and quality improvement. Some institutions scored poorly on elements such as patient-centered appointment access and referral tracking [24]. PCMH recognition is crucial for enhancing primary care delivery, underscoring the need for improvement in this domain.

In this study, we found that CHCs had the lowest scores in PCAT for Coordination (Referral system) and Coordination (Information system), as well as in PCMH for Care coordination and care transitions. This indicates that both institutions and patients currently perceive significant deficiencies in the delivery of referral services and the coordination of medical information. The NCQA-PCMH tool and PACT are sensitive in evaluating the quality of primary care services and can objectively address organizational shortcomings. The joint use of these tools makes primary care quality evaluation more comprehensive.

The low evaluation of appointment referrals from both supply and demand perspectives highlights issues in China, where a clear policy system is slow to advance, and a qualified system has not yet been formed. The root problems include the absence of referral-related rules from health administrations or industry associations, unclear divisions of responsibility between general practitioners and specialists, lack of collaboration between different levels of medical institutions, an imperfect medical information network, and poor linkage between medical institutions [25–26]. Additionally, medical personnel’s general understanding of two-way referrals has not reached a consensus on the criteria for disease referral. To better implement hierarchical diagnosis and treatment and a two-way referral system in China, more efforts are needed to develop a clear policy system, form a qualified system, and reach a consensus on referral criteria [27].

Exploring the link between provider self-assessment and patient experience revealed that higher patient experience scores on the PCAT dimensions of Ongoing care and Cultural competence correlated with higher NCQA-PCMH recognition levels of CHCs. This suggests that higher NCQA-PCMH scores correlate with better healthcare staff and a strong patient-physician relationship. In contrast, higher patient experience scores on First contact (utilization) and Community orientation in the PCAT corresponded with lower NCQA-PCMH recognition levels. This indicates that high-level CHCs need to strengthen their role as health gatekeepers and implement the Chinese gatekeeping policy. Based on a cross-sectional study in China that used the PCAT tool and surveyed 553 patients with chronic diseases, lower ratings of community-oriented, information system, and referral service continuity dimensions were identified as directions for future improvement of service quality [28].

This study further stratified the quality of CHCs based on NCQA-PCMH criteria and compared differences in patient experience across strata, using objective data to identify strengths and weaknesses. The results urge CHCs to adopt distinctive improvement measures to become higher-level CHCs and conduct annual NCQA-PCMH accreditation to maintain excellence [12]. The findings support the institutional analysis and development framework proposed by Ostrom, suggesting that CHCs significantly impact patient experience at the micro level [29]. To improve patient experience, changes need to be made at the level of CHC construction.

Despite the novelty of our study in terms of quality assessment, several limitations warrant caution in interpreting the results. Firstly, the statistical findings may be influenced by the wide variation in the number of patients and institutions included in the study. Although a multistage sampling approach was employed to ensure representativeness, the inclusion of only 9 Community CHCs in Shanghai may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader CHC population. Secondly, the patient participation rate of less than 10% raised concerns about selection bias, as systematic differences between non-participants and participants could potentially influence the study outcomes. Thirdly, the survey instrument, originally developed in English, was translated into Mandarin Chinese through a rigorous translation and back-translation process; however, the translated version may not have undergone formal linguistic validation in the local context.

Conclusion

Our research indicates that from the perspective of service users, primary healthcare in Shanghai is well-regarded in the First contact (utilization) dimension. However, the Coordination (Information system) aspect requires significant improvement, and the government should focus on enhancing this area to provide better primary health services to the public. From the service providers’ perspective, the quality of health services offered by CHCs across different regions of Shanghai is uneven. Notably, all institutions exhibit weaknesses in Care coordination and care transitions, presenting a major challenge for each community health service center.

Additionally, community health service centers need to pay attention not only to the capabilities of the services they provide but also to the evaluations from service users. They should understand these evaluations and conduct assessments based on their actual conditions to improve overall service quality.
