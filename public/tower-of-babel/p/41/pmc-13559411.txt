Association of postoperative orthostatic intolerance with early recovery quality and clinical outcomes following thoracoscopic pulmonary resection: a propensity-matched study
Authors: Li Tan, Hongjie Yi, Ying Shen, Xia Li, Wenfeng Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative orthostatic intolerance (OI) is frequently observed during early mobilization after surgery, yet its impact on early recovery following thoracoscopic pulmonary resection remains unclear. This study evaluated the association between OI and early recovery outcomes.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 211 patients undergoing thoracoscopic pulmonary resection. OI was recorded during the first mobilization. Recovery was assessed using Quality of Recovery-9 (QoR-9) scores (postoperative days 1–3), discharge 6-minute walk distance (6MWD), drainage duration, and hospital stay. Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to adjust for baseline differences.ResultsBefore matching, 64 patients experienced OI and 147 did not, differing significantly in sex and smoking history (P<0.05). After PSM (n=64/group), QoR-9 scores were consistently lower in the OI group on postoperative day 1 (9.72 vs. 11.08, P<0.001), day 2 (12.05 vs. 12.95, P=0.006), and day 3 (13.89 vs. 14.58, P=0.02). Patients with OI had longer drainage duration (87.75 vs. 79.00 hours, P=0.004) and higher odds of postoperative hospital stays of 5–10 days [odds ratio (OR) =2.86, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.20–6.82] and ≥11 days (OR =10.03, 95% CI: 1.18–84.96). However, no significant difference in discharge 6MWD was observed (351.64 vs. 376.52 meters, P=0.10).ConclusionsPostoperative OI is associated with clinically meaningful reductions in early recovery quality, particularly during the first two postoperative days, and with prolonged hospitalization, but not with reduced functional walking capacity at discharge. These findings suggest that OI represents a transient but clinically relevant factor in early recovery trajectories following thoracoscopic lung surgery.Keywords: Orthostatic intolerance (OI), thoracoscopic pulmonary resection, enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS), quality of recovery (QoR), propensity score matching (PSM)

Introduction

Early postoperative mobilization is a central component of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways and is essential for mitigating postoperative functional decline. By counteracting the loss of physical endurance and muscle strength associated with postoperative bed rest, early mobilization facilitates recovery across multiple physiological systems, including respiratory, gastrointestinal, and musculoskeletal function (1,2). Consistent evidence indicates that successful early mobilization is associated with reduced postoperative complications, shorter hospital stays, lower mortality, and decreased healthcare costs (3,4). Despite these well-established benefits, the implementation of early mobilization in clinical practice remains frequently disrupted by orthostatic intolerance (OI) (5).

OI is a common postoperative condition that manifests during postural transitions to an upright position and is characterized by symptoms such as dizziness, nausea, visual dimming or blackout, diaphoresis, palpitations, and, in severe cases, syncope. These symptoms are typically relieved by returning to a supine position (6,7). Joshi et al. (7) recently emphasized that postoperative OI encompasses a spectrum ranging from benign positional dizziness to significant hemodynamic compromise, and highlighted the importance of understanding its pathophysiological classification—including orthostatic hypotension, postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, and initial orthostatic hypotension—to guide targeted perioperative management strategies. The underlying pathophysiology is thought to involve gravity-induced blood pooling in the abdominal and lower extremity vasculature during upright posture, leading to reduced central blood volume, decreased cardiac output, and subsequent cerebral hypoperfusion (8). Perioperative factors—including intraoperative blood and fluid loss, surgical stress responses, pain-related autonomic dysregulation, and residual anesthetic effects—may further exacerbate postural cardiovascular instability in the early postoperative period (9,10). As a result, OI represents a critical physiological barrier to the successful execution of early mobilization strategies after surgery.

Patients undergoing thoracoscopic pulmonary resection appear to be particularly susceptible to postoperative OI during early ambulation, with reported incidence rates ranging from 29.8% to 45.7% (5,11,12). In a prospective observational study, Hanada et al. reported an OI incidence of 45.7% during early postoperative ambulation following thoracoscopic surgery, with nearly half of affected patients unable to complete initial out-of-bed mobilization attempts, frequently leading to delayed recovery and prolonged hospitalization (11). While these findings highlight the high prevalence and immediate functional consequences of postoperative OI, recovery after surgery is increasingly recognized as a multidimensional construct that should incorporate patient-reported outcomes in addition to traditional clinical indicators.

Although postoperative OI has been extensively described, its specific association with patient-reported early recovery quality following thoracoscopic pulmonary resection remains insufficiently characterized. In particular, whether transient OI during early mobilization translates into measurable impairment in early recovery trajectories has not been systematically examined.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the impact of postoperative OI on early recovery outcomes in patients undergoing thoracoscopic pulmonary resection using a propensity score-matched cohort design. By integrating patient-reported recovery quality with objective functional and clinical recovery indicators, this study seeks to clarify the role of postoperative OI in shaping early recovery patterns following minimally invasive lung surgery. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0196/rc).

Methods

Research participants

This prospective observational study enrolled a total of 211 patients who underwent thoracoscopic pulmonary resection at The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University between February and May 2023. Participants were enrolled via consecutive sampling within this fixed enrollment period.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) confirmed pulmonary lesions identified by preoperative thoracic computed tomography (CT) or other relevant imaging examinations; (II) eligibility for thoracoscopic partial lung resection under general anesthesia; (III) age ≥18 years; (IV) intact cognitive function and the ability to communicate symptoms; and (V) provision of written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria included: (I) pre-existing conditions associated with impaired mobility or autonomic dysfunction (e.g., cerebrovascular accidents or femoral hip fractures); (II) inability to reliably report subjective symptoms such as dizziness, nausea, or visual disturbances (e.g., visual dimming or transient visual blackout); and (III) intraoperative or postoperative complications (such as intraoperative major vascular injury, uncontrolled bleeding requiring reoperation, cardiac arrhythmias or acute hemodynamic instability, acute respiratory compromise, or acute renal injury) that precluded early postoperative mobilization.

Withdrawal criteria were: (I) withdrawal of informed consent; (II) discontinuation due to adverse events; or (III) loss to follow-up.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (No. K2023-197). Informed consent was obtained from each study participant after explaining the study’s merits. Participants were informed of their right to refuse to participate in the study at any time. Confidentiality was guaranteed by avoiding questions containing identifiers and keeping completed questionnaires and results in a well-secured area. The study protocol was retrospectively registered at the Medical Research Registration Information System (registration number: MR-50-24-030580, registration date: August 6, 2024).

Perioperative management

All patients received perioperative care in accordance with standardized ERAS protocols. The ERAS protocol implemented at our institution (The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University) is a localized version adapted from the Chinese ERAS guidelines (2), with certain elements also incorporating recommendations from the European Society of Thoracic Surgeons ERAS guidelines (ESTS-ERAS 2019) (1). Chest tube management protocol: chest tube removal was considered when the following criteria (1,2) were met: (I) 24-hour drainage volume was less than 200 mL; (II) no persistent air leak was observed (assessed by water seal or digital monitoring); and (III) chest radiography confirmed adequate lung re-expansion. The decision for tube removal was determined by the attending surgeon based on objective clinical criteria and was not contingent upon the patient’s ambulatory status.

Early mobilization protocol

Patients were encouraged to initiate out-of-bed mobilization within 24 hours after surgery, calculated from the completion of the surgical procedure (1,13). The exact timing of first mobilization was systematically recorded. All participants followed the same standardized early mobilization protocol. Prior to surgery, patients received structured instruction regarding the purpose, procedures, and safety considerations of postoperative mobilization. Once patients were fully awake from anesthesia, they were assisted with in-bed activities, including turning, limb movements, and simple range-of-motion exercises. Before mobilization, patients were evaluated by healthcare professionals and required to meet the following criteria: (I) oxygen saturation ≥90%; (II) respiratory rate ≤30 breaths/min; (III) heart rate ≤120 beats/min; (IV) blood pressure within an acceptable clinical range; and (V) absence of acute complications such as severe arrhythmia, myocardial ischemia, or deep vein thrombosis (DVT).

Mobilization was conducted in a stepwise manner, progressing from sitting at the bedside to standing and ambulating within the ward room and corridor, with assistance from healthcare staff and family members when necessary. During mobilization, all drainage tubes were securely fixed and vital signs were continuously monitored. Mobilization was immediately discontinued if patients developed symptoms of intolerance or if any safety concerns arose.

Assessment tools

General information survey

Baseline demographic and clinical data were collected, including sex, age, height, weight, comorbidities, smoking and alcohol consumption history, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, duration of surgery, and extent of lung resection.

OI symptom assessment

OI was assessed using a structured symptom checklist developed in accordance with internationally recognized diagnostic criteria (14,15). Postoperative OI was defined as either a decrease in systolic blood pressure of ≥30 mmHg within 3 minutes of postural change or the occurrence of typical hypoperfusion-related symptoms during early mobilization. Recorded symptoms included dizziness, nausea, visual dimming or visual blackout, profuse sweating, vomiting, headache, hand tremor, palpitations, and syncope (10,16).

Blood pressure was measured sequentially in the supine, sitting, and standing positions during mobilization. Supine blood pressure was measured after at least 5 minutes of rest. Sitting blood pressure was recorded after maintaining a seated position for 3 minutes. Standing blood pressure was measured after 3 minutes of standing when tolerated, or immediately if significant discomfort occurred. If a ≥30 mmHg decrease in systolic blood pressure was observed upon standing, patients were promptly assisted back to the supine position. Any severe OI symptoms during mobilization were considered diagnostic of OI.

Postoperative complication assessment

Postoperative complications were systematically recorded for all enrolled patients during the hospitalization period. Complications were classified according to the Clavien-Dindo classification system (17) and categorized as (I) pulmonary complications [e.g., prolonged air leak >5 days, pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), atelectasis requiring bronchoscopy]; (II) cardiovascular complications [e.g., atrial fibrillation (AF), hypertensive crisis, and cardiac arrhythmias requiring intervention]; (III) wound-related complications (e.g., surgical site infection); and (IV) other complications (e.g., urinary tract infection, and DVT). The type and frequency of complications were compared between the OI and non-OI groups both before and after propensity score matching (PSM). Complications that were present prior to the mobilization assessment were considered potential confounders and included in the propensity score model.

Outcome measures

The Quality of Recovery-9 (QoR-9) score, developed by Myles et al. in 1999, is a validated instrument for assessing the quality of anesthesia and postoperative recovery (18). It comprises nine items evaluating pain, patient comfort, recovery of independent function, and communication ability, each scored from 0 to 2, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 18, with higher scores indicating better recovery quality. The QoR-9 has demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity in previous studies (19).

6-minute walk test (6MWT)

In accordance with the Chinese expert consensus on the standardized clinical application of 6MWT (20), patients and their families were provided with a detailed explanation of the test’s purpose and precautions before its administration. Vital signs, including blood pressure and heart rate, were measured prior to the test, and patients were instructed to wear comfortable clothing and appropriate footwear. During the test, investigators accompanied and instructed patients to walk at their maximum sustainable pace for 6 minutes along a designated 50 m corridor in the ward. The starting point was clearly marked with a ribbon, and distance markers were placed every 1 m along the route. Talking, running, or jumping were prohibited during the walk. Patients were allowed to slow down or take a rest if needed but were encouraged to continue walking as far as possible. At the end of the test, the total distance covered during the 6 minutes was recorded as the 6-minute walk distance (6MWD). If a patient stopped walking before 6 minutes and chose not to continue, they were allowed to sit and rest. The walking distance covered, time of cessation, and the reason for early discontinuation were recorded.

Evaluation indicators

Postoperative QoR score

The patients’ recovery quality was assessed using the QoR-9 scale on postoperative days 1, 2, and 3, with the scores calculated accordingly.

6MWD

On the day of discharge, the investigators administered the 6MWT and recorded the patients’ final walking distance as the 6MWD.

Other clinical indicators

Duration of chest tube drainage and length of postoperative hospital stay were also recorded. Length of postoperative hospital stay was categorized into three groups: ≤4 days (routine discharge), 5–10 days (delayed discharge), and ≥11 days (significantly prolonged stay). This categorization was based on (I) the distribution characteristics of the hospital stay in our study sample and (II) clinical thresholds commonly reported in thoracic ERAS literature, where 3–5 days is considered routine discharge, 5–10 days represents delayed discharge, and >10 days indicates significantly prolonged hospitalization (21).

Data collection methods

One day before surgery, the investigators screened patients according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria described above, thoroughly explaining the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits, and obtained signed informed consent from each participant. General information was collected through electronic medical records and patient interviews. The investigators assisted patients with early mobilization activities 24 hours after surgery and completed the OI symptom checklist, with the specific timing of first mobilization systematically recorded. Patients completed the QoR-9 scale on postoperative days 1, 2, and 3, and their 6MWD was measured and recorded by the researchers on the day of discharge.

Statistical analysis

Measurement data that met normal distribution were presented using the mean ± standard deviation, with pairwise comparisons performed using the t-test. For data that did not conform to a normal distribution, results were expressed as the median and interquartile range, with the Mann-Whitney U test used for pairwise comparisons. Enumeration data were presented as frequencies and percentages, and pairwise comparisons were performed using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. Repeated QoR-9 measurements were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models, with Bonferroni’s adjustment for multiple comparisons.

PSM was performed to minimize baseline differences between the OI and the non-OI groups. A 1:1 matching approach was conducted using a greedy nearest-neighbor matching algorithm without replacement. No caliper was specified, as the OI group comprised only 64 patients and all were successfully matched; applying a caliper in this context could have unnecessarily reduced the matched sample size and statistical power. Covariates included in the propensity score model were baseline demographic and perioperative variables. After matching, standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated to assess covariate balance, with an SMD <0.10 indicating adequate balance between the groups.

PSM was preferred over conventional multivariable regression for several reasons. First, the OI and non-OI groups exhibited notable baseline differences in key covariates (e.g., sex P<0.001; smoking history P=0.02), and PSM provides a more intuitive and transparent approach to achieving covariate balance between groups (22). Second, PSM reduces reliance on the functional form assumptions inherent in regression models and can be more robust when the overlap of covariate distributions between groups is limited (22). Third, PSM has been widely adopted in observational studies examining exposure effects in surgical populations, where it facilitates a quasi-experimental comparison by constructing matched groups with similar covariate profiles (23).

A two-sided probability of P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were entered by two investigators and analyzed using SAS 9.4 (Copyright©2016 SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Given the observational nature of this study, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed. However, a post hoc power analysis was conducted based on the observed effect sizes to evaluate the adequacy of the sample size. Based on the observed difference in QoR-9 scores on postoperative day 1 (mean difference =−1.36, pooled standard deviation ≈1.81), with n=64 per group and a two-sided α=0.05, the achieved statistical power exceeded 0.90, indicating that the current sample size was adequate for detecting the primary outcome. For the secondary outcomes, particularly the subgroup with hospital stay ≥11 days, the post hoc power was limited due to the small number of events, which is reflected in the wide confidence intervals (CIs) of the corresponding odds ratio (OR) estimates.

Results

A total of 238 patients were initially screened before surgery. However, five patients withdrew, and another five were excluded due to intraoperative changes in the surgical procedure (three pneumectomies, one thoracotomy exploration, and one lobectomy combined with mediastinal lesion resection). Additionally, six patients were unable to perform early ambulation postoperatively due to changes in their condition, and 11 were lost to follow-up. Ultimately, 211 participants were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). Among these, 64 patients developed OI (19 males and 45 females), while the non-OI group included 147 cases (81 males and 66 females).

Comparison of basic characteristics

Before matching, statistically significant differences were observed between the non-OI group and the OI group in sex (P<0.001) and smoking history (P=0.02), whereas no significant difference was found in age (P=0.18). To reduce potential confounding, PSM was applied using a 1:1 greedy nearest-neighbor matching approach. Before matching, the OI group comprised 64 patients and the non-OI group comprised 147 patients. After 1:1 matching, 64 pairs were successfully established (128 patients total), while 83 non-OI patients were excluded. The 83 unmatched non-OI patients were broadly comparable to the matched non-OI subgroup in terms of age and surgical characteristics, though they had a higher proportion of males and smokers, consistent with the direction of the initial baseline imbalance. After matching, (I) there was no significant difference between the OI and non-OI groups in the average time from operation to first mobilization (23.80 vs. 23.92 hours, P=0.88); and (II) no statistically significant differences remained between the two groups in any baseline characteristics or disease-related parameters. All covariates achieved adequate balance, with SMDs less than 0.10, indicating satisfactory comparability between the groups (Table 2).

Comparison of outcome measures

After matching, the longitudinal analysis of QoR-9 scores showed no statistically significant group-by-time interaction effect (F value =2.074, P=0.13), indicating that the recovery trajectories were parallel between the two groups. Although QoR-9 scores increased in both groups during this period, the scores in the OI group were significantly lower than those in the non-OI group at all postoperative time points: postoperative day 1 (difference =−1.36, 95% CI: −1.99 to −0.73), day 2 (difference =−0.91, 95% CI: −1.47 to −0.34), and day 3 (difference =−0.69, 95% CI: −1.18 to −0.20) (all P<0.05), as shown in Table 3. Similar results were observed before matching (Table S1).

Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrate that the occurrence of postoperative OI during early mobilization significantly compromise the quality of early recovery in patients undergoing thoracoscopic pulmonary resection even after accounting for postoperative complications. Specifically, patients with OI reported lower QoR-9 scores and experienced prolonged chest tube drainage, which subsequently contributed to an extended postoperative hospital stay. Importantly, the comparability of postoperative complication rates between the matched OI and non-OI groups provides evidence that these recovery impairments are primarily attributable to OI itself rather than to differential complication burden. Interestingly, while OI negatively impacted subjective recovery experiences and discharge timing, it did not significantly impair functional exercise capacity (as measured by the 6MWD) on the day of discharge. This dissociation between subjective quality of recovery and objective physical capacity highlights the specific burden of autonomic dysfunction in the early postoperative phase.

Occurrence of postoperative OI impacts the quality of early postoperative recovery

The QoR-9 scores in the OI group were substantially lower than those in the non-OI group on postoperative days 1 to 3. The QoR-9 scale provides a comprehensive assessment of the quality of anesthesia and postoperative recovery, evaluating patients’ health status in terms of pain, physical comfort, emotional state, and the ability to resume independent living and communication (19). The reduction in QoR-9 scores is likely directly attributable to the distressing symptoms of OI. Previous studies have identified that the primary symptoms of OI reported by patients after thoracoscopic pulmonary resection include dizziness, nausea, visual dimming or visual blackout, and vomiting (5,24). These symptoms not only cause immediate physical discomfort but may also induce anxiety regarding mobilization, ultimately compromising the overall quality of recovery. Consistent with our findings, a study of 125 patients by Yan et al. (25) demonstrated that postoperative nausea and vomiting were associated with a decrease in the quality of recovery on the first postoperative day, with a dose-dependent decline in recovery quality as symptom severity increased. Furthermore, a positive feedback loop may exist between OI and pain. Other studies have revealed that patients who experience OI tend to report notably higher pain scores during mobilization compared to non-OI patients (5,10,26), and pain exacerbates autonomic instability while fear of OI symptoms heightens pain sensitivity (27). Therefore, the lower QoR-9 scores in the OI group reflect a composite burden of autonomic symptoms and potential pain-autonomic interactions, rather than surgical failure.

Regarding the clinical significance of the observed QoR-9 differences, although a widely accepted minimal clinically important difference (MCID) has not been established for the QoR-9 scale, the QoR-15 scale—a related and well-validated recovery instrument—has a reported MCID of approximately 0.9 to 1.5 points on a normalized scale (28). By analogy, the observed QoR-9 difference on postoperative day 1 (−1.36 points) approaches or reaches this MCID threshold, suggesting that the early impact of OI on recovery quality is clinically meaningful. In contrast, the difference on postoperative day 3 (−0.69 points) may fall below the MCID, indicating that the clinically relevant impact of OI on recovery quality is most pronounced during the first two postoperative days and diminishes thereafter. This temporal pattern is consistent with the understanding that OI is primarily a transient autonomic disturbance that is most severe in the acute postoperative phase.

Occurrence of postoperative OI is associated with an extended duration of chest tube drainage

A critical finding of this study is the association between postoperative OI and an extended duration of chest tube drainage. This relationship reveals a mechanistic pathway through which OI may impede the ERAS process. Primarily, OI hinders effective early mobilization. Several studies have confirmed that the occurrence of postoperative OI is a primary barrier to patients’ early ambulation (5,14). Early mobilization plays a crucial role in recovery by promoting gravitational drainage and lung re-expansion (29). We postulate that the primary mechanism is the restriction of effective mobilization: when patients experience OI, they limit their ambulation frequency, which likely leads to insufficient pleural negative pressure generation and fluid stasis, thereby delaying the clinical criteria for tube removal. It is important to note that chest tube removal at our institution was based on objective criteria (drainage volume <200 mL/24 h, absence of persistent air leak, and radiographic confirmation of lung re-expansion) independent of ambulatory status. Therefore, the prolonged drainage duration in the OI group is unlikely to be an artifact of the chest tube removal protocol. However, an alternative explanation should be considered: autonomic dysfunction associated with OI may influence local blood flow and serous fluid production through sympathetic dysregulation, potentially contributing to prolonged pleural drainage (30,31). On a physiological level, autonomic nervous system (ANS) dysfunction itself may play a physiological role. OI is characterized by an inability to maintain cerebral and central perfusion upon standing, driven by sympathetic withdrawal or excessive parasympathetic tone (30).

Perioperative factors such as surgical stress and residual anesthetic effects further exacerbate this dysregulation (7,30). Autonomic dysfunction is believed to potentially influence local blood flow, interfere with inflammatory responses, and impede tissue repair processes (31), which could theoretically prolong serous fluid production. Joshi et al. (7) recently highlighted that postoperative OI encompasses a spectrum ranging from benign positional dizziness to significant hemodynamic compromise, and emphasized the importance of risk stratification and targeted perioperative management strategies. Our findings support their call for proactive OI screening and individualized management protocols, as the downstream consequences of OI—including prolonged drainage and delayed discharge—are clinically significant. Consequently, preventing OI is not merely about symptom management but is crucial for achieving the mechanical milestones of lung recovery. Proactive and tailored measures should be implemented, including precise multimodal analgesia (12), postural adaptive training (32), incremental exercise protocols (30), and standardized repetitive activity procedures (33). Furthermore, integrating OI risk assessment into preoperative evaluation, as suggested by Joshi et al. (7), may enable early identification of high-risk patients and timely implementation of preventive strategies. Encouraging patients to begin ambulation as early as safely possible remains a key strategy for reducing drainage duration.

Occurrence of postoperative OI impacts the length of hospital stay

The results indicated that patients in the OI group experienced a notably prolonged postoperative hospital stay. Although the risk of prolonged hospitalization (≥11 days) was statistically higher in the OI group, the wide CI (OR =10.03, 95% CI: 1.18–84.96) suggests that while the risk is real, the precise magnitude requires verification in larger cohorts. Nevertheless, the trend aligns with previous research by Skarin et al. (16) and others (14,26). The extension in length of stay is logically interpreted as a downstream consequence of the prolonged chest tube drainage and the physiological instability associated with OI. As early out-of-bed activity is a cornerstone of ERAS, the onset of OI represents a significant obstacle to successful recovery trajectories. During early mobilization, patients may experience acute OI symptoms such as orthostatic hypotension and cerebral hypoperfusion, manifested as dizziness, nausea, transient vision loss, or syncope (5,34). The absence of an adaptive cardiovascular response leads to decreased cardiac output, which not only hinders mobilization but also raises safety concerns. Specifically, OI increases the risk of fall-associated complications, such as delayed wound healing, fractures, and potentially life-threatening craniocerebral injuries (8,26). Therefore, the extended hospitalization may also reflect a necessary precaution by healthcare providers to ensure patient safety and hemodynamic stability before discharge.

It should also be acknowledged that the ERAS protocol implemented at our institution differs from the ESTS-ERAS 2019 recommendations (1) in certain aspects, including the timing of postoperative oral intake and early mobilization (see Section “Perioperative management” and Table 1). Had a more aggressive early mobilization strategy (e.g., ambulation within 4–6 hours postoperatively as recommended by the ESTS-ERAS guidelines) been adopted, the incidence and severity of OI might have differed. Future studies comparing ERAS protocols with varying mobilization timelines are warranted to determine the optimal balance between early ambulation and OI risk.

Occurrence of postoperative OI has no significant impact on the postoperative complications

A critical methodological consideration in this study is whether the observed associations between OI and recovery outcomes might be confounded by postoperative complications. Complications such as pulmonary atelectasis, arrhythmia, and wound infections could independently delay chest tube removal and prolong hospitalization, and might also increase OI susceptibility through hemodynamic instability or prolonged bed rest (35,36). However, our systematic complication assessment revealed that, after PSM, the overall complication rates were comparable between the OI and non-OI groups (6.25% vs. 12.50%, P=0.23). Therefore, the differential recovery trajectories observed between the two matched groups are unlikely to be explained primarily by the postoperative complication burden, supporting the conclusion that OI has an independent adverse effect on early recovery quality.

Occurrence of postoperative OI has no significant impact on the 6MWD at discharge

Contrary to the early recovery scores, there was no significant difference in the 6MWD between the OI and non-OI groups on the day of discharge. This dissociation suggests that OI in this population is primarily a transient autonomic regulation disorder rather than a reflection of permanent cardiopulmonary impairment. Specifically, OI is likely triggered by transient autonomic dysfunction or insufficient circulatory compensation during postural changes (7), whereas the 6MWD reflects the patient’s overall sustained cardiopulmonary reserve and skeletal muscle function (20). Since thoracoscopic surgery is minimally invasive and preserves thoracic cage stability (13), patients’ physical and musculoskeletal capacity for ambulation remains preserved. The discrepancy between the low QoR-9 scores in the early phase and the preserved 6MWD at the discharge phase implies that OI symptoms are most severe in the acute postoperative phase when fluid shifts are prominent. By the day of discharge, autonomic regulation may have stabilized. However, this does not negate the importance of managing OI, as the associated early distress and delayed discharge represent significant clinical burdens, even if functional capacity eventually normalizes.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the single-centre design and relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings to other institutions or broader patient populations. Furthermore, due to the small sample size, we did not conduct a subgroup analysis of the timing of the first mobilization based on a narrower postoperative time interval. Which may have limited our ability to detect differential OI effects by timing. Additionally, for low-frequency event outcomes (such as the subgroup with a hospital stay ≥11 days), the post hoc statistical power was insufficient, and the wide CIs of the OR estimates reflect this imprecision. Second, as with any observational study, despite our rigorous use of PSM to balance baseline characteristics, the potential for residual confounding from unmeasured variables (such as preoperative hydration status, baseline autonomic tone, intraoperative fluid balance, and psychological resilience) cannot be entirely excluded. Additionally, the 1:1 matching approach resulted in the exclusion of 83 non-OI patients (56.5%), which may affect the generalizability of our results. Future studies could consider using multivariable regression or inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) as sensitivity analyses to utilize the full sample and validate the robustness of the findings. Third, despite these adjustments, unmeasured aspects of complication severity could still introduce residual confounding, and future studies with more granular complication coding (e.g., the Clavien-Dindo classification) are warranted. Fourth, although chest tube removal was based on objective criteria independent of ambulatory status, we cannot entirely exclude the possibility that autonomic dysfunction associated with OI may have contributed to prolonged pleural drainage through mechanisms other than reduced mobility, such as altered fluid regulation. Finally, the absence of preoperative baseline 6MWD measurements prevents us from evaluating the change in individual exercise tolerance (Δvalues). However, given that the groups were well-matched in demographic and clinical characteristics, the post-discharge comparison still provides valid cross-sectional evidence of functional capacity.

Conclusions

Postoperative OI is a frequent and clinically significant complication during early mobilization following thoracoscopic pulmonary resection. The occurrence of OI is associated with clinically meaningful reductions in early recovery quality, particularly during the first two postoperative days, and serves as a mechanical barrier to ERAS efficiency by prolonging chest tube drainage and hospital stay. Notably, while OI creates substantial early postoperative distress and logistical delays, it appears to be a transient autonomic dysfunction that does not permanently impair functional walking capacity at discharge. Therefore, preventing OI should be prioritized as a modifiable target for quality improvement. Healthcare providers should integrate active OI risk screening and tailored mobilization protocols into standard care to optimize the trajectory of enhanced recovery.
