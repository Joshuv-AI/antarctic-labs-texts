Toxin-Antitoxin Systems Reflect Community Interactions Through Horizontal Gene Transfer
Authors: Jonathan H Bethke, Jeffrey Kimbrel, Yongqin Jiao, Dante Ricci, Adi Stern
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacterial evolution through horizontal gene transfer (HGT) reflects their community interactions. In this way, HGT networks do well at mapping community interactions, but offer little toward controlling them—an important step in the translation of synthetic strains into natural contexts. Toxin–antitoxin (TA) systems serve as ubiquitous and diverse agents of selection; however, their utility is limited by their erratic distribution in hosts. Here we examine the heterogeneous distribution of TAs as a consequence of their mobility. By systematically mapping TA systems across a 10,000 plasmid network, we find HGT communities have unique and predictable TA signatures. We propose these TA signatures arise from plasmid competition and have further potential to signal the degree to which plasmids, hosts, and phage interact. To emphasize these relationships, we construct an HGT network based solely on TA similarity, framing specific selection markers in the broader context of bacterial communities. This work both clarifies the evolution of TA systems and unlocks a common framework for manipulating community interactions through TA compatibility.Keywords: toxin–antitoxin system, horizontal gene transfer, community, plasmid, network

Introduction

Much of the diversity in bacterial genomes stems from horizontal gene transfer (HGT), connecting otherwise asexual cells to a communal gene pool that crosses taxonomic boundaries. The availability of genes in the pool depends on the local environment, selection pressures, and population densities (Smillie et al. 2011). Bacterial evolution through HGT is therefore a reflection of community dynamics and interactions (Norman et al. 2009). HGT network analyses effectively survey complex bacterial communities at scales beyond single environment sampling, but provide little guidance toward interfacing with them (Brito 2021; Garcillan-Barcia et al. 2023). Knowing what species are where, and how to interact with them, are both needed to address long-standing challenges in real-world applications, such as protecting engineered strains from or introducing them to target communities (e.g. biocontainment or probiotic therapy) (Garci and Diaz 2014; Lee et al. 2018; Lawson et al. 2019; Yim and Wang 2021).

Toxin–antitoxin (TA) systems serve as genetically encoded antibiotics to regulate microbial populations (Hayes and Van Melderen 2011; Wright et al. 2015; Jurenas et al. 2022; Boss and Kedzierska 2023). Generally, stable toxins pair with unstable antitoxins, forming systems that inhibit or kill their host under disruptive conditions. Viral infection, plasmid competition, and other consequences of HGT naturally trigger TA systems (Jurenas et al. 2022; LeRoux and Laub 2022). Synthetic triggers, such as unsuccessful cloning or expression, can be incorporated for custom selection (Wright et al. 2015; Chan et al. 2016; Lee et al. 2018). However, practical use of TA systems is limited by their irregular distribution in nature. For example, engineered suicide strains can be rescued by local antitoxin, while toxins acquired through HGT can eliminate otherwise stable, probiotic microbes.

Little is known about the evolution and distribution of TA systems. Despite their ubiquity and relevance to cell physiology, TA systems are generally not conserved, even within the same species (Makarova et al. 2009; Fiedoruk et al. 2015; Akarsu et al. 2019; Kandel et al. 2021; Jurenas et al. 2022). From the perspective of a host cell, their actions seem paradoxical: TA systems can kill hosts when lost (i.e. post-segregation killing) yet save hosts from phage and, controversially, other stress (LeRoux et al. 2020). We propose these contradictory actions originate from mobile genetic elements, which derive fitness benefits from both host replication and horizontal transfer (Bethke et al. 2023). Independence from specific hosts supports selfish TA maintenance, even at a cost to the current host. Indeed, most TA systems are associated with mobile genetic elements and chromosomal TAs are often found in genomic islands, undergoing genetic decay as if detached from their original selection pressure (Hayes and Van Melderen 2011; Leplae et al. 2011). HGT networks may then reveal how TA systems evolve and spread, and TA systems may provide a common framework for influencing community interactions through HGT (Yim and Wang 2021).

Only recently have large-scale, comparative studies of mobile genetic elements become feasible, providing an opportunity to gain insight into TA evolution and distribution. Here we explored an established plasmid HGT network using Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) from the TASmania TA database (Akarsu et al. 2019; Redondo-Salvo et al. 2020). Over 40,000 toxins and antitoxins were found in total, with TAs present in virtually every community and most plasmids over 10 kb. We show that HGT communities have unique TA signatures such that plasmid membership can be accurately predicted from common TA systems. While host taxa and mobilization (MOB) relaxase showed little correlation with plasmid's TA signature, they may broadly shape the communal TA pool. Discovering order in the distribution of TA systems clarifies long-standing questions about their evolution and provides broad context for their application in natural community settings. From TA prevalence and frequency, we further predict TA efficacy, host range, and host ecology for rational community design.

Materials and Methods

Sequences and Metadata

Plasmid sequences and metadata were derived from the network published in Redondo-Salvo et al., containing 10,634 putative complete plasmid sequences from the NCBI RefSeq database. Additional curation was performed to remove suspected duplicate plasmids with <1,000 bp size difference, same host species, and the same number of TA sequences. In total, 853 plasmids were removed as suspected repeats listed in supplementary information, Supplementary Material online. Plasmids without TAs were not considered for removal given their often small size and increased ambiguity in comparisons.

Annotating TA Systems, MOB Relaxases, and Incompatibility Groups

Type I to IV toxins and antitoxins were annotated with the HMM profiles provided by the TASmania database as of 2023 February 11. HMMsearch version 3.3.2 was used with e-values set to <0.001 (Finn et al. 2011). From ∼300,000 hits, repeat annotations were consolidated for a total of ∼40,000 unique toxins or antitoxins across the plasmid sequence library. Summary results per plasmid are available in supplementary information, Supplementary Material online.

MOB relaxase typing was performed using MOBscan and incompatibility groups were assigned with PlasmidFinder version 1.3, using BLAST + v-2.6.0, >80% sequence identity and >60% length as in Redondo-Salvo et al. (Carattoli et al. 2014).

Statistical Analyses and Modeling

Statistical analysis was performed in R version 4.2.2. Predicted toxins and antitoxins were considered individually such that 1 toxin and 1 antitoxin equaled a TA value of 2. All TA data was included in total TA, TA ratio, and TA frequency analyses. For inclusion in similarity and prevalence analyses, we required TAs to be present in ≥5 plasmids. This was to improve quality and avoid erroneous conclusions from biased or limited data.

We constructed our random forest with the randomForest package version 4.7-1.1 with parameters: proximity = T, ntree = 1000, mtry = 25, importance = T. Follow-up importance calculations were performed with parameters: type = 1, scale = F (Liaw and Wiener 2002). For consistency, the dataset used to construct the TA network (i.e. ≥30 kb or ≥3 total TAs thresholds) was also to generate our random forest model. PTUs with <15 plasmids and TAs present in <20 plasmids were included to avoid drawing conclusions from limited data, improve the ratio of features: plasmids, and fit within memory constraints.

Network Construction

The TA network was constructed based on meaningful TA similarity or dissimilarities not caused by rare TAs, plasmid size constraints, or generic annotations. Sørensen distance indices were chosen for comparison under the assumption that repeat TAs are functionally redundant. Outside of calculations of total TAs and TA frequencies per plasmid, generic TAs labeled as hypothetical proteins were excluded when considering TA similarity.

Small plasmids tend to have less total TAs than large plasmids. With too great a difference in total TAs, a small plasmid may never match with a large plasmid under a Sørensen distance threshold. To ensure pairings between most plasmids were possible, we determined at what size the majority of plasmids have TAs (∼30 kb) and the median number of TAs (4 to 5) per plasmid. Requiring plasmids to be ≥30 kb or have ≥3 TAs therefore allows for most plasmids in the network to pair under a 0.3 Sørensen distance threshold. In this way, small plasmids with many TAs and large plasmids with few are both preserved and meaningful.

We used Gephi version 0.10.1 to visualize the TA similarity network (Bastian et al. 2009). The ForceAtlas 2 model was applied with the following parameters: tolerance = 1.0, approximate repulsion = T, approximation = 1.2, scaling = 5.0, stronger gravity = F, gravity = 15.0, dissuade hubs = F, lin-log mode = T, prevent overlap = F, edge weight influence = 1.0, normalize edge weights = F, inverted edge weights = F (Jacomy et al. 2014). Edges were weighted by 1—Sørensen distance. To reduce minor visual clutter, we filtered out plasmids with <4 connections. Edges with Sørensen distances >0.3 were filtered out according to our intra-cluster diversity analysis. A Sørensen distance threshold of 0.3 served as an optimized tradeoff of intra-cluster PTU, MOB, and Inc group consistency with inter-cluster edge diversity.

Results

The Distribution of TA Systems

Toward a general classification system of plasmids, several whole-sequence-based HGT networks have recently been published (Acman et al. 2020; Redondo-Salvo et al. 2020; Garcillan-Barcia et al. 2023). From 10,634 bacterial and archaeal plasmids in RefSeq84 database, Redondo-Salvo et al. defined a set of Plasmid Taxonomic Unit (PTU) clusters formed by plasmid pairs having ≥70% nucleotide identity over ≥50% of the smaller plasmid sequence. The 50% length threshold filters out connections based solely on common, small elements like transposons, prophages, or TA systems. Utilizing this curated set of plasmid sequences, established network structure, and PTU classification system, we examined the distribution of TA systems across plasmids.

We employed the TASmania database to identify TA systems across the HGT network (Akarsu et al. 2019). TASmania uniquely scans for diverse Type I to IV TA loci and allows for unexpected combinations. Across the 10,634 plasmids, TASmania predicted 40,000 distinct toxins and antitoxins. A toxin or antitoxin was predicted in 63% of all plasmids with an average of 3.4 per plasmid. The frequency of plasmids with TAs increases dramatically over 40 kb and virtually every plasmid exceeding 100 kb was predicted to have at least one T or A (Fig. 1a). This threshold aligns with the division in the size distribution attributed to smaller, mobilizable versus larger, conjugative plasmids (Smillie et al. 2010). The spike in TA carriage may be attributed to behaviors that initiate competition, such as conjugation, which likely selects genes that grant competitive advantages, such as TA systems (Samuel et al. 2024).

Regardless of plasmid mobility, there exists a log-linear relationship between plasmid size and total number of TAs (Fig. 1b, R2 = 0.49, P < 0.001). To account for this size effect in downstream analyses, we used TA frequency (TA per bp), compared within plasmid size bins, and set minimum TA thresholds for similarity comparisons. TA frequency revealed an opposing size effect: whereas larger plasmids tend to contain more coding sequences and TA systems, smaller plasmids often exhibit higher frequencies of TA systems (Fig. 1c). On average, TA systems are 2.1 times more prevalent in small plasmids (<50 kb) than large plasmids (>150 kb), accounting for size and including plasmids without any TA systems. Elevated TA frequencies were not limited to the smallest plasmids; instead, they continuously declined with plasmid size until approximately 500 kb. As seen with restriction-modification systems (Shaw et al. 2023), this trend may indicate a changing fitness benefit of TA systems with plasmid size. Smaller plasmids tend to have fewer beneficial accessory genes and higher copy number while being more likely to cohabit (Smillie et al. 2010), increasing opportunities for plasmid competition and consequently, selection for TA systems (San Millan et al. 2014). Conversely, large plasmids may reach saturation points where additional TA systems offer marginal benefits and cross-talk between genetic elements may alleviate TA selection.

Host burden and pairing requirements for toxins also affect numbers of TAs. The abundance, continuous expression, and potential impact of TA systems imply a degree of burden beyond a TA threshold. For example, Escherichia coli K-12 and Mycobacterium tuberculosis were found to have 12 and 76 Type II TA systems, respectively, and the expression of a single TA system is sufficient to reduce host fitness in vitro (Cooper and Heinemann 2000; Fraikin et al. 2020). To assess whether negative selection against increasing TAs was reflected in plasmid genomes, we related TA frequency and total number of TAs per plasmid. We found TA frequency remains relatively stable with a minor reduction at higher numbers of TAs (Fig. 1d). This may indicate hosts' ability to harbor multiple TA systems without major fitness loss, or alternatively, host burden having little impact on the selfish propagation of plasmid TAs.

Toxins must be restrained from inhibiting their host, typically through a cognate antitoxin. Antitoxins can exist on their own without harm to the host, but they may undergo gene decay when separated from their selected function. We therefore expected to find an approximate 1:1 ratio between toxins and antitoxins within and across all plasmids as a positive control for TA discovery. Our findings generally supported this expectation (Fig. 1e, R2 = 0.64, P < 0.001). There was a skew toward more antitoxins with 73% of A:T ratios >1. This aligned with intuition given the toxic alternative. Still, there were plasmids carrying more toxins than antitoxins, suggesting either antitoxin promiscuity or toxin inactivity. Pairing with chromosomal antitoxins may also occur, although a split arrangement limits the spread of mobile plasmids (Wright et al. 2015; Chlebek et al. 2023).

TA Systems Cluster by HGT

We next examined the distribution of TA systems within the HGT network structure independently validated by Redondo-Salvo et al. Inconsistent carriage within species and conditional protective or harmful effects on hosts support hypotheses of TA systems' mobile origins (Jurenas et al. 2022). However, mobility alone would not structure the distribution of TAs across plasmids. Instead, structure is predicted to emerge from TA–TA interactions and plasmid ecology (Cooper and Heinemann 2000; Cooper et al. 2010). Plasmid competition through HGT, not vertical transfer through host addiction, is thought to drive TA selection. We outline these interactions using a model of two competing plasmids unable to stably coexist within the same host cell (Fig. 2).

Selection dynamics of TA systems. Each panel represents a plasmid competition scenario in which two plasmids (inner circles) cannot stably coexist within the same host cell. Numbers and colors reflect the number and type of TA systems in either plasmids or host chromosomes. The scenarios progress in a logical, but not necessary order. a) No TA systems are present, allowing either plasmid to win post-segregation. b) A plasmid acquires a TA system (TA+) and competes against a TA− plasmid, resulting in the TA+ plasmid winning or the TA− plasmid losing due to post-segregation toxin activity. c) Both plasmids acquire the same TA system, allowing either plasmid to win as long as they maintain it. d) Chromosomal integration of the TA system grants the host immunity against post-segregation killing by plasmids. Host immunity removes the competitive advantage of TA+ plasmids, allowing either plasmid to win while facilitating TA mutation, loss, or gain. e) Acquiring one novel TA more than the host and competitor combined (i.e. TA+1) restores the competitive TA advantage.

We hypothesized that HGT communities, or PTUs, evolve distinct and conserved sets of TAs through competition. To compare sets of TA systems across plasmids, we quantified Sørensen distances and displayed TA similarity through edge color in the HGT network (Fig. 3a). Sørensen distance, ranging from 0 to 1, serves as a simple measure of similarity between datasets (i.e. TAs per plasmid) where lower distances indicate greater similarity. Grouping edges by low (0 to 0.33), medium (0.34 to 0.66), and high (0.67 to 1.00) Sørensen distance provided clearer insight into how TA relationships change across the HGT network (supplementary fig. S1, Supplementary Material online). We found edges with low distance (i.e. high TA similarity) formed cohesive clusters, which became more diffuse and interconnected as TA similarity decreased. This suggests HGT communities have internally consistent TA systems that differ from other communities, exemplified by the distribution of 8 select TAs across the network (Fig. 3b).

However, our analysis was constrained to edges formed by sequences with ≥70% identity over ≥50% of the smaller plasmid's length. As TAs are themselves sequences, they may influence edge formation and contribute to the observed patterns. To examine the impact of TAs on the average nucleotide identities (ANI) used to construct the HGT network, we related TA similarity to ANI distances. We found only a weak positive correlation (supplementary fig. S2, Supplementary Material online, R2 = 0.10, P < 0.001) while also accounting for the effects of plasmid size on TA similarity. Indeed, TA systems represent a small fraction of the median 72 kb plasmid size, and the 50% plasmid length threshold requirement for ANI pairings was intended to mitigate the influence of common genetic elements on network structure.

We next calculated Sørensen distances between the set of TA systems on every plasmid, regardless of network structure or classification groupings (Fig. 3c and d). To reduce bias, we only included plasmids ≥30 kb or with ≥3 TA systems. This approach allowed meaningfully large, TA-poor plasmids and small, TA-rich plasmids in the analysis while reducing high Sørensen distances resulting solely from the correlation between TA number and plasmid size. These thresholds were established based on the plasmid size where the majority carries TAs and the median number of TA systems per plasmid, respectively (supplementary fig. 3a–d, Supplementary Material online). In addition to PTUs from the HGT network, we considered three traditional classifications as controls: host taxa, MOB relaxases, and incompatibility (Inc) groups. For each classification structure, high intra-group TA similarity and low inter-group similarity indicate relevance to TA evolution. Similarity by host taxa would suggest ties to host physiology; by MOB relaxase, ties to the mechanism of plasmid conjugation; by incompatibility, ties to plasmid replication and competition; by PTU, ties to HGT community interactions.

Both PTUs and specific Inc groups displayed significant intra-group TA similarity and inter-group dissimilarity (Fig. 3c). In contrast, intra-MOB TA systems could not be distinguished from inter-MOB TA systems and, when grouped by lowest common host taxa, plasmid TA systems were generally inconsistent down to the species level (Fig. 3d). These results strongly support HGT-mediated TA evolution for both plasmids and their hosts, especially considering the mobile origin of many chromosomal TAs. Furthermore, this distribution of TAs suggests HGT communities have unique TA signatures, such that community membership can be predicted and manipulated through TAs.

Predicting TA Systems for Community Design

HGT communities displayed internal TA consistency and external TA differences. We next conducted random forest modeling to assess whether individual plasmids could be sorted into specific HGT communities from their TA profiles alone. For accuracy, PTUs and TAs with limited data were excluded. Our model achieved an out-of-bag error rate of 5%, indicating 95% accuracy in assigning plasmids to their corresponding PTUs based solely on TA profiles. With additional data, the inclusion of more TA systems would likely improve prediction accuracy.

Our random forest analysis also revealed the TA systems that effectively distinguish HGT communities from each other (supplementary fig. S4a, Supplementary Material online). ParB/RepB/Spo0J, Hok/Gef, RelE/ParE, and PemI coding sequences were among the most influential on model accuracy. Interestingly, DNA methylases emerged as a significant form of antitoxin against restriction enzymes. Most PTUs exhibited clear distinctions from one another, with a few exceptions (supplementary fig. S4b, Supplementary Material online). PTUs B/O/K/Z, FK3, and X4 were confused with PTUs I1, FE, and Cam1, respectively. PTUs are generally named after dominant plasmid Inc groups, taxa, or both (e.g. IncF Escherichia as PTU-FE or IncF Klebsiella as PTU-FK). Such confusion may reflect inherent limits of using TA systems to represent whole plasmid sequences. However, it may also offer unique, focused insight into plasmid competition dynamics. Within the same PTU, multiple Inc groups and taxa may co-exist, leading to potential competitive subdivisions that TA similarity can help disentangle. For example, analysis of the incompatibility confusion matrix suggests that IncFI and IncFII plasmids compete more with IncFI/FII plasmids than with each other (supplementary fig. S4c, Supplementary Material online).

If TA systems align with HGT communities and affect competitive outcomes, hosts and plasmids could be designed for specific roles within target communities. Shared TA systems foster coexistence and coevolution by allowing plasmid exchange without host death, whereas unshared TA systems reinforce division. Therefore, a plasmid carrying TA systems common to its target community faces less challenge for engraftment than the same plasmid without. This falls in line with the broader theory of community stabilization through competition (Coyte et al. 2015), which is now being extended to localized antibiotic producers and resistance genes (Spagnolo et al. 2021).

Toward community-focused design, we analyzed the prevalence of specific TAs across PTUs. In each PTU, we identified TAs that were prevalent or absent, comprising consistent core signatures (Fig. 4). Intermediate TA prevalence may indicate a transitory state, diverse competition, or variable TA functionality across hosts within a given HGT community. Together, prevalent and absent TAs can be used in designing constructs that are compatible or incompatible, respectively, with the target community.

In general, the prevalence of a gene implies functionality and predicted TAs that are rare have increased need for functional validation. For reference, in tests of putative TASmania hits not found by alternative TAfinder or TADB2 databases, 6/11 (55%) TAs were confirmed. However, neither overall and local prevalence, nor PTU and host ranges necessarily align. We, therefore, extended our analysis to hosts, considering both overall and local prevalence down to the genus level (supplementary table S1, Supplementary Material online).

Plasmids with complete host taxonomy spanned a diverse set of 23 phyla, 49 classes, 103 orders, 193 families, 437 genera, and 1,428 species. Predicted TAs displayed varied host ranges, from single species to 34% of all species present in the dataset, with ParB/RepB/Spo0J, RelE/ParE, and VapC being the most widespread. The median unique taxa per TA were 2 phyla, 3 classes, 3 orders, 4 families, 5 genera, and 7 species. TA host diversity showed a general correlation with prevalence, although many uncommon, predicted TAs were found across broad host ranges. While most TAs with narrow host ranges also exhibit low overall prevalence, there were notable exceptions. For example, two putative antitoxins exclusive to Coxiella burnetii were found on 9/10 plasmids isolated from C. burnetii. Interestingly, C. burnetii is an obligate intracellular pathogen, which may explain the prevalence of these isolated antitoxins.

Predicting Plasmid Competition for Community Design

Plasmid competition selects for TA systems and more competition increases this selection pressure (Fig. 2) (Cooper and Heinemann 2000; Pandey and Gerdes 2005; Cooper et al. 2010). Shared TAs ensure host survival regardless of the competitive outcome, while newly acquired TAs confer a competitive advantage. For a plasmid to minimize losses and ensure survival following competitive exclusion, a simple aim is to maintain competitors' TAs plus one additional TA. This TA+1 concept represents a positive feedback loop, whereby increased competition leads to higher TA frequencies, up to limits imposed by burden, plasmid size, hosts, etc. A similar relationship has been proposed between whole-genome HGT and chromosomal TA systems (Cooper et al. 2010). High rates of HGT, high burdens, and shared replication machinery all contribute toward plasmid–plasmid competition, and therefore TA frequency. As a general reflection of plasmid–plasmid competition, TA identity and frequency may then return to the original, functional premise of the plasmid incompatibility classification system on a community scale.

Here we examined whether plasmids demonstrate unique and consistent TA frequencies at community scales, suggesting high-level differences in their degrees of competition. For reference, small TA-dense plasmids set the upper bound of TA frequency near 1 TA per kb, while larger plasmids typically ranged between 1 TA per 5 to 40 kb. Interestingly, plasmids of a given PTU tended to have similar TA frequencies, as may be expected from sharing the same competitive environment (Fig. 5a). This consistency emerged despite internal differences in plasmid size, Inc, MOB, and host taxa. Of particular note, PTU-N1 and PTU-FE represent two ends of the TA frequency range, yet both are comprised of Escherichia, Klebsiella, and Salmonella hosts and MOBF plasmids. A difference in IncN and IncF groups therefore appeared to drive significant TA frequency differences (P < 0.001, Tukey HSD), equating to a difference of approximately 6 TAs for comparable 75 kb plasmids.

It may be that the narrow host range of IncF plasmids concentrates competition, in agreement with their TA overlaps in supplementary fig. S4c, Supplementary Material online. We then expanded our TA frequency analysis to compare Inc host ranges. Overall, narrow host range plasmids had significantly higher TA frequencies relative to broad host range plasmids within similar size ranges (F[5, 819] = 3.891, P= 0.0017, ANOVA, Fig. 5b) (Suzuki et al. 2010). The ecological factors driving frequencies of broad HGT events may therefore extend to TA frequencies (Smillie et al. 2011). Such an effect was apparent in the relative abundance of TAs in free-living versus host-associated bacteria (Pandey and Gerdes 2005). Although it remains unclear if host lifestyle is reflected in plasmid TA frequency, plasmids from intracellular Borrelia, spore-forming Bacillus, and antibiotic-producing Streptomyces consistently displayed among the lowest TA frequencies (supplementary fig. S5, Supplementary Material online).

Creating a TA Network

Whole plasmid networks suffer from computationally intensive alignments and repetitive sequences that artificially inflate similarity (Redondo-Salvo et al. 2020). Mitigating the effects of repetitive sequences often requires lengthy sequence matching or meticulous curation. However, it is unlikely that all elements contained within large DNA regions are relevant to network applications. Narrowing sequence comparisons to specific features (e.g. antibiotic resistance genes) can grant practical clarity, but may fail to convey broader, network-scale interactions among plasmids.

A network based on TA systems offers practical specificity and broad applicability. Decades of research have elucidated TA mechanisms and our results indicate that TA systems can accurately reflect HGT communities assembled from whole plasmid sequences. A TA-based network therefore retains the identities of HGT communities while emphasizing plasmid interactions, guiding design choices for invasion or biocontainment strategies. In this network, nodes represent plasmids carrying TA systems and undirected edges indicate TA similarity that exceeds a predetermined threshold. The similarity threshold is defined by balancing cluster homogeneity and edge diversity, ranging from isolated communities with identical TA systems to a unified community with every node interconnected.

We achieved this balance by constructing 100 TA networks with varying similarity thresholds and measuring the proportion of intra-group edges across MOB, Inc, PTU, and host taxa classifications (supplementary fig. S3e, Supplementary Material online). As expected, stricter TA thresholds led to increased cluster homogeneity within PTU, Inc, and MOB groups, suggesting TAs primarily distributed by HGT. Conversely, taxonomic groupings showed minimal sensitivity to similarity thresholds, with intra-Phyla edges increasing with more relaxed TA thresholds. Cluster homogeneity markedly declined past a similarity threshold of 0.33, prompting a cutoff at 0.30 to balance edge diversity and cluster agreement with traditional plasmid classification. With a TA similarity threshold set at 0.30, we assessed the significance of the plasmid arrangement within the network structure. Out of 1,000 permutations of plasmid features, only the true plasmid data performed well in the network structure, yielding >50% intra-edges among PTUs and Inc groups compared with the <5% intra-group edges observed for randomized features (supplementary fig. S3f, Supplementary Material online).

This HGT network, structured by TA similarity across 337 predicted TAs, is composed of 2,850 plasmid nodes and 28,959 edges (Fig. 6). Host diversity is retained despite filtering by TA number and plasmid size, with 641/1,034 (62%) species, 245/358 (68%) genera, 120/162 (74%) families, 62/76 (82%) orders, 27/35 (77%) classes, and 15/19 (79%) phyla from the TA-containing plasmids in the complete dataset. Well-characterized Enterobacteriaceae hosts remain over-represented, constituting 36% of nodes.

Clusters align well with PTUs, except for PTU-FE (supplementary fig. S6, Supplementary Material online). Interestingly, Redondo-Salvo et al. noted that PTU-FE had the lowest intra-cluster density of any PTU and suggested it may be better presented as multiple different clusters. With TA similarity, PTU-FE naturally separates into IncFII and IncFI/FII incompatibility groups, highlighting competitive distinctions that were overshadowed in whole sequence comparisons (supplementary fig. S7, Supplementary Material online). While PTUs and Inc groups defined individual clusters, higher level organization spontaneously emerged along host taxa and MOB group lines. Major TA divisions manifested between the gram-negative Gammaproteobacteria, gram-positive Bacilli, and oligotrophic Alphaproteobacteria classes consistent with fundamental HGT barriers (supplementary fig. S8, Supplementary Material online) (Thomas and Nielsen 2005). MOBF, MOBP, and to a lesser extent, MOBQ also occupied distinct regions of the TA network (supplementary fig. S9, Supplementary Material online). Therefore, while specific HGT communities and competitive interactions may influence the specific TAs a plasmid carries (Figs. 3 and 4), the host and the plasmid's mobility potential likely determine the pool of TAs available to plasmids (Fig. 6 and supplementary figs. 8 and 9, Supplementary Material online). In this network context, we visualized the range of 3 individual TAs in supplementary figs. 10 to 12, Supplementary Material online.

The unique, interaction-focused scope of our TA network uncovered many new associations involving uncharacterized plasmids. These plasmids often occupied positions at the intersections of minor clusters and major taxa divisions within the network (i.e. black nodes, supplementary figs. S6, S7 and S9, Supplementary Material online). This in-between placement was not due to a lack of TAs or plasmid size, particularly given the 30 kb or 3 TA minimum thresholds we applied (supplementary figs. S13 and S14, Supplementary Material online). These plasmids may represent important yet understudied intermediates in gene transfer between distinct bacterial communities.

Discussion

Decades of work on TA systems have yielded mechanistic understanding of multiple TA families without clear drivers of their distribution and evolution (Jurenas et al. 2022). Confusion arises from TAs' ubiquity without conservation, and context-dependent protective (e.g. against phage infection) or harmful effects (e.g. post plasmid-segregation killing) on the host. This confusion limits practical application of diverse TA selection markers in natural settings, where unknown local toxins or antitoxins may disrupt synthetic systems and targeted antibiotic therapies through HGT (Boss and Kedzierska 2023). Metagenomic data provides broad compositional perspectives by abstracting species to 16S rRNAs, selection dynamics to conserved genes, and HGT to shared genes, but lacks clear mechanisms for manipulating identified communities. With growing evidence tying TA selection to mobile genetic elements, the issues surrounding TA distribution and community control may be complementary. Systematic mapping of TA systems across an established 10,634 plasmid network revealed consistent community-level TA organization that may be exploited for ecologically conscious strain designs and antibiotic therapies (Figs. 3 and 6).

TA systems are well-suited for natural community selection. Most plasmids (63%) have TAs, many have multiple, and strains appear robust to their burden (Fig. 1). Their diverse, but consistent poison and antidote mechanism not only selects for TA carriers but promotes carriage of the same TAs by interacting strains (Figs. 2 and 3). This effect manifests in natural HGT communities having identifiable TA signatures, with models achieving up to 95% accuracy in assigning plasmids solely by common TA systems (Fig. 4 and supplementary fig. S4, Supplementary Material online). Through repeat competition, communities may build internal TA cohesion while also defending against external invasion, thereby expanding upon the formation, maintenance, and practical application of HGT communities.

TA frequency in genomes may reflect degrees of competitive interactions. For example, higher TA frequencies are predicted for chromosomes experiencing increased competition, while intracellular host lifestyles are linked to a distinct absence of TA systems (Pandey and Gerdes 2005; Cooper et al. 2010). Indeed, we find plasmid TA frequencies cluster both by the HGT community and Incompatibility group (Fig. 5). Furthermore, plasmid competition is mechanistically tied to host ecology through conjugation, with environments favoring close cell-cell contact more likely to initiate plasmid–plasmid interaction. Ignoring species that physically isolate through tissue invasion or sporulation, TA frequency may also reveal producers of bioactive metabolites that affect local cell density, like Streptomyces (supplementary fig. S5, Supplementary Material online).

As a general reflection of plasmid–plasmid competition, TAs and TA frequency return to the original, functional premise of the plasmid incompatibility classification system (Garcillan-Barcia et al. 2023). Today, Inc groups refer to shared plasmid replicon sequences because physically testing plasmid coexistence becomes prohibitive with increasing plasmid diversity and specific culture requirements. However, shared replicons can only go so far as to predict degrees of incompatibility, or competition between plasmids. Plasmids often contain multiple different replicons, minor variations in replicon sequence may affect competition, and other common factors affect plasmids' ability to coexist (e.g. burden on host), which may only be revealed through culturing or more inclusive metrics. MOB relaxase typing and PTUs serve as valuable, alternative classification schemes, but these are neither universal nor as directly actionable as incompatibility, respectively. Given their presence on all types of plasmids, strong ties to existing classification systems, and basis in plasmid competition, we propose that TA systems could serve as a missing functional arm of plasmid taxonomy in the modern genomics era. Genetic embedding of plasmid competition in situ is especially valuable given that ex situ pairwise measurements inadequately capture community dynamics (Chang et al. 2023).

TA systems and HGT remain at the forefront of biocontainment solutions and challenges. Decoupled toxins serve as kill-switches that activate upon host or gene escape, while decoupled antitoxins bestow genetic stability onto nonessential genes (Chan et al. 2016; Halvorsen et al. 2022; Chlebek et al. 2023). Though effective at limiting HGT outward, these and even intensive genome recoding approaches fail to limit HGT inward, as seen with viral tRNA or TA supplementation (Nyerges et al. 2023). TA organization by HGT community suggests synthetic strains can be built to naturally invade target communities with TA matches and avoid communities with TA mismatches, turning HGT into a containment mechanism. Selective strain invasion and containment has already been demonstrated for maintaining synthetic circuit stability (Liao et al. 2019). Such designs effectively incorporate synthetic ecology, manipulating bacterial interactions to boost engraftment and limit escape.

Understanding the distribution of TA systems across HGT communities also informs antibiotic therapies. TA systems serve as an untapped pool of antibiotic drug targets that self-select, inherently sustaining susceptibility in hosts (Williams and Hergenrother 2008; Boss and Kedzierska 2023). Similarly, using TA systems as antibiotics creates an interesting counter-dynamic to plasmids being vehicles of resistance. The complete loss of TA systems, and therefore susceptibility to TA-based antibiotics, facilitates plasmid loss and susceptibility to traditional antibiotics (Yang and Walsh 2017). TA systems can also interact with phage, defending against them by inhibiting hosts, acting as a form of antibiotic resistance (LeRoux and Laub 2022). The distribution of TA systems across hosts can thereby guide phage therapy or grant phage resistance to synthetic strains. It is this combination of antibiotic, anti-resistance, and antibiotic resistance properties that positions TA systems at the forefront of alternative antibiotic development.

Our analysis has several limitations. Not all bacteria carry plasmids, well-characterized plasmids often have a clinical bias, and metagenomic techniques struggle to link complete plasmids to their hosts. In addition, not all TA systems predicted by TASmania HMMs are experimentally validated, although many may be functional (Akarsu et al. 2019). While our data support HGT driven TA evolution, with hosts influencing TA distribution at a high level (Fig. 6 and supplementary fig. S8, Supplementary Material online), plasmid competition represents only one of multiple selection mechanisms. Selfish, gene-centric behaviors, stabilization of genomic islands, and phage resistance also shape TA evolution, with indirect contribution to the communal TA signatures we discover here (LeRoux and Laub 2022; Richard et al. 2024). Expanded metagenomic analyses comparing TAs across mobile and static genetic elements should clarify these roles.
