Integrated proteomics and machine learning for identifying candidate serum biomarkers in acute myocardial infarction-complicated cardiogenic shock: a prospective exploratory study
Authors: Xi Wang, Qian-feng Xiao, Fang-yang Huang, Si Wang, Ying Xu, Xiao-bo Pu, Yan Yang, Mao Chen, Xin Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiogenic shock secondary to acute myocardial infarction (AMI-CS) prohibitively impacts survival. This prospective study aimed to discover and internally verify candidate serum protein biomarkers and evaluate their potential prognostic value for 30-day mortality in AMI-CS patients.MethodsAMI-CS patients were consecutively enrolled into discovery (n = 30) and verification (n = 60) cohorts. Candidate biomarkers were screened using Data-Independent Acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry, analyzed via differential abundance and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), and verified via targeted Parallel Reaction Monitoring (PRM). Boruta feature selection for five machine learning algorithms were embedded within a rigorous nested cross-validation scheme. Incremental prognostic value over clinical predictors was evaluated using Cox regression and metrics including the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI).ResultsDIA proteomics identified 216 proteins differentially abundant between 30-day survivors and non-survivors, and WGCNA defined an outcome-associated module linked to shock severity and enriched for oxidative stress and energy metabolism. During PRM verification, leakage-free nested cross-validation random forest model selected a seven-protein panel (YWHAZ, QDPR, MDH2, FAH, PSMA1, FABP5, and AHCY), which achieved a mean area under the ROC curve of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.69–0.95) for 30-day mortality. Incorporating this panel significantly enhanced the IDI of the SCAI model (IDI 0.143, P = 0.016) and the IABP-SHOCK II model (0.138, P = 0.034), which remained independent after adjusting for core clinical confounders.ConclusionThis exploratory study identifies a seven-protein candidate panel with potential value for early short-term risk stratification in AMI-CS patients. Introduction

Despite significant advancements in coronary revascularization technologies and intensive care management, short-term mortality remains prohibitively high for patients experiencing acute myocardial infarction-complicated cardiogenic shock (AMI-CS). The clinical course of AMI-CS is characterized by extreme heterogeneity, with 30-day mortality rates fluctuating between 30 and 60% across different cardiac centers [1]. While early, intensive mechanical circulatory support (MCS) escalation (e.g., Impella or venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation) holds potential to salvage AMI-CS patients with severe transient cardiac dysfunction, recent landmark clinical trials have yielded inconsistent efficacy results, which largely attributed to distinct patient sub-phenotypes [2–4]. Consequently, there is an urgent, unmet clinical need to refine risk stratification strategies, allowing clinicians to tailor therapeutic interventions and optimize resource allocation during the hyper-acute phase of shock.

Current risk stratification tools for AMI-CS patients show inherent limitations. Established clinical risk scores, such as the CardShock and IABP-SHOCK II scores, rely heavily on macro-hemodynamic parameters (e.g., mean arterial pressure) or delayed biochemical markers of profound end-organ failure (e.g., serum creatinine, arterial lactate). However, by the time a patient accumulates a high-risk score through overt tissue hypoperfusion or severe lactic acidosis, widespread and irreversible systemic cellular collapse has frequently already ensued. Furthermore, recent evidence indicates that arterial lactate—the current clinical gold standard for shock severity—lacks prognostic granularity and specificity during the early stages of myocardial infarction; mild baseline lactate elevations are frequently confounded by stress-induced adrenergic surges or altered hepatic clearance rather than pure cardiogenic hypoperfusion [4–6]. This creates a critical knowledge gap: we currently lack objective tools capable of identifying ultra-high-risk patients before macrovascular failure and irreversible multi-organ failure manifest.

High-throughput serum proteomics offers a promising paradigm shift to bridge this knowledge gap. Circulating proteins are highly sensitive to initial cellular stress and metabolic shifts, altering their expressions long before macro-hemodynamic decompensation or traditional laboratory abnormalities occur. Besides, serum proteomic profiling captures the real-time, coordinated molecular shedding from the myocardium, vascular endothelium, hepatic parenchyma, and renal tubules simultaneously, providing a holistic snapshot of systemic hypoperfusion [7]. Therefore, establishing an AMI-CS-specific molecular signature holds possibilities for earlier and more accurate risk stratification, and more crucially, profound translational relevance, particularly in guiding the optimal initiation timing of MCS.

Although a prior proteomic study successfully identified a protein-based panel for short-term mortality risk assessment in cardiogenic shock [8], that cohort blended diverse etiologies (e.g., chronic heart failure decompensation, acute valvular disease), leaving the unique proteomic signature of AMI-specific cardiogenic shock uncharacterized. The present study aims to identify and verify a robust candidate serum protein biomarker panel capable of predicting 30-day mortality, thereby providing both a systems biology lens into shock pathophysiology and a practical tool to advance precision critical care in AMI-CS.

Methods

Patient cohorts

Patients with AMI-CS on admission in West China Hospital of Sichuan University from January 2022 to January 2024 were prospectively, consecutively included. Patients were excluded if they had cardiogenic shock due to mechanical complications (e.g., cardiac rupture, ventricular septal perforation, or mitral valve prolapse), ventricular tachycardia electrical storm, septic shock, hemorrhagic shock, or had undergone resuscitation for more than 30 min. AMI was defined according to the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction [9]. All included CS patients were classified as stages B to E according to the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) classification of CS: Stage A represents patients “at risk” of developing CS. Stage B indicates “beginning” CS (pre-shock), where patients present with hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg, or requiring vasopressors to achieve a systolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg) but without signs of hypoperfusion (e.g., altered mental status, oliguria, cold and clammy skin and extremities, and arterial lactate levels > 2 mmol/L). Stage C denotes “classic” CS, featuring both hypotension and hypoperfusion. Stage D is “deteriorating” CS, requiring further escalation of therapy. Stage E represents “extremis” CS, involving circulatory collapse. The treatment and management of all patients were meticulously determined by the physicians, strictly adhering to the guideline recommendations. During follow-up, patient vital status was assessed through direct contact with the patients or their family members, as well as through hospital records. The primary clinical endpoint was 30-day mortality.

Serum for proteomic analysis was collected within 24 h of admission and of the diagnosis of AMI-CS; the SCAI shock stage was assessed at the time of sample collection, so that each sample corresponds to a defined hemodynamic state. The precise time intervals between sampling and individual clinical milestones (symptom onset, coronary angiography/percutaneous coronary intervention, onset of shock, cardiac arrest and resuscitation, and initiation of MCS) were not pre-specified as study variables and were therefore not uniformly available.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Sichuan University (2021–1770) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500099275). Written informed consent was obtained from the patients or their family members.

Biomarker discovery by screening proteomics

Serum samples were collected by venipuncture and stored at − 80 °C. Quantitative proteomics was performed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) to identify candidate protein biomarkers among proteins showing differential abundance between 30-day survivors and non-survivors. Serum from patients in the discovery cohort was trypsin-digested and analyzed by Data-Independent Acquisition (DIA) proteomics. Detailed sample preparation, chromatographic separation, and MS acquisition parameters are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Data processing and normalization. Raw DIA spectra from the discovery cohort were processed in Spectronaut to obtain protein-level intensities. For downstream analysis, protein abundance was expressed as a within-protein centered relative quantity: for each protein, its mean intensity across all samples was first computed, and each sample’s intensity for that protein was then divided by this mean to yield the relative abundance of the protein in each sample. This transformation was performed independently for each protein, using only that protein’s own values across samples; it rescaled each protein to its own mean and involves no comparison across different proteins and no group (survivor vs. non-survivor) information.

Quality control of discovery data. To ensure that candidate biomarkers identified in the discovery cohort could be reliably carried forward to targeted quantification, a technical quality-control (QC) step was applied to the DIA data prior to any between-group comparison. Three parallel criteria were used: (i) average intensity within the top 60% across samples (i.e., the lowest 40% were excluded); (ii) sequence coverage > 2%; and (iii) ≥ 2 unique peptides per protein. This filtering was unsupervised and used no outcome information.

Principal component analysis (PCA). PCA was performed on the QC-passed proteins using log2-transformed intensities with the prcomp function (scale = TRUE). Samples were visualized on the first two principal components using the sample scores (pca$x), with 95% confidence ellipses representing the confidence interval (CI) of the group means. To assess whether the technical filter introduced group-based bias, PCA was additionally performed on (i) all identified proteins (unbiased) and (ii) the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) only (biased control).

Differential abundance. Fold change (FC) was computed as the ratio of mean centered relative abundances between groups. Statistical significance was assessed by a two-sided t-test on log2-transformed data, with p-values adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate procedure. Proteins with FC > 2 or < 0.5 and q < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA (R package WGCNA; Langfelder & Horvath, 2008) was used to relate modules of co-expressed proteins to sample traits (clinical group, age, sex, lactate, and SCAI shock stage) [10]. Sample clustering was first used to detect and exclude outliers. A scale-free co-expression network was constructed with the one-step function; the soft-thresholding power (β) was selected with pickSoftThreshold. The adjacency matrix was transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM) and modules were detected by hierarchical clustering with dynamic tree cutting.

Module–trait correlations. Each module was summarized by its module eigengene (ME, the first principal component of the module). Module–trait associations were quantified as the Pearson correlation between each ME and the clinical trait, with corresponding P values from the Student asymptotic test (corPvalueStudent). Gene significance (GS) and module membership (MM) were computed to identify trait-associated modules. Hub proteins in the most significant module were ranked by betweenness centrality (Cytoscape/CytoHubba) from an edge file exported via exportNetworkToCytoscape. Enrichment analysis used clusterProfiler, visualized with cnetplot (enrichplot) and GOChord (GOplot).

Robustness assessment. Network robustness was evaluated by (i) confirming scale-free topology (signed R2 > 0.8 at the selected power) and (ii) 100 bootstrap resamples, in which the network was reconstructed de novo and the frequency with which each module protein remained co-clustered was recorded.

Biomarker verification by targeted proteomics

Proteins from the discovery phase that were both differentially abundant and members of trait-associated WGCNA modules were intersected to form an overlapping candidate pool, which underwent gene-ontology enrichment (clusterProfiler); the top-ranked proteins from the GO chord plot were retained. To these we added the proteins with the highest single-protein discrimination for 30-day mortality in the discovery cohort (ranked by area under the ROC curve, AUC) and a further set chosen on the basis of prior knowledge and clinical relevance. The union of these sources, after removing duplicates, constituted the candidate panel for future model development, subsequently quantified by targeted Parallel Reaction Monitoring (PRM) proteomics, in an independent verification cohort.

Serum samples were obtained under the same protocol with the discovery cohort, trypsin-digested and analyzed by targeted LC–MS/MS in PRM mode, detecting both peptide precursor ions and fragment ions. Acquisition used time-of-flight MS with Parallel Accumulation–Serial Fragmentation (PRM-PASEF) to enhance sensitivity and quantification accuracy. Protein abundances were estimated and relative quantification compared between survivors and non-survivors. Detailed methods are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as n (%); continuous variables as mean ± SD or median (IQR). Between-group comparisons used the t-test, χ2 test, or Fisher exact test as appropriate, with two-sided P < 0.05 considered significant. Clinical analyses were performed in STATA v17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA); proteomic and machine-learning analyses in R.

To avoid information leakage and to compare algorithms objectively, model development and evaluation used a nested cross-validation (CV) scheme (10 repeats of fivefold outer cross-validation). Within each outer training fold, feature selection (Boruta; R package Boruta, maxRuns = 1000, pValue = 0.01) and hyperparameter tuning were performed using only the training data; the held-out outer fold was used solely for evaluation, so that no test sample influenced feature selection or tuning.

Five algorithms were compared within the identical nested-CV framework: k-nearest neighbors, logistic regression, LASSO, random forest, and XGBoost. Feature-selection stability was summarized as the frequency with which each protein was selected across outer folds. A fixed protein panel was defined from proteins selected in ≥ 80% of folds, supported by differential abundance, functional enrichment, AUC-based screening, and prior knowledge.

Model evaluation. Performance was assessed from leakage-free out-of-fold predictions using: the ROC curve and AUC with 95% CI; a calibration curve (predicted vs. observed risk); sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive and negative predictive values (PPV/NPV) at the Youden-optimal threshold; and decision-curve analysis of net benefit. The AUC CI was obtained from the distribution of the nested cross-validation repeats; the CIs for sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, and NPV are exact (Clopper–Pearson) binomial intervals derived from the out-of-fold confusion matrix.

Adjustment for clinical confounders. To assess prognostic value independent of established clinical risk factors, each panel protein was entered into Cox proportional-hazards models adjusted one clinical covariate at a time (SCAI shock stage, IABP-SHOCK II score, NT-proBNP, troponin T, mechanical ventilation, cardiac arrest, intra-aortic balloon pump/IABP, and left ventricular ejection fraction/LVEF), with 30-day mortality as the event and follow-up time as the time scale [11]. The discrimination of the combined protein panel was also compared with that of conventional clinical predictors (SCAI shock stage, IABP-SHOCK II score, NT-proBNP, and troponin T) by ROC analysis. The incremental value of the panel over established clinical risk scores (SCAI shock stage; IABP-SHOCK II) was further quantified by ROC analysis together with the change in Harrell’s C-index, the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), and the continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI), with CIs from 1000 resamples. Analyses used the R packages caret, randomForest, xgboost, glmnet, pROC, rms, and survIDINRI.

Subgroup analyses. The prognostic association of each panel protein was additionally assessed within subgroups defined by SCAI shock stage, IABP use, cardiac arrest/CPR, mechanical ventilation, and LVEF, summarized as a subgroup forest plot (hazard ratio per 1-SD increase in protein abundance). Besides, a 1:1 propensity-score matching between the discovery (DIA) and verification (PRM) cohorts was performed. Propensity scores were estimated by logistic regression including age, sex, BMI, diabetes, smoking, systolic blood pressure, heart rate, arterial lactate, LVEF, resuscitated cardiac arrest, SCAI shock stage, and IABP use—with nearest-neighbor matching (caliper 0.2 SD, no replacement).

When patients were compared by outcome within each cohort, non-survivors consistently presented with more severe shock and greater treatment intensity. In both the discovery and verification cohorts, non-survivors had higher arterial lactate (both P < 0.001), lower mean arterial pressure (P = 0.004 and 0.034), worse renal function (eGFR, P = 0.029 and 0.004), and higher NT-proBNP (P = 0.029 and < 0.001). A more advanced SCAI shock-stage distribution was observed among non-survivors in the discovery cohort (P = 0.006), and in the verification cohort non-survivors more often required mechanical ventilation (95.5% vs 57.9%, P = 0.002) and inotropic support (86.4% vs 60.5%, P = 0.070).

Serum proteomic profiling distinguishes survivors from non-survivors

DIA LC–MS/MS in the discovery cohort identified 2,636 proteins, of which 975 passed the QC criteria. PCA of the 975 QC-passed proteins separated the group means of survivors and non-survivors along the first two components (PC1 28.8%, PC2 12.2%; Fig. 1A). Differential-abundance analysis identified 216 of the 975 proteins with at least a twofold change between non-survivors and survivors (q < 0.05), of which 211 were up-regulated and 5 down-regulated (Fig. 1B). The most strongly up-regulated proteins in non-survivors included GLUD1, PEBP1, CMBL, FABP1, SOD1, ABHD14B, AKR1A1, SORD, DCXR, and CYB5A, whereas IGKV2D-24, PCSK9, BPIFB1, CBLN4, and FN1 were down-regulated. PCA was repeated on all 2,636 identified proteins (unbiased), and on the 216 DEPs only (post-selection control); the QC-passed set reproduced essentially the same separation as the full unbiased set (PC1 t-test P = 0.0039 vs 0.0033), whereas the DEP-only set was, as expected, more strongly separated (Supplementary Figure S1).

Co-expression network analysis links a protein module to outcome and shock severity

In parallel, WGCNA was used to build a scale-free network from the 975 proteins using a soft-thresholding power of β = 9, and hierarchical clustering with module merging (height < 0.2) yielded four modules; the grey module contained unassigned proteins. Among the modules, the brown module showed the strongest associations with clinical outcome (r = 0.48, P = 0.008), arterial lactate (r = 0.68, P = 4 × 10⁻5), and SCAI shock stage (r = 0.57, P = 0.001) (Fig. 2A,2B). Within the brown module, module membership was strongly correlated with gene significance for clinical group (cor = 0.52, P = 2.3 × 10⁻31; Fig. 2C), indicating that the most central proteins were also the most outcome-associated. Hub proteins were ranked by betweenness centrality from the top proteins of the brown module (Fig. 2D); the most connected, differentially expressed hubs included SOD1, AKR1A1, CYB5A, FTH1, AK2, QDPR, and GLOD4. Functional enrichment of the brown-module proteins centered on cellular detoxification and oxidative-stress responses, energy and small-molecule metabolism, protein metabolism/folding, and intracellular transport (Fig. 2E). Network construction parameters and quality, the module dendrogram, and module-membership–gene-significance relationships for arterial lactate (cor = 0.88, P = 1.9 × 10⁻141) and SCAI shock stage (cor = 0.70, P = 5.2 × 10⁻⁶5) were shown in Supplementary Figure S2. Given the discovery sample size, brown-module robustness was confirmed by 100 bootstrap resamples (mean co-clustering stability 0.58).

Selection of prognostic candidate protein biomarkers

Integrating the differential-abundance and WGCNA results yielded 161 overlapping proteins as a candidate pool. Gene-ontology enrichment of this pool was dominated by cellular aldehyde metabolism, pyridine and nicotinamide nucleotide metabolism, cellular detoxification, monosaccharide metabolism, small-molecule catabolism, actin-filament organization, protein folding, sulfur-compound metabolism, and tricarboxylic-acid metabolism (Fig. 3). To balance biological relevance with between-group separation, the top 25 proteins from the GO chord plot were retained as candidates. To these we added the 10 proteins with the highest single-protein discrimination by ROC in the discovery cohort—FABP5 (AUC 0.907), SOD3 (0.907), TALDO1 (0.884), CYB5A (0.880), SOD2 (0.880), PSMA6 (0.876), PSMA4 (0.871), PSMA1 (0.862), GBE1 (0.858), and PSMB6 (0.858)—together with 22 additional proteins selected on the basis of prior knowledge and clinical relevance. In total, after removing proteins selected by more than one criterion, 54 candidate serum proteins were carried forward to targeted verification; their selection sources and discovery-cohort statistics are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

Feature selection identifies a stable seven-protein panel

The 54 candidate proteins were measured by PRM in an independent verification cohort (n = 60), and high-quality chromatographic data were obtained for 44 proteins. Seven proteins selected by Boruta feature within a nested CV scheme in at least 80% of folds—YWHAZ, QDPR, MDH2, FAH, PSMA1, FABP5, and AHCY were taken forward as a fixed seven-protein panel (Fig. 4).

Five algorithms (k-nearest neighbors, logistic regression, LASSO, random forest, and XGBoost) were compared within the identical nested CV framework; random forest gave the best discrimination and was selected for the final model, outperforming k-nearest neighbors (mean AUC 0.806), LASSO (0.789), logistic regression (0.779), and XGBoost (0.751) (Supplementary Figure S3). Evaluated on leakage-free out-of-fold predictions, the seven-protein panel achieved a mean AUC of 0.82 (95% CI 0.69–0.95) across the 10 nested-CV repeats (Fig. 5A). The model showed a sensitivity of 0.818 (0.597–0.948), specificity of 0.868 (0.719–0.956), accuracy of 0.850 (0.734–0.929), positive predictive value of 0.783 (0.563–0.925) and negative predictive value of 0.892 (0.746–0.970) (Supplementary Table 2). Calibration was good, with predicted probabilities tracking observed mortality (Fig. 5B), and decision-curve analysis demonstrated a positive net benefit exceeding both the treat-all and treat-none strategies across the clinically relevant range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 5C).

Subgroup analyses across SCAI stage, IABP use, cardiac arrest/CPR, mechanical ventilation, and LVEF showed directionally consistent associations (HR > 1) for most proteins in the larger strata, with unstable estimates in subgroups containing very few events (e.g., resuscitated cardiac arrest, n = 7 with 6 events) (Supplementary Figure S4). Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, we leveraged high-throughput DIA-MS combined with targeted PRM verification to systematically profile the serum proteome of patients with AMI-CS (Graphical Abstract). We successfully identified and verified a novel, machine learning-based 7-protein candidate biomarker panel that demonstrated potential predictive value for 30-day mortality in this critically ill population. Crucially, adding this 7-protein panel to established clinical risk frameworks yielded incremental value, as evidenced by improvements in Harrell’s C-index and IDI. By capturing early proteomic alterations on admission, our protein panel offers refined risk stratification insights.

The DIA platform during the discovery phase showed 211 upregulated vs. only 5 downregulated DEPs, and the same direction was further corroborated by orthogonal PRM in an independent verification cohort. A predominance of up-regulated proteins is biologically expected in patients dying of AMI-CS. This extreme state is accompanied by massive release of intracellular proteins from necrotic/ischemic tissue, accumulation of damage-associated molecular patterns, cascade activation of inflammation, compensatory up-regulation of antioxidant enzymes (e.g., SOD1, SOD2, PRDX1), activation of metabolic-stress enzymes, and heat-shock responses (e.g., HSPA8, HSP90AB1, HSPB1). Detoxification in the response to oxidative stress was found to be the top enriched biological process associated with outcomes of AMI-CS patients. The results were consistent with previously published studies that have reported increased antioxidant enzyme levels were associated with extensive tissue damage and worse prognosis of patients with AMI-CS [12–15]. Meanwhile, the seven proteins constituting our prognostic model are critically involved in extracellular matrix remodeling, acute-phase inflammatory responses, and energetic dysregulation [16–21]. Our descriptive proteomic findings do not provide direct experimental proof of these underlying mechanisms. These biological themes serve primarily as highly plausible hypotheses, highlighting that AMI-CS is driven not merely by isolated myocardial necrosis, but by a complex, systemic metabolic and microvascular collapse.

To lower the risk of severe overfitting—a common pitfall in small-cohort biomarker discovery—we executed a rigorous machine learning pipeline with five algorithms in robust internal cross-validation frameworks. However, a noteworthy biological limitation of MS-based discovery proteomics is the immense dynamic range of the human blood proteome. In our discovery phase using DIA-MS, low-abundance proteins fell below our stringent QC thresholds or missing-value filters. Notably, the 81 removed proteins in this study consist mainly of lower-abundance regulators and effectors (e.g., IFI35, MAVS, TNFRSF1B, LILRB1/2, ISG15), among that 78 (96%) were up-regulated in the non-survivor group and only 3 (4%) were down-regulated. This is the same direction as the principal finding that inflammatory response is elevated in the non-survivor group. Besides, hub proteins of the same pathways are present in and verified by the PRM panel (Supplementary Material 1,2). Future targeted, highly sensitive methodologies with lower limits of detection, such as Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM), high-density antibody arrays, or standard ELISA, are strictly required for the dedicated validation of these low-abundance inflammatory candidates.

Within our study population, the candidate 7-protein panel exhibited promising potential incremental prognostic value when integrated with established clinical risk frameworks currently utilized in intensive care units, including the SCAI shock stage and the IABP-SHOCK II score. Although the continuous NRI did not achieve statistical significance in our verification cohort, the significant enhancements in both Harrell’s C-index and the IDI provide an encouraging exploratory proof-of-concept, suggesting that this proteomic signature holds a valuable translational relevance for early risk stratification. Notably, the most pronounced statistical gain was observed when the panel was added to the baseline single-biomarker TnT model, where the AUC significantly increased from 0.658 to 0.818 (DeLong P = 0.035), indicating that capturing systemic proteomic alterations may yield richer prognostic value than evaluating isolated myocardial necrosis alone.

Conventional risk scores inherently rely on macro-hemodynamic alterations or delayed biochemical indicators of manifest end-organ failure [11]. This reactive nature poses a major clinical challenge for the hyper-acute identification of AMI-CS patients who appear temporarily stable but are biologically predisposed to a malignant clinical course. Conceptually, our candidate proteomic panel is hypothesized to reflect hyper-acute, subclinical multi-organ alterations at admission, offering a potential molecular window before macrovascular failure becomes irreversible. This introduces a hypothetical clinical application scenario for optimizing the decision-making process regarding high-stakes, invasive interventions, such as the initiation timing of MCS. For instance, in patients presenting in the early or evolving stages of shock (such as SCAI stages B or C), rather than adopting a conservative "watch-and-wait" strategy with escalating vasopressors, such an ultra-high-risk proteomic signature might conceptually prompt the shock team toward early, proactive MCS escalation. Conversely, a low proteomic risk signature might support a conservative medical-stabilization strategy, potentially sparing lower-risk individuals from the severe vascular and hemorrhagic complications associated with unnecessary device deployment [22, 23]. While this capacity to personalize the timeline of circulatory mechanical support represents an attractive paradigm for advancing toward precision critical care medicine in AMI-CS, we emphasize that these insights remain preliminary. Given the single-center design and the relatively small verification sample size of this exploratory study, extensive external validation in larger, prospective, and multi-center cohorts is strictly mandatory before this panel can be translated into routine bedside algorithms.

Study limitations

This study was conducted at a single medical center with a relatively modest sample size, which limits the immediate generalizability of our predictive model. Large-scale, external multi-center validation cohorts are mandatory to confirm the clinical utility of this panel.

The discovery cohort (n = 30) meets the WGCNA minimum recommendation (n ≥ 15) but remains modest, which may limit the internal stability and cross-dataset reproducibility of the co-expression modules. To mitigate this, we adopted a multi-tier strategy in which WGCNA served only as an upstream filter, followed by differential abundance analysis, PRM-based targeted verification on an orthogonal platform, and confirmation of the final seven-protein panel in an independent verification cohort (AUC = 0.82). The translational claims rest on this independently verified panel rather than on the WGCNA module itself. Larger, multi-center studies are nonetheless warranted to confirm module reproducibility.

To minimize the influence of baseline clinical confounders and selection bias between cohorts, we executed a 1:1 propensity-score matching analysis. However, the sample size restriction (23 well-balanced pairs) inevitably led to wider confidence intervals (95% CI: 0.685–0.991) and minor residual imbalances in treatment intensity variables like mechanical ventilation. Thus, these estimates should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Blood samples were collected exclusively at admission. The 24-h window may encompass dynamic changes in the circulating proteome around reperfusion, resuscitation, and the initiation of mechanical circulatory support, and the lack of precise event-to-sampling intervals precludes adjustment for, or formal testing of, sampling-time effects. Future studies with serial, time-anchored sampling will be required to characterize the temporal kinetics of these proteins and to confirm the robustness of the panel to sampling time. The functional mechanisms of some novel candidate proteins remain partially uncharacterized in the specific context of AMI-CS, demanding further in vitro and in vivo experimental validation. Finally, MS remains resource-intensive with a longer turnaround time compared to standard clinical assays; translating this 7-protein panel into rapid, point-of-care immunochemical test kits represents an essential step before real-world clinical deployment.

Conclusion

Using high-throughput proteomics combined with a rigorous, leakage-free bioinformatic pipeline, this exploratory study identified novel candidate biomarkers and key biological themes associated with AMI-CS prognosis. A candidate seven-protein signature comprising YWHAZ, QDPR, MDH2, FAH, PSMA1, FABP5, and AHCY demonstrated potential prognostic value for 30-day mortality. These findings not only enhance understanding of the systemic metabolic and oxidative stress mechanisms driving shock progression but also offer a promising molecular framework to optimize early clinical decision-making. Nevertheless, further validation in larger, prospective, and multicenter cohorts is strictly warranted to confirm the generalizability and real-world clinical utility of this candidate panel.
