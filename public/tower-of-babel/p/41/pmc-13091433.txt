Early Mobilization Outcomes With Tenecteplase Treatment in Acute Ischemic Stroke (EMOTE-TNK) Study: Safety and Tolerability
Authors: Isabelle Delos Reyes, Saptarshi Ghosh, Olivia Boykin, Carlos Perez Vega, Christopher Joseph Yelvington, Greg M. Worsowicz, David O. Hodge, Pablo Moreno Franco, Lesia H. Mooney, William D. Freeman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the safety and tolerability of early mobilization among patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) receiving intravenous (IV) tenecteplase (TNK). We hypothesized that physical therapy and occupational therapy would be relatively safe and tolerable when mobilization was attempted shortly after thrombolysis.Patients and MethodsIn this single-center, single-arm retrospective cohort study, we included adults with AIS treated with IV TNK who underwent first physical therapy or occupational therapy screening and mobilization 13 hours or more but less than 24 hours after thrombolysis. The primary outcome was mobilization-associated adverse events (AEs; screening through the immediate recovery period). Tolerability was defined by whether patients could continue mobilization after screening. We used health record data to determine stroke severity (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, modified Rankin scale score), vital signs, AE type, and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage occurring after thrombolysis but before mobilization.ResultsOf 180 patients, 159 (88.3%) underwent early mobilization without any AE. Twenty-one patients (11.6%) had an AE (orthostasis, dizziness, pain, heart rate >120/min, or systolic blood pressure >180 mm Hg); all were mild, transient, and not life threatening. Adverse events were significantly associated with baseline mean arterial pressure (P<.01). Moreover, AE type (pain vs autonomic; P=.01) and initial National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score (P=.04) were associated with successful mobilization. Three patients had symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage.ConclusionEarly mobilization (<24 hours after IV TNK) was relatively safe and well tolerated. Our findings suggest that screening-based early mobilization strategies can be integrated into standardized protocols for select patients with AIS receiving TNK.

Stroke, a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, is notorious for its sudden onset of symptoms. Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) constitutes approximately 87% of all strokes1 and is caused by decreased blood flow to part of the brain. If a patient is diagnosed with AIS within 3 and 4.5 hours after the onset of symptoms, then they may be eligible for treatment with a thrombolytic agent to dissolve the clot, which decreases the risk of long-term disability and death. Furthermore, AIS can also be treated with mechanical thrombectomy (in addition to a thrombolytic agent) for up to 24 hours after the onset of symptoms, provided that computed tomographic or magnetic resonance imaging shows evidence of a large-artery occlusion.2

Intravenous (IV) tenecteplase (TNK), a thrombolytic agent, has shown promise in treating AIS.3, 4, 5 Tenecteplase is a bioengineered tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) variant; compared with conventional tPA (eg, alteplase), TNK has a longer half-life through increased resistance to plasminogen activator inhibitor-1, faster administration through a single bolus, and greater fibrin specificity. The greater fibrin specificity also helps preserve the integrity of systemic coagulation, which may decrease bleeding risk during thrombolysis. Some studies have investigated this concept, with mixed results.6, 7, 8

Early rehabilitation with physical therapy (PT) and occupational therapy (OT) is not conventionally initiated within 24 hours after TNK administration, owing to concerns about intracranial or systemic hemorrhage, increased risk of death, and other complications. Although studies have shown that earlier mobilization may improve outcomes for patients with neurologic disorders,9, 10, 11 the safety and efficacy of early mobilization are not well established for patients receiving TNK. This study therefore aimed to evaluate the safety and tolerability of early mobilization among patients with AIS receiving TNK. We hypothesized that early rehabilitation would be safe, in terms of major bleeding and other neurologic adverse events (AEs), when mobilization was attempted within 24 hours after TNK administration for patients with AIS.

Patients and Methods

This study was approved by Mayo Clinic institutional review board. Because the protocol was implemented as an extension of quality improvement efforts by Mayo Clinic Comprehensive Stroke Center practice team, and because data were collected solely through health record review, this study was deemed to pose minimal risk to patients. Retrospectively obtaining formal written consent from all patients was deemed not practical for publication of anonymized results, and informed consent was waived by Mayo Clinic institutional review board. The reporting of this study follows the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) statement12 and the RECORD (Reporting of Studies Conducted Using Observational Routinely-Collected Data) statement.13

American Heart Association and American Stroke Association guidelines recommend early rehabilitation and formal functional assessment for hospitalized stroke patients, with rehabilitation intensity commensurate with anticipated benefit and tolerance; however, high dose (ie, high intensity in terms of duration or physical effort) and very early mobilization within 24 hours of stroke onset are not recommended.14 Because those guidelines do not specify TNK-specific timing for initiating out-of-bed mobilization in the first 24 hours, early mobilization practices may vary by hospital site and local institutional protocols; thus, our screening-based program reflects practice at our institution, rather than a universal standard of care. Our institutional protocol consisted of a single, criteria-based screening and mobilization attempt between 13 and less than 24 hours after TNK. Inclusion Criteria

We retrospectively reviewed routinely collected electronic health record data from patients who underwent an established, screening-based protocol for early mobilization at our institution. Patients with AIS were admitted to Mayo Clinic (Jacksonville, FL) from February 13, 2021, through March 18, 2024. Eligible patients were aged 18 years or older, received IV TNK, and underwent the first out-of-bed mobilization screening and attempt between 13 and less than 24 hours after TNK administration. Patients were identified by querying an institutional thrombolysis and stroke quality registry, and we verified that TNK administration was documented in the electronic health record. The AIS diagnosis was confirmed by treating clinicians using neuroimaging and was crossreferenced with discharge diagnosis codes (eg, International Classification of Diseases-10 [ICD-10] I63.x). Mobilization timing and eligibility were confirmed through PT and OT documentation. The multiyear study period was selected to include a wide range of patient ages, stroke severities, and baseline hemodynamic characteristics. The sample size was determined by the number of patients meeting eligibility criteria during the study period (Figure 2).Figure 2Cohort selection. Early Mobilization Protocol After Stroke Thrombolysis

Screening

We followed standard treatment guidelines for early mobilization. During a window of 13 to 24 hours after TNK treatment, a licensed physical therapist or occupational therapist assessed patients by using a checklist-based approach that considered vital signs and neurologic parameters to gauge their ability to tolerate mobilization. Screening included assessing the patient’s ability to actively engage in evaluation, documenting the condition of the intervention site, and measuring blood pressure (BP) while the patient was supine, sitting, and standing.

This approach was based on an institutional, evidence-informed practice document for physical medicine and rehabilitation (Supplemental Protocol), which is part of Mayo Clinic’s framework of evidence-based rehabilitation interventions and protocols for an array of medical diagnoses and conditions. These documents are based on a review of the literature, best available evidence, and nuances specific to Mayo Clinic practice setting. They serve as recommendations for the practice to improve quality, decrease costs, decrease variation, and contribute to meaningful clinical outcomes.

Safe Initiation of Services

Physical therapy and OT services could be safely initiated if patients were hemodynamically stable (not receiving vasopressors or IV antihypertensive medication, not requiring multiple IV boluses of either medication). Contraindications to initiating services included an existing or recently removed femoral sheath, hemodynamic instability, angioedema, heart rate (HR) exceeding 120/min, systolic BP (SBP) greater than 180 mm Hg, diastolic BP (DBP) greater than 110 mm Hg, marked diaphoresis, facial pallor, intensely anxious or painful facial expression (especially in aphasic patients), and active bleeding from lines, catheters, intervention sites, or wounds.

We took precautions to safely initiate PT and OT services for patients with femoral arterial lines. We could proceed with services for patients with radial arterial lines and upper extremity peripherally inserted central catheters if they had been flushed with normal saline (not heparin) by nursing staff. Some patients with other types of lines or orders for bed rest also could start services. When appropriate, we sought approval to begin services from the on-call physician in the neurology intensive care unit. The plan of care was discussed with a registered nurse.

Monitoring for Worsening Conditions

The physical or occupational therapist reviewed each patient’s record and verified the time elapsed since thrombolytic administration (≥13 hours since initiation of thrombolysis). Therapists were directed to monitor for worsening signs or symptoms during screening (eg, marked diaphoresis, facial pallor, intensely anxious or painful facial expression), a decrease in SBP or mean arterial pressure (MAP) of 30 mm Hg or more while standing, increased lightheadedness (or faint feeling), and worsening numbness or weakness. If any of these signs or symptoms were present, then patients were returned to a supine position, and nursing staff were notified. The patient’s ability to independently complete activities of daily living (Barthel index) also was recorded. If no abnormal signs or symptoms were present and BP and MAP remained stable, then the therapist progressed with the evaluation and mobilization of the patient.

Follow-up for the primary safety outcome spanned the first therapy screening and mobilization attempt; this period included the immediate postmobilization recovery time documented in the electronic health record. We additionally recorded symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) events that occurred after thrombolysis and before mobilization was attempted.

Data Collection

The primary outcome was AEs after early mobilization. We extracted the following variables from the electronic health record: patient demographic characteristics (age at stroke, sex, race, and ethnicity); clinical stroke severity (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale [NIHSS] score at admission and discharge and preadmission and discharge modified Rankin scale [mRS] score); TNK administration time; type of acute reperfusion therapy (TNK alone vs TNK plus thrombectomy); time from IV TNK bolus to the first PT or OT screening and mobilization attempt; HR and BP (SBP, DBP, and MAP) before screening (baseline) and after completion of the first mobilization attempt (recovery); and mobilization-associated AEs documented during screening, mobilization, and the immediate recovery period. Adverse events were categorized as orthostasis, dizziness, pain, HR exceeding 120/min, syncope, SBP exceeding 180 mm Hg, and sICH. Tolerability was defined as successful continuation of the first mobilization attempt after screening; discontinuation was defined as stopping the first attempt and reattempting on a subsequent day, as documented in therapy notes. A complete list of extracted variables and the proportion of missing values for each variable are shown in Supplemental Table (available online at http://www.mcpiqojournal.org).

Data cleaning included range and plausibility checks (eg, physiologically implausible vital sign values), verification of time stamps for TNK administration and therapy evaluation, and review of therapy documentation to confirm AE classification. When discrepancies were identified across documentation sources (eg, nursing flowsheets vs therapy notes), the therapy and nursing notes were used as the primary source for AE determination, and the closest time-adjacent vital sign recordings were used for baseline and recovery values.

Data from stroke-related encounters, medication administration records, nursing vital sign flowsheets, and therapy documentation were linked within the electronic health record using a unique patient encounter identifier. Linkage quality was assessed by manual verification of key fields (eg, TNK time, therapy evaluation time, and AE documentation) during chart abstraction.

Statistical Analyses

Numeric variables were summarized with medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were summarized as numbers and percentages. To evaluate the primary outcome of safety after early mobilization, we calculated the percentage of patients with AEs among all patients undergoing early mobilization. Secondary analyses stratified patients into AE and non-AE groups and compared vital signs and demographic, NIHSS, and mRS data. Odds ratios for variables (with respect to AE occurrence) and corresponding P values were calculated by using logistic regression. We calculated the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve when analyzing the ability of baseline SBP, DBP, and MAP to predict AEs. We also conducted a subgroup analysis using logistic regression to determine whether baseline or stroke severity features were associated with discontinuation of early mobilization. Given the involvement of the autonomic nervous system in stroke, we defined autonomic events as changes in HR, orthostatic BP events, and pain-mediated BP changes during screening; this approach was used to help classify AE events. Missing data were uncommon (<10%) for most variables; however, when data were missing, analyses used available case data with sensitivity checks, using single imputation for vital sign fields when required for modeling. Discharge mRS data were missing for 28% of patients and were analyzed using available case data. No power calculation was done; the study sample was determined by the number of eligible patients seen during the study period. SPSS statistical software (version 30.0.0; IBM Corporation) was used for descriptive and statistical analysis. P values less than .05 were considered statistically significant.

The median time between TNK bolus administration and initiation of evaluation for early mobilization was 17.9 hours (IQR, 14.8-21.0 hours). Twenty-one of the 180 patients (11.7%) had AEs. Of these patients with AEs (orthostasis, n=7; dizziness, n=6; pain, n=5; HR >120/min, n=2; and SBP >180 mm Hg, n=1), 10 successfully continued with early mobilization according to the protocol. The other 11 tried again on another day. Among the patients who were successfully screened and mobilized, no serious, permanent, or life-threatening bleeding complications occurred. Three patients (1.7%) had sICH that occurred before mobilization was attempted.

Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized after stratifying patients by AE occurrence (Table 1). Some of the baseline vital signs were significantly associated with AEs. For baseline SBP, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.699 (95% CI, 0.597-0.802; P<.001). For baseline DBP, the area was 0.693 (95% CI, 0.583-0.802; P=.001), and for baseline MAP, it was 0.740 (95% CI, 0.642-0.838; P<.001). The median discharge mRS score (data available for n=130) was higher among those with AEs than without, but the difference was not statistically significant (P=.43). We conducted a subgroup analysis of the 21 patients with AEs and categorized them based on the type of AE and continuation of early mobilization (Table 2). When we grouped all AEs other than pain as autonomic events, those with pain were able to complete early mobilization (5/5), whereas only 5 of 16 with autonomic events were able to do so. Discussion

Many studies have concluded that early rehabilitation prevents functional impairment and improves quality of life for patients with neurologic disorders.9, 10, 11,15 A systematic review reported that mobilization could be safely started after 24 hours of stroke.9,11 A multicenter randomized clinical trial15 showed that health-related quality of life 12 months after a stroke was not markedly different when comparing patients who were mobilized within 24 hours and patients who received conventional care. However, the early mobilization group had reduced odds of a favorable outcome (mRS score, 0-2) at 3 months post stroke (the primary study outcome). Our study findings argue that mobilizing patients at 13 to 24 hours after thrombolysis with TNK is relatively safe and tolerable.

The 2026 American Heart Association and American Stroke Association guidelines recommends that all patients with AIS receive a formal, in-hospital, interdisciplinary assessment to provide rehabilitation at a level appropriate for the individual (class 1 recommendation).14 They advised against high-dose, very early mobilization within 24 hours of stroke onset because it may be harmful (based on findings from the older AVERT [A Very Early Rehabilitation Trial] study).15 These guidelines do not provide TNK-specific recommendations for initiating out-of-bed mobilization in the first 24 hours; therefore, the timing of early mobilization as a standard of care varies by the stroke severity, by hospital site, and local institutional stroke protocols. To our knowledge, the current study is the first to specifically address early mobilization in a sample of patients with AIS receiving TNK. We focused on patients receiving TNK because many institutions are switching from conventional tPA to TNK for thrombolysis: TNK can be administered faster (as a bolus) and, therefore, it simplifies the work for nursing personnel and has a decreased risk of medication errors. In 2025, TNK was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for AIS.14

In the current study, most patients tolerated early mobilization. Reported AEs were mild, transient, and not life threatening. Our findings suggest that vital signs, measured at different time points, might be important predictors of AEs when early mobilization is attempted. Notably, some patients with AEs were able to complete early mobilization, whereas others had to try again later. The nature of the AE, whether it is pain or autonomic symptoms or signs, may influence the potential for successful early mobilization. The initial NIHSS score may also have a similar association.

Identification of credible AE predictors may enable further risk stratification of patients who clear the screening criteria, and it may facilitate development of targeted mobilization protocols that are tailored to individual patients. Measures of functional performance, such as the Activity Measure for Post-Acute Care score,16 may be studied for association with AEs. Future research should focus on precisely identifying the most effective and safe timing for mobilization in a manner that considers patient-specific factors. This approach would enable stratification of patients to various subgroups (eg, by comorbid conditions and stroke severity) with customized stroke protocols. A longer follow-up period along with a concrete record of mortality data would facilitate a survival analysis, thus forming a better picture of the long-term consequences of early mobilization in general and identifying specific sequelae in patients with AEs during early mobilization. Quality of life (eg, 3 and 12 months after stroke)15 may also be studied. Factors such as the type of AE and the initial and discharge NIHSS and mRS scores could be investigated in the future to assess their associations with long-term outcomes and quality of life. Cost effectiveness of early mobilization is another important factor when considering downstream health care (ie, after hospital discharge). Future studies should evaluate financial outcomes more comprehensively by comparing factors such as hospital readmission rates and time spent in rehabilitation after discharge between those with early vs conventional mobilization.

As described in our protocol, early mobilization was attempted after measuring vital signs and checking patients for other signs and symptoms and confirming that they had cleared the screening criteria. However, some patients did have AEs. Further research could delve into whether vital signs could be used to predict the occurrence of AEs and, if so, at what time points should they be measured. One drawback is that the recovery vital signs were measured after early mobilization, whereas the discharge NIHSS and discharge mRS scores were measured days later, after AE occurrence (including ΔNIHSS, which is calculated by using the discharge NIHSS score). Thus, the credibility of these variables as predictors of AEs is limited in this logistic regression model. However, even the recovery vital signs seemed to have a strong association with AE occurrence. The nature of the AE also could be analyzed for its ability to predict whether early mobilization would be successful. Other variables, such as the initial NIHSS score, may be investigated further for predicting success. The median discharge mRS score was higher among those with AEs than that among those without, but the difference was not statistically significant. However, because discharge mRS data were limited (available for only 130 patients), the analysis may have been underpowered to detect a difference. Moreover, there is an element of multicollinearity while using SBP, DBP, and MAP as predictors in the model because these values are dependent on each other. However, because only univariate and no multivariate analyses were done, all variables were explored for association with AE occurrence.

In this study, baseline BPs were significantly associated with occurrence of AEs. Among those who had AEs, a high initial NIHSS score was associated with discontinuation of early mobilization. However, our sample size was small, and prospective studies with larger cohorts would be needed to verify these associations using logistic regression. For assessing overall outcomes, patients should be stratified by the type of treatment (TNK vs TNK+thrombectomy), age at stroke, type of AE, and whether early mobilization had to be discontinued. Long-term mortality rate and incidence of morbidities (eg, subsequent stroke, transient ischemic attack, and hospital readmission) should be assessed using Cox regression or Kaplan–Meier curves. Although variables such as discharge mRS score and exact time to early mobilization after thrombolysis were not significant in this study with respect to occurrence of AEs, they should be assessed for association with long-term outcomes. Time to good recovery (defined as an mRS score of 0-2)15 and the number of days (poststroke) until the patient can independently walk 50 m15 could also be compared in a future study to assess for significant differences between groups. Quality of life and total costs at 12 months15 are also potential outcomes that could be evaluated in a future cohort study.

Study limitations include a relatively small sample size, lack of a control group, lack of systematic data on comorbid conditions and concomitant medications, and missing values for discharge mRS scores (>10%). The retrospective design and use of routinely collected electronic health record data limit generalizability and introduce potential for misclassification bias (eg, variability in documentation of symptoms such as dizziness or pain) and unmeasured confounding (eg, prestroke frailty, sedation, or nursing activity before therapy evaluation). Because eligibility for early mobilization was determined clinically using a standardized screening algorithm, changes over time in documentation practices, staffing, or clinician thresholds could have influenced which patients were mobilized and when. Missing data may have biased associations, particularly for discharge mRS, which was analyzed using available case data. The lack of a control group hinders our ability to estimate the risk of AE that is attributable to early mobilization. This study offers a framework to inform stroke mobilization practice for patients receiving TNK; however, prospective studies with appropriate comparator groups are needed to better define optimal timing, dose, and patient selection. The occurrence of AEs was our primary outcome. The other analyses to investigate potentially prognostic associations of patient demographic characteristics, vital signs, type of treatment, and NIHSS and mRS scores with occurrence of AEs, and treatment discontinuation were strictly exploratory and hypothesis generating (Table 1, Table 2, Table 3). There could also be the risk of multiplicity of analyses. The findings of this study must be verified with larger, prospective studies that use control groups.

Conclusion

Our study provides retrospective observational data about the safety of early mobilization of AIS patients within 24 hours of thrombolysis with TNK. Early mobilization was relatively safe and feasible, including for those who underwent thrombectomy. Our findings provide preliminary evidence, supporting the integration of early mobilization strategies into standardized safety protocols for patients with AIS receiving TNK. This approach has the potential to enhance patient outcomes and streamline postthrombolytic care by providing a foundation for developing future treatment guidelines. However, to solidify these findings and refine clinical guidelines, larger, prospective studies are essential. Such studies should aim to further validate the safety and efficacy of early mobilization in this patient population and ultimately contribute to the development of robust, evidence-based protocols for stroke rehabilitation.
