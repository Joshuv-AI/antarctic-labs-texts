Multiple instance learning approach for automated gallbladder cancer detection using ultrasound imaging: multi-center validation of a deep learning model with the public dataset contribution
Authors: Pankaj Gupta, Kartik Bose, Priya Mudgil, Niharika Dutta, Ajay Gulati, Gaurav Prakash, Thakur Deen Yadav, Lileswar Kaman, Santosh Irrinki, Harjeet Singh, Divya Khosla, Himangi Unde, Nitin Shetty, Ishan Kumar, Viswaja Chalapati, Ashish Verma, Himanshu Pruthi, Himika Bansal, Jyotsna Sen, Rakesh Kapoor, Rajesh Gupta, Chetan Arora, Usha Dutta

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundGallbladder cancer (GBC) diagnosis is challenging due to overlapping imaging features. We developed and validated a multiple instance learning (MIL) model for automated GBC detection using a large-scale multi-center ultrasound dataset and benchmarked it against state-of-the-art architectures.MethodsThis was a retrospective and prospective multi-center cohort study. We trained a gated attention MIL (GAIA-MIL) model on the prospective AURORA-GB dataset (August 2022–July 2024) and two public datasets. The model was evaluated on a temporally independent internal test set (August 2024–December 2024) and three retrospective external cohorts. The area under curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity of GAIA-MIL was compared to Clustering-constrained Attention MIL (CLAM), Dual-Stream MIL (DS-MIL), and Transformer-based MIL (TransMIL).FindingsThe datasets comprised 11,012 images from 1151 patients. Cross-validation achieved a mean AUC of 0.874 (95% CI 0.846–0.902). On the internal test set (n = 97), GAIA-MIL achieved 87.7% sensitivity (78.9–95.1%), 86.2% specificity (72.4–96.9%), and an AUC of 0.883 (0.786–0.963). Pooled external validation (n = 122) showed an AUC of 0.778 (0.698–0.852). Performance varied by external center (AUCs: 0.722, 0.950, and 0.749). In comparative benchmarking, while TransMIL excelled internally (AUC 0.871), its performance degraded significantly in external validation (Pooled AUC 0.654). GAIA-MIL demonstrated superior stability, maintaining robust sensitivity (78.2%), specificity (73.4%), and AUC (0.778) pooled across all diverse external centers where complex transformers struggled. Interpretability analysis confirmed the model focused on clinically relevant features like wall thickening.InterpretationWhile complex architectures like TransMIL perform well internally, GAIA-MIL offers the optimal balance of performance and generalizability for multi-center deployment. The AURORA-GB benchmark dataset is publicly released to advance research.FundingNone.Keywords: Gallbladder cancer, Multiple instance learning, Deep learning, Ultrasound, Medical imaging, Artificial intelligence, Computer-aided diagnosis

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Deep learning has shown potential for gallbladder cancer (GBC) detection using ultrasound. However, previous studies (e.g., GBCNet, RadFormer) typically relied on single-center datasets with limited sample sizes or did not address the practical challenge of analyzing multiple images per patient. Furthermore, there has been no standardized benchmarking of different multiple instance learning (MIL) architectures (e.g., Attention-based vs. Transformers) to determine the optimal approach for this specific clinical task.

Added value of this study

We introduce AURORA-GB, a large ultrasound dataset for GBC. We developed GAIA-MIL, an interpretable MIL framework, and rigorously benchmarked it against state-of-the-art methods (CLAM, TransMIL, DS-MIL). Our study provides the first multi-center external validation of these models, revealing that while complex Transformer models achieve high internal accuracy, simpler attention-based models (GAIA-MIL) demonstrate superior generalizability across diverse clinical centers.

Implications of all the available evidence

These findings suggest that for ultrasound-based diagnosis, where image quality varies significantly between centers, attention-based MIL architectures offer a more robust path to clinical deployment than the Transformers. The release of the AURORA-GB dataset provides a critical resource for the community to further advance generalizable AI in abdominal imaging and GBC.

Introduction

Gallbladder cancer (GBC) represents one of the most aggressive malignancies of the biliary tract, with a five-year survival rate of less than 5% for advanced stages.1 It shows marked geographical prediction with highest incidence in the Indian subcontinent.2 Early detection is crucial for improving patient outcomes, as the prognosis dramatically improves when the tumor is confined to the gallbladder wall.2 However, early-stage GBC often presents asymptomatically, and when symptoms appear, they frequently mimic benign gallbladder diseases, leading to delayed diagnosis.3

Ultrasound (US) serves as the primary diagnostic modality for gallbladder pathology due to its widespread availability and cost-effectiveness.4 However, differentiating between benign and malignant gallbladder lesions remains challenging, even for experienced radiologists, as imaging features often overlap significantly between conditions.5,6 This diagnostic uncertainty leads to delayed treatment or unnecessary interventions.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence have shown promise for automated GBC detection on US.7 Gupta et al. demonstrated that deep learning models could achieve diagnostic performance comparable to experienced radiologists.8 Basu et al. developed GBCNet, which outperformed both state-of-the-art CNN models and expert radiologists.9 More recent studies have incorporated transformer architectures, with Hasan et al. achieving 96.21% diagnostic accuracy using anatomy-aware models.10

Despite these promising developments, critical limitations persist. Existing public datasets are severely limited. Most studies employ conventional image-level classification approaches that fail to account for the clinical reality of reviewing multiple images per patient. Furthermore, no studies have validated their models across multiple centers, raising questions about real-world applicability.

Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) offers a promising paradigm by learning at the patient level while automatically identifying diagnostically relevant images through attention mechanisms.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 This approach is well-suited for medical imaging where multiple images are acquired per patient with varying quality and diagnostic value.

To address these gaps, the primary aims of this study were: (1) to curate and publicly release AURORA-GB, the large diverse ultrasound dataset for GBC to date, (2) to develop a patient-level gated-attention MIL (GAIA-MIL) framework that mimics clinical workflow, (3) to rigorously validate this approach via multi-center external testing to assess real-world generalizability, and (4) to perform comprehensive benchmarking study comparing the proposed GAIA-MIL framework against state-of-the-art methods (CLAM, TransMIL, DS-MIL) to establish an evidence-based baseline for model selection in GBC diagnosis.

Methods

The study employed a mixed design comprising two public datasets (detailed below), a large prospectively recruited internal cohort and three retrospective external validation cohorts.

Internal Cohort (AURORA-GB): Consecutive patients with suspected gallbladder pathology (based on prior imaging) were recruited prospectively at the Postgraduate Institute of Medical Education and Research (PGIMER), Chandigarh, between August 2022 and July 2024 for model training and validation. A separate, temporally independent test set was recruited between August 2024 and December 2024 to evaluate internal performance. Patients were included if a definitive final diagnosis could be established. GBC diagnosis was confirmed via histopathology (cholecystectomy, biopsy, or FNAC). Benign diagnoses were confirmed via histopathology or clinical/radiological stability over at least six months.

External Cohorts: Data from three external centers were collected retrospectively. Each participating center obtained independent institutional review board approval.

Our study utilized three complementary ultrasound datasets: GBCU, UIdataGB, and AURORA-GB datasets (Supplementary Figure S1). This multi-dataset approach addresses the critical challenge of data scarcity in gallbladder cancer research while incorporating diverse acquisition protocols, equipment specifications, and patient populations to enhance model robustness and generalizability.

GBCU dataset

The original GBCU dataset does not provide the patient level data.9 We update the GBCU dataset (GBCU-v2) to group the images from each patient into separate folder providing 235 unique patient folders with patient level label. This approach yielded 48 malignant (20.4%) and 187 benign (79.6%) folders with a malignancy ratio of 0.25.

UIdataGB dataset

The UIdataGB dataset provided US images across nine gallbladder disease categories.16 Similar to the original GBCU dataset, the patient level data is not provided. Additionally, the available dataset had been augmented by the authors of the dataset using random rotations, flips, translations, as well as changes in brightness, contrast, and saturation. A radiologist with 10 years' experience in abdominal US reviewed all the images and retained unique high quality gallbladder US images (without augmentations) and restructured them into patient level folders (not originally provided) based on the visual similarity.

AURORA-GB dataset: a new benchmark dataset in GBC research

We publicly release the AURORA-GB (Advanced Ultrasound Repository for Oncological Research and Analysis—Gallbladder) dataset, a comprehensive and high-quality resource for gallbladder cancer (GBC) research and algorithmic benchmarking. AURORA-GB consists of B-mode ultrasound (US) images. To support reproducibility and facilitate downstream granular analyses, the dataset is accompanied by a comprehensive metadata CSV file. This file includes patient-level demographic details and specific clinical characteristics, such as lesion location (fundus, body, neck, or diffuse), lesion morphology, and gallbladder distension status.

Consecutive patients referred for abdominal US evaluation of suspected gallbladder pathologies, spanning both benign and malignant conditions were recruited. The imaging protocol included both axial and sagittal static images, systematically obtained by radiologists with 1–3 years of post-training experience in abdominal US using transducer with a convex transducer with a frequency range of 2–8 MHz on a high-end US machine (Logiq S8, GE Healthcare). All the images were reviewed by an expert gastrointestinal radiologist with 10 years of post-training experience. Images were acquired according to standard clinical practice, with particular attention to capturing representative views of the gallbladder and surrounding liver parenchyma.6 Imaging was performed prior to any therapeutic intervention. All images were stripped of patient-identifiable metadata to ensure privacy and full anonymization.

The dataset reflects the real-world clinical spectrum of GBC, including challenging and subtle cases.

Each patient's final diagnosis was determined via:

AURORA-GB is intended as a benchmark resource for the development and evaluation of AI methods for gallbladder cancer detection, differential diagnosis, and related tasks. The dataset's diversity, high image quality, detailed annotations, and robust reference standards make it suitable for both training and external validation of machine learning models, supporting robust algorithmic generalization and reproducibility.

We developed a comprehensive multi-stage preprocessing pipeline to standardize input quality and ensure diagnostic relevance. The initial stage focused on isolating the ultrasound cone from non-diagnostic elements including text overlays, measurement markers, and background artifacts. Each image underwent grayscale conversion followed by 7 × 7 Gaussian blur application to reduce high-frequency noise. Binary masking with a low threshold (value = 10) enabled effective segmentation of the primary imaging region.

Contour analysis identified the largest area region corresponding to the ultrasound cone, with bounding box computation including a 10-pixel margin to ensure complete region capture. All cropped images were standardized to 600 × 600 pixels for uniform model input. A rigorous two-stage quality filtering protocol removed suboptimal images through channel difference analysis (excluding color Doppler with high inter-channel variance) and noise assessment via pixel intensity standard deviation (threshold = 70 for artifact removal).

Multiple instance learning (MIL) architecture

Our model implements a novel MIL framework with gated attention mechanisms, enabling patient-level classification from variable-sized image bags while automatically identifying diagnostically relevant instances (Supplementary Figure S2).

Feature extraction and transformation

We employed a pre-trained EfficientNet-B4 backbone initialized with ImageNet weights as our feature extractor, removing the final classification layer to output instance-level feature vectors. Raw features undergo transformation through a dedicated network projecting them into a 512-dimensional embedding space via linear transformation, ReLU activation, dropout (p = 0.3), and layer normalization for training stabilization.

Gated attention mechanism

Instance-level features aggregate into patient-level representations through our gated attention mechanism, which dynamically weights instance importance within each patient bag. For each transformed feature vector, parallel pathways compute V-branch (linear layer with Tanh activation) and U-branch (linear layer with Sigmoid activation) outputs. Element-wise multiplication followed by linear transformation produces attention scores, normalized via softmax to generate weights summing to unity.

The final patient-level representation emerges as a weighted sum of transformed instance features using attention weights. A two-layer classifier (512→256→2 dimensions) with ReLU activation and dropout generates binary predictions. All new layers utilize Xavier normal initialization to ensure stable gradient flow.

Training protocol and optimization strategy

We implemented a sophisticated multi-stage training protocol centered on progressive unfreezing to effectively leverage pre-trained knowledge while adapting to gallbladder US imaging characteristics. Initially, the entire EfficientNet-B4 backbone remained frozen while newly initialized components (feature transformation, attention mechanism, classifier) underwent training to learn high-level task structure without catastrophic forgetting.

Validation performance monitoring triggered progressive unfreezing when improvements plateaued (patience = 6 epochs). Beginning with the deepest backbone block, we systematically unfroze layers while re-initializing the AdamW optimizer to include new parameters with reset learning rate schedules. This strategy enabled gradual, stable fine-tuning progression from abstract high-level features toward general low-level representations.

Training employed weighted cross-entropy loss addressing inherent class imbalance. Label smoothing (coefficient = 0.1) discouraged overconfident predictions, improving calibration and generalization. AdamW optimization (weight decay = 1e-5) utilized OneCycleLR scheduling (maximum learning rate = 2e-4) for enhanced convergence. Gradient clipping (L2 norm ≤1.0) prevented gradient explosion, with batch size of four patients.

Data augmentation and bag management

Comprehensive data augmentation operated at both image and bag levels to enhance generalization while addressing MIL-specific challenges. Image-level transformations included horizontal/vertical flips (p = 0.1), rotations (±15°), and color jittering of brightness, contrast, and saturation. All images underwent resizing to 380 × 380 pixels with ImageNet normalization.

MIL-specific strategies addressed variable bag sizes through synthetic instance creation for bags with fewer than two images, ensuring sufficient information for attention mechanisms. Maximum bag size constraints maintained computational feasibility: training employed random five-instance sampling for oversized bags (providing regularization through varied instance combinations), while validation used deterministic uniform sampling for reproducible evaluation.

Cross-validation and temporally independent internal testing

Rigorous evaluation employed 5-fold stratified cross-validation and internal testing on temporally independent cohort with patient-level partitioning, preventing data leakage while maintaining class distribution representativeness across folds.

External validation and multi-center testing

Beyond cross-validation and validation on internal held out test set, we conducted comprehensive external validation across three independent centers to assess real-world generalizability and domain adaptation challenges. External test sets comprised patients from different institutions with varying imaging protocols, equipment specifications, and patient demographics, providing robust assessment of clinical deployment readiness.

Performance evaluation across centers utilized identical metrics to internal validation, enabling direct comparison and identification of domain shift effects.

Model interpretability and visualization

We implemented complementary visualization techniques providing comprehensive insight into model decision-making processes. Attention weight visualization overlaid MIL-generated weights as heatmaps on corresponding instances, highlighting influential images within patient bags for final classification decisions.

Gradient-Weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) generated image-level heatmaps indicating salient regions for feature extraction, providing granular interpretability complementing bag-level attention analysis. This dual approach enables understanding of both image selection (which images drive decisions) and feature localization (which regions within images are diagnostically relevant), supporting clinical acceptance and trust in automated decision-making systems.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Python (v3.8.10) with SciPy (v1.7.3), scikit-learn (v1.0.2), and statsmodels (v0.13.2) libraries. Performance evaluation utilized comprehensive metrics including balanced accuracy, sensitivity (or recall), specificity, precision [or positive predictive value (PPV)], F1 score, negative predictive value (NPV), and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). For all performance metrics, we calculated 95% confidence intervals to assess the reliability of our estimates. For cross-validation results, 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using the t-distribution method based on the mean and standard deviation across the five folds. For test set results, we employed the Wilson score interval method for binary metrics (sensitivity, specificity) and bootstrap resampling (2000 iterations) for continuous metrics (AUC).

To evaluate the agreement between the model's attention weights and radiologist assessments, a radiologist with 10 years of experience in abdominal US independently reviewed a randomly selected subset of 50 patient cases (25 malignant, 25 benign) from the test set. The radiologist scored every image within each patient's bag on a 5-point Likert scale (1: not diagnostically useful, 5: highly diagnostically important). Pearson's correlation coefficient was calculated between the model's attention weights and the radiologist's scores, with statistical significance assessed at p < 0.05.

For subgroup analyses, performance differences between subgroups were assessed using the chi-squared test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate.

Results

Dataset characteristics and composition

GBCU dataset

The GBCU dataset contributed 1255 images from 235 patients [48 malignant (20.4%) and 187 benign (79.6%) with a malignancy ratio of 0.25].

UIdataGB dataset

The curated UIdataGB dataset comprised 242 patients (2964 images) [47 (19.4%) malignant and 195 (81.6%) benign cases with a malignancy ratio of 0.24].

AURORA-GB dataset

AURORA-GB consists of 8048 grayscale B-mode ultrasound (US) images (mean resolution: 1204 × 864 pixels) from 674 patients. The cohort includes 439 females (65.1%) and 235 males, with a mean age of 51.3 ± 9.0 years. There were 450 malignant (66.8%) and 224 benign (33.2%) patient cases, yielding a malignancy ratio of approximately 2:1.

Supplementary Table S1 summarizes the training dataset composition, which comprised 11,012 images from 1151 patients. The combined dataset included 629 malignant cases (54.6%) and 522 benign cases (45.4%), achieving a near-balanced overall malignancy ratio of 1.20. The AURORA-GB dataset contributed the majority of malignant cases (450 patients, 71.5% of all malignant cases), addressing the severe class imbalance present in existing public datasets.

The held-out internal test set consisted of temporally independent cohort of 97 patients (benign: 32, malignant: 65). The external dataset comprised 122 patients from three centers (benign: 64 and malignant: 58).

Cross-validation performance

Five-fold stratified cross-validation demonstrated robust model performance with a mean balanced accuracy of 82% (95% CI: 80%–84%), as shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Internal and external validation results

On the held-out internal test set (n = 97), the model achieved superior performance with 87.7% sensitivity (95% CI: 78.9%–95.1%), 86.2% specificity (95% CI: 72.4%–96.9%), and 0.883 AUC (95% CI: 0.786–0.963), as detailed in Table 1.

Attention and interpretability analysis

The gated attention mechanism identified diagnostically relevant images, with attention weights correlating with radiologist assessments (r = 0.74, p < 0.001). Analysis of 50 randomly selected cases revealed significantly higher mean attention weights for images rated as highly diagnostic by radiologists (4–5 on Likert scale) compared to those rated less important (1–2 on Likert scale): 0.86 ± 0.11 vs. 0.23 ± 0.14, p < 0.001.

The model appropriately attended to the gallbladder or gallbladder–liver interface in 82.4% of malignant cases, an important diagnostic region for assessing invasion (Figs. 3 and 4).

Discussion

This study represents a comprehensive evaluation of deep learning for gallbladder cancer detection on US to date, introducing both methodological innovations and substantial dataset contributions that advance the field significantly. A critical contribution of this study is the rigorous benchmarking of MIL architectures. Our results highlight a common trade-off in medical AI: complexity vs. generalizability. While the Transformer-based TransMIL model achieved the highest internal AUC (0.871), likely by learning complex intra-patient feature correlations, it suffered significant performance degradation in external validation (Center 1 AUC: 0.617). This suggests that Transformer models may overfit to site-specific imaging artifacts (e.g., specific ultrasound machine noise patterns) when training data is moderate. In contrast, the proposed GAIA-MIL and CLAM architectures, which rely on simpler attention mechanisms, demonstrated superior robustness to domain shifts. GAIA-MIL provided the most consistent performance across the heterogeneous external centers. These findings suggest that for US-based GBC detection, where bag sizes are relatively small and image features vary widely, standard gated-attention mechanisms provide a more reliable and clinically safer backbone than complex Transformers.

Our MIL approach addresses critical limitations in existing research while demonstrating robust performance across multiple clinical centers. Our results demonstrate substantial improvements over previous deep learning studies. Gupta et al. achieved 92.3% sensitivity and 74.4% specificity on 273 internal test patients, representing the largest previously reported held-out validation cohort.8 In comparison, our model achieved 87.7% sensitivity and 86.2% specificity on internal testing, with notably improved specificity that reduces false positive rates. More importantly, our study validates performance across 122 patients from multiple centers, representing the first effort at establishing generalizability of a US-based deep learning model for GBC detection.

Basu et al. reported a CNN model (GBCNet) that achieved impressive performance with 95.0% specificity and 97.6% sensitivity using curriculum learning techniques.9 However, their study was limited to single-center validation and employed conventional image-level classification and report only cross validation results (no external test set). Our MIL approach offers several advantages over their method: (1) automatic selection of diagnostically relevant images from patient bags, eliminating the need for manual image curation; (2) more clinically realistic evaluation by handling variable numbers of images per patient; and (3) multi-center validation that demonstrates real-world generalizability. While our sensitivity (87.7%) is lower than GBCNet's reported performance, our approach provides more robust validation and addresses practical deployment challenges.

Recent transformer-based approaches have shown promising results, with GBCHV model achieving 96.21% accuracy using anatomy-aware transformations.10 Several other studies have shown promising results using US based deep learning techniques for classification of gallbladder abnormalities.17, 18, 19, 20 However, these studies were limited to single datasets (mostly employing the public GBCU dataset) and did not address the fundamental challenge of multiple images per patient. Our MIL framework incorporates attention mechanisms similar to transformers but operates at the patient level, providing more clinically relevant predictions. The attention weights generated by our model (correlation r = 0.74 with radiologist assessments) demonstrate interpretability comparable to transformer approaches while handling the practical challenges of variable bag sizes.

The MIL paradigm represents a significant methodological advancement for medical imaging applications.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 Unlike conventional approaches that treat each image independently, our MIL framework mirrors clinical practice where radiologists review multiple images to make diagnostic decisions. This approach is particularly relevant for US imaging, where image quality varies significantly due to patient factors, operator expertise, and equipment settings. The gated attention mechanism automatically identifies the most diagnostically valuable images within each patient's study.

Our analysis of attention patterns reveals that the model successfully focuses on established indicators of malignancy, including irregular wall thickening, loss of layered pattern, and extramural masses.6 This interpretability is crucial for clinical acceptance and provides confidence that the model's decisions align with established medical knowledge. The ability to visualize both bag-level attention (which images are most important) and image-level features (which regions within images drive decisions) through Grad-CAM provides comprehensive interpretability that supports clinical decision-making.

The introduction of the AURORA-GB dataset represents a paradigm shift for gallbladder cancer AI research. Prior to this study, the research community had limited GBC US images across all public US datasets, with severe class imbalance that made robust model training extremely challenging. Our dataset contributes US images of 450 additional GBC cases. This contribution will enable balanced model training. Furthermore, the demographic diversity represented by 674 patients from a major tertiary care center provides also promises to enhance gallbladder imaging research.

The multi-center validation reveals both the promise and challenges of clinical deployment for AI-based GBC detection. Performance varied across external centers (AUC range: 0.699–0.950). The excellent performance at Center 2 (90.0% sensitivity, 100.0% specificity, AUC: 0.950) demonstrates the model's potential when imaging protocols align with training data characteristics. This suggests that standardized imaging protocols and equipment calibration could significantly improve cross-center performance. Conversely, the moderate performance at Center 1 (AUC 0.749) underscores the challenges posed by domain shift in medical imaging. Factors contributing to performance variation likely include differences in US equipment, imaging protocols, patient populations, and operator techniques. These findings emphasize the need for robust domain adaptation strategies (such as adversarial training or federated learning and local fine-tuning before clinical deployment. Our subgroup analysis provides important insights into the clinical contexts in which the model excels or faces challenges. The model achieved consistently high performance across all lesion morphologies, with no statistically significant differences for polypoidal, wall-thickening, or mass-forming subtypes. However, performance varied considerably based on lesion size and gallbladder distension, mirroring well-known challenges in US diagnosis. While the model excelled at detecting large lesions (>20 mm), sensitivity dropped for lesions smaller than 10 mm (internal: 75.0%; external: 69.4%). This performance gap represents a significant limitation, given that early detection is crucial to ensure curative resection. Similarly, diagnostic accuracy was significantly higher in distended gallbladders compared to contracted ones (p < 0.01). The reduced sensitivity in contracted gallbladders (internal: 75.0%; external: 69.6%) likely results from the obscuration of key anatomical features, such as mural layering and liver interface, which our interpretability analysis identified as a primary decision-making feature.

These findings align with prior work by Gupta et al.8 and underscore that, like human experts, the model's efficacy is partly dependent on optimal patient preparation. The presence of gallstones did not significantly impact diagnostic accuracy. Collectively, while the system demonstrates strong generalizability, future optimization must target these challenging edge cases, specifically small lesions and suboptimal exams, to maximize clinical benefit.

Our detailed analysis of misclassified cases provides valuable insights for clinical implementation. False predictions predominantly occurred in challenging scenarios including contracted gallbladders, wall echo shadow complexes, tumefactive sludge, gallbladder neck lesion, and GBC mimickers like xanthogranulomatous cholecystitis. These patterns align with known diagnostic challenges in clinical practice, where these conditions frequently confound even experienced radiologists.6 The model's difficulty with these cases suggests areas for future improvement through targeted data augmentation or specialized training protocols.

There were several limitations to our study. First, the retrospective nature of our study limits the assessment of the model's performance in a dynamic clinical environment. While we employed multi-center validation to simulate diverse settings, retrospective designs cannot fully capture temporal variations in clinical practice, such as updates to US imaging protocols or real-time operator variability. Consequently, the performance metrics reported here may not fully reflect diagnostic accuracy in a prospective clinical workflow. Second, the sample sizes of the individual external validation cohorts (Center 1: n = 30; Center 2: n = 20) were relatively small, which may result in wide confidence intervals and limit the statistical power of center-specific performance estimates. Similarly, interpretation of subgroup analyses, particularly regarding p-values for stone presence or lesion morphology, should be approached with caution due to the small number of patients in specific categories. Future studies with larger prospective cohorts are necessary to confirm these subgroup findings. Third, while our dataset isvery large, it primarily focuses on common benign and malignant categories and does not fully represent rare gallbladder pathologies, such as adenomas or metastases.

In conclusion, this study establishes a benchmark for AI-assisted GBC detection on US using MIL approach. Through rigorous comparison, we demonstrated that attention-based models (GAIA-MIL) offer superior cross-center generalizability. While challenges remain regarding small lesions and contracted gallbladders, our results demonstrate the significant potential for AI to improve GBC diagnosis through earlier, more accurate detection of this aggressive malignancy on US.
