Gait Biomechanical Profiles Following Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction in Sexually‐Immature Pediatrics
Authors: Elizabeth Bjornsen, J. Troy Blackburn, Jason R. Franz, W. Zachary Horton, Darin Padua, Sandra Shultz, Samantha Tayne, Brian Pietrosimone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAberrant gait biomechanics following ACL reconstruction (ACLR) are linked to knee osteoarthritis development in adult patients. However, limited research exists to characterize walking biomechanics profiles of pediatric ACLR patients. The study purpose was to determine (1) differences in biomechanical profiles between pediatric ACLR patients (Tanner Stage I–IV) and two comparison‐control groups (i.e., uninjured, matched pediatrics, adult‐matched ACLR); (2) associations between biomechanical and Knee Osteoarthritis Outcomes Score (KOOS)‐Child outcomes in pediatric ACLR patients. Gait biomechanics were collected in pediatric ACLR (n = 25), pediatric controls (n = 25), and adult ACLR groups (n = 25). Pediatric patients completed the KOOS‐Child at the same session. A functional mixed effects model determined between‐group differences in biomechanical variables. Uncorrected partial correlations, controlling for gait speed, were utilized to determine the association between discrete biomechanics and KOOS‐Child scores. Pediatric ACLR patients demonstrated lesser first and second peak vertical ground reaction force (vGRF) and greater midstance vGRF than pediatric controls. Pediatric ACLR patients exhibited lesser midstance and greater late stance vGRF compared to the adult ACLR group. Pediatric ACLR patients demonstrated lesser knee flexion angle, knee extension moment, and knee abduction moment profiles compared to pediatric controls and adult ACLR patients throughout the majority of the stance phase. Greater midstance vGRF was associated with greater KOOS‐Child Quality of Life scores in the contralateral limb (r = 0.54, p = 0.006). Pediatric ACLR patients exhibit unique biomechanical profiles compared to pediatric controls and adult ACLR patients; however, the associations with patient‐reported outcomes remain unclear. Pediatrics may experience an exaggerated response to ACLR that may impact knee joint health.

Introduction

Incidences of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries are increasing at higher rates in pediatrics than adults [1, 2, 3]. A retrospective analysis of insurance data in the United States (2007–2011) observed individuals between 10 and 14 and 15–19 years of age exhibited an 18% increase in ACL injury diagnoses [3]. Additionally, the Norwegian Knee Register identified 40% and 55% increases in ACL injuries in boys (≤ 16 yrs.) and girls (≤ 14 yrs.), respectively, between 2005 and 2007 and 2019–2021 [4]. Extensive literature has been devoted to understanding unique factors associated with knee osteoarthritis development in adults following ACLR [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Despite the known rise in pediatric ACL injuries and high risk of concomitant injuries at the time of ACL injury or graft rupture [3, 11], less than 3% of ACL literature is focused on pediatrics [12], and little is known about the factors driving long‐term outcomes in pediatric patients post‐ACLR.

Adult ACLR patients demonstrate aberrant gait biomechanical profiles and knee‐related symptoms early post‐ACLR that persist for years following surgery despite undergoing standard post‐operative rehabilitation. Hallmark biomechanical features of adult ACLR aberrant gait profiles include less dynamic vertical ground reaction force (vGRF) (i.e., characterized by lesser bimodal vGRF peaks and greater vGRF during midstance) [7], reduced knee flexion angle (KFA), and lesser knee joint moments [7, 13, 14, 15], all of which have been associated with deleterious changes to the knee joint tissue (i.e., articular cartilage) and worse patient‐reported outcomes (PROs) [16, 17, 18] suggestive of early osteoarthritis development [6, 10, 19, 20]. Nearly 40% of adult patients continue to report persistent knee‐related symptoms at 6 years post‐ACLR on the adult Knee Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS), similar to the 43% that reported symptoms at 2 years post‐ACLR [21]. While extensive literature has documented the aberrant gait profiles of adult ACLR patients compared to uninjured, adult controls [7, 9, 13, 15, 22], and the connection between aberrant gait and worse PROs in adults [16, 17, 18], previous literature assessing gait biomechanics in the pediatric population post‐ACLR is fundamentally lacking and often includes combined pediatric and adult cohorts. In addition, limited research exists to characterize healthy pediatric gait profiles beyond spatiotemporal outcomes. An initial analysis comparing older pediatric (16–17 yrs.; n = 13) and adult (18–35 yrs.; n = 13) patients between 6 and 12 months post‐ACLR found older pediatric patients demonstrated greater KFA (maximum mean difference of 4.16°) throughout stance phase and greater KEM in late stance phase (81–98%; maximum mean difference of 0.004 BW x height) [23]. However, this previous work did not assess maturation stage, did not include younger pediatric patients, and did not compare these participants to adult and maturation‐matched controls [23]. Additionally, PROs are the primary assessment collected in pediatric ACLR patients with high variability in the questionnaires delivered, some of which are not well‐understood by pediatrics [24]. Further, we are not aware of any research that has sought to identify similar links between PROs and biomechanical outcomes, as observed in adult ACLR patients, in pediatric ACLR patients.

Rapid physiological and body compositional changes occur in pediatrics, especially during pubertal years, to prepare the body for adulthood [25, 26, 27]. Although chronological age is commonly used to characterize patients into pediatric subsets [26], pediatrics of the same chronological age can differ substantially in maturation [28]. Therefore, considering chronological age alone may lead to misclassification of participants. Tanner Staging provides a cost‐effective method to determine the stage of sexual maturity [29].

The primary purpose of the study was to compare gait biomechanical profiles in the ACLR limb (i.e., vGRF, KFA, KEM, and KAM) throughout the stance phase between pediatric ACLR patients and two comparison‐control groups (i.e., matched, uninjured pediatrics and adult ACLR patients). The secondary, hypothesis‐generating purpose of this study was to determine associations between discrete, bilateral biomechanical outcomes (i.e., vGRF, KFA, KEM, and KAM) and PROs in pediatric ACLR patients using the Knee Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS)‐Child scores. Based on preliminary findings [23], we hypothesized that pediatric ACLR patients would exhibit: (1) less dynamic vGRF profiles, (2) greater KFA throughout the stance phase, and (3) greater KEM and KAM surrounding the first peak in comparison to uninjured pediatrics and adult ACLR patients. We also hypothesized that (1) greater peak KFA and KEM, (2) lower first and second peak vGRF and lesser peak KAM, and (3) greater midstance vGRF would associate with worse PROs as measured by the KOOS‐Child Quality of Life (QOL) subscale.

Methods

Study Design

We performed a cross‐sectional study in which gait biomechanics were collected from pediatric ACLR and matched pediatric, uninjured control participants at a single time point. We also matched pediatric ACLR participants to adult ACLR participants who were part of a larger cross‐sectional cohort. A previous study reported a strong effect (d = 0.83) for the difference in knee flexion angle (4.16°; occurring between 29% and 100% of stance) between older pediatrics and adult ACLR patients within 12 months post‐ACLR [23]. With 25 participants per group, we were powered (1‐β = 0.8; two‐sided 0.05 alpha) to detect similarly strong between‐group differences in each biomechanical variable of interest (d > 0.83; G*Power, v3.1.9.2). Pediatric participants were recruited from multiple large health systems and private practice orthopedic clinics, physical therapy clinics, travel sport clubs, and local schools. All study procedures were approved by the University's Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent and assent.

Participants

Pediatric Participants: ACLR and Pediatric Uninjured Control

Pediatric ACLR patients were between 6 and 24 months post‐primary unilateral ACLR and self‐classified as sexually immature (i.e., Tanner Stage classification of I–IV). The pediatric uninjured controls were recruited concurrently to match to the pediatric ACLR group on sex, Tanner Stage category (i.e., Early Puberty [Tanner Stage I–II] or Mid/Late Puberty [Tanner Stage III‐IV]), and Tegner Activity Score (±3). We excluded individuals from either cohort that self‐classified as a Tanner Stage V, were pregnant, had a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 35, required a multi‐ligament reconstruction at the time of ACLR, had a lower extremity orthopedic injury within the past 6 months, sustained a lower extremity fracture at the time of ACL injury, or were diagnosed with osteoarthritis.

Adult ACLR Participants

We leveraged data from a previous cross‐sectional sample of adult ACLR patients to serve as a comparison‐control group. All biomechanical data were collected in the same laboratory. Adult patients (18–35 yrs.) all underwent primary, unilateral ACLR and were matched to the pediatric ACLR group [sex, months post‐ACLR (±2), and Tegner Activity Score (±3)]. We excluded adult ACLR patients that were pregnant, had a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 35, had a multi‐ligament reconstruction at the time of ACLR, had a lower extremity orthopedic injury within the past 6 months, suffered a lower extremity fracture at the time of ACL injury, or were diagnosed with osteoarthritis.

Tanner Stage Questionnaire

Pediatric ACLR and pediatric controls completed a self‐report questionnaire [30] based on the 5 stages of Tanner maturity [29]. Self‐reported Tanner Staging has been shown to be valid (79% to 88% agreement between children and physicians) and reliable in children (test‐retest concordance ranging between 84% and 90%) [31, 32]. Participants were instructed to identify the Tanner stage (I–V) with which the individual most closely identified (i.e., breast and pubic hair for girls; genitalia and pubic hair for boys). The Tanner Stage questionnaire was provided via paper and pencil and was completed in a private room. After participants completed the survey, they were instructed to place the questionnaire in a sealed envelope to maintain privacy. We anticipated that the majority of participants would report mid‐to‐late pubertal stages, as the highest incidence of pediatric ACL injury occurs in adolescence [1]. Therefore, we categorized pediatric participants into Early Pubertal (i.e., Tanner Stage I–II) and Mid/Late Pubertal groups (i.e., Tanner Stage III–IV). We excluded individuals that ranked Tanner Stage V on both genitalia/breast and pubic hair categories. Pediatric uninjured controls were matched to pediatric ACLR patients within the same Early Pubertal or Mid/Late Pubertal categories.

KOOS‐Child QOL Instrument

The 5‐subscale KOOS instrument is commonly utilized to track patient‐reported recovery status in adults post‐ACLR [8, 21, 33, 34]. However, the KOOS is poorly understood or irrelevant to pediatric, school‐aged populations, thus a KOOS‐Child instrument was developed [35, 36]. Pediatric ACLR participants and pediatric controls electronically completed the KOOS‐Child QOL subscale, which is recognized as the most responsive subscale in adults [33], during the same laboratory visit. Adult ACLR participants completed the original KOOS QOL at the time of the original study data collection, and scores were reported for reference purposes only. The QOL subscale includes 4 questions on a 5‐point Likert scale, ranging in continuous scores between 1 and 100 [35]. Lower scores are interpreted as experiencing worse QOL post‐ACLR.

Gait Biomechanics Collection and Analysis

Participants were outfitted with 26 retroreflective markers using a modified Helen‐Hayes marker set and a 3‐marker sacral cluster as previously described [17, 37, 38]. Standard instructions were given to participants to collect habitual walking speed using infrared timing gates (Dash‐R), instructing them to walk as if “walking over a sidewalk at a comfortable walking pace.” After the participant acclimated to the laboratory environment, a minimum of 5 walking trials were collected and averaged to determine habitual walking speed. Kinematic data was collected using a 10‐camera motion capture system (Vicon Motion Systems, Oxford, UK) and sampled at 120 Hz. Kinetic data was collected over 3 staggered, embedded force plates at 1200 Hz (Bertec, Columbus, OH). Trials were considered successful if the stride was not visibly altered, each foot fully contacted the force plate, and the participant maintained a speed within 5% of the collected average habitual gait speed [39]. Five walking trials were utilized for analysis and visually assess for outliers. All kinematic and kinetic data were low‐pass filtered at 10 Hz (4th order Butterworth) and time‐normalized to the stance phase [i.e., defined as the interval between heel strike (vGRF > 20 N) and toe off (vGRF < 20 N; Visual 3D, C‐Motion, Germantown, MD; 2020 × 64)]. vGRF was normalized to body weight; KEM and KAM were normalized to the product of height (m) and weight (N). Knee joint moments were calculated as internal moments, with greater extension and abduction reflective of more negative values. Discrete biomechanical variables were extracted prior to time normalization (i.e., first peak vGRF, midstance vGRF, peak KFA, peak KEM, peak KAM). All peak values were identified as the greatest value occurring within the first 50% of the stance phase. Midstance vGRF was defined as the local minimum between the first and second vGRF peak values. The average of the 5 walking trials per person, for each discrete biomechanical variable, was utilized for statistical analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Primary: Functional Mixed‐Effect Model

A functional mixed effect, Bayesian hierarchical model [40] was utilized to determine between‐group differences in each biomechanical variables (vGRF, KFA, KEM, and KAM) throughout the entirety of stance phase (1%–100%) in both the ACLR and uninvolved limbs. For general reference, 1% denotes initial heel strike, 50% of stance represents midstance of gait, and 100% references toe‐off. For general descriptive purposes in the narrative, early stance refers to the period between heel strike and initial vGRF peak and late stance refers to the period between the second vGRF peak and toe‐off. The model hierarchy was composed of two levels. The top level consisted of penalized B‐splines [41] fitted to the walking trials, including a smoothness penalty on the spline coefficients to avoid overfitting. The second level modeled the spline coefficients with a multivariate mixed effects model incorporating subject‐specific random effects. The regression structure of the mixed effects model was constructed using a normal distribution for the errors and dummy variables to represent group effects. When included, covariates were centered around key values. Model parameters were then estimated, and the accompanying 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated around the mean group difference. We defined regions of stance phase where the 95% CI of the mean difference at each 1% increment of stance phase did not include zero as a statistically significant difference. Point‐wise Cohen's d effect sizes were determined from the group means and pooled standard deviations. Cohen's d effect sizes were reported at the percentage of stance with the greatest between‐group difference in proportions of stance exhibiting statistically significant differences. Walking speed is associated with walking gait kinetics and kinematics [42]; therefore, we performed a post hoc sensitivity analysis to assess the impact of gait speed on our functional biomechanical findings. All analyses were performed in R (R Core Team, 2021.09.2) using open‐source code available at GitHub [43]. The uninvolved limb data was reported in Supporting Table 1, –2. Discrete biomechanical data outcomes were reported in Supporting Tables 3, –7.

Secondary: Associations Between Discrete Gait Outcomes and KOOS‐Child QOL

Box‐and‐whisker plots were utilized to assess the normality of the KOOS‐Child QOL scores. Partial correlations were used to identify the association between KOOS‐Child QOL scores in the pediatric ACLR group and each biomechanical predictor variable, after accounting for gait speed. We reported the partial correlation coefficient and interpreted the strength of the partial associations as weak |r| between 0.2 and 0.49, moderate |r| between 0.5 and 0.79, and strong |r| > 0.8 [44]. The alpha level was set at p < 0.05, and due to the exploratory nature of the analysis, we did not correct for multiple comparisons.

Results

We included a total of 75 participants in our analysis (25 pediatric ACLR, 25 uninjured pediatric controls, and 25 adult ACLR patients). The adult ACLR group was older and reported lower Tegner activity scores compared to the pediatric ACLR group (all p < 0.001; Table 1). There were no differences in gait speed or months post‐ACLR between the pediatric and adult ACLR groups (p > 0.05). As expected, the pediatric ACLR group demonstrated significantly lower scores on the KOOS‐Child QOL score compared to pediatric controls (p < 0.0001; Table 1). No differences in chronological age, Tegner activity score, or gait speed were observed between the pediatric ACLR and pediatric control groups (all p > 0.05).

Primary Analysis: Functional Mixed‐Effect Model

Vertical Ground Reaction Force

The pediatric ACLR group walked with lesser vGRF in the ACLR limb during early stance (10%–11%, 16%–29% of stance phase; −4.00% BW, −7.00% BW maximum between‐group difference; Cohen's d = −0.36, −0.72), greater vGRF in midstance (46%–60%; 5.00% BW; d = 0.73), lesser vGRF in the ACLR limb surrounding late stance (75%–86%; −5.7% BW; d = −0.85) compared to the pediatric control group (Figure 1). After controlling for walking speed, the pediatric ACLR group still demonstrated lesser vGRF in early (21%–26%, −4.00% BW; d = −0.76) and late stance (77%–85%; −5.00% BW, d = −0.85) compared to pediatric controls. Conversely, the pediatric ACLR group exhibited lesser vGRF during midstance (57%–67%; −4.00% BW; d = −0.88) and greater vGRF in a small portion of late stance (91%–95%; 4.00%BW; d = 0.54) in comparison to the adult ACLR group (Figure 1). Pediatric ACLR patients still exhibited lesser midstance vGRF (62%–67%; −4.00% BW; d = −0.93) and greater vGRF in late stance (90%–96%; 4.00% BW; d = 0.54) in comparison to the adult ACLR group after controlling for walking speed.

Knee Flexion Angle

In the pediatric ACLR group, lesser KFA in the involved limb was observed in early and late stance (4%–46%, 92%–101%; −2.81°, −2.33°; d = −0.55; 0.50) and greater KFA in mid to late stance (59%–83%; 1.77°; d = 0.54) compared to the pediatric control group (Figure 1). After controlling for walking speed, the pediatric ACLR group still demonstrated lesser KFA in early and late stance (6%–45%, 92%–101%, −1.97°, −2.39°; d = −2.74, −3.08) and greater KFA in midstance (60%–83%; 1.58°, d = 0.80) compared to pediatric controls. Pediatric ACLR individuals also demonstrated lesser KFA throughout the entirety of stance phase when compared to adult ACLR patients (1%–101%; −5.11°; d = 1.00; Figure 1). Pediatric ACLR patients still exhibited lesser KFA throughout stance phase (1%–101%; −5.10; d = −1.00) compared to the adult ACLR group after controlling for walking speed.

Knee Extension Moment

The pediatric ACLR group walked with lesser KEM in the ACLR limb in early stance (9%–61%; −0.01 BW × height; d = 0.95) and lesser knee flexion moment in late stance (74%–79%; −0.003 BW × height; d = −0.38) compared to the control ACLR group (Figure 1). In our post hoc analysis, pediatric ACLR patients also exhibited lesser KEM in early to midstance (9%–62%; −0.01 BW × height; d = −0.95) and in late stance (75%–84%; −0.003 BW × height, d = −0.38) compared to pediatric controls after controlling for walking speed. Similarly, the pediatric ACLR group exhibited lesser KEM in early stance, transitioning to a greater knee flexion moment in late stance compared to adults (18%–90%; 0.01 BW × height; d = 0.81; Figure 1). After controlling for gait speed, the pediatric ACLR group also demonstrated lesser KEM in the early portion of stance phase and greater knee flexion moment into late stance compared to ACLR adult patients (15%–90%; 0.01 BW × height; d = 0.81).

Frontal Plane Moment

Pediatric ACLR patients demonstrated lesser KAM throughout the majority of stance phase compared to both pediatric controls (10%–38%, 86%–92%; 0.01; 0.003 BW × height; d = 0.59, 0.44) and adult ACLR patients in the ACLR limb (14%–74%; 0.004 BW × height; d = 0.72; Figure 1). After controlling for gait speed, pediatric ACLR patients exhibited lesser KAM during early to midstance (8%–47%; 0.004 BW × height; d = 0.58) and late stance (88%–92%, 0.003 BW × height; d = 0.47) compared to pediatric controls (Figure 1). Similarly to the primary analysis, pediatric ACLR patients still demonstrated lesser KAM throughout most of stance phase (13%–75%; 0.004 BW × height; d = 0.72) compared to adult ACLR patients after controlling for gait speed.

Secondary Exploratory Analysis: Associations Between Discrete Gait Outcomes and KOOS‐Child QOL

Greater midstance vGRF in the uninvolved limb was associated with greater KOOS‐Child QOL scores after accounting for the effects of walking speed (partial r = 0.54, p = 0.01; Table 1). Only negligible, non‐statistically significant associations were found between the other biomechanical variables and KOOS‐Child QOL scores after controlling for gait speed (Table 2).

Discussion

Consistent with our primary hypothesis, the pediatric ACLR patients walked with less dynamic vGRF loading profiles (i.e., characterized by lesser first and second peak vGRF and greater midstance loading) [45] compared to pediatric controls. However, pediatric ACLR patients demonstrated elements of a more dynamic loading profile (i.e., lesser vGRF surrounding midstance and greater vGRF loading during late stance) compared to adult ACLR patients. Clear sagittal plane differences were apparent throughout stance phase, as the pediatric ACLR group walked with lesser KFA surrounding the first peak compared to the adult ACLR group and uninjured controls. Pediatric ACLR patients exhibited lesser KFA and KEM profiles compared to both the pediatric control and the adult ACLR patient groups. Further, frontal plane loading profiles in both limbs were lower in the pediatric ACLR group than when compared to both the adult ACLR and uninjured controls throughout the majority of stance, suggesting KAM gait changes may be unique to the pediatric ACLR patient population.

Pediatric ACLR patients demonstrated a stiffened knee gait strategy throughout stance phase compared to controls, which has been previously characterized as lesser KFA surrounding the first peak and lesser KEM in the first half of the stance phase [14, 46]. Our KFA findings are supported by Urseï et al., who found lesser peak KFA in the ACLR and contralateral limb at initial contact and early stance (10%–25% gait cycle) in pediatric patients (9–15 years old) 24 months post‐ACLR compared to uninjured pediatrics [47]. In the present study, the pediatric ACLR group exhibited approximately a −0.01BW × height difference surrounding the first KEM peak compared to uninjured controls, a value similar to the difference observed between adult ACLR and adult controls at 6 and 12 months post‐ACLR [7]. Additionally, pediatric ACLR patients walked with less dynamic vGRF loading profiles, a profile in adult patients 6–12 months post‐ACLR that has been observationally linked to older adults with early radiographic knee osteoarthritis [48]. The pediatric ACLR group also walked with lesser KAM profiles compared to matched pediatric controls. KAM is considered a proxy measure of medial compartment loading [45]. Our finding of lesser KAM loading in pediatric ACLR patients is likely not a developmental characteristic, as pediatric ACLR patients demonstrated lesser KAM compared to uninjured, pediatric individuals. Further, adults that developed radiographic osteoarthritis at 5 years post‐ACLR demonstrated lesser KAM loading than adults who did not develop osteoarthritis at 6 months post‐ACLR [6]. Changes in loading parameters (i.e., magnitude, location) can induce an associated biological response in the articular cartilage [49, 50]. Therefore, a stiffened knee gait strategy, combined with a less dynamic vGRF profile and lesser KAM loading profiles in pediatric ACLR individuals, may contribute to localized and sustained loading on the tibiofemoral articular cartilage. Our observational study findings suggest that pediatric ACLR patients exhibit altered gait patterns within the first 24 months post‐ACLR compared to uninjured, pediatric controls agrees with previous research that has also identified biomechanical differences between adult ACLR patients and adult uninjured controls [6, 7, 9, 13, 15].

Minimal differences in vGRF loading were observed between pediatric and adult ACLR patients. However, KFA profiles in pediatric ACLR patients reflected walking in a stiffer (i.e., more extended) sagittal plane position compared to adults and demonstrated even lesser KEM surrounding the first peak (−0.005 BW × height difference) compared to adult ACLR patients. Previous literature describing the stiffened knee gait has only been qualitatively described in adult ACLR populations [14, 46]; therefore, it is unknown if an exaggerated stiffened knee strategy would present with worse clinically‐relevant biological outcomes in pediatric ACLR patients. While previous research assessing biomechanics in pediatric ACLR patients is scarce, our findings are in contrast to some previous work. A recent exploratory analysis identified that older adolescent ACLR patients (16–17 yrs.) demonstrated greater KFA and KEM gait profiles compared to adult ACLR patients (18–35 yrs.) [23]. However, the former study was comprised of a different population, as it included older pediatrics between 16 and 17 years of age and did not assess developmental stage. Additionally, differences in KAM loading profiles throughout stance phase were most apparent between pediatric and adult ACLR patients. In adults, walking with lesser peak KAM at 6 months post‐ACLR is linked to various biochemical markers consistent with early osteoarthritis development [10]. Further, lesser peak KAM and lesser KAM loading profiles assessed throughout the stance phase of gait are associated with worse MRI estimates of tibiofemoral articular cartilage composition in adults within the first 12 months post‐ACLR [20, 51]. Lesser KAM loading profiles in adult ACLR participants have been linked to an acute swelling response of the medial femoral cartilage, as assessed via ultrasound, following an acute walking bout [52]. Early tibiofemoral cartilage thickening in adults may be suggestive of early osteoarthritis development [53]. Therefore, it is important to determine the link between the unique KAM profiles exhibited by pediatric ACLR patients and biological markers consistent with osteoarthritis development. Future research should determine if a more pronounced stiffened knee gait strategy combined with lesser KAM profiles is linked to worse outcomes in pediatrics post‐ACLR than compared to adult patients.

In our exploratory analysis, we identified only one statistically significant association between greater midstance vGRF in the uninvolved limb and higher scores on the KOOS‐Child in pediatric ACLR patients. While our sample size was small (n = 25), we were powered (1‐β = 0.8; two‐sided 0.05 alpha) to detect moderate associations (r = 0.55) between biomechanics and PROs. Our findings contrast previous literature that has reported multiple, strong associations between worse biomechanical outcomes and worse PROs in adult ACLR patients [16, 17, 18, 54]. Adult ACLR patients demonstrate greater midstance vGRF at both 6 and 12 months post‐ACLR compared to uninjured controls, with adult ACLR patients further increasing midstance vGRF loading between 6 and 12 months post‐ACLR [7]. Further, greater vGRF loading magnitudes in midstance have also been associated with worse MRI estimates of T1rho relaxation times of the medial tibiofemoral articular cartilage in young adults at 12 months post‐ACLR [19]. Therefore, longitudinal assessments of biological outcomes in the pediatric ACLR patient population should be assessed to determine if greater midstance loading in the uninvolved limb is associated with worse biological outcomes, a finding that would be unique from adult ACLR patients. Conversely, the relationship between greater vGRF loading in midstance and greater scores on the KOOS‐Child QOL could reflect differences in how pediatric ACLR patients approach knee‐related survey questionnaires. Previous work has suggested ceiling effects may be inherent to the KOOS‐Child QOL subscale [55] and may not best capture patient‐reported recovery in high‐level, pediatric athletes. The majority of the cohort in the present study were elite, youth athletes with 88% of the sample reporting Tegner Activity scores of 9 and 10's. Thereby, the structural components of the KOOS‐Child QOL subscale may not effectively capture meaningful changes in QOL for a young, highly athletic population.

The present study identified biomechanical profile differences, primarily in sagittal plane kinematics and kinetics and KAM loading, between pediatric ACLR and adult ACLR patients. Further, we found that gait biomechanics were similar between limbs, highlighting that bilateral limb changes are likely occurring in pediatric ACLR patients within the 6–24 month post‐operative window. Bilateral biomechanical changes are occurring in pediatrics post‐ACLR, which is consistent with previous research demonstrating bilateral biomechanical changes in adult ACLR patients [7]. Our findings provide support for gait interventions that seek to modify biomechanics in both limbs in pediatric ACLR patients. Current mechanisms exist to acutely address aberrant gait profiles in adults. By cuing adults to increase peak vGRF, adult ACLR patients exhibit kinematic and kinetic profiles that resemble gait profiles of uninjured controls [56, 57, 58]. In a separate study, adult ACLR patients who demonstrated an increase in serum cartilage oligomeric matrix protein (COMP), a biochemical marker of cartilage degradation, during an acute bout of walking, demonstrated lesser serum COMP concentrations after receiving the same cue to increase peak vGRF [59]. Adult patients only 6–12 months post‐ACLR demonstrate similar vGRF biomechanical profiles as older adults with mild radiographic knee osteoarthritis [48] and radiographic osteoarthritis patients who report knee‐related symptoms demonstrate less‐dynamic vGRF profiles compared to asymptomatic radiographic osteoarthritis patients [60]. However, using the same biomechanical targets (i.e., increased vGRF) in pediatric patients in the presence of known biomechanical differences (i.e., exaggerated stiffened knee strategy and lesser frontal plane loading), could potentially be problematic. Future research should determine the biomechanical response, and associated mechanobiological and PROs, of acutely cuing an increase in peak vGRF in pediatric populations to determine if pediatrics should be included in clinical trials currently designed for adult ACLR participants.

While the present study sought to comprehensively characterize the walking gait profiles of pediatric ACLR patients, there are limitations that should be considered. Our sample included physically active pediatric individuals in the Mid/Late Pubertal Tanner Stage category only (i.e., Tanner Stage III and IV's). Future work should seek to identify if pediatric gait biomechanical profiles differ by Tanner Stage post‐ACLR. Further, while the present study collected Tanner Stage in all participants, Tanner Stage of our adult participants was not previously collected due to differences in study designs. Previous research has estimated that both boys and girls self‐reporting Tanner Stage V's are approximately 15 (25th percentile), 16 (50th percentile) and 17 (75th percentile) years old [61]. However, all adult participants were between 18 and 35 years old, indicating a high likelihood that those individuals would have self‐reported Tanner Stage V's. Further, although we matched our pediatric groups on ±3 on the Tegner Activity Scale a priori, it did not impact our overall matching as nearly 90% of the pediatric groups reported 9's and 10's on the Tegner. Additionally, while we reported the adult KOOS QOL scores for demographic purposes only, we did not statistically compare the adult KOOS QOL scores to the KOOS‐Child QOL scores, as we acknowledge the psychometric properties of the questionnaires may differ. Additionally, we did not assess the skeletal maturity of pediatric patients or exclude pediatric ACLR patients based on ACLR procedure or graft type. Approximately 80% of our pediatric ACLR patients had documented meniscal pathology. Future work may seek to determine the effect of concomitant meniscal injuries on gait biomechanics and self‐reported outcomes in pediatric ACLR patients. Although not a focus of the present study, it is also possible that spatiotemporal aspects of gait may also be contributors to decreased KFA and KEM in the pediatric groups. It is also possible that there are unique challenges in characterizing patient‐reported recovery in pediatric ACLR population and future work should assess the association between biomechanical and biological outcomes.

Conclusion

Pediatric ACLR patients demonstrated unique walking gait biomechanical profiles in comparison to both uninjured, pediatric controls and adult ACLR patients. Specifically, pediatric ACLR patients exhibited minor differences in vGRF, but lesser KEM and KAM loading, and adopted more extended sagittal plane kinematics throughout the entirety of stance phase compared to adult ACLR patients. Pediatric ACLR patients may require developmentally‐appropriate gait interventions post‐ACLR to improve altered gait profiles early post‐ACLR.
