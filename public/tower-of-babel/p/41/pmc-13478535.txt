Realization of the Bienenstock-Cooper-Munro rule in a single memristor
Authors: Jiangshun Huang, Anping Huang, Qiaofeng Yang, Pengzhan Li, Qin Gao, Mei Wang, Zhisong Xiao, Xingwang Zhang, Juan Gao, Xueli Geng, Limin Liu, Yi Du, Ruifeng Lu, Paul K Chu, Zengfeng Di
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuromorphic computing aims to build electronic systems that mimic the brain’s remarkable efficiency and adaptability. A key feature of brain is homeostatic plasticity, which stabilizes neural activity; the Bienenstock–Cooper–Munro (BCM) rule accurately describes this by adjusting a neuron’s sensitivity threshold based on past activity. However, implementing the homeostatic rule in synaptic device remains challenging. Here we show that a CuInP₂S₆ memristor naturally realizes the complete BCM rule. We reveal that the device’s intrinsic junction capacitance generates reverse spikes that cause spike-rate-dependent conductance depression, combining with second-order ionic dynamics to replicate key BCM features. Through simulations, we demonstrate that adding a similar capacitance can universally enable this behavior in conventional memristors. Finally, we build a physical reservoir computing system based on this strategy that exhibits robust, noise-tolerant spatiotemporal processing. This work provides a viable path for deploying adaptive homeostatic plasticity in hardware, enhancing the reliability of neuromorphic systems.Subject terms: Electronic devices, Synaptic plasticity, Electronic devices

Introduction

Neuromorphic computing (NC), inspired by the brain’s structure and function, offers an efficient computing paradigm well-suited for distributed intelligence and edge computing1–3. Its advantages stem from highly parallel neuron–synapse architectures that integrate algorithm and hardware at the physical level4–6. Recent advances in synaptic devices have accelerated NC by enabling in-memory weight storage and efficient multiply–accumulate operations, allowing direct hardware mapping of neural network algorithms. Prototype systems have implemented various network algorithms, demonstrating energy-efficient inference and online learning capabilities7–9. Beyond reproducing conventional algorithms, synaptic devices can exhibit rich dynamical behaviors, including frequency- and timing-dependent plasticity as well as short- and long-term memory mechanisms that support dynamic tasks such as reinforcement learning and reservoir computing7,10,11. However, practical applications still face substantial stability challenges, including device non-idealities and error accumulation under Hebbian rules, as shown in Fig. 1a12,13. In biological neural systems, inherent homeostatic plasticity mechanisms enable tolerance to such errors, and incorporating this feature into the NC system offers a promising route to improved stability14,15.

The Bienenstock–Cooper–Munro (BCM) rule, one of the most accurate models of synaptic plasticity, incorporates a key homeostatic plasticity mechanism16–18. As illustrated in Fig. 1b, synaptic weight in the BCM rule is determined by spike rate, where high rates induce synaptic potentiation and low rates result in depression. The complete BCM rule encompasses two key features: threshold sliding and a non-monotonic enhanced depression effect (EDE). Threshold sliding refers to the dynamic modulation of the frequency threshold based on the history of neuronal activity, while EDE describes the phenomenon where depression is strongest at intermediate spike rates but weakens at low rates. Threshold sliding can be emulated by second-order memristors that involve a spontaneous relaxation process19–21. In such devices, conductance serves as the first-order state variable, evolving through the competition between spike-induced potentiation and spontaneous relaxation, thereby producing spike rate-dependent plasticity (SRDP)22,23. The second-order state variable, such as device temperature, ionic concentration, and defect distribution, interacts with the first-order dynamic process to enable threshold sliding19,21. To date, most memristors exhibit a monotonic spike-rate dependence, which differs from the EDE24,25. Such monotonic behavior leads to continuous weight decay in the absence of input, undermining long-term synaptic weight retention.

To achieve EDE behavior, various strategies have been explored, including specialized pulse-encoding schemes, additional gate electrodes, and load resistances, all of which introduce an extra dynamic process25–32. These methods inevitably increase circuit complexity and rely on external control parameters, diverging from the self-regulated nature of biological synapses. More importantly, such approaches do not rely on spike‑rate coding; instead, they employ non‑rate‑based methods such as triplet‑STDP coding, gate‑voltage modulation, or spike‑voltage encoding, which deviate from the spike-rate coding principle common in neural systems. In principle, non-monotonic EDE behavior can be established through the competition between two opposite monotonic processes27,32,33. In an ideal φ–q-type memristor, the conductance-change rate increases monotonically with spike rate, while the internal relaxation provides a rate-independent decay that does not alter this inherent monotonicity34,35. Therefore, realizing spontaneous EDE requires introducing a spike-rate-dependent depression process that gradually saturates at higher rates (Fig. 1c). When coupled with the intrinsic potentiation and depression process, this enables spontaneous EDE under natural spike-rate coding. Furthermore, such an intrinsic strategy offers enhanced biological compatibility and hardware efficiency compared to external approaches.

Here, we report the realization of the BCM rule using a second-order CuInP₂S₆ (CIPS) memristor, which intrinsically possesses a junction parasitic capacitance (Fig. 1d). In the intervals between spikes, the parasitic capacitance generates a reverse voltage spike, driving a spike-rate-dependent depression behavior that gradually saturates with frequency. By coupling this depression behavior with second-order ionic dynamics, the full BCM rule is realized in a single CIPS memristor. The underlying working mechanism is revealed by the dynamic simulations and equivalent circuit experiments. By replacing this capacitance with an equivalent circuit, we successfully reproduce the EDE in conventional memristors, demonstrating the approach’s generality. Moreover, a physical reservoir computing system based on this principle exhibits robust spatiotemporal information processing and long-term stability. These findings establish a device-level strategy for emulating homeostatic synaptic plasticity in neuromorphic systems.

Results

Electrical properties of CuInP2S6 memristor

The lateral CIPS device was fabricated by mechanically exfoliating layered CIPS flakes and transferring them onto a SiO₂/Si substrate pre-patterned with Au electrodes (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 1). The thickness of the CIPS flake is approximately 44 nm. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) confirmed the presence of Cu, In, P, and S, with Cu existing predominantly in the Cu⁺ oxidation state, consistent with the expected stoichiometric composition of CIPS (Supplementary Fig. 2)36,37. In addition, scanning electron microscopy combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDS) mapping revealed a pronounced Cu distribution, supporting Cu⁺ ion migration as the most plausible mechanism responsible for the resistive switching behavior (Supplementary Fig. 3)38,39. Such ion migration enables nonvolatile resistance modulation in CIPS, thereby allowing the emulation of synaptic functions.

To investigate the electrical properties of the CIPS device, we characterized its resistive switching (RS) behavior. As shown in Fig. 2b, the device exhibits a counterclockwise hysteresis loop and a gradual increase in currents upon 32 consecutive voltage sweeps (0–4 V), demonstrating a transition from a high-resistance state (HRS) to a low-resistance state (LRS) that confirms typical memristive behavior40,41. When the sweep voltage decreases to 1 V, the device current falls to near zero, which is attributed to the cut-in voltage rather than conductance forgetting39,42. Under bidirectional voltage sweeps, the CIPS device exhibits asymmetric bidirectional resistive switching, distinct from conventional memristors (Fig. 2c). During the positive sweep (0 V to 9 V to 0 V), the device gradually transitioned from HRS to LRS; whereas under the negative sweep (0 V to −9 V to 0 V), no direct LRS to HRS transition occurred. Instead, after switching to the negative voltage, the device exhibits the HRS state and gradually returns to the LRS state after a prolonged interval. The asymmetric RS behavior originates from field-driven Cu⁺ migration that dynamically forms p–n homojunctions, as supported by bidirectional I–V characteristics and reversible switching between p–n and n–p configurations (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Note 1 for details)43. The reverse-sweep curve (blue) in Fig. 2c further confirms the reconfigurability of the device and the presence of ohmic contacts44. Figure 2d shows the rectifying effect in both p–n and n–p states. Reversible transitions between these states were maintained over more than 1200 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 5), confirming the endurance and reliability. The retention measurement indicates that the LRS remains distinguishable from the HRS after at least 4 × 10⁴ s, retaining approximately 70% of its initial conductance (Supplementary Fig. 6). This validates the nonvolatile nature of the resistive states and the device’s suitability for neuromorphic applications where long-term information retention is essential40. To determine the voltage threshold for forming a homojunction, the device was tested under constant bias from 0.5 to 3.5 V (Fig. 2e). Conductance potentiation occurs only above ~2 V, indicating a threshold voltage associated with Cu⁺ migration. Supplementary Fig. 7 presents the threshold voltage statistics for a subset of devices, showing that the threshold varies across devices (ranging from approximately 1.5 to 2.5 V) depending on factors such as interface contact quality. This threshold likely corresponds to the activation barrier for ion motion, which is further supported by voltage-sweep measurements (Supplementary Fig. 8)45,46.

The synaptic behavior of the CIPS memristor was further evaluated by applying consecutive voltage pulses to emulate biological learning processes. The CIPS device exhibits typical synaptic behavior, achieving over 1024 linear conductance states and demonstrating repeatable programming and erasing capabilities (Supplementary Fig. 9)12,40. Within the device, the homojunction serves as a parasitic element that modulates the synaptic behavior of the memristor, introducing asymmetric ion migration and dynamic behaviors39,42. The built-in field of the CIPS homojunction introduces asymmetric energy barriers for ion migration, leading to asymmetric potentiation and depression (Fig. 2f). Under positive voltages (4–6 V) above the threshold, conductance continuously increases, with the potentiation rate scaling with the pulse amplitude. Conversely, a sub-threshold negative bias ( − 1 V) effectively depresses the conductance. Although this asymmetry limits linear conductance modulation, it enables reliable device initialization: sub-threshold reverse pulses can reset the conductance state without forming a reverse p–n junction (Supplementary Fig. 10). Interestingly, the homojunction also introduces additional dynamic characteristics. As shown in Fig. 2g, by applying pulse sequences (high level: 5 V, low levels: 0, 1, 2 V), the sub-threshold voltages still significantly modulate ion migration, and the non‑volatility of the device improves with increasing low‑level bias. Furthermore, when the pulse switches from high (5 V) to low (0 V or 1 V), a negative current spike appears, and this spike transitions to positive as the low-level voltage increases (Fig. 2h). This unconventional response deviates from classical memristor behavior, implying an additional capacitive contribution within the CIPS device. Such capacitive–ionic interplay is crucial in governing the device’s dynamic synaptic characteristics.

Realization of the complete Bienenstock–Cooper–Munro rule

The capacitive–ionic interplay in the CIPS memristor directly modulates its transient spike response, resulting in non-monotonic SRDP. As shown in Fig. 3a, the device exhibits conductance potentiation at higher frequencies (3 Hz) and depression at lower frequencies (1 Hz) under pulse sequences (5 V, 150 ms). Increasing the low-level voltage enhances potentiation at relatively high frequencies, while gradually weakening depression at low frequencies. Notably, depression under low-frequency pulses (Fig. 3a) is more pronounced than the spontaneous decay observed under constant bias (Fig. 2g). To compare the effects of spontaneous decay and capacitive effect, we compared the depression behavior under constant 1 V bias with that under 0.5 Hz pulsed stimulation (5 V, 150 ms), after initializing the device to the same LRS using 3 Hz pulses (Fig. 3b). The results indicate a conductance decay of only 4.9% under constant bias, versus 31.1% under low-frequency pulses, confirming that depression in CIPS is due to pulse-induction rather than spontaneous decay19,25,27. Since a constant 1 V bias corresponds to no input (0 Hz), its weaker depression compared to 0.5 Hz stimulation indicates its spontaneous EDE behavior. This frequency-dependent phenomenon establishes a non-monotonic relationship between spike rate and conductance, enabling spike-rate-coded weight modulation. As shown in Fig. 3c, the device exhibits symmetric conductance responses to positive pulses with different frequencies, demonstrating that spike-rate-coded weight modulation in CIPS devices is markedly superior to pulse-number-based schemes.

The CIPS memristor successfully emulates history-dependent plasticity, a key adaptive feature of the BCM rule25,31. To demonstrate this, we applied four pulse trains at different frequencies (1–10 Hz, 50 ms) to stimulate the CIPS memristor. The resulting conductance changes in Fig. 3d reveals distinct frequency-specific responses: potentiation at 10 Hz, depression at 3 Hz, further depression at 2 Hz, and restored potentiation upon re-applying 3 Hz and 10 Hz. The fact that identical 3 Hz stimuli induce diametrically opposite outcomes depending solely on prior stimulation history provides direct evidence for history-dependent plasticity. Furthermore, when the 2 Hz pulses were replaced with a constant 1 V bias, the device conductance remained stable, demonstrating the EDE behavior of the CIPS memristor (Supplementary Fig. 11, and the reproducibility across multiple devices in Supplementary Fig. 12). This feature is further supported in Fig. 3e, which shows that the rate of conductance change systematically decreases with increasing intensity of historical stimulation.

To implement the complete BCM rule, we further examined how historical spike activity modulates the frequency threshold between potentiation and depression. As shown in Fig. 3f, the SRDP curve shifts after historical stimulation of the same duration (see the testing method and data in Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14). Specifically, the sliding threshold frequency increases in proportion to the historical spike rate. The emergence of distinct depression and EDE at low frequencies confirms that all essential characteristics of the BCM learning rule are successfully captured in a single device. A key innovation of our approach is its mechanism for conductance depression: instead of relying on spontaneous decay, it exploits reverse voltage spikes from the inherent parasitic capacitance. This enables synaptic depression while preserving non-volatility critically. Junction capacitance and pulsed conductance depression

Conductance depression in the CIPS memristor, essential for realizing the BCM rule, is driven by reverse voltage spikes from its parasitic capacitance. Unlike conventional interfacial capacitance, which merely filters transient signals, the capacitance in CIPS directly modulates conductance47,48. We probed this unique response via pulsed measurements. Figure 4a and b compare the single-pulse responses in HRS (initial state) and LRS (after a 15 V sweep) under pulses ranging from 2 to 10 V, with a duration of 3000 ms. While the HRS shows no significant current relaxation, distinct discharge spikes emerge in the LRS after each pulse. Integration of these discharge peaks across voltages reveals a linear charge–voltage (Q–U) relationship, confirming the existence of a stable capacitive element in the CIPS device (Fig. 4c). From the relation C = Q/U, the capacitance is estimated to be ~12.1 pF, substantially larger than the value (0.21 fF) predicted by a parallel-plate model of equivalent dimensions (Supplementary Note 2). This notable discrepancy indicates that the capacitance in CIPS stems from a mechanism distinct from conventional interfacial capacitance.

To clarify the physical origin of this parasitic capacitance, we employed Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) to map the surface potential in HRS and LRS. Given the limitations of the original bottom-electrode configuration, a device with an Au/Gr/CIPS/Gr/Au structure was fabricated for KPFM characterization (Supplementary Fig. 15). Initially, the uniform surface potential distribution indicates no significant Cu⁺ gradients (Fig. 4d). After a 15 V bias sweep, a ~ 120 mV potential drop emerges along the channel, signifying Cu⁺ ions migrate toward the right electrode (Fig. 4e). As this measurement was performed at zero bias, this potential drop reflects the spatial distribution of migrated Cu⁺ ions rather than the distribution of free carriers under operating conditions. This ion distribution is consistent with the EDS elemental mapping shown in Supplementary Fig. 3 and aligns with previous reports on CIPS-based devices38,39,42. The contact potential difference (VCPD) distributions recorded after 30 min relaxation and subsequent −15 V sweep exhibit reconfigurability and nonvolatility (Supplementary Fig. 16), consistent with the RS behavior observed in electrical measurements. The resulting Cu⁺ gradient establishes a built-in electric field and, based on prior studies is expected to dope the CIPS into n-type and p-type regions, forming a reconfigurable p–n homojunction (Supplementary Fig. 17). Under a forward bias, electrons injected from the n-type accumulate as minority carriers of the junction (Fig. 4f). This charge accumulation creates a diffusion capacitance that substantially exceeds conventional interfacial capacitance49. Upon pulse termination, the release of these stored charges generates a transient reverse voltage spike, promoting ion diffusion. Since this junction capacitance is embedded within the channel, its charging–discharging dynamics induce a spike-rate-dependent depressive effect. As a result, the actual memristor voltage (Vm) becomes the input voltage (Vin) minus the capacitive voltage (Vc), producing a voltage redistribution effect (Supplementary Fig. 18).

Simulations in Fig. 4g show that high-voltage pulses lead to charge screening, whereas low-voltage levels induce a transient reverse bias (simulation parameters provided in Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 22). Furthermore, the resulting negative voltage transients exhibit pronounced frequency dependence: the cumulative reverse bias increases linearly at low-frequencies (the pulse interval > the RC time constant) but diminishes at high-frequencies (the pulse interval <the RC time constant). Figure 4h summarizes the frequency-dependent suppression effect due to the junction parasitic capacitance, using normalized magnetic flux ( ∫ Vdt), and reveals a distinct low-frequency depression interval. By integrating the second-order ion dynamics with the junction capacitance model, we successfully reproduced the full BCM rule, including both the EDE and history-dependent plasticity (Fig. 4i). The model’s response to varying spike-rate (Supplementary Fig. 23) demonstrates that the non-monotonic frequency response stems from the dynamic equilibrium between depolarizing and hyperpolarizing voltage phases—a waveform modulation that closely mimics neuronal action potentials55.

The waveform modulation effect induced by the junction parasitic capacitance is electrically equivalent to a series RC circuit, as analytically derived in Supplementary Fig. 24. To experimentally validate this concept, an equivalent circuit, in which a MoS₂ memristor provides RS behavior and an external RC circuit emulates the junction capacitance, is constructed (see Supplementary Fig. 25). As recorded by a two-channel oscilloscope, the external RC branch produces the same voltage division effect as the intrinsic junction capacitance under pulsed inputs (Supplementary Fig. 26a–e). Importantly, statistical analysis of the positive and negative flux ( ∫ Vdt) across the memristor reproduced the characteristic EDE behavior (Supplementary Fig. 26f), confirming the generality of the strategy.

Homeostatic spatiotemporal reservoir computing

The stability of synaptic weights is crucial to the reliability of neuromorphic computing systems. Figure 5a schematically illustrates a spiking neural network comprising a reservoir and a readout layer, where each synapse represents a connection between two neurons34,35. Our BCM-rule-based single-memristor strategy provides a natural hardware mapping of neural connections, eliminating the need for additional gates or encoding schemes. To evaluate the practical applicability of this mechanism in large-scale neuromorphic systems, we constructed an array-based resistive computing unit (Supplementary Fig. 27), where input signals are encoded as voltages and synaptic weights are mapped to device conductance, and device behavior is modeled using coupled ionic-capacitive dynamics. The detailed modeling and simulation methodology are provided in the Methods section. We demonstrated two operation modes of the BCM-rule device: a static mode for non-volatile memory arrays used in the readout layer and a dynamic mode for constructing physical reservoir nodes. In both modes, the synaptic weights are modulated through rate-coded pulse stimulation.

To validate the effectiveness of the BCM-based weight update and sliding-threshold mechanisms in maintaining network stability, we constructed a direction-selective classifier with 32 input and 3 output neurons (Supplementary Fig. 28a). The input consists of 4×4-pixel images containing horizontal, vertical, and diagonal patterns, whose pixel intensities are encoded pulse frequencies ranging from 0 to 15 Hz. Training follows a winner-take-all scheme, where input pulses are synchronously applied, but only the neuron corresponding to the correct label remains connected at the output (Supplementary Fig. 29a). During training, synaptic connections to the correct output are selectively potentiated until full weight convergence is achieved. In the inference phase, all output neurons are connected, and the neuron with the highest output voltage is selected as the result (Supplementary Fig. 28b). The weight update dynamics of the CIPS device, illustrated in Fig. 5b, follow a positive feedback rule where high-frequency stimuli induce potentiation and low-frequency stimuli induce depression, with an EDE in the low-frequency regime. Meanwhile, a built-in state variable introduces a sliding threshold that provides negative feedback for homeostatic regulation. Figure 5c shows the weight evolution under a typical voltage pulse sequence, demonstrating both Hebbian positive feedback and homeostatic negative feedback, while EDE contributes to weight stability during idle periods. To assess the robustness of this homeostatic mechanism, we introduced 10% random fluctuation in the input pulse frequency during training. Each pixel is represented by two input neurons connected to all 32 input nodes. During training, synapses associated with the correct class undergo high-frequency stimulation, resulting in sustained potentiation and a gradual improvement in classification accuracy. After 100 training iterations, the classifier reaches 98% accuracy (Supplementary Fig. 29b). In contrast, when the sliding threshold or EDE terms are disabled, the system exhibits unavoidable weight saturation or catastrophic forgetting (Supplementary Fig. 30).

To further assess the potential of this hardware strategy for dynamic information processing, we simulated a reservoir computing system for analyzing spatiotemporal video data. In this framework, individual video frames encode spatial information, with high spatial frequencies corresponding to edges and textures and low frequencies representing uniform regions. The temporal dimension, on the other hand, captures motion-induced frequency variations. The inherent memory of RC systems makes them well-suited for tasks that require spatiotemporal processing. Figure 5d shows three frames of a video sequence containing vertical bars and a stationary car, recorded by a moving camera. The different motion speeds of the bars and car produce distinct temporal frequencies, which are encoded as pulse-rate sequences (Fig. 5e) and fed into the reservoir. The simulated memory computation process (Fig. 5f) shows that, over time, the negative feedback in the weight update rule suppresses irrelevant pixel fluctuations, while positive feedback filters out transient noise (moving bars) and preserves key static features (the car). Spatial features are extracted via a simulated Fourier transform, with low-frequency and noisy components gradually filtered out, thereby retaining high-frequency edge information essential for object recognition (Supplementary Fig. 31). To evaluate pattern recognition performance, the RC system is applied to an extended MNIST dataset with stripe-like noise (Supplementary Fig. 32)56. As shown in Fig. 5g, handwritten digits are converted into noisy video streams and processed in both time and frequency domains. The reservoir enhances salient features across both domains while maintaining stable synaptic weights over 1.2 million update iterations. On the full test set, classification accuracy improves from 55.8% for the raw images to 92.4% in the temporal domain and 95.3% in the frequency domain, demonstrating that the dynamic characteristics of the BCM rule significantly enhance the accuracy and robustness of spatiotemporal information processing.

Discussion

In this work, we implement the complete BCM rule in a single two-terminal CIPS memristor via natural spike-rate encoding, leveraging its two inherent features: second-order ionic dynamics and junction parasitic capacitance. The memristive behavior follows second-order ionic dynamics, simulating sliding-threshold behavior, while the junction parasitic capacitance generates reverse-voltage spikes during pulse intervals, resulting in a spike-rate-dependent depression effect. By coupling the pulse-induced potentiation and depression in the CIPS memristor, a non-monotonic EDE is achieved. Furthermore, a general dynamic framework is established in which the coupling of junction capacitance and second-order ionic dynamics fully reproduces the BCM rule. The mechanism is experimentally validated by emulating the junctional voltage-division effect with a series RC circuit, successfully reproducing the EDE behavior in conventional memristors. While the intrinsic RC time constant of the CIPS device limits its operating frequency to the order of 0–25 Hz, the equivalent-circuit strategy enables much higher-frequency operation (kHz range) on conventional memristor platforms. Owing to its strong biological compatibility with existing synaptic devices, this hardware-oriented strategy can be seamlessly integrated into established neuromorphic architectures. It thus provides an intrinsic solution to the long-standing stability–plasticity dilemma and opens a path toward adaptive, brain-inspired computing systems.

Methods

Device fabrication

CIPS devices for electrical measurements: The electrodes were fabricated by depositing 5 nm/50 nm Cr/Au via magnetron sputtering on a Si substrate with a 300 nm SiO2 layer (purchased from Harbin Tebo Technology). Standard photolithography and argon-ion etching were used to pattern the electrodes, with a 5 μm channel width between them. CIPS flakes were mechanically exfoliated from bulk crystals (purchased from SUNANO GROUP) and transferred onto the electrodes using a dry transfer technique. To prevent ambient degradation and ensure long-term stability, the devices were immediately encapsulated after fabrication with a thin PMMA or a hBN capping layer. Encapsulated devices have been shown to retain their functionality for over one year under ambient conditions.

CIPS devices for KPFM and SEM/EDS characterization: The CIPS flakes were mechanically exfoliated from bulk crystals (SUNANO GROUP) and dry-transferred onto a Si substrate with a 300 nm SiO2 layer (Harbin Tebo Technology). To prevent damage to the CIPS surface during electrode deposition, graphene flakes exfoliated from bulk graphite were transferred onto the CIPS to form 6 μm-wide channel electrodes, which were then connected to Au electrodes.

Device characterization

All the DC electrical measurements were performed at room temperature on a Keithley 4200 semiconductor parameter analyzer. The pulse measurements were carried out using an arbitrary function generator (Tektronix AFG 31000) to generate input pulses. The output current was detected using a low-noise current preamplifier (Stanford Research Systems SR570) and an oscilloscope (PicoScope 4000). The thickness of the CIPS flakes was measured using atomic force microscopy (AFM, Bruker Dimension Edge). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS Sigma 360, Germany) was used to obtain SEM images and elemental analysis by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, USA) was employed to analyze the elemental composition of the bulk CIPS crystals.

Simulation of CuInP2S6 memristor

The simulation and modeling of the CIPS memristor are performed by numerically solving the device dynamics equations with a time step of Δt = 0.001 s. For a given input voltage sequence, a stepwise method is applied at each iteration: first, the memristor resistance is updated according to the dynamical equations using an explicit Euler method; then, based on the updated resistance, the algebraic constraints of the circuit (capacitive/resistive voltage division) are solved to obtain the new voltage and current values, which are subsequently used for the next time step. For the simulation of CIPS device arrays, the state variables Wi of each device are stored in a matrix of the same dimensions as the array (the circuit configuration and input/output scheme are described in Supplementary Fig. 27). By simulating the voltage sequences applied to the array inputs and the grounding conditions at the outputs, the conductance change of each cell under the applied voltage is computed sequentially, and the output current is read accordingly. In the CIPS-based reservoir computing framework, two operating modes of the device are utilized. In static mode, the weights obtained from the backpropagation algorithm are mapped to conductance values using the device's measured LTP/LTD curves. In the dynamic mode, the output current evolution under continuous voltage inputs is computed by solving the device dynamics equations.
