IHC p53 Performance in NordiQC: Recalibration of IHC Assays is Needed
Authors: Kristi Bøgh Anderson, Søren Nielsen, Rasmus Røge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The TP53 gene is the most frequently mutated gene in human cancers, with alterations occurring in ∼50% of all malignancies. When properly optimized, p53 immunohistochemistry (IHC) can serve as a reliable surrogate for detecting TP53 mutations. However, many laboratories struggle to identify p53 IHC reaction patterns beyond overexpression, particularly the absence, cytoplasmic, and wild-type patterns. As the clinical relevance of these additional patterns became recognized, NordiQC updated its assessment in 2021 to include wild-type and absence patterns, thereby increasing the complexity of p53 IHC and revealing widespread difficulties across laboratories. In this study, we analyze data from 6 consecutive NordiQC external quality assessment rounds from 2007 to 2024, covering 1,796 submitted p53 IHC assays. Our findings show that while most laboratories can reliably detect high antigen expression of overexpression, many protocols fail to adequately demonstrate low-level p53 expression, limiting the validity of the IHC assay. Ready-To-Use products, particularly when used according to manufacturer recommendations, performed suboptimally. These kits are calibrated exclusively to detect overexpression, leading to false-negative or too-weak results for other mutation-associated patterns. Our data represents the most comprehensive evaluation of p53 IHC assay performance to date. The findings underscore an urgent need to recalibrate p53 IHC protocols, with a special focus on improving analytical sensitivity and ensuring consistent use of both internal and external controls. Aligning IHC assays calibrated to identify the full spectrum of TP53 mutations, including those that result in absent and wild-type patterns, can significantly enhance the diagnostic accuracy of p53 IHC.

The p53 protein, encoded by the TP53 gene, plays a central role in regulating cell growth and division. When DNA damage is detected, p53 either pauses the cell cycle to allow for repair or induces apoptosis when repair is not possible, preventing potential tumor formation.1 The TP53 gene is the most frequently mutated gene in cancers, with mutations occurring in ∼50% of cases.1–5

In normal, nonmutated cells, p53 levels are tightly regulated by rapid MDM2-mediated degradation, resulting in low protein levels with limited immunohistochemical detectability. The characteristic p53 reaction pattern seen in normal cells is a wild-type pattern, showing varying nuclear reaction intensities across cells, ranging from negative to strongly positive and heterogeneous occurring.6,7

In contrast, the most common TP53 mutations, missense mutations, often result in the production of a structurally altered, nonfunctional p53 protein that escapes degradation. This leads to abnormal intracellular accumulation of unfunctional p53 and manifests as a strong, diffuse nuclear reaction on IHC, known as the overexpression reaction pattern.5–8

However, not all TP53 gene mutations lead to overexpression. Instead, various p53 reaction patterns have been observed.5

For instance, loss-of-function mutations often result in the production of shortened mRNAs that degrade rapidly. Consequently, no detectable p53 protein is produced, resulting in a lack of IHC reactivity. Normal wild-type reaction in background cells, such as stromal cells, lymphocytes, and endothelial cells, serves as an important intrinsic control in this context.5–7

Mutations affecting the nuclear localization domain or nuclear export signal can lead to an abnormal cytoplasmic reaction pattern,5,7 where p53 accumulates in the cytoplasm instead of the nucleus.6,7 This is a rare pattern observed in about 3% of gynecologic carcinomas.9,10 However, intense nuclear reaction with a faint cytoplasmic blush should be interpreted as nuclear overexpression rather than true cytoplasmic reaction.7

Historically, p53 IHC has been interpreted using a binary approach, categorizing expression as simply “positive” or “negative.” However, this oversimplification fails to reflect the biological diversity of TP53 mutations. Tumors with TP53 mutations that cause complete loss of p53 protein expression were previously misclassified as “negative” and grouped with truly unmutated cases, despite having very different underlying genetics and clinical behavior. Accurate recognition of specific p53 IHC reaction patterns is therefore essential to avoid such misclassification.5 IHC can be a valuable asset and provides a faster, more cost-effective alternative to molecular testing for TP53 mutations; however, as described above, it must be validated to ensure that the p53 expression patterns accurately reflect different TP53 mutations.

The Nordic Immunohistochemical Quality Control (NordiQC) program is an independent, academic external quality assessment (EQA) scheme for diagnostic IHC, including >700 laboratories across 60+ countries. NordiQC distributes unstained tissue slides to participating laboratories, which stain them using their routine protocols. Slides are centrally reviewed against reference standards by an expert panel, and detailed assessment reports are published, highlighting performance metrics, common pitfalls, and best‑practice recommendations. NordiQC has assessed p53 6 times and has reported a significant decrease in the pass rate for p53 assays since the assessment was adjusted to include the detection of reaction patterns beyond overexpression, specifically wild-type and absence patterns.11

This paper provides an overview of NordiQC assessment results for p53. We demonstrate that all Ready-To-Use (RTU) assays designed to detect p53 overexpression fail to meet the necessary standards for identifying other reaction patterns when used according to the manufacturers’ recommendations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

In early NordiQC p53 runs (20, 2007; 38, 2013), the Tissue microarray (TMA) and the purpose of the assessment runs primarily focused on demonstrating p53 overexpression and validating appropriate control tissues, reflecting the limited use of p53 IHC at the time. In the last 4 NordiQC runs (63, 2021; 65, 2022; 67, 2023; 70, 2024), the purpose of the assessments was expanded to comprise both p53 overexpression, absence of p53, and wild-type patterns, and the TMAs were constructed to meet these updated requirements (Table 1).

Sections from different levels of the TMAs were analyzed by a validated IHC assay and evaluated by the NordiQC reference laboratory to ensure consistent p53 expression throughout the blocks before distributing slide sets to participants.

All participants submitted their assay protocols via the NordiQC website and received slide sets prepared from the TMAs. After performing the assay according to their submitted protocol, they returned the slides to NordiQC for evaluation.

An expert panel of pathologists and technicians conducted an anonymized consensus assessment of all slides returned. Each slide was rated as “optimal,” “good,” “borderline,” or “poor” based on technical quality and alignment with the expected reaction pattern and criteria outlined in Table 1. An “optimal” rating required a reaction pattern that matched the reference laboratory’s results. “Optimal” and “good” ratings were considered sufficient, while “borderline” and “poor” ratings were deemed inadequate for diagnostic use. A detailed overview of the criteria for each tissue core is available in the assessment reports on the NordiQC website (www.nordiqc.org).

For statistical analysis, IHC assays were stratified by antibody format and protocol application into concentrated antibodies, RTU systems with modified protocols, and RTU systems applied in compliance with vendor-recommended protocol settings (VRPS). Differences in pass rates were evaluated using a χ2 test.

RESULTS

From 2007 to 2024, 6 NordiQC assessments on p53 IHC were conducted. The number of participating laboratories increased significantly, from 93 in 2007 to 397 in 2024. In total, 1,796 p53 IHC assays were evaluated during the 6 assessments. Over the years, the percentage of laboratories achieving sufficient (optimal or good) results varied, with notable fluctuations: 68% in 2007, 79% in 2013, a drop to 46% in 2021, and an improvement to 65% in 2023 and 2024 (Fig. 1). Despite the slight improvement in the pass rate in recent years, p53 IHC remains challenging, illustrated by the fact that 35% of the participating laboratories still use IHC assays that are not at an acceptable level for diagnostic use.

Pass rate (%) of laboratories of 6 NordiQC assessments for p53.

Over the years, the monoclonal antibody clones DO-7 and BP53-11 have consistently achieved optimal results. The widely used DO-7 clone demonstrated optimal results across all major IHC systems: Dako/Agilent, Ventana/Roche, and Leica Biosystems. For all clones, effective Heat-Induced Epitope Retrieval (HIER), precise calibration of the primary antibody titer, and the use of a 3-layer detection system were crucial for achieving optimal performance.

Since 2021, RTU antibodies have been the most popular choice among participating laboratories. In the 2024 assessment, 77% of laboratories chose an RTU product, compared with 6% in 2007. However, a performance analysis between RTU and concentrated antibodies revealed that the concentrated forms generally achieved higher pass rates. In 2024, IHC assays based on concentrated antibodies had a 71% pass rate (optimal or good), compared with 63% for RTUs. Differences in pass rates between antibody formats were statistically significant (χ² test = 24.5, df = 2, P < 0.001), largely driven by poor performance of RTU systems when used in accordance with vendor-recommended protocol settings (VRPS). In the 2024 assessment, the overall pass rate for RTU systems from Dako/Agilent, Ventana/Roche, and Leica Biosystems using VRPS was as low as 30% (Table 2).

Since 2021, NordiQC assessments have consistently shown that the most common cause of an insufficient result is a too weak or false-negative staining reaction. All laboratories demonstrated high-level antigen expression in p53-overexpressing cases. However, detecting p53 in low-level antigen-expressing cells, such as stromal cells, lymphocytes, and endothelial cells, as well as in germinal center and mantle zone B-lymphocytes in the tonsil, proved more challenging. Accurate identification of the wild-type p53 pattern in neoplastic cells without a TP53 mutation as well as the absence pattern in TP53-mutated neoplastic cells, required a carefully calibrated protocol (Fig. 2). For example, in 2024, only 15% of all insufficient results were attributable to causes other than false negative or weak staining reactions, such as technical issues related to poor signal-to-noise ratio or excessive background staining, with true false positive reactions accounting for 5% of all insufficient results.

The primary factors contributing to insufficient reaction were identified as the use of less sensitive detection systems, ineffective HIER, and suboptimal primary antibody concentration. These issues were addressed in individual feedback to laboratories.

DISCUSSION

Over the years, our understanding of the correlations between molecular changes in TP53 and p53 IHC reaction patterns has increased, presenting new challenges for p53 IHC. The latest NordiQC assessments indicate that while all participating laboratories can detect p53 overexpression, p53 assays often fail to identify other reaction patterns. Following a shift in clinical expectations for p53 testing, the NordiQC assessment criteria were updated in 2021 to include wild-type and absence patterns in addition to overexpression, leading to a significant drop in pass rates.

One of the main issues is the poor performance of RTU antibodies when VRPS are applied. This seems to be related to the scenario that virtually all RTU products on the market are calibrated to show only the overexpression pattern, as they were developed before other reaction patterns were known. While these products are effective at detecting overexpression, they are not designed to meet today’s diagnostic expectations, creating an urgent need for recalibration. Our concern is further amplified by the fact that all the RTU antibodies in question are validated under the In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (IVDR) using VRPS. This means that if IVDR regulations were strictly followed, the performance of p53 IHC would decline noticeably. Clinically, this could lead to significant underdiagnosis or misclassification of TP53-mutant tumors, particularly those that display null or cytoplasmic staining patterns. As a result, patients may be excluded from receiving appropriate therapies, thereby directly affecting treatment decisions and outcomes.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest study comparing p53 IHC quality across multiple laboratories. No previous study has quality-assured p53 IHC assays at this level, covering such a wide range of technical conditions. By including diverse methodologies and laboratory settings, our study provides a comprehensive evaluation of assay performance and highlights critical factors influencing p53 IHC assay quality and diagnostic reliability.

While external quality assurance provides only a snapshot of individual laboratory performance, with expected fluctuations, evaluating many laboratories simultaneously allows us to identify systemic issues. When many participating laboratories encounter the same challenges, it reflects the global state of p53 IHC rather than isolated laboratory errors. Not all laboratories switch to new antibodies or make the same technical mistakes at the same time. The large dataset of protocols and results analyzed in this study confirms a systemic problem first identified in 2021. Fortunately, laboratories that have modified their protocols are showing improvements, but recalibration and validation of most commercially available RTU IHC systems remain necessary to fully resolve the ongoing challenges with p53 IHC.

To support laboratories in this process, NordiQC has published on its website the protocols that, in the assessments, have demonstrated the best overall performance. These protocols are available at www.nordiqc.org on the NordiQC “Recommended protocol” page and are intended to provide laboratories with a robust starting point for local validation and optimization of p53 IHC assays. In addition, all participants who do not pass the p53 assessment receive a specific and tailored recommendation from NordiQC on how to optimize their protocol. The recommendation is based on the NordiQC data analysis and is specifically tailored to the individual protocol submitted and applied by the participant for p53.

However, the findings of this study must be interpreted within its limitations. Our TMAs included a limited selection of tumor and normal tissue samples. Broader studies representing a wide variety of pan-cancer tissues would be beneficial.

Comparing our results with those of other external quality assessment programs, we observe similar findings. The College of American Pathologists survey of p53 from 2021 to 2022 showed that only 5 out of 10 TMA cores achieved consensus among ≥80% of 117 participating laboratories. The main cause of incorrect result was weak reaction, leading to the misinterpretation of the overexpression pattern as the wild-type pattern and the absence pattern.5 The results from the Canadian Pathology Quality Assurance run 206 in November 2023, testing p53 in endometrial cancer, showed that approximately one-third of laboratories could not adequately stain for absence pattern, as the reaction was too weak to show the presence of internal control in form of wild-type pattern in the background.12 Despite this, all laboratories passed proficiency testing due to their internal evaluation criteria.

A key element in verifying a successful p53 IHC assay is its intrinsic tissue quality control, which allows assessment of the assay’s analytical sensitivity. This is based on the well-established observation that stromal cells, endothelial cells, and lymphocytes, which are commonly present in most tissue samples, consistently exhibit a wild-type p53 reaction pattern. The presence of this wild-type pattern in the non-neoplastic tissue surrounding the carcinoma provides a reliable reference indicating that the reaction is adequate, has relevant analytical sensitivity, and is crucial for identifying tumors with TP53 mutations causing p53 loss.

In addition to recognizing the value of internal controls, our results emphasize the importance of selecting appropriate external controls, preferably on-slide controls. As the primary issue is false-negative results and decreased analytical sensitivity, using only carcinoma tissue with an overexpression pattern would not provide relevant information on assay sensitivity and reproducibility. Therefore, low-level p53-expressing external tissue controls, like the tonsil and appendix/colon, are recommended.

In conclusion, optimizing p53 IHC assays to correspond with different TP53 mutations is key to improving diagnostic accuracy. NordiQC results reveal significant challenges are seen globally and systematically compromising the diagnostic performance of p53 IHC assays. There is an urgent need to recalibrate and validate IHC p53 assays, especially concerning the widely used commercial RTU systems.
