Single image deraining via wide rectangular regional blocks and dual attention complementary enhancement network
Authors: Yue Shen, Yuduo Zhang, Wentao Li, Changjie Qin, Yongdong Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Rain is a common weather phenomenon, and the challenge of removing rain streaks from a single image is crucial due to its detrimental impact on image quality and the extraction of valuable background information. Existing methods commonly rely on specific assumptions regarding rain models, which restricts their ability to accommodate a wide range of real-world scenarios. To overcome this limitation, these methods often require complex optimization techniques or stepwise refinement strategies. In this paper, we propose a novel wide rectangular regional block and dual attention complementary enhancement deraining kernel prediction subnet to meet the challenge. The network called WRRDANet consists of a kernel prediction subnet and pixel-wise dilation filtering. In the kernel prediction subnet, we capture more specific contextual background information and complex pixel-wise kernels. Afterward, the learned pixel-wise multi-scale kernels from the kernel prediction subnet are used to perform dilation filtering on the original rainy image, effectively restoring richer background details by expanding the scope of deraining to a larger extent. We conducted a comprehensive evaluation using synthetic and real rainfall datasets to demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach. The results, both qualitatively and quantitatively, indicate that our approach outperforms other popular rain removal methods.

Introduction

Rain streaks cause significant degradation in the quality of rainy images or video content, posing challenges for various outdoor computer vision systems, including target detection and recognition1, drone piloting2, surveillance systems3, and scene analysis4. Depending on the kind of input data, the existing algorithms may be divided into two categories: single image-based5–7 and video-based8–11 rain removal methods. Since a video is composed of frames of images, developing specialized image-deraining solutions is essential and highly meaningful to solve this problem. In real life, rain can be categorized into three types: rain streaks, raindrops, and rain mist12. Raindrops and rain mist usually appear as discrete points or blurry areas, with relatively simple textures and shapes. However, rain streaks exhibit complex textures and shapes, including variations such as heavy rain, light rain, and dense rain, which increases the difficulty of accurately identifying and separating them. Furthermore, rain streaks are typically continuous. Due to the complex interaction between rain streaks and the image content, as well as the variability and uncertainty associated with them, removing rain streaks from an image is more challenging compared to removing raindrops and fog. Therefore, we present a method for removing rain streaks from a single image in this paper.

Single-image deraining methods aim to separate raindrops or rain streaks from the background scene. Rain layer and background layer modeling were key components of early model-driven methods for single-image rain removal. In these methods, such as sparse coding models7, low-rank regularization models5,6, and Gaussian mixture models13, manual priors were incorporated into the construction of an optimization objective function. While these techniques produced observable deraining effects, their high temporal complexity and sluggish processing times were drawbacks. Significant progress has been made in single-image rain removal in recent years due to breakthroughs in deep-learning techniques. Deep learning-based approaches have revolutionized the field by leveraging the power of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and other advanced architectures. The shortcomings of previous model-driven techniques have been overcome by these methods, which have shown greater performance in limiting quality and computing economy.Figure 1The structure of WRRDANet. Rain models can aid algorithms in modeling and analyzing the shape, distribution, and orientation of rain streaks. Understanding the rain model enables algorithms to predict the spatial distribution of rain streaks in an image and adjust the parameters and strategies of rain removal algorithms accordingly. A common model assumption is to decompose the input rain image R into a linear combination of a rain-free background image B and a rain streak layer S, represented by Eq. By removing the rain streak layer S from the rain image R, a rain-free background scene B can be obtained. Therefore, existing deep learning-based deraining methods frequently rely heavily on rain models or make assumptions about rainfall generation to learn the features of the rain layer. And the methods based on this assumption often assume that rain streaks exhibit similar characteristics, such as shape and falling direction. Rain streaks in real-life scenarios possess diverse appearances and uncertain physical features beyond the scope of the model assumption. This results in numerous iterative optimization stages and further refinement procedures to restore high-quality rain-free background images. So the details overlooked by these techniques can be summarized as follows: (1) Many algorithms make assumptions about rain models. However, in real-world scenarios, the variability exhibited by rain streaks cannot be fully captured by such assumptions. (2) Existing methods either employ recurrent progressive deraining frameworks14 or rely on intricate iterative optimization techniques15 to obtain rain-free background information, leading to high computational expenses. Specifically, the current popular progressive rain removal models can achieve rain removal tasks through either staged processing or a multi-stage architecture. However, in the earlier stages, complete removal of rain streak details may not be achieved, leading to residual rain streaks in subsequent stages. Then, the smoothing operations introduced in each stage may result in excessive image smoothing, causing a loss of some details and texture features, making the final rain removal results less clear and natural. Additionally, progressive rain removal models typically require multiple processing stages, each with complex network structures and computational operations, necessitating more computational resources and time to complete the rain removal task.

To address these limitations, we propose a single-stage model-free method based on image filtering techniques for removing rain streaks that can handle many scenarios such as heavy rain, light rain, dense rain, and real rain scenes. This method employs a kernel prediction subnet to estimate pixel kernels that adaptively capture rain streak features to break the limitations of the rain model assumptions. It utilizes them for dilated filtering and image fusion operations to remove rain streaks from the image. Compared to the progressive draining models, it avoids the necessity for successive refining processes and iterative optimization, thereby achieving effective rain removal performance. Related work

Single image-based rain removal method

Traditional model-driven methods in rain removal utilize different priors to eliminate rain streaks from the rainy image. These priors are typically derived from image decomposition, sparse coding, and Gaussian mixture models. For instance, Kang et al.16 and Luo et al.7 employed sparse coding and dictionary learning to separate the rain layer from the background layer. Li et al.13 applied a Gaussian mixture model-based patch before handling rain streaks of various orientations and scales in the original rain image. Zhu et al.17 utilized a joint optimization approach incorporating three priors to extract the background layers from the rain map. Wang et al.18 adopted a step-by-step layered approach combining image decomposition and dictionary learning to remove rain streaks. Gu et al.19 extracted background information by jointly applying convolutional analysis and a synthetic sparse representation model after removing rain streaks. When dealing with real-world rain streaks that are varied and not fixed, however, these conventional model-driven methods may not produce satisfying results since they are better suited for scenarios where the rain streaks have identical sizes and orientations. While these methods have the advantage of effectively handling raindrop noise, they often suffer from slow running times and high computational complexity.

Based on the number of paired images and training approaches, current deep learning-based single-image rain removal techniques can be categorized as supervised learning, semi-supervised learning, and unsupervised learning methods. Supervised learning utilizes accurate label information to guide the learning process and can directly use evaluation metrics specific to supervised learning to assess model performance. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are a popular tool in supervised learning techniques for rain removal because of their exceptional performance in this domain. These methods utilize substantial datasets of rainy images for training and prediction and some noteworthy approaches have made remarkable technological breakthroughs. Fu et al.20 developed a CNN-based architecture that incorporates prior knowledge during the training process to improve rain removal effectiveness. Yang et al.21 introduced a recurrent network for detecting and eliminating rain streaks iteratively, incrementally improving the results. To improve rain removal while maintaining valuable information, Li et al.22 integrated deep convolutional and recurrent neural networks with squeeze-and-excitation blocks and dilated convolutions. Li et al.23 proposed a non-locally enhanced end-to-end neural network architecture that utilized a pooled indexed embedded encoder–decoder framework to effectively learn feature representations for more accurate modeling of rain streaks. Zhang et al.24 developed a multistream dense network for joint rain density estimation and removal, efficiently capturing diverse scales and shapes of rain streaks. Hu et al.25 designed a deeply guided attention mechanism to learn deep attention features and obtain the physical properties of rain streaks for strengthening the end-to-end draining effect. To facilitate gradual draining, Ren et al.26 coupled ResNet with a recurrent layer to learn dependencies between background characteristics and rain patterns at each stage. Yang et al.27 employed hierarchical enhancement and recurrent units to extract and retain effective features for rain removal. Lian et al.28 designed two subnets to estimate high as well as low-frequency components of the rainy image. In the optimization process, two components were combined to obtain a rain-free output. Wang et al.29 performed iterative rain removal using a convolutional dictionary mechanism and proximal gradient descent technique. Zamir et al.14 created a multi-stage network structure with connections between stages to balance contextual information and spatial details for collaborative rain removal. Fu et al.30 devised a framework incorporating two graph-convolutional networks to explore pixel relationships in feature maps, leveraging multi-scale dilated convolutions and a symmetric skip-connected network for rain-free image generation. These deep learning-based methods have demonstrated their effectiveness in rain removal tasks, leveraging the power of CNNs and incorporating various architectural enhancements and techniques. Ye et al.31 utilized the disentangle–entangle strategy to transform between real rain images and synthetic rain images, thereby establishing a transformation relationship through iterative rain generation and rain removal. The method also introduced MSE and adversarial losses to subtract the disentangled background from the rain images, ensuring background consistency. Chen et al.32 proposed a multi-stream residual architecture based on an encoder–decoder framework. They utilized group normalization33 and SE34 to form feature fusion residual blocks, enabling the extraction of more contextual information. They also employed multi-scale convolution kernels to extract concentrated features at different shapes, densities, and scales of rain streaks. Park et al.35 invented multi-connection technology and non-local regional division method to separate the rain layer from the original image level by level. Similar to the image de-raining task, Cui et al.36 proposed the enhanced context aggregation network (ECANet) based on an encoder–decoder architecture for image dehazing. ECANet introduces three feature aggregation blocks composed of residual blocks and smoothed dilated residual blocks to learn contextual features across different scales. Subsequently, the channel attention mechanism is employed to fuse the features, and an enhancement module refines the decoding features to obtain a vivid and colorful background image. Du et al.37 decomposed the rainy images into high-frequency and low-frequency layers. In the first stage, they utilized a multi-level attention mechanism to generate a rain-streak attention map, which determined the location information of rain streaks. This attention map guided the Smooth Dilation Network (SDN) to remove rain streaks. In the second stage, the rain-streak removed image was input into a network with a similar structure as SDN to remove haze and obtain a clear background image. In general, these current methods share some commonalities. Some of them are based on assumptions about the rainfall model (e.g., Wang et al.29, Li et al.22, Yang et al.27), while others employ multi-stage or iterative approaches to rain removal by processing the results of previous stages (e.g., Zamir et al.14; Park et al.35; Liu et al.15). However, these methods may have certain impacts on the performance and efficiency of rain removal. Therefore, we propose a single-stage model-free rain removal method to tackle this issue in this paper.

Semi-supervised learning and unsupervised learning utilize partially or completely unlabeled data during the training process. Wei et al.38 parameterized rain patterns for rain removal with the residual information between the input rain map and the expected rainless image produced by the network. However, the rain removal effect attained by this method was not particularly excellent. Huang et al.39 proposed a memory-based encoder–decoder network for exploring rain streak characteristics based on semi-supervised learning, targeting both synthetic and real datasets. They invented a self-supervised storage module to adaptively simulate various manifestations of rain streak degradation. Yue et al.40 explored a semi-supervised approach that constructs a dynamic rain generator to better capture the intrinsic features of rain patterns and improve deraining performance. Jin et al.41 proposed an unsupervised rain removal method based on unpaired rainy and rain-free images. The method utilizes a rain-guided module to distinguish real rain images from fake rain images, and the background-guided module ensures the consistency of the output background through hierarchical Gaussian blur gradient error. Guo et al.42 implemented an unsupervised rain removal method using an attention mechanism and a CycleGAN with two constraint branches. They addressed the information asymmetry between rainy and rain-free images by progressively extracting rainwater, effectively removing rain from the images.

Due to the highly irregular shape and direction of real rain streaks, it is challenging to accurately establish the mapping relationship between rain images and background images. This can lead to instability and class imbalance issues during the training process of semi-supervised and unsupervised models. Attention mechanism

The attention mechanism43–46 has become renowned in the field of deep learning and has been extensively implemented to achieve impressive results. Advanced visual missions including semantic segmentation47, image classification48, and structured information extraction49 have all shown success with it. Moreover, the application of the attention mechanism in low-level visual tasks like image rain removal has also been influential. In single-image deraining, attention mechanisms have been utilized to reduce image artifacts and retain fine details. To fully utilize spatial contextual information in 2020, Deng et al.50 integrated residual blocks and squeeze-and-excitation (SE) blocks. For rain removal, Jiang et al.51 developed a multi-scale pyramid structure and included several cascaded channel attention units. This allowed the network to concentrate on the most beneficial channels and encouraged the fusing of the multi-scale rain signals. Nanba et al.52 configured one of the subnets using channel attention and spatial attention mechanisms to retain fine details during rain removal, capturing detailed spatial information. In 2023, Liu et al.15 utilized feature-enhanced residual blocks to preserve more details for rain removal in addition to using non-local block enhancement to collect positional information. Then, through the channel attention mechanism and the spatial attention mechanism in turn, the feature-enhanced residual blocks concentrated on the spatial dimension, the channel dimension, and the relationship between the intermediate features. Attention mechanisms can improve the efficiency of image-processing tasks such as rain removal by making it easier to extract and amplify important characteristics.

Proposed method

In the kernel prediction subnet, DACEB learns important rain streak features from both the channel attention mechanism and spatial attention mechanism. It passes the feature information to WRRB to ensure a relatively uniform distribution of rain streaks among regions, generating pixel-wise kernels adapted to multi-scale rain streak features as the output of the kernel prediction subnet. Subsequently, we propose a multi-scale kernel dilation filtering method to expand each predicted kernel to three scales for filtering rain images and removing rain streaks. To recover more background texture information, we perform image fusion operations on the filtered results.

In this section, we present a detailed description of our proposed rain removal network architecture for individual images called WRRDANet. This architecture is inspired by U-Net and is depicted in Fig. 1. Through training on image pairs, our kernel prediction subnet learns to predict spatial variation kernels that can adapt to rain streaks with different thicknesses and intensities while preserving the target boundaries. The prediction process begins with three convolution layers, followed by a convolution layer and two novel blocks called DACEB and WRRB, which are introduced in Section III-A. Every DACEB consists of a residual block incorporating channel attention (CA) and spatial attention (SA). Dual attention complementary enhancement block

The rain streak features in the rainy image are ill-posed, and the background layer contains complex details. Therefore, learning the distinctive spatial details in the rain image is crucial. To address this, we devised and created a dual attention complementary enhancement block (DACEB) to enhance feature learning and representation. The schematic diagram is shown in Fig. 1c. DACEB aims to capture spatial details while preserving all the fine-grained information from the input features to the output features. To fully leverage both channel and spatial information, we integrate channel attention and spatial attention in DACEB to effectively highlight region-specific information and significantly enhance the expressive power of feature mappings. This further facilitates extracting essential features for recovering clean, rain-free background images.

For channel attention, it learns the dependencies among channels adaptively through a global average pooling layer, followed by convolutional layers, ReLU activation, another convolutional layer, and a sigmoid function. The global average pooling compresses the spatial information into the channel dimension to extract global information of the entire feature map. For spatial attention, we utilize average pooling and max pooling to learn the importance of each spatial position in the input feature map and enhance the features of significant spatial positions. To extract deep-level background details, we employ convolutional layers with a Sigmoid activation function to generate a spatial attention map, which highlights or suppresses information at different positions. This spatial attention map is then passed to the WRRB for further processing. Multi-scale kernel dilation filtering and image fusion

We expand each predicted kernel into three scales based on pixel-wise image filtering to capture multi-scale rain streak features and gather comprehensive image information. The dilation filtering operation makes the multi-scale kernels effectively remove rain streaks. In particular, in cases where rain streaks cover a significant area, the multi-scale kernel can effectively remove rain streaks by considering related pixels located far away from the rain region. Therefore, we expand the pixel-wise filtering described in Eq. (10) to pixel-wise dilation filtering55 before performing image fusion, as shown in Eq. (11) and Fig. 1b. Although NLEDN23, ReHEN27, and RCDNet29 perform well on synthetic images, they tend to introduce residual checkerboard artifacts and overlook fine details, resulting in excessive smoothing during processing. In the highlighted area shown in Fig. 4, we can observe rain marks and fuzzy details retained by many methods, such as the rain marks near the chimney in the blue box and the over-smoothing effect produced by RCDNet in the yellow box. Furthermore, in Fig. 6, although NLEDN achieves a satisfactory visual effect, it over-smooths certain details, neglecting the preservation of background details. However, our method effectively learns rain streak features using regional blocks and leverages multi-scale fusion and attention mechanisms to perform rain removal while preserving complex scene details. Through these comparative visual analyses, it is obvious that our proposed method outperforms existing approaches in terms of rain removal effectiveness and preservation of fine details in the image background.

Conclusion

In this paper, we delve into a kernel prediction subnet that incorporates multiple dual attention complementary enhancement blocks (DACEB) and wide rectangular regional blocks (WRRB) to tackle the challenge of removing rain from a single image. Initially, we introduce DACEB consisting of residual structure with CA and SA attention mechanisms to learn and preserve critical deep-level feature information. At the same time, the expression ability of feature mapping can be significantly enhanced. Concurrently, we create wide rectangular regional blocks to not only make the rain streaks relatively more uniformly distributed over the region but also obtain rich remote background information. Subsequently, we develop multi-scale kernel dilation filtering to expand the pixel kernel estimated by the kernel prediction subnet to multiple scales, removing the rain streaks from the original rainy image on a larger scale. Furthermore, extensive evaluations on diverse synthetic datasets (Rain100H, Rain100L, and Rain1200) and challenging real-world datasets (SPA-DATA) validate the efficacy of our proposed method. In summary, our approach exhibits advantages in deraining effect and retaining image details when compared to traditional methods for rain removal and other deep learning-based alternatives.
