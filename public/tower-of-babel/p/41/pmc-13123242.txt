UBE2M promotes malignant phenotypes of prostate cancer through mediating NAA10 neddylation
Authors: Qixia Wang, Junyu Zhang, Xiang Lin, Ruxue Shi, Chenglong Pan, Qingxia Hu, Linlin Xu, Qiaoyun Chen, Dezhao Xue, Junling Yin, Qiang Fu, Shuhong Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProstate cancer (PCa) is a common malignant tumor in men, with a high incidence rate, posing a serious threat to human health and quality of life. Neddylation is a major post-translation modification, which is essential for multiple cellular behaviors. Aberrant neddylation has been implicated in the development and progression of various diseases. The NEDD8-conjugating enzyme UBE2M is frequently overexpressed in multiple types of malignancies and is closely associated with tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapy resistance. However, the potential role of UBE2M in prostate cancer remains unknown.MethodsIn our study, a bioinformatics analysis and Immunohistochemistry were employed to validate elevated UBE2M expression in prostate cancer compared to adjacent tissues. We assessed the impact of UBE2M on tumor progression using in vitro and in vivo functional experiments, including CCK8, colony formation, transwell assay and several animal models including PCa xenografts were employed to investigate the role of UBE2M in PCa progression. To elucidate the mechanism, we utilized Coimmunoprecipitation, Mass spectrometry analysis and Proximity ligation assay to elucidate the mechanisms by which UBE2M functions in prostate cancer cells.ResultsWe discovered that UBE2M expression is significantly elevated in prostate cancer tissues compared to adjacent paracancerous tissues, and this high UBE2M expression indicates a poorer prognosis for patients. Functional experiments demonstrated that overexpression of UBE2M promotes the proliferation and migration of prostate cancer cells, whereas knockdown of UBE2M inhibits these malignant phenotypes. Furthermore, we identified that NAA10 undergoes neddylation modification, and elucidated that the RBX1-CUL4A complex acts as the critical E3 ligase responsible for this modification, with the K148 site being the key neddylation site. We further confirmed that UBE2M enhances the protein stability and functional activity of NAA10 by promoting its neddylation. Knockdown of NAA10 significantly suppressed the UBE2M-driven proliferation of prostate cancer cells.ConclusionsThis study reveals that UBE2M drives prostate cancer tumorigenesis by facilitating NAA10 neddylation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08001-8.Keywords: UBE2M, NAA10, Prostate cancer, Protein stability, EMT, Neddylation

Background

Prostate cancer remains the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality in men worldwide [1, 2]. Androgen deprivation therapy, which targets the androgen receptor (AR) signaling axis, serves as the primary treatment [3, 4]. While initially effective, the almost universal progression to castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) underscores the critical need to identify novel therapeutic targets beyond the canonical AR pathway [5–7].

The dysregulation of post-translational modifications is a hallmark of cancer. Among them, neddylation, a process analogous to ubiquitination that conjugates NEDD8 to substrate proteins, has garnered increasing attention [8]. The neddylation cascade, involving E1 (NAE), E2 (UBE2M or UBE2F), and E3 enzymes, critically activates Cullin-RING ligases (CRLs), the largest family of E3 ubiquitin ligases [9–11]. Hyperactivation of neddylation, frequently observed in human cancers, promotes the CRL-mediated degradation of tumor suppressors (e.g., p21, p27), thereby fueling tumorigenesis [10, 12]. Notably, the NEDD8-conjugating enzyme UBE2M emerges as a central node in this pathway, exhibiting the strongest correlation with global neddylation levels [13]. UBE2M is overexpressed in multiple malignancies, and its depletion impairs cancer cell proliferation by stabilizing tumor suppressors [10, 14]. However, its role and regulatory mechanisms in prostate cancer pathogenesis remain incompletely understood.

Intriguingly, the oncogenic effects of neddylation may converge with other critical signaling pathways in prostate cancer [15]. Acetylation is another pivotal modification, and the acetyltransferase NAA10 (ARD1) has been implicated in acetylating and activating AR, thereby promoting prostate cancer progression [16]. While the role of NAA10 in various tumors is recognized, the mechanisms governing its own stability and activity are not fully elucidated [17, 18]. Given that many acetyltransferases are regulated by other post-translational modifications, we hypothesized that NAA10 might be a target of the oncogenic neddylation pathway in prostate cancer.

Herein, we set out to investigate the role of UBE2M in prostate cancer and explore its potential crosstalk with the NAA10. We discovered that UBE2M is upregulated in prostate cancer and drives cancer cell proliferation and invasion. Mechanistically, we identified that UBE2M (functioning as the E2 conjugating enzyme) and the RBX1-CUL4A complex (functioning as the E3 ligase) facilitate the neddylation of NAA10 at the specific K148 residue. This neddylation event critically enhances NAA10 protein stability. Our functional rescue experiments further demonstrated that the oncogenic functions of UBE2M are largely mediated through stabilizing NAA10.

Collectively, our work unveils a previously unrecognized UBE2M-NAA10 regulatory axis, establishing NAA10 as a novel neddylation substrate and highlighting the neddylation pathway as a promising therapeutic target for prostate cancer.

Methods

Tissue samples

This study utilized six pairs of PCa and adjacent normal tissues from patients who underwent radical prostatectomy at Shandong Provincial Hospital. Informed consent was obtained, and the diagnosis of all samples was pathologically confirmed. The study protocol adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Shandong First Medical University (Approval No. R202504160309).

Tissue microarray and immunohistochemistry

A prostate cancer tissue microarray (TMA) slide, containing 59 cancer cases and 36 adjacent normal tissue cores, was obtained from Shanghai Outdo Biotech Co., Ltd. The TMA included comprehensive clinicopathological. After deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a microwave oven. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H₂O₂, and nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary rabbit monoclonal anti-UBE2M antibody (dilution 1:200; cat. no. ab109507; Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Subsequently, the sections were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (cat. no. ab205718; Abcam) at room temperature for 1 h. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit, and the nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Stained sections were imaged using a Nikon microscope. Protein expression was evaluated independently, based on the intensity of staining (scored 0–3) and the percentage of positive tumor cells. A final immunoreactivity score was calculated by multiplying these two values.

Xenograft mouse model

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shandong First Medical University (Approval No. LS2024105) and were performed in strict accordance with the institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. Four-week-old male BALB/c nude mice were purchased from Jinan Pengyue Experimental Animal Breeding Co., Ltd. and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. To establish the xenograft models, PC-3 cells stably knocked down for UBE2M via lentiviral infection, along with empty vector control cells, were harvested. Single-cell suspensions (2 × 10⁶cells in 100 µL of a 1:1 mixture of PBS and Matrigel) were subcutaneously inoculated into the mice, with six mice per group. Tumor size was measured every five days using a caliper, and the volume was calculated using the formula: Volume = 0.5 × length × (width)². The mice were monitored daily and humanely euthanized immediately if the tumor volume exceeded 1500 mm³ or if any significant signs of distress were observed. All mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation on day 20 post-inoculation. The tumors were completely excised, weighed, and photographed. Portions of the tumor tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent protein extraction. Total protein was extracted from the tumor tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (CWBIO, #CW2333S) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The expression levels of UBE2M and related proteins were detected by Western blotting.

Bioinformatic analysis

The UALCAN database (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) was utilized to compare UBE2M protein expression between primary prostate tumor samples and normal tissues, based on the CPTAC dataset. The correlation between UBE2M mRNA expression and patient survival was assessed using the GEPIA2 server (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/). Overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) analyses were conducted using PCa data from TCGA.

Cell lines and cell culture

Cell culture and authentication

The human immortalized prostate epithelial cell line RWPE-1 and prostate cancer (PCa) cell lines, including C4-2B, PC-3, DU-145, and 22Rv1, were purchased from KeyGEN BioTECH (Jiangsu, China). The HEK293T cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling, performed by the respective vendors (KeyGEN BioTECH or GENEWIZ for HEK293T). RWPE-1 cells were maintained in Keratinocyte Serum-Free Medium (K-SFM) (ScienCell, #2101) supplemented with Keratinocyte Growth Supplement (KGS). C4-2B, PC-3, and 22Rv1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (KeyGEN BioTECH), while DU-145 and HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (KeyGEN BioTECH). All RPMI-1640 and DMEM media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, #A3160802) and 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco, #35050061). Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination and used at low passages.

Plasmid transfection and RNA interference

For plasmid transfection, Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, #11668019) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For siRNA-mediated gene knockdown, Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, #13778150) was employed. All transfections were performed in opti-MEM reduced serum medium (Gibco). The culture medium was replaced with fresh complete medium 6 h post-transfection. The siRNA sequences (synthesized by GenePharma, China) targeting specific genes were as follows:

si-CUL1: 5’-GGUCGCUUCAUAAACAACA-3’, si-CUL5: 5’-GUCUCACUUCCUACUGAACUG-3’, si-CUL2: 5’-GCCCUUACGUCAGUUGUAAAUUACA-3’, si-CUL4A: 5’-GAAGCUGGUCAUCAAGAAC-3’, si-CUL4B: 5’-AAGCCUAAAUUACCAGAAA-3’, si-RBX1: 5’-GACAACAGAGAGUGGGAAU-3’, si-UBE2M:5’-CAGAGGUCCUGCAGAACAA-3’, si-NAA10: 5’-CUGACUGCGCCUUCACGAUUU-3’.

A non-targeting scrambled siRNA was used as a negative control.

Lentiviral infection

Lentiviruses expressing a non-targeting control shRNA (Vector) or two independent shRNAs targeting UBE2M (shUBE2M#1 and shUBE2M#2) were packaged in HEK293T cells. Briefly, the cells were co-transfected with the psPAX2 packaging plasmid, the pMD2.G envelope plasmid, and the respective pLKO.1-based transfer plasmid using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 h post-transfection, pooled, and filtered through a 0.45 μm PVDF filter. The lentiviruses were then concentrated by ultracentrifugation or using a commercial lentiviral concentration solution. For infection, PC-3 cells were incubated with the concentrated lentiviruses in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene. After 24 h, the virus-containing medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. To establish stable knockdown cell lines, the infected cells were selected with 1 µg/mL puromycin for at least 72 h. The working concentration of puromycin was predetermined by a kill curve assay on non-infected PC-3 cells. The resulting polyclonal populations were maintained in a culture medium containing 0.4 µg/mL puromycin for long-term culture.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using a CCK-8 kit (Proteintech, #PF00004). Cells from each group were trypsinized, resuspended, and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 10³ cells per well in 100 µL of culture medium. The plates were pre-incubated for 24 h in a 37 °C incubator to allow for complete cell attachment and recovery. To measure cell viability at each time point (0, 24, 48, and 72 h), 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was directly added to the corresponding wells, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1–4 h. The optimal incubation duration was determined in preliminary experiments to ensure that the optical density (OD) values at 450 nm, measured using a microplate reader, remained within the linear range of the assay. For each group, at least five replicate wells were set up for every time point to allow for sequential measurement without cross-interference. The experiment was independently repeated three times. The data are presented as the mean OD values ± standard deviation (SD) from one representative experiment or as the combined results from all replicates.

Colony formation assay

The colony-forming ability of PC-3 cells stably expressing a non-targeting vector, shUBE2M-#1, or shUBE2M-#2 was assessed. Cells were trypsinized into a single-cell suspension and seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10³ cells per well. The seeding density was predetermined to allow the formation of discrete, countable colonies without excessive overlapping. The cells were then cultured in complete medium for 12 days, with the medium being refreshed every 3 days. After the incubation period, when macroscopic colonies were visible, the cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). They were then fixed with 2 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde per well for 20 min at room temperature. Following fixation, the cells were washed twice with PBS to remove the fixative. Subsequently, the colonies were stained with 1 mL of 0.1% crystal violet solution per well for 30 min. After staining, the plates were thoroughly rinsed with tap water to remove excess dye and air-dried at room temperature. The number of colonies (defined as cell clusters containing > 50 cells) was manually counted under a microscope for each well. The experiment was performed with three technical replicates per group and independently repeated three times.

Cell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion abilities were assessed using 12-well Transwell chambers with 8.0 μm pores (Corning). For the migration assay, 1 × 104 cells in 200 µL of serum-free medium were seeded into the uncoated upper chamber. For the invasion assay, 2 × 104 cells in 200 µL of serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber, which had been pre-coated with 50 µL of Matrigel (BD Biosciences, #356234) diluted 1:8 in serum-free cold medium and polymerized at 37 °C for 4 hours. The lower chamber was filled with 600 µL of complete medium containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 24 h at 37 °C, the non-migrated/non-invaded cells on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed with a cotton swab. The cells that had traversed the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, washed with PBS, and then stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. After staining, the chambers were thoroughly rinsed with PBS to remove background stain and air-dried. The stained cells were photographed under an inverted microscope (×100 magnification) in five random fields per membrane. The number of cells in each field was counted using ImageJ software. Each experimental group was set up in triplicate Transwells, and the entire experiment was independently repeated three times.

Western blotting analysis

Cells were washed twice with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed on ice using RIPA lysis buffer (CWBIO, #CW2333S) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (MedChemExpress, #HY-K011). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, #P0012S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein samples were diluted with 5× SDS-PAGE loading buffer (Solarbio, #P1016) to a 1× final concentration and denatured by boiling at 98 °C for 5 min. Equal amounts of protein (20–30 µg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE on 10% or 12% gels (as appropriate for the target protein’s molecular weight) and electrophoretically transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, #ISEQ00010). The transfer was performed at a constant current of 300 mA for 90 min in an ice bath. After transfer, the membrane was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Solarbio, #A8020) in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies diluted in TBST. Following three washes with TBST (10 min each), the membrane was incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary antibody (Proteintech, 1:5000 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence kit (Proteintech, #PK10002) and imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system. The primary antibodies used were as follows: UBE2M (Proteintech, #14520-1-AP, 1:1000), NAA10 (Proteintech, #14803-1-AP, 1:3000), GAPDH (Proteintech, #60004-1-Ig, 1:5000), NEDD8 (Proteintech, #16777-1-AP, 1:1000), Flag (Proteintech, #80010-1-RR, 1:1000), HA (Proteintech, #51064-2-AP, 1:5000), His (Proteintech, #66005-1-IG, 1:5000), Ubiquitin (Proteintech, #10201-2-AP, 1:2000).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

Cells were lysed on ice for 30 min using IP lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA, and a protease inhibitor cocktail (MedChemExpress, #HY-K0010). The cell lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected as whole-cell extracts. For each Co-IP reaction, 500–1000 µg of total protein was diluted to a consistent volume with lysis buffer. For immunoprecipitation with specific antibodies (e.g., anti-UBE2M, anti-NAA10), the lysates were pre-cleared by incubation with 20 µL of Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (Vazyme, #PB101-02) for 1 h at 4 °C. The pre-cleared lysates were then incubated with the indicated primary antibody (1–2 µg) or corresponding normal IgG as a negative control, with gentle rotation overnight at 4 °C. The following day, 30 µL of Protein A/G Magnetic Beads were added to capture the antigen-antibody complexes and incubated for an additional 2–4 h at 4 °C. For co-immunoprecipitation using tagged proteins, the pre-cleared lysates from cells expressing Flag- or HA-tagged constructs were directly incubated with 20 µL of Anti-Flag Magnetic Beads (Biolinkedin, #L-1103) or Anti-HA Magnetic Beads (Biolinkedin, #L-1101) overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Following incubation, the bead-bound complexes were collected using a magnetic rack and washed five times with cold IP lysis buffer. The bound proteins were eluted by boiling the beads in 30–50 µL of 2× SDS-PAGE loading buffer at 98 °C for 10 min. The eluted proteins were then subjected to Western blotting analysis with the specified antibodies.

Fluorescent western blotting

Proteins separated by SDS-PAGE were electrotransferred onto PVDF membranes, which were then blocked with Odyssey® Blocking Buffer (LI-COR Biosciences) for 1 h at room temperature with gentle shaking. Following blocking, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in Odyssey® Blocking Buffer supplemented with 0.1% Tween-20 at 4 °C overnight. After three sequential washes with TBST (10 min per wash), membranes were incubated with species-matched fluorescent secondary antibodies—IRDye 680RD Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) (1:15,000, LI-COR Biosciences, Cat# 926-68070) or IRDye 800CW Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:15,000, LI-COR Biosciences, Cat# 926-32211)—for 1 h at room temperature under light-protected conditions. Membranes were subsequently washed three times with PBST and briefly rinsed once with distilled water. Fluorescent signals were visualized using the Odyssey CLx Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences), a near-infrared laser imaging platform. Image acquisition was conducted at a resolution of 21 μm with channel parameters optimized for 680 nm and 800 nm fluorescence emissions, respectively.

Mass spectrometric analysis

To identify proteins that interact with UBE2M, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) was performed as described in the previous section. The resulting protein complexes were separated by SDS-PAGE and visualized by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining. The protein bands of interest were excised and subjected to in-gel digestion with trypsin. The resulting peptides were analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) using a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A full service, including sample processing, LC-MS/MS analysis, and primary data processing, was provided by GeneChem (Shanghai, China). The raw MS data were searched against the Swiss-Prot human database using the MaxQuant software suite to identify specific interacting proteins.

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

Protein-protein interactions were detected in situ using the Duolink® PLA kit (Sigma-Aldrich, #DUO92101 - Red). Briefly, cells were seeded onto sterile glass coverslips in a 12-well plate. After 24 h, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After washing, cells were blocked with the provided Duolink® Blocking Solution in a pre-heated humidity chamber for 60 min at 37 °C. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a mixture of two primary antibodies raised in different host species: mouse anti-UBE2M (Proteintech, #14520-1-AP, 1:200) and rabbit anti-NAA10 (Proteintech, #14803-1-AP, 1:200). Control samples were incubated with only one primary antibody or with species-matched non-specific IgG to confirm the specificity of the signal. The following day, cells were washed twice with Duolink® Wash Buffer A. Subsequently, the PLA probes—Duolink® Anti-Mouse MINUS (Sigma, #DUO92004) and Duolink® Anti-Rabbit PLUS (Sigma, #DUO92002)—were applied and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. After further washing, ligation was performed with the ligase-containing solution for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by amplification with the polymerase-containing solution for 100 min at 37 °C. All incubation steps were performed in a dark, pre-heated humidity chamber. Finally, cells were washed with Duolink® Wash Buffer B, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Abcam, #ab104139) before mounting with Duolink® in Situ Mounting Medium. Images were acquired using an Olympus microscope.

Statistical analysis and reproducibility

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent biological replicates, unless otherwise stated in the figure legends. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for data that passed the normality test. For comparisons among more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for normally distributed data. For non-normally distributed data involving multiple groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test was used. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

UBE2M is highly expressed in prostate cancer and correlates with adverse clinicopathological features

Although UBE2M upregulation has been reported in various malignancies, its role in prostate cancer remains to be fully elucidated [19–23]. To address this, we first analyzed UBE2M expression in the TCGA dataset using GEPIA2. The results showed that UBE2M mRNA levels were significantly elevated in prostate tumor tissues compared with normal prostate tissues (Fig. 1A). We further evaluated UBE2M protein expression and its clinicopathological relevance using immunohistochemistry (IHC) in 59 prostate cancer specimens and 36 adjacent normal tissues. The results showed that UBE2M expression was significantly upregulated in prostate cancer tissues. (Fig. 1B, C). UBE2M expression was significantly higher in tumors with advanced pathological stages (T8–T10) than in those with earlier stages (T3–T7). Moreover, high UBE2M expression was strongly associated with advanced clinical stage (Table 1). Consistent with the IHC findings, western blot analysis of clinical prostate samples confirmed that UBE2M protein levels were markedly upregulated in tumor tissues compared with matched normal counterparts (Fig. 1D). We also examined UBE2M expression in a panel of prostate cell lines, including the normal epithelial cell line RWPE-1, two AR-negative (PC-3, DU145), and two AR-positive (22Rv1, C4-2B) prostate cancer cell lines. As shown in Fig. 1E, UBE2M was significantly overexpressed in all cancer cell lines compared to RWPE-1 cells, with the highest level in PC-3 cells and the lowest in DU145 cells. To evaluate the prognostic value of UBE2M, we stratified patients into high- and low-expression groups. While overall survival (OS) did not differ significantly between groups, its expression was strongly associated with disease-free survival (DFS). Patients with high UBE2M expression had significantly shorter DFS, with a 50% increased risk of disease recurrence or progression (Fig. 1F, G). Taken together, these results indicate that UBE2M is frequently overexpressed in prostate cancer and may drive tumor progression.

UBE2M promotes malignant phenotypes of prostate cancer cells in vitro and in vivo

To investigate the biological functions of UBE2M in prostate cancer, we performed loss-of-function and gain-of-function studies. We first established stable UBE2M-knockdown PC-3 cells (with high endogenous UBE2M expression) using lentivirus-mediated shRNA (Fig. 2A). CCK-8 assays showed that UBE2M knockdown significantly suppressed cell proliferation (Fig. 2B). This finding was corroborated by colony formation assays, which revealed a marked reduction in clonogenic ability upon UBE2M depletion (Fig. 2C). Given the critical role of migration and invasion in prostate cancer progression, we next assessed these capabilities using Transwell assays. UBE2M knockdown notably impaired the migratory capacity of PC-3 cells (Fig. 2D). Similarly, Matrigel invasion assays demonstrated a significant suppression of invasiveness (Fig. 2D), indicating that UBE2M promotes metastatic potential. At the molecular level, western blot analysis of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers showed that UBE2M knockdown increased the expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin, while decreasing the levels of the mesenchymal markers N-cadherin and Vimentin (Fig. 2E). To complement the loss-of-function data, we overexpressed UBE2M in DU145 cells, which exhibit low endogenous UBE2M levels (Fig. 2F). Consistent with the knockdown results, UBE2M overexpression enhanced cell proliferation (Fig. 2G) and colony-forming ability (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, UBE2M overexpression potentiated both migration and invasion (Fig. 2I), and induced a corresponding EMT shift, characterized by downregulation of E-cadherin and upregulation of N-cadherin and Vimentin (Fig. 2J) We further validated the oncogenic role of UBE2M in vivo using a xenograft mouse model. Subcutaneous injection of control (WT) and UBE2M-knockdown (KD) PC-3 cells into the left and right flanks of nude mice, respectively, revealed that UBE2M depletion significantly attenuated tumor growth (Fig. 2K). This was evidenced by smaller tumor volumes from day 10 onwards and significantly reduced final tumor weights in the KD group (Fig. 2L, M). Western blot analysis of the xenografted tumors confirmed that UBE2M knockdown consistently upregulated E-cadherin and downregulated N-cadherin and Vimentin in vivo (Fig. 2N). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that UBE2M acts as a critical oncoprotein in prostate cancer, driving tumor growth and metastatic progression by enhancing proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT.

UBE2M directly interacts with NAA10

To identify downstream effectors mediating UBE2M’s oncogenic functions, we performed UBE2M pull-down assays coupled with mass spectrometry (MS) in DU145 cells (Fig. 3A). To validate the specificity of UBE2M interactors, we compared unique peptide counts from mass spectrometry data (Supplementary Table 1). NAA10 (N-alpha-acetyltransferase 10) was identified as a high-confidence candidate (Fig. 3B). To validate this interaction, we co-expressed Flag-UBE2M and HA-NAA10 in HEK293T cells. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays using an anti-Flag antibody readily pulled down HA-NAA10 (Fig. 3C). Reciprocally, anti-HA antibody immunoprecipitation also retrieved Flag-UBE2M (Fig. 3D), confirming a specific physical interaction between UBE2M and NAA10 in an overexpression system. We further examined whether UBE2M interacts with NAA10 at endogenous levels in prostate cancer cells. Endogenous Co-IP in PC-3 cells demonstrated that UBE2M and NAA10 form a natural protein complex (Fig. 3E, F). To visualize their direct interaction in situ, we performed a proximity ligation assay (PLA), which produces fluorescent signals when two proteins are within 40 nm. In DU145 cells, a pronounced PLA signal (white arrows) was detected predominantly in the cytoplasm using antibodies against endogenous UBE2M and NAA10 (Fig. 3G). Together, these results provide multi-level evidence that UBE2M directly binds to NAA10 under both exogenous and endogenous settings, and that their interaction occurs primarily in the cytoplasmic compartment.

UBE2M positively regulates the expression of NAA10 in prostate cancer cells

Having established the physical interaction between UBE2M and NAA10, we next asked whether UBE2M influences NAA10 expression. We found that knockdown of UBE2M in PC-3 cells decreased NAA10 protein levels, whereas overexpression of UBE2M in DU145 cells increased them (Fig. 4A). In contrast, neither knockdown nor overexpression of NAA10 significantly altered UBE2M expression (Fig. 4B), indicating that UBE2M acts upstream of NAA10 in a unidirectional regulatory manner. We further validated this regulatory relationship in vivo using xenograft tumor tissues from the experiment shown in Fig. 2. Consistent with the in vitro data, UBE2M knockdown led to a marked decrease in both NAA10 and its key downstream effector ADAM9 (Fig. 4C), supporting the notion that UBE2M stabilizes or upregulates NAA10 expression in a physiological context. To further validate the in vitro findings, we performed immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis on xenograft tumor tissues. Results revealed that UBE2M knockdown significantly reduced both NAA10 protein levels and the proliferation marker Ki67 (Fig. 4D). To determine whether NAA10 acts as a functional downstream effector of UBE2M, we performed rescue experiments in prostate cancer cells. As shown in Fig. 4E, compared to the vector control group, UBE2M knockdown significantly impaired the proliferative capacity of PC-3 cells, while re-expression of NAA10 largely reversed this defect. We subsequently assessed metastatic potential using Transwell migration and Matrigel invasion assays. Consistent with the proliferation results, depletion of UBE2M markedly suppressed both migratory and invasive abilities, and these impaired phenotypes were effectively rescued upon restoration of NAA10 expression (Fig. 4F). To reciprocally verify the specific dependence of UBE2M function on NAA10, we performed complementary experiments in DU145 cells. As shown in Fig. 4G, knockdown of NAA10 significantly inhibited cell proliferation; notably, even when UBE2M was overexpressed, it failed to substantially reverse the proliferation suppression caused by NAA10 deficiency. Similarly, Transwell assays demonstrated that NAA10 knockdown strongly suppressed cell migration and invasion; although UBE2M overexpression showed a partial restorative trend in NAA10-deficient cells, the effect was far from complete rescue (Fig. 4H). Taken together, these complementary sets of rescue experiments demonstrate that the tumor-promoting function of UBE2M in prostate cancer is predominantly dependent on NAA10 and is executed through its regulation. To further evaluate the biological role of NAA10 in prostate cancer, we first examined NAA10 expression using TCGA data via the GEPIA2 platform. The analysis revealed a significant upregulation of NAA10 mRNA in tumor tissues compared to normal prostate tissues (Fig. S1A). We then performed survival analysis by stratifying patients into low and high NAA10 expression groups based on transcript-per-million levels. For overall survival (OS), a non-significant trend toward poorer outcomes was observed in the high-expression group (Fig. S1B). However, analysis of disease-free survival (DFS) demonstrated a significant association: patients with high NAA10 expression had a markedly shorter DFS, with a hazard ratio of 1.9, indicating a nearly two-fold increased risk of recurrence or progression (Fig. S1C). The high-frequency overexpression of NAA10 suggests that it may be a key molecular driver of prostate cancer progression; its overexpression likely constitutes a critical step in tumor malignancy. Collectively, these results demonstrate that NAA10 is essential for UBE2M-mediated promotion of proliferation, migration, and invasion in prostate cancer cells.

UBE2M promotes neddylation of NAA10

Since UBE2M is a key E2 enzyme in the neddylation cascade [19, 24, 25], we hypothesized that it might mediate neddylation of its interacting partner, NAA10. To test this, we first examined whether NAA10 undergoes neddylation using an in vitro neddylation assay with MLN4924, a selective NEDD8-E1 inhibitor [26]. Upon NEDD8 overexpression, a clear slower-migrating band corresponding to NEDD8-conjugated NAA10 was detected; this band was markedly diminished by MLN4924 treatment (Fig. 5A). Reciprocally, when NAA10 was overexpressed, a NEDD8-NAA10 conjugate was also observed and similarly suppressed by MLN4924 (Fig. 5B), confirming that NAA10 is a bona fide neddylation substrate.

To validate the neddylation of NAA10 under endogenous conditions, we performed endogenous co-immunoprecipitation assays in the prostate cancer cell line PC-3. The results showed that the NEDD8 antibody could co-precipitate endogenous NAA10 from PC-3 cell lysates, and this interaction was markedly attenuated upon MLN4924 treatment (Fig. 3C). Reciprocally, the NAA10 antibody specifically enriched NEDD8-conjugated protein bands, and this modification signal was significantly reduced when the neddylation pathway was inhibited by MLN4924 (Fig. 3D). Together, these endogenous data demonstrate that NAA10 undergoes neddylation in prostate cancer cells in a pathway-dependent manner, thereby confirming NAA10 as a bona fide neddylation substrate under physiological conditions. We next asked whether UBE2M is the specific E2 enzyme responsible for NAA10 neddylation. While overexpression of UBE2F had no effect, UBE2M significantly enhanced NAA10 neddylation in 293T cells (Fig. 5E). Consistent with this, UBE2M overexpression increased (Fig. 5F), while its knockdown decreased (Fig. 5G), NAA10 neddylation levels. To further confirm the enzymatic specificity, we used a catalytically inactive UBE2M mutant (C111S) [27]. Overexpression of wild-type UBE2M, but not the UBE2M-C111S mutant, enhanced NAA10 neddylation (Fig. 5H). Taken together, these results demonstrate that NAA10 is a neddylation substrate and that its modification depends specifically on UBE2M-mediated catalytic activity.

The neddylation modification of NAA10 enhances its stability

Ubiquitination is a prerequisite for protein degradation [33, 34]. We first investigated whether the E2 ligase UBE2M regulates NAA10 stability. Overexpression of UBE2M markedly reduced polyubiquitination of NAA10 (Fig. 7A), whereas depletion of UBE2M significantly shortened its protein half-life (Fig. 7B). These findings indicate that UBE2M stabilizes NAA10, likely through promoting its neddylation to competitively inhibit ubiquitin-dependent degradation. Under physiological conditions, NAA10 is distributed in both the nucleus and cytoplasm. We further observed that UBE2M overexpression promoted nuclear accumulation of NAA10 (Fig. 7C). To directly test whether neddylation competes with ubiquitination on NAA10, we employed a neddylation-deficient mutant (K148R). This mutation resulted in substantially increased ubiquitination of NAA10 (Fig. 7D) and a notably shorter protein half-life compared to the wild-type protein (Fig. 7E), confirming that neddylation at lysine 148 protects NAA10 from ubiquitin-mediated degradation. We next examined the functional consequence of NAA10 neddylation on cancer cell phenotypes. In DU145 prostate cancer cells, overexpression of the K148R mutant significantly attenuated cell proliferation, migration, and invasion compared to wild-type NAA10 (Fig. 7F, G). These results demonstrate that neddylation is required for the full oncogenic activity of NAA10. To elucidate how neddylation influences NAA10 regulation of its downstream target ADAM9, we assessed ADAM9 protein abundance. Although the K148R mutant exhibited higher ubiquitination, its ability to promote ADAM9 expression was not significantly different from that of wild-type NAA10 (Fig. 7H), suggesting that neddylation does not primarily regulate ADAM9 steady-state levels. Importantly, co-immunoprecipitation analysis revealed that the K148R mutation markedly weakened the physical interaction between NAA10 and ADAM9 (Fig. 7I), indicating that neddylation modulates the protein-protein interaction interface rather than substrate stability. This key finding demonstrates that K148 neddylation acts as a molecular switch that specifically modulates the protein-protein interaction interface, rather than globally regulating substrate abundance. To synthesize our findings into a cohesive mechanistic model, we propose that UBE2M promotes prostate cancer progression through neddylation-dependent stabilization of NAA10 (Fig. 7J). In prostate cancer cells (blue), UBE2M-mediated neddylation of NAA10 competitively inhibits its ubiquitination, thereby shielding it from proteasomal degradation. The resulting accumulation of NAA10 enables the full exertion of its oncogenic functions. Conversely, adjacent normal or control cells (red) do not exhibit this aberrant activation of the UBE2M-NAA10 axis. Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of neddylation using MLN4924 disrupts this axis, leading to enhanced ubiquitination and degradation of NAA10 in cancer cells, ultimately suppressing its tumor-promoting activities. This model integrates our molecular, cellular, and functional data, positioning the UBE2M-NAA10-neddylation axis as a central driver of prostate cancer progression and a potential therapeutic target.

Discussion

Dysregulation of neddylation, a critical ubiquitin-like post-translational modification, has been implicated in tumorigenesis by modulating the stability and function of both oncoproteins and tumor suppressors [10, 35]. However, its specific roles and substrates in PCa remain incompletely understood. Concurrently, although the acetyltransferase NAA10 is known to be overexpressed and promote progression in PCa, the mechanisms governing its own regulation are poorly defined [36–38]. Our study bridges this gap by identifying UBE2M-mediated neddylation as a novel, stabilizing modification of NAA10, thereby unveiling a previously unrecognized oncogenic axis in PCa.

We first established UBE2M as a clinically relevant oncogene in PCa. Our data demonstrate that UBE2M is significantly upregulated in PCa tissues, with its expression correlating positively with advanced clinical stage. Functional assays confirmed that UBE2M drives PCa cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro, and tumor growth in vivo.

Mechanistically, we identified the acetyltransferase NAA10 as a key downstream effector of UBE2M. We discovered that UBE2M does not merely interact with NAA10 but directly regulates its protein stability. The observation that the oncogenic phenotypes induced by UBE2M are largely abolished upon NAA10 knockdown strongly positions NAA10 as a critical functional substrate of UBE2M in PCa.

Most importantly, we provide the first evidence that NAA10 undergoes neddylation, a modification catalyzed by UBE2M in concert with the CRL4A–RBX1 E3 ligase complex. We further pinpointed K148, a highly conserved lysine residue, as the primary neddylation site on NAA10. This discovery adds a new layer to the complex post-translational regulation of NAA10, which was previously known to be modulated by phosphorylation, acetylation, and hydroxylation. We demonstrated that neddylation, in this context, serves to enhance NAA10 protein stability, leading to its accumulation and thereby promoting PCa aggressiveness.

However, the mechanism by which this stabilization drives pro-tumorigenic phenotypes requires further elaboration. Accumulating evidence indicates that NAA10 acts as a critical hub in multiple oncogenic pathways. First, as shown in our study, stabilized NAA10 upregulates ADAM9, a metalloproteinase that promotes invasion through extracellular matrix degradation and growth factor shedding [37]. Second, NAA10 acetylates and destructs HIF-1α under normoxic conditions, activating hypoxia-responsive genes that support proliferation and metabolic adaptation [39]. Third, NAA10 enhances tumor invasiveness by coordinating the c-Myc/PAI1 regulatory axis, further contributing to metastatic progression [40]. Thus, we propose that UBE2M-dependent neddylation of NAA10 not only extends its protein half-life but also amplifies its pleiotropic oncogenic functions. This multi-pathway model positions the UBE2M–NAA10 axis as a central regulator of malignant progression and highlights its potential as a therapeutic target for intercepting downstream oncogenic effects.

Based on our findings, we propose that NAA10 neddylation at K148 drives tumor progression through a conformation-dependent dual mechanism. On one hand, this modification stabilizes NAA10 by allosterically masking ubiquitination sites; on the other hand, it fine-tunes the functional output of NAA10 by precisely reshaping its interaction interface with substrates such as ADAM9—independent of altering overall substrate abundance. Collectively, our study reveals a compelling paradigm of post-translational crosstalk in cancer, wherein neddylation governs both the “stability” and “specificity” of an acetyltransferase, thereby exerting precise control over oncogenic signaling. These insights position the UBE2M–NAA10 axis as a functionally coordinated layer of regulation and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target in prostate cancer characterized by hyperactive neddylation.

Conclusions

Our findings uncover a pivotal oncogenic axis in prostate cancer wherein UBE2M promotes tumor progression by stabilizing the acetyltransferase NAA10 via K148-linked neddylation. This study provides the first evidence of NAA10 neddylation, revealing a crucial regulatory mechanism for its overexpression in prostate cancer and establishing a functional link between the neddylation and acetylation pathways. Targeting the UBE2M-NAA10 interface thus emerges as a promising therapeutic strategy.
