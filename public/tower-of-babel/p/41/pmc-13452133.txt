AI-Driven discovery and experimental validation of covalent FGFR4 inhibitors for hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Mingjie Gao, Weiyi Zhao, Pengfei Zhang, Mengmeng Pei, Zhenyu Zhang, Min Chen, Bin Han, Xuemei Chen, Biao Ni, Jiawei Yang, Zengwu Wang, Xiaolong Du, Shaoqiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFGFR4 signaling is an essential driver in hepatocellular carcinoma. However, traditional screening is often time-consuming, highlighting a need for efficient strategies to identify covalent chemotypes and accelerate FGFR4 drug discovery.MethodsWe established an integrated AI-driven virtual screening framework to discover FGFR4 covalent inhibitors. The theoretical predictions were evaluated through a biochemical pipeline, encompassing in vitro FGFR4 kinase assays, immunoblotting of intracellular signaling cascades, and bottom-up LC-MS/MS peptide mapping.ResultsBiological validation of the computational predictions identified five distinct chemical scaffolds (hits 1, 2, 5, 7, and 8) exhibiting antiproliferative activity. The two most active candidates, hit 1 and hit 2, were selected for further mechanistic profiling. These compounds demonstrated dose-dependent FGFR4 kinase inhibition with IC50 values of 1.06 μM and 3.57 μM, respectively. Cellular assays revealed that both compounds attenuate FGFR4 autophosphorylation and its downstream FRS2/ERK1/2 signaling cascade without inducing non-specific protein degradation. Furthermore, bottom-up LC-MS/MS peptide mapping provided direct structural evidence that hit 1 and hit 2 engage the target cysteine residue via a Michael addition mechanism.ConclusionsOur AI-guided computational workflow identified multiple covalent FGFR4 inhibitors with measurable biological activity. Hit 1 and hit 2 represent structurally characterized covalent scaffolds. This study provides chemical starting points for targeted HCC therapy and demonstrates the integration of theoretical prediction and experimental validation in covalent drug discovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08669-y.

Introduction

Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most prevalent malignancy worldwide and the third leading cause of cancer mortality, underscoring the clinical necessity for effective therapeutic interventions [1]. Among the oncogenic drivers, the fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) signaling network plays a central role in hepatobiliary malignant progression, with the aberrant activation of FGFR4 being of particular significance [2, 3]. To address the limitations of early-generation therapies, pharmacological intervention targeting the FGFR kinase domain has evolved. Initial ATP-competitive, reversible inhibitors often struggled to balance efficacy and safety. For instance, AZD4547 is a reversible inhibitor that exhibits selectivity and potency against FGFR1–3 but demonstrates weaker activity against FGFR4, limiting its utility in FGFR4-driven malignancies [4]. Conversely, erdafitinib acts as a pan-FGFR inhibitor (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{IC}_{50} = 1.2$$\end{document}-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5.7\textrm{ nM}$$\end{document} for FGFR1–4); however, its broad-spectrum inhibition disrupts the physiological homeostasis maintained by FGFR1–3, leading to dose-limiting toxicities [5].

To achieve durable target engagement and address these bottlenecks, targeted covalent inhibitors (TCIs) provide an alternative strategy. Within the FGFR kinase domain, two nucleophilic cysteine residues offer distinct opportunities for covalent engagement. A frequently investigated target is Cys552, a non-conserved residue located in the middle-hinge region of FGFR4, targeted to achieve isoform selectivity. Representative agents targeting Cys552 include irreversible covalent inhibitors, such as BLU554 (fisogatinib), which targets the middle-hinge cysteine of FGFR4 [6], and H3B-6527, an inhibitor developed for FGF19-driven HCC [7]. To minimize potential off-target interactions associated with permanent covalent binding, reversible covalent inhibitors have been developed. FGF401 (roblitinib) is a reversible covalent inhibitor that dynamically engages Cys552, demonstrating selectivity for FGFR4-driven HCC [8, 9]. Conversely, the kinase P-loop harbors Cys477 (in FGFR4), a reactive residue that is conserved across the FGFR family (FGFR1–4). Targeting this conserved cysteine offers a structural approach to achieve pan-FGFR inhibition and address acquired resistance to early-generation inhibitors. Representative covalent inhibitors targeting this P-loop cysteine include FIIN-2 and FIIN-3 [10]. Futibatinib (TAS-120) is a pan-FGFR covalent inhibitor targeting this conserved pocket, which has reached clinical use [11]. Furthermore, dual-warhead strategies have been explored, exemplified by the inhibitor CXF-009, which can simultaneously engage both Cys477 and Cys552 [12]. This “double lock” mechanism reduces the likelihood that a single point mutation confers complete drug resistance. Therefore, both residues represent functionally distinct covalent anchoring points for targeted inhibitor design. Nevertheless, the balance between reactivity and selectivity is a key factor in covalent drug development. Low reactivity renders the warhead ineffective, while excessive reactivity leads to non-specific binding with other endogenous nucleophiles (e.g., glutathione). Addressing these factors requires the optimization of warhead properties beyond empirical screening, as noted by Gai et al. [13].

In view of these challenges, Artificial Intelligence (AI)—encompassing both Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL)—provides a computational framework for the screening of covalent inhibitors. These data-driven models can be utilized to predict molecular activity, selectivity, and binding energetics by integrating high-dimensional features from SMILES sequences, molecular fingerprints, and physicochemical properties [14–17]. In covalent drug design, the binding event involves a two-step process: initial non-covalent pocket recognition followed by covalent bond formation. Computational methods can be applied to evaluate both aspects. While deep learning models are utilized to predict non-covalent binding affinity based on structural complementarity, assessing the subsequent electrophilic reactivity of the warhead typically requires quantum mechanical calculations. To address this, hybrid approaches integrating Quantum Mechanics and Machine Learning (QM/ML) have emerged. For instance, a recent study by Danilack et al. employed a QM/ML framework to simulate the reaction dynamics between acrylamide warheads and cysteine targets, providing a computational approach for predicting the covalent step [18]. This integration of QM calculations with ML models provides a strategy to prioritize candidate molecules with balanced reactivity—sufficiently reactive to engage the target, yet stable enough to minimize off-target interactions.

Herein, we report an integrated in silico and experimental screening framework to identify structurally distinct covalent FGFR4 inhibitors. By coupling AI-driven virtual screening with structural modeling—encompassing hierarchical dual-target covalent docking against both Cys552 and Cys477, alongside 200 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations—we prioritized candidates from the evaluated chemical libraries. To experimentally validate our computational predictions, the selected hits were subjected to a biochemical evaluation pipeline. In vitro kinase assays identified two chemical scaffolds, hit 1 and hit 2, exhibiting dose-dependent FGFR4 inhibition. Subsequent cellular profiling demonstrated that these compounds attenuate the FGFR4/FRS2/ERK1/2 signaling cascade without inducing non-specific protein degradation. Furthermore, bottom-up LC-MS/MS peptide mapping provided structural evidence of target engagement via site-specific covalent adduct formation. Together, these findings demonstrate the practical application of our computational pipeline and provide chemical starting points for the development of targeted therapeutics for HCC.

Materials and methods

Cell culture media, including RPMI-1640 for A549 cells and Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) for Huh7 cells, were purchased from BaseMedia and Gibco (Thermo Fisher Scientific), respectively. Both formulations were supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS; Shuangru Biotech) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin solution (ABM). For routine maintenance and subculturing, cells were washed with Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS; Solarbio) prior to detachment using Trypsin (LANCOSA). Test compounds were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) obtained from MedChemExpress (MCE). Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) sourced from GLPBIO. All sterile cell culture consumables, including 6-well and 96-well plates, were provided by CellPro. Recombinant human FGFR4 protein (biotinylated, sf9-expressed, Avi-tagged) was purchased from MCE. For immunoblotting analysis, the following primary antibodies were utilized: rabbit polyclonal anti-Phospho-FGFR4 (Tyr642) antibody (Cat. No. AF7262) and rabbit polyclonal anti-Phospho-FRS2 (Tyr436) antibody (Cat. No. AF3466) were both purchased from Affinity Biosciences. The anti-FGFR4 antibody (Cat. No. HY-P81843) and anti-Phospho-Erk1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204) antibody (Cat. No. HY-P86225) were acquired from MCE.

Two datasets were curated for analysis: (i) covalent FGFR4 inhibitors and (ii) non-covalent FGFR4 inhibitors. For the covalent dataset, priority was given to compounds bearing electrophilic warheads capable of targeting the critical reactive cysteines within the FGFR4 ATP-binding pocket. For the non-covalent dataset, compounds with verified FGFR4 activity were selected, while pan-FGFR inhibitors (targeting FGFR1–3) were excluded to ensure isoform selectivity. Construction of machine learning models

In this study, established machine learning models were employed to screen for FGFR4 inhibitors. Input features were generated by converting SMILES strings into 2048-bit molecular fingerprints using RDKit. Multiple classification models were evaluated, including Naive Bayes, Support Vector Machine (SVM), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Random Forest (RF), and XGBoost. To further enhance predictive performance and generalizability, a stacked ensemble model was constructed using these base classifiers, with logistic regression serving as the meta-classifier. A 10-fold cross-validation strategy was employed to ensure the robustness and reliability of all models. Model performance was assessed using metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{F}_1$$\end{document} score, ROC-AUC, and Cohen’s Kappa. The model exhibiting the best comprehensive evaluation performance (the stacked ensemble) was subsequently selected to rank the compound library. From this ranked list, the top-scoring compounds (top 20%) were prioritized for downstream structural analysis and candidate selection.

Development of deep learning architectures

The Chemprop architecture, a graph-based deep learning framework, was utilized to predict molecular properties directly from SMILES strings [21]. The model comprises two core components: a Directed Message Passing Neural Network (D-MPNN) for feature extraction and a Feedforward Neural Network (FNN) for property prediction [22]. Initially, Chemprop converts the input SMILES into a molecular graph where atoms and bonds are treated as nodes and edges, respectively. During the message-passing phase, the D-MPNN captures local chemical environments to generate a latent feature vector representing the entire molecule. To maintain structural robustness based on standardized configurations, the depth of the message-passing network was set to 3, and the hidden layer dimension was specified as 300. To capture richer physicochemical information, this learned representation was augmented by concatenating an additional vector of 200 molecular descriptors calculated using RDKit (e.g., partial charges, heavy atom count, and hydrogen bond donors). The combined feature vector is then fed into the FNN module, which maps features to activity predictions through unidirectional information flow. This integration of graph-based feature learning with explicit molecular descriptors aims to ensure a comprehensive molecular representation. During the model training and evaluation phase, the dataset was partitioned into 70% for training, 10% for validation, and 20% for independent testing. The maximum number of training epochs was strictly limited to 30, and model performance was evaluated using accuracy as the primary metric. Optimization was performed without introducing a dropout penalty (dropout = 0.0). Furthermore, the learning rate scheduling followed Chemprop’s default Noam optimizer policy, starting at an initial rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times10^{-4}$$\end{document}, building up to a peak value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times10^{-3}$$\end{document}, and subsequently applying exponential decay across the 30-epoch progression.

Scaffold truncation and fragmentation strategy

For small molecules (MW < 250~Da), the electrophilicity index was directly used to estimate reactivity. However, for larger, lead-like molecules (MW > 250~Da), global reactivity descriptors often fail to accurately reflect local reactivity at the warhead. To address this, the truncation algorithm described by Palazzesi et al. was employed to simplify structures prior to Quantum Mechanics (QM) calculations [23]. The algorithm isolates the reactive center by retaining atoms within a three-bond radius. To maintain chemical integrity, specific rules were applied: if any atom within this radius belongs to a functional group or a ring system (aromatic or non-aromatic), the entire functional group or ring is included in the truncated structure. Substituents on these rings are also retained according to a predefined dictionary. This truncation strategy ensures the correct localization of HOMO/LUMO orbitals on the reactive warhead, allowing for an accurate estimation of its local electrophilicity without interference from the rest of the scaffold. Based on the initial 3D structures generated via the Jaguar module, geometry optimization was performed at the B3LYP/6–31++G(d,p) level of theory, followed by the calculation of ω using Equations (1)–(3). Solvation effects were accounted for using the Conductor-like Polarizable Continuum Model (CPCM) with water as the solvent. For conformational analysis, the Jaguar conformational search tool was employed with an energy window of 10~kcal/mol and a maximum of 200 conformers per compound. The lowest-energy conformation was selected for subsequent QM calculations and the determination of ω. All DFT calculations were carried out within the Schrödinger software suite.

Covalent molecular docking

Covalent docking simulations were performed using the CovDock module within the Schrödinger Suite to explore the potential binding modes of the candidate inhibitors. To ensure robust evaluation, a dual-target docking strategy was employed. The crystal structure of the FGFR4 kinase domain (PDB ID: 6JPJ) was first utilized as the primary receptor for an initial coarse screening, specifically targeting the Cys552 residue. Subsequently, the identified hits from this primary screen were cross-validated through a second round of covalent docking against another FGFR4 crystal structure (PDB ID: 4QQC), targeting the Cys477 residue. This hierarchical approach provided comprehensive structural insights into the covalent binding mechanisms and poses of the candidate inhibitors within the respective FGFR4 binding pockets, thereby validating their target engagement and guiding downstream drug design and optimization.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the Desmond module in the Schrödinger Suite. The system was solvated in an orthorhombic box with a 10 Å buffer distance using the TIP3P water model, and neutralized with 0.15 M NaCl to simulate physiological conditions. The OPLS4 force field was employed to parameterize the protein and ligand. After initial minimization and equilibration, a 200 ns production simulation was conducted in the NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1.01325 bar. Temperature and pressure were maintained using the Nosé-Hoover chain thermostat (τ = 1.0 ps) and Martyna-Tobias-Klein barostat (τ = 2.0 ps), respectively. Trajectory coordinates were recorded every 200 ps for subsequent analysis using the Simulation Interaction Diagram (SID) tool.

Immunoblotting analysis

To evaluate the on-target modulation of the FGFR4 signaling pathway, western blot analyses were performed using the Huh7 hepatocellular carcinoma cell line. Cells were seeded in multi-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. Upon reaching appropriate confluency, the cells were treated with hit 1 or hit 2 at a designated concentration gradient (5, 10, 20, 40, and 80 μM) for 24 hours. A vehicle control containing 0.1% DMSO and a positive control treated with fisogatinib (1 μM) were included in parallel. All experimental conditions were performed in biological triplicates. Following the 24-hour treatment, cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors to preserve protein phosphorylation states. Total protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay. Equivalent amounts of protein lysates were resolved by SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk or bovine serum albumin (BSA) and then incubated overnight at 4 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}^{\circ}$$\end{document}C with specific primary antibodies targeting total FGFR4, phospho-FGFR4 (p-FGFR4), phospho-FRS2 (p-FRS2), phospho-ERK1/2 (p-ERK1/2), and β-Actin, which served as the internal loading control. After rigorous washing, the membranes were incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. The immunoreactive protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system, and the target protein expression levels were evaluated relative to the vehicle control.

LC-MS/MS analysis of covalent sites

To map the covalent binding sites of the hit compounds on FGFR4, high-resolution liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was employed using a bottom-up peptide mapping strategy. Briefly, the protein-inhibitor complexes were denatured, reduced, and alkylated, followed by proteolytic digestion with trypsin at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$37^{\circ}\textrm{C}$$\end{document} overnight. The resulting peptides were desalted, vacuum-dried, and separated utilizing an Easy-nLC 1200 system (Thermo Scientific) equipped with C18 trap and analytical columns. A 60-minute linear gradient of 0.1% formic acid in water and 0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile was applied. The eluted peptides were analyzed on a Q-Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) operating in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode, where the top 20 most intense precursor ions were fragmented via higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD). Raw MS data were processed using MaxQuant software. Trypsin was specified as the cleavage enzyme with up to two missed cleavages allowed. To identify the specific covalent adducts, variable modifications were configured to include the exact mass additions corresponding to the engagement of the respective test compounds onto cysteine residues. The false discovery rate (FDR) for peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs) and modification sites was strictly controlled at < 1%.

High-throughput virtual screening of candidate libraries

A virtual screening campaign was performed on a library of 538,599 compounds utilizing both deep learning (DL) and machine learning (ML) approaches. To prioritize candidates, the models incorporated structural features derived from distinct covalent and non-covalent datasets. Adopting a parallel strategy, the compound library comprising six reactive warhead types was evaluated using both approaches. A prediction score threshold of 0.9 was applied to select the top-ranked compounds. This workflow yielded the following candidate sets: the DL model prioritized 418 covalent candidates, while the ML model independently identified 199 compounds. These results demonstrate the application of both architectures to screen the compound library and isolate candidate chemotypes.

Structure-based screening and binding mode analysis

Molecular characteristics analysis

To assess the drug-like properties of the starting chemical library, the raw compounds were subjected to physicochemical filtering. Density plot analysis and Quantitative Estimate of Drug-likeness (QED) evaluations indicated that the filtered screening ligands exhibited a narrower distribution aligned with typical drug-like chemical space compared to the raw ligands (Supplementary Figures S1 and S2). Specifically, the screening set demonstrated reduced conformational flexibility and higher Lipinski compliance, with QED scores predominantly distributed in the 0.7–0.9 range. This preprocessing step enriched the library with scaffolds possessing predicted oral bioavailability and membrane permeability, providing a starting point for subsequent molecular docking and AI-based prioritization.

From this substructure library, the top 20 candidate scaffolds were ranked by reactivity indices and subjected to a final round of molecular docking to identify candidate compounds with predicted reactivity and binding complementarity.

In parallel simulations targeting the Cys552 site (Fig. 4B), the compounds exhibited binding poses within the hydrophobic cleft, with their electrophilic warheads positioned within the reaction distance of the Cys552 nucleophile. These computational models tend to emphasize polar contacts in a static conformation, often underrepresenting desolvation penalties and the conformational entropy of the covalent reaction. Therefore, while docking models evaluate the theoretical spatial positioning of the warhead, these static energy scores are insufficient as the sole criterion for efficacy. This observation supports the application of time-resolved molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and empirical biological validation to assess structural stability and target engagement.

However, we note the limitations of these computational methods. Standard MD simulations evaluate the structural compatibility of the final post-reaction adduct; they do not account for kinetic reaction barriers, cellular membrane permeability, or complex physiological microenvironments. Cellular FGFR4 pathway inhibition

To evaluate the cellular target engagement and downstream effects of the candidate compounds, we analyzed the FGFR4-mediated signal transduction cascade in FGFR4-dependent cells. Immunoblot analysis indicated that treatment with hit 1 and hit 2 reduced the levels of phosphorylated FGFR4 (p-FGFR4/β-actin). This inhibition extended downstream through the signaling axis, as evidenced by a statistically significant reduction in the phosphorylation of the adaptor protein FRS2 and the effector kinase ERK1/2 (Fig. 10). The total protein abundance of FGFR4 did not change significantly across the treatment groups (p > 0.05), suggesting that the observed effects stem from the modulation of kinase signaling rather than non-specific protein degradation. Conclusion

This research provides a computational-to-experimental framework for the discovery of covalent FGFR4 inhibitors, integrating machine learning algorithms with structural analysis and biological validation. We performed a series of in vitro evaluations, including enzymatic kinase assays, hepatocellular carcinoma cell viability assays, intracellular signal transduction immunoblotting, and NCM460 cytotoxicity profiling. These experiments characterized hit 1 and hit 2 as active candidates with measurable safety margins.

Furthermore, bottom-up LC-MS/MS peptide mapping established that these scaffolds covalently modify the conserved Cys477 residue, providing physical evidence of natural target engagement at this site. While computational models indicate a theoretical capacity for interaction with Cys552, the empirical data confirm Cys477 as the primary covalent anchor in vitro. Future structural studies utilizing site-directed mutagenesis (e.g., C477A) will be necessary to experimentally determine whether these compounds can engage Cys552 when the primary site is inaccessible [26].

The discrepancies between theoretical structural stability and empirical cellular activity demonstrate that cellular inhibition is influenced by kinetic reaction barriers and membrane permeability, factors not fully captured by standard MD simulations. Future optimization will focus on fine-tuning the lipophilicity of hit 1 to enhance cellular uptake and refining non-covalent interactions to improve selectivity. Ultimately, hit 1 and hit 2 represent structurally characterized covalent scaffolds, providing validated chemical starting points for the development of targeted therapeutics in FGFR4-driven malignancies.
