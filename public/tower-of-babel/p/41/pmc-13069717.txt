Effect of liner geometry on jumping distance: as relevant as head size
Authors: Mikko Pulkkanen, José Á Ochoa, Aleksi Reito, Antti Eskelinen, Jari Viik, Jari Hyttinen, Perttu Neuvonen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDislocation is a common complication after total hip arthroplasty (THA). Previously, we found that different liner models provided varying degrees of articulating head coverage. Jumping distance (JD) is a key indicator of joint stability. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the differences in JD values among various liner models.MethodsJD values were determined using previously published articulating head coverage data for 25 modular polyethylene liners used in 6 uncemented cup designs from 4 major manufacturers using a mathematical model. For each liner, the average values corresponding to each femoral head size within each cup size were used in the analysis.ResultsWithin the safe zone (5–25° anteversion and 30–50° inclination), average JD values ranged from 10.79 to 17.29 mm, depending on cup positioning, liner design, and femoral head size. On average, enlarging the femoral head size from 28 to 36 mm resulted in a 3.53 mm increase in JD within the safe zone. The JD variation between different liner designs was of the same magnitude as the effect of increasing the femoral head size from 28 to 36 mm: 3.16 mm with 32 mm and 3.32 mm with 36 mm femoral heads. The JD of the liner with the lowest coverage combined with a 36 mm femoral head was lower than that of the liner providing the greatest coverage, even when coupled with a 28 mm femoral head.ConclusionsLiner design can influence JD to a degree comparable to increasing femoral head size from 28 to 36 mm. Among the evaluated designs, liners providing greater coverage, such as the X3, yielded higher JD values for the same femoral head size. As optimal joint stability cannot be ensured solely by selecting a larger head size, liner geometry should be carefully considered alongside head size in THA component selection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09688-y.

Introduction

The success of total hip arthroplasty (THA) has resulted in it becoming one of the most common elective surgical interventions worldwide [1, 2]. The use of highly cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE) in acetabular liners has enhanced the durability of bearing surfaces and enabled the use of larger femoral heads to increase prosthesis stability [3, 4]. Along with the widespread use of XLPE liners, a marked reduction in revision rates after THA has been observed [5]. Even though wear-related problems have become rare, dislocation rates of between 1.2 and 5.1% are still reported, with dislocation being one of the most common reasons for revision surgery [4, 6, 7]. It has been argued that the drop in dislocation rates has plateaued and the full benefits of using ≥ 32 mm femoral head sizes have now been realized [4].

The effect of femoral head size on the clinical results of THA has been studied widely. However, the theoretical stability of a hip prosthesis is determined not only by the femoral head size, but also by the geometry of the articulating surface of the acetabular liner, as they both affect jumping distance (JD). JD is a measurement that represents the lateral translation of the femoral head center on the liner to generate a dislocation [8]. Thus, the larger the JD, the greater the theoretical resistance to dislocation for a given implant geometry.

Previously, we measured the geometry of 25 neutral polyethylene liners (PE) from four major manufacturers in a metrology laboratory, where the liner coverage of the articulating surfaces in contact with the femoral head were calculated [9]. We observed that each of the liner designs classified as neutral offered a distinct liner coverage, that predicted different kinds of stability properties for each of the designs. The aim of this study was to address the following questions: (1) What are the average JD values for each liner design positioned within Lewinnek’s safe zone, and (2) how do these values vary across different liner designs and femoral head sizes?

Materials and methods

Implant selection

We evaluated 25 neutral PE liners from four different manufacturers. For the evaluation, X3 liners for the Trident and Trident II cup systems (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA), R3 XLPE liners for the R3 cup system (Smith & Nephew, Watford, England), Marathon liners for the Pinnacle cup system (DePuy Synthes, Raynham, MA, USA), E1 liners for the G7 cup system, and Longevity liners for the Continuum cup system (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, In, USA) were selected to represent the liners of the cementless porous-coated modular cup systems currently marketed for primary THA.

The liners for three different cup sizes (46 mm, 52 mm, and 58 mm) were measured. Liners for 32 mm and 36 mm head sizes were evaluated in cup sizes of 52 mm and 58 mm. As there is variation between brands with head sizes available in a cup size of 46 mm, the largest head size available was chosen (28 mm for the Marathon, R3, and Longevity, and 32 mm for the X3 and E1). For the Pinnacle (ALTRX) and G7 (ArCom, ArComXL, Vivacit-E) cup systems, liners made of alternate PE compounds were available in addition to Marathon and E1. We included one ALTRX and one ArComXL liner in the study to be compared to their doublets. The ALTRX liner was measured in size 52/36 mm, as there is no Marathon liner available in that size. The ArComXL liner was measured in size 58/32 mm.

Study design

The geometry of the liners was measured using a Coordinate Measuring Machine (CMM, Metrology Laboratory Services, Tampere University, Tampere, Finland). The methodology and description of the process has been described in Ochoa et al. [9]. Measurements were performed in a licensed metrology laboratory under controlled conditions using a high-precision CMM. Each feature was measured twice, and a mean surface was generated; if any point deviated more than 4 μm from the fitted geometry, the measurement was repeated until the maximum deviation was below 4 μm. The reported maximum deviation was 2 μm. Only one liner per size and design was measured. As a result, the reported values describe design-specific geometric characteristics and do not allow estimation of variability or standard deviations across multiple devices of the same design.

The JD values in a fixed cup position (45° abduction, 15° anteversion) and inside the “safe zone” (cup inclination of 40° ± 10° and anteversion of 15° ± 10°) and beyond the safe zone positioning (cup inclination of 20–70° and anteversion 0–45°) were computed.

In addition, the average JD values within the safe zone were calculated for a theoretical neutral liner with completely hemispherical coverage (180°). These values were computed for 36 mm and 28 mm femoral heads for comparison with the study liners.

Statistical analysis

Measurement uncertainty was quantified from repeated measurements, with the maximum observed difference of 2 μm corresponding to +/- 1 μm uncertainty. The smallest reported difference (0.02 mm) exceeded measurement variability by an order of magnitude (signal-to-noise ratio ≈ 10). As the present study is based on high-precision geometric measurements rather than sampling from a biological population, conventional hypothesis testing and p-value -based statistical methods were not applied. Instead, results were evaluated according to principles of measurement uncertainty and error analysis.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was not required for this study because it did not involve human participants or identifiable patient data.

Results

The results of the empirical measurements are presented in an additional file [see Additional file 1]. For acetabular sizes of 58 mm and 52 mm, we used average geometrical values based on femoral head sizes for each liner in the JD calculations, as the inner surface for the femoral head was the same in diameter and design, and empirical measurements yielded very similar data. The JD of the evaluated liners varied from 8.25 to 20.35 mm inside the safe zone, depending on the positioning of the cup, the liner model, and the femoral head size. On average, enlarging the femoral head size from 28 to 36 mm resulted in a 3.53 mm (range 3.18–4.03 mm) increase in JD. The JD variation between different liner designs with 32 mm (3.16 mm) and 36 mm (3.32 mm) femoral heads was of the same magnitude as the effect of increasing femoral head size when average values within the safe zone were evaluated. The average JD inside the safe zone for all liner models and head sizes was 14 mm, ranging from 10.79 (Continuum, 28 mm femoral head) to 17.29 mm (X3, 36 mm femoral head). The average JD values were 15.59 mm for 36 mm femoral heads, 13.83 mm for 32 mm femoral heads, and 11.65 mm for 28 mm femoral heads. The calculated average JD values of a theoretical completely hemispherical liner were 15.51 mm with a 36 mm femoral head and 12.06 mm with a 28 mm femoral head. The calculated values were subsequently used to assess the differences in JD values for different liners.

To assess the range of JD values when using these implants, we compared the liners in the study to a theoretical liner with 180° coverage. The probability of achieving a better JD than an average value inside the safe zone of a 180° liner using a 36 mm femoral head (15.51 mm) was 80.7% with X3, 66.9% with Pinnacle, 44.0% with G7, 42.6% with R3, and 24.7% with Continuum (Fig. 3). When comparing liners with a femoral head size 32 mm to the same value (15.51 mm), the probabilities were 53.1% for X3, 27.0% for Pinnacle, 9.5% for G7, and 9.5% for R3 (Fig. 4). Continuum in 32 mm femoral head size and all the liners with femoral head sizes of 28 mm femoral head failed to achieve the JD of 15.51 mm with any positioning, so they can be compared to the average JD inside the safe zone of a 180° liner using 28 mm femoral head (12.06 mm). The probability of achieving a worse JD than 12.06 mm was 41.7% for Continuum with a 32 mm femoral head. The probabilities of achieving worse JD than 12.06 mm with 28 mm femoral heads were 40.8% for Pinnacle, 57.4% for R3, and 77.1% for Continuum (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this study we presented the JD values for 25 neutral polyethylene liners. Our results revealed a substantial range in the JD properties of these contemporary THA liners. The average JD values inside the safe zone varied from 10.79 to 17.29 mm, whereas the overall variation in the safe zone ranged from 8.25 to 20.35 mm, depending on cup positioning, liner design, and femoral head size (Additional file 1). For hip surgeons seeking higher stability in THA, it is a common practice to enlarge the femoral head size. When comparing the average JDs in safe zone positioning, enlarging the femoral head size from 28 to 36 mm results in an increase in JD of between 3.18 mm (Continuum) and 4.03 mm (Pinnacle). A novel finding of our study was that JD variation between different liner designs with 32 mm (3.16 mm) and 36 mm (3.32 mm) femoral head sizes was of the same magnitude as the effect of increasing the femoral head size (Additional file 1). Further, in a fixed cup position, the JD of a liner with the smallest coverage (Continuum Longevity) with a 36 mm femoral head was smaller than that of a liner with the largest coverage (X3), even when a 28 mm femoral head was used (Fig. 2). The results of this study clearly demonstrate the strong relationship between the liner coverage design and the JD of a hip prosthesis. Thus, the use of larger femoral heads may give surgeons a false sense of stability when the design of a liner is not known. Instead, changing a liner to one which offers more coverage might be a more practical way to improve JD and thus also the stability of the hip prosthesis. Among the evaluated designs, liners providing greater effective coverage, such as the X3, yielded higher JD values for the same femoral head size.

The FHO correlates with the coverage of a liner through the geometrical relationship. The difference between the direct liner coverage and FHO is that FHO is also affected by femoral head size. FHO increases with increasing head size if the coverage remains constant [9]. Scifert et al. found that insetting the head center improved the peak resisting moment to dislocation by 5.8% per millimeter of inset [10]. Sariali et al. found that FHO is also the geometrical factor that has the highest influence on JD, a finding reflected in our results too. Indeed, a 1 mm increase in FHO decreases JD by 0.92 mm, for a given femoral head size [8]. Interestingly, liners with higher FHO—and consequently lower JD properties—have been associated with an increased incidence of dislocation complications in both clinical series and a retrieval study [6, 11, 12]. As early as 1993, Brien et al. observed a lower dislocation rate when using 22-mm femoral heads in combination with a liner extending beyond a hemispherical geometry and providing a greater jumping distance, compared with a hemispherical liner [12]. The Continuum Longevity liner had the lowest JD in this study. At a recent study from our institution, the 1-year dislocation rate for the Continuum Longevity neutral liner was 5.1%, whereas for the Pinnacle neutral liner it was 1.3% [6]. Dislocation rates stratified by liner type have not been reported for the other cup systems assessed in this study. According to the Australian Orthopaedic Association National Joint Replacement Registry, the Continuum has shown higher revision rates due to dislocation than other total hip replacements, and dislocation is also the most common reason for revision of the Continuum cup system [13]. The present study provides a comparative geometric assessment, and these findings support the clinical relevance of liner geometry and jumping distance.

The advantage of higher stability gained by insetting the femoral head may be compromised, as the higher coverage of a liner may reduce the range of motion (ROM) of a hip joint by causing impingement [10]. However, the association between component-on-component impingement and dislocation remains unclear. Some retrieval studies have reported an association between dislocation and impingement damage, whereas others have not [11, 14–16]. In all of these studies, dislocations were also reported without any signs of component-on-component impingement. Further, in a study by Waddel et al. [16] elevated liners were shown to have more frequent impingement and more severe impingement damage while having a lower rate of revision due to dislocations. Tanino, et al. [11] found that liners with impingement damage did not have a deeper femoral head inset, but liners with dislocation history had a shallower head center inset.

ROM can be enhanced and the risk of impingement reduced by using larger femoral heads and enlarging the head-to-neck ratio [10, 16, 17]. Larger femoral heads seem to decrease the incidence of component-on-component impingement [16]. In addition, enlarging a femoral head also increases peak resisting moment to dislocation and JD, although the proportional change per millimeter is less when compared to that of FHO [8, 10, 17]. Change in JD is also highly dependent on cup abduction angle and decreases as the abduction angle increases. Therefore, the advantage in JD gained using larger femoral heads may be lost with high abduction angles [8]. In summary, dislocation is a multifactorial event and other reasons such as bone-on-bone impingement, soft tissue impingement, and soft tissue tension, in addition to component-on-component impingement may play a major role in a dislocation event [18–20].

We acknowledge that our study has some limitations. Only liners classified as neutral were evaluated. Elevated-rim and face-changing liners were not included in this study, despite their frequent use in patients at higher risk of instability. As their asymmetric geometry introduces additional design variables, their jumping distance characteristics warrant separate investigation. The setting of our study exposes our results to manufacturing random outliers variation, which has been discussed in more detail previously [9]. We are unaware of any studies that have systematically tested the clinical relevance of JD and thus, it remains a theoretical value. While earlier studies have associated implants with an intrinsic tendency to lower JDs to dislocation complications, actual target values for optimal JD remain to be defined [6, 11]. It can be considered as a limitation that ROM measurements of the liners were not included in the study. However, limited ROM is not a common clinical problem after THA, whereas dislocation is. As stated previously, dislocation following THA is a multifactorial event and the relation between component-on-component impingement and dislocation is controversial based on current literature. We investigated JD using a vertical force vector as it corresponds to each liners’ JD as it was measured from an X-ray taken in the standing position. We did not aim to identify positions associated with dislocation risk. However, the direction of the force vector is irrelevant, as it only affects the absolute values, while the gradient and relative changes remain the same regardless of whether the measurements are referenced to the vertical line or to the neck-directed vector.

The independence of our study can be considered as a strength. The implant manufacturers received no financial benefit from the study as the implants were purchased by our hospital as they would have been in daily practice. To our understanding, this is the first study to report JD comparison of actual THA implants. The aim of this study was to assess the inherent stability between different liner designs and this chosen method for measuring JD allows determination of the magnitude of the differences. Such research has not been done before, as the previous literature covers studies that have simulated the dislocation event and reported the JD values in component-on-component impingement situations [21]. Because simulating a dislocation event is difficult due to its multifactorial nature, we consider it more reasonable to compare JD values that reflect the intrinsic stability of the components.

In contemporary neutral liners positioned within Lewinnek’s safe zone, we found marked variation in JD values, indicating that femoral head size alone does not adequately reflect theoretical stability. Incorporating liner coverage data alongside head size offers a more accurate description of THA design characteristics and may help identify strategies to further reduce dislocation rates.
