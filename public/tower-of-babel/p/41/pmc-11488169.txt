Sulforaphane regulates AngII-induced podocyte oxidative stress injury through the Nrf2-Keap1/ho-1/ROS pathway
Authors: Wen Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the therapeutic effects of sulforaphane and the role of the Nrf2-Keap1/HO-1/ROS pathway in AngII-induced oxidative stress in podocyte injury.MethodsMouse mpc5 podocytes were divided into four groups: control (Con), AngII, AngII + sulforaphane (AngII + SFN), and control + sulforaphane (Con + SFN). Western blotting was used to detect protein expression of Nrf2-Keap1, antioxidant enzyme HO-1, and apoptosis-related proteins. ROS levels were measured using a ROS assay kit, and cell survival and viability were assayed using the CCK-8 kit. Molecular interactions between Nrf2 and sulforaphane were analyzed computationally.ResultsCompared with the Con group, podocytes treated with AngII alone exhibited inhibited proliferation, reduced cell viability, lower Bcl-2 expression, and higher cleaved caspase 3 expression. In the presence of sulforaphane, AngII group showed a mild inhibition on podocyte proliferation but did not induce the aforementioned changes in Bcl-2 and cleaved caspase 3 expression. Similarly, compared to the Con group, AngII treatment alone had lower Nrf2 expression and higher Keap1 expression in podocytes, accompanied by a significant decrease in ROS content. However, in the presence of sulforaphane, AngII failed to induce increases in Nrf2 and a decrease in Keap1 expression, as well as ROS levels. Furthermore, cells treated with sulforaphane exhibited higher HO-1 levels than control cells, and co-incubation with AngII did not alter HO-1 levels. Computational modeling revealed hydrophobic interactions between sulforaphane and the amino acid LYS-462 of Nrf2, as well as hydrogen bonding with amino acid HIS-465. The binding score between sulforaphane and Nrf2 was −4.7.ConclusionSulforaphane alleviated AngII-induced podocyte oxidative stress injury via the Nrf2-Keap1/HO-1/ROS pathway, providing new insights into therapeutic compounds for mitigating chronic kidney disease.

Introduction

The escalating prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) is emerging as a global concern. With approximately 10% of the world’s population affected by this condition and projections indicating that CKD will become the fifth leading cause of death worldwide by 2040 [1], urgent action is imperative to mitigate the escalating incidence. One of the current therapeutic aims in managing renal damage is to decrease both systemic and local glomerular pressure through modulation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS). Blocking this system can help slow the progression of glomerular disease. In numerous kidney disease scenarios, inhibiting AngII is beneficial. Podocytes, being the terminally differentiated epithelial cells of the glomerular plexus, play a pivotal role in maintaining the integrity of the glomerular filtration membrane. Once podocyte loss exceeds 20%, irreversible kidney tissue damage can occur, ultimately leading to glomerular scarring and end-stage renal disease if timely intervention and treatment are not administered.

Sulforaphane, a naturally occurring isothiocyanate extracted from broccoli buds, exhibits diverse pharmacological activities, including antioxidant, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective effects. Some studies [2–5] have demonstrated that sulforaphane can mitigate tissue damage by activating the Nrf2 pathway. As an Nrf2 activator, sulforaphane can indirectly exert antioxidant functions. Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), a transcription factor, plays a pivotal role in mediating redox reactions. In a physiological state, Nrf2 is degraded by Keap1, keeping its levels low. However, in a pathological state, Nrf2 degradation is inhibited, leading to its translocation to the nucleus and activation of target gene transcription. This activation results in the upregulation of antioxidant cytoprotective enzymes, making Nrf2 a critical regulator of cell and organ function [6,7]. Nrf2 signaling has emerged as a promising therapeutic target for various diseases. However, current research on Nrf2 remains controversial. For instance, studies have demonstrated that activating Nrf2 can attenuate Ang II-induced apoptosis in vascular smooth muscle cells, and suppressing Nrf2 expression through siNrf2 reverses this protective effect [8]. Conversely, other studies have shown that Nrf2 activation exacerbates glomerular injury, characterized by podocyte depletion, loss of foot processes, increased interstitial fibrosis, and worsening proteinuria. Genetic knockout of Nrf2 mitigates the detrimental effects of Ang II on proteinuria [9]. These findings highlight the complex and multifaceted nature of Nrf2’s role in disease pathophysiology, necessitating further investigation to fully elucidate its therapeutic potential. In this study, we aimed to investigate the modulatory effect of sulforaphane on AngII-induced podocyte injury and its underlying molecular mechanisms. We found that AngII can inhibit Nrf2 expression and increased podocyte oxidative stress. The application of sulforaphane significantly alleviated podocyte oxidative stress in an Nrf2-dependent manner. Our data provide important experimental evidence that sulforaphane can be an effective compound for the treatment of AngII-dependent renal injury.

Materials and methods

The immortalized mouse podocyte cell line, MPC-5, was kindly donated by Professor Ding Guohua from the Department of Nephrology at Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University. Antibodies specific to Nrf2 (T55136), Keap1, HO-1, bcl-2, caspase3, and β-actin were procured from Abmart. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection kits were obtained from Beyontime (S0033S). Sulforaphane was sourced from MCE (HY-13755), the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was purchased from Biosharp, and the electrophoresis gel imaging analysis system was imported from the United States (Bio-Rad).

Cell culture

Podocytes were retrieved from liquid nitrogen and, following standard resuscitation procedures, were inoculated into 1640 medium (consisting of Type I collagen and DMEM/F12, supplemented with 10% FBS, 10 IU/ml gamma interferon, 100 u/ml penicillin, and 100 mg/L streptomycin). These podocytes were then placed in a cell incubator maintained at 33 °C, with saturated humidity, and 5% carbon dioxide. After a three-day incubation, the podocytes were transferred to 1640 medium devoid of gamma interferon and cultured for an additional 10–14 days to allow for differentiation and maturation. Subsequently, the podocytes were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of approximately 2 × 10^5 cells per well. Once the podocytes reached 70%-80% confluence, they were starved in serum-free medium for 24 h to synchronize their growth and prepare them for experimentation.

To assess the effects of AngII and sulforaphane, podocytes were stimulated with varying concentrations of AngII (0, 0.5 μM, 1 μM, 2 μM) and sulforaphane(0 μM, 1 μM, 2 μM, 4μM, 8 μM) for 24 h. Cell viability was then evaluated by adding 20 μl of CCK-8 reagent, a common reagent used for cell viability assessment, to each well and incubating for 1.5 h. Absorbance readings were measured, and the cell survival rate was calculated. The AngII concentration that resulted in a significantly lower survival rate compared to the control group was selected for subsequent experiments. The maximum sulforaphane concentration with no statistically significant difference in survival rate compared with the control group was selected for follow-up experiments.

The experimental setup consisted of four groups: Control group (Con), AngII group (AngII), AngII + sulforaphane group (AngII + SFN), and control + sulforaphane group (Con + SFN). The specific treatments were as follows: Control group (Con): Cells in the Con group were cultured in DMSO-containing 1640 medium (0.02% DMSO) without any additional treatment. Control + sulforaphane group (Con + SFN): Podocytes in the control medium were supplemented with 4 μM sulforaphane. AngII group (AngII): A pre-determined dose of AngII was added to the culture medium of podocytes and incubated for 24 h. AngII + sulforaphane group (AngII + SFN): Both a pre-determined dose of AngII and 4 μM sulforaphane were added to the culture medium of podocytes and incubated for 24 h. Subsequent experiments were conducted following the completion of these culture conditions. For the cellular tissue samples, there were 2 internal repetitions and 3 biological repetitions, with each biological repetition involving 3 biological samples.

Measurement of oxidative stress levels

The levels of oxidative stress were meticulously quantified through a rigorous protocol. Initially, the tissues from each group were precisely weighed and homogenized in PBS (100 mmol/L) at a mass-to-volume ratio of 1:20. Subsequently, the homogenate was centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min, and the supernatant was carefully collected for further analysis. The supernatant was then combined with a fluorescence probe working solution prepared at a ratio of 10uM DCFH-DA to PBS (1:1000). This mixture, along with PBS, was thoroughly blended and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The fluorescence intensity was measured at an optimal excitation wavelength of 500 ± 15 nm and an emission wavelength of 530 ± 20 nm, and the results were expressed as arbitrary units (AU).

Cell viability assessment

To assess cell proliferation and viability, a cell suspension was inoculated into 96 culture plates, with a density of over 1000 cells per well. These cells were then cultured in DMEM medium containing no fetal bovine serum for 24 h to synchronize their growth cycles. Subsequently, 10ul of CCK8 reagent, a commonly used reagent for cell viability assays, was added to each well, thoroughly mixed, and incubated in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C for 1 h. Finally, the OD value at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to quantify cell proliferation and viability.

Western blotting

The podocytes that had been treated were processed and boiled, and 50 micrograms of protein were extracted from each sample. Subsequently, the protein samples were subjected to electrophoresis under constant voltage conditions, initially at 90 V for the concentration gel. Once the transition to the separation gel was reached, the voltage was adjusted to 110 V. The membrane transfer occurred at a specific voltage range for a duration of one hour. Following this, the PVDF membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk powder dissolved in TBS-T at 37 °C for a period of two hours.

Molecular docking simulation

The protein crystal structure utilized for the docking process was retrieved from the PDB database, whereas the three-dimensional structure of the small molecule was sourced from PubChem. Prior to docking, the energy of the small molecule was minimized using the MMFF94 force field. In this investigation, AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 software served as the tool for executing molecular docking simulations. Prior to docking, PyMol 2.5.2 was employed to preprocess the receptor proteins, encompassing the elimination of water molecules, salt ions, and other small molecules. Subsequently, a docking box was established to encompass the entire receptor protein, ensuring thorough exploration during the docking process.

Furthermore, ADFRsuite 1.0 was utilized to convert both the preprocessed small molecules and receptor proteins into the PDBQT format, which is prerequisite for AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 docking. For the docking calculations, the global search detail was set to 32, while all other parameters were maintained at their default settings. The docking conformation exhibiting the highest output score was deemed as the optimal binding configuration. Lastly, the docking results were visualized using PyMol 2.5.2, facilitating a comprehensive analysis of the intermolecular interactions and binding poses.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using SPSS 13.0 software (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL). To compare across multiple groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, and statistical significance was determined at a p value of < 0.05 (two-tailed). A post-hoc test was performed using the Tukey method in the Methods section. When the variances were unequal, the Games-Howell test was adopted for pairwise comparisons.

Result

Sulforaphane ameliorates AngII-induced podocyte apoptosis and injury

We established a concentration gradient of AngII ranging from 0 μM, 0.5 μM, 1 μM, to 2 μM. CCK-8 assay results indicated a significant decrease in cell survival rates at an AngII concentration of 0.5 μM. Specifically, compared to the Con group, which exhibited a survival rate of 100 ± 0.68, the 0.5 μM, 1 μM, and 2 μM groups exhibited significantly lower survival rates of 78 ± 0.88 (p = 3.14 × 10−9), 75 ± 1.00 (p = 1.15 × 10−9), and 74 ± 0.51 (p = 9.78 × 10−10), respectively. These findings are graphically represented in Figure 1a. For the subsequent experiment, we selected a concentration of 1 μM AngII. We used sulforaphane at concentrations of 0 μm, 1 μm, 2 μm, 4 μm, and 8 μm for intervention. When sulforaphane was at 8 μM, cell proliferation was significantly inhibited, with statistically differences. However, at 4 μm, there were no statistical differences in cell proliferation. (sulforaphane (0 μM):100.00 ± 2.52 vs. sulforaphane (4 μM):95.97 ± 2.89, p = 0.469; sulforaphane (0 μM): 100.00 ± 2.52 vs. sulforaphane (8 μM):81.86 ± 1.63, p = 0.004, Figure 1b.). Since using 4 μm sulforaphane did not introduce interference, we selected this concentration for our experiments. The CCK cell viability assay demonstrated that, in the absence of sulforaphane, the AngII group inhibited cell proliferation compared to the control group (Con: 1.21 ± 0.06 vs. AngII: 0.89 ± 0.03, p = 0.001). Cells treated with sulfloraphane alone had a similar cell proliferation activity as the control cells (Con: 1.21 ± 0.06 vs. Con + SFN: 1.26 ± 0.10, p = 0.798). When sulforaphane was present, AngII still inhibited cell proliferation, albeit to a lesser extent than in its absence (Con + SFN: 1.26 ± 0.10 vs. AngII + SFN: 1.06 ± 0.04, p = 0.025). Podocytes treated with sulforaphane exhibit decreased oxidative stress induced by AngII

To assess the oxidative stress level, the ROS fluorescence kit was used to quantify the ROS content. The findings revealed that AngII significantly elevated the oxidative stress level compared to the Con group (Con: 3053.67 ± 193.1 AU vs. AngII: 3692.33 ± 85.70 AU, p = 0.007). The treatment with sulforaphane alone, when compared to control cells, did not reach statistical difference in comparison (Control: 3053.67 ± 193.1 AU vs. Control + SFN: 2636 ± 107.62 AU, p = 0.063). In the presence of sulforaphane, the effect of AngII was completely inhibited (Con + SFN group: 2636 ± 107.62 AU vs. AngII + SFN group: 2713 ± 238.53 AU, p = 0.941). ROS levels were not different between cells treated with sulfloraphane alone or those that were cotreated with both sulfloraphane and AngII, indicating that sulfloraphane ameliorated the effect of AngII, as depicted in Figure 3

Sulforaphane up-regulates the expression of Nrf2 and its downstream expression of antioxidant enzymes

The expression of Nrf2-Keap1 and its downstream antioxidant enzymes was evaluated by western blotting. Compared with the Con group, the Con + SFN group showed no statistically difference in the expression of Nrf2 (Con group: 1.00 ± 0.05 vs. Con + SFN group: 0.84 ± 0.44, p = 0.115) and Keap1 (Con group: 1.00 ± 0.68 vs. Con + SFN group: 0.94 ± 0.19, p = 0.967). Compared with the control group, AngII treatment inhibited the expression of Nrf2 (Con: 1.00 ± 0.05 vs. AngII: 0.59 ± 0.11, p = 0.001) and augmented the expression of Keap1 (Con: 1.00 ± 0.07 vs. AngII: 1.59 ± 0.19, p = 0.012). Compared with the control group, AngII did not induce a change in the expression of the antioxidant enzyme HO-1 (Con: 1.00 ± 0.24 vs. AngII: 1.29 ± 0.29, p = 0.945). Cells treated with sulforaphane had higher levels of HO-1 than the control cells (Con + SFN: 9.05 ± 1.19 vs. Con: 1.00 ± 0.24, p = 0.000002) and co-incubation with AngII did not alter HO-1 levels (Con + SFN: 9.05 ± 1.19 vs. AngII + SFN: 8.00 ± 0.35, p = 0.273), as shown in Figure 4

Computer simulation of molecular docking

We conducted a computer simulation of molecular docking to mimic the putative recognition process occurring between sulforaphane and Nrf2. The outcomes of the computational model revealed that sulforaphane exhibited hydrophobic binding affinity with the Nrf2 amino acid LYS-462, establishing a hydrogen bond with amino acid HIS-465. Furthermore, the binding score between sulforaphane and Nrf2 was determined to be −4.7. Illustrative images are presented in Figure 5

Discussion

AngII is one of the significant inducers of podocyte injury. Multiple studies have found that oxidative stress is one of the important factors contributing to podocyte injury. Specifically, the imbalance between oxidants and antioxidants, the increased production of ROS, and the reduced function of antioxidant enzymes contribute to the occurrence and progression of podocyte injury. Biomarkers of oxidative stress can be used to assess the severity of podocyte injury and treatment outcomes. Previous studies have shown that sulforaphane possesses antioxidant, anti-tumor, anti-angiogenic, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial properties [10]. However, its role in AngII-related renal injury remains unclear. Our research confirms that AngII can increase the production of ROS, exacerbate oxidative stress, inhibit podocyte proliferation, and induce podocyte apoptosis. When cells are treated with sulforaphane alone, the level of ROS showed no statistically significant difference compared to the control group, which suggests that under physiological conditions, sulforaphane does not alter the equilibrium state of oxidative stress in the body. When sulforaphane is co-incubated, AngII fails to induce an increase in ROS levels, indicating that sulforaphane effectively counteracts AngII-induced changes in Bcl-2 as well as cleaved caspase 3 and oxidative stress, thereby demonstrating the anti-apoptotic and antioxidant properties of sulforaphane. Furthermore, when sulforaphane is treated alone in control cells, no significant changes in apoptosis-related protein expression are observed, suggesting that sulforaphane itself does not cause damage to podocytes, thereby excluding the possible interference of sulforaphane on the experimental results. This finding is consistent with previous studies [11–17] on the therapeutic effects of sulforaphane on diabetic nephropathy and other chronic kidney diseases.

To explore the molecular mechanism of sulforaphane-mediated antioxidant protection, we investigated the expression of Nrf2 and its downstream molecular pathways. Our study showed that AngII significantly reduced Nrf2 protein expression in podocytes and increased Keap1 expression. However, under co-incubation with sulforaphane, AngII failed to induce alterations in the expression of Nrf2 and Keap1, suggesting that the presence of sulforaphane can block the effect of AngII on the Nrf2/Keap1 signaling pathway. When sulforaphane is treated alone in control cells, no significant changes in Nrf2/Keap1 expression are observed, indicating that sulforaphane itself does not exert an effect on the expression of the Nrf2/Keap1 pathway under physiological conditions. The Nrf2/Keap1 pathway possesses antioxidant properties and plays a crucial role in maintaining renal tissue homeostasis. Studies [13,18]have reported that Nrf2 activation can effectively defend against renal toxicity caused by different etiologies. Yi Zuo et al. [19]discovered that overexpression of Nrf2 exhibits protective effects against podocyte damage induced by high glucose, mitigating podocyte pyroptosis and reactive oxygen species levels. Yanqin Fan et al. [20]reported that Nrf2 activation reduced podocyte apoptosis and DNA damage induced by AngII. The protective effect of sulforaphane on podocytes is still not fully understood, and it may be associated with improved mitochondrial function and enhanced podocyte autophagy [11]. Some studies also suggest that the enhancement of oxidative stress may exacerbate endoplasmic reticulum stress, and Nrf2 may interact with endoplasmic reticulum stress as a compensatory mechanism to enhance antioxidant defense [21,22]. The activated Nrf2, which positively regulates autophagy, reduces the accumulation of ubiquitin proteins, alleviates podocyte apoptosis, and thus maintains cellular homeostasis [23,24]. The protective effect of sulforaphane seems to be dependent on Nrf2, as a study [25] found that Nrf2-deficient mice exhibited an increase in the severity of acute kidney injury compared to wild-type mice. Compared with Nrf2+/+ mice, Nrf2−/− mice experience more severe oxidative stress, apoptosis, and fibrotic change in acute kidney injury [26]. This finding supports our hypothesis that Nrf2 plays a protective role in the pathogenesis of AngII-induced podocyte injury, and sulforaphane acts through Nrf2-related pathways.

HO-1, a representative Nrf2 downstream protein, was selected for this study. HO-1 catalyzes the decomposition of free hemoglobin, releasing free iron, CO, and biliverdin, which exhibit anti-apoptotic properties [27]. Evidence has accumulated suggesting that HO-1 is involved in the regulation of podocyte apoptosis [28–32]. Early induction of HO-1 expression can prevent AngII-induced renal injury [33]. Our study revealed that treatment of podocytes with AngII alone did not induce an increase or decrease in HO-1 expression. Regardless of whether in control cells or cells treated with AngII, co-incubation with sulforaphane significantly upregulated HO-1 expression. sulforaphane promotes HO-1 expression, thereby reducing oxidative damage and ROS production [34]; this, in turn, enhances the antioxidant capacity of podocytes and facilitates their recovery from oxidative injury. This also underscores the role of the Nrf2/Keap1/HO-1 pathway in podocyte oxidative stress injury.

Moreover, using computer simulation technology, we strengthened our understanding of the molecular interaction between sulforaphane and Nrf2; this, in turn, provides indirect support for our hypothesis that the Nrf2-Keap1/HO-1/ROS pathway may be implicated in AngII-induced oxidative stress injury to podocytes, thereby affecting their function. Overall, our study offers a potential therapeutic target and mechanistic basis for the pathogenesis and treatment of chronic kidney disease.
