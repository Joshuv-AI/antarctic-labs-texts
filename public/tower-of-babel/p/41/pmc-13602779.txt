EECP intervention and coronary flow reserve in chronic coronary syndrome: a protocol of a multicentre, randomised controlled study in China (ECP-Core Trial)
Authors: Yanlong Leng, Yuan Li, Xue Jia, Shuai Tian, Yundan Jiang, Ling Lin, Yinfen Wang, Wei Pan, Jianwen Liang, Fensheng Wu, Wenjuan Zhou, Zhijuan Liao, Xinxia Zhang, Guifu Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFew non-invasive approaches are available for improving coronary function in intermediate-risk chronic coronary syndrome (CCS) patients. Over the past 30 years, enhanced external counterpulsation (EECP) has been widely accepted and recommended for treating CCS in clinical guidelines. However, the effect of EECP on coronary function remains to be quantitatively validated in intermediate-risk CCS patients. Fractional flow reserve (FFR) is a gold standard (quantitative index) of coronary artery function, and FFR < 0.80 is regarded as an indicator for percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in clinical decision-making. However, FFR is invasive and cannot be performed repeatedly within a short time interval; therefore, FFR is not suitable for large-scale verification of the effect before and after EECP treatment. Conversely, with advancements in numerical theory and computation hardware, FFR derived from coronary computed tomography angiography (FFRCT) performs similarly to FFR used in coronary artery function measurement. Additionally, FFRCT is non-invasive and highly acceptable to patients. Therefore, in this study, we aim to explore whether EECP therapy can improve FFRCT in patients with intermediate-risk CCS and further enhance the long-term prognosis of CCS.MethodsThis prospective, multicentre, randomised controlled trial is blinded to the examination executors and analysts. One hundred four participants will be recruited, and they will be divided into the EECP and control groups. The primary outcome of this study is the between-group difference in FFRCT changes from baseline to follow-up. The secondary outcomes refer to between-group differences in ΔFFRCT, wall shear stress, axial plaque stress, total exercise time, time to 1 mm ST-segment depression, Seattle Angina Questionnaire score, and cardiovascular-related composite endpoint events.DiscussionThis is the first study to use the non-invasive coronary artery function index, FFRCT, to evaluate the efficacy of EECP in patients with CCS. It comprehensively evaluates the treatment efficacy from multiple dimensions, including exercise capacity, symptom changes, and prognosis. Therefore, this will potentially provide reliable evidence to inform treatment strategies for CCS.Trial registrationTrial registration number: ChiCTR2400079915. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Epidemiological surveys between 1990 and 2019 have consistently shown that coronary heart disease (CHD), especially chronic coronary syndrome (CCS), poses a significant health and economic burden globally [1]. Currently, the primary treatment methods for CCS mainly include drug therapy, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) [2]. Notably, PCI is one of the most significant, widespread, and effective treatment modalities for revascularisation therapy [3]. However, many clinical studies have found that PCI has some limitations in dealing with multi-vessel lesions, diffuse lesions, severely calcified lesions, and myocardial microvascular dysfunction [4, 5]. Moreover, PCI can only treat blood flow-limiting lesions and is generally not used to treat early lesions that do not affect haemodynamics [6]. The ISCHEMIA Trial demonstrated that, among patients with CCS who were clinically stable but had anatomically moderate-to-severe coronary stenosis, an early revascularisation strategy did not significantly reduce the risk of major cardiovascular events [7]. Therefore, PCI is not universally indicated for all patients with intermediate-risk CCS. This population is defined by 50–90% coronary stenosis, stable angina symptoms, and absence of high-risk anatomical or functional features, including left main coronary artery disease and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) < 35%, per the 2024 Chinese Guidelines for CCS [8].

Enhanced external counterpulsation (EECP) is an important method for the treatment and rehabilitation of CCS and was approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 1994. Large-scale randomised controlled trials (RCT), such as the multicentre study of EECP (MUST-EECP) and Prospective Evaluation of EECP in Congestive Heart Failure (PEECH), offer unequivocal proof of the efficacy of EECP in treating CCS and heart failure [9, 10]. Furthermore, to some extent, these studies have provided evidence supporting the effectiveness and safety of EECP, leading multiple guidelines to classify EECP as a class IIa or IIb recommendation [11]. However, since previous studies have not validated the effectiveness of EECP from the perspective of influencing clinical treatment decisions, the recommendation class of EECP in guidelines remains low.

In the past 20 years, fractional flow reserve (FFR) has gradually become a recognised invasive functional evaluation index for lesions. The selection of FFR-guided CHD treatment strategies has been proven to be safe, economical, and able to improve patient prognosis [12, 13]. Recently, the integration of artificial intelligence and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) has significantly propelled the clinical application of FFR derived from coronary computed tomography angiography (FFRCT), which combines the dual assessment advantages of morphology and functionality. FFRCT requires only computed tomography angiography (CTA) images for calculation and value assignment, avoiding the risks associated with traditional invasive FFR testing and eliminating the need for additional medications and economic burden. The results of FFRCT-guided treatment decisions are highly consistent with those of FFR-guided treatment decisions; therefore, FFRCT has great potential as a non-invasive indicator to replace FFR [14].

The Art.Net-2 Trial (conducted by Buschmann et al.) found that the average coronary artery FFR increased by approximately 0.11 after EECP treatment, whereas there was no change in the control group [15]. However, there are specific defects in the Art.Net-2 Trial. It was a small sample, single-centre study. Furthermore, it only evaluated the FFR of the most severe lesions, while ignoring many other lesions. Thus, it cannot be considered high-quality evidence for the efficacy of EECP in treating intermediate-risk CCS. Therefore, in this study, we will evaluate the effect of EECP on FFRCT in patients with intermediate-risk CCS, demonstrating the effect of EECP on improving coronary artery function and quality of life from multiple dimensions and parameters. Our results may provide a safe and effective non-invasive intervention for the treatment and rehabilitation of patients with intermediate-risk CCS.

Trial design {8}

This study is designed as a prospective, multicentre, randomised controlled clinical trial with a superiority design. The primary hypothesis is that EECP intervention yields a better improvement in coronary physiological function assessed by FFRCT compared with the control treatment in patients with CCS. According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria established for this study, researchers will carefully screen potential participants to guarantee that only individuals who satisfy the eligibility criteria will be selected for participation. The flowchart of this study is detailed in Fig. 1. Through randomisation grouping, the participants will be evenly divided into EECP treatment and control groups in a 1:1 ratio to evaluate the efficacy of EECP therapy for CCS.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {9}

This study is being mainly conducted at the Eighth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Shenzhen, China). Other sub-centres are hospitals in China, including the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, the Third Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University, the Third Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University, the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University, Shanghai MCC Hospital, Taiyuan Central Hospital, and Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital.

Eligibility criteria {10}

This study focused on the intermediate-risk CCS population, a group of patients with long-term persistent myocardial ischaemia symptoms and intermediate-risk coronary anatomical characteristics [2, 8]. Researchers who should be licensed medical practitioners will screen outpatients and inpatients who meet the CCS diagnosis at various research centres. The patients enrolled in this study must meet the eligibility criteria.

The exclusion criteria for this study can be summarised as follows: Patients presenting with left main coronary artery disease or left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVEF < 35%) are excluded from the trial. Other major exclusion criteria include: Agatston calcium score ≥ 400, history of CABG, uncontrolled arrhythmia, concomitant uncontrolled cardiovascular diseases, peripheral vascular disease, bleeding tendency, and pregnancy. Severe coronary calcification impairs coronary computed tomography angiography image quality and the accuracy of CFD-based FFRCT measurement. CABG can lead to significant differences in coronary haemodynamics compared with normal conditions, and the current FFRCT technology is yet to undergo large-scale validation in patients who underwent CABG. EECP is based on the working principle of electrocardiographic triggering; therefore, frequent extrasystoles or atrial fibrillation interfere with the working rhythm of EECP and affect its efficacy. The remaining criteria are contraindications for EECP and are included in the exclusion criteria.

Who will take informed consent? {26a}

Qualified researchers at each research centre fully communicate with potential participants or authorised representatives, informing them of the potential benefits and risks of participating in this study. Based on the adequate disclosure of participants’ rights, the researchers obtain informed consent forms.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {26b}

The informed consent form details the collection and use of participants’ data and biological specimens for the ancillary study, and the participants have the right to agree or refuse to participate in the ancillary study.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

The comparator of this study is using guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) alone, which is the standard therapy that all patients with CCS should adopt.

Intervention description {11a}

Participants are randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio after enrolment. Following the random assignment, each participant receives a unique random identification number. Participants randomly assigned to the EECP group will undergo EECP therapy (1 h per session, five times weekly, for a total of 36 sessions, with a treatment pressure of 30 kPa). During each treatment session, vital signs (including blood pressure and heart rate) and the D/S ratio during EECP are recorded (a D/S ratio ≥ 1.2 is considered a marker of achieving the desired therapeutic effect). In addition to receiving EECP therapy, participants in the EECP group also undergo GDMT to ensure effective control of risk factors associated with CCS. In contrast, those assigned to the control group only receive GDMT alone during the entire study period. There is frequent contact between the participants in the EECP group and the researchers; however, the control group does not typically have such contact. Therefore, to avoid bias, we minimise changes in treatment measures for all participants during EECP therapy. Additionally, researchers should maintain weekly contact with participants in the control group to ensure that treatment measures are implemented as planned.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

During the study, any eligible participants may withdraw based on specific criteria (Table 1). If a participant withdraws from the study halfway, the researcher should record the reason for withdrawal in the case report forms (CRFs).

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

To improve participants’ adherence to interventions, researchers conduct regular on-site or telephone follow-ups for all participants weekly, aiming to establish a good doctor-patient relationship and provide detailed answers to their feedback during the treatment process. During the follow-ups, the treatment status of the participants is recorded to ensure that the trial is conducted according to the established grouping.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

During the research process, PCI and CABG are prohibited because they will completely change the haemodynamic state of coronary arteries. If PCI or CABG is necessary due to the illness, the participant should withdraw from the study. Conversely, the oral medication regimen can be changed due to the changes in illness during the study; the researchers must record the changes and reasons for oral medications in detail on the medication record form. Pre-enrolment and on-site management will be strengthened to minimise unnecessary medication adjustments. All investigators at participating centres will receive standardised training. During follow-up, investigators are required to prioritise dose titration of existing drugs rather than initiating new antianginal agents, unless clinically necessary.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

After the study ends, participants can consult researchers at any time for any questions regarding the study. Researchers have purchased clinical trial liability insurance for all participants through the China Taiping Insurance Company (insurance number: 66021127020230000133). Therefore, if any research-related injuries occur, corresponding compensation will be provided.

Outcomes {12}

The primary outcome of this study is the change in FFRCT from baseline, compared between the EECP intervention group and the control group. Secondary outcomes include changes in each indicator before and after treatment across the two groups: (1) ΔFFRCT, wall shear stress (WSS), and axial plaque stress (APS); (2) total exercise time and time to 1 mm ST-segment depression based on an exercise stress test; (3) assessment of angina symptoms based on the Seattle Angina Questionnaire (SAQ); and (4) cardiovascular-related composite endpoint events. Cardiovascular-related composite endpoint events are defined as non-fatal myocardial infarction, hospitalisation due to unstable angina, unexpected coronary intervention surgery, hospitalisation due to heart failure, and cardiovascular death that occur in the participants during the overall study period. FFRCT reflects the direct result of EECP intervention on coronary artery function. ΔFFRCT, WSS, and APS are also haemodynamic parameters derived from coronary CTA, which can reflect local mechanical changes in plaques and may help reveal the mechanism of EECP. The total exercise time and time to 1 mm ST-segment depression based on an exercise stress test and the SAQ score assess symptoms associated with myocardial ischaemia during maximum exercise and daily activities, respectively, and they can reflect the participants’ comprehensive abilities. Finally, cardiovascular-related composite endpoint events are used to evaluate the differences in long-term prognosis between EECP treatment and GDMT.

Participant timeline {13}

Participants are followed up at visits 0–7. Table 2 shows the contents of each visit, and all follow-up results are recorded in CRFs for subsequent analyses. The study is divided into three periods, including the screening period (visit 0), intervention period (visits 1–2), and follow-up period (visits 3–7). After completing the screening, participants in the intervention group receive corresponding interventions based on their allocations and are followed up before and after the intervention. During the follow-up period, participants receive follow-ups according to Table 2 and complete examinations at different time points.

Sample size {14}

The sample size is determined based on the research hypotheses and the expected efficacy level calculations. Sample size calculation was performed based on the primary endpoint, the change in FFRCT from baseline. This trial adopts a superiority design with two-sided statistical tests, with an overall significance level of α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.80. We used R software and the dedicated formula for two independent samples in superiority trials:

The pwr package in R was applied for cross-validation. According to our preliminary trial data, the mean change in FFRCT from baseline was 0.018 in the EECP group and −0.020 in the control group, with a pooled standard deviation of 0.046 and a predefined superiority margin of 0.01 (Relevant studies show that for every 0.01 reduction in FFRCT, the adjusted hazard ratio (HR) for major adverse cardiovascular events is 1.040, 95% CI: 1.029–1.051, P < 0.001) [16]. The primary formula indicated that 43 participants per group were required, and cross-validation via the pwr package suggested 44 participants per group, as each patient corresponds to one coronary lesion in this study. Without considering loss to follow-up, a total of 86 to 88 participants are needed for both groups. After adjusting for an anticipated 15% loss to follow-up, the final total sample size is 104 patients.

Recruitment {15}

This study employs multiple methods to recruit participants. Researchers focus on patients in hospital wards and outpatient clinics, directly communicating with those who meet the eligibility criteria. Additionally, recruitment posters are placed in outpatient clinics and hospital wards, and recruitment information is published on social media to increase the visibility of the trial.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

This study employs simple randomisation. Using R version 4.3.0, we generate a table of random numbers. Subsequently, we randomly assign the participants to the EECP or control groups in a 1:1 ratio based on these random numbers.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

After generating the table of random numbers, each random number is printed onto a separate random allocation card. These random allocation cards are then randomly shuffled and placed into sealed, opaque envelopes. Subsequently, each centre is provided with a predetermined number of these envelopes. When a participant is enrolled, the researcher randomly selects an envelope and retrieves the random allocation card inside. The participant is then assigned to the corresponding group based on the random number on the card. Finally, the unique random allocation card is affixed to the CRFs as identification information.

Implementation {16c}

Statistical personnel who do not participate in subject recruitment generate the random sequence, whereas dedicated researchers are responsible for recruiting participants. However, another group of dedicated researchers handle the participant allocation.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

The control group in this study does not receive EECP treatment; therefore, it is impossible to blind the treatment executor, follow-up investigator, or the participants. However, the examination executors and analysts are blinded. At each visit, the follow-up investigator coordinates between the examination executors and participants. During the examination and data processing, the allocation of participants is concealed from the examination executors, and the participants are instructed not to communicate relevant details with them. Therefore, the analyst conducts a blinded data evaluation.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

This study adopts the final unblinding, which means that after completing the entire study and data analysis, researchers will unblind the examination executors and analysts. However, because the participants cannot be blinded, emergency unblinding is not applicable to this study.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

This is a multicentre study. Therefore, to ensure consistent data quality across all centres, we have established standard operating procedures (SOPs) for different outcome measures. The SOPs for all outcomes are as follows:

FFRCT

FFRCT

Pre-examination preparation involves ensuring that the computed tomography equipment operates under normal conditions. Participants are provided with electrocardiographic receiving devices and trained to hold their breath. During the examination phase, plain scanning of the coronary arteries is performed first. The tracheal bifurcation level of the thoracic aorta is selected as the trigger point for enhanced scanning with a trigger threshold of 100 Hounsfield units (HU). An appropriate amount of iodine contrast agent is then injected using a high-pressure injector during dynamic scanning monitoring. When the trigger point reaches the threshold, the device automatically performs retrospective spiral scanning and collects the patient data during the scanning process.

After completing the scan, thin-slice axial images of the entire cardiac cycle are reconstructed. The post-processing software provided by the device is used to reconstruct the coronary artery images (maximum intensity projection, three-dimensional) and the morphology and potential stenoses are carefully analysed. Cardiac function data are collected as boundary conditions for subsequent numerical analysis.

The FFRCT requires CFD calculations based on CTA images. The input coronary CTA data is first subjected to artificial intelligence (AI)-automated segmentation based on the deep learning three-dimensional U-Net. Subsequently, the centreline of each blood vessel is extracted. Hundreds of lumen contours are then generated through the contour AI method. The most critical step is to interpolate the lumen contours and generate a three-dimensional model of the coronary blood vessels. The complete model is automatically discretised into small unstructured cells for the subsequent numerical simulation. Using the boundary conditions determined in advance, the governing equations are solved by a numerical scheme of finite element method or finite volume method on a high-performance computing cluster, and the simulation results are obtained (Fig. 2). Finally, the FFRCT value at 2 cm distal to each lesion can be calculated based on the pressure.

Workflow of FFRCT. The framework of FFR derived from coronary computed tomography angiography (FFRCT) is divided into reconstruction (a–d) and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) (e–g). A three-dimensional model of coronary arteries (e) is obtained after pixel segmentation (b), centreline extraction (c), and blood vessel contour (d) generation from CTA data (a). The 3D computational domain is discretised into millions of grid cells (f). Then, coronary flow is calculated based on the morphological characteristics obtained from CTA images (g). Finally, velocity and pressure are converted into FFRCT, ΔFFRCT, wall shear stress (WSS), and axial plaque stress (APS) (h)

(2)ΔFFRCT, WSS, and APS

ΔFFRCT, WSS, and APS

During the calculation process of FFRCT, the spatial distribution of velocity and pressure within this whole domain is obtained. Once the flow field is resolved, ΔFFRCT induced by the lesion can be worked out using the FFRCT values at the distal and proximal ends of the lesion [17]. Numerical simulation is used to calculate WSS and APS by combining flow velocity and pressure data, as well as geometric and physical parameters of plaques.

(3)Total exercise time and time to 1 mm ST-segment depression based on an exercise stress test

Total exercise time and time to 1 mm ST-segment depression based on an exercise stress test

The standard operating procedure for cardiopulmonary exercise testing includes resting, warm-up, incremental exercise, and recovery phases. In this study, all participants performed exercises using a cycle ergometer, and the RAMP is the adopted exercise program. Personalised initial power and target power are set. During the exercise test, doctors closely monitor the participants’ reactions, heart rate, blood pressure, and electrocardiograms. The test is immediately terminated when termination indicators appear, based on the American Heart Association practice guidelines for these indicators [18]. Finally, total exercise time and time to 1 mm ST-segment depression are recorded.

(4)SAQ score

SAQ score

The SAQ is used to evaluate symptomatic changes in participants. Based on their conditions, subjects rate themselves on five dimensions: physical activity limitation, angina stability, angina attacks, treatment satisfaction, and disease awareness (Supplementary Material 1). Participants are asked to remain objective and truthful during the filling process to avoid subjective biases. Each dimension is scored based on the patient’s responses, and the change in SAQ score before and after the intervention will be analysed.

(5)Cardiovascular-related composite endpoint events

Cardiovascular-related composite endpoint events

Finally, during the study period, researchers engage in regular communication with patients, utilising either on-site or telephone follow-ups to document the occurrence of cardiovascular-related composite endpoint events. This approach aims to assess the long-term prognosis of the participants.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

To ensure the retention of participants and their completion of follow-up, we fully communicate with all participants throughout the entire process, informing them of the trial’s objectives, potential benefits, and risks, using easily understandable language. After the analysis of the outcomes is completed, the researchers promptly provide feedback to the participants, which can enhance their participation and adherence. Participants also receive subsidies for transportation expenses.

Data management {19}

Two trained independent researchers at each centre will enter the data recorded in this study’s CRFs separately into the Electronic Data Capture (EDC) database established by the Eighth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Shenzhen Futian). The data will be properly stored in the database, and unauthorised access is prohibited.

Confidentiality {27}

To protect the privacy of the participants, we anonymise the data (using randomly assigned numbers as substitutes) after obtaining informed consent. Only authorised personnel are able to access the participants’ personal data in the CRFs and EDC. All researchers and statisticians are required to strictly adhere to privacy protection rules and are prohibited from disclosing any personal information of the participants. If researchers are to share data, they must use de-identified data.

Plans for collection, laboratory evaluation, and storage of biological specimens for genetic or molecular analysis in this trial/future use {33}

We will conduct an ancillary study to further explore the molecular mechanism of EECP on coronary artery function. Blood samples are collected from the participants using anticoagulant tubes during visit 1 and visit 2, respectively. The samples are then centrifuged under sterile conditions at 4 °C to separate the plasma. The plasma samples are stored in a −80 °C freezer. It is anticipated that plasma samples from 30 participants in the EECP and control groups, respectively, will be collected for non-targeted proteomics and metabolomics analysis.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

All data are collected via case report forms and double-entered independently by two researchers. Statistical analyses are performed using R 4.4.3. All statistical tests are two-sided, with the significance level set at P < 0.05 and the overall type I error controlled at α = 0.05 (two-sided). The primary endpoint is defined as the sole confirmatory endpoint and is tested at α = 0.05 without multiple comparison correction. To control the false discovery rate (FDR) induced by repeated tests across multiple secondary endpoints and pairwise comparisons at different time points, the Benjamini-Hochberg method is applied for multiple comparison adjustment, and the adjusted P values are denoted as Padj. Subgroup analyses are considered exploratory rather than confirmatory, and thus no multiple comparison correction is performed; results are interpreted descriptively only. Both original P values and Padj are reported to fully present the statistical findings.

Baseline statistical analysis

All baseline continuous variables are assessed for normality (Shapiro-Wilk test and QQ plots) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test) by study group. Continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range, IQR). Between-group comparisons are conducted using the independent two-sample t-test (normal distribution, equal variances), Welch’s t-test (normal distribution, unequal variances), or Mann-Whitney U test (skewed distribution). Categorical data are summarised as frequency (percentage). Pearson’s chi-square test, continuity-corrected chi-square test, and Fisher’s exact test are used sequentially based on total sample size and expected frequencies.

Efficacy analysis

All randomised participants are included in the intention-to-treat (ITT) set for primary analysis. Missing data are handled via multiple imputation by chained equations with the predictive mean matching method, and a total of 10 imputed datasets are generated. The per-protocol (PP) set is applied for sensitivity analysis.

For the primary endpoint FFRCT and other coronary functional indices: The data present a three-level nested structure of study centre-participant-coronary target lesion, with random centre effects and longitudinal repeated measures. Given the significantly skewed distribution of FFRCT, the generalised linear mixed model (GLMM) is applied. The fixed effects consist of group, time, and group-by-time interaction, and baseline characteristics such as medication changes are adjusted as covariates. A three-level nested random intercept (study centre/participant/target lesion) is specified for random effects. Variance components and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) are calculated to quantify the influence of random effects at each nested level. The emmeans package is used to estimate net between-group differences at the post-intervention follow-up. A stratified Bootstrap method with 1000 resamples is adopted to calculate the two-sided 95% confidence interval (CI) of net differences. Superiority of the experimental group over the control group is concluded if the lower bound of the 95% CI exceeds the predefined superiority margin of 0.01. The identical modelling strategy is applied to other coronary functional indices, including ΔFFRCT, WSS, and APS.

Subgroup analysis

Prespecified subgroup stratification is performed based on coronary luminal stenosis (cut-off value ≥ 70%) and functional index (FFRCT < 0.8) to explore the heterogeneity of EECP intervention effects. Subgroup analysis compares differences in endpoints including coronary functional indices (FFRCT, ΔFFRCT, WSS, APS), quality of life (Seattle Angina Questionnaire, SAQ), exercise capacity, and long-term cardiovascular-related composite endpoint events among populations with different stenosis severity and functional ischaemia status. Group-by-subgroup interaction terms are incorporated into the aforementioned mixed-effects models and Cox proportional hazards models. The statistical significance of interaction terms is tested to assess whether the efficacy of EECP differs across subgroups.

Discussion

Cardiovascular rehabilitation has become an indispensable part of the cardiovascular disease prevention and treatment system, and EECP therapy is increasingly recognised as a standard cardiovascular rehabilitation technique by many cardiovascular rehabilitation centres. However, the limitations of most EECP-related research include single-centre, small-sample, non-randomised controlled studies, and lack of objective evaluation indicators. Their study designs lack scientific evaluation of coronary artery function [19]. This study focuses on the impact of EECP on FFRCT and aims to explore whether EECP can improve coronary function in patients with intermediate-risk CCS through a multicentre RCT. The research findings will clarify the role and advantages of EECP in the treatment and rehabilitation of coronary heart disease, thereby promoting the standardisation and scientific application of EECP therapy for CCS. Through this study, we hope to obtain medical evidence that provides a safer and more effective non-pharmacological and non-surgical approach for the comprehensive management of patients with intermediate-risk CCS.

Similar to coronary angiography, coronary CTA can evaluate the morphology of coronary stenosis, but cannot evaluate coronary artery function. Compared with the combined application of FFRCT, CTA alone has a poor ability to identify high-risk plaques [20]. With FFR as the gold standard, FFRCT has shown better diagnostic ability for ischaemic lesions than single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) and positron emission tomography (PET) [21]. Similar to the diagnostic threshold of FFR, FFRCT ≤ 0.8 is closely related to poor cardiovascular prognosis [22]. Therefore, FFRCT is an ideal indicator for evaluating the efficacy of EECP in patients with intermediate-risk CCS.

Previous studies have shown that EECP produces continuous improvement in the symptoms of coronary heart disease by changing the shear force of blood flow [23]. Current advances in CFD technology allow us to simultaneously perform non-invasive haemodynamic detection of coronary arteries, including ΔFFRCT, WSS, and APS, during the calculation of FFRCT. Current research suggests that the haemodynamic characteristics of high-risk plaques include high ΔFFRCT (ΔFFRCT > 0.06), high WSS (WSS > 154.7 dyne/cm2), and high APS (APS > 1606.6 dyne/cm2) [20]. By combining FFRCT and comparing the shear force before and after EECP, we can further elucidate the possible treatment mechanism of EECP.

In addition to radiographic assessments, this study also uses the SAQ to evaluate and compare the symptoms of CCS from five aspects and combines it with CPET to assess cardiopulmonary exercise capacity. In summary, the ECP-CORE Trial will comprehensively evaluate coronary artery function, myocardial perfusion and function, symptoms and exercise capacity, and long-term prognosis to provide more scientific and objective evidence-based medical support for the role of EECP in intermediate-risk CCS.

Despite full demonstration in the design process of this study, there are still some limitations. Coronary microcirculatory dysfunction (CMD) and CHD have become major causes of angina and myocardial ischaemia. Pre-arterioles and arterioles provide most of the resistance in coronary microvascular circulation [24]. CHD and CMD may coexist in some patients. Currently, FFR and FFRCT can assess the functionality of epicardial coronary arteries, but cannot evaluate microcirculation. The index of microcirculatory resistance (IMR), measured by thermodilution, is an important indicator for diagnosing CMD [25]. Currently, haemodynamic analysis based on CTA faces difficulties in directly calculating the IMR. Although incorporating microcirculation function indicators such as IMR can improve the ability to distinguish risks to some extent for lesions with normal FFR, FFR ≤ 0.8 remains the gold standard for poor prognosis and is recommended for PCI by the guidelines [26]. A study has analysed the impact of abnormally elevated IMR on FFRCT calculated through CFD, and the results indicate that abnormal IMR has little effect on the calculation of FFRCT [27]. Another important issue in this study is the subjects’ compliance, as they may be concerned about potential damage caused by the additional radiation exposure from coronary CTA. Owing to advancements in scanning technology, the radiation exposure associated with CTA has been significantly reduced compared to more than a decade ago, without compromising the quality of the generated images [28]. Furthermore, the acquisition of FFRCT requires no additional radiation exposure. Researchers have investigated the cumulative total radiation exposure from coronary CTA in patients with stable angina pectoris treated under its guidance within 5 years and found that the average total radiation exposure was 8.1 mSv [29]. The cancer risk posed by radiation exposure of 10 mSv is minimal, while the number of cardiovascular events in patients with CCS guided by coronary CTA and FFRCT has significantly decreased within 3 years [28]. Therefore, the benefits for the subjects are far greater than the risks. Researchers should carefully inform participants of the benefits and risks involved in the research process, which will significantly improve their compliance.
