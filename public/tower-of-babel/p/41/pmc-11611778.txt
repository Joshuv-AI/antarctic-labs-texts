Loss of USP10 promotes hepatocellular carcinoma proliferation by regulating the serine synthesis pathway through inhibition of LKB1 activity
Authors: Chi Ma, Zhikun Lin, Jiaqi Yao, Wangshu Qin, Xiaolin Wang, Qi Li, Yaorui Ye, Xinyu Liu, Fating Chen, Jinlong Hu, Guowang Xu, Guang Tan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic dysregulation is emerging as a critical factor in tumorigenesis, and reprogramming of serine metabolism has been identified as an essential factor in the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Studies have shown that LKB1 deficiency can activate mTOR to upregulate the serine synthesis pathway (SSP) and promote tumor progression. Our team discovered that ubiquitin‐specific protease 10 (USP10) can inhibit HCC proliferation through mTOR, but its relationship with SSP needs further investigation. The metabolite assays revealed a significant increase in serine content in HCC tissues. Through the LKB1/mTOR/activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) axis, loss of USP10 may increase serine biosynthesis and promote the proliferation of HCC in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, it was found that USP10 could activate LKB1 through deubiquitination. Analyzing clinical HCC tissues revealed a positive correlation between USP10 and LKB1. Additionally, those with high expression of USP10 in HCC tissues showed a better degree of tumor differentiation and longer overall survival time. Moreover, we found increased expression of both serine and its synthase in liver tumor tissues of USP10 liver‐specific KO mice. Loss of USP10 inhibits the activity of LKB1, contributing to the stimulation of the mTOR/ATF4 axis and SSP and then promoting the proliferation of HCC. This work presents a novel approach for serine‐targeted treatment in HCC.

INTRODUCTION

Primary liver cancer is the fifth most common malignant tumor worldwide and has the second highest cancer‐related mortality rate. 1 Therefore, undertaking in‐depth studies on the molecular mechanisms of its occurrence is crucial to improving the diagnosis and treatment of liver cancer. The liver is an essential metabolic organ for three major nutrients: protein, sugar, and lipid. Metabolic abnormalities are associated with a variety of related diseases, such as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). 2 In HCC, the demonstrated abnormal metabolism includes reprogramming of amino acid metabolism, enhanced glycolysis, inhibited tricarboxylic acid cycle, gluconeogenesis, accelerated fatty acid synthesis and β‐oxidation, and decreased levels of inflammation‐associated polyunsaturated fatty acids. 3 Among the aberrant metabolic pathways, the Warburg effect, an aerobic glycolytic pathway, is widely recognized as a crucial aspect of tumor metabolism, and the serine synthesis pathway (SSP) is an essential turning point for glucose conversion. 4 , 5 As a nonessential amino acid, serine provides precursors for lipid synthesis, including sphingolipids and phosphatidylserine, which are components of cell membranes. The SSP is activated in various cancers, promoting tumor growth and metastasis by providing precursors for macromolecular syntheses and methylation reactions, such as proteins, nucleotides, and lipids. 6 , 7 , 8 Furthermore, serine synthesis‐coupled carbon metabolism generates two primary intrinsic oxidants, glutathione and NADPH, which maintain redox homeostasis in tumor cells and promote malignant tumor progression. 9 Recent studies have reported a significant increase in serine content in HCC tissues, 10 and SSP plays a crucial role in tumor progression.

Ubiquitin‐specific protease 10 (USP10), a member of the USP family, is a cysteine protease in the cytoplasm. Structurally, it includes a conserved His box and a Cys box with ubiquitin‐specific modifier enzyme features. 11 USP10 can cleave and remove ubiquitin molecules from target proteins like other USPs. In recent years, evidence has shown that USP10 plays a crucial role in tumorigenesis and development. USP10 can stabilize P53 12 and SIRT6, 13 two well‐known oncogenes, through deubiquitination and thus affect tumor progression. mTOR is an atypical serine/threonine protein kinase that regulates cell growth by influencing anabolism, and activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), as a transcription factor, can directly bind to and activate the promoter of SSP. 14 Under conventional conditions, mTOR can instantly activate ATF4 and its downstream pathway. 15 The tumor suppressor LKB1, a serine/threonine protein kinase, is a major upstream kinase of the AMPK signaling pathway, and deletion of LKB1 can upregulate serine synthesis in tumors through activation of mTOR phosphorylation. 16 Our previous studies have shown that USP10 inhibits mTOR activation and suppresses the proliferation of HCC. 17 This paper investigates whether USP10 can impact SSP in HCC through the LKB1/mTOR/ATF4 axis and influence the progression.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell culture and colony assay

Within the previous 3 years, the HCC cells involved in the study, MHCC‐97H and HUH7, were acquired from the BeNA Culture Collection and verified using short tandem repeat profiling. Mycoplasma‐free cells were used for all studies. These cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, Invitrogen) and incubated in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. The culture medium was supplemented with FBS. A total of 1000 cells were inoculated into 6‐well or 12‐well plates. Cells were washed with PBS 12 h after inoculation, cultured in DMEM or serine‐deficient DMEM, treated with 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, P1110), and stained with crystal violet (Solarbio, P1110) after 10 days.

Western blot analysis

Standard methods were used for western blot analysis. Briefly, cells or minced tissues were placed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime) containing protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were separated by PAGE after treatment and blotted onto nitrocellulose (NC) membranes, which were bound to proteins with specific primary Abs, and then peroxidase was used to bind proteins. The proteins were blotted onto the NC membrane, the exact primary Ab (Table S1) was used to secure the membrane, the peroxidase secondary Ab was applied to bind the primary Ab, and the chemiluminescence solution was used to visualize the bands.

Tissue microarray

In this study, human HCC tissue and paracancer tissue microarrays were constructed by the Biological Sample Bank of the First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University. The brief procedure was as follows: the array pattern of tissue microarrays was designed, 50 pairs of tissues were arranged according to the order of cancer and paracancerous tissues, and wax blocks were made. The wax blocks were continuously sliced using a slicer to produce the tissue microarrays.

Immunofluorescence

Initially, cells were cultured in a DMEM medium with slides for one night. Subsequently, they were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by infiltration with Triton X‐100. The cells were then incubated with an immunofluorescence sealing solution for 1 h. Finally, specific primary Abs were incubated with the cells. After applying DAPI dye, samples were securely enclosed and captured using a laser‐scanning confocal microscope.

Immunohistochemical analysis

The human tissue microarrays, USP10 KO, and WT mice liver cancer tissues were preserved in formalin, encased in paraffin, and divided into sections. After incubation with specified Abs, the samples were stained with DAPI, treated with hematoxylin, dried, scanned, and analyzed.

Endogenous and exogenous immunoprecipitation assay

Cells were lysed with EBC buffer (50 Mm Tris pH 7.5, 120 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP‐40, and 1% protease inhibitor), endogenous immunoprecipitation was added 2 μL IgG/USP10/LKB1 Ab to the lysate and incubated at 4°C for 4 h, and A/G magnetic beads were added to the lysate and incubated at 4°C for 12 h. Exogenous immunoprecipitation was added directly to the lysate with Flag/Myc‐tagged magnetic beads, set at 4°C for 12 h, and boiled at 99°C for 5 min. Western blot analysis was used to detect protein expression.

Transfections

The research plasmid was purchased from the Public Protein/Plasmid Library, China. The following shRNA sequences were used: USP10‐shRNA1, 5′‐GCCTCTCTTTAGTGGCTCTTTT‐3′; USP10‐shRNA2, 5′‐CACCTGAAACGATTCGTTTAT‐3′; USP10‐shRNA3, 5′‐CCTATGTGGAAACTAAGTATT‐3′; USP10‐shRNA4, 5′‐CCCATGATAGACAGCTTTGTT‐3′; USP10‐shRNA5, 5′‐GCTGTGGATAAACTACCTGAT‐3′; USP10‐shRNA6, 5′‐CGACAAGCTCTTGGAGATAAA‐3′; PLKO‐shControl, 5′‐CCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCG‐3′, pLVX‐Puro‐LKB1‐Flag, and pLVX‐Puro‐USP10‐Myc. According to the instructions, plasmids were transfected into HEK‐293 T cells using Lipo2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The medium with recombinant lentivirus was collected 48 h posttransfection, filtered using 0.45 μm nonpyrogenic filters, added to the target cells, and infected with medium containing puromycin (2 μg/mL) for 48 h after infection. The transfection efficiency was verified by western blot analysis.

Xenograft tumor model

Sterile male nude mice were supplied by Changsheng Biotechnology company (4–5 weeks old, 18–20 g). They were housed in the Institute of Animal Model for Human Disease Genome Engineering, Dalian Medical College, in a specific pathogen‐free environment. For tumorigenesis experiments, MHCC‐97H cells (1 × 106) were injected into the axilla of mice. Tumor volume was assessed at 3‐day intervals. The backfill experiment was carried out by injection of metformin (250 mg/kg i.p.) after dissolution in sterile double distilled water 1 week after tumor formation. We then measured the weight and volume of the tumors every 3 days. Animals were handled according to relevant institutional and national regulations. The maximum diameter of tumors did not exceed 1.5 cm. After 3 weeks, mice were anesthetized by isoflurane inhalation and killed.

USP10 KO mice

In this research project, we commissioned Saiye Biotechnology Co., Ltd to construct USP10 KO mice (USP10flox/flox mice) and then cross‐bred with USP10flox/flox mice with AlbCre mice to obtain USP10flox/flox‐AlbCre mice, which are USP10 liver‐specific KO mice. One‐week‐old WT and USP10 liver‐specific KO mice were induced with tamoxifen (20 μg/g, i.p. injection, 5 days). Diethylnitrosamine (DEN) treatment was then given at 25 μg/g i.p. Nine months later, the mice were killed, the livers between the two groups were collected, and the tumor size and number were observed.

Ubiquitination experiment

In the ubiquitination experiment, MHCC‐97H and HUH7 cells were first transfected with ubiquitin (Ub) and the plasmids involved in this study, and then the proteasome inhibitor MG132 was added to inhibit the proteasome function after 48 h, and the protein expression was verified by immunoprecipitation and western blot analysis after 6 h.

Metabolomics sample preparation

Approximately 20 mg tissue samples were weighed and 400 μL methanol extractant was added containing internal standards (carnitine C2:0‐d3, carnitine C8:0‐d3, carnitine C16:0‐d3, LPC 19:0, FFA C16:0‐d3, FFA C18:0‐d3, CDCA‐d4, CA‐d4, Trp‐d5, and Phe‐d5). The sample was then homogenized, and 100 μL suspension was diluted to 1 mL with methanol extractant. Then 2 mL chloroform and 400 μL water was added to the mixture for removing nonpolar lipids. Subsequently, 300 μL of the lower layer and 500 μL of the upper layer were transferred to a new tube and dried in nitrogen for metabolic analysis. Before liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC‐MS)‐based metabolomics analysis, the samples were reconstituted in 80 μL acetonitrile/water (v/v, 1:4). The cells in 10 cm Petri dishes were rinsed by applying cold PBS and quenched with liquid nitrogen, 1 mL methanol extractant was added, and the cells were lysed and scraped. Subsequently, the mixture was prepared using the same process as the tissue samples for metabolomics analysis.

Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry

The acquisition conditions for metabolomics analysis were based on previous studies with slight modifications. 18 A Vanquish UPLC‐Q Exactive spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was applied for LC‐MS analysis. A 50 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm BEH C8 column (Waters Corp.) as well as mobile phase ultrapure water and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid was used in LC separation in positive mode. A 50 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm HSS T3 column (Waters Corp.) as well as mobile phase ultrapure water and 95% methanol ultrapure water with 6.5 mM NH4HCO3 was used in negative mode. The MS full scan range was set to 80–1200 m/z and resolution to 7e4. The spray voltage was set to 3.5 and 3.00 kV in positive and negative modes, respectively. The capillary temperature and aux gas heater temperature were 300° and 350°C, respectively. The sheath gas and aux gas were 45 and 10 (in arbitrary units), respectively.

Quantitative RT‐PCR and RNA sequencing analysis

RNA was isolated from HCC cells or mice tissues using TRIzol reagent, RT‐PCR was carried out using RevertAID First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (BIOER, TC‐E‐48D), and real‐time fluorescence quantitative PCR was carried out using StepOne Plus (BIOER, Gene‐9660) and dsDNA‐specific reagent SYBR Green I. The data were standardized to the GAPDH level, and Table S2 shows the primer sequences. The Illumina HiSeq 2000 129 platform was used to produce RNA sequence libraries.

Pathway enrichment analysis

Pathway analysis for metabolomics datas were performed using an online program (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/home.xhtml). The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datas were undertaken utilizing an internet application (http://gepia.cancer‐pku.cn/detail.php). Part of the diagrams were created using BioRender.

Statistical analysis

In this study, all the tests were done three times, and GraphPad Prism 9.0 software was used for statistical analysis. Sample statistical values are shown as mean ± SD. To conclude, the study used the two‐tailed t‐test or one‐way ANOVA for measurement data, the χ2‐test or Fisher's exact probability method for count data, and the Kaplan–Meier method for survival data. A p value of 0.05 indicates that the difference was statistically significant.

RESULTS

Increased serine levels in human HCC tissues

In this study, we first applied LC‐MS technology to detect metabolites in cancer and paracancerous tissues from 10 pairs of patients with HCC (the samples were obtained from the biospecimen bank of the First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University and were approved by the Ethics Committee) (Figure 1A). The principal component analysis showed that the two groups of metabolites had better separation values (Figure S1A). We screened out 156 metabolites with statistically significant differences (Figure 1B). After enrichment analysis, we noticed significant changes in beta oxidation of very long chain fatty acids, methylhistidine metabolism, methionine metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, glutathione metabolism, and glycine and serine metabolism (Figure 1D). After the enrichment of amino acid‐related metabolites (n = 25), it was found that methylhistidine metabolism, methionine metabolism, and glycine and serine metabolism differed most significantly in HCC and paracancerous tissues (Figure S1B–D). As serine is a nonessential amino acid, cancer cells can add to their internal serine pool by importing it from outside the cell or making it from scratch. Recent studies have found that targeted strategies for serine metabolism, including dietary serine restriction and inhibition of serine synthesis, show promising anticancer therapeutic effects. In addition, metabolite analysis in this study revealed that the difference in serine content between HCC and paracancerous tissues was the most obvious (p = 1.2914E−07), and the amount of serine in HCC was substantially more significant than that in paracancerous tissues (Figure 1C), which demonstrates abnormal levels of serine in HCC.

USP10 deficiency promotes tumor proliferation and influences SSP in HCC

Numerous studies have revealed that USPs have a role in developing HCC 19 , 20 , 21 and may be linked to processes such as serine metabolism. 22 Therefore, we constructed serine deletion models in MHCC‐97H and HUH7 cell lines to explore changes in the levels of USP associated with HCC. Compared to other members of the USP family, USP10 showed statistically significant downregulation of its expression in both serine‐deficient HCC cell lines (p = 0.0235, p = 0.0217) (Figures S1E and 1E). This suggests that USP10 could play a key role in reprogramming serine metabolism in HCC.

To investigate the correlation between serine and USP10 in HCC, we constructed USP10 knockdown and overexpression cell lines in MHCC‐97H and HUH7 (Figure 1H). The clone formation experiments indicated that USP10 knockdown significantly inhibited the proliferative effect of HCC, while overexpression had the opposite effect (Figure 1I). Tumorigenic experiments carried out on nude mice showed a statistically significant increase in the size (p = 0.0116, p = 0.0004) and weight (p = 0.0044, p = 0.0007) of subcutaneous tumors in the USP10 knockdown group (Figure 1J,K). These results suggest that USP10 might have an inhibitory effect on the proliferative ability of HCC. We used LC‐MS to detect metabolite differences between the USP10 knockdown and control groups (Figure 1F,G). The results showed a significant increase in serine content after USP10 knockdown, with statistically significant differences between the groups (p = 8.7546E−08, p = 3.631E−06). Furthermore, the RNA sequencing results indicate that the knockdown of USP10 led to an inverse increase in the key enzymes involved in SSP, namely PHGDH, PSAT1, and PSPH (Figure S2A). Additionally, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analysis revealed differences in the expression of glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism (Figure S2B). These data indicate that USP10 could influence the reprogramming of serine metabolism in HCC through SSP.

USP10 affects SSP through the LKB1/mTOR/ATF4 axis

Activating transcription factor 4 was found to be a core transcription factor affecting serine synthase at the transcriptional level. 23 The expression level of ATF4 is affected by mTOR, 24 and LKB1 can inversely regulate mTOR activity in affecting the phosphorylation of AMPK. 25 LKB1 deficiency has been shown to activate mTOR to upregulate tumor serine synthesis and promote tumor progression. 26 Therefore, this study investigated whether USP10 affects serine synthesis and tumor proliferation through the LKB1/mTOR/ATF4 axis in HCC. Our experiments first revealed that USP10 knockdown could inhibit the protein expression of phosphorylation of LKB1 at S334, promote the phosphorylation of mTOR at 2481 and ATF4, by western blot analysis (Figure 2B,D), and the quantitative RT‐PCR findings revealed that ATF4 was likewise elevated at the transcriptional level. Subsequently, the transcription and protein expression of PHGDH, PSAT1, and PSPH (Figure 2A,C), which are pivotal enzymes in SSP, was increased after USP10 knockdown, suggesting that USP10 loss can promote serine synthesis in HCC.

To further explore the role of serine in promoting HCC proliferation by USP10, we constructed a serine deletion model in USP10 knockdown cell lines. The results indicated that after serine deletion, protein levels of USP10 and pLKB1‐S334 decreased in both cell lines, while downstream pmTOR‐2481, ATF4, PHGDH, PSPH, and PSAT1 increased inversely (Figure 2F). Interestingly, the differences in protein levels of all the above enzymes were significantly reduced in the USP10 knockdown group compared to the control group. Furthermore, clone formation experiments showed a decrease in the proliferative capacity of cells in both groups after serine deletion, with reduced differences between the groups (Figure 2E). In conclusion, the LKB1/mTOR/ATF4 axis may be affected by USP10, and the proliferative ability of HCC was reduced under serine‐deficient conditions, suggesting that the reprogramming of serine metabolism plays a vital role in promoting the proliferation of HCC by USP10 deficiency.

Interaction between USP10 and LKB1

To test the regulation of LKB1 activity by USP10, this study investigated the direct interaction between USP10 and LKB1. We discovered a positive correlation between USP10 and LKB1 in HCC tissues by searching the TCGA database (r = 0.41, p = 4.4e−16) (Figure S2C). In vitro, the colocalization of USP10 and LKB1 in the cytoplasm was examined using immunofluorescence in MHCC‐97H and HUH7 cell lines (Figure 3A). Immunoprecipitation verified that USP10 and LKB1 could bind to each other in the cell under physiological conditions (Figure 3B). We next transfected Myc‐USP10 and Flag‐LKB1 plasmids into MHCC‐97H and HUH7 cells, and the immunoprecipitation examination of exogenous transduction confirmed the unique association between USP10 and LKB1 (Figure 3C).

As a deubiquitinating enzyme, USP10 can remove ubiquitin from the substrate. Therefore, USP10 may regulate LKB1 activity through deubiquitination. The MHCC‐97H and HUH7 cells were transfected with Myc‐USP10, Flag‐LKB1, and Ub plasmids. To inhibit proteasome activity, MG132 was applied. The study found that the transfer of USP10 significantly reduced the ubiquitin of LKB1 and weakened its binding to Ub (Figure 3D). Conversely, the transfer of LKB1 enhanced the ubiquitin expression of USP10, indicating its ability to remove Ub molecules from LKB1 and regulate its activity and that USP10 interacted with LKB1 through deubiquitination.

Activation of LKB1 reverses the effect of USP10 deletion on SSP in HCC

The study created shUSP10 + LKB1 cell lines by introducing viral fluids containing LKB1 plasmid into shUSP10 cell lines. The western blot analysis indicated that the protein levels of pLKB1‐S334, pmTOR‐2481, ATF4, PHGDH, PSAT1, and PSPH were reversed in the USP10 knockdown group after the transfer of the LKB1 plasmid (Figure 4A). Metformin, an LKB1 agonist known to activate phosphorylated LKB1 and affect the downstream signaling pathway, was chosen for this study. 27 After applying metformin (1.0 mmol/L), pLKB1‐S334 and downstream protein expression were reversed in USP10 knockdown cell lines (Figure 4B). The results of the clone formation (Figure 4C) and nude mice tumor formation assays indicate that overexpression of LKB1 or using LKB1's agonist can reverse the impact of USP10 on HCC proliferation (Figure 4D–G). The findings suggest that USP10 impacts the proliferation of HCC by modulating serine synthase expression through LKB1.

USP10 KO mice confirm that USP10 deletion upregulates SSP

The USP10 liver‐specific KO mice were obtained by crossing USP10flox/flox mice with AlbCre mice and inducing DEN to construct an orthotopic hepatoma model. After 9 months, the mice were killed and liver tissues were isolated (Figure 5A). In the USP10 KO mice group, both the number (p = 0.0039) and weight (p < 0.0001) of the tumors were higher than in the WT group (Figure 5B–D). Tumor tissues from USP10 KO mice and the WT group were selected, and qRT‐PCR (Figure 5E) and western blot (Figure 5F) analyses revealed that ATF4, PHGDH, PSAT1, and PSPH showed a trend of increase after USP10 KO. In addition, the liver tumor tissues were tested for LC‐MS analysis, and 135 relevant metabolites were found to be statistically different (Figures S2D and 5G). Enrichment analysis revealed that the metabolites were mainly concentrated in methionine metabolism, methylhistidine metabolism, ammonia recycling, arginine and proline metabolism, beta oxidation of very long chain fatty acids, and glycine serine metabolism pathways (Figure 5I), and the abundance of serine was considerably more significant in the USP10 KO group than the WT group (p = 0.00366639) (Figure 5H). Tumor tissues were stained by immunohistochemistry, and PHGDH/PSTA1/PSPH showed a trend of upregulation in the KO group (Figure 5J).

USP10 expression decreased in HCC tissues and linked to unfavorable prognosis

Western blot analysis was used to examine the expression levels of USP10 in 50 pairs of HCC and paracancerous tissues. The results showed that the protein levels of USP10 were significantly lower in most cancer tissues compared to paracancerous tissues (35:15) (Figure S2E). Immunohistochemical staining was undertaken on matrix microarrays that covered 50 cases. The H‐score was then calculated. Of the 49 pairs of organizations with complete information, USP10 levels were substantially lower in HCC compared with paracancerous tissues in 34 patients and significantly higher in 15 patients. (Figure 6A,B). These results were almost the same as the western blot findings, and t‐tests showed that the expression levels of USP10 in HCC were significantly lower than in paracancerous tissues (p = 0.0079). The expression of LKB1 was also lower in tumor tissues (p < 0.0001) (Figure 6C,D), and linear analysis revealed a positive correlation between USP10 and LKB1 in tumor tissues (r = 0.4783, p = 0.0006) (Figure 6E), consistent with findings from the TCGA datas.

We defined the USP10 high expression group (n = 15) as individuals with higher H‐scores of USP10 in HCC tissues compared with paracancerous tissues, and those with the opposite were defined as the USP10 low expression group (n = 34). No statistical difference was found between them regarding age, gender, maximum tumor diameter, AFP level, tumor Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer stage, microvascular invasion grade, or vascular invasion (Figure 6F). The result suggested that the tumor had low‐degree malignancy in the USP10 high expression group (p = 0.0039), and their overall survival (OS) was better than that of the low expression group (p = 0.0331) (Figure 6G). Although no statistically significant distinction was observed regarding disease‐free survival among the groups, the USP10 high expression group showed a longer median time to recurrence (Figure 6H). The findings suggest that individuals with high USP10 expression in HCC show better tumor differentiation and more prolonged OS.

DISCUSSION

Tumor cells often upregulate their biosynthesis, enhance energy generation, and maintain redox equilibrium through metabolic reprogramming to sustain cell proliferation and survival under nutrient‐poor settings. 28 , 29 They also need adequate amounts of serine and glycine, usually produced inside the cells or obtained from the surrounding environment. Through SSP, tumor cells can control methylation 30 and tRNA formylation, 31 , 32 increase macromolecule synthesis, 33 , 34 and counteract high levels of oxidative stress. 35 This study undertook LC‐MS analysis on 10 pairs of HCC and paracancerous tissues and found a significant increase in serine content in cancer tissues, which is consistent with previous studies. 10 Enrichment analysis revealed that serine and glycine metabolic pathways were the major differential pathways. An in vitro study indicated that the clonogenic ability of HCC was significantly reduced under serine‐deficient conditions.

As a member of the USP family, USP10 participates in the cell cycle and influences various processes, such as DNA damage repair, autophagy, and immune‐inflammatory response. 36 Recent studies have also shown that USP10 plays a crucial role in developing malignant tumors, but it plays different roles in different types of tumors and even in the same kind of tumor. For instance, USP10 is reduced in gastric, 37 lung, 38 liver, and colon 39 cancer tissues and inhibits tumor growth. However, other studies have reported that USP10 overexpression promotes tumor metastasis or proliferation in adult T cell leukemia, 40 glioblastoma multiforme, 41 chronic myeloid leukemia, 42 non‐small‐cell lung cancer, 43 and liver cancer. Regarding HCC, our team discovered that the deletion of USP10 can downregulate AMPK activity and PTEN protein expression, promoting HCC proliferation. This suggests a negative correlation between USP10 and HCC proliferation. In contrast, another study found that USP10 can stabilize YAP/TAZ expression, promoting HCC metastasis through deubiquitination. 44 These paradoxical phenomena have also been observed in colorectal, pancreatic, 45 , 46 renal, 47 and lung cancers. In summary, the function of USP10 is mainly related to the substrate it acts on, and it cannot be defined as an oncogene or antioncogene. Our study found that loss of USP10 significantly promotes the proliferative ability of HCC, as demonstrated through cell cloning and subcutaneous tumor formation in nude mice. Interestingly, the number and weight of the orthotopic hepatoma model in USP10 KO mice induced by DEN were higher than those in the control group, confirming the suppressive impact of USP10 on the proliferation of HCC. Immunohistochemistry revealed that USP10 expression was decreased in most human HCC tissues. The group with high USP10 expression had better HCC differentiation and OS than those with low expression.

Hepatocellular carcinoma requires higher proliferation rates and greater demand for serine and glycine, primarily supplied by the synthesis pathway. 48 The process of synthesizing serine can be summarized as follows: first, the intermediate product of glycolysis, 3‐phosphoglyceric acid, is oxidized to 3‐phosphohydroxypyruvate (3‐PHP) by PHGDH. Next, using glutamate as a nitrogen donor, 3‐PHP is transaminated by PSAT1. Finally, phosphoserine is dephosphorylated to serine by PSPH. 49 Several studies have found that PHGDH, PSAT1, and PSPH, the critical enzymes in SSP, are genetically amplified in tumors. 50 , 51 , 52 In addition, copy number alterations in serine and glycine synthesis genes were found in 6.1% of pan‐cancer samples encompassing 10,712 patients, with 4.8% showing copy number amplification. PHGDH was amplified in 5.5% of melanomas and 2.2% of breast cancers. PSPH was amplified in 12.2% of glioblastomas, 9.1% of head and neck tumors, and 4.3% of non‐small‐cell lung cancers. PSAT1 showed copy number amplification of the gene in 1% of ovarian tumors and 9% of sarcomas. Elevated expression of PHGDH was associated with poor prognosis in breast, lung, and gastric cancers. In HCC studies, increased PSPH expression promotes tumor proliferation, while the knockdown of PSPH reduces the proliferation rate in mice. 53 Recent studies have also shown that PHGDH activity promotes HCC proliferation by promoting serine synthesis. 54 In this study, liver‐specific USP10 KO mice were induced by DEN to establish an orthotopic hepatoma model. According to the LC‐MS assay, serine abundance in liver tumor tissues increased substantially in the USP10 KO group, and the expression of critical enzymes for SSP was similarly increased at the transcriptional and protein levels. In vitro, the abundance of serine in shUSP10 HCC cells was more significant than in the control group. Additionally, serine synthase was increased at the transcriptional and protein levels after USP10 knockdown. These findings suggest that loss of USP10 can upregulate the expression of serine synthase, leading to an increase in the serine content of tumor cells. Under serine‐deficient conditions, the expression of critical enzymes involved in SSP increased in both the USP10 knockdown and control groups. Interestingly, the difference in protein expression between the groups was reduced, and clone formation also revealed a reduction in the proliferation difference between the two groups. These results indicate that the reprogramming of serine metabolism is essential in facilitating the proliferation of HCC following the loss of USP10.

In addition to the direct effects of serine synthases, traditional oncogenes, and antioncogenes can promote SSP and are strongly influenced by transcriptional, epigenetic, 55 translational, and posttranslational modifications. 56 , 57 For example, the transcription factors ATF4, NRF2, MYC, and HIF‐α can regulate the transcriptional levels of genes involved in SSP. Direct binding to regulatory areas or inclusion in transcription factor complexes are the primary mechanisms by which this control regulates serine synthases. As a central transcriptional regulator, ATF4 activates SSP by directly binding to the promoter regions of PHGDH, PSAT1, and PSPH. Furthermore, mTOR can promote the translation of ATF4 and the stabilization of mRNA to influence downstream activities. Studies on pancreatic cancer have shown that the deletion of LKB1, combined with KRAS mutations, promotes SSP through the mTOR signaling pathway by increasing the expression of PSAT1, PSPH, SHMT1, and SHMT2. 26 USP10 has been shown to affect the proliferative ability of HCC through mTOR. 17 Therefore, we inferred that USP10 could affect the reprogramming of serine metabolism through the LKB1/mTOR/ATF4 axis to regulate HCC proliferation. Initially, a direct relationship between USP10 and LKB1 was identified in the TCGA database and 48 human HCC tissues. The utilization of immunofluorescence and immunoprecipitation techniques provided comprehensive evidence of the interaction between USP10 and LKB1. USP10 can eliminate ubiquitin molecules from LKB1 and promote its phosphorylation. Furthermore, the inhibition of USP10 leads to a decrease in the levels of pLKB1‐S334 and triggers the activation of pmTOR‐2481. This activation, in turn, facilitates the stimulation of SSP in tumor cells through the ATF4/PHGDH/PSAT1/PSPH axis, ultimately promoting HCC proliferation. Activation of pLKB1‐S334 reversed the effects of USP10 on serine synthesis and, subsequently, on HCC proliferation.

Our study shows that loss of USP10 can enhance SSP in HCC through the LKB1/mTOR/ATF4 axis and promotes HCC proliferation. A combination of USP10 agonist and exogenous serine restriction is hoped to provide a potential target for HCC therapy in future studies.
