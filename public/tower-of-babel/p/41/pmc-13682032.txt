Controlling nutritional status score as a novel prognostic tool for hospital mortality risk stratification in sepsis: a large-scale retrospective cohort analysis
Authors: Wei Song, Hai-bin Yin, Dan Liu, Di-en Yan, Hui-lei Zeng, Er-liang Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMalnutrition is associated with poor outcomes in critically ill patients. However, the prognostic value of the Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) score in sepsis remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the association between CONUT score and hospital death in sepsis patients.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, 2,143 sepsis patients from the eICU Collaborative Research Database were categorized into Mild (2–4 points), Moderate (5–8 points), and Severe (9–12 points) malnutrition groups based on the CONUT score. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the relationship between CONUT score groups and hospital death. Subgroup analyses were performed to assess the stability of the association across different populations. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was conducted to examine the potential non-linear relationship between CONUT score and hospital death.ResultsHospital death rates increased progressively across malnutrition groups: 8.13% in Mild, 16.14% in Moderate, and 23.27% in Severe groups (P < 0.001). After fully adjusting for confounding factors, compared with the Mild group, the risk of hospital mortality was significantly higher in the Moderate group (adjusted OR: 1.60, 95% CI: 1.07–2.38, P = 0.021) and Severe group (adjusted OR: 2.66, 95% CI: 1.73–4.07, P < 0.001). Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent associations across different demographic and clinical characteristics (all P for interaction > 0.05). RCS analysis revealed a significant linear dose–response relationship between CONUT score and risk of hospital mortality (P for overall < 0.001, P for nonlinearity = 0.685). Each one-point increase in CONUT score was associated with a 15% higher adjusted odds of hospital mortality (adjusted OR 1.15, 95% CI 1.09–1.21). The fully adjusted model showed good discrimination (AUC 0.773, 95% CI 0.747–0.799) and excellent calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow P = 0.390).ConclusionHigher CONUT scores were independently associated with increased risk of hospital mortality in sepsis patients. The CONUT score might serve as a valuable prognostic indicator for risk stratification in sepsis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14063-2.

Introduction

Sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction induced by a dysregulated host response to infection, presenting a significant global healthcare challenge [1]. Despite considerable advances in medical care, sepsis remains a leading cause of death in intensive care units worldwide [1, 2]. Current epidemiological data indicate that there are approximately 31.5 million cases of sepsis globally each year, potentially leading to 5.3 million deaths [3]. Although the age-standardized incidence of sepsis has decreased by 37% and mortality by 52.8% from 1990 to 2017 [4], the total number of sepsis cases and related deaths has increased due to population growth and aging [5]. Given the high mortality rate and substantial healthcare burden associated with sepsis, early identification of high-risk patients and accurate prognostic assessment are crucial for optimizing therapeutic strategies and improving clinical outcomes.

Malnutrition is a common complication among critically ill patients, particularly those with sepsis, affecting up to 10% of sepsis patients in the intensive care unit [6]. In sepsis patients, malnutrition significantly impairs immune function and inflammatory responses, leading to poor clinical outcomes. Studies have shown that malnourished sepsis patients have a 53% longer hospital stay, a 54% increase in hospitalization costs, and notably higher mortality compared to well-nourished patients [7, 8]. Despite its clinical importance, accurately assessing the nutritional status of sepsis patients remains difficult due to the complex interplay of acute illness, inflammatory responses, and metabolic changes.

Previous studies have shown that nutritional parameters, such as anemia and frailty [9, 10], are significantly associated with patient outcomes in the intensive care unit population. Among various assessment methods, the Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) score has emerged as an effective nutritional screening tool, particularly valuable for the early detection of undernutrition among hospitalized individuals. The clinical utility of this score lies in its incorporation of three key parameters: serum albumin levels, total cholesterol concentration, and total peripheral lymphocyte count. The CONUT score offers distinct advantages over traditional nutritional assessment methods by integrating markers of protein reserves (albumin), caloric depletion (total cholesterol), and immune defense (lymphocytes) into a single comprehensive index. Recent studies have explored the prognostic capabilities of the CONUT score in relation to post-treatment complications, clinicopathological factors, and long-term outcomes across various diseases, with a particular focus on cardiovascular diseases and cancer [11–15].

Evidence regarding the prognostic value of CONUT score in sepsis remains limited. One study suggested that higher CONUT scores were associated with a greater prevalence of sepsis [16], while another study in elderly ICU sepsis patients demonstrated a link between elevated CONUT scores and increased mortality [17]. However, these prior studies were limited by small sample sizes or restricted populations, and the dose–response relationship between CONUT score and mortality risk has not been characterized. To address these gaps, we conducted a large-scale, multicenter retrospective cohort study using the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD) to investigate the association between admission CONUT scores and hospital death in sepsis patients, aiming to assess its potential as a tool for risk stratification.

Methods

Participants and study design

This retrospective observational study utilized data from the eICU-CRD, a multi-center intensive care unit (ICU) database established between 2014 and 2015 [18]. The database contained high-granularity clinical data from over 200,000 ICU admissions across 208 hospitals and 335 ICUs throughout the United States. The comprehensive dataset included vital signs, laboratory measurements, medication records, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) components, care plan documentation, admission diagnoses, and patient medical histories, along with time-stamped diagnoses from structured problem lists and corresponding treatment selections. All data were automatically collected and stored through the Philips Healthcare eICU program. The database was released under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) safe harbor provision, with its re-identification risk independently certified by Privacert (Cambridge, MA) (HIPAA Certification no. 1031219-2). Access to the database was granted after our research team completed the Protecting Human Research Participants examination (Certificate number: 9728458).

All patients diagnosed with sepsis on admission to the ICU were included. Sepsis was defined as suspected or documented infection plus an acute increase in Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score ≥ 2 points [19], in accordance with the Sepsis-3 criteria. Infection was identified from ICD-9 codes recorded in the eICU-CRD. The SOFA score was calculated as the sum of six organ-system component scores — respiratory, coagulation, hepatic, cardiovascular, central nervous system, and renal — each ranging from 0 to 4 based on the original definition by Vincent et al. [20], using the worst value of each parameter within the first 24 h of ICU admission. SOFA scores were extracted from the eICU-CRD using validated open-source code from the MIT Laboratory for Computational Physiology (https://github.com/MIT-LCP/eicu-code), leveraging the comprehensive physiological data routinely captured through the APACHE IV documentation pipeline [21]. The following exclusion criteria were used: [1] without sepsis [2], age < 18 years [3], length of ICU stay < 1 day [4], missing data of albumin, total cholesterol, white blood cell count or lymphocyte percentage (required for calculating total lymphocyte count) within the first 24 h of ICU admission. In addition, for patients with multiple eligible ICU admissions within the same hospitalization, only the first ICU stay was retained to ensure that each patient contributed a single observation to the analysis. After screening, a total of 2,143 patients were included in the final analysis. The study flowchart was presented in Fig. 1. As this study utilized a large, pre-existing multicenter critical care database, all eligible sepsis patients meeting the inclusion criteria were included to maximize statistical power and ensure generalizability, rather than relying on an a priori sample size calculation.

Data collection

The following patient information was collected within 24 h of ICU admission. Demographic data included age, sex, and race. Anthropometric measurements comprised body mass index (BMI). Vital signs consisted of systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), temperature, heart rate, respiration rate, and SaO2. Laboratory parameters included white blood cell count, lymphocyte percentage, neutrophil percentage, monocyte percentage, red blood cell count, hemoglobin, platelet count, glucose, hematocrit, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), albumin, creatinine (CR), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), sodium, potassium, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), total cholesterol (TC), and triglycerides (TG). The total lymphocyte count was calculated by multiplying the white blood cell count by the lymphocyte percentage. Comorbidities and medical history included coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, congestive heart failure, hypertension, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic kidney disease, acute kidney injury, and stroke. Medication use data encompassed antiplatelet drugs, warfarin, vasoactive agents (including norepinephrine, epinephrine, and vasopressin), albumin treatment, and antibiotics. Interventions included dialysis and mechanical ventilation. CONUT classes were defined based on serum albumin (g/dL), total lymphocyte count (count/mm3), and total cholesterol (mg/dL) as reported in Table 1.

Grouping and outcome

According to the CONUT score, all patients were divided into three groups: Mild malnutrition (CONUT score 2–4, n = 504), Moderate malnutrition (CONUT score 5–8, n = 973), and Severe malnutrition (CONUT score 9–12, n = 666) [22]. The primary outcome was hospital death. Of note, all enrolled patients had a CONUT score ≥ 2, indicating that this sepsis cohort exhibited universal nutritional risk at ICU admission.

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using ANOVA analysis, while skewed distributed continuous variables were presented as median (Q1, Q3) and compared using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages) and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The association between CONUT score and hospital death was evaluated using multivariable logistic regression models. Three models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for sex, age, and race; Model 3 was fully adjusted for sex, age, race, SBP, SaO2, coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, acute kidney injury, stroke, red blood cell count, hemoglobin, platelets, hematocrit, glucose, ALT, AST, CR, BUN, warfarin, vasoactive drugs, norepinephrine, epinephrine, albumin treatment, antibiotics, dialysis, and mechanical ventilation. The covariates included in Model 3 were selected based on clinical experience and stepwise regression, with only those variables showing a significance level of p < 0.05 being retained. Subgroup analysis was conducted to estimate the consistency of the effect in different groups including age (≤ 65 or > 65 years), sex (male or female), race (Caucasian, African American, or Hispanic), hypertension (yes or no), and diabetes (yes or no). The same covariates as Model 3 were adjusted in all subgroup analyses. The potential non-linear relationship between CONUT score and hospital death was examined using restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the CONUT score. The number of knots was selected based on the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The RCS model was adjusted for the same covariates as Model 3. Missing values for Model 3 covariates were minimal (mean 0.21%, maximum 2.05%; Supplementary Table S1); a complete-case approach was used for multivariable analyses (n = 2,056), and a sensitivity analysis based on multiple imputation by chained equations (m = 5) yielded consistent results (Supplementary Table S2). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using the R software environment (Version 4.3.2; The R Foundation; available at http://www.R-project.org).

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

The baseline characteristics were shown in Table 2. A total of 2,143 sepsis patients were included in this study and categorized into Mild malnutrition (median CONUT score 3.00 [3.00, 4.00]), Moderate malnutrition (6.00 [5.00, 7.00]), and Severe malnutrition groups (10.00 [9.00, 11.00]). Hospital deaths occurred in 41 (8.13%), 157 (16.14%), and 155 (23.27%) patients in the three groups, respectively (P < 0.001). Notably, all enrolled patients had a CONUT score ≥ 2, indicating varying degrees of nutritional risk in this critically ill sepsis cohort.

Regarding demographic characteristics, significant differences were observed in age and sex distribution among the three groups. For anthropometric measurement and vital signs, BMI, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure decreased significantly with higher CONUT scores. Temperature also differed significantly across groups (P = 0.024). Most laboratory parameters showed significant differences among groups. With increasing CONUT scores, white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, AST, creatinine, and BUN levels increased progressively, while lymphocyte percentage, monocyte percentage, red blood cell count, hemoglobin, platelet count, glucose, hematocrit, albumin, sodium, LDL, total cholesterol, and triglycerides levels decreased significantly. Regarding comorbidities, the prevalence of atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, and acute kidney injury increased with higher CONUT scores, while coronary artery disease, hypertension, and COPD showed an opposite trend. For therapeutic interventions, the rates of vasoactive drug use, norepinephrine use, albumin treatment, mechanical ventilation, and dialysis all increased significantly with higher CONUT scores. Other variables showed no significant differences among groups.

Relationship between CONUT score and hospital death in sepsis patients

The association between CONUT score and hospital death was evaluated using logistic regression analysis (Table 3). In the unadjusted model (Model 1), compared with the Mild group, the odds of hospital death were significantly higher in both the Moderate group (OR 2.17, 95% CI 1.51–3.12, P < 0.001) and the Severe group (OR 3.43, 95% CI 2.37–4.94, P < 0.001). After adjustment for demographic characteristics (Model 2), the association remained significant for the Moderate (adjusted OR 2.09, 95% CI 1.45–3.00, P < 0.001) and Severe (adjusted OR 3.33, 95% CI 2.30–4.81, P < 0.001) groups. In the fully adjusted Model 3, the elevated odds persisted for both the Moderate group (adjusted OR 1.60, 95% CI 1.07–2.38, P = 0.021) and the Severe group (adjusted OR 2.66, 95% CI 1.73–4.07, P < 0.001). The dose–response relationship is further characterized in Section "CONUT score and hospital death connection assessed using RCS curve".

CONUT score and hospital death connection assessed using RCS curve

The RCS analysis was performed to examine the dose–response relationship between CONUT score and hospital death (Fig. 2), with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the CONUT score and adjustment for the covariates included in Model 3. A significant overall association was observed (P for overall < 0.001), and the test for non-linearity suggested a linear relationship between CONUT score and risk of hospital death (P for nonlinearity = 0.685). Each one-point increase in CONUT score was associated with a 15.1% higher adjusted odds of hospital death (adjusted OR 1.15, 95% CI: 1.09–1.21). The curve demonstrated that the risk of hospital death increased monotonically with higher CONUT scores. The fully adjusted Model 3 also demonstrated good discrimination (AUC = 0.773, 95% CI 0.747–0.799) and excellent calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow P = 0.390), with the addition of the CONUT score providing a small but statistically significant gain in discrimination compared with a base model excluding it (ΔAUC = 0.011, P = 0.031; Supplementary Table S3).

Discussion

After evaluating 2,143 sepsis patients from eICU-CRD, we demonstrated that the CONUT score can serve as a valuable prognostic indicator for sepsis outcomes. Higher CONUT scores were independently associated with increased risk of hospital mortality in sepsis patients. Compared with patients with Mild malnutrition, those with Moderate and Severe malnutrition showed significantly higher odds of hospital mortality, even after adjusting for potential confounders.

These findings were consistent with previous studies that have established the prognostic value of the CONUT score in predicting clinical outcomes, extending beyond sepsis patients. Yamaguchi et al. conducted a retrospective analysis of 307 elderly ICU patients with sepsis and found that higher CONUT scores were independently associated with increased mortality (OR = 1.214, p = 0.002) [17]. In a study involving 1,000 medical inpatients, Miano et al. demonstrated that elevated CONUT scores were significantly associated with increased in-hospital mortality risk (OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.17–1.49, p < 0.001) [16]. A meta-analysis by Arero et al., including 11,837 patients with coronary artery disease, showed that malnutrition, as defined by the CONUT score, was an independent predictor of all-cause mortality (HR = 2.45, 95% CI: 1.88–3.19, p < 0.00001) [11].

Several pathophysiological mechanisms underlie the prognostic value of each CONUT component in sepsis. Hypoalbuminemia reflects impaired hepatic synthesis under inflammatory stress, renal albumin loss, and increased capillary permeability [23–27], and predicts adverse outcomes by signaling systemic illness severity and by reducing the efficacy of protein-bound antibiotics [28]. Hypocholesterolemia results from suppressed hepatic apolipoprotein synthesis and accelerated cholesterol catabolism mediated by inflammatory cytokines and acute-phase enzymes such as cholesterol-25-hydroxylase and phospholipase A2 [29–32], and lower cholesterol levels impair the binding and neutralization of bacterial endotoxins by lipoproteins. Lymphocytopenia, a hallmark of sepsis-induced immunosuppression, arises from accelerated lymphocyte apoptosis and lymphoid redistribution and has been shown to triple the risk of severe sepsis [33–36], reflecting failure of adaptive immune defense. Taken together, by simultaneously capturing protein reserve (albumin), caloric and lipid-metabolic depletion (total cholesterol), and immune competence (lymphocyte count), the CONUT score integrates three distinct yet interconnected dimensions of host vulnerability — a feature that likely explains its robust independent prognostic performance in our cohort and supports its use as a parsimonious bedside risk indicator beyond established clinical variables.

A notable observation in our cohort was the universal presence of nutritional risk, as all included patients had a CONUT score ≥ 2. While previous studies have reported that around 10–18% of sepsis patients are diagnosed with malnutrition upon admission to ICU [6, 8], our study found a markedly higher prevalence of nutritional deficiencies. This discrepancy might be due to the CONUT score’s ability to comprehensively assess nutritional status through multiple parameters, including protein reserves, caloric depletion, and immune function [37–39]. The high prevalence of nutritional risk observed in our cohort reinforces the critical need for systematic nutritional evaluation upon ICU admission for sepsis patients. Furthermore, our multivariable logistic analyses and RCS curve collectively demonstrated that CONUT score was independently associated with hospital mortality, with a significant linear dose-response relationship. These findings not only underscored the importance of systematic and timely nutritional assessment upon ICU admission, but also suggested that interventions aimed at improving nutritional status may benefit patient survival across the full spectrum of CONUT scores. Previous studies have supported this notion, showing that higher energy and protein intakes during the first week could improve outcomes in sepsis patients with high nutritional risk [40], and adequate nutritional support could significantly lower mortality rates [41]. Building on this evidence, patients with an admission CONUT score ≥ 5 (Moderate-to-Severe malnutrition) — corresponding to a 60–166% adjusted increase in odds of hospital death in our cohort — may particularly warrant consideration for early, intensified nutritional support.

Our subgroup analyses further strengthened the clinical utility of these findings. The association between CONUT scores and hospital mortality remained stable across age groups, gender, race, and various comorbidities, with no significant interactions observed. This consistency was particularly valuable in sepsis management, where patients often presented with multiple comorbidities and heterogeneous clinical scenarios. Our findings demonstrated that regardless of patient characteristics or underlying conditions, CONUT score served as a reliable prognostic tool, making it a robust indicator for risk stratification in the complex and diverse population of sepsis patients.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a retrospective single-database study based on U.S. ICUs from 2014 to 2015, residual selection bias is possible and the generalizability of our findings to other healthcare settings warrants further validation. Although the eICU-CRD covers admissions from 2014 to 2015, the Sepsis-3 definition has remained the standard since 2016, and the pathophysiological basis of the three CONUT components has not materially changed; the database continues to be widely used in contemporary critical care research. Second, CONUT was assessed only at ICU admission, and we were unable to evaluate how dynamic changes in nutritional status during hospitalization, including the effect of nutritional interventions, may further refine risk prediction. Third, although infection was identified using ICD-9 codes, the specific infection site was not incorporated as a covariate in the multivariate model, as the eICU-CRD encodes infection sites primarily through the APACHE IV single-best-fit admission diagnosis, which does not align fully with our Sepsis-3-based cohort definition; nevertheless, our subgroup analyses suggested that the prognostic value of CONUT was consistent across patient characteristics. Additionally, because the eICU-CRD’s de-identification protocol precludes linkage across separate hospitalizations, a small number of individuals admitted more than once during the study period may have contributed multiple records to our cohort, though the impact on the overall findings is expected to be minimal. Finally, the predominance of Caucasian patients (71.8%) in this U.S.-based cohort may limit the generalizability of our findings to other racial/ethnic groups and healthcare settings. Prospective studies are needed to confirm our findings and assess whether dynamic monitoring of CONUT scores could improve outcome prediction in sepsis patients. Additionally, investigating the potential benefits of targeted nutritional interventions based on CONUT scores would be valuable. Lastly, comparative studies examining the predictive performance of CONUT versus established severity scores (e.g., SOFA, APACHE IV) and other nutritional assessment tools (e.g., NUTRIC, PNI) using metrics such as AUC and net reclassification improvement are warranted.

Conclusion

Higher CONUT scores were independently associated with increased risk of hospital mortality in sepsis patients. The CONUT score may serve as a valuable prognostic indicator for risk stratification in sepsis.
