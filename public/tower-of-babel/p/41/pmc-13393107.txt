A Lung Transplant Recipient With Unusual Cells on a Biopsy From a Pulmonary Nodule
Authors: Christina M Homer, Anastasiya Pigal, Gail Cunningham, Doug Chin, Steve Hays, Sarah Doernberg
Published: 2026

A 59-year-old man who underwent a bilateral orthotopic lung transplant 13 years prior presented with cough and chest pain. His transplant history was remarkable for chronic lung allograft dysfunction, for which he received photopheresis. His current immunosuppression included prednisone 10 mg daily, tacrolimus, and mycophenolate mofetil. There was no recent history of acute rejection. He initially presented for care with a flu-like illness with nonproductive cough, sneezing, rhinorrhea, and odynophagia. Respiratory viral testing was negative, and symptoms improved spontaneously after several days. Two weeks later, his cough returned. Chest radiographs showed no pulmonary consolidation. Sputum respiratory culture grew only oronasal flora. He then developed progressive pleuritic chest pain with ongoing cough. A noncontrast computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest was done. The scan revealed a 1-cm left upper lung soft tissue nodule, new since the last CT scan done a year prior, abutting the pleura. Imaging appearance was thought to be consistent with a neoplasm or an inflammatory or infectious nodule. A complete blood count at the time of the CT chest scan showed a normal white blood cell count but was remarkable for isolated mild peripheral eosinophilia (330/mm3, 3.9%, laboratory cutoff >3%) on the differential. His history was notable for exposure to dust from dirt bike racing, although he routinely wore N95 masks during these events. He lived in a wooded area of rural Oregon and had not traveled. He denied significant occupational or animal exposures. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) yielded oronasal flora on bacterial culture, negative cultures for fungal and mycobacterial organisms, negative galactomannan, and negative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing for Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Pneumocystis jirovecii. The BAL cell count was notable for 14% eosinophils. A CT-guided biopsy of the lung nodule was performed and yielded the following image of the fine needle aspirate (FNA; Figure 1).

What is your diagnosis?

Diagnosis: Cryptococcal pulmonary infection with titan cell formation.

Grocott's methanamine silver staining revealed large, round fungi that were significantly larger than surrounding white blood cells (Figure 2A and 2B). Given the unusually large size of the fungal cells (20–40 µm), serum testing for Coccidioides complement fixation, immunodiffusion, and antigen were sent and returned negative. A fungal antibody panel by complement fixation was negative for Histoplasma, Blastomyces, and Aspergillus. Serum cryptococcal antigen (CrAg) testing returned positive (titer 1:40). Per guidelines, a lumbar puncture was performed. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis revealed <1 white blood cell, negative CrAg, and negative Coccidioides immunodiffusion. Treatment with fluconazole was initiated for pulmonary cryptococcosis, and his pleuritic chest pain and cough resolved. This was possibly due to improving pleuritic inflammation, although there was no clear imaging correlate. The soft tissue nodule was unchanged on repeat chest CT after 1 year of fluconazole treatment, so he was transitioned to posaconazole treatment. He remains asymptomatic with stable size of the left upper lobe lung nodule after 1 year of posaconazole treatment.

The vast majority of infections that involve round fungal cells that are significantly larger than neutrophils (Figure 2A) are caused by Coccidioides, which forms large, spherical structures with regular internal segmentations, known as spherules [1]. Given this patient's lack of exposure to Coccidioides-endemic regions (Arizona and central/southern California) and negative Coccidioides testing, coccidioidomycosis was improbable. Other extremely rare causes of large round fungal giant cells [2], such as Blastomyces dermatitidis (no exposure history), Emmonsia spp. (would expect thicker cell walls and a miliary pattern on pulmonary imaging), and Candida spp. (not expected to cause pulmonary nodules, would have expected culture growth), seemed unlikely. Given the positive serum CrAg and polysaccharide capsule observed on pathology, the diagnosis of cryptococcal infection was made. This case illustrates that one must consider titan cells, a rare variant of cryptococcal morphology, when identifying large, round fungal cells.

Although most yeast cells of Cryptococcus spp. that cause human infections are 5–7 µm in diameter, this fungus can grow as a unique cell morphology, known as the titan cell, reaching up to 100 µm in diameter [3]. Titan cells have been rarely observed in human specimens [2, 4, 5] and are likely underdiagnosed as they revert to normal morphology during standard laboratory culture [4]. Thus, data regarding titan cells primarily derive from animal studies, where titan cells are more resistant to host clearance and promote dissemination outside of the initial pulmonary site of infection [6, 7]. Additionally, in vitro studies have shown that titan cells have a different polysaccharide capsule structure and give rise to offspring cells that have genomic instability that allows them to adapt to new environments; both factors contribute to potential antifungal drug resistance [8]. This patient's slow imaging response to treatment may be related to the large number of titan cells found in his biopsy, although it is difficult to distinguish from slow response due to transplant-related immunosuppression or residual scar tissue that may not improve further.

The species of Cryptococcus in this case could not be identified because cultures and universal fungal PCR sent on the FNA sample were negative. However, the differential must include Cryptococcus deuterogattii, the causative agent of the cryptococcal outbreak in the Pacific Northwest, where this patient lives. Intriguingly, in laboratory studies, C. deuterogattii also displayed the highest rates of titanization [9] compared with other cryptococcal species.

The other prominent part of this patient's presentation is the eosinophil accumulation in the BAL fluid and borderline peripheral eosinophilia. Increased pulmonary eosinophil accumulation has been observed in a mouse model of infection with titan cells [6, 7] but, to our knowledge, not reported in the limited number of human infection cases published [2, 4, 5]. This characteristic of the clinical presentation is shared with coccidioidomycosis, and its mechanism remains unknown. An intriguing hypothesis is that the eosinophilia is due to their shared large cell size [10], which presumably causes the fungal cells to remain extracellular, mimicking parasites.

This case illustrates that, when evaluating round fungal organisms that are larger than neutrophils, the clinician should consider cryptococcal titan cells, especially if the exposure history is not consistent with coccidioidomycosis. The presence of titan cells should also increase suspicion for non-neoformans cryptococcal species.
