Advances in detection and treatment of maternal sepsis
Authors: Michael J Fassett
Published: 2025

In response to rising maternal morbidity and mortality, many nations, states and health systems have convened maternal mortality reviews to describe maternal morbidity and mortality and evaluate the preventability of each associated cause [1,2]. One such review, the California Maternal Quality Care Collaborative Pregnancy Associated Mortality Review (CMQCC-PAMR), found that deaths from hemorrhage, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease were the most common and had the most opportunity for prevention; sepsis accounted for 9 % of deaths and 63 % of those deaths were deemed to have a good to strong chance of prevention [3]. In response, educational programs, collaboratives, and teaching toolkits to reduce morbidity and mortality due to obstetric hemorrhage, hypertensive disease, and venous thromboembolism were developed and widely implemented throughout California, resulting in a significant reduction in preventable mortality in these areas. As a result, obstetric sepsis is now the second leading cause of preventable maternal morbidity and mortality in California and remains a leading cause worldwide [4].

While early identification and treatment of maternal sepsis can improve outcomes, the normal physiological changes of labor can overlap with changes in vital signs seen with maternal sepsis [5]. As a result, multiple early warning systems modified for pregnancy incorporating available clinical data have been developed, evaluated, and validated to assist the clinician with detection of maternal sepsis. An important feature of these pregnancy-adjusted approaches is an in-person evaluation of the patient after warning signs are triggered, allowing for clinical judgment to be used to distinguish the more common diagnosis of chorioamnionitis from maternal sepsis. Main et al. have evaluated several sets of screening criteria [[6], [7], [8], [9]] for maternal sepsis and found that the CMQCC and UK Obstetric Surveillance System (UKOSS) pregnancy-adjusted screening criteria have the lowest false-positive results while maintaining greater than 90 % sensitivity [10].

In the United States, with the inclusion of obstetric inpatients in the Joint Commission sepsis core performance metric, greater attention will be brought to the approach to screening and treatment of maternal sepsis as those results will now be included in the overall hospital composite score for sepsis treatment [11]. Previous Joint Commission quality improvement efforts have brought about significant improvements; for example, that concerning the administration of antenatal corticosteroids resulted in its eventual retirement [12]. It is hoped that the response to sepsis will similarly benefit from such an approach.

Electronic medical records, repositories for the often real-time clinical data points included in maternal early warning systems, may be able to play a role in alerting clinicians to the meeting of diagnostic criteria suggestive of maternal sepsis. This alert would prompt a face-to-face evaluation for the source and severity of the clinical infection and provide decision support for appropriate fluid resuscitation and selection of evidence-based source-directed antimicrobials. Evaluation of the feasibility of incorporating the components of maternal early warning systems into electronic health records has begun, with some logistical considerations and feasibility of this approach being evaluated [13]. Care should be taken, however, in ensuring the alerts and decision support are meaningful and do not contribute significantly to alert fatigue [14].

With improved screening tools to detect maternal sepsis, evidence-based treatment protocols, and electronic medical record decision support, improvements in short- and long-term outcomes due to maternal sepsis may be realized and should be validated by well-designed clinical research evaluating outcomes.
