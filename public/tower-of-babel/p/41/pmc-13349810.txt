Reporting patterns of renal failure associated with immunosuppressant combinations following liver transplantation: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Aiko Urawa, Chihiro Shiraishi, Toru Ogura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmunosuppressant regimens are essential for preventing graft rejection in liver transplant recipients; however, their use has also been associated with increased reporting of renal failure. This study evaluated disproportional reporting patterns of renal failure associated with immunosuppressant combinations using the U.S. Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS).MethodsA retrospective signal detection analysis was conducted using FAERS data from 2004 (Q1) to 2024 (Q2). Five exposure groups were defined for each core calcineurin inhibitor (tacrolimus or cyclosporine), administered as monotherapy or combined with mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), prednisone, or everolimus. Reporting odds ratios (RORs) and adjusted RORs were calculated for renal failure outcomes, using the corresponding monotherapy as the reference. Regional subgroup and sensitivity analyses were also performed.ResultsAdding MMF to tacrolimus- or cyclosporine-based regimens was associated with lower disproportional reporting of renal failure versus monotherapy. Regional analyses revealed variability in reporting patterns MMF-containing regimens showed lower disproportionality in Europe, whereas in North America, the tacrolimus-MMF-prednisone combination demonstrated lower reporting signals. In Latin America and Asia, overall reporting proportions of renal failure were lower, and additional agents did not consistently reduce signals. Sensitivity analyses across renal failure-related preferred terms yielded consistent patterns.ConclusionsThese findings reflect disproportional reporting patterns rather than causal or protective effects and should be interpreted cautiously given inherent FAERS limitations, including missing clinical details and lack of denominator data. The results are hypothesis-generating and warrant further investigation using real-world clinical datasets to better characterize renal safety across immunosuppressant combinations.Keywords: Calcineurin inhibitors, Immunosuppressive agents, Liver transplantation, Mycophenolate mofetil, Renal insufficiency

INTRODUCTION

Liver transplantation has become the standard of care for patients with end-stage liver disease and hepatocellular carcinoma, accompanied by substantial improvements in both patient and graft survival over the past several decades. With global 5-year survival rates now exceeding 75%, a growing proportion of recipients achieve long-term survival [1]. However, these advances have introduced new clinical challenges that require careful management during the posttransplantation period. Among these challenges, renal impairment remains a major concern, as it can substantially affect long-term health outcomes and quality of life in liver transplant recipients [2]. A principal contributor to posttransplant renal dysfunction is nephrotoxicity associated with calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs), particularly tacrolimus and cyclosporine, which form the backbone of most immunosuppressive regimens. The onset and progression of CNI-related renal impairment vary according to exposure duration and cumulative dose. Renal dysfunction may gradually worsen over time, ultimately progressing to renal failure, which represents a more advanced and clinically severe stage of kidney function decline. Importantly, acute kidney injury (AKI) can evolve into acute renal failure, while chronic kidney disease (CKD) may progress to chronic renal failure. The Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) categorizes renal adverse events using distinct preferred terms, including “renal failure acute” and “renal failure chronic” for renal failure-related outcomes, as well as “acute kidney injury” and “chronic kidney disease” for kidney injury conditions. Within MedDRA, renal failure generally denotes a substantial and often irreversible loss of kidney function, typically observed in chronic clinical settings, whereas kidney injury refers to an abrupt decline in kidney function that may be reversible. The temporal distinction between acute and chronic kidney conditions is clinically meaningful: AKI develops rapidly over hours to days, while CKD is defined by structural or functional kidney abnormalities persisting for more than 3 months [3]. Recognizing these distinctions is critical, as timely identification and management of AKI may allow renal recovery, whereas CKD more often reflects ongoing or irreversible damage requiring long-term management strategies. Previous clinical studies have reported that approximately 20% of liver transplant recipients develop chronic renal failure within 5 years following transplantation [4]. Multiple factors have been associated with posttransplant renal impairment, including preexisting renal insufficiency, such as hepatorenal syndrome, perioperative ischemia-reperfusion injury, and early posttransplant AKI, which may subsequently progress to CKD [4]. Recipient-related characteristics, including age, sex, and baseline kidney function, as well as treatment-related factors, further contribute to interindividual variability in renal outcomes [5]. The established relationship between immunosuppressive therapy and kidney function has driven extensive research into CNI-sparing and CNI-minimization strategies [6]. The introduction of mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) and mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitors, including sirolimus and everolimus, has enabled alternative combination regimens designed to maintain adequate immunosuppression while mitigating nephrotoxicity. Additionally, previous studies have suggested that genetic polymorphisms affecting drug-metabolizing enzymes, particularly cytochrome P450 3A5, can influence tacrolimus metabolism and its associated nephrotoxic risk [7].

Given the multifactorial nature of posttransplant renal impairment, a nuanced understanding of the clinical and pharmacologic contexts associated with renal outcomes remains essential [8]. Accordingly, this study utilized the U.S. Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) [9] to examine reporting patterns suggestive of potential associations between immunosuppressant combinations and renal failure in liver transplant recipients. It is important to emphasize that FAERS is a spontaneous reporting system that lacks detailed information on exposure duration, transplant timing, comorbidities, and baseline kidney function. As a result, while such exploratory analyses can identify disproportional reporting patterns suggestive of potential safety signals, they cannot provide incidence estimates, comparative risk assessments, or causal inferences. Within this framework, the present analysis aims to generate hypotheses that may inform future clinical studies and support multidisciplinary collaboration among transplant physicians, pharmacists, and transplant coordinators to optimize posttransplant care [2].

METHODS

As the FAERS data is an unlinkable anonymized database open to the public, approval from an Institutional Review Board was not required.

Data Source

This study utilized the FAERS, a publicly accessible pharmacovigilance database that collects global reports of adverse events. FAERS has been operational since 2004, succeeding the Adverse Event Reporting System (AERS). Data files were retrieved from the official FAERS website on September 29, 2024. To ensure compatibility across reporting periods, differences in variable names and data structures between AERS and FAERS were reconciled in accordance with the accompanying documentation. For example, identifiers such as the unique number {ISR} in AERS were aligned with {primaryid} in FAERS, and the identifier used for {CASE} was mapped to {caseid}. Throughout this manuscript, curly braces are used to denote variable names as they appear in FAERS. Five key data files were utilized: DEMOyyQq.txt (report demographic information), DRUGyyQq.txt (drug information), REACyyQq.txt (adverse events), THERyyQq.txt (therapy start and end dates), and INDIyyQq.txt (indications). Consistent with the structure of the FAERS database, only records with the highest safety report version number {caseversion} for each report were included. Although the AERS database does not provide {caseversion}, a comparable approach was implemented using {ISR} and {CASE}. Comprehensive preprocessing was conducted to clean and standardize the data prior to statistical analysis. LTR age at event {age} was converted to years, {weight} was standardized to kilograms, and unexpected or inconsistent entries for variables such as {sex}, {age}, {weight}, and {reporter_country} were systematically addressed. In addition, formatting issues in specific AERS files required manual correction; missing line breaks were inserted at lines 322,967, 247,896, and 446,738 of DRUG11Q2.txt, DRUG11Q3.txt, and DRUG11Q4.txt, respectively. These corrections restored the intended data structure without altering the underlying content, thereby preserving data integrity for reliable analysis. Detailed methodologies for all preprocessing steps are provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Study Design

This analysis focused on reports involving liver transplant recipients who were administered one or more of five immunosuppressants—tacrolimus, cyclosporine, prednisone, MMF, or everolimus—between the first quarter of 2004 (2004Q1) and the second quarter of 2024 (2024Q2). Inclusion was restricted to adverse event reports documenting the use of these agents for liver transplantation, as recorded in the variable {indi_pt}. Adverse events reported in FAERS are coded using standardized preferred terms from MedDRA. Because FAERS applies the MedDRA version current at the time of each quarterly data release, MedDRA versions varied across reporting periods, as summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The primary outcome was the reporting of renal failure, defined by three preferred terms in FAERS: “renal failure,” “renal failure acute,” and “renal failure chronic.” When multiple renal failure-related preferred terms were reported within the same case, the case was counted only once to prevent duplicate counting. Other terms, including “acute kidney injury” and “chronic kidney disease,” were not included in the primary outcome because they are classified separately in MedDRA. Immunosuppressants were identified using both generic and brand names recorded in {drugname} for reports submitted between 2004Q1 and 2014Q2. Beginning in 2014Q3, FAERS introduced the variable {prod_ai}, which specifies product active ingredients. For reports from 2014Q3 through 2024Q2, immunosuppressants were therefore primarily identified using {prod_ai}. However, when {prod_ai} data were missing, searches reverted to both generic and brand names recorded in {drugname}. All generic and brand names used for case identification are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Reports were categorized according to immunosuppressant combinations. Group naming followed standardized abbreviations: T for tacrolimus, C for cyclosporine, P for prednisone, M for MMF, and E for everolimus. Each regimen group included only the specified drugs and explicitly excluded all others. Accordingly, the T group represents tacrolimus monotherapy, whereas the TMP group includes tacrolimus, MMF, and prednisone while excluding cyclosporine and everolimus. In total, ten distinct immunosuppressant regimen groups were analyzed: T, TM, TMP, TME, TMPE, and C, CM, CMP, CME, and CMPE. This classification framework enabled an exploratory assessment of renal failure reporting patterns across different immunosuppressant combinations.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive analyses summarized categorical variables as frequencies with reporting proportions (RPs) [10] and continuous variables as medians with first and third quartiles. The RP was calculated as follows: RP = (number of reports in the category of interest) / (number of reports in the target group). To evaluate disproportional reporting patterns between immunosuppressant regimens and renal failure, reporting odds ratios (RORs) [11] with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using univariate binomial logistic regression. Adjusted RORs (aRORs) with 95% CIs were estimated using multivariable logistic regression models that accounted for {age}, {sex}, and {reporter_country}. For tacrolimus-based regimens, the T group served as the reference category, whereas the C group was used as the reference for cyclosporine-based regimens. Cases with unknown values for any of the variables {age}, {sex}, or {reporter_country} were not imputed and were excluded from the aROR analyses. Imputation was not performed because reliable imputation generally requires a low proportion of missing data and the availability of multiple correlated covariates, conditions that were not met within the FAERS dataset. Although both imputation and case exclusion have inherent limitations, exclusion was selected for this analysis. To reduce potential bias arising from missing data, a signal was considered meaningful only when both the ROR and aROR were statistically significant and demonstrated effects in the same direction. This combined criterion was intended to minimize spurious signals attributable to incomplete covariate information, thereby balancing confounder adjustment with data inclusiveness while maintaining transparency regarding the methodological constraints inherent in large-scale spontaneous reporting systems. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using R ver. 4.4.1 (R Foundation) [12]. Sample R code sufficient to reproduce the results presented in Tables 1–5 and Fig. 1 is provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Because the FAERS dataset consists of spontaneous reports lacking exposure denominators and detailed clinical context, conventional epidemiological methods are not applicable. To address this inherent limitation, all analyses were conducted at the reporting level, consistent with established pharmacovigilance methodologies. Accordingly, each metric was explicitly prefixed with the term “reporting” to emphasize that the estimates reflect disproportionality in adverse event reporting rather than true incidence or risk.

To address potential geographic bias and enhance analytical robustness, subgroup analyses were performed by continent to explore regional variation in renal failure reporting. Subgroups included North America, Latin America, Asia, Europe, Oceania, and Africa. Within each subgroup, RORs and aRORs were calculated using the same analytical approach applied to the overall dataset. These analyses functioned as sensitivity assessments to evaluate whether geographic factors influenced the observed reporting patterns. In addition, to assess the robustness of findings related to the primary outcome, separate analyses were conducted for each Preferred Term—“renal failure,” “renal failure acute,” and “renal failure chronic”—as defined in FAERS. These complementary analyses facilitated evaluation of signal consistency across related renal failure definitions.

RESULTS

Report Characteristics

Analysis of the FAERS database from 2004Q1 through 2024Q2 identified 11,826 case reports related to liver transplantation involving one or more immunosuppressants. After application of the exclusion criteria, 1,216 reports were removed, yielding a final analytical dataset of 10,610 reports. Fig. 1 illustrates the distribution of reports across the ten immunosuppressant regimens examined. The distribution was as follows: T group (n=4,847), TM group (n=1,148), TMP group (n=793), TME group (n=57), TMPE group (n=32), C group (n=703), CM group (n=159), CMP group (n=71), CME group (n=8), and CMPE group (n=2). Table 1 provides a descriptive summary of case characteristics for each regimen. Across all regimens, reports involving male cases consistently outnumbered those involving female cases, a pattern that may reflect reporting practices or underlying demographic characteristics rather than true differences in clinical sex distribution. The median reported age was broadly similar across regimens, typically ranging from 54 to 58 years, suggesting that these immunosuppressive treatments are most frequently reported among middle-aged to older adult recipients. Weight information was largely unavailable, with proportions of unknown values ranging from 73.7% to 100% across regimens, a level of missingness that substantially limits interpretation of weight-related patterns. Geographic reporting trends also varied across regimens. For the T group, the majority of reports originated from North America, whereas for other combinations, European countries contributed the highest proportion of reports. These distributional differences supported the inclusion of continent-based subgroup analyses to further explore potential geographic variation in renal failure reporting patterns. Overall, these observations reflect reporting distributions within the FAERS database and should not be interpreted as estimates of incidence or true prevalence among liver transplant recipients.

Adverse Events Related to Renal Failure

Table 2 presents a detailed summary of adverse events related to renal failure, kidney impairment, and other known adverse events associated with immunosuppressants across treatment groups. Table 3 summarizes the RORs and aRORs for three adverse events—renal failure, AKI, and renal impairment—for tacrolimus-based regimens, using the T group as the reference category. Corresponding results for other adverse events are provided in Supplementary Table 3. Together, these measures enable comparative evaluation of RPs for adverse events, particularly renal failure, across different immunosuppressant combinations. Table 4 presents the RORs and aRORs for the same three adverse events—renal failure, AKI, and renal impairment—for cyclosporine-based regimens, with the C group serving as the reference. The corresponding results for additional adverse events are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Compared with the T group, the TM group demonstrated lower reporting ROR and aROR values for renal failure (ROR 0.64, 95% CI 0.44–0.93, P=0.02; aROR 0.48, 95% CI 0.31–0.74, P<0.01). In the TMP group, the ROR similarly suggested a reduced reporting signal (ROR, 0.60; 95% CI, 0.38–0.94; P=0.02); however, the aROR did not meet the predefined threshold for signal consistency (aROR, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.40–1.01; P=0.06). Among cyclosporine-based regimens, the CM group also exhibited lower reporting ROR and aROR values compared with the C group (ROR 0.46, 95% CI 0.23–0.91, P=0.03; aROR 0.45, 95% CI 0.21–0.97, P=0.04).

Table 5 summarizes regional analyses of renal failure reporting patterns by immunosuppressant combination. Detailed ROR and aROR estimates for tacrolimus-based regimens stratified by continent are provided in Supplementary Table 5, with corresponding estimates for cyclosporine-based regimens presented in Supplementary Table 6. In North America, the RP of renal failure for tacrolimus-based regimens was 4.5% in the T group, 4.0% in the TM group, and 1.2% in the TMP group, with the TMP group demonstrating decreased disproportional reporting relative to the T group. For cyclosporine-based regimens in North America, the RP was 15.2% in the C group, 10.3% in the CM group, and 6.7% in the CMP group. Although a trend toward lower RPs was observed with the addition of agents, these differences did not meet the criteria for signal consistency, possibly due to small sample sizes. In Europe, a more consistent pattern emerged. For tacrolimus-based regimens, the RP of renal failure was 6.9% in the T group and 2.6% in the TM group, with the TM group demonstrating both ROR and aROR signals consistent with lower reporting odds relative to the T group. Similarly, among cyclosporine-based regimens in Europe, the RP was 14.3% in the C group and 3.7% in the CM group, with the CM group again showing lower reporting odds supported by both ROR and aROR estimates. Across Latin America and Asia, baseline RPs for renal failure were lower in both T and C groups compared with North America and Europe, and the addition of other agents did not consistently result in further reductions in RPs. Collectively, these findings highlight substantial regional variability in disproportional renal failure reporting associated with immunosuppressant combinations. The observed signal patterns are hypothesis-generating in nature and may inform the design of future prospective studies examining the renal safety profiles of immunosuppressive regimens.

To further assess the robustness of the primary outcome, additional analyses were conducted for each Preferred Term—“renal failure,” “renal failure acute,” and “renal failure chronic”—as defined in the FAERS database. The overall distributions of these adverse events are summarized in Supplementary Table 7, and detailed ROR and aROR estimates for tacrolimus- and cyclosporine-based regimens are reported in Supplementary Tables 8 and 9, respectively. In these analyses, sample sizes were generally small, and most comparisons did not meet the predefined criteria for signal consistency. Nevertheless, several patterns were directionally similar to those observed in the aggregated analysis. For instance, the TM group demonstrated lower reporting odds of “renal failure” compared with the T group, and a comparable, although less consistent, tendency was observed for both “renal failure acute” and “renal failure chronic.” Geographic subgroup analyses for each renal failure term are presented in Supplementary Tables 10–12. Although the limited number of reports within each region precluded definitive signal evaluation, the overall reporting profiles showed trends broadly consistent with those observed in the primary analysis, particularly the lower frequency of renal failure reporting among MMF-containing regimens in Europe. Collectively, these complementary findings support the analytical robustness of the primary results while acknowledging the descriptive and exploratory nature of the FAERS-based assessments.

Other Renal-Related Adverse Events

Analyses of renal-related adverse events revealed distinct reporting patterns for AKI, renal impairment, and “nephropathy toxic.” Among tacrolimus-based regimens, the TM, TMP, TME, and TMPE groups exhibited higher reporting signals for AKI relative to the T group. The TMP group also showed higher reporting signals for renal impairment and “nephropathy toxic” compared with the T group, whereas the TMPE group demonstrated higher reporting signals for proteinuria. In contrast, cyclosporine-based regimens did not show notable differences in disproportional reporting of renal-related events compared with the C group. Although the present study primarily focused on renal-related events, the addition of agents to cyclosporine-based combinations may be associated with higher reporting signals for nonrenal adverse events; however, this observation remains exploratory and is constrained by small subgroup sizes. The overall discrepancy in reporting patterns between tacrolimus- and cyclosporine-based regimens underscores the complexity of renal-related reporting tendencies in immunosuppressive therapy. Taken together, these findings indicate variability in signal patterns across regimens and suggest that the observed tendencies warrant further investigation rather than causal interpretation.

DISCUSSION

Immunosuppressants are often administered in combination because each agent targets distinct immunological pathways [13]. Although the use of multiple agents may enhance immunosuppressive coverage, it can also increase the likelihood of adverse event reporting [14]. Conversely, regimens comprising fewer drugs may appear safer in certain contexts but may be associated with different patterns of adverse event reporting. Notably, renal failure remains a major clinical concern among liver transplant recipients. Accordingly, this study aimed to explore reporting patterns of renal failure associated with different immunosuppressant combinations within a spontaneous reporting database. Because this analysis was based on the FAERS spontaneous reporting system, the findings reflect disproportional reporting patterns rather than causal relationships or incidence-based risk estimates.

Across all regimens, case reports involving male recipients consistently outnumbered those involving female recipients. This male predominance is consistent with demographic patterns reported in liver transplantation studies. Previous evidence suggests that chronic liver disease etiologies, including alcoholic liver disease and hepatitis C, are more prevalent among men, which may influence the sex distribution of transplant recipients [15–17]. However, given the voluntary nature of FAERS reporting, the observed imbalance may reflect demographic composition or reporting bias rather than true sex-based differences in clinical risk.

The addition of MMF to tacrolimus- or cyclosporine-based regimens was associated with lower reporting signals of renal failure compared with monotherapy, suggesting a signal potentially related to reduced CNI exposure. MMF exerts its immunosuppressive effects through inhibition of purine synthesis in lymphocytes, a mechanism distinct from that of CNIs [18]. This combination may therefore alter reporting patterns of renal impairment without necessarily implying a causal protective effect on kidney function.

Regional subgroup analyses demonstrated variability in renal failure reporting across geographic regions. In North America, a lower RP was observed for prednisone-containing regimens, particularly the TMP group, whereas in Europe, lower reporting odds were observed for MMF-containing regimens such as TM and CM. These findings suggest regional differences in reporting signals rather than true differences in renal outcomes. Potential explanations include regional variation in reporting practices, pharmacogenomic profiles such as CYP3A5 polymorphisms, or differences in treatment protocols across healthcare systems. Collectively, these regional signal patterns indicate that personalized investigations into immunosuppressant safety merit further study [7].

Reports of AKI demonstrated higher reporting odds in certain tacrolimus-based combinations containing MMF, prednisone, or everolimus. This divergent signal pattern underscores the complexity of renal adverse event reporting in liver transplant recipients and highlights the need for further hypothesis-driven investigation. Taken together, these observations suggest variability in reporting tendencies for renal-related events across immunosuppressant combinations rather than consistent reductions in renal risk. They emphasize the importance of integrating pharmacovigilance findings with clinical evidence before drawing conclusions regarding relative safety or therapeutic efficacy.

This study has several important limitations. First, the FAERS database does not capture reports of nonoccurrence of adverse events, which precludes estimation of true incidence rates for renal failure. In addition, the spontaneous nature of FAERS reporting introduces inherent reporting bias, as severe or unexpected adverse events are more likely to be reported, potentially distorting observed associations. The limited clinical detail available in FAERS, including the absence of granular transplantation-related information and case-specific factors, further constrains interpretation. Another notable limitation is the lack of information on medication adherence, which is a critical determinant of posttransplant renal outcomes. Moreover, the cross-sectional structure of FAERS data limits assessment of long-term renal trajectories and prevents the establishment of temporal relationships between immunosuppressant exposure and adverse events. Although adjustments were made for selected variables, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Finally, variability in sample sizes across treatment groups, particularly for less common combination regimens, may have reduced statistical power to detect meaningful associations.

Despite these limitations, the FAERS database offers a valuable global perspective on posttransplant immunosuppressant safety. Its large scale enables exploratory evaluation of drug combinations that are rarely examined in randomized clinical trials. Nevertheless, the observed findings should be interpreted as reporting signal patterns within a pharmacovigilance framework rather than as evidence of causality. These results provide hypothesis-generating insights into renal complication reporting across immunosuppressant combinations and may inform the design of future confirmatory studies. Ultimately, validation through prospective clinical research and mechanistic investigations will be necessary to more definitively evaluate renal safety among liver transplant recipients.
