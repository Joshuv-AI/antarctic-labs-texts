LAPTM5-dependent lipophagy enhances ferroptosis sensitivity in glioma cells
Authors: Yan Zhang, Lu Lu, Qiuyu Zhao, Aiyu Ma, Meihua Jin, Xu Wang, Yuzhong Yang, Xuan Meng, Yiping Sun, Jinhua Zheng, Xiang Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLysosomal-associated protein transmembrane 5 (LAPTM5) is a transmembrane protein predominantly localized to late endosomes and lysosomes, with preferential expression in hematopoietic cells. Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent, non-apoptotic form of programmed cell death (PCD) that is driven by the lethal accumulation of lipid peroxides. Many studies have shown that ferroptosis is dependent on autophagy. Lipophagy is a selective form of autophagy by degrading lipid droplets (LDs) within autophagic vesicles to regulate lipid balance. Lipophagy can promote RAS-selective lethal 3 (RSL3)-induced ferroptosis. However, whether LAPTM5 regulates lipophagy to sensitize glioma cells to ferroptosis remains to be explored. Therefore, the objective of our study was to evaluate the potential role of LAPTM5 in ferroptosis.MethodsThe expression profile of LAPTM5 in human glioma tissues was analyzed by using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) databases, and immunohistochemistry. Glioma cells were treated with ferroptosis inducer erastin, and then the cell viability, glutathione (GSH), and malondialdehyde (MDA) were detected. Lipophagy was tested by laser confocal microscopy and proteins expression were detected by western blot.ResultsElevated expression of LAPTM5 was observed in glioma tissues compared to normal brain tissues, and its high expression level was associated with poorer patient prognosis. LAPTM5 promoted lipophagy in glioma cells. LAPTM5 enhanced the sensitivity of glioma cells to ferroptosis inducer erastin, while suppression of lipophagy inhibited LAPTM5-mediated sensitization to erastin.ConclusionsThe finding that glioma cells with high LAPTM5 expression were more sensitive to ferroptosis inducers suggests that glioma patients with high LAPTM5 expression may be more responsive to ferroptosis-inducing therapies.Keywords: Lysosomal-associated protein transmembrane 5 (LAPTM5), glioma, lipophagy, ferroptosis

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study demonstrates that lysosomal-associated protein transmembrane 5 (LAPTM5) enhanced the sensitivity of glioma cells to ferroptosis inducer. Mechanistically, LAPTM5 enhances ferroptosis sensitivity by promoting lipohagy.

What is known and what is new?

• LAPTM5 is a transmembrane protein predominantly localized to late endosomes and lysosomes. LAPTM5 is highly expressed in various tumors, such as bladder cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. LAPTM5 has been proven to enhance autophagy.

• This study validated that LAPTM5 was highly expressed in glioma patients and associated with poor prognosis. LAPTM5 enhanced ferroptosis sensitivity by promoting lipophagy in glioma cells.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Our findings elucidate the function of LAPTM5 in glioma and provide a potential therapeutic target. Ferroptosis should be applied in clinical practice to improve treatment precision and efficacy.

Introduction

Malignant gliomas, including glioblastoma (GBM), are the most prevalent primary malignant neoplasms of the brain, a category defined by its highly aggressive and invasive properties (1). Histologically, they share characteristics of normal glial cells and are generally classified into multiple subtypes, such as astrocytoma, GBM, and oligodendroglioma. Among them, the mutation of the isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) gene is the most common molecular alteration, which is of great significance for the classification diagnosis and prognosis evaluation (2). Surgical resection combined with radiotherapy and chemotherapy remains the standard treatment for gliomas in clinical practice. Temozolomide (TMZ) is a first-line chemotherapy drug. Clinical studies have shown that the use of TMZ after surgery can improve the survival rate of patients, but the development of TMZ resistance is a common challenge during treatment (3). Therefore, conquering the challenge of TMZ resistance is currently the key to improving the prognosis of patients.

Lysosomal-associated protein transmembrane 5 (LAPTM5) is a transmembrane protein predominantly localized to late endosomes and lysosomes, and exhibits preferential expression within cells of the hematopoietic lineage. Northern blot analysis reveals that LAPTM5 messenger RNA (mRNA) is abundant in hematopoietic lineages, but low in the lung and fetal liver or none in other non-hematopoietic tissues (4,5). LAPTM5 contains five hydrophobic transmembrane domains, three polyproline-tyrosine motifs, and a C-terminal ubiquitin-interacting motif (6). LAPTM5 has been reported to function in proteolytic activity of lysosomes and antigen processing in myeloid lineage immune cells. Studies on LAPTM5-deficient mice demonstrate that LAPTM5 negatively regulates T cell and B cell receptors by lysosomal degradation, thereby suppressing cell surface receptor-mediated activation of these lymphocytes (7). In contrast, LAPTM5 is a positive modulator of inflammatory signaling pathways via interacting with A20 so as to activate nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK) signaling pathways, and promote inflammatory cytokines secretion in macrophages (8,9). In rat cerebellar cell cultures, LAPTM5 in microglia is increased in response to neuron cells death and degeneration, suggesting that LAPTM5 may involve in the enhancement of phagocytosis toward dead neurons in microglial (10). Yeast two-hybrid analysis reveals that LAPTM5 interacts with E3 ubiquitin ligase Smurf2, responsible for the ubiquitination and degradation of Smad pathway components, including transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) receptor (11). LAPTM5 is a negative regulator of the TGF-β pathway. which is part of the ubiquitin-proteasome machinery that degrades key Smad signaling proteins and the TGF-β receptor, thereby implicating LAPTM5 in this regulatory network. Further study shows that LAPTM5 binds the ubiquitin-ligase Nedd4 to promote the LAPTM5-positive vesicles sorting from Golgi to lysosome (12). These results indicate the regulatory role of LAPTM5 in targeting proteins for lysosomal degradation.

Reports show that LAPTM5 involves in neoplastic transformation. The inactivation of LAPTM5 gene is observed in human multiple myeloma (MM) cell lines due to chromosome rearrangement and DNA methylation (13). Experiment showed that the accumulation of LAPTM5 induced programmed cell death (PCD) with autophagic vacuoles and lysosomal destabilization implicating in the spontaneous regression of neuroblastomas (14). The cell death induced by restoration of LAPTM5 expression in neuroblastoma cells is significantly increased when cells are treated with proteasomal or lysosomal inhibitors, suggesting that LAPTM5 induces a form of autophagic cell death. While in HeLa cells, overexpression of LAPTM5 induces apoptosis in a mitochondria-dependent manner by cleavage of Mcl-1 and Bid and subsequent activation of the caspase cascade (5). Furthermore, LAPTM5 is significantly higher in bladder cancer (BCa) cells, and down-regulation of LAPTM5 delays BCa cell growth resulted from a G0/G1 phase cell cycle arrest via deactivation of ERK1/2 and p38 (15). LAPTM5 also involves in neuropathic pain. RNA sequencing of chronic constriction injury (CCI) models and control groups reveals that LAPTM5 is up-regulated in CCI models (16). In CD40-positive GBM, knockdown of LAPTM5 facilitates cell invasion, clonogenicity, and TMZ resistance (17). However, the role of LAPTM5 in regulating cell death modalities, particularly in the context of ferroptosis, and its underlying mechanism in glioma remain largely unknown. Given the established links between LAPTM5, lysosomal function, and autophagy, we hypothesized that LAPTM5 might modulate ferroptosis sensitivity in glioma cells through lipophagy, a selective autophagic process.

In this study, we demonstrated that LAPTM5 was up-regulated in human gliomas compared to normal brain tissues and LAPTM5 promoted glioma cells’ sensitivity to ferroptosis inducer erastin dependent on lipophagy. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1-2719/rc).

Methods

Data collection

LAPTM5 gene expression in various cancers was analyzed in the TIMER2.0 database (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/) (18). Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) was used to analyze LAPTM5 expression in low-grade glioma (LGG) and GBM compared to normal brain. The RNA sequencing data and corresponding clinicopathological features of glioma patients were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database and Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA), including mRNAseq_693 and mRNAseq_325.

Cell lines

293T (catalog No. GNHu17) and U251 (catalog No. TCHu 58) cell lines were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Primary cultured glioma cells [1216] were described in our previous report (19), which were separated and cultivated from GBM patient. All these cells were free from mycoplasma contamination. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) high-glucose medium (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Lonsera, Suzhou, China; S711-050S) and incubated at 37 ℃ and 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere.

Human tissue samples

A total of 68 glioma patients and 19 normal brain tissues derived from autopsy were collected for this study. Among them, there are 50 males and 37 females and ages range from 20 to 78 years. The slices were obtained from the Department of Pathology, The First Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University (No. 2025XYSYJSLL-05). Informed consent was taken from all the patients. Paraffin sections were sliced by 3 µm according to manufacturing procedure for immunohistochemical staining.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin sections of human glioma and normal brain sections were deparaffinized, dehydrated, and antigen retrieved. Sections were blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min and normal goat serum for 1 hour at room temperature. Then the sections were incubated with anti-LAPTM5 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA; PA5-23585; dilution ratio: 1:200) antibody overnight at 4 ℃ and MaxVision-HRP (Maxim Biotechnologies, China, KIT-5030) for 15 min at room temperature. The bound antibody was visualized using 3,3'-diaminobenzidine (DAB; Maxim Biotechnologies, Fuzhou, China; DAB-1031) and counterstained with hematoxylin. Briefly, the staining intensity was scored 0 to 3 (0: negative, 1: weakly positive, 2: moderately positive, 3: strongly positive). The H-score was calculated by adding up each intensity score multiply by percentage, with a score range of 0 to 300.

RNA interference

The target sequence of LAPTM5 short hairpin RNA (shRNA) was as follows: sh-LAPTM5: 5'-GCGGTGCTACAGATTGATCAA-3'. All these DNA segments were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Company (Shanghai, China) and subsequently cloned into the pSuper Vector.

Transient transfection

Plasmids were transiently transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA; L3000015), as per the manufacturer’s manual. Cells were seeded in plate 1 day before transfection to reach 70% confluence. Mixed the plasmids, p3000, and lipofectamine 3000 in Opti-MEMTM (Gibco; 31985070) and stood still for 20 min, and then added to the cells. The cells were cultured for 48 h after transfection, and total RNA was extracted.

Lentivirus infection

The lentivirus system contained four plasmids: pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-Puro, pLP1, pLP2, and pLP/VSVG. LAPTM5 CDS sequence was constructed into pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-Puro and transfected into 293T cells with pLP1, pLP2, and pLP/VSVG. shRNA sequences were inserted into pLKO.1-Puro plasmid and transfected into 293T cells with pCMV-dR8.74, pMD2G, and pRSV-Rev. The viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 h post-transfection, and centrifuged to concentrate the lentivirus. 1216 and U251 cells were infected with lentivirus and selected with puromycin; subsequently, positive cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total cellular RNA was extracted with the RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara, Shiga Prefecture, Japan; 9109). 2 µg of RNA was reversely transcribed into cDNA using Revert Aid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher; K1622) according to manufacturer’s procedure. Real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Tolobio, Shanghai, China, 22204). The sequences of the LAPTM5-specific primers were: forward: 5'-CCCCAGCCAGGAGGATA-3', and reverse: 5'-TAGGACGGCAGGACCAC-3'. As an internal control, the expression of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was checked using the primers: forward: 5'-ATCATCAGCAATGCCTCCT-3', and reverse: 5'-CATCACGCCACAGTTTCC-3'.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed following established protocols. Cells were lysed with ice-cold radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; P0013B) supplemented with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (Solarbio, Beijing, China; P0100) and protease inhibitor cocktail (Solarbio; P6730). Cell lysates were mixed with sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) loading buffer and then boiled for 10 minutes to denature proteins. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The PVDF membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk (1 h, room temperature) followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 ℃ and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. The primary antibodies were used as follows: light chain 3B (LC3B) (Proteintech, Wuhan, China; 14600-1-AP; dilution ratio: 1:1,000), LAPTM5 (Invitrogen; PA5-23585; dilution ratio: 1:500), glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) (Cohesion Biosciences, Suzhou, China; CQA1094; dilution ratio: 1:1,000), and GAPDH (Proteintech; 60004-1-Ig; dilution ratio: 1:10,000). Signals were visualized using the chemiluminescent HRP substrate (Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China; SQ201) by chemiluminescence imaging system.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

CCK-8 (GlpBio, Montclair, CA, USA; GK10001) assay was performed according to manufacturers’ procedures. Briefly, 5×103 cells were seeded in 96-well plate to grow. Gradient concentration of ferroptosis inducer erastin (Aladdin, Shanghai, China; E126853) was added into cells the next day. After the cells adhered, cells were treated with 10 µL CCK-8 per well, incubated at 37 ℃ for 2 h, and absorbance at 450 nm wavelength was measured using a microplate reader. The values of optical density (OD)450 nm were used to draw the graph.

Lipid droplet (LD) analysis

The level of LDs was measured by using BODIPY 493/503 (Thermo Fisher; D3922). The cells were incubated with 2 µM BODIPY 493/503 solution for 30min and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The images of the LDs were obtained by confocal laser scanning microscopy (Nikon AX, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

Data shown were representative of at least three independent experiments. Statistical significance between two groups was assessed using the Student’s t-test, and three or more than three groups was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) by using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 and IBM SPSS Statistics version 17.0. OS curves for the glioma patients were calculated by the Kaplan-Meier method. P values of less than 0.05 were considered to be significant.

Results

Glioma tissues exhibited a marked up-regulation of LAPTM5 expression relative to normal brain tissues

To evaluate the expression profile of LAPTM5 in pan-cancer, the expression of LAPTM5 in various tumor tissues in the Timer 2.0 database was analyzed. Compared to normal tissues, LAPTM5 was highly expressed in various tumor tissues such as GBM and breast invasive carcinoma, etc. (Figure 1A). To evaluate the LAPTM5 expression in LGG, GBM, and normal tissues, GEPIA (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/) was used to verify that LAPTM5 was up-regulated in LGG and GBM compared to normal control (Figure 1B). To ascertain LAPTM5 expression in human glioma tissues and normal brain tissues, LAPTM5 expression was detected by immunohistochemistry with LAPTM5 antibody. Compared to normal brain tissues, LAPTM5 expression was up-regulated in glioma tissues (Figure 1C,1D). These results suggested that LAPTM5 was up-regulated in human glioma tissues.

Correlations between LAPTM5 expression and clinicopathology features in glioma

Next, we analyzed the correlation between LAPTM5 expression and clinicopathological features of gliomas in the CGGA and TCGA datasets. Significant differences in LAPTM5 expression were observed among subgroups stratified by World Health Organization (WHO) grade (II, III, and IV), IDH status (mutant and wildtype), and 1p/19q codeletion status in the CCGA and TCGA datasets (Figure 2A-2L). The expression of LAPTM5 was significantly higher in glioma patients with grade IV, IDH wild type, and 1p/19q non-codeletion. Survival analyses were applied to further verify the correlation of LAPTM5 expression with prognosis in glioma patients. KM plotter results showed that high LAPTM5 expression had shorter overall survival time in glioma patients (Figure 2M-2O).

LAPTM5 promoted lipophagy in glioma cells

LAPTM5 has been shown to directly facilitate intrinsic macroautophagic/autophagic flux involves in autophagy (6). We suspected whether LAPTM5 plays a role in lipophagy. LAPTM5 was knockdown in 1216 cells by lentiviral vectors containing shRNA or overexpressed in U251 cells containing LAPTM5 coding sequence (Figure 3A-3C). To investigate LAPTM5 function in lipophagy, GFP-LC3 fluorescence spots were used to detect lipophagy. The results showed that knockdown of LAPTM5 decreased GFP-LC3 fluorescence spots, while overexpression of LAPTM5 increased GFP-LC3 fluorescence spots (Figure 3D). Furthermore, transmission electron microscopy was used to detect autolysosomes. The results showed that the number of autolysosomes in cells decreased after down-regulation of LAPTM5, while it increased after overexpression of LAPTM5 (Figure 3E). Western blot discovered LC3B II/I level decreased after down-regulation of LAPTM5, while increased after overexpression of LAPTM5 (Figure 3F). Further, BODIPY 493/503 neutral LD fluorescence probe was used to detect LDs in cells. The number of LDs in cells increased after down-regulation of LAPTM5, while it decreased after overexpression of LAPTM5 (Figure 3G). These results suggested that LAPTM5 promoted lipophagy in glioma cells and the degradation of LDs.

LAPTM5 regulated ferroptosis sensitivity of glioma cells

Studies showed that ferroptosis depended on lipophagy. We further explored whether LAPTM5 influence cells sensitivity to ferroptosis inducers. Different concentrations of erastin were added into glioma cells to induce ferroptosis, and the cellular viability was decreased after LAPTM5 overexpressed, but increased after LAPTM5 knockdown (Figure 4A,4B). Glutathione (GSH) increased but malondialdehyde (MDA) decreased when LAPTM5 knockdown, while overexpression of LAPTM5 had the opposite effect (Figure 4C-4F). GPX4 expression was increased after LAPTM5 knockdown, but decreased after LAPTM5 overexpressed, when treated with various concentrations of ferroptosis inducer erastin (Figure 4G). RhoNox-1 fluorescent probe was used to detect the divalent iron. The results showed that fluorescence decreased when LAPTM5 knockdown, while increased when LAPTM5 overexpressed (Figure 4H). These results suggested that LAPTM5 facilitated glioma cells sensitive to ferroptosis inducer.

LAPTM5 facilitated ferroptosis sensitivity dependent on lipophagy

In order to further verify that whether LAPTM5 promoted ferroptosis sensitivity dependent on lipophagy. 1216 cells were treated with different concentrations of erastin concurrently induced lipophagy by rapamycin (RAPA). The cellular viability was increased after LAPTM5 knockdown, whereas RAPA reduced that elevation brought about by LAPTM5 knockdown (Figure 5A). In U251 cells, different concentrations of erastin were used to induce ferroptosis and chloroquine (CQ) to inhibit lipophagy. The cellular viability was decreased after LAPTM5 overexpressed, but increased when treated with CQ (Figure 5B). Meanwhile, we detected GSH and MDA contents in glioma cells. In erastin-treated 1216 cells, GSH increased when LAPTM5 knockdown, but suppressed by RAPA (Figure 5C). GSH decreased in erastin-treated U251 cells with overexpression of LAPTM5, but CQ promoted the production of GSH (Figure 5D). On the other hand, MDA production was reduced by LAPTM5 knockdown, but increased by RAPA (Figure 5E). Overexpression of LAPTM5 produced more MDA, and CQ suppressed this effect (Figure 5F). These results suggested that LAPTM5 facilitated ferroptosis sensitivity dependent on lipophagy.

Discussion

Here, we demonstrated that LAPTM5 expression is significantly elevated in human glioma tissues relative to normal brain tissues by immunohistochemistry with human tissues and data analysis from the TCGA and CGGA databases. High LAPTM5 expression was associated with a poorer prognosis, including shorter overall survival, in glioma patients. Studies showed that in LGG patients, LAPTM5 was up-regulated and associated with unfavorable prognosis (20). In some other cancers, LAPTM5 level was reduced in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients, and patients with low LAPTM5 expression had a significantly worse prognosis (21). In BCa, LAPTM5 was markedly elevated in cancer tissues relative to paracancerous tissues and normal bladder tissues (15).

Autophagy is a self-degradation process of cells, through which cells recycle damaged proteins and organelles to maintain intracellular homeostasis (22). Under conditions of nutrient deficiency and starvation, cells recycle substances and energy sources through autophagy (23). In addition, hypoxia, oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and protein aggregation can also induce autophagy in cells (24). LAPTM5 has been proven to regulate autophagy, which aids in sorting of ubiquitinated cargo to the lysosome and facilitates substrate incorporation into the multivesicular body for degradation (8). Lipophagy is a selective form of autophagy, as LDs are encapsulated in autophagic vesicles with a double-membrane, fused with lysosomes, and degraded within them to regulate lipid balance (25). We found that LAPTM5 promoted autophagy by increased autophagosomes and LC3 enzymatic hydrolysis. Moreover, LAPTM5 promoted LDs degradation. Our results suggested that LAPTM5 facilitated lipophagy.

Ferroptosis is a PCD driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, leading to oxidative membrane damage and cell demise (26,27). The accumulation of lipid peroxides is a major characteristic of ferroptosis. Ferroptosis occurs when the promoting factors of ferroptosis within cells significantly exceed the cells antioxidant capacity (28). GPX4 detoxify lipid hydroperoxides (L-OOH) by reducing them to alcohols (L-OH). Thus, GPX4 inhibition disrupts this critical defense, causing lipid peroxide accumulation in membranes, a key event triggering ferroptosis. RAS-selective lethal 3 (RSL3), as a ferroptosis inducer, can directly act on GPX4 and inhibit its activity, reducing the cells antioxidant capacity and causing the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby inducing ferroptosis (29). Studies have shown that ferroptosis is a key tumor suppressive mechanism and holds great potential in cancer treatment. Our results demonstrated that LAPTM5 promoted glioma cells sensitive to ferroptosis inducer erastin. Therefore, our study proposes that patients with LAPTM5-high gliomas might benefit from therapeutic strategies that induce ferroptosis.

Numerous studies have shown that the execution of ferroptosis is closely linked to and often dependent on autophagy. The use of ferroptosis inducers such as erastin and RSL3 can promote autophagy, and autophagy-related proteins, autophagy-related protein (ATG)3, ATG5, ATG4B, ATG7, ATG13, and Beclin 1, are associated with the occurrence of ferroptosis (30). Moderate autophagy is beneficial for cell survival, but excessive autophagy, especially selective autophagy that degrades certain proteins [such as ferritin, solute carrier family 40 member 1 (SLC40A1), aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator-like (ARNTL), GPX4, and CDH2 (cadherin)] or organelles (such as LDs or mitochondria), can induce iron overload or lipid peroxidation leading to ferroptosis (31). Lipophagy can promote RSL3-induced ferroptosis, with LD content increasing in the early stage of ferroptosis and decreasing in the late stage. Overexpression of the tumor protein D52 (TPD52) increases LDs and inhibits ferroptosis, while knockdown of Rab7a or ATG5 inhibits LD degradation and also inhibits ferroptosis (32). We found in U251 cells, CQ treatment does not significantly alter cell viability upon erastin exposure, which may be due to the relatively low autophagic activity in these cells. Inhibiting lipophagy by CQ can eliminate the ferroptosis sensitivity induced by LAPTM5, while promoting lipophagy by RAPA can support ferroptosis sensitivity in glioma cells. We conclude that LAPTM5 promotes lipophagy and LD degradation, which constitutes a key mechanism by which it increases sensitivity to ferroptosis inducers in glioma cells.

Conclusions

Taken together, our work defined a novel and critical function for LAPTM5 in human glioma cells. LAPTM5 was up-regulated in human glioma and negatively correlated with the patient’s survival time. High expression of LAPTM5 increased glioma cells’ sensitivity to ferroptosis inducer erastin. These findings suggested that ferroptosis inducers may have potential therapeutic value in patients with high expression of LAPTM5.
