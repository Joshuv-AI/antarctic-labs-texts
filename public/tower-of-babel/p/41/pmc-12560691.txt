Microvascular Decompression in a Patient With Positional Hemifacial Spasm: Case Report
Authors: Christopher Adams, Ali Samii, Turner Peckham, Manuel Ferreira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Hemifacial spasm (HFS) is characterized by spasms of the muscles innervated by the facial nerve. In most cases, it is unilateral and intermittent and involves involuntary ipsilateral muscle spasms including the eyelid. In the brainstem, the anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) originates from the basilar artery at the level of the facial, vestibulocochlear, and trigeminal nerves at the base of the pons. Therefore, the AICA could compress the vestibular, facial, and trigeminal nerves. The posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) originates from the vertebral artery at the level of the hypoglossal nerve in the medulla. The PICA often travels up the brainstem to the pons and then travels back to the medulla before traveling to the cerebellum. So, the PICA can also compress the trigeminal, vestibulocochlear, and facial nerves.

We report a case of left HFS, shooting pain, tinnitus, and numbness induced by lying in a prone position. The patient was found to have compression of the trigeminal, facial, and vestibulocochlear nerves by the AICA, PICA, and 2 small vessels. Microvascular decompression resulted in relief from symptoms. This is the first case of HFS caused in a patient when lying in a prone position that completely resolved after surgical decompression.

CLINICAL PRESENTATION

A 29-year-old female presented with episodes of left HFS provoked by lying in a prone position. Left HFS could also be provoked by drinking, eating, or lifting weights. During the episodes, the patient described left facial twitching and pulling, tinnitus, shooting pain, and numbness. At times, they were so severe that she had to leave work. Between episodes, the facial pain subsided. The pain was not triggered by touch. She described the pain as an electric shock. The patient provided videos of previous episodes usually involving the left side but sometimes involving the right. The patient reported that the right-sided facial pulling episodes were less frequent than they were on the left and were not position-dependent. The patient had tried carbamazepine before but was unable to tolerate it because of side effects. Carbamazepine did help with her facial pain though. She was offered botulinum toxin injections but declined as she was concerned about having facial weakness. An episode of left facial pulling was observed in the office after she lay in a prone position. During the episode, her left face began twitching, followed by pulling. The episode lasted for about 1 minute. The patient denied a family history of HFS or trigeminal neuralgia. The patient gave informed written consent for publication of her case. Institutional Review Board approval was not required.

Microvascular Decompression and Electrophysiology

On left retrosigmoid craniotomy, there were 4 offending vessels: the PICA, the AICA, and 2 small vessels, lying on the facial and auditory nerves. Lateral spread was demonstrated before the microvascular decompression and ceased to exist after elevation of the offending vessels. Stimulation of the facial nerve at the brainstem root exit site at 0.05 mA revealed very robust responses at all levels of the face. Decompression resulted in complete resolution of HFS on the left side. Surgical decompression eliminated HFS on the right as well.

Follow-Up and Outcomes

The patient reported that she was satisfied with the results of the decompressive surgeries and denied any residual weakness after the surgeries. She commented on some pain after the first surgery, which was controlled by pain medications and gradually subsided. The pain was associated with the incision site. The last follow-up was 6 months after surgery on the left side.

DISCUSSION

Schiess et al1 described a case of HFS in a 32-year-old man after a brainstem stroke induced by turning his head to the left. Moore et al reported on a 52-year-old male with an aneurysm compressing the facial nerve who developed HFS with his head resting on a pillow that resolved when he held his head back.2 The patient died before the aneurysm could be removed. Perlmutter et al3 reported on a 45-year-old man with an ectatic vertebrobasilar artery who developed right-sided HFS, hearing loss, numbness, tinnitus, and vertigo when he turned his head to the left. Furthermore, they found that brainstem auditory evoked potentials decreased when the patient turned his head to the left, which they hypothesized was due to decreased blood flow. This resolved after surgical decompression. Xie and Christoforidis4 described a case of HFS in a 48-year-old woman with a vertebral artery compressing her facial and trigeminal nerves induced by extending her head back. The HFS improved with botulinum toxin injections. Patrick et al.5 described a case of idiopathic intracranial hypertension who developed HFS that only occurred when the patient stood up. Acetazolamide completely resolved the HFS.

Compression of the facial nerve by vasculature is a known cause of HFS, but only a few cases have been reported of position-dependent HFS. Furthermore, while 2 other cases have HFS induced by inclining the head forward or leaning back,2,4 in neither case, surgical decompression was performed. This is the first case of HFS induced by lying in the prone position that was relieved by decompression. While head turning has been found to decrease brainstem auditory evoked potentials in the contralateral side in a previous case of HFS likely caused by decrease blood flow,3 this is the first definitive case caused by position-dependent compression. In this case, the AICA, the PICA, and 2 vessels abutted the trigeminal, facial, and acoustic nerves on the left, which caused the position-dependent HFS, numbness, and shooting pain.

The main strength of this article is that it is the first case of HFS that was due to position-dependent compression of the facial nerve that completely resolved after decompression. While Perlmutter et al3 reported on a patient with positional HFS as well, they hypothesized that this was due to decreased blood flow when the patient turned their head. Furthermore, while Xie and Christoforidis4 presented a case of likely position-dependent compression, decompression was never performed to see if the HFS ceased as the patient responded to botulinum toxin injections.

The main weakness of the study is that the patient did not want to try botulinum toxin injection before getting microvascular decompression. It is difficult to say whether the patient would have had sufficient relief from her HFS with botulinum toxin injections alone. Perlmutter et al and Xie and Christoforidis found that botulinum toxin injections completely relieved the HFS in those cases.3,4

CONCLUSION

We report a case of HFS in a 32-year-old woman that was provoked by lying in a prone position. Microvascular decompression completely resolved the HFS. In cases where vessels abut but do not compress the facial nerve, there can still be position-dependent HFS because of position-dependent compression of the facial nerve, which can be relieved by microsurgical decompression. This case is unique as it is the first case of position-dependent compression of the facial nerve that was completely resolved by microsurgical decompression.
