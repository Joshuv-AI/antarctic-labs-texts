Clinical study on the relationship between exposure to ultrafine particles (PM0.1) and cardiovascular diseases in petroleum workers
Authors: Nagham Jawad Kadam AL-Lami, Nadhum AN Awad, Saad Shaheen Hamadi Al-Taher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study examines the health of petroleum industry employees in Basrah City, southern Iraq, with a focus on their exposure to toxic chemicals, specifically the impact of oxidative stress on their hearts. This study included two groups of men: in the first group, ninety employees were exposed to crude oil well sites in Basrah, and ninety individuals were in the control group. This study evaluated two ultra-fine particles in the participants' blood: the polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon metabolite [Benzopyrene diol epoxide (BPDE)] level and the toxic cadmium. The study also aimed to evaluate the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) in the serum of the study participants and monitor the lipid profile. The results showed high levels of BPDE, a high concentration of cadmium in the blood, increased lipid peroxidation, and decreased SOD in the exposed group compared to the control group. The results also showed a significant increase in triglycerides. The increase in reactive oxygen species production is a major risk factor for atherosclerosis, and high triglycerides indicate artery wall deposits, leading to cardiovascular disease.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, PM0.1, Benzo [a] Pyrene, Cadmium, Malondialdehyde (MDA), Superoxide dismutase (SOD), Benzo[a]Pyridyl Epoxide (BPDE), Lipid profile

Introduction

The rapid increase in oil production in Iraq over the past twenty years has led to more wells and oil extraction activities in Basrah City [1]. The processes of extracting, refining, and burning crude oil are major sources of rising gaseous pollutants, hydrocarbons, and heavy metals [2], [3]. Workers are exposed to chemicals associated with oil and related industries, including toxins, carcinogens, and irritants, through skin contact or inhalation of crude oil fumes [4]. These chemicals can enter the bloodstream and activate inflammatory pathways, affecting the cardiovascular system through oxidative stress [5], [6]. These pollutants contribute to the high rates of cancer, cardiovascular disease, and respiratory diseases in southern Iraq[7], [8].

Ambient particulate matter (PM) is a diverse mixture of hundreds of different airborne chemicals that form fine solid particles or liquid droplets, varying in size and shape [9], [10]. The aerodynamic diameter of particles is significant and is categorized into subgroups based on their consistency in regulating air quality and toxic effects. PM10 are coarse, inhalable particles that settle in the upper bronchi of the human respiratory system [11], [12]. PM2.5 and PM0.1 can easily enter narrow airways, penetrate the alveoli and alveolar-capillary membrane, and diffuse into the circulation. Additionally, aerosols are formed when ambient particles interact with atmospheric gases [13].

When the gas released from crude oil cannot be processed or stored due to technical, economic, or safety reasons, natural gas is burned off during extraction, causing significant particle emissions from oil wells [14]. Particulate matter (PM) includes various organic compounds, including aliphatic, aromatic, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. In addition to mineral pollutants, there are toxic volatile metal oxides, salts, hydroxides, and soot. Emissions include nitrogen oxides, sulfur dioxide, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, ozone, free radicals, noise, and thermal radiation [8], [15], [16], [17]. Continuous exposure to the fumes of this mixture in the workplace exposes workers to the risk of contracting various diseases that attack different body systems, such as the respiratory, immune, and renal systems [18]. There is also a strong association between PM2.5, PM0.1, and cardiovascular diseases [19]. Several known mechanisms explain how PM0.1 affects human health: the first is the direct pathway, in which PM0.1 is transported directly to the bloodstream and distant target organs [20]. The other two pathways are indirect. The indirect mediators of pulmonary oxidative stress and inflammation are less severe and occur several hours or days after inhalation [21].

One of the particulate pollutants emitted from petroleum sources is benzopyrene B[a]P, a highly toxic and carcinogenic nonpolar polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAHs), which damages blood vessel wall cells, causing apoptosis, oxidative stress, inflammation, production of reactive oxygen species, release of inflammatory cytokines, and apoptosis [22], [23]. Due to its high lipophilicity, benzopyrene B[a]P enters cells quickly and passes through the plasma membrane. B[a]P is metabolized by cytochrome P450 (CYP P450) to a more hydrophilic metabolite: 7β,8α-dihydroxy-9α,10α-epoxy-7,8,9,10-tetrahydrobenzo[a]pyrene (BPDE) [22]. The conversion to hydroxyl compounds or ketones is a detoxification reaction, while this conversion is unfortunately an activation reaction. Therefore, the resulting compound BPDE has a strong oxidative capacity, causing oxidative stress and reactive oxygen species (ROS), which leads to DNA damage and affects DNA replication [24].

Cadmium, a divalent cation, has a body of evidence supporting its role in cardiovascular disease [25]. With a half-life of 10–30 years, cadmium can disrupt many biological systems when taken in low doses [26]. It can damage vascular tissue, cause endothelial dysfunction, and promote atherosclerosis through oxidative mechanisms [25].

Exposure to fine particles induces irreversible lipid peroxidation reactions, resulting in reactive carbonyl species (RCS), molecules containing a highly reactive carbonyl group (aldehyde and ketone) [27]. It is worth noting that the end products of lipid peroxidation, such as MDA and other metabolites, have cytotoxic properties and can covalently bind to nuclear groups, causing DNA malformation and damage [28].

Cardiovascular diseases make up about 32 % of all yearly deaths worldwide [29]. Atherosclerosis is a chronic disease of the arteries marked by the buildup of inflammatory cells and lipids in the blood vessel walls. Atherosclerotic plaques can become unstable over time and can block the blood supply to vital organs such as the brain and heart, leading to clinical consequences such as stroke and myocardial infarction [30].

This clinical study aimed to assess the impact of occupational exposure to particulate emissions from crude oil well sites on the cardiovascular system, with a focus on oxidative stress. The study included: Estimating the concentration of cadmium and determining the BPDE level. Assessment of markers of oxidative stress, including malondialdehyde (MDA) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) levels, in the serum of the study participants.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was conducted in Basrah City, Iraq. Two groups of participants were examined: the first group included 90 employees who were exposed to polluted working conditions in a crude oil field. Each employee had at least three years of experience and worked ten hours daily, five days a week. The second group, consisting of 90 individuals, was not exposed to these conditions (the control group).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The research was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of medical research involving human subjects, as outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki issued by the World Medical Association. All procedures were conducted by relevant laws and institutional guidelines and were approved by the appropriate institutional committees. The privacy rights of the individuals participating in the experiment were respected, and informed consent for human experiments was obtained from all participants.

Questionnaire

The required information was obtained from the participants through a questionnaire that included demographic data (age), personal lifestyle (smoking and alcohol), occupational history, duration of exposure (years worked and number of hours per day), and health condition (genetic diseases, medications taken, other blood diseases such as diabetes, kidney disease, and high blood pressure).

Sample collection

After receiving approval from the Studies and Research Ethics Committee at the Basrah Oil Company, all participants in the study provided written informed consent. Five milliliters of blood were drawn from each participant by venipuncture and divided into two tubes. The blood samples were stored in a refrigerated, humidified ice box until they were analyzed. The samples were measured on the same day at a specialized analytical laboratory. Two milliliters of venous blood in a tube containing EDTA (diethylendiaminetetraacetate) were used to determine the blood's cadmium (Cd) concentration and BPDE levels.

Determination of cadmium concentration in blood

Cadmium was extracted from blood samples using a standard acid digestion method. The samples were digested by transferring 1 mL of whole blood into a Pyrex test tube, adding 1 mL of concentrated nitric acid and 1 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid. The tubes were then placed in an oil bath at 130 °C for 45 min until the nitric acid evaporated, producing brown vapors. After cooling, 5 mL of hydrochloric acid was added, and the tubes were returned to the bath at 130 °C for another 45 min. Once cooled, the tubes were diluted to 10 mL with 5 M hydrochloric acid [31]. The digested samples were analyzed using a HORIBA Scientific JY2000–2 inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES) from France. The wavelength for measuring cadmium is 226.502 nm. Cadmium levels were measured by extrapolating the calibration curve and then multiplying by the dilution factor.

ELISA assay

Biomarker analysis of Benzo(a)pyrene

BPDE (pg/mL) was determined in extracted white blood cell DNA by the OxiSelect™ BPDE DNA Adduct ELISA kit from Cell Biolabs, Inc. (USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm as the primary wavelength. The concentration of BPDE-DNA in the samples was determined by comparing the OD to a standard curve. Concentrations are extracted from the standard logarithmic curve.

Biomarker analysis of MDA

According to the manufacturer's protocol, serum malondialdehyde was measured using Human MDA ELISA (ELK Biotechnology, Ltd., USA). This assay uses a competitive enzyme immunoassay technique. A spectrophotometer detects the color change at 450 nm ± 10 nm. The concentration of MDA in the samples is calculated by comparing the OD of the samples to the standard curve.

Biomarker analysis of SOD

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) inhibition in serum was determined using a Human SOD Determination Kit (ELK Biotechnology, Ltd., USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. ELK Biotechnology, Ltd., USA, following the manufacturer's instructions. The kit employs a sandwich enzyme immunoassay as its test principle. A spectrophotometer measures the color change at 450 nm ± 10 nm. The concentration of SOD in the samples is calculated by comparing the OD of the samples to the standard curve.

Determination of lipid profile

The study measured total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL, and HDL using Biolabo kits from Maizy, France.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS 22 (IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 22, Chicago, IL, USA). The current study data were analyzed with the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact probability (F.E.P.) test to compare percentages (qualitative data). The T-test was used to compare two numeric variables. Pearson Correlation: to examine the degree of relation between variables. The correlation coefficient value (r) is either positive (direct correlation) or negative (inverse correlation). The sensitivity and specificity of the parameters studied were evaluated using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. The comparison of significance (p-value) in any test was considered as follows: p-value greater than 0.05 (p > 0.05) was non-statistically significant, p-value of less than or equal to 0.05 (p ≤ 0.05) was statistically significant, p-value of less than 0.01 (p < 0.01) was highly statistically significant.

Exclusion criteria for the control group

To ensure accurate results in the study of toxic effects on oil workers, a control group must be selected to prevent interference from other factors. Since female workers in the oil production department were excluded, a male-only control group was chosen to ensure the validity of the comparison. The group should be of the same age as the oil workers exposed to the toxic substances and should not include individuals with chronic diseases or health conditions. Regular smokers or drug users should also be excluded, as they can affect the respiratory system and other biological systems. Those working in different occupations or work environments that may expose them to toxic substances should also be excluded. This ensures a valid comparison and avoids interference from other factors.

Comparison between petroleum employees exposed and the unexposed control in the Cd and (BPDE) levels

Table 2 shows the clinical findings in the blood of petroleum workers exposed to ultrafine particles, compared to the unexposed control group. The results indicated a significant difference (p < 0.001) in the mean BPDE value between the two groups.

Discussion

A clinical study was conducted to measure the levels of cadmium and BPDE in the blood of oilfield workers, to investigate their effects on the cardiovascular system through oxidative stress disruption, and to monitor the lipid profile. The results showed higher levels of cadmium in exposed workers compared to the control group. Chronic exposure to cadmium directly damages DNA or influences gene expression, and disrupts essential minerals such as zinc, copper, and calcium through competitive binding and displacement, thereby increasing the risk of various tumors. Cadmium can also cause mitochondrial damage and increase reactive oxygen species (ROS) by replacing endogenous Fenton metals, Fe2+ [32]. Cadmium is distributed in endothelial cells, causing cell death and stimulating inflammatory processes [33]. Cadmium can also target thiol (-SH) groups of Cysteines in proteins. Disruption of sulfhydryl groups in enzymes leads to several functional deficits in the nucleus, endoplasmic reticulum, and mitochondria. [34]. Recent research provides strong evidence of a positive link between higher cadmium levels and an increased risk of cardiovascular disease. All dose-response curves analyzing cardiovascular outcomes from whole blood biomarkers showed a consistent positive linear relationship [35].

The results also showed an increase in the BPDE levels in the exposed workers compared to the control group. BPDE is formed during the initial phase of exposure to aromatic compounds, through the oxidation of B[a]P by P450 at positions 7 and 8, which is considered one of the most dangerous reactions leading to the formation of toxic and carcinogenic metabolites of B[a]P [36]. Exposure to B[a]P significantly contributes to the development of atherosclerosis, leading to endothelial dysfunction, cell death, and local inflammatory responses in the vascular endothelium [23], [37]. BPDE has a strong oxidative capacity that can cause oxidative DNA damage, create DNA adducts that interfere with DNA replication, induce apoptosis, and lead to genetic mutations in vascular wall cells [36]. A recent study demonstrated that analyzing VOCs in blood can reflect the dose of internal exposure, as they are stable biomarkers of this exposure [38]. Previous studies have also shown a positive association between exposure to PAHs and the risk of cardiovascular disease in both occupational and general populations [39].

The results also showed lower levels of antioxidant SOD and higher amounts of MDA, the end product of lipid oxidation. The oxidative stress state of oilfield workers, assessed by MDA determination, appears to be exacerbated by elevated levels of cadmium and BPDE. MDA showed a positive correlation with cadmium and BPDE and an inverse relationship between MDA and SOD. The results also showed that SOD analysis showed an inverse relationship with cadmium and BPDE. This antagonistic relationship suggests that the antioxidant system of workers is impaired by exposure to particulate pollutants, as SOD is a metalloenzyme that acts as a first line of defense against reactive oxygen species (ROS). It captures the free radical superoxide anion in molecular oxygen and hydrogen peroxide, decreasing the level of oxygen that is harmful to cells at high concentrations [40]. When the extent of oxidative damage exceeds the ability to repair, cell damage occurs. Redox imbalance has been associated with many diseases, including heart disease, the aging process, and cancer [41].

Lipid profiles have shown that high triglycerides are an important indicator of the thickening of the arterial walls (atherosclerosis), which increases the risk of stroke, heart attack, and heart disease [42]. Very high triglycerides can also lead to severe inflammation of the pancreas (pancreatitis). In addition, high triglycerides may indicate type 2 diabetes or prediabetes [43]. A recent study aimed at determining the role of triglyceride levels in various outcomes in patients with heart failure found that high triglyceride levels were associated with hospitalization for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or death. Conversely, low triglyceride levels were associated with an increased risk of hospitalization for heart failure [44].

Environmental factors, such as high temperatures in Basrah, southern Iraq, significantly influence the concentration of particulate pollutants in oil work sites [8], [45]. Few studies have examined the measurement of fine particulate matter concentrations in the human body caused by emissions from the oil industry and the early effects of low-level exposure to particulate matter on cardiovascular disease.

Conclusion

The study emphasizes the serious health risks of long-term exposure to ultra-fine particles among petroleum industry workers, highlighting oxidative stress as a key factor in cardiovascular damage. It also shows that workers had higher levels of BPDE (a substance linked to DNA damage) and cadmium (a toxic metal) in their blood compared to controls. These substances can cause significant health problems, especially affecting the cardiovascular system. Additionally, elevated levels of MDA (an indicator of lipid peroxidation) and low levels of SOD (an antioxidant enzyme) were found in the workers, indicating oxidative stress from exposure to ultra-fine particles. Oxidative stress is known to damage cells and can lead to inflammation and blood vessel problems. A notable rise in triglyceride levels was observed among the workers, which is often linked to a higher risk of cardiovascular disease. Although the findings suggest that exposure to petroleum emissions negatively impacts cardiovascular health, further research is needed to understand how these emissions affect the body's oxidative systems and contribute to the development of cardiovascular disease.
