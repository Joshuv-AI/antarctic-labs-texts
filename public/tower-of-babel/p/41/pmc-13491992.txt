The role of AI-assisted drug repurposing in neurological disorders: a systematic review of validation strategies, challenges and opportunities
Authors: Fengshou Chen, Limei Wang, Hao Liu, Qasim Khan, Bing Tang, Naren Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeurological disorders refer to a diverse group of conditions that affect the brain, peripheral nerves, and spinal cord and impair socioemotional, cognitive, motor, and sensory functions. Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Multiple Sclerosis (MS), Parkinson’s disease (PD), Huntington’s disease (HD), and Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) are some of the well-known neurodegenerative diseases that affect millions of people worldwide. Despite the advanced technologies and nano-drug delivery systems, the success rate of developing drugs for neurological disorders is significantly low. Among several constraints, including gastrointestinal irritation, rapid metabolism, and low stability, the blood-brain barrier (BBB) emerges as one of the key challenges in the development and application of drugs against neurological disorders. These challenges necessitate innovative approaches to develop cost-effective therapeutic strategies. Drug repurposing, the discovery of new therapeutic benefits of existing drugs, is a promising drug discovery approach for discovering potential treatment options for complex neurological disorders. This review aims to explore the advanced and significant progress in drug repurposing for major neurological disorders, including MS, AD, PD, ALS, HD, stroke, and neuropsychiatric conditions. It places an explicit emphasis on discussing the potential role of artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted drug repurposing and understanding of the biological mechanisms in discovering new drugs for these neurological conditions. This also examines current challenges in drug repurposing and provides a critical review of the available opportunities and limitations in AI-assisted drug repurposing.Graphical abstract Keywords: Neurological Disorders, Alzheimer’s Disease, Parkinson’s Disease, Multiple Sclerosis, Artificial Intelligence, Drug Repurposing

Introduction

Neurological disorders, the most widespread of which are neurodegenerative, cerebrovascular, autoimmune, craniocerebral trauma, intracranial tumors, and spinal cord diseases, cause substantial harm to cognitive functioning, leading to one of the highest incidences of mortality, morbidity, and disability worldwide [1–4]. Over time, the spatial and temporal patterns of these neurological disorders have changed significantly due to ageing, population growth, urbanisation, and increased life expectancy, raising concerns about their global prevalence [5–8] (Fig. 1).

Neurological disorders are categorised into neurodegenerative, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [9], Parkinson’s disease (PD) [10], and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [11], neurodevelopmental, such as epilepsy [12], cerebrovascular, such as stroke [13], and neurological-immunological, such as multiple sclerosis (MS) [14] (Fig. 1). These conditions are classified [15] by the specific regions of the brain affected [16], the type of neurons damaged [17], and the class of protein that accumulates and causes neuronal injury [18]. Common clinical manifestations include progressive cognitive decline [19], motor dysfunction [19], functional impairment [20, 21], and neuropsychiatric disorders such as memory deficits [22, 23], sleep disturbances [22, 24], and psychomotor performance deficits [25, 26].

The exact molecular mechanisms and etiological factors for neurodegeneration remain incompletely understood; however, people with a family history are at high risk, which suggests the role of genetics in its pathophysiology [27]. Increasing age is another important risk factor of neurodegeneration and has attracted considerable attention due to the growing proportion of ageing populations in most developed countries. This also suggests that the prevalence of neurodegeneration-related conditions will increase over the next few years [28, 29]. Neuropathological examinations of aged individuals frequently reveal characteristic features of neurodegenerative diseases, such as Lewy bodies in PD and neurofibrillary tangles in AD [30, 31]. These observations raise the important question of whether neurodegeneration is an intrinsic component of the normal aging process or an early phase of a neurodegenerative disease [32, 33].

Drug development is a long and complex process that often results in products that fail to reach the market, primarily due to adverse side effects, high product costs, efficacy issues, and safety and regulatory concerns [34, 35]. It has been reported that the average time required to develop a new drug from target identification to the regulatory approval stage is approximately 12–15 years [35]. Similarly, the mean cost of a new drug is estimated at up to 2.6 billion, including clinical trial, research, and development costs [36, 37]. In addition, the low approval rate for newly developed drugs is another potential problem for the pharmaceutical industry and drug development institutions. Nearly nine out of ten drug candidates that enter clinical development fail during phase I, II, or III trials or at the regulatory approval stage [38, 39]. Notably, this ~ 90% attrition rate applies only to drugs that have already entered phase I clinical trials and does not account for failures occurring during preclinical development. When preclinical candidates are included, the overall failure rate of drug discovery and development is substantially higher than 90% [40].

Drug repurposing, also called therapeutic switching, drug repositioning, or indication expansion, is the process of discovering new therapeutic benefits for existing known drugs that are already tested, developed, and approved [46–50]. Given the high cost and lengthy process of new drug development, drug repurposing has attained enormous attention as an alternative approach to treating various diseases, including cancer [51, 52], cardiovascular [53], metabolic [54–56], and neurological diseases [57]. The biggest benefit of drug repurposing is that safety, toxicity, manufacturing protocols, and pharmacokinetics are already known, making the approach less risky compared to de novo drug development. In addition, drug repurposing can bypass several hurdles at the early stages of development, leading to a higher rate of regulatory approval [58].

Over the last few decades, drug repurposing has been increasingly supported by the emerging advanced technologies, including integrated omics, computational approaches, and systems pharmacology [59, 60]. These approaches assist the process of repurposing through phenotypic screening, target prediction, and elucidating the mechanisms of action. In addition to in silico strategies, high-throughput screening and data mining of clinical trial databases provide complementary approaches for validating repurposing hypotheses [61]. Despite these advances, drug repurposing continues to face challenges, such as regulatory uncertainties, limited commercial incentives, and intellectual property issues, which can impede successful translation [62, 63].

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in drug repurposing holds significant promise for overcoming some of these challenges. AI algorithms, including machine learning (ML) [64], deep learning (DL) [65, 66], graph neural networks (GNNs) [67], reinforcement learning (RL) [68], recurrent neural networks (RNNs) [69], and convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [70], have emerged as an important tools that can have a significant impact on drug repurposing for neurological diseases. These AI tools can perform diverse tasks such as the prediction of molecular interactions, analysis of extensive biomedical data, identification of potential drug candidates, and optimization of drug repurposing, with significantly reduced time and financial demands [71–73].

Thus, AI-assisted drug repurposing could be a powerful strategy against the complex neurodevelopmental, neurodegenerative, and neuroinflammatory diseases. This review article provides a detailed analysis of the emerging computational and AI-assisted drug repurposing approaches for the management of neurological diseases. It aims to examine AI methodologies, including ML, DL, data integration techniques, and network-based models, used in drug repurposing for major neurological diseases. It also explores current challenges and future opportunities for advancing AI-driven repurposing strategies, with an emphasis on improving clinical translation and personalized therapeutic development in neurology.

Methodological approaches

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across major electronic and bibliographic databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, BioMed Central, Scopus, Web of Science (WoS), JSTOR, Google Scholar, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. In addition, several publishers and platforms with high-impact journals, such as Nature Portfolio, Cell, ScienceDirect (Elsevier), SpringerLink, SAGE Journals, Taylor & Francis, and BMJ Journals, were also searched. These databases and platforms were systematically searched to ensure a broad coverage of related topics, including biomedicine, neuroscience, computational, and translational medicine. A range of specific keywords, such as artificial intelligence, neurodegenerative diseases, neurological disorders, drug repurposing, AI-assisted drug repurposing, computational drug repurposing, and the role of AI in neurological diseases, were used. To enhance reproducibility and transparency, representative Boolean search strategies were applied across databases. For instance, MEDLINE/PubMed search was carried out including combinations such as (“artificial intelligence” OR “deep learning” OR “machine learning” OR “computational methods” OR neural networks) AND (“drug repositioning” OR “drug repurposing” OR “therapeutic switching”) AND (“neurological disorders” OR “Parkinson’s disease” OR “Alzheimer’s disease” OR “multiple sclerosis” OR “amyotrophic lateral sclerosis” OR “epilepsy” OR “multiple sclerosis”). Similar keyword combinations were adapted for other databases based on their indexing systems. The literature search was limited to studies published between 2010 and 2026.

A total of 790 articles were retrieved from the databases mentioned above, of which 73 were duplicates. Based on topic mismatch and insufficient data availability, a total of 148 and 129 articles were excluded from the remaining articles, respectively. As shown in the flowchart (Fig. 2), a total of 364 articles were deemed suitable for inclusion in this review article.

Eligibility criteria

To ensure consistency, a structured selection process was conducted for this article. Initially, the selection process included screening the titles and abstracts of the selected articles, during which studies not related to drug repurposing, neurological disorders, or AI-based approaches were excluded. In the next phase, full articles were analysed for eligibility based on the inclusion and exlusion we had for this article. Studies were eligible for inclusion if they were full-text articles published in English and analysed the role of AI and computational-based approaches in drug repurposing for neurological disorders, with a focus on stroke, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, multiple sclerosis, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Original research articles, meta-analyses, and systematic reviews on AI-assisted drug repurposing were included. Articles analysing the biochemical and molecular mechanisms that helped explain the drug pharmacokinetics were also included.

Based on the exclusion criteria, commentaries, editorials, letters, articles lacking relevant data, and articles published in languages other than English were all excluded. Original and review articles with insufficient data were also excluded. The screening process was conducted independently by two reviewers, and any discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus to ensure consistency in study selection. Neurological disorders: pathophysiology and conventional treatment options

The collective term “neurological disorders” refers to a group of conditions that affect the structure and function of the neurons and nervous system, including the spinal cord, brain, and peripheral nerves [74–76]. AD, PD, HD, MS, stroke, and ALS are some of the major neurological disorders that are gradually increasing in the ageing populations worldwide [77]. Despite immense technological advancements in the diagnosis and treatment of diseases, the exact underlying molecular mechanisms and disease-modifying treatment options for neurological diseases remain challenging.

Pathophysiology of neurological disorders

Common physiological signs of neurological disorders include neuronal death, loss of dendrites, dysregulated neurotransmitter release, elevated oxidative stress, demyelination, neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and abnormal neuronal migration [78, 79] (Table 2). The pathophysiology of neurological disorders is complex, and that is one of the fundamental factors for the scarcity of drugs against these disorders [80, 81].

Among these, ALS, AD, PD, and HD are categorised as neurodegenerative diseases, characterized by progressive neurodegeneration. In AD, a number of proteins, such as tau tangles and beta-amyloid plaques, accumulate, disrupting the normal signalling and leading to functional and structural decline of the brain [82] (Fig. 3). On the other hand, PD is characterized by the progressive deterioration of motor function, associated with the continuous decline of neurons [83, 84]. The exact causes of AD and PD diseases are not precisely defined; however, ageing [85], genetic [86], epigenetic [87], and environmental factors [88] contribute to their onset (Fig. 3). Therefore, the heterogeneity of gene-environment interactions complicates the development of a single, universal therapy for these diseases [89].

Schematic illustration of the common pathophysiology, molecular mechanisms, etiological triggers, and downstream clinical phenotypes. It shows how environmental, genetic, epigenetic, and ageing triggers dysregulate cellular and molecular pathophysiology, including Aβ, tau, and α-syn aggregation, inflammation, cytokine release (TNF-α, IL-1β), mitochondrial failure, activated microglia, disrupted BBBs, and oxidative stress

HD is another inherited neurological condition that significantly affects patients’ physical and cognitive capabilities [118]. A genetic mutation in the huntingtin gene (HTT), followed by the synthesis of a defective protein variant, is the primary cause of this disease [119]. Unfortunately, this is an autosomal dominant genetic disorder, and inheriting just one mutated copy of the gene from either parent is enough to cause the disease [118, 120]. ALS, also known as Lou Gehrig’s disease [121], is another major neurodegenerative condition that affects nerve cells in the spinal cord and the brain. It particularly deteriorates motor neurons, and as the disease progresses, the brain loses its ability to contract muscles, leading to difficulty with speaking, swallowing, and breathing [122]. Contrary to the above diseases, MS is an immune-mediated neurological disorder characterized by the deterioration of myelin due to the immune system [123]. This results in hyperinflammation, cytokine release, and the disruption of normal impulse propagation in the body [124].

Stroke is another neurological condition that results from reduced blood supply to the brain, most commonly due to blood leakage or obstruction of blood flow. The pathophysiology of epilepsy is diverse and not fully elucidated. The fundamental factor that is common to all epilepsies is the imbalance between GABAergic signaling-based excitatory and glutamatergic signaling-based inhibitory activity [125, 126]. The etiology of partial epilepsies consists of developmental malformations [127], cortical lesions [128], tumors [129], and acute cortical injury [130] resulting from trauma or stroke.

Treatment options for neurological disorders

The etiologies of neurological disorders are complex, and a multitude of factors, including genetic predisposition [131], traumatic injuries [132], environmental triggers [133], and lifestyle influences [134, 135]. Despite the technological advancements, there is no one-size-fits-all approach to treating neurological disorders [136] (Fig. 4). For instance, HD [137] and PD [138] are characterized by movement-related abnormalities, while AD is marked by a progressive cognitive decline [139]. Similarly, stroke is characterized by paralysis, weakness, and slurred speech [140], while epilepsy is associated with recurrent seizures, which can be in the form of loss of consciousness, convulsions, or staring spells, depending on the fundamental cause of epilepsy [141].

Schematic overview of pharmacological, non-pharmacological, and advanced therapeutic strategies targeting the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms across several neurological disorders such as AD, PD, ALS, MS, and epilepsy

Depending on the neurological condition, a number of non-pharmacological therapies, including cognitive rehabilitation, behavioral, physical, vagus nerve stimulation, occupational, speech, and language therapies have been used to reduce the symptoms of the underlying condition. Within pharmacological treatment strategies, cholinesterase inhibitors such as donepezil [142] and N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor antagonists, such as memantine [143], are commonly prescribed to alleviate the symptoms of AD [142]. In addition, supplemental therapies such as occupational therapy and cognitive training may offer further benefits [144].

Levodopa [145] or levodopa in combination with carbidopa [146] are effective in reducing motor symptoms in PD. MAO-B inhibitors [147] such as selegiline and dopamine agonists [148] such as pramipexole can also be prescribed as supplementary medications. In the later stages, PD patients may experience impairments in cognitive function, verbal communication, and swallowing. Regular physical activity, such as exercises that help enhance balance, has been shown to support symptom management and overall well-being [149]. In the management of ALS, edaravone, an adjuvant, helps slow the deterioration of physical abilities [150]. Riluzole is another disease-modifying neuroprotective drug prescribed for ALS, which helps reduce the glutamate-mediated excitotoxicity [151]. Unfortunately, there is no drug that can treat this disease, and patients with ALS die due to complications such as respiratory failure within a few years of the appearance of symptoms [152].

Although HD is a genetic neurological disorder with no approved treatment, VMAT2 inhibitors such as tetrabenazine can be prescribed to reduce dopamine in the brain and control the motor symptoms [153]. Similarly, there is no definite treatment for MS, and most of the medications, such as interferon beta, natalizumab, and glatiramer acetate, are based on symptom management. However, some individuals experience only mild symptoms or go long stretches without them, while others face a more gradual yet progressively disabling decline. Valproate, which increases GABA activity, and carbamazepine, which blocks sodium channels, are commonly prescribed to reduce seizures in epilepsy. Other than medicine, epilepsy surgery, the ketogenic diet, and deep brain stimulation therapies are helpful in the management of epilepsy.

Alteplase, an effective thrombolytic agent approved by the FDA, is the only drug against ischemic stroke. However, this drug has a short time window and causes intracerebral haemorrhage as its potential side effect.

Overview of AI-assisted drug repurposing and the role of nanotechnology in neurological disorders

Overview of AI-assisted drug repurposing

The estimated budget of new drug development is approximately $2.6 billion, with an average time of 13–15 years, from basic research to the clinical trial stage [154, 155]. Drug repurposing, also known as drug repositioning, involves evaluating clinically approved drugs used for one disease to determine whether they can provide therapeutic benefits without causing adverse effects in other diseases (Fig. 5). Today, it has become a crucial strategy in drug discovery, enabling the identification of new therapeutic applications for existing drugs or drug candidates across a wide range of human diseases, including neurological disorders [156].

Schematic overview of AI-driven drug repurposing for neurological disorders. These models identify disease mechanisms and predict candidate repurposed drugs targeting key pathological pathways. Predicted candidates are subsequently validated through in vitro, in vivo, and biomarker-based experimental studies

One of the ongoing challenges in neurological disorders is the identification of disease-modifying therapies. Drug repurposing provides the opportunity to fill this gap and discover new drugs with less cost in comparatively less time. However, over the last few decades, only 30% of repurposed drugs have gained market approval. The low success rate could be attributed to several fundamental factors, including biological complexity, limited understanding, and safety concerns [57].

Several drugs have been repurposed, which have shown potential in slowing the progression or relieving the debilitating symptoms of neurological disorders. For instance, memantine, which was initially developed against diabetes, was successfully repurposed as an NMDA receptor antagonist to treat AD [157]. Similarly, apomorphine [158], which was developed for treating erectile dysfunction, and amantadine [159], developed as an antiviral drug, were repurposed to treat PD. Several other drugs, including valproic acid, propranolol, fingolimod, citicoline, and riluzole, which were developed for other diseases, have been subsequently repurposed for epilepsy [160], migraine [161], MS [162], stroke [163] and ALS [164], respectively.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has attracted substantial attention for its revolutionary role in reasoning and problem-solving, mimicking and enhancing human cognitive abilities [165]. However, despite these advances, and given the multifactorial nature of neurological diseases, these models do not necessarily translate into considerable clinical success rates. These models help in cutting down the size of potential candidates and predicting strong interactions between drugs and targets; they still have shortcomings when it comes to making accurate predictions because of the presence of biases in the training data set, inadequate coverage of biological processes, and a lack of incorporation of factors such as blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration. All of these factors contribute to the gap between prediction and experimental validation, leading many candidates to fail in preclinical or clinical trials [166, 167].

AI-based models and computational methods, such as DL, ML, natural language processing (NLP), graph-based learning, and network biology-based algorithms, have enabled rapid identification of drug candidates and evaluation of large compound libraries. This can significantly reduce the significantly high developmental costs and extensive laboratory experimental work [168–170]. However, their accuracy depends heavily on the quality of the dataset. For instance, inadequate coverage of complex biological mechanisms, such as pharmacokinetics and toxicity, renders certain AI-generated predictions inapplicable.

Nanotechnology in drug delivery: addressing blood-brain barrier (BBB) challenges

Over the last few decades, nanotechnology has attained enormous attention due to its ability to deliver neurotherapeutics across the blood-brain barrier (BBB). Owing to their physicochemical properties, compatibility, small size, biodegradability, and customizable surfaces, nanotechnology-based drug delivery approaches hold great promise for the site-specific delivery of therapeutics in neurological disorders, including AD, PD, HD, ALS, and MS [171].

Several innovative nanocarriers, such as liposomes, micelles, dendrimers, solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs), and polymeric nanoparticles (PNPs), have emerged as effective delivery systems for several neurological disorders. For instance, micelles, composed of amphiphilic copolymers or amphiphilic surfactants, are among the most studied drug carrier systems. It has been demonstrated that polymeric micelles are comparatively stable with a high distribution and prolonged circulation as compared to non-polymeric micelles [172]. In recent years, studies have reported useful modifications to micelles that enable them to cross the BBB more easily. For example, in a study, modifying the amphiphilic segment micelle system increased drug uptake across the BBB [173]. Similarly, blocking of the P-gp (P-glycoprotein) efflux transporters, which are highly expressed in the BBB, would facilitate the delivery of drug-loaded micelles to the central nervous system (CNS) [174].

Liposomes, which are small spherical vesicles, are another key nanocarrier composed of a hydrophilic structure enclosed by phospholipid bilayers. Traditionally, liposomes have a short circulation time; therefore, various attempts have been made to modify these nanocarriers to prolong their circulation time [175]. One such modification reported by several studies is the polyethylene glycol (PEG) coating on liposomes [176, 177]. However, further modifications, such as targeting these liposomes with ligands, such as monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), are required to facilitate their delivery across the BBB [178]. Brain drug delivery via immunoliposomes is also employed in gene therapy using TRsMAb-conjugated liposomes carrying a plasmid encoding tyrosine hydroxylase to treat PD in rats [179]. Another approach to facilitate liposome delivery across the BBB is to modify them with cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) [180]. Another key type of nanocarrier is polymeric nanoparticles, which can be composed of synthetic or biodegradable polymers, with a dense core that encapsulates a hydrophilic corona and lipophilic drugs to enhance stability. Surface modification approaches have been used to increase the systemic circulation time of these NPs [181].

In nanocarrier systems, dendrimers are emerging polymeric structures that have attracted attention for their versatility in delivering drugs for the treatment of neurological disorders. These nanocarriers are 3-D symmetrical structures, with an inner core that can be engineered with functional groups and ligands. Even though dendrimers have extensive applications in drug delivery, their use in biological systems remains constrained by complex synthesis processes and potential toxicity issues [182]. Nanoemulsions, which refer to water-in-oil (W/O) or oil-in-water (O/W) are colloidal particulate systems composed of surface-active agents, edible oils, and water. These nanocarriers are preferred due to several limitations of conventional nanocarriers, such as limited permeability across the BBB and low bioavailability [183]. For instance, formulation of nanoemulsions containing omega-3 PUFA rich oils gives unique features to nanocarriers for crossing the BBB, thus leading to the fast delivery of drugs to target peripheral locations, in particular the brain [184].

In the treatment of neurological disorders, the BBB is the most challenging and pharmacologically active barrier that limits drug delivery. These challenges have been addressed by various approaches that modify the drug-loaded nanocarriers to enable easy BBB crossing [185]. Despite the important role of these nanocarriers in the treatment of several neurological disorders, their systemic toxicity is now a key concern in nanotechnology. Other factors, such as immunogenicity and protein corona, may also affect the performance of nanoparticles, further influencing their efficacy and safety profile. Thus, greater emphasis should be placed on refining the experimental methods used in developing nanoparticles. Nanoparticle dosages should depend on the level of exposure. Looking at the consequences of a realistic dose of nanoparticles prevents the possibility of observing artificial toxicity from an overly high dose and enables the actual toxicity of the nanoparticles to be observed, thereby avoiding systemic toxicity.

Role of nanocarriers in AI-assisted drug repurposing

The successful completion of drug repurposing for neurological disorders starts with another challenge: the successful delivery of these drugs to the CNS. Combining nanobiotechnology with AI-assisted drug design may provide an excellent platform for the efficient delivery to the targeted site [186]. AI-assisted repurposing is based on molecular interactions, gene expression, or clinical trial outcomes. At the same time, it fails to incorporate pharmacokinetic parameters, such as the drug’s bioavailability, BBB permeability, and metabolic stability, which can negatively affect its effectiveness. As a result, there is always a possibility that the selected candidate might exhibit excellent efficacy but fail in delivery. Therefore, nanocarriers can help by providing [187].

Over the last few years, the integration of AI has revolutionised the design of nanocarriers, including their size, composition, surface charge, and drug release profile, which play key roles in their behaviour and treatment outcomes [188]. ML algorithms can learn from experimental data sets and predict the most efficient nanoparticle formulations for a given drug and disease target. For example, supervised ML models may establish correlations between nanoparticle parameters and efficacy/toxicity, whereas DL may discover non-linear connections beyond traditional experimental designs [189].Besides optimizing the structure itself, artificial intelligence is also important in nanoformulation design, where its aim is to predict drug-carrier interactions and their stability. Repurposed drugs are often not optimized for delivery to the central nervous system and may pose issues related to poor water solubility, rapid clearance from the body, or biological instability. The use of nanoformulations, such as liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, or lipid-based systems, can improve drug stability and increase bioavailability. Predictive AI models allow us to identify the most efficient drug carriers based on predicted encapsulation efficiency and release kinetics [190].

Biomedical databases for drug repurposing

The process of drug repurposing is multilayered and, in part, depends on drawing information from several healthcare-focused databases [191]. These databases hold information on gene sequences, protein structures, pathways, clinical trials, and interactions between molecular targets, which in turn provide a foundation for therapeutic purposes [192]. These databases are reliable, and they source their data from studies conducted by academic institutions, research laboratories, private research companies, and government agencies.

The controlled-access Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database curates studies on gene expression and genetic variation from several types of human tissues. These tissues are taken from donors and assayed to collect genetic data [193, 194]. Combining the data with analytical tools can help elucidate the link between disease and underlying genetic disorders. However, GTEx does not provide raw experimental data as the available data is processed and tabulated [195, 196]. The ENCODE (Encyclopaedia of DNA Elements) database is another useful open-access resource that houses multi-omics datasets from human and mouse cell lines, with the aim of defining their functional elements [197, 198]. Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) is another open-access functional genomics database that accepts array and sequence-based data submissions and provides tools to find and manage transcriptomic profiles [199].

As the drug repurposing pipeline demonstrates, transcriptomic data are complemented with information on approved drugs to identify novel therapeutic targets, and with chemical databases such as ChEMBL and PubChem to elucidate interactions between molecular targets and drugs. ChEMBL is an authentic, open-access database of bioactive molecules with therapeutic details, which is useful in the validation, development, and application of in silico methods such as machine learning and AI-assisted tools [200]. Likewise, PubChem is a freely available database that provides information on diverse chemical compounds, including lipids, carbohydrates, peptides, nucleic acids, and synthetic compounds, and their properties. PubChem also provides curated collections on specific topics such as COVID-19 [201, 202]. For drug-target and pathway interactions, DrugBank and the manually curated KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) database serve as comprehensive repositories of therapeutic compounds, providing information on their physicochemical properties and molecular target mechanisms of action [203].

Integration of biomedical databases with neurological drug repurposing

Integration of biomedical databases with computational methodologies has played a significant role in the discovery of biomarkers associated with certain diseases and possible treatment targets in neurological disorders [204, 205]. These databases have been useful in studies of neurological disorders, as they provide readily available data and tools for comparative and validation research. For instance, Madrid et al. used GTEx in order to classify blood-based biomarkers for AD. GTEx was also used to identify expression patterns of the selected genes through expression quantitative trait loci (eQTls) analysis in blood and brain transcriptomics [206]. Liu et al. identified ferroptosis-related differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in ischemic stroke using GEO datasets. Zhu and colleagues identified 16 hub genes associated with epilepsy diagnosis by comparing DEG expression in healthy and epileptic patients from the GSE143272 dataset in the GEO database [207].

In another study, Salapa et al. (2024) used ENCODE to validate their findings regarding the hnRNP A1 dysregulation in MS, which modifies RNA splicing and promotes neurodegeneration [208]. Chen et al. used ChIP-Seq data to explore potential underlying PD risk loci to identify candidate genes for characterization of causal elements and future drug development [209]. Gao et al. examined the association between epilepsy and subarachnoid haemorrhage using transcriptomic data from GEO and performed KEGG pathway enrichment analyses to characterize their functions [210].

Still, despite such studies, it is important to draw on biomedical clinical trials and other real-world datasets. These include electronic health records, insurance claims data, imaging data, and others, which aid in advancing therapeutics and elucidating disease mechanisms [211]. ClinicalTrials.gov ranks among the most significant archives of clinical trials; however, it primarily contains the United States-based trials, limiting its international relevance [212, 213]. The curation of such data thus provides a multifunctional understanding of disease mechanisms, diverse, patient-specific therapeutic strategies and improved health outcomes in neurological disorders [214]. Nevertheless, there are limitations to the clinical implications of such databases, including differences in data quality, insufficient standardisation, and incomplete coverage of important clinical variables. For example, transcriptomic databases cannot account for post-translational modifications, whereas chemical and drug-target databases rely on curated/predicted interaction networks that may not reflect real-life conditions. The underrepresentation of failures in training data leads to bias in AI model training, potentially resulting in overestimation of their efficacy [215]. Such issues point to the need for integrated approaches that incorporate multi-omics and clinical data, along with the use of benchmarking measures.

The role of artificial intelligence (AI) in drug repurposing for neurological disorders

Drug repurposing in neurological disorders has immense importance, representing the challenge of identifying potential drugs from thousands of already existing drugs. The process of drug repurposing typically involves data collection, screening, prediction, validation and clinical translation (Table 3). At all these phases, integrating AI can make drug repurposing significantly easier, more economically viable, and more accurate (Fig. 6). However, the AI techniques used for drug repurposing have their own pros and cons. ML methods perform well when it comes to recognizing patterns in structured data; however, their lack of interpretability remains an issue [216]. DL techniques can recognize more complex relationships between the variables involved; however, the need for large amounts of data and the lack of interpretability remain drawbacks [217]. GNNs offer a notable advantage for representing biological processes and interactions. Nonetheless, the performance of such models will depend heavily on the quality of the underlying interaction information [218]. Multi-omics integration techniques improve relevance to disease; however, the heterogeneity and scarcity of multi-omics data remain obstacles [219]. All this highlights the importance of choosing models based on the particular biological problem of interest.

A schematic representation of the process of drug repurposing involving several strategies, including AI and computational modelling, multi-modal data integration, drug repurposing procedures and validation processes. Various AI-based strategies, such as knowledge graph, machine learning and LLM-based reasoning, are highlighted

In addition to the above traditional ML and DL methods, another new class of AI, called large language models (LLMs), has gained attention for its effectiveness in biomedical research [220]. For instance, models like PubMedBERT [221] and BioGPT [222] can retrieve information from large-scale biomedical literature, identify connections between drugs and diseases, and generate hypotheses for drug repurposing. Similarly, general-purpose models like ChatGPT can be used for heterogeneous information integration and literature-based discovery; however, their use in drug repurposing is still hindered by factors such as hallucination, inability to reason mechanistically, and data dependency [223].

The following sections present studies investigating the role of AI in drug repurposing for different neurological disorders. The process of drug repurposing typically involves data collection, screening, prediction, validation and clinical translation.

Alzheimer’s disease (AD)

Owing to the limited disease-modifying therapeutic options and the low success rates of traditional drug discovery approaches, the AI-based drug repurposing models may play a key role in identifying new drugs against AD. Most drug repurposing approaches target single or limited pathways, without considering important disease risk factors, such as ageing and somatic mutations. Li et al. [212] developed DeepDrug, a GNN-based model to identify novel therapeutic indications for FDA-approved drugs in AD. Using this approach, five existing drugs, including empagliflozin, tofacitinib, doxercalciferol, niraparib, and baricitinib, were identified as potential drugs for treating AD. To increase disease relevance, they incorporated various biological dimensions, including long genes, somatic mutation markers, and several pathways related to ageing and the immune system (Fig. 6). This study reflects the broader trend in AI-based repurposing, in which graph- and network-based methods are particularly ateffective in integrating multi-level biological data.

Identifying sensitive and specific biomarkers of very early AD progression is important for supporting researchers and clinicians in developing novel therapies and assessing their efficacy, while also reducing the duration and associated expenditure of clinical trials. To improve the early diagnosis of AD, Salvatore et al. [224] developed a machine learning-based model to identify specific brain imaging markers. They identified neural biomarkers to accurately categorize neuroimaging data based on atrophic structural patterns in MRI. This was accomplished by categorizing variations in the thalamus, hippocampus, temporal cortex, entorhinal cortex, insular cortex, and other regions associated with the pathological AD morphology and by distinguishing those patients who will continue to develop it [224]. Imaging-based AI approaches supplement molecular approaches with phenotypic knowledge; however, their integration remains limited to date, creating another gap in contemporary drug repurposing models.

AD is a heterogeneous disease with a myriad of genes, molecules and pathways being implicated in its pathogenesis. One key challenge in drug development and drug repurposing is the lack of understanding of this complexity. In a study, Mottaqi et al. [225] developed PRISM-ML, an AI-assisted model to characterize the molecular heterogeneity of AD and identify suitable drugs for repurposing. They identified six FDA-approved drugs that, together, target multiple bottleneck genes and AD-relevant pathways, validated using large-scale real-world insurance claims data. Notably, they identified promethazine as a potential repurposed drug associated with an approximately 60% reduction in AD risk. In all these studies, there is a common trend of integrating multiple sources to improve the clinical relevance of disease; however, variations in dataset quality and verification techniques play an important role in determining predictive efficacy.

The integration of systems biology, multiomics and AI algorithms has emerged as a powerful approach for drug repurposing. By analyzing multiple omics layers, including transcriptomic, proteomic, genomic, epigenomic, and metabolomic, a better understanding of the disease and target drug repurposing can be established [226]. For instance, Grabowska et al. [227] conducted a multiomics study combining genome-wide association studies (GWAS), transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS), and drug perturbation analyses, identifying aspirin as a strong candidate for reversing AD-associated gene signatures.

Similarly, Fang et al. [228] found that the use of sildenafil, a hypertensive drug, was associated with a lower risk of AD by surveying insurance claims data regarding multiple amyloid and tau endophenotypes. They constructed and validated an endophenotype-based drug repurposing framework, used network proximity analysis for in silico repurposing, and tested health outcomes in populations. Another study suggested Gabapentin and Baricitinib as the most likely candidates to treat AD-associated pathological changes in the hippocampus and cerebral cortex. This was based on a computational framework evaluating data from several AD-related gene expression studies, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based phenotypes, genome-wide association profiles, and drug-target interactions [229]. Similarly, Xie et al. [230] applied an in silico fragment-based drug discovery (FBDD) method to identify molecules that inhibit amyloid peptide aggregation. Through fragment clustering analysis, they identified 17 fragment clusters prominent among inhibitors of amyloid peptide aggregation.

Overall, these studies show the increasing role of AI methods in the discovery of potential AD therapies, but each varies significantly in data integration, model interpretability, and verification processes. Network analysis and graph learning models have been shown to be highly effective in modelling biological interactions, while multi-omics modelling has demonstrated high relevance to the disease, but suffers from limited and heterogeneous data. Notably, most of these studies use either retrospective datasets or in silico verification, with few proceeding to experimental or clinical verification.

Parkinson’s disease (PD)

Most of the existing pharmacological strategies for PD are largely symptomatic and do not address the underlying molecular and cellular mechanisms of the disorder. Current treatments for PD can be broadly categorized into dopamine-based therapies and agents targeting other neurotransmitter systems [231–233]. Several drugs, such as amantadine, were primarily designed for other diseases, but due to drug repurposing drives, they were found to be effective in treating PD motor symptoms [234–236]. AI and computational network-based analysis have now substantially accelerated this process and enabled the identification of multiple candidate targets as novel therapeutic solutions for treating PD.

Guo et al. [237] formulated a computational network based on interactions between molecular targets and treatment compounds. DrugBank was used to retrieve information on AI-repurposed drugs that were previously approved by the FDA. Earlier literature was reviewed in order to choose potential drugs with possible anti-PD activity. The selected drugs were assessed for neuroprotective effects using in vitro studies in the SH-SY5Y cell and an in vivo mouse model of 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium (MPP+)-induced PD. Cyproheptadine and Omaveloxolone emerged as the best candidates due to their efficacy in improving PD-associated pathologies and neuroprotective effect in dopaminergic neurons [237]. This is a PD-centric drug repurposing approach, in which computational methods are usually corroborated by limited experimental evidence, emphasizing the continued struggle to translate computerized results into clinical applications.

Another study by Gorenflo and colleagues [238] focused on preventive strategies for PD. They performed a retrospective cohort analysis on a comprehensive electronic health record dataset of patients diagnosed with ADHD or attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder using an AI-built drug discovery framework called “Knowledge Graph (KG)-Predict”. ADHD was selected, as the treatment of this disorder is through the prescription of amphetamine-derived stimulant medications. KG-Predict revealed formerly unknown links between these drugs and PD, followed by clinical corroboration and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. Ultimately, the study demonstrated that regulated and properly prescribed use of amphetamines may block or avert the onset of PD [238]. Knowledge Graphs are especially useful for identifying hidden correlations; however, they require high-quality datasets for reliable predictions.

Kankariya et al. [239] developed a novel computational platform that integrates biological networks, gene expression profiles, and the PD Ontology Database (PDOD) to identify potential drug repurposing candidates for PD therapy. Machine learning–based approaches, including the PDOD-derived scoring system and the Random Walk with Restart (RWR) algorithm, were employed to prioritize drug-disease associations and rank candidate compounds according to their predicted therapeutic relevance [239]. Similarly, preclinical studies have shown the promising potential of dimethyl fumarate (DMF) in PD treatment, approved by the FDA for the treatment of psoriasis and MS. DMF has shown neuroprotective effects by regulating oxidative stress and inflammation, which characterize the onset of PD. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that DMF has therapeutic effects, as evidenced by both in vivo and in vitro models in PD. DMF modulates the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway, thereby improving PD-linked alterations in brain regions associated with motor function [240]. By combining AI and computational models, these studies highlight several promising therapeutic candidates warranting further investigation for PD treatment.

To summarise, these findings emphasise the promise of using AI-assisted strategies to develop repurposed drugs for PD, there are still many issues that need to be addressed. The models focus on drug-target interactions but do not sufficiently account for disease-specific mechanisms, including mitochondrial dysfunction [241], α-synuclein accumulation [242], and progressive degeneration of dopaminergic neurons [243]. Furthermore, most candidates identified by computational methods have been verified through in vitro and in vivo experiments, which may not reflect the disease in humans. There is thus a translational issue, as some drugs that appear promising in computational methods may not have clinical efficacy.

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)

ALS, the most common form of motor neuron disease (MND), is a neurodegenerative condition for which developing treatments that alter the natural history of the disease has proven extremely difficult. Despite more than 120 Phase II and III clinical trials conducted in the decade till 2019, riluzole, developed for ALS nearly three decades ago, remains the only widely licensed therapy [244, 245]. Significant progress has been made in the development of antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) therapies targeting ALS-associated genes such as SOD1 and FUS, but these approaches are expected to benefit only a small proportion (< 3%) of ALS patients who carry these pathogenic variants [246]. For the remaining majority of ALS patients without a known genetic cause, there is an urgent need to identify new treatments that can meaningfully alter disease progression [245].

Drug repurposing, which is a significantly less expensive and less time-consuming process, emerged as a potential strategy to find new drugs. The rapid advancement of AI and computational technologies has made this process even more feasible, effective, and precise. A study by Dogra et al. [247] examined the inhibition of proteins, casein kinase 1, ephrin type-A receptor 4, and protein tyrosine kinase 2, using a machine learning model, with biological structures serving as a basis for training AI networks for repurposing the FDA-approved drugs. The structures of the selected drugs were fed into the AI models, and the inhibitory abilities of the selected proteins were observed. The drugs were narrowed down to 18 putative candidates and prioritized through in silico protein-ligand docking analysis, with risperidone showing the highest predicted efficacy [247]. This highlights another similarity in the use of AI in drug repurposing for ALS: candidates are selected effectively, but there is little biological validation of their results.

Another study carried out variant-led analyses that combined functional and positional gene tests using genome-wide ALS association study SNP-genotype data. The analyses demonstrated that B-Raf inhibited the downregulation of RAS/RAF/MAPK (mitogen-activated protein kinase signalling). This technique evaluated the rare-variant modulated risk on whole-genome-sequencing datasets, with emphasis on B-vitamin-associated targets and provided the existing drugs’ genetic mechanisms for ALS. Clinical analysis further examined transcriptomic data from an ALS cell line and found that it harboured a range of pathogenic gene variants underlying familial disease forms, including SOD1, TARDBP, and FUS. New evidence also suggests that BRAF inhibitors (BRAFi) may be used as monotherapy or in combination with trametinib to treat adverse downstream neurodegenerative effects caused by abnormalities in the RAS/RAF/MAPK signal transduction cascade [248]. However, most clinical trials have shown that current ALS treatments are ineffective at prolonging longevity but may mitigate symptoms to some extent by reducing inflammation [248, 249].

In a recent meta-analysis, Temiz and colleagues [250] applied machine learning network biology to investigate four transcriptomic datasets derived from ALS patients, including motor neuron and muscle tissue samples, alongside healthy controls. They identified shared differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in motor neurons and muscle tissue separately. Using these common DEGs as proxies, they defined two highly interconnected co-expression modules: a “Muscle Tissue Cluster” and a “Motor Neuron Cluster.” As a result, five novel drug candidates, including carboplatin, trovafloxacin, nilotinib, clinafloxacin and apratoxin A, were identified as potential therapies for ALS [250].

Despite the encouraging findings of these studies, there are unique challenges associated with AI-based drug repurposing for ALS. ALS involves several overlapping mechanisms, such as oxidative stress [251], TDP-43 proteinopathy [252], and neuroinflammation [253], but these mechanisms have not been incorporated into these studies’ models. Furthermore, the rapid progression of the disease and the lack of longitudinal data from clinical practice hinder the development of AI-based systems to model disease trajectories and treatment responses. Therefore, several candidate drugs identified in silico show promise in cell culture and animal studies, but many do not yield the desired results when applied clinically [254].

Multiple sclerosis (MS)

MS is an autoimmune disease affecting the nervous system, and is marked by immune cell infiltration that leads to relapsing episodes followed by intermittent remission, with the disease course often progressing over time as neurodegenerative processes become more prominent [255]. Given the complex, chronic, and heterogeneous nature of MS, AI-assisted drug repurposing has emerged as a highly promising strategy for discovering novel therapies, especially for progressive MS, in which therapeutic options remain limited [256].

In a study, Menestrina and Recanatini [257] developed an automated computational framework for drug repurposing, along with a network-based analysis to filter drugs based on their association with MS. The influence of the selected genes was identified through enrichment analysis and the human protein-protein interactome. Out of 138 drugs, many mitigated the MS symptoms, and only escitalopram, an antidepressant drug, was found to reduce the disease’s flare-ups. This implies that MS repurposing often depends on immunomodulatory signaling; however, the success rate varies by disease stage and the individual’s immune status. Similarly, Mihai et al. [258] used AI-based drug repurposing and identified paliperidone, febuxostat, and desvenlafaxine as the most likely candidates for treating MS. They designed a pipeline with chemical graph mining and quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) modelling. This was followed by applying a global prediction model incorporating in silico molecular docking data to screen for TRPA1 antagonist interaction drugs that aim to inhibit non-covalent TRPA1 channels. The results highlight the value of multi-stage computation pipelines; however, the accuracy of predictions is strongly dependent on the quality of the data fed into these systems.

Several other studies have employed similar strategies to investigate drug repurposing for MS. For instance, Jiang and colleagues [259] employed an integrative multi-omics framework that combined epigenomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data with protein-protein interaction (PPI) network modeling to systematically prioritize MS-specific drug targets. Another study applied a network-based target prioritization strategy that integrated omics datasets with druggability simulations to identify actionable molecules that modulate key MS-associated pathways [260]. In another study, Afief et al. [261] conducted a GWAS to identify MS-linked genes, followed by extensive molecular pathway analysis and screening of candidate repurposed drugs from DrugBank. Based on further research from MS clinical trials on ClinicalTrials.gov, belatacept, an immunosuppressant, was determined to be suitable for MS management as it targets the CD80 and CD86 genes, which have immune-associated functions [262].

While several potential AI-based therapies for MS have been suggested by the above studies, their ability to predict disease treatment depends on several key factors. For example, MS is characterised by interactions between CNS-resident and peripheral immune cells [263], which are challenging to represent in conventional datasets and computational methods that focus mainly on molecular profiling, without accounting for longitudinal immune profiling, therapy history, progression stage, or relapsing behaviour. Thus, therapies identified via AI-based approaches might significantly differ in efficacy in real clinical settings.

Epilepsy

Epilepsy is a major neurological disorder that can arise from a wide range of direct or indirect insults to the CNS and has been associated with psychological and cognitive impairments. In a meta-analysis, Fiest et al. [264] concluded that the global lifetime prevalence of epilepsy was approximately 1,099 per 100,000 individuals [264]. In addition, the current therapeutic strategies for epilepsy primarily focus on symptom control, with alternative interventions, including surgical approaches [265], dietary therapies [266], and neuromodulation techniques [267].

Epilepsy is treated through a variety of antiepileptic compounds, but more effective treatments are being sought through drug repurposing. However, owing to the urgent need to identify new drugs for the treatment of epilepsy, modern techniques and technologies, including AI and computational models, can significantly accelerate this process. In a study, Lv et al. [268] developed a drug repurposing pipeline for epilepsy with a transformer-based DL approach. By calculating in silico molecular binding affinities of candidate psychiatric inhibitor medications, drug repurposing was carried out targeting proteins encoded by genes associated with epileptogenesis. Lomitapide, a cholesterol-lowering drug, ultimately demonstrated the highest binding affinity for 10 molecular targets. The simulation of the molecular interactions and mechanism analysis provided validation for this data [268]. It is indicative of the increasing usage of molecular docking and binding affinity analysis in epilepsy research that, although valuable, they may fail to represent all nuances of neural network behavior.

In another study, drug repurposing was carried out by evaluating anti-epileptic drugs on the basis of clinical side effects. The side effects of drugs in the SIDER index were compared to existing FDA-approved drugs and their ability to permeate the BBB. In silico analysis was used to select drugs with the highest affinity for known epilepsy-associated receptors, such as the GABAα1-β1 receptor and the voltage-gated calcium channel. Paroxetine, a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) antidepressant, and ziprasidone, an antipsychotic, were found to have the most satisfactory binding affinities compared to other standard anti-epileptic drugs. Ultimately, paroxetine was identified as the most promising candidate for repurposing as an antiepileptic therapy [269]. Side-effect-driven drug repositioning strategies offer an alternative perspective; however, their predictive power depends on the accuracy of clinical data and may not align with the biology of the disease itself.

Mamidala et al. [270] developed NeuroCADR, an algorithm for drug repurposing that randomises forests, k-nearest neighbours (KNN), and decision trees. Data on disease symptoms, target genes and affiliated drug molecules were obtained from several databases. The model achieved high accuracy, outperforming nearly all in silico approaches. NeuroCADR was performed on epilepsy, identifying novel drug candidates for further investigation in clinical trials [270]. Thevissen et al. used a drug repurposing database to evaluate potential drugs for drug-resistant epilepsy through the mechanism of phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH) activators. Clioquinol, an anti-infective haloquinoline, reduced seizures and neuroinflammation in in vivo mouse and zebrafish models.

To conclude, these findings demonstrate that AI- drug repurposing approaches have identified several promising candidates for epilepsy using clinical datasets, molecular interactions and advanced computational models. However, several limitations should be noted in future studies. Most available computational models use static molecular data [271], such as transcriptomic data, which cannot fully replicate the dynamics of neuronal excitability [272]. Additionally, epilepsy is known to be accompanied by specific alterations in the excitatory-inhibitory ratio, the functioning of ion channels, and synapses, features that differ among various brain areas and types of seizures [273]. These alterations and differences in genetics, lesions, and causes of epileptic seizures in different patients will always be challenging, due to the inconsistency of AI-identified drug’s outcomes in the clinic.

Stroke

Stroke remains a leading cause of mortality and long-term disability worldwide and has long been regarded as a “clinical graveyard” due to repeated translational failures [274]. Despite numerous small-molecule candidates advancing to clinical trials, recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA) is the only FDA-approved therapy for acute ischemic stroke. In this context, repurposing drugs already approved by the FDA for other indications represents a promising strategy for accelerating therapeutic development in stroke [275]. With the emergence of AI and other computational methods, the process of drug repurposing is expected to accelerate.

Meng et al. [276] identified sulbutiamine, a synthetic thiamine derivative, as a potential neuroprotective drug for ischemic stroke using a multidimensional data-driven computational drug repositioning strategy (MDCDR). GEO datasets on the topic of ischemic stroke were analysed for DEGs expressed during a stroke, and processed with KEGG enrichment analysis to predict the most suitable drug candidates. Sulbutiamine stood out for its neuroprotective properties, validated in rat and cell line models [276]. This shows how effective data-driven drug repurposing strategies can be for stroke treatments; nevertheless, their clinical translation is sometimes hindered by the small therapeutic window and complicated pathophysiology associated with the disorder.

To conclude, these studies demonstrate that neurological disorders are complex and multifactorial, with genetic and environmental factors playing significant roles in their pathophysiology. Owing to the scarcity of drugs for these neurological disorders, the time invested in de novo drug development, it is high time to find new drugs for treating neurological disorders. AI has the potential to substantially improve efficiency and accuracy while minimizing financial costs, development timelines, and regulatory barriers.

In conclusion, drug repurposing with AI has shown promise in several neurological diseases, but at present, its significance has been limited mainly to the initial discovery process. An overarching challenge common in all research studies on drug repurposing is the use of retroactive information with no established standards for prospective validation. Also, biological determinants such as differences in disease presentation, patient-specific variations, and other pharmacokinetic constraints have not been fully accounted for, leading to mismatches between predicted results and actual findings [277]. Future advancements in this approach will require integrating different data types and mechanisms, along with reliable validation methods.

Validation strategies in AI-driven drug repurposing

In silico validation

In silico validation is one of the most important steps in the drug repurposing process, as it lessens the expense and duration required. It also improves the precision and reliability of the drugs being developed [278, 279] (Fig. 7). This validation approach predicts drug interactions by modelling them using computational frameworks such as pharmacokinetics, toxicity evaluation, and molecular docking, which is one of the most common in silico techniques [280]. For genomic data, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) is a helpful in silico technique. It validates and screens drug candidates based on their gene expression profiles associated with disease modulation and symptom mitigation, such as in AD (AD) [281]. In a study, Tanjin et al. [282] developed an in silico drug repurposing framework integrating dynamics simulation, cheminformatics, molecular docking, and machine learning (ML), to identify and validate therapeutic candidates for AD. Using 1475 molecular descriptors, the model included 615 compounds, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.81, a precision of 0.69, an accuracy of 0.81, a recall of 0.82, and an F1-score of 0.75. The study screened 500 compounds, of which 60 were predicted as potential candidate drugs, yielding a hit rate of approximately 12%. On further screening, the number was reduced to 9 compounds, of which roluperidon and donepezil showed excellent binding affinities of − 11.8 kcal/mol and − 11.9 kcal/mol, respectively. This study reported dynamic simulations lasting over 100 ns and robust predictive performance (AUC > 0.80), further confirming the effectiveness of this in silico model.

A schematic overview of the methods used for the AI-assisted drug repurposing validation, including in silico validation, in vivo, in vitro, and clinical validation through clinical trials

In another study, Lazarczyk et al. [283] developed Adera (v2.0), a deep neural network, to identify candidates for several neuroimmunological diseases, including AD, PD, MS and depression. The model demonstrated strong predictive performance, with a sensitivity of 73.2% (± 11.5%), markedly exceeding the random baseline (~ 6.9%). The model also demonstrated stable predictive performance, with absolute errors of 0.02–0.03 and a loss of approximately 0.0013. In a case study application, the model predicted 10 compounds, of which 2 were identified as biologically relevant upon further validation (20% of validation rate). These findings highlight both the strength of AI-driven candidate prioritization and the substantial attrition that occurs during downstream validation. Nevertheless, in silico validation needs to be followed by experimental and clinical data to support it [283, 284].

In vitro/cell-line models

In vitro validation evaluates AI drug repurposing predictions in biological media, such as enzymes and cell cultures. Aside from efficacy, in vitro models also play an important role in providing information regarding the immunogenicity, proteolysis, and stability in serum of the candidates, which is necessary for evaluating their translational feasibility. In vitro models further branch into 3D cell cultures, organoids, and microfluidic chips such as organ-on-a-chip (OOAC) systems [285–287] (Fig. 7). In neurodegenerative disorders such as AD and PD, most studies use specific neural cell lines, enzyme assays, and primary cultures. Silva Franco et al. [288] tested concentrations of AI-screened ruxolitinib, ranging from 0.1 to 5 µM in C6 glioma and SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells to target ROCK kinases. This repurposing approach demonstrated strong predictive performance, increasing AUC to 0.80 for ROCK1 and 0.75 for ROCK2, with fair discrimination between active and inactive compounds. Out of a total of 500 drugs that were included in this study, 15 candidates were selected for experimental validation. Of the 15 selected candidates, 6 showed excellent inhibitory activity, yielding an overall validation success rate of 40%. Baricitinib and ruxolitinib exhibited significantly high IC₅₀ values in the low nanomolar range (0.007–0.025 µM), revealing their therapeutic potential. To disrupt the aggregation of beta-amyloid (Aβ) peptides in the brain in AD, Xie et al. [230] assessed the effect of computationally derived drug candidates in different concentrations with in vitro synthetic human Aβ42 peptide assays. They screened 454 anti-infective drugs using 148 known amyloid-β inhibitors, selecting 5 for further evaluation. Among these, anidulafungin showed significant potency, exhibiting a validation success rate of 20%. They conducted in vitro assays that showed that 1 µM anidulafungin significantly reduced Aβ oligomer-induced cytotoxicity and restored cell viability from 68.66% to 93.30% (P < 0.01). The nucleation rate of Aβ aggregation was reduced by more than 2-fold, demonstrating the successful application of AI-guided repurposing with biologically validated therapeutics.

In another study, Fang et al. [289] tested pioglitazone in human microglia cells in AD. They developed an AI-based model integrating protein–protein interaction, multi-omics profiles, and genome-wide association study (GWAS) data. A total of 103 risk genes were predicted based on network proximity. Among the identified candidates, pioglitazone showed excellent clinical relevance, with large-scale population validation involving about 7.23 million individuals per year. Its use was associated with a reduced risk of AD (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.916, 95% CI 0.861–0.974, P = 0.005). This effect was further validated by a comparison with glipizide (HR = 0.921, P = 0.0159). With further in vitro experiments, the study concluded that pioglitazone plays a significant role in modulating key neurodegenerative pathways by downregulating GSK3β and CDK5 in human microglial cells.

Animal models

In vivo studies are essential for additional confirmation of drug safety and its therapeutic effectiveness. In vivo studies may include prokaryotic models such as bacteria and yeast, invertebrate models such as nematodes, and mammalian models including rodents and macaques [290]. They provide details on pharmacodynamic responses [291] and bridge the gap between in silico and in vitro studies, allowing their translation into clinical trials [292].

Kim et al. [293] used a coculture assay to evaluate the inhibition of α- synuclein propagation by efavirenz in PD. They developed the AI-driven model for drug repurposing and validation by analyzing drug-gene expression datasets of 166,164 labelled and 984,929 unlabeled drug-response profiles across 1815 already approved compounds. A total of 14 candidate drugs were initially identified, among which nevirapine and efavirenz were experimentally validated through a series of in vivo experiments. After injecting α-synuclein fibrils in transgenic mouse models, efavirenz showed significantly improved pSer129-positive inclusions and pathological α-synuclein propagation (P < 0.05–0.001) across multiple regions in the brain.

Johnston et al. [294] developed an AI framework to identify lorcaserin, ganaxolone, and acamprosate as possible drug candidates to counter the L-DOPA-induced dyskinesia in PD. They analysed 1.3 million PubMed abstracts and ranked 3539 candidate drugs for potential repurposing and validation. The validation analysis revealed significantly high predictive performance (AUC 0.958), with 12 of 15 drugs ranked in the top 2.6%. Model validation demonstrated strong predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.958 and 12 out of 15 known anti-dyskinetic compounds ranked within the top candidates. During screening of the top 5.5% of drugs, 3 candidate compounds were consequently selected for further evaluation, of which lorcaserin showed significant efficacy. It reduced dyskinesia severity by 82% and rotational asymmetry by 58% in in vivo models, demonstrating a validation success rate of approximately 33%. In another study, Guo et al. [237] used a network-based computational repurposing approach to evaluate the effects of cyproheptadine and omaveloxolone in PD. A total of 2453 FDA-approved drugs were screened, of which 176 were selected as potential PD candidates, yielding a selection rate of approximately 7.2%. Further screening and evaluation reduced this number to 8 compounds for experimental validation. Among them, cyproheptadine and omaveloxolone showed significant neuroprotective effects, demonstrating a validation success rate of approximately 25%.

Clinical validation and trial design

Based on the outcomes of in vitro and in vivo studies, a drug may proceed to evaluation through clinical trials, which have three main phases. In the first phase, the drug is tested in a cohort of otherwise healthy volunteers, albeit at a smaller scale [295, 296]. This allows for the assessment of safe dosages and possible side effects. Next, in Phase II, the trial is carried out at a larger scale- this time with patients of the target disease, followed by Phase II, where the outcomes of Phase II are further validated, and more data is collected on long-term use of the drug and its safety [297, 298]. To successfully design and implement clinical trials, factors such as patient demographics, study duration, active comparators, case-controls, and statistical considerations must be carefully considered. Using real-world data and machine learning, Zang et al. [299] implemented a high-throughput clinical trial emulation framework to identify and validate drugs for AD at a population scale. They included 170 million patients from large healthcare databases and generated 430,000 emulated trials over 10 years. The study identified 8 candidate drugs which significantly reduced AD in these trials, with hazard ratios ranging from 0.50 to 0.83 and a 2-year survival improvement of approximately 1.2% to 5.0%. This demonstrates that AI-based trial emulation can provide useful quantitative clinical validation for drug repurposing against neurological disorders.

Marketing needs to ensure appropriate labels and modify packaging to indicate new safety information. As such, regulatory bodies enforce rules and monitor product compliance, even after the drug has been approved [300]. Among successfully repurposed drugs, niclosamide, an anthelmintic, has been identified as a potential candidate for neurological disorders, due to its neuroprotective abilities [301]. Mortberg et al. [302] suggested that most trials are geared toward symptom management, and only a few involve early intervention for pre-symptomatic patients; emphasis is needed for gene-based drug development. A large-scale analysis of 3238 neurodegenerative disease clinical trials revealed important translational limitations, with overall drug success rates remaining low at approximately 8–14%. Notably, only 2.7% of trials included pre-symptomatic individuals, despite the recognized importance of early intervention. Furthermore, only a small fraction (16 targets) of therapeutic hypotheses were supported by human genetic evidence, while the majority (~ 75%) lacked strong biological validation [303].

In addition to computational and experimental validation, many regulatory considerations should be taken into account to successfully implement the repurposed drug in medical practice. The FDA 505(b)(2) pathway is one of them, whereby the sponsor uses information regarding the safety and pharmacokinetics of the substance from other sources, but at the same time provides new proof of efficacy in a different indication [304]. This pathway enables the drug to be developed in significantly shorter time and with fewer resources than with traditional approaches. Yet there may be barriers like intellectual property issues, low commercial value, and regulatory uncertainties [305]. Critical evaluation, failure modes, opportunities and future directions in AI-assisted drug repurposing

From the review of these studies, it can be concluded that AI-driven methodologies have played a substantial role in drug repurposing for neurological disorders; significant gaps exist between AI-based models and clinically validated outcomes. Several promising drugs, including nevirapine, efavirenz, roluperidon, lorcaserin, donepezil, omaveloxolone, and cyproheptadine, have been identified against major neurological disorders, the overall success rate remains low. This gap is further exacerbated by physiological barriers and the complex, heterogeneous nature of neurological disorders. In the following sections, we summarise the systematic evaluation of strengths, failure modes, and future directions in the drug repurposing process for neurological disorders.

Strengths of AI-driven drug repurposing

AI-enabled frameworks have emerged as a crutial approach that addresses several limitations of traditional drug discovery practices. These models have demonstrated strong predictive performance, with external validation accuracies of approximately 81–82% and AUC values of 0.92–0.95 [306]. One of the strengths of these models lies in their ability to analyse large-scale, complex datasets [307]. Given the multifactorial pathophysiology of neurological disorders, including environmental [134], metabolic [308], genetic, and epigenetic factors [309], traditional drug discovery approaches often fail to capture these complex, interconnected factors. Several network-based frameworks, such as graph theory and GNNs, have played a significant role in identifying critical relationships among genes, proteins and molecular pathways [310]. For example, ML models such as XGBoost have shown strong predictive performance, with correlation coefficients of about 0.56 for drug combinations and 0.75 for single drug-disease associations [311]. The integration of AI models with heterogeneous biomedical datasets, such as BindingDB, DrugBank, and PubChem, with 495,000, 115 million, and 2780 approved drugs, respectively, has further enabled the drug repurposing process to be more precise and less time-consuming [312]. Advanced DL-based approaches such as AlphaFold2, which has the capacity ot model nearly 98.5% of the human proteome with high confidence and precision, provide valuable opportunities for mechanistic-based drug repurposing practices [312]. Another important aspect of AI-driven models is the significant advantage they offer in reducing the time and financial demands associated with drug repurposing. Traditional approaches typically required high costs and 10–15 years; however, the emergence of AI-assisted models has reduced this expense to approximately $300 million and the development timeline to several years [313]. These models have enabled researchers to skip early-stage screening, including toxicity and safety testing, thereby accelerating the process and reducing the expense of identifying and repurposing drugs for neurological disorders.

Failure modes beyond current challenges

As mentioned earlier, the advancement of AI-assisted models has been groundbreaking; several quantitative failure modes and limitations persist. These limitations extend beyond general challenges and reflect deeper conceptual and methodological limitations that affect translational impact. One of the major setbacks in using AI-based models for general drug repurposing, and particularly for complex neurological disorders, is the clinical translation. Approximately 14% of drug candidates progress from clinical trials to market for other diseases, and around half of that (7%) for drugs against neurological disorders [306]. Another key challenge is the CNS-related toxicity that contributes to approximately 30% of clinical trial failures. Some of the drugs are even withdrawn due to severe toxicity after even the market approval. Moreover, the electronic medical record (EMR) datasets used in the AI-based models lack significant information: approximately 30% lack dosage information, 25–60% lack organ toxicity biomarker information, and 41% lack concomitant drug use details [306, 314].

The pathophysiology of neurological disorders is multifactorial and complex, and this continues to account for exceptionally high failure rates. Another contributing factor is the BBB, which hinders approximately 100% of large molecules and 98% of small molecules. For instance, from 2002 to 2012, approximately 99.6% of drugs developed for AD failed, and a very low success rates retained during the following years. Moreover, many drugs that showed excellent neuroprotective effects in early-stage models failed in phase III clinical trials due to the complex nature of neurological diseases [315].

There are issues with training robust computational models, especially for rare neurological disorders or lesser-known disease subtypes [316, 317]. Inconsistency in data acquisition procedures and institutional policies also limits their sharing and access for training models, and heterogeneity poses a further challenge during the standardization and processing of large volumes of data [318, 319]. With heterogeneous brain disorders such as AD and PD, it is essential to implement categorization of disorders and identification of their subtypes for personalization of treatment and better health outcomes [320, 321]. For example, in the case of AD, age as a risk factor and other symptom manifestations, such as brain atrophy and its progression, require defined sampling criteria. In turn, the specific inclusion and exclusion criteria may yield distinct distributions in AI models, leading to bias within a cohort [322]. Poor data standardization can thus impair the performance of a computational model [323].

In ML and other AI models, the lack of explanation regarding their inner workings and interpretations of the model outputs can also pose challenges. These blind spots, which mean users cannot access or analyse a model’s decision-making, are known as black boxes [324]. While AI outputs certainly exhibit a degree of precision, the opacity of their internal processes undermines trust in their decision-making [325]. In real-world clinical scenarios, healthcare workers need to understand the reasoning behind an AI model’s suggestions, such as in diagnostics, to avoid ethical complications and risks that may compromise a patient’s wellbeing [326]. To circumvent the issue of black boxes, computer-aided decisions need to be supported by clinical data. Bloch et al. [327] developed a framework to correlate physiologically plausible connections with moderate-to-strong correlations with neurological features in order to classify stages in the progression of AD. Similarly, Hernandez et al. [328] designed a machine learning model with explainability for MS diagnosis by associating scores with the characteristic thinning of the ganglion cell layer, a biomarker, and training it on clinical patient data.

Bias represents a significant concern in AI-based developments for neurological illnesses, as biased frameworks tend to perform poorly when it comes to underrepresented demographics by at least 15–20%. This is undesirable, as it can lead to discrepancies in drug efficiency and increase the risk of side effects [329]. Some strategies, such as federated learning, attempt to mitigate bias through maintaining a decentralized algorithm for training across several organizations, thus ensuring increased diversity in databases and regulating privacy concerns that may increase bias and the reproducibility of results [330]. Other strategies include intercessions such as sampling data and pre-processing it before an algorithmic framework is developed, or through internal data processing with mathematical approaches to promote diverse predictions in a model, and ultimately, post-data training processing It has been observed that models with internally high accuracy of predictions will more often than not fail to perform well when validated through external resources [331].

The use of AI raises questions about data ownership, surveillance, and possible unauthorized use of patient information. When managing sensitive patient records, data security and privacy are of the utmost concern. Their importance requires robust data governance frameworks and secure approaches to facilitate machine learning without direct access to raw data [332]. Raw data may include clinical records, including MRIs, and biological sample analysis, such as those required for the diagnosis and management of MS [333]. One of the major hurdles in the drug repurposing pipeline is the regulation of AI-generated repurposing claims. Generally, computational model-based candidates are adequate for academic publishing, but fall short of regulatory standards [334]. To meet the standards set by regulatory agencies, researchers must work with large-scale, multimodal medical data, which could lead to more dubious approaches, such as clinical gene modification studies [335]. Thus, regulatory frameworks need to legislate regulations for more controversial research and push for greater patient privacy [336, 337]. Furthermore, translating research and its outcomes into clinical practice remains challenging, particularly balancing model accuracy with real-world clinical practice [338, 339].

Opportunities and future directions

The evolution of personalized neurological therapies with AI-based tools has led to a paradigm shift in facilitating healthcare, allowing for the design of multiscale models and tailored medical services that account for the complexity of a patient’s medical history [340]. AI technologies, such as natural language processing systems, DL frameworks, and machine learning algorithms, can be used to identify patterns in patients’ data [341]. Datasets may be built on a patient’s medical history, genetic profile, clinical indications, imaging data, and other health records [342–344]. Combining extensive datasets with AI enables making more precise neuroscience interventions fitted to a patient’s individual circumstances. By identifying trends in these datasets [345], AI tools can recommend drug regimens and predict patient responses to disease susceptibility and potential treatment side effects [346, 347].

An interesting use of AI for predicting patient responses is the concept of Digital Twins, or DTs. A DT is a virtual representation of an object that receives consistent, real-time data updates from sensors [348]. DTs in healthcare incorporate biometric, demographic, and clinical data to build comprehensive patient profiles [349, 350]. Human-based DTs can be considered computer-generated replicas of patients and represent their corresponding physiological features, including tissues and organs, as well as any disease indications. Through modelling interactions between environmental factors and the complex disease multi-omics, DTs enable better monitoring of disease development and its equivalent therapies [351]. DTs have been used for several neurological disorders, such as AD, PD and epilepsy [352–354]. In the future, research should focus on DTs, which can serve as virtual physiological models to determine the most suitable drug regimens and health outcomes.

AI drug repurposing relies on the availability of high-quality data, and the outcomes are defined by the accuracy of the data it is trained on. Thus, it is essential to filter any noise or biases to avoid subpar results [355]. Training datasets may heavily favors positive results, leading to impaired accuracy during the model’s training phase [356]. To address this problem, computational experts may develop techniques for data augmentation or pre-trained neural network models. Meta-learning or few-shot learning is another technique for designing models that extrapolate from limited data sources [357].

While AI drug repurposing has many applications, the databases it depends on may have varying levels of accessibility due to security, privacy and ethical considerations. These concerns, in turn, limit the availability of data and hinder the efficiency of computational techniques. An algorithm to uphold privacy while accessing data is model-driven federated learning (MD-FL). This method facilitates AI training by using model parameters in datasets distributed across multiple devices, rather than relying on a single central machine or cloud server, without extracting the datasets themselves [358, 359].

Several institutions have developed and maintained datasets with varying degrees of access for other researchers, including electronic health records (EHRs). EHRs are an extensive source of patient-focused pathophysiological and longitudinal data, which enables the framework for drug repurposing and validation. EHR datasets provide a realistic viewpoint of clinical care [360]. Data mining can accelerate AI drug repurposing with techniques such as machine learning and natural language processing (NLP), thus parsing unstructured data libraries, extracting the relevant data, and arranging it for analysis. Co-occurrence analysis is another method of data mining, which consists of systems like LION LBD that scrape information from the relevant biomedical literature and arrange the data [361].

Usually, formulating hypotheses for research has been a lengthy and time-consuming process, as it requires the thorough review of literature and input from subject experts in some cases. Generative AI, such as LLMs or Language Learning Models, however, is transforming the way scientists develop novel hypotheses by automating the processes [362]. Collective efforts and initiatives allow for the integration of the joint expertise of scientists, medical establishments, and academic associates to fast-track drug repurposing [363]. This is through the sharing of resources, data, and computational tools. These collective efforts enable the identification of new potential uses for existing FDA-approved medications and promote innovation in AI-based drug repurposing [364].

Conclusion

The current review article discusses selected neurological disorders, including AD, PD, MS, and HD, and outlines the potential of AI in the treatment of these neurological conditions. Studies from extensive biological and chemical structure datasets, non-clinical studies, genomic research, patient information and other sources demonstrate promising results. However, owing to the complex pathophysiology of neurological disorders, there is an extreme scarcity of disease-modifying drugs. Another reason could be attributed to the high cost of developing de novo drugs, followed by years of validation and regulatory approval. Interestingly, most of the existing drugs for neurological disorders, including amantadine, riluzole, valproic acid, propranolol, fingolimod, and citicoline, came through the drug repurposing process. It’s interesting that a drug approved by the FDA for a viral infection could be used for a very different disease, such as neurological disorders. Thus, the next 10 to 20 years may see a surge in the repurposed drugs for several diseases, especially for neurological disorders. AI and computational tools may play a vital role in drug repurposing processes, including screening, target identification, clinical trials, validation, and regulatory affairs. It may also completely change the interface of healthcare by curated databases, biomarker identification, diagnosis, and treatment of neurological disorders.
