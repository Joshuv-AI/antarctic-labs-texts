Determination of α‑Synuclein Protein Interactions by μMap Photoproximity Labeling
Authors: Marshall G. Lougee, Grace S. H. Park, Hee Jong Kim, Jiya Z. Fowler, Zongtao Lin, Hossein Fazelinia, Evan Yanagawa, Vinayak V. Pagar, Tak Ian Chio, Lynn Spruce, Virginia M.-Y. Lee, Benjamin A. Garcia, Melike Lakadamyali, E. James Petersson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fibrillar aggregates of the natively disordered protein α-synuclein (αS) are hallmarks of Parkinson’s disease and related neurodegenerative disorders termed synucleinopathies. Here, we used micromap (μMap) photoproximity labeling to determine the interactomes of αS monomers and fibrils in mouse brain lysate to better understand both the loss of healthy function and gain of toxic function aspects of synucleinopathies. Several αS variants were synthesized and characterized, showing that the small size (1 kDa) of the Ir catalyst attached through a Cys-maleimide linkage makes it minimally perturbing to αS, with a narrow labeling radius that allows one to identify interactome differences between different regions of αS. Monomer and fibril interactomes were compared to each other and to previous proximity labeling data sets for validation, and several examples of further investigations are demonstrated, including Western blotting, affinity pulldowns, fluorescence and super-resolution microscopy, and μMap in primary neurons.

Introduction

Identifying protein interactions is pivotal for a deeper understanding of biological function. A powerful way of ascertaining this information is proximity labeling (PL), which involves fusing a protein of interest (POI) with a labeling system capable of covalently modifying nearby interactors, particularly with affinity purification handles like biotin. These interacting molecules, which are often other proteins, can then be enriched by streptavidin/biotin pulldown and analyzed using mass spectrometry (MS) proteomic workflows. − Numerous technologies exist for achieving this goal that take advantage of a genetically incorporated POI-enzyme fusion utilizing biotin ligase (BioID , /TurboID/miniTurbo − ), ascorbate peroxidase (APEX1 , /APEX2 , ), or a modified light-dependent enzyme (LITag/miniSOG). With each generation of these technologies, an emerging trend has been optimizing the labeling parameters to reduce perturbation to the native system while also highlighting the unique benefits of each PL platform. − In this sense, there is not a single PL technology that should be utilized over all others, but rather, the benefits and drawbacks should be weighed based on the scientific hypothesis to be tested.

An alternative to enzymatic fusion was presented in 2020 by the MacMillan laboratory. This novel PL platform instead utilizes an Ir photocatalyst attached to a POI paired with an accompanying biotin-diazirine (Dz-BTN) probe molecule (Figure A,B). Upon irradiation with blue light, the Ir catalyst can transfer energy to the nearby Dz-BTN, which then extrudes N2, generating a reactive carbene capable of covalently labeling nearby proteins or biomolecules. Due to the strict distance constraints of the energy transfer process (≤10 Å) as well as the very short lifetime of the singlet carbene (∼2 ns), biotinylation is tightly clustered around the interacting area. This technology was termed “micromap” (μMap) due to the unprecedented small labeling (∼3–6 nm theoretical) radius versus other PL platforms and has since shown utility in multiple instances including immune cell profiling, small molecule interactome profiling, SARS-CoV-2 entry mechanisms, e9 glycoprotein interactions, and chromatin interactome dynamics. Furthermore, redshifted variants have been developed using either Sn or Os photocatalyst , as well as a tunable variant using the organic photocatalyst Eosin Y termed “multimap”. Although photocatalytic systems such as μMap have smaller probe sizes (1 kDa) and labeling radii than enzymatic variants, there has thus far been limited exploration of direct protein attachment to truly exploit these features.

Here, we investigated a variant of the μMap system to determine protein–protein interactions for a protein system that could adopt multiple conformations, the small intrinsically disordered protein α-synuclein (αS). The native function of αS is still not entirely clear, and it has been reported to participate in a wide variety of biological processes, with a correspondingly wide array of protein interactions. αS is perhaps best known for its role in Parkinson’s disease (PD), where it forms amyloid-type fibrils that are part of pathological inclusions called Lewy bodies or Lewy neurites. − These inclusions are thought to be both a result of upstream neurodegeneration and a contribution directly to further neuronal stress. Therefore, the loss of healthy αS interactions as well as the gain of pathological αS interactions may contribute to PD. Thus, the protein interactomes of both the monomeric and fibrillar forms of this protein are of great interest.

As a consequence of the interest in its healthy and PD interactomes, αS has been extensively studied in PL experiments. Chung et al. utilized the peroxidase-based APEX2 system to examine the interactome of monomeric αS in both HEK-293 cells and rat cortical neurons, identifying several key interactors including Mapt, Dnaj6C, and Synj1. In 2022, Killinger et al. followed up this initial study with in situ labeling of Lewy body interactors using an αS antibody paired with a secondary antibody fused to a peroxidase. BioID has also been used to assess the αS interactome by Griffin et al., who used differentiated embryonic stem cells paired with an αS-BioID2 fusion to identify the interactome of both monomeric and fibrillar αS. However, μMap offers a unique opportunity to interrogate the positional dependence of the αS interactome and compare the results to those in studies using these other PL technologies.

The Ir catalyst is expected to be less perturbing to protein folding and protein–protein interactions than an ∼20–30 kDa protein fusion (BioID/APEX). This is particularly important since αS is smaller than these proteins (14.5 kDa), and it is known that point mutations and post-translational modifications can have a dramatic impact on αS folding. The theoretical labeling radius of the carbene is also smaller than the activated ester of BioID or the phenoxy radical of APEX, yielding a tighter interactome map. Another appealing characteristic of μMap is that the catalyst can be site-specifically attached to αS at a side chain, not limited to N-terminal or C-terminal fusions of labeling proteins. The combination of variable attachment points and the tight labeling radius could lead to the generation of site-specific interactomes, identifying not only which proteins are interacting but also with what portion of αS they are interacting (Figure A).

Results and Discussion

μMap Probe Development and αS Labeling Optimization

To identify sites for attachment of the Ir catalyst that would not be expected to disrupt fibril formation, we consulted the available cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) and solid-state NMR (ssNMR) structures of αS fibrils made under conditions similar to those used in our laboratory. , We chose locations in the N-terminus (1–60), the NAC-domain (61–95), and the C-terminus (96–140): lysine 12, glutamine 62, phenylalanine 94, glutamate 114, and tyrosine 136. These are sites that are either in the disordered regions of the fibrils or on the exterior faces of the folded core region (Figure A). They are also positions that we have previously determined to tolerate functionalization with fluorescent probes similar in size to the Ir catalyst. −

In several previous μMap studies, researchers utilized a dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO) strain-promoted click reaction to attach their iridium catalyst to an antibody. With this in mind, we postulated that by using a DBCO-functionalized catalyst paired with azido-phenylalanine (AzF) unnatural amino acid mutagenesis, we would be able to incorporate the μMap system site-specifically in αS. As an alternative to this, we could also take advantage of the lack of cysteine residues in αS. By site-specifically mutating to cysteine at various positions, we rationalized that a maleimide functionalized catalyst would also enable site-specific αS functionalization. Several accompanying diazirine probe molecules were synthesized, some of which have not been used in μMap labeling platforms before. These include a tetramethyl rhodamine (TAMRA) diazirine derivative (Dz-TMR) for fluorescent labeling and a desthiobiotin derivative (Dz-DTB) for elution from streptavidin and subsequent cross-linking site identification, as well as the standard biotin derivative (Dz-BTN) for enhanced chemiluminescent (ECL) studies and proteomic pulldown experiments (Figure B).

αS AzF mutants were obtained by introducing the amber stop codon (TAG) in an αS-intein fusion vector for use in a traceless intein-based purification strategy described previously in our lab. Amber suppression was then conducted, and mutants with AzF at phenylalanine 94 (αS-Z94), glutamate 114 (αS-Z114), and tyrosine 136 (αS-Z136) were isolated. Initial functionalization studies were conducted using a TAMRA-DBCO probe to assess the efficiency of the click reaction in AzF mutants. Unfortunately, poor modification efficiency was observed for all mutants with no construct proceeding past ∼50% conversion by matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization mass spectrometry (MALDI-MS) analysis (Figure S1, Supporting Information). This factor, compounded by low expression yields (0.5 mg protein per L culture) from unnatural amino acid mutagenesis, led us to pursue a different strategy in attaching the μMap catalyst to αS. αS contains no native cysteines, so mutation to a Cys residue followed by reaction with a maleimide affords a site-specifically modified protein. Five αS cysteine mutants were expressed and purified using a similar intein-based purification strategy (Figure C), to generate αS-C n , n = 12, 62, 94, 114, or 136 in yields of 2.8–5.8 mg/L of the initial culture.

To assess the modification efficiency, a new variant of the iridium catalyst was synthesized that contained maleimide (Ir-Mal) for conjugation rather than DBCO (Figure C). Initial functionalization studies showed improvement versus the previously attempted DBCO reaction, and rigorous optimization led to a set of conditions resulting in quantitative conversion to iridium-functionalized αS (Figure S2, Supporting Information). Interestingly, decreasing both the tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP) reducing agent and αS concentrations during modification was crucial for optimal conversion to functionalized protein. Since excess TCEP was not removed during the addition of Ir-Mal, it is likely that reaction of TCEP with Ir-Mal limited reactivity with Cys. The unconjugated Ir-Mal photocatalyst was removed via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) purification, and the five Ir-modified αS positional variants, αS-CIr n , n = 12, 62, 94, 114, or 136, were obtained in yields of 0.7–2.0 mg/L of the initial culture (Figure S3, Supporting Information). Successful purification and functionalization were determined by MALDI-MS (Figure S4, Supporting Information), Coomassie gel analysis, and fluorescence analysis (Figure D), as the iridium catalyst itself shows green wavelength fluorescence at 474 nm (Figure E).

To assess the activity of our αS μMap system, a preliminary set of in vitro benchmarking experiments were conducted with monomeric αS-CIr 12 in the presence of Dz-TMR as a measure of fluorescent self-labeling (Figure S5, Supporting Information). Samples containing αS-CIr 12 and Dz-TMR were irradiated with blue light for 30 min with shaking. Rewardingly, all controls without Dz-TMR probe or light showed no TAMRA fluorescence by gel analysis. Boiling of samples prior to gel analysis did not lead to any unanticipated side reactions, and diazirines did not show any more labeling in ambient light versus complete darkness, indicating that aluminum foil and/or excessively careful handling is unnecessary in these types of studies. Upon irradiation, samples containing Dz-TMR showed a TAMRA fluorescence signal on the gel, consistent with the expected self-labeling of the αS-CIr 12 monomer. Iridium fluorescence (green signal) was also observed for all samples and was overlaid with the TAMRA signal. Interestingly, a slight decrease in iridium fluorescence is observed in irradiated samples, which could be due to photobleaching of the catalyst after extended irradiation times (Figure S5, Supporting Information). Because the Dz-TMR molecule allows for direct fluorescence labeling of interacting proteins, this technique bypasses the need for traditional Western blotting of biotinylated interactors. Although the fluorescent probe lacks the sensitivity of an ECL blot, it is a robust way of comparing catalyst activity between Ir-functionalized residues in αS.

Following these self-labeling tests with αS-CIr 12, we performed an initial interactome experiment with all five αS-CIr n constructs. The monomeric proteins were incubated in mouse brain lysate (10 μM, or 0.15 mg/mL in 10 mg/mL lysate, near the physiological αS level of 1% of neuronal protein and generated without detergent to preserve protein interactions), irradiated for 2 min in the presence of 250 μM Dz-TMR, and then subjected to fluorescence gel analysis. As seen in Figure , all five constructs successfully labeled a large number of proteins in the lysates. Significant self-labeling was observed, as seen from the overlay of red TAMRA and green Ir catalyst fluorescence (yellow bands). Interestingly, αS-CIr 136 migrates differently on the gel than the other variants and shows lower levels of self-labeling. From these experiments, constructs αS-CIr 12, αS-CIr 62, and αS-CIr 114 were chosen for further experiments to sample the three regions of αS while providing optimal labeling levels. In particular, among the two NAC region sites, αS-CIr 62 outperforms αS-CIr 94, and among the C-terminal sites, αS-CIr 114 outperforms αS-CIr 136 (Figure ).

Before interactome analysis, we sought to identify labeling events on αS, enabling determination of the μMap labeling radius to inform analysis of interactomes for each Ir catalyst site. μMap labeling was conducted for the three chosen αS constructs, incubated at 10 μM concentrations (determined based on Ir catalyst absorbance at 316 nm) with 250 μM Dz-DTB in mouse brain lysate. The DTB-labeled proteins were then enriched using streptavidin resin, eluted by incubation with biotin, and digested. In gel analysis of the proximity labeling reactions, we observed significant enrichment of specific proteins in the elution lanes versus the flow through and virtually no nonspecific background in unirradiated controls (αS-CIr 12 gel in Figure A and αS-CIr 62 and αS-CIr 114 gels in Figure S6 of the Supporting Information). For each Ir-labeled variant, all of the modified αS peptides were identified using liquid chromatography-tandem MS (LC-MS/MS). The abundances of these peptides were normalized internally and color-mapped onto the sequence of αS to enable visualization of the labeling radius (Figure B).

From these data, a library of covalently modified amino acid residues was assembled for αS-CIr 12, αS-CIr 62, and αS-CIr 114. Next, a conformational ensemble (n = 1000) of αS monomer structures was generated using Fast Floppy Tail in PyRosetta, and a theoretical distance (Cα–Cα) was calculated from the position of the Ir catalyst to each DTB-labeled residue, for each member of the ensemble (Figure C). The average radius was computed from the ensemble distance list to approximate the radius of labeling based on experimental data. Theoretical labeling radii can also be obtained easily using literature values for the reactivity of aryl trifluoromethyl diazirines in bulk solvent (for H2O, k = 3.1 × 108 s–1) paired with known diffusion coefficients of similarly sized small molecules (2.0–9.0 × 10–6 cm2 s–1) and a simple Brownian random walk model. Comparing these models, the computed radii were remarkably close to the theoretical value for diazirine photo-cross-linking (2–5 nm depending on the diffusion coefficient used). Although our models are still within the range of these theoretical calculations, the slight discrepancy in the αS-CIr 114 radius could be due to the lack of tryptic cut sites in the C-terminus, biasing statistics to peptides identified farther away from the iridium labeling position. Since αS is an intrinsically disordered protein, this method probably gives a more accurate estimation of the μMap labeling radius than previously demonstrated examples, which were limited by the size and orientation of the protein to which the iridium catalyst was attached.

While these studies were valuable for determination of the labeling radius, they also revealed several issues that required optimization for full proteomic analysis. Desthiobiotin was used for competitive pulldowns due to its lower affinity for streptavidin compared to biotin, which allowed us to use biotin to elute the labeled peptides for identification by LC-MS/MS. However, subsequent studies using Dz-BTN found it to be a superior probe by providing enhanced sensitivity in chemiluminescent gel assays and MS proteomic experiments. The higher Dz-BTN affinity for streptavidin beads allowed stringent bead wash conditions to minimize nonspecific protein binding and the use of direct on-bead trypsin digest to simplify procedures. Additionally, gel experiments with the Ir catalyst alone revealed significant background labeling, requiring correction using matched control samples (Figure S7, Supporting Information). Finally, before performing full proteomic analysis, we determined that we could form fibrils with Ir-functionalized αS.

Catalyst Attachment Is Stable and Does Not Perturb αS

To test the potential for minimal perturbation from the small μMap catalyst in place of large APEX/BioID fusions, recombinant αS-CIr 12, αS-CIr 62, and αS-CIr 114 monomers were aggregated in vitro to generate preformed fibrils (PFFs) by shaking for 3 days at 37 °C (Figure A). As expected, the Ir-labeled PFFs partitioned into the insoluble pellet after centrifugation and could be visualized by both Coomassie staining and fluorescence gel imaging, with almost no protein remaining in the supernatant (Figure B). Transmission electron tomography (TEM) images validated the fibrillar nature of the αS-CIr n aggregates, with bulk morphologies similar to those of wild-type (WT) αS PFFs formed under the same conditions (αS-CIr 12 PFF TEM in Figure C and WT αS, αS-CIr 62, and αS-CIr 114 PFF TEM in Figure S8). We also used αS-CIr 12 PFFs to seed pathology in neurons and saw potency similar to that of WT PFFs (Figure S33, Supporting Information). These experiments indicate that the chosen Ir catalyst sites are indeed nonperturbing and support their use in interactome studies of the PFFs.

We also tested fibril formation using mixtures of αS-CIr n proteins with WT αS, since Ir-labeled αS could still be useful for proximity labeling and would then be even less likely to perturb protein folding and interactions. TEM images of PFFs made with 10% αS-CIr 12 showed that they also resembled WT αS PFFs and appeared to be homogeneous (Figure S8, Supporting Information). While this is perhaps not surprising since the 100% αS-CIr 12 fibrils were unperturbed, it is important to confirm that the αS-CIr 12 monomers cofibrillize with the WT αS monomers for any future applications with fractional labeling, such as using patient-extracted αS seeds.

It is important to confirm that the labeled αS-CIr constructs are chemically stable and that the PFFs do not shed monomer units over time. To identify any significant retro-Michael exchange between the αS catalyst conjugate and thiols in solution, we performed experiments with β-mercaptoethanol treatment of the αS-CIr n monomer and observed no significant change by MALDI-MS (Figure S9, Supporting Information). Additionally, αS-CIr n (n = 12, 62, and 114) PFFs were incubated with either bovine serum albumin (BSA), a promiscuous cysteine-containing protein, or mouse brain lysate, after which they were fractionated by centrifugation (Figure S10, Supporting Information). No iridium fluorescence was found in the soluble fraction of either the BSA or brain lysate conditions, and in the insoluble fraction, only the αS-Ir band showed iridium fluorescence. Similarly, we observed no evidence for αS-CIr monomer dissociation in these experiments (Figure S10, Supporting Information). Finally, before proceeding to MS experiments, ECL gel analysis tests using streptavidin horse radish peroxidase (STRP-HRP). ECL were performed to confirm that monomers and fibrils had different protein labeling patterns in mouse brain lysate (Figure D). While comparison of intensities of specific bands across conditions is inadvisable since they may not correspond to the same labeled protein, one can clearly see that the patterns are different within each lane, indicating that the interactomes are different. Also, as noted earlier, the Ir catalyst itself clearly generates a background interactome that must be accounted for in MS proteomics.

Conformation-Dependent αS Interactomes

To identify potential interactors with regional and monomer/fibril specificity through MS, αS-CIr 12, αS-CIr 62, and αS-CIr 114 monomers and PFFs were incubated at 10 μM concentration in 10 mg/mL mouse brain lysate and irradiated in the presence of 250 μM Dz-BTN probe for downstream LC-MS/MS analysis with four replicates per condition. All samples were quantified using label-free methods and baseline corrected with respect to identically treated 10 μM Ir catalyst-only control samples before comparison. This baseline subtraction strategy was adopted due to the large amount of labeled protein under the catalyst-only condition.

Analysis of conformation-specific proteins revealed a modest preference for monomer labeling over PFF labeling with 447 monomer-specific hits, 163 fibril-specific hits, and 724 shared hits (Figure A). Over-representation analysis (ORA) was used to identify pathways in the condition-enriched hits. Monomer-enriched proteins preferentially involved proteins in the cytoskeleton, vesicle transport, cell adhesion, and synaptic organization across all regions (Figure A and Figure S15 of the Supporting Information). These cellular networks have been previously implicated in αS trafficking and intracellular fate. − One protein, S100β, was identified as a high-confidence monomer-enriched hit. S100β is a calcium-binding protein with implications for synaptic transmission. Previous studies have implicated high concentrations of S100β as a biomarker for a myriad of different neurodegenerative diseases, and inhibition of astrocytic production of S100β has been shown to improve amyloid-β deposits with implication in AD. To validate its selective preference for labeling by the αS-Ir monomer, mouse brain lysate (5 mg/mL) was irradiated with 10 μM αS-CIr 12 and 500 μM Dz-BTN for both monomer and PFF constructs. Labeled lysate proteins were subsequently enriched using streptavidin magnetic beads, separated on a gel, and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane for Western blotting. Consistent with our μMap MS data, S100β was selectively enriched by the αS-Ir monomer but not fibril (Figure S26, Supporting Information). Prior to our studies, S100β has not been known to interact with αS. However, S100β is upregulated in the PD brain tissue, potentially modulating dopaminergic neuron function and neuroinflammation.

In contrast, αS PFFs μMap hits were preferentially enriched in ribosome subunit proteins and some ubiquitin-proteosome proteins (Figure A and Figure S15, Supporting Information). While one may be concerned that these proteins are simply being labeled due to their abundance, we note that they have been previously implicated to interact with αS. The inhibition of the 26S proteasomal complex in particular has been shown to decrease the clearance of intracellular αS aggregates seeded by recombinant PFFs. Another study suggests that αS PFFs are inhibitory substrates, in which binding to the proteosome and/or sequestering of other proteasomal substrates creates a positive feedback mechanism leading to increased cytoplasmic αS aggregates. Regarding the ribosomal hits, a recent MS study of proteomic differences in PD patient tissue samples identified ribosomal biogenesis as an important area of difference between healthy control, PD, and Alzheimer’s disease patient samples. ORA of high-confidence fibril hit proteins suggests enrichment in ribosomal metabolism and translation at the synapse. Camk2a, a key protein involved in synaptic plasticity in glutamatergic neurons, was slightly enriched with PFFs (Figure A). To assess this result, 2.5 μM Camk2a with an N-terminal His-tag was incubated with either αS monomer or PFF (10 μM protein concentration). Proteins were captured using Ni-NTA agarose beads, washed with low imidazole buffer, and eluted off the beads with high imidazole buffer. Comparison of the monomer and fibril elution bands shows a modest preference toward Camk2a-αS PFF binding compared to the monomer, consistent with μMap MS data (Figure B).

Another key fibril-enriched group was metabolic proteins involved in fatty acid hydrolysis (Fabp7, Iah1) and synthesis (Acaca). While αS has been linked to the dysregulation of lipid composition in disease states, these specific proteins have not been previously identified as αS fibril interactors. , To validate one of these hits, 5 μM recombinant Acaca with an N-terminal His-tag was incubated with either a 10 μM αS WT monomer or PFF. A portion of Acaca was captured using Ni-NTA agarose beads, after which the protein was washed and eluted off the beads using high imidazole buffer. Only αS WT PFFs, but not monomers, coeluted with the His-tagged Acaca, suggesting preferential binding to the fibril conformation (Figure B). Collectively, these experiments support the monomer/fibril conformation selectivity of our μMap experiments. Tables of monomer, fibril, and shared hits are provided in the Supporting Information.

Site-Dependent αS Interactome

For positional level analysis of interactions, we again quantified hit proteins using label-free methods and benchmarked them using catalyst-only control samples. As with the previous conformation-dependent results, labeling was higher for the αS-Ir monomer across all three labeling sites, although bulk proteomic labeling was maintained across all samples (Figures S11–S14, Supporting Information). The top five hits in each condition are reported in (Figure C). Heatmaps showing relative enrichment or depletion in each condition were generated for each catalyst site for both monomers and fibrils (Figure C). Sections of both the monomeric and fibril heatmaps show regions of higher enrichment based on the labeling site as well as shared proteins between regions. Conditional analysis also reveals regional enrichment patterns. Volcano plots showing enrichment of hits for each construct in monomer and PFF forms, corresponding ORA analysis, and Venn diagrams quantifying condition-enriched hits are found in Figures S16–S25 of the Supporting Information.

αS-CIr 12 and αS-CIr 62 monomers showed the greatest number of shared hits, further supporting the overlapping radii between the two labeled regions (Figure C). One such shared N-terminal/NAC protein, glutamate dehydrogenase 1 (Glud1), is strongly enriched by both αS-CIr 12 and αS-CIr 62. Glud1 is a highly localized mitochondrial enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of glutamate to α-ketoglutarate but is a novel hit in αS-interactome studies. In addition to being a high-confidence protein of interest, Glud1 has broad implications in the neurodegenerative disease due to its role in mitochondrial metabolism, as its dysregulation has been shown to be correlated with numerous neurodegenerative disorders.

S100β was identified as a high-confidence monomer hit. Site-dependent analyses suggest that its interaction is primarily with the N-terminus (Figure C and Figure S19 and Table S1, Supporting Information). To validate the regional-dependency of S100β-αS interaction, purified human S100β-His-tag protein (hS100β) was incubated with full-length WT αS or truncated αS constructs spanning the N-terminus (αS1–68) or NAC to the C-terminus (αS56–140). It was found that the αS1–68 fragment binds and coelutes with hS100β similarly to full-length αS and twofold better than αS56–140 (Figure D). These experiments support the regional specificity of our interactome data.

All three PFF regions have relatively distinct enrichment profiles. The N-terminus, for example, seems to preferentially enrich for E2 ligase proteins including Ube2l3 and Ube2r2 as well as 20S proteasomal proteins Psma2, Psma7, and Psmb5 (Figure C and Table S1, Supporting Information). This is consistent with the distribution of lysine residues in the protein, some of which are known as ubiquitination sites. Lower labeling by the fibril forms can again be seen in the enrichment analysis. While there may be bulk solubility issues involved, we believe that this result reflects higher interactome selectivity due to the folded nature of fibrils compared to disordered monomers. These and other hits that are unique to PFFs versus monomers and LBs may provide insight into the fibril maturation process. Tables of enrichment hits for each Ir catalyst position for monomer and fibril conditions are provided in the Supporting Information.

Comparison to Previous Interactome Studies

To further validate and contextualize our results, we compared our μMap PL proteomic data set to previous PL studies, such as APEX-based PL studies of the αS monomer conducted in rat cortical neurons (Figure S28, Supporting Information). APEX hits were BLAST-sequence aligned to find the top scoring mouse homologues and cross-referenced to the μMap-enriched proteome. We found that 78 of the 171 APEX monomer hits were shared by our μMap data set with major overlapping hits and pathways including endosomal maturation and trafficking proteins, cytoskeletal proteins, and RNA processing, splicing, and translation proteins.

In addition, PFFs represent a transitionary structure between monomers and pathological LBs, which include many other proteins and organelles. As such, we expect that probing PFF interactomes will provide additional insight into the molecular mechanisms of disease progression and propagation. With this in mind, we compared LB APEX PL hits derived from PD and Lewy body disease (LBD) brain tissue to our αS-Ir PFF μMap interactomes. We verified that 115 of 558 LB APEX hits were also found in our analysis (Figure S28, Supporting Information). Shared hits between both studies included proteins involved in bioenergetics, particularly mitochondrial metabolism and lysosomal proteins.

Both the monomer and fibril μMap data sets thus had significant similarity to previous data sets but also novel pathways. This is not surprising given the differences in the experiments. For example, some of our PFF hits, such as ribosomal and proteasomal components, may be excluded from mature LBs but bind to newly formed PFFs in our study. Interestingly, the region-dependent and monomer-dependent proteins Glud1 and S100β were not found in the APEX monomer study but were found in the APEX LB study. While this seems counterintuitive, it may imply that changes in these interactions are related to αS pathology.

Validation in Hippocampal Neurons with Endogenous αS

While an exhaustive validation of every hit from our monomer and PFF interactomes is not feasible in this initial report, we wished to demonstrate several physiological mechanisms for further analysis based on the hits discussed above. One limitation of our in vitro μMap experiments is the loss of information about the native subcellular localization and compartmentalization. To address this point and to validate the physiological significance of select hit proteins, we used multiple microscopy strategies showing αS interactions in the relevant cell type, i.e., neurons. Primary mouse hippocampal neurons were grown until 7 days in vitro, after which they were fixed and probed for both hit protein and endogenous αS via immunofluorescence (Figure A). For these analyses, Glud1, S100β, and synapsin 1 (Syn1, Figure A) were chosen as representative abundant, high-confidence proteins. In addition, S100β was chosen as a target due to its primary expression in astrocytes with low levels of synuclein expression, while Glud1 was chosen for its localization to mitochondria, and Syn1 was chosen because it is a known αS interactor that localizes to synaptic termini, , a very different subcellular location than Glud1. Finally, we investigated Camk2 colocalization since our pulldown experiments revealed that it had an affinity for αS monomers despite being a fibril μMap hit (Figure B).

Fluorescence intensity analysis following microscopy imaging shows a high correlation between αS and S100β signals within the cell body of neurons, despite S100β’s primary localization to astrocytes (Figure A). For Glud1, the fluorescence intensity correlation with endogenous αS shows similar results (Figure B). We note that S100β and particularly Glud1 are highly abundant and standard fluorescence microscopy is limited in its ability to resolve colocalization of such abundant proteins. To overcome this limitation, dual-color DNA point accumulation for imaging in nanoscale technology (DNA-PAINT) was employed as a super-resolution imaging method (20–30 nm resolution) to visualize and quantify localization overlap between Glud1 and endogenous αS in hippocampal neurons. A similar cell culture protocol was performed, followed by DNA-PAINT immunostaining (Figure C). Quantitative analysis was then used to calculate the percentage of αS/Glud1 localization within the cell body. The Glud1 signal was found to primarily localize to mitochondrial substructures, as expected, and 18% of all mitochondrial structures labeled with Glud1 were found to overlap with endogenous αS (additional fields of view used in analysis shown in Figure S29 of the Supporting Information). Fluorescence microscopy experiments to image Camk2 and Syn1 colocalization with αS are shown in Figures S30 and S31 of the Supporting Information. In spite of being relatively weakly enriched in μMap analysis (Figure A), our pulldown assays showed Camk2 to interact with both monomers and PFFs, and microscopy shows strong correlation of Camk2 with endogenous αS. Syn1 colocalization is variable, as would be expected since not all αS is found at synaptic termini. Collectively, these microscopy analyses demonstrate how one can verify μMap hits for high abundance proteins with a disordered and promiscuous interactor such as αS, which is highly abundant itself.

μMap Interactome Labeling in Hippocampal Neurons

To further increase the physiological relevance of our studies, we have carried out proximity labeling in the primary mouse hippocampal neurons, which we have previously shown to uptake exogenous fluorescently labeled αS. , Monomers or fibrils of each αS-CIr n construct were incubated with the cells overnight, then 250 μM Dz-BTN was added, and the cells were irradiated for 2 min (shorter irradiation times used to preserve cell health). The cells were then fixed and imaged with AlexaFluor555-conjugated streptavidin (STRP-AF555) to visualize Dz-BTN, as well as antibodies to endosomal marker LAMP1 and microtubule-associated protein MAP2.

We observed both successful intracellular uptake of Ir-functionalized αS and photoinduced labeling via Dz-BTN for both monomers and PFFs of all three αS-CIr n constructs (αS-CIr 12 in Figure ; αS-CIr 62 and αS-CIr 114 in Figure S30 of the Supporting Information). Neurons that were treated without αS-Ir (WT or no αS treatment) or Dz-BTN probe did not show significant photolabeling. We observed clear differences between monomer and fibril treatments. The interactome of αS PFFs was seen to be distinctly puncta-like with colocalization to the late endosomal marker LAMP1, consistent with other work implicating the endosomal pathway in αS PFF internalization. The fluorescent signal also was dependent on the concentration of the Dz-BTN probe more so than the amount of αS-CIr n , consistent with its catalytic role (Figures S31 and S32, Supporting Information). These results suggest the compatibility of μMap labeling within the complexity of the intracellular environment and act as a proof-of-concept toward future μMap PL studies in a physiological neuron environment.

Conclusions

μMap stands out among PL methods for its tight labeling radius (∼5 nM) and small (∼1 kDa) bioorthogonally incorporable PL motif, which allows for site-specific conjugation. However, μMap is still a new method, and relatively few laboratories have yet adopted it. We sought to integrate the μMap platform in our own PL studies and demonstrate the ease with which this transformative technology can be incorporated into the experiments in chemical biology. Thus, we developed a cysteine-reactive version of the μMap iridium catalyst and optimized labeling on our model protein αS. Despite a tight theoretical labeling radius, experimental validation often has been limited by the orientation of the antibody-μMap platform. By incorporating the photocatalyst directly on αS and using our Dz-DTB probe, we were able to experimentally obtain a labeling radius remarkably close to the theoretical value (2–4 nm). Next, by incorporating the catalyst at different positions on the αS monomer and fibril, we identified proteins that preferentially interact with the three different regions of both PFF and monomeric αS in a total mouse brain homogenate. Protein–protein interactions were identified whose novelty relative to previous αS PL experiments is likely a direct consequence of the site-specific incorporation and small size of the μMap photocatalyst. We have performed a set of exemplary experiments to demonstrate how interactome hits can be pursued in more depth and will continue to pursue additional hits in this fashion for subsequent reports. Finally, we were able to demonstrate labeling in live primary hippocampal neurons. While preliminary experiments indicate that additional optimization will be required to perform MS proteomics on neuronal μMap samples, these studies serve as proof of principle of this system and help show how it can be incorporated into chemical biology workflows in the future.
