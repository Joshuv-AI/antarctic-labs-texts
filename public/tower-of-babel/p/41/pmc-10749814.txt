Association of Myelin Oligodendrocyte Glycoprotein Antibody-associated Disease with Coronavirus Disease 2019 Vaccination and Infection: A Case Report of Cortical Encephalitis and Transverse Myelitis Relapse
Authors: Yohei Himeno, Takahisa Tateishi, Ken-Ichi Irie, Shuntaro Ueno, Makoto Morimitsu, Shino Mizoguchi, Tamon Koga, Takayuki Taniwaki
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe herein report a case of myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) antibody-associated disease (MOGAD) that occurred following coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccination and its subsequent relapse after COVID-19 infection. A 34-year-old woman developed cortical encephalitis in the right temporoparietal lobe one week after receiving the mRNA-1273 vaccine. The cerebrospinal fluid was positive for anti-MOG antibody. Her symptoms gradually improved after three courses of intravenous methylprednisolone therapy. Six months later, she experienced a relapse of transverse myelitis following COVID-19 infection. Despite treatment with plasma exchange, the patient remained paralyzed in both lower limbs. We herein review the relationship between MOGAD and COVID-19 vaccination/infection. Keywords: anti MOG antibody, MOG antibody associated disorder, COVID-19 vaccination, cortical encephalitis, COVID-19 infection, transverse myelitis

Introduction

Myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) antibodies are associated with a range of clinical manifestations, collectively known as MOG antibody-associated disease (MOGAD), including optic neuritis (ON), acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM), transverse myelitis (TM) (1), and even unilateral cerebral cortical encephalitis (2). Previous studies have reported cases of MOGAD attacks following vaccination against tetanus, measles, Japanese encephalitis, and rubella (3). Reports of central nervous system inflammatory diseases, including MOGAD, following coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccination, have also been published (4). In addition, COVID-19 has been linked to the onset and recurrence of MOGAD (5,6).

We herein report a case of cortical encephalitis with anti-MOG antibody occurring one week after mRNA-1273 vaccination for COVID-19, followed by a relapse of transverse thoracic myelitis after COVID-19 infection. We also investigated the association between MOGAD and COVID-19 vaccination/infection through a literature review.

Case Report

A 34-year-old woman experienced a right-side headache, fever, memory loss, and left upper extremity weakness 1 week after receiving the third dose of the COVID-19 vaccine (mRNA-1273). The patient had no history of infection and had not received any other type of vaccination within the past few years. Her symptoms gradually worsened, prompting her to consult a physician.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain revealed cortical T2-weighted hyperintensity in the right temporal lobe. The following day, the patient was admitted to our department for a diagnosis and treatment. Upon admission, she complained of a right temporal headache, short-term memory impairment, and prosopagnosia. A neurological examination revealed muscle weakness in the left upper and lower extremities; paresthesia on the left side of the face and upper and lower limbs; and neck stiffness. The left knee jerk and Wartenberg reflexes were hyperreflexive, but no pathological reflexes were present. The headache was persistent and moderate in intensity. Jolt accentuation was noted; however, no other signs of meningeal irritation, such as neck stiffness or Kernig's sign, were observed.

Laboratory test results, including hematological and biochemical analyses, were normal. Autoantibody testing for antinuclear, anti-SS-A, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic, antithyroid peroxidase, anti-glutamic acid decarboxylase, anti-aquaporin 4 antibodies, hepatitis B virus antigen, anti-hepatitis C virus antibody, and anti-human T-cell leukemia virus type I/II antibodies were all negative. A cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed pleocytosis of 113 /μL (97% mononuclear cells), slightly elevated protein levels (63 mg/dL), normal glucose levels, and a normal immunoglobulin G index. Oligoclonal bands were not observed. MOG IgG was evaluated in the CSF because stored serum was not available, and a positive reaction was obtained using the live cell-based assay method. Brain MRI demonstrated hyperintense cortical lesions in the left and right temporal and parietal lobes on T2-weighted imaging, with no gadolinium enhancement. Arterial spin-labeling perfusion showed asymmetric hyperperfusion of the right cerebral hemisphere (Figure A-L).

Head magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed at the initial onset (A-L), and thoracic spinal MRI was performed at the time of recurrence (M-O). A-L images were obtained on the same day and are presented in alignment. Gray matter hyperintensities were observed in the right temporal and parietal lobes on fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (A-C) and T2-weighted (D-F) images. Leptomeningeal enhancement was observed on gadolinium-enhanced MRI T1-weighted images (G-I). Arterial spin-labeling perfusion revealed asymmetric hyperperfusion of the right cerebral hemisphere (J-L). In addition, spinal MRI revealed a T2-high-signal lesion extending to the single-vertebral level at the Th9 thoracic level (M: sagittal, N, O: axial, both Th9 levels). Contrast-enhanced spinal MRI was not available.

We administered three courses of steroid pulse therapy with methylprednisolone (1,000 mg/day; duration 3 days) on days 1, 8, and 15 after admission, following a diagnosis of autoimmune encephalitis. After the third course of steroid pulse therapy, the patient experienced gradual improvement in her fever, headache, and neurological symptoms. Follow-up brain MRI showed improvement of the lesion. Single-photon emission computed tomography revealed a decreased accumulation in the right temporal and parietal lobes. A neurological examination on day 20 of admission showed normal findings, except for mild visual impairment; therefore, the patient was discharged.

She was started on oral prednisolone (30 mg/day) after discharge. We measured serum anti-MOG antibodies one month after the onset, as it is not possible to exclude non-MOGAD diseases, such as multiple sclerosis, by measuring CSF anti-MOG antibodies alone. Measurements using the live cell-based assay method yielded positive results (7). Therefore, we diagnosed this case as MOGAD based on the presence of cortical encephalitis, a high titer of serum MOG antibodies, and the absence of findings suggestive of other diseases, which aligned with the diagnostic criteria for MOGAD proposed by the international MOGAD panel (8).

Three months later, when the prednisolone dosage was tapered to 5 mg/day, the patient developed an abnormal sensation in both limbs. Four months later, the patient experienced a gait disturbance. Both symptoms improved with pulse steroid therapy. Six months after the initial attack, she was diagnosed with COVID-19 infection based on the results of polymerase chain reaction testing using a nasal swab. Within four days of the infection, she began experiencing subacute worsening of muscle weakness and sensory disturbance in both lower extremities. She was admitted to a different hospital one week after the onset of symptoms, where she received one course of steroid pulse therapy; however, the symptoms did not improve. Subsequently, she was transferred to our hospital nine days after the initial onset.

Laboratory test results, including hematological and biochemical analyses, were normal. CSF tests revealed a normal cell count and protein level. Spinal MRI revealed a T2 high-signal lesion extending to a single vertebral level at the Th9 level in the thoracic spinal cord, without any enhancing lesions (Figure M-O). Based on the subacute course and imaging findings, the patient was diagnosed with recurrent anti-MOG antibody-associated thoracic TM.

We administered four courses of plasma exchange on days 4, 10, 18, and 32, along with one course of steroid pulse therapy on day 5. However, paralysis of both lower extremities continued to progress despite the initiation of plasma exchange. Following steroid pulse therapy, prednisolone (20 mg/day) and tacrolimus (3 mg/day) were administered. Despite our efforts, the moderate paralysis of the lower extremities persisted after treatment. The unfavorable prognosis was possibly due to the limited inflammatory findings observed at the time of transfer, suggesting that the peak stage of inflammation had already passed.

Discussion

We encountered a case of anti-MOG antibody-associated cortical encephalitis that developed one week after the patient received a third dose of the COVID-19 vaccine. Although we administered steroid pulse treatment and oral steroids, the patient experienced a relapse of thoracic TM four days after contracting COVID-19. We treated her with steroid pulse therapy, plasma exchange, and oral steroids; however, she still had a moderate residual disability.

Optimal maintenance therapy for patients with MOGAD remains a topic of debate given that approximately 50% of patients experience a single episode and do not require long-term immunosuppression (9,10). Currently, it is challenging to predict whether or not a patient with initial MOGAD will experience relapse. Typically, long-term immunotherapy is initiated after the second MOGAD attack (11). However, in this case, despite receiving immunotherapy after the first attack, the patient experienced a relapse, indicating a high disease activity state of MOGAD. General maintenance therapies included oral steroids, azathioprine, and mycophenolate mofetil. In this case, the NUDT15 gene polymorphism test revealed that the patient had the Cys/Cys genotype, which is found in approximately 1% of Japanese patients and is associated with a significantly elevated risk of severe side effects, such as profound leukopenia and complete hair loss. Consequently, tacrolimus 3 mg was added to the treatment regimen, along with prednisolone (20 mg), following the second relapse.

Recent studies have reported an association between MOGAD development and COVID-19 vaccination. A literature search was conducted in the PubMed database using the search terms “vaccination,” “SARS-CoV-2,” “anti-covid vaccination,” in combination with the terms “MOG,” “Myelin Oligodendrocyte Glycoprotein,” “MOGAD,” for the period between October 2020 to April 2023. Initially, 55 titles were identified from the PubMed database. We included case reports and systematic reviews that specifically focused on the new onset of MOGAD after COVID-19 vaccination, while excluding other articles. In a total of 17 case reports and 1 systematic review, 53 cases were documented in patients who developed symptoms within 1-42 days of vaccination (12-29). The ages of the patients ranged from 15 to 68 years old, with no sexual predilection. Among these patients, 80% (32/39) developed symptoms after the first dose, 17.5% (7/39) after the second dose, and only 1 after the third dose. The most common symptoms were TM (27/53, 51%), often in combination with ADEM-like brain/brainstem lesions (20/53, 38%), and ON (17/53, 32%). Only one case of cortical encephalitis has been previously reported. As vaccines, ChAdOx1n was used in 87% (46/53) of cases, BNT162 CoV-19 in 7% (3/53), and mRNA-1273 and BBIBP-CorV in 1 case each. Cases positive for anti-aquaporin-4 (AQP4) antibody have also been reported following vaccination. A similar literature search was conducted for “AQP4” and “aquaporin 4.” We identified 34 relevant articles in the PubMed database, including 9 case reports and 1 systematic review focusing on the new onset of anti-AQP4 antibody-associated diseases after COVID-19 vaccination. It was observed that MOGAD cases tended to be reported more frequently; however, both MOGAD- and AQP4-related diseases are rare and pose challenges for a direct comparison.

In the present case, the patient developed symptoms one week after receiving the third dose of the mRNA-1273 vaccine. To our knowledge, this is the first case report of MOGAD following a third dose of a COVID-19 vaccine. Notably, our patient developed cortical encephalitis, rather than typical TM or ADEM-like lesions, which have been more commonly reported in previous cases. This suggests that MOGAD following COVID-19 vaccination may have a broader spectrum of clinical manifestations than X the typical MOGAD cases not associated with COVID-19.

The mechanism by which COVID-19 vaccination induces MOGAD remains unknown, and several hypotheses have been proposed. One possibility is that there may be molecular mimicry between the vaccine and the MOG or other self-antigens, which can trigger an autoimmune response in susceptible individuals. Another possibility is that the vaccine may activate nonspecific immune responses, leading to MOGAD in genetically predisposed individuals. It has also been suggested that COVID-19 vaccines employ TLR7 or TLR9 agonists as adjuvants. Stimulation of TLR7 and TLR9 elicits elevated levels of type I interferon, which upregulates interferon-stimulated genes. Both TLR7 and TLR9 play roles in the production and amplification of autoreactive immune responses (30), which could potentially contribute to the development of MOGAD after vaccination. Another possibility is that the patient may have had subclinical MOGAD that relapsed after vaccination, as some patients with MOGAD may experience relapse following COVID-19 vaccination (31).

In our case, MOGAD recurred following COVID-19 infection. We were unable to find any reports demonstrating a correlation between COVID-19 infection and the recurrence rate in patients with MOGAD. However, the number of newly diagnosed MOGAD cases between 2019 and 2021, when the COVID-19 pandemic began, was higher than the pre-pandemic levels (32). In addition, a previous study indicated an elevation in serum anti-neural antibodies, including MOG antibodies, after COVID-19 (33). Thus, a possible relationship between COVID-19 and MOGAD is suggested. TLR2, TLR3, TLR4, TLR6, TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 may be important factors in COVID-19 (34). If the vaccine activates TLR7 and TLR9, which can potentially induce MOGAD, it is possible that COVID-19 infection could reactivate these receptors and trigger MOGAD recurrence. However, a limitation of this theory is that it cannot be objectively proven, as we did not measure the TLR values in our case. Further analyses of the immune response in the central nervous system after COVID-19 vaccination and infection are needed.

In conclusion, vaccination has been widely implemented in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. Although post-vaccine MOGAD is very rare and does not necessarily indicate vaccination as the cause, vaccine-induced neuroinflammatory disease may recur following infection, as observed in our case. Clinicians should be aware that MOGAD following COVID-19 vaccination can manifest with atypical clinical features, such as cortical encephalitis, and a prompt diagnosis and treatment are crucial for improving patient outcomes. Further studies are essential for a better understanding of the pathogenesis of MOGAD following COVID-19 vaccination, and to develop effective prevention and treatment strategies.
