Effectiveness of a mindful eating program among Brazilian healthcare workers: a mixed-methods pragmatic randomized controlled trial protocol
Authors: A Bottoni, FG Magalhães, CAM Guimarães, ALS Spínola, JAN Oliveira, VLMA Salvo, AR Noto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness of a Mindful Eating for Health Program among Brazilian healthcare workers, compared to a Food and Nutrition Education Program and a Control Group.MethodsThis mixed-methods pragmatic randomized controlled trial will enroll 270 healthcare workers aged 20–59 years in Brazil from October 2023 to November 2026. Participants will be randomized into three groups: Mindful Eating for Health Program, Food and Nutrition Education Program, or Control Group. Interventions comprise 8 weekly online sessions. Quantitative data including anthropometric measurements (waist circumference, body mass index) and psychometric assessments (eating behavior, mindfulness, anxiety, depression, self-care, self-compassion, perceived stress) will be collected at baseline, post-intervention, and at 3, 6, and 12-month follow-ups. Qualitative data including interviews and field observations will be gathered from participants at 3 and 12 months post-intervention. Statistical analyses will include paired t-tests, repeated measures analysis of variance, structural equation modeling for mediation analysis, and generalized estimating equations. Qualitative data will undergo content analysis using Bardin's method.ResultsNot applicable for this protocol study.ConclusionsThis study expects to confirm the effectiveness of the Mindful Eating for Health Program in improving eating behaviors and well-being among Brazilian healthcare workers.Keywords: Mindfulness, Mindful eating, Healthcare workers, Eating behavior, Dietary guidelines, Mixed methods

Introduction

Healthcare workers face demanding routines, long shifts, and unpredictable schedules that disrupt their biological rhythms, often dealing with complex patient needs (Alqahtani and Alhazmi, 2025; Baek et al., 2024; Hassanein et al., 2025). This environment, coupled with pressure to make quick and accurate decisions and exposure to suffering, leads to emotional exhaustion, anxiety, depression, and burnout (Baek et al., 2024). Such stressors can impact eating habits, contributing to overweight and obesity through emotional and binge eating, with higher Body Mass Index (BMI) linked to these patterns (Alqahtani and Alhazmi, 2025). Professionals over 30 and nurses show increased obesity risk, potentially influenced by age-related metabolic changes and work-related stress, which limits time for physical activity and healthy meals. Inadequate dietary patterns worsen physical and mental health, creating a vicious cycle that affects overall well-being, quality of care, productivity, and motivation to encourage healthy habits among patients (Hassanein et al., 2025). Mindfulness-Based Interventions (MBIs), like Mindful Eating, offer promising alternatives to enhance healthcare workers' well-being (Baek et al., 2024; Ong et al., 2024; Choi and Lee, 2019; Falconiere et al., 2025; Han et al., 2024; İnözü and Köse, 2023).

Mindful Eating transforms the relationship with food by fostering awareness of hunger/fullness, acknowledging emotional factors, and cultivating an accepting and non-judgmental stance towards eating habits (Choi and Lee, 2019; İnözü and Köse, 2023).

This approach involves intentional attention to the eating experience, free from distractions or judgments (Han et al., 2024). Self-awareness (Han et al., 2024) helps professionals recognize emotional eating triggers (e.g., stress, anxiety, boredom) (İnözü and Köse, 2023), enabling development of coping mechanisms (Falconiere et al., 2025). Mindfulness also promotes reconnecting with the pleasure of eating, savoring food, and recognizing satiety signals (Han et al., 2024), leading to more conscious and balanced food choices (Alqahtani and Alhazmi, 2025; Choi and Lee, 2019). MBIs contribute to short-term reductions in anxiety, burnout, and stress (Ong et al., 2024), and promote self-compassion and empathy, essential professional skills.

Mindful Eating correlates with greater mental well-being and reduced occupational stress (Choi and Lee, 2019), essential for quality of life and professional performance (Alqahtani and Alhazmi, 2025). This contributes to higher job satisfaction, a positive work environment, and improved patient safety (Choi and Lee, 2019). Mindfulness programs aid stress coping, facilitating clinical practice and promoting calm, focus, and acceptance of limitations (Falconiere et al., 2025), increasing resilience and fostering a balanced work environment. İnözü and Köse (2023) highlight Mindful Eating's effectiveness over nutritional education and traditional diets in combating emotional eating, promoting a healthier relationship with food based on needs and pleasure, not just emotional relief.

Despite favorable literature, Ong et al. (2024) highlight the need for rigorous studies with extended follow-up to confirm long-term sustainability and deepen understanding of intervention impact. Research in other settings (Hassanein et al., 2025) suggests mindfulness programs reduce stress and enhance well-being in healthcare staff, but further research is needed for Brazilian professionals (Falconiere et al., 2025). Studies in Latin America, especially Brazil, are scarce, requiring more objective measures and psychometric scales to understand underlying mechanisms (Katterman et al., 2014). Most research focuses on the American Mindfulness-Based Eating Awareness Training (MB-EAT) protocol. However, in 2019, the Mindful Eating for Health Program (MEHP) was proposed as the first Brazilian protocol (Salvo et al., 2019). MEHP integrates mindfulness, mindful eating, compassion, and psychoeducational activities in eight weekly online or face-to-face sessions. Unlike standard MBIs, self-compassion is integrated from the first session, a relevant difference as it is proposed as a mechanism of change in MBIs (Golden et al., 2021). MEHP was developed for the Brazilian context, considering sociocultural differences and validated program approaches. This protocol aims to help participants develop inner wisdom for physiological needs, identify hunger types, understand food's role, comprehend emotional states and eating, make conscious food choices, improve mindfulness during eating, reduce automatic eating, and establish a caring, respectful relationship with body and food (Salvo et al., 2019). Therefore, MEHP is the object of this proposed study.

Background and objectives

Addressing the urgent need to promote health and combat obesity among healthcare workers, a group with heightened illness susceptibility post-COVID-19, this study aligns with the Ministry of Health's policy emphasizing healthy eating and obesity prevention. Given rising obesity prevalence and limited conventional intervention effectiveness, exploring innovative approaches like MEHP is crucial for sustainable, long-term benefits in eating behavior. This study uniquely evaluates MEHP against other Brazilian dietary guideline-based interventions, making it original research. Its design and target audience (270 participants across nutritional statuses and sexes) contribute significantly to mindfulness and mindful eating literature.

The study also addresses research gaps with an extended 12-month follow-up (assessments at 3, 6, and 12 months) to determine result sustainability. This extended period addresses prior study limitations and enhances understanding of long-term health promotion and eating behavior change strategies.

The main objective of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of the MEHP in comparison to the Food and Nutrition Education Program (FNEP) and Control Group (CTG) among Brazilian healthcare workers. The study will also seek to: i) evaluate the impacts of the MEHP in comparison to the FNEP and CTG on waist circumference and BMI, and eating behavior, mindfulness, anxiety, depression, self-care, self-compassion, and perceived stress, based on assessments conducted at baseline, immediately following the intervention, and at 3, 6, and 12-months post-intervention; ii) understand the difficulties and motivations related to maintaining the practices outlined in the MEHP; and iii) investigate participants' perceptions of positive post-intervention (at 3 and 12 months) life changes that go beyond eating-related aspects; iv) investigate adherence to both programs (MEHP and FNEP); and v) determine whether self-compassion mediates the adherence to both programs (MEHP and FNEP); and v) determine whether self-compassion mediates the effects of the MEHP on eating behaviors among Brazilian healthcare workers.

Methods

Study design and population

We will conduct a mixed-method pragmatic randomized controlled trial (RCT) with Brazilian healthcare workers. The quantitative component will follow the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines (Moher et al., 2010), including the CONSORT 2010 statement checklist and extensions for pragmatic trials (Zwarenstein et al., 2008). The qualitative component will use individual interviews and field observations, incorporating the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist (Tong et al., 2007), translated and validated for use in Portuguese (Souza et al., 2021).

The minimum sample size was 240 participants (16% effect size, 5% significance, 80% power), increased by 10% (264) for anticipated losses. To improve allocation and considering program availability, the final sample size will be 270 participants. Effect size was based on previous MB-EAT intervention studies (Kristeller et al., 2014; Salvo et al., 2022), applying parsimony. Sample size was calculated using G*Power v. 3.1.9.7 (Faul et al., 2009).

Inclusion criteria

Adults aged 20–59 years, both sexes, BMI ≥ 8.5 kg/m2 and <40 kg/m2, divided into 3 groups: G1 – Control Group (CTG); G2 – MEHP - Mindful Eating for Health Program; G3 – FNEP - Food and Nutrition Education Program. The inclusion criteria were restricted to adults aged 20–59 years. This age range aligns with the World Health Organization's classification of adults for anthropometric assessment and BMI interpretation. The lower limit of 20 years corresponds to the World Health Organization (WHO) definition of adulthood, while the upper limit of 59 years excludes older adults (≥60 years), who are classified as a distinct population group with different anthropometric standards according to WHO guidelines (World Health Organization, 1995).

Exclusion criteria

Adults undergoing obesity pharmacotherapy; pregnant women; untreated hypothyroidism/hyperthyroidism (TSH < 0.2 or >5.5 mlU/mL) or treatment <6 months; acute depression (<6 months), diagnosed schizophrenia/psychotic disorders, or using medications causing cognitive/attentional impairments (e.g., potent anxiolytics); mindfulness/meditation/yoga/similar practice ≥once/week in last 6 months; bariatric surgery; extreme obesity (BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2); participation in other interventional studies; chronic kidney disease, heart failure, or conditions affecting fluid balance; nutritional guidance in last 6 months; anorexia nervosa.

The Mindful Eating Research Group will recruit participants via institutional emails (BCC) for an introductory video conference explaining objectives. Those agreeing will sign an Informed Consent Form, and a baseline assessment will follow.

Participants will be randomly assigned to one of three homogeneous groups (G1, G2, or G3) using a computer-based simple randomization procedure by a statistics specialist. The random allocation sequence, generated in R software (v.4.0.1) with a fixed random seed (set.seed(123)) for reproducibility, programmatically shuffled the eligible participants' dataset, saving randomized lists in password-protected Excel files (.xlsx).

To preserve allocation concealment, the sequence was generated prior to intervention and stored securely. The research team remained blinded to allocation. Participants were sequentially assigned to groups (CTG, MEHP, or FNEP) per the pre-randomized list, maintaining concealment until assignment (Fig. 1).

In both programs, weekly sessions covering the themes and content presented in Table 1 will be held via Zoom. The first weekly session will last 2 h30min and subsequent sessions will last 2 h. The FNEP is based on the Dietary Guidelines for the Brazilian Population (Brasil, 2014).

Ethical Aspects. The study adheres to National Health Council Resolution 466/2012 and was approved by the Universidade Federal de São Paulo's research ethics committee (reference N° 7.322.419). All participants will sign an informed consent form. The project is registered with the Brazilian Clinical Trials Registry (RBR-23h6k87). Research data will be anonymized, and participants can withdraw at any time.

The MEHP will be conducted by nutritionists who are members of the research team and have completed formal training in the MEHP protocol. This training comprises five modules with a minimum duration of one year and includes individual mentorship of one hour for every two sessions, conducted by the protocol developer. The FNEP will be conducted by contracted nutritionists who are trained and supervised by the research team. This approach was adopted to ensure specialized human resources for the MEHP protocol, which requires specific expertise available within the research team. Given that FNEP is implemented simultaneously with MEHP, external professionals were recruited to meet the demands of the active control condition and to minimize potential bias. All facilitators will complete a standardized training program on their respective protocol to ensure protocol fidelity and consistency across all program sessions.

Several strategies will be implemented to maximize participant adherence and engagement throughout the intervention period. First, participants will receive reminders via email and SMS before each session to reduce forgetfulness and increase attendance. Second, a dedicated website will be created containing all research content, including text-based information, explanatory videos, and registration links to facilitate participant recruitment and access to program materials. Third, for each group formed, WhatsApp groups will be created to share session-specific information, distribute didactic materials, assign weekly tasks, send reminders, and foster peer exchange to stimulate motivation and engagement among participants. The WhatsApp groups will also enable participants to communicate anticipated absences in advance, allowing facilitators to reallocate participants to alternative groups occurring in the same week. Fourth, facilitators will employ interactive teaching methods, including group discussions and practical demonstrations, to enhance engagement and create a sense of community among participants. These combined strategies aim to minimize dropout rates and ensure consistent engagement throughout the intervention period.

Measures

Anthropometric (waist circumference, BMI) and psychometric (eating behavior, mindfulness, anxiety, depression, self-care, self-compassion, perceived stress, behavior change stage) assessments will occur at baseline, post-8-week interventions (MEHP/FNEP), and at 3, 6, 12 months post-intervention (Fig. 1). Qualitative data will involve online individual Zoom interviews with MEHP completers at 3 and 12 months post-intervention and program dropouts (MEHP/FNEP), invited via WhatsApp/email. Sociodemographic and screening data, including alcohol-related problems (Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test – AUDIT-C, short version) (Meneses-Gaya et al., 2010), will be collected via Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) after Informed Consent Form signing and before randomization. Anthropometric and psychometric assessments will be conducted only with participants meeting inclusion criteria. Body mass, height, and waist circumference will be self-reported using a research team guidance video. Psychometric assessments will use scales validated for Brazil at baseline, post-intervention (3, 6, 12 months), via REDCap.

Sociodemographic and screening questionnaire

Personal data (address, education, marital status) and screening (body mass, height, BMI, inclusion criteria) will be collected via questionnaires.

Anthropometric measurements

Body mass, height, and waist circumference will be assessed. Nutritional status will be classified by WHO BMI cutoff points (WHO, 1995), and waist circumference by WHO reference values (WHO, 2000).

Eating behavior

Eating behavior will be assessed using the 21-item Three-Factor Eating Questionnaire (TFEQ-R21 short version), validated for Brazil (Natacci and Ferreira, 2011), assessing cognitive restraint, emotional eating, and uncontrolled eating.

Mindfulness

Mindfulness will be assessed using the Philadelphia Mindfulness Scale (PHLMS), validated for Brazil (Silveira et al., 2012). This 20-item scale has two dimensions: “Awareness” (monitoring internal/external experiences) and “Acceptance” (non-judgmental/open attitude).

Anxiety and depression

Measured using anxiety and depression subscales of the 21-item Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21, short version), adapted and validated for Brazil (Vignola and Tucci, 2014).

Self-compassion

Self-compassion will be tested using the Self-Compassion Scale Short Form (SCS-SF) (Raes et al., 2011), 12 items from the original version. The scale was validated for use in Portuguese (Rocha et al., 2022) based on an adaptation of the long version for Brazilian populations. It has six dimensions: self-kindness, mindfulness, common humanity, self-judgment, isolation, and over-identification (Rocha et al., 2022).

Self-care

The Brazilian version of the 15-item Appraisal of Self-Care Agency Scale – Revised (ASAS-R) (Damásio and Koller, 2013) will be used.

Perceived stress

The Brazilian version of the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (BPSS-10), translated and validated by Reis et al. (2010) will be applied.

Stage of behavior change

The Brazilian version of the University of Rhode Island Change Assessment (URICA) (Bittencourt et al., 2012) will be used.

Individual interviews are a widely used qualitative research method for gathering detailed information on research objects and relevant topics (Minayo, 2014, p.261). They offer advantages such as obtaining in-depth information on human behavior and flexibility, allowing adaptation of questions to various objectives. This method aids exploratory studies for deeper insights, hypothesis generation, and instrument construction. Qualitative data will be collected via semi-structured individual interviews with MEHP completers and program dropouts (MEHP/FNEP), using closed and open-ended questions for discussion beyond pre-determined topics (Minayo, 2014, p.261–262). Interview questions will be pre-tested to refine discussion topics; the guide may be modified for significant unforeseen topics (Minayo, 2014, p.192). Interviews will be conducted by trained qualitative researchers, lasting 20–30 min.

Field Observations will be conducted by researchers who will record personal impressions, informal conversations, behavioral observations contradicting statements, and interviewee statements in a field diary (Minayo, 2014, p.295). These data, including notes on discrepancies between reported and observed behaviors, enhance research authenticity. In this study, information and observations related to the research theme not recorded in interviews will be noted in a field diary from the intervention phase onwards. All relevant records will be included in data analysis.

Interview guide and thematic axes for semi-structured interviews will be guided by a set of open-ended questions organized around two distinct participant groups. For participants who discontinue the MEHP (dropouts), the interview guide will focus on: reasons for program discontinuation and perceived barriers to adherence. For participants who complete the MEHP, the interview guide will explore: strategies used to maintain learned practices post-intervention, new insights gained about eating behaviors and mindfulness, perceived changes in eating behaviors and mindfulness, and broader impacts of the program on other life domains such as sleep, mood, anxiety, social relationships, and overall well-being. These thematic axes will allow for in-depth exploration of both facilitators and barriers to program success.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive data will be analyzed using simple/relative frequencies for qualitative/categorical variables and central tendency/dispersion measures for quantitative variables, depending on distribution. Graphical representations may also be used. For quantitative variable comparisons: paired t-test (or non-parametric equivalent) for baseline/post-intervention; repeated measures Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) or Friedman test for 3, 6, 12-month follow-up. For categorical variables: McNemar's test for baseline/post-intervention/follow-up. Between three groups: ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test for quantitative variables; chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Mediation analysis will assess indirect effects (via self-compassion) of MEHP on eating behaviors. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) analysis will test the mediation hypothesis using process MACRO or lavaan and R. Normality of quantitative variables will be assessed via graphical analysis (histograms, Q–Q Plots). Generalized Estimating Equations will determine treatment type and time effects. Multiple imputation will address missing data. Data analysis will follow the Intention-to-Treat principle, including all participants regardless of allocation. A 5% significance level will be adopted. Questionnaire data will be inputted into REDCap, then exported to R4.2.1 for statistical analysis.

Qualitative data will be analyzed using content analysis, a technique describing message content via systematic, objective procedures to infer knowledge from production conditions (Bardin, 1977).

This allows researchers to make replicable, valid inferences from contextual data using specialized scientific procedures (Minayo, 2014). Individual interviews will be recorded and transcribed, then inputted into NVivo software for coding and categorization. Analysis will follow Bardin's (1977) steps: pre-analysis (skim reading, defining comparable categorization units for thematic analysis/coding); detailed exploration (coding, decomposition, enumeration); data treatment and interpretation. Codes will be iteratively refined through systematic comparison and researcher reflexivity.

Qualitative data analysis will be triangulated by research group peers to maximize reliability.

This study adopts a mixed-methods design following the Good Reporting of a Mixed Methods Study guidelines (O'Cathain et al., 2008), ensuring transparency in the rationale, integration, and interpretation of quantitative and qualitative components. The quantitative strand RCT evaluates the effectiveness of the MEHP, FNEP, and control conditions using anthropometric and psychometric outcomes, while the qualitative strand explores participants' experiences, perceived changes, adherence, and barriers through semi-structured interviews and field observations. The two strands address complementary aspects of the same research problem and will be integrated during interpretation to enable meta-inferences that combine numerical results and experiential perspectives. Integration will occur through triangulation, comparison of convergent and divergent findings, and use of qualitative insights to contextualize and explain quantitative trends over time.

Discussion

Diet has a direct impact on quality of life and well-being and is a key element of public health. Mindfulness contributes significantly to a conscious relationship with food. Despite its relevance, few studies focus on populations in developing countries, which comprise a significant global portion (Baek et al., 2024; Ong et al., 2024; Choi and Lee, 2019; Falconiere et al., 2025; Han et al., 2024; İnözü and Köse, 2023; Salvo et al., 2022).

This study protocol proposes a mixed-method RCT to evaluate MEHP effectiveness among Brazilian healthcare workers, addressing a literature gap. Unique to this study is the MEHP's unprecedented evaluation, comparing it to both a no-intervention control and an active control (FNEP), developed by the research group with compatible format in terms of duration and intensity. The research design and target audience, distinct from previously studied contexts, also contribute significantly to mindfulness and mindful eating literature.

Furthermore, self-compassion is incorporated into the MEHP from the first session, a potentially important component for promoting physical and mental health. This is supported by Rahimi-Ardabili et al. (2018), which found promising results from self-compassion interventions in improving eating habits, supporting weight loss, and addressing psychological factors. Similarly, Palmeira et al. (2017) reported reductions in BMI and maladaptive eating behaviors in women through an intervention intervention combining mindfulness acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), and compassion. Thus, considering self-compassion's potential in physical and mental health and eating behavior change, MEHP's early integration of it, and the absence of experimental studies on its mediating effect, this work will be the first to explore self-compassion's mediating effect on eating behavior change among healthcare workers.

As a primary outcome, both programs are expected to improve participants' eating behavior (primary outcome). As secondary outcomes, MEHP participants are also expected to show higher mindfulness, self-compassion, self-care, better nutritional status, and lower anxiety, depression, and stress than CTG and FNEP groups. Broader positive life changes are also anticipated for MEHP participants.

Despite MBIs' potential, this study has limitations, including difficulty engaging healthcare workers due to workload and program duration/format, and challenges in 12-month follow-up (psychometric scales, interviews, anthropometric assessment).

This protocol could stimulate further research on mindful eating and behavior for health promotion in developing countries. Its results will provide important inputs that can inform actions to promote self-care among healthcare workers.
