Developing lifesaving skills in children: a simulation-based randomized controlled trial in schools
Authors: Pedro Aguiar Molinari, Giulia Pistor Galimberti, Antonio Cipriani Gomes da Silva, Fernando de Avila Teixeira, João Pedro Grasseli Engel, Pedro Henrique Oliveira Tietz, Pedro Ubirajara Gratieri Marca, Marina Pistor Galimberti, Marcelo Sabedotti, Thyago Anzolin Coser

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Basic life support; BLS, resuscitation training; School-based intervention; Simulation; Emergency education

Introduction

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) represents a major global health burden, with incidence ranging from 30 to 100 cases per 100,000 inhabitants per year and survival to hospital discharge rarely exceeding 15 %.1 In Brazil, recent alarming data indicate about 720 cardiac arrests per day, with survival estimated at less than 10 %.2, 3 Notably, the chance of survival decreases by approximately 5.5 % for every minute without cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR).4 Although bystander CPR markedly improves survival, its frequency varies widely across regions, remaining critically low in many settings.1

Leading resuscitation councils worldwide, including the American Heart Association (AHA),5 the European Resuscitation Council (ERC),6 and the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR),7 recommend widespread Basic Life Support (BLS) training with emphasis on compression-only CPR and simplified recognition of cardiac arrest. High-quality chest compressions, with adequate depth, rate, full recoil and minimal interruptions, maintain cerebral perfusion, improve survival and lead to favorable neurological outcomes.8, 9, 10

Compression-only CPR programs, centered exclusively on chest compressions, increase delivery of appropriate compressions and superior skill retention when compared to conventional CPR programs.11, 12 Furthermore, real-time feedback devices during training improve compression quality over training without such tools.13

Schools are strategic for early CPR training, and although evidence suggests these interventions improve willingness and preparedness to respond to cardiac emergencies, their effectiveness within this population has yet to be adequately characterized.14 In Brazil, research has explored the feasibility of introducing CPR education in the school curriculum, however initiatives have focused primarily on theoretical instruction, with limited evaluation of skills or long-term outcomes.15, 16

Furthermore, retention of CPR skills has been shown to decay within months, and prior studies frequently adopt a 6-month interval as a cutoff to evaluate long-term retention.17, 18 To ensure a robust assessment of this retention, the Little Anne QCPR® manikin provides an objective overall CPR performance score that integrates compression rate, depth, and recoil, offering a reliable, accurate, and reproducible evaluation of compression quality, whereas subjective human assessment tends to overestimate performance.19

So, to address these gaps and to reinforce international efforts toward the global integration of resuscitation training in school curricula, we aimed to evaluate whether a standardized school-based BLS training led by medical students can improve and sustain CPR knowledge and skills among middle school students, supporting the broader integration of CPR education into school curricula worldwide.

Methods

This study was reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2025 guidelines.20

Study design

This was a simulation-based parallel-group superiority randomized controlled trial with blinded outcome assessors, using a 1:1 allocation ratio.

Participants and randomization

The study was conducted between May and November 2024, encompassing recruitment, intervention, and follow-up assessments. Students aged 11 to 16 years from six schools (two private, two municipal, and two state-run) in Caxias do Sul, Brazil, were assessed for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were enrollment in grades 6–9, parental/guardian consent, and student assent. Exclusion criteria included previous formal BLS training, physical or cognitive limitations preventing participation, or absence on the day of the intervention.

Eligible participants were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the Intervention Group (IG), which received a theoretical-practical BLS session, or the Control Group (CG), which received no intervention. Randomization was conducted using computer-generated simple randomization with RStudio 2023.12.1+402.pro1 to ensure equal allocation between groups. Allocation concealment was maintained with sealed, opaque envelopes prepared by a researcher not involved in recruitment or assessment. Outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation for the entire study and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this study.

Interventions

The instructors were medical students previously trained by faculty members who are board-certified specialists in emergency medicine and cardiology, with extensive clinical experience in acute care, BLS and ATLS teaching and formal academic roles in medical education. Under their supervision, medical students were deemed qualified to teach BLS classes. Training was conducted in a single standardized session per school and was delivered simultaneously to all participants in the Intervention Group at each institution, regardless of age, grade level, or prior knowledge. Each class was divided into three phases: administration of a pre-class questionnaire (see Appendix A), theoretical instruction, and supervised hands-on practice in BLS.

The questionnaire, developed by the authors based on the AHA guidelines,9 consisted of 10 multiple-choice questions on cardiopulmonary arrest and BLS. It was completed individually by participants before the class began. The answer key (see Appendix B) was not disclosed to participants at any time during the study to avoid bias. This was followed by a didactic lecture using slides, covering first aid, recognition of cardiac arrest, activation of emergency services, and the performance of high-quality chest compressions.

The practical phase was conducted using Little Anne QCPR® manikins, during which each participant performed two one-minute cycles of chest compressions, monitored in real time by the equipment’s software. Immediate individualized feedback was provided by the instructors. The total duration of each session was 1 h and 50 min, divided into 10 min for the initial questionnaire, 20 min of theoretical instruction, and 1 h and 20 min of supervised practice.

Data collection and outcomes

The instant learning of participants in the Intervention Group regarding BLS were assessed immediately after the theoretical-practical training through the reapplication of the same questionnaire, completed within 10 min. Knowing the timeframe in which CPR knowledge and skills typically decline, a comprehensive evaluation was conducted six months after the intervention with both the Intervention and Control Groups at each school to compare theoretical and practical performances between them.

During the practical assessment, participants were randomly called into a testing room where they encountered a simulated scenario involving an unconscious victim and an unlocked mobile phone placed nearby. Fig. 1 illustrates the simulated environment used during the assessment. Participants received the following standardized instruction:

Simulated environment used in the practical evaluation.

“An elderly man was walking in downtown Caxias do Sul and collapsed unconscious in the middle of the street. You are the first person to find him in this state. You are alone, and there is an unlocked cell phone on the ground next to you. You do not know what happened prior to the incident. You will have 2 min to act in the way you consider appropriate. During this time, you must narrate all your actions aloud to the evaluators.”

The verbal narration allowed assessors to objectively track and score each step of the participant’s actions. The evaluation included three essential BLS steps: checking for responsiveness, activating emergency services (dialing 192), and performing chest compressions. If a participant began compressions, they were encouraged to continue for one minute, even if it exceeded the time limit, to allow for later comparative analysis.

A blinded evaluator recorded whether each step was performed correctly, while the Little Anne QCPR® manikin software analyzed participants who performed chest compressions, providing separate measurements of compression rate, depth, and an overall CPR performance score, thereby offering a comprehensive, quantitative, and objective assessment of practical skills.

After the practical assessment, participants exited the room and completed the questionnaire for the theoretical evaluation. Fig. 2 illustrates the complete methodology.

Schematic diagram of the methodology: Over a 2-h period, the Intervention Group received a questionnaire on Basic Life Support, followed by a theoretical-practical BLS class, and then completed the same questionnaire immediately after the lesson. Six months later, both the Intervention and Control Groups underwent a theoretical-practical assessment, consisting of a simulated cardiac arrest scenario and the administration of the questionnaire at the end.

The primary outcome of the study was the comparison of scores (0–100) obtained through the manikin software between the Intervention Group and the Control Group six months after the training. Secondary outcomes included: the score on the multiple choice theoretical questionnaire, verification of the victim’s responsiveness, activation of emergency services (dialing 192), performance of chest compressions, correct execution of the three fundamental steps of BLS, as well as the depth and rate of compressions. The progression of the Intervention Groups’s theoretical performance was also assessed at three time points: before the intervention, immediately after the training, and six months later.

Statistical analysis

The sample size calculation considered the total population of middle school students (6th to 9th grades) in Caxias do Sul (23,309 students, according to municipal data) as the target population. Based on the expected difference of 20 points in the primary outcome (overall CPR performance score), assuming α = 0.05 (two-sided) and power = 95 %, a minimum of 98 subjects per group was required. To account for an estimated 15 % dropout rate and considering the feasibility of conducting the intervention with approximately 20 students per teaching session, the final sample size was projected at 240 participants. Analyses were conducted by an analyst blinded to group allocation including participants who completed all study stages. This constituted the Intention-to-Treat Complete Case (ITT-CC) population and formed the basis for the analysis of secondary outcomes. The primary outcome (CPR practical score) and compression rate and depth were first analyzed using a Per-Protocol Complete Case (PP-CC) approach, including only participants who performed chest compressions. Additionally, a Intention-to-Treat Complete Case (ITT-CC) analysis for CPR score was conducted, assigning a factual score of 0 to non-performers. There was no cross-over between groups.

Descriptive statistics are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and counts (percentages) for categorical variables. Data were compared between groups using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the two-tailed Student’s t-test for continuous variables. Levels of theoretical knowledge and performance in BLS among Intervention Group students were compared at three time points: before the theoretical class, immediately after the class, and six months later, using the paired t-test. Theoretical and practical knowledge levels between Intervention Group students (after six months) and Control Group students were compared using the independent t-test.

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests and found to be within acceptable parameters. Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi 2.4.11 and RStudio 2023.12.1+402.pro1 software. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No adjustments for multiple testing were performed.

At the 6-month follow-up, 11 participants from the Intervention Group (9.5 %) and 23 from the Control group (19.8 %) were lost to follow-up or withdrew. A total of 198 students completed the study (Intervention: 105; Control: 93), of whom 131 (Intervention: 94; Control: 37) performed chest compressions and were included in the PP-CC analysis of the compression metrics.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first simulation-based randomized controlled trial with blinded outcome assessors to demonstrate that a single theoretical-practical BLS intervention delivered by medical students significantly improves both knowledge and practical CPR skills in middle school students, with sustained retention at six months. The use of a control group strengthens the internal validity of these findings, supporting a causal relationship between the intervention and the observed outcomes. By addressing a critical gap in literature regarding high-quality, prospective controlled trials,22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27 these results constitute a major step forward in providing robust evidence that even single-session, school-based BLS trainings can be effective in resource-limited settings.

The immediate theoretical improvement across all grades observed in our study aligns with prior evidence from heterogenous BLS training methods, including online instruction, grade-level comparisons, and defibrillator use in high school students.28, 29, 30, 31 However, our model demonstrated sustained theoretical retention in children six months after a single session, an enduring benefit that adds to the established short-term gains reported in the literature.32

Students in the intervention group demonstrated significantly better practical performance, particularly in achieving adequate compression depth and rate, as well as initiating key steps of the chain of survival essential for high-quality CPR.33 A higher proportion of students in the Intervention Group initiated chest compressions, who not only performed them more often, but also did so more effectively than the Control Group, as evidenced by the markedly higher overall compression score measured by the manikin’s software, the primary outcome of this study. Moreover, the ITT-CC analysis confirmed these results, reinforcing the robustness of the intervention’s efficacy and effectiveness. However, both groups had difficulty checking responsiveness, a step frequently overlooked in simulations. This highlights the need to reinforce this component in future training sessions, especially through instructor-guided and hands-on approaches, as supported by recent evidence,34 as well as potentially using a stepwise progression in which students only advance to calling emergency services and performing compressions after correctly recognizing the victim. The control group’s poor performance, particularly regarding practical AHA quality targets,5 underscores the severe consequences of lacking structured training. This data not only reinforces school-based training recommendations from WHO,35 but also provides critical baseline values for future comparisons in this age group.

Taken together, our results highlight the feasibility and impact of low-cost, time-efficient BLS training programs in schools. Expanding such initiatives and assessing reinforcement strategies over time should be a public health priority, providing school-based opportunities for individual development and fostering foundational principles of health promotion and emergency preparedness, while reaching populations who might otherwise lack access to CPR training.

Limitations

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. CPR performance was assessed on a manikin, which may not fully reflect real-life outcomes, and the manikin-derived CPR score is not directly related to patient survival. The knowledge questionnaire, although reviewed by faculty experts, has not undergone external validation. Follow-up was limited to six months, leaving longer-term retention unknown. Performance may also have been influenced by the assessment setting as participants knew they were being evaluated and were required to narrate their actions aloud, introducing a potential Hawthorne effect. Hand-placement during compressions was not measured, which could have influenced CPR quality. However, these factors likely affected both groups similarly and do not undermine the primary findings. Finally, the study was conducted in a single region of Brazil, which may limit generalizability, although inclusion of both public and private schools increases sample diversity, partially mitigating this concern.

Future research should explore longer-term retention, assess performance in real-life scenarios, the relation of school training and patient outcomes and examine reinforcement strategies over time, ideally in multicenter studies to strengthen external validity. Moreover, our methodology could serve as a foundation for studies aiming to test large-scale or even nationwide implementation of school-based resuscitation training programs.

Conclusions

In this randomized clinical trial, a single theoretical-practical BLS intervention delivered by medical students significantly improved long-term knowledge and practical CPR skills in middle school students. Six months after the intervention, the intervention group outperformed the control group in key steps of high-quality resuscitation.
