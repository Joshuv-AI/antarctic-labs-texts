Kyasanur Forest Disease: feasible strategies for vaccine development and evaluation
Authors: Manoj V Murhekar, Jeromie Wesley Vivian Thangaraj, Deepak Y Patil, Gudadappa S Kasabi, Nivedita Gupta, Pragya Yadav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryKyasanur Forest Disease (KFD) is a tick-borne zoonotic infection endemic to certain parts of India, with expanding geographic distribution and recurrent focal outbreaks among forest-dependent populations in Western Ghats. The discontinuation of the formalin-inactivated KFD vaccine in 2022 has created a critical gap in prevention. New vaccine candidates are under development, but conventional Phase 3 efficacy trials are difficult to implement because of low incidence of disease, and clustered and unpredictable nature of outbreaks. KFD vaccine evaluation may require alternate regulatory approaches. An initial step would involve studies to identify candidate correlates of protection in laboratory confirmed KFD patients. These immune markers could then be evaluated in bonnet macaques challenge models to assess their association with protection against infection and clinical disease and define protective immune thresholds or composite immunological endpoints. A phase 3 immunobridging study could subsequently assess seroresponse rates against predefined immune thresholds and provide supportive evidence for vaccine efficacy and regulatory decision-making. Post-licensure studies can assess real world vaccine effectiveness and help refine vaccination strategies. Vaccine deployment should prioritise high-risk populations and align with seasonal transmission patterns of KFD. This framework may provide a feasible pathway for KFD vaccine deployment in endemic regions.Keywords: India, Kyasanur Forest Disease, Vaccine development, Regulatory pathway, Vaccine effectiveness

Introduction

Kyasanur Forest Disease (KFD), an emerging zoonotic infection, is caused by KFD virus, a member of the Flaviviridae family, and is transmitted to humans through the bite of infected ticks. KFD was first recognised during an outbreak of febrile illness with haemorrhagic manifestations in Shimoga district of Karnataka in 1957.1 For several decades, KFD remained largely confined to four districts of Karnataka, causing sporadic cases and focal outbreaks,2 especially among people with regular exposure to forest environment. However, since 2006, KFD has expanded geographically and has been reported from several districts of Karnataka as well as neighbouring states including Kerala, Maharashtra, Goa and Tamil Nadu.3 Annually, 400–500 individuals are reported to be affected by KFD, with outbreaks occurring in localised areas, and a case fatality ratio of 2–12%.4 Conventional control measures such as application of tick repellents, use of protective clothing, acaricide application in forest areas are challenging to implement consistently, making vaccination an important preventive strategy.

A formalin-inactivated KFD vaccine developed by the ICMR National Institute of Virology, Pune wasused for the prevention of KFD in four endemic districts of Karnataka.5 Two doses of the vaccine were administered to individuals between 7 and 65 years of age at an interval of one month, with booster doses recommended within 6–9 months after primary vaccination and repeated for five consecutive years after the last confirmed KFD case in the area.6,7 A field evaluation conducted during 1990–1992 reported vaccine effectiveness of 82.2% (95% CI: 69.8–89.6) after one dose and 94.5% (95% CI: 89.7–97.1) after two doses.6 However, subsequent studies showed lower effectiveness, with 62.4% (95% CI: 26.1–80.8) among individuals who received one dose and 82.9% (95% CI: 71.3–89.8) among those who received two doses followed by a booster dose, compared to unvaccinated individuals.7 In addition, vaccine coverage remained low because of the need for multiple booster doses and concerns regarding adverse events following immunisation.8,9 In October 2022, the Public Health Department of Karnataka discontinued the use of the earlier KFD vaccine due to non-compliance with good manufacturing practices, and low potency of the vaccine.10

A few KFD vaccine candidates are currently under development and evaluation. Two live attenuated Vesicular Stomatitis Virus–Ebola (VSV-EBOV) vectored vaccines, incorporating either full-length or truncated EBOV glycoprotein to express the precursor membrane (prM) and envelope (E) proteins of KFDV, were shown to protect mice against lethal KFD virus challenge.11 A single intramuscular dose of VSV vector expressing the full-length EBOV GP and the KFDV prM and E proteins vaccine was found to be safe and elicited strong humoral and cellular immune responses in pigtailed macaques.12 Despite these promising results, the candidate did not progress to clinical trials due to concerns related to the presence of EBOV proteins in the vaccine.

The whole virion BPL inactivated KFD vaccine developed by Indian Immunologicals Limited, Hyderabad was found to be safe and immunogenic in small animal studies. Two doses of the vaccine elicited a strong IgG response and elevated neutralizing antibody as well as T cell cytokines responses in Balb/c mice model and provided complete protection against KFDV challenge.13 Following approval from the Central Drugs Standard Control Organization (CDSCO), a first-in-human randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase I study has been initiated in healthy Indian adults aged 18–49 years.14

Challenges in conducting efficacy trials for KFD vaccine

With new KFD vaccine candidates entering clinical evaluation, it is timely to define an evidence pathway for regulatory approval and programmatic deployment. The traditional pathway for vaccine licensure includes Phase 1 (safety and immunogenicity), Phase 2 (immunogenicity, safety, dose ranging, and preliminary efficacy), Phase 3 (definitive efficacy, safety, and lot-to-lot consistency leading to regulatory approval), and Phase 4 (post-marketing safety and effectiveness) studies.15 However, conducting individual randomised Phase 3 trials for KFD vaccines could be challenging. For other vector-borne diseases such as dengue, large individually randomised clinical trials were feasible because of predictable transmission patterns and higher disease incidence. In contrast, the incidence of KFD is low. The disease generally occurs as outbreaks which are geographically clustered and unpredictable in nature. The populations at risk are dispersed. Hence, an individual randomised efficacy trial would require very large sample sizes and extended follow-up periods, which may not be operationally or financially feasible.

Alternative trial designs could be considered. For example, the efficacy of Ebola vaccine Ervebo, a live recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus–vectored vaccine, was demonstrated using a cluster randomized design during the West African Ebola epidemic. In this trial, contacts of confirmed cases and their contact were considered as clusters (rings), which were then randomized to receive either immediate or delayed vaccination.16 However, this approach may be less suitable for KFD, which is transmitted through infected tick bites with no documented human-to-human transmission.17 A modified cluster-based trial design involving individuals with high-risk forest exposure (e.g. forest workers, firewood collectors, and cattle grazers) may be feasible. However, such trials may still face practical limitations because exposure to infected ticks varies substantially between individuals, and the risk of KFD exposure is strongly influenced by behaviour rather than geography alone. Furthermore, outbreaks often involve multiple scattered villages, and the overall number of cases may remain too small to reliably measure vaccine efficacy.

Feasible strategies for vaccine development

When conventional human efficacy trials are difficult to conduct, regulatory pathways based on animal protection data and human immunogenicity may be considered. The development of VLA1553/Ixchiq, a live attenuated chikungunya virus (CHIKV) vaccine, provides a relevant example for KFD vaccine development.

For Chikungunya, evidence from natural human infection and preclinical studies indicated that neutralising antibodies were the most plausible mediator of protection against CHIKV infection and disease.18 To establish a serological surrogate of protection, sera from vaccinated individuals with a range of neutralising antibody titres were passively transferred to CHIKV-naive non-human primates, which were subsequently challenged with wild-type CHIKV. The μPRNT50 titre measured immediately before challenge was then correlated with virological and clinical outcomes. Transfer of immune sera protected animals against viraemia, fever, other clinical signs, and viral persistence in tissues. Using absence of detectable CHIKV RNA in plasma as the endpoint, a μPRNT50 titre of ≥150 was selected as a conservative serological surrogate of protection.17 This threshold was further supported by human seroepidemiological data. In phase 3 studies, immunogenicity endpoints, including sero-response rates and the proportion of participants achieving antibody titres above the predefined threshold, were used instead of clinical efficacy endpoints.19 Regulatory approval was subsequently granted based on immunobridging data.20

Similar immunobridging pathway could be considered for evaluation of KFD vaccine. The first step would be to identify candidate immune correlates of protection in laboratory confirmed KFD patients. Available evidence suggests that KFD virus clearance from blood coincides with appearance of KFDV-specific IgG antibodies and peak CD8+ T cell activation. In a longitudinal study, KFDV-specific IgG appeared by day 7–8 of illness, and virus clearance correlated with the emergence of IgG, while robust CD8+ T-cell activation was also observed during acute infection.21 Kaushal et al. also reported significant lymphoproliferative responses and a high frequency of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells secreting IFN-γ in recovered KFD cases.22 In a prospective follow-up study of 72 KFD patients, KFDV-specific IgG was detected from the 5th post-onset day up to the last available sample at 474 days, although the study assessed binding antibodies rather than functional neutralising titres.23 Earlier serological studies also demonstrated persistence of KFDV-neutralising antibodies after KFD.24 However, the kinetics, magnitude, and durability of KFDV-specific neutralising antibodies after natural infection have not been adequately characterised using standardised PRNT or microneutralisation assays.

Although both humoral and cell-mediated immune responses are likely to contribute to control of KFD infection,25 cell-mediated immunity is difficult to use as a primary endpoint in large Phase 3 immunobridging studies because cellular assays are complex, resource-intensive, and less standardised across laboratories. Therefore, KFDV specific neutralising antibody titres could be prioritised as the lead candidate humoral markers in future KFD immune-correlate studies.

Once the correlate of protection is identified, candidate immune markers could then be tested in a relevant animal challenge model, and, where feasible, through passive-transfer experiments, to determine whether they predict protection against viraemia and clinical disease. Bonnet macaques are ideal for establishing and validating candidate correlates of protection. They develop viremia and pathological findings that resemble human KFD infection, making them a relevant translational model for vaccine evaluation.26 In bonnet macaques, passively transferred immune sera could be assessed in relation to prespecified challenge endpoints such as viraemia, fever or clinical illness, pathology, and survival.

Once a KFD-specific protective threshold is defined and validated, phase 3 clinical trials could evaluate the proportion of vaccine recipients achieving this threshold, supported by geometric mean neutralising antibody titres, durability of response, and selected cell-mediated immune markers. In addition, vaccine-induced neutralising antibody titres could be compared with those observed in individuals with laboratory-confirmed KFD infection, providing supportive evidence for potentially protective immune responses.

A similar immunogenicity-based regulatory approach was used in India. The licensure of JENVAC, inactivated Japanese encephalitis vaccine was based primarily on immunogenicity data, using established correlates of protection defined by neutralising antibody titres of 1:10 or more, without relying on conventional efficacy trials.27 This experience supports the feasibility of considering immunobridging approaches for KFD vaccine evaluation, once the candidate protective immune thresholds are identified and adequately validated.

Protective immune thresholds established in animal models may not fully predict protection in humans under field conditions. Geographic variation in KFDV strains and heterogeneity in the intensity of exposure may also influence vaccine performance. Following licensure, continued monitoring of vaccine recipients and the generation of real-world evidence on vaccine effectiveness would be essential. During 1990–1992, Dandawate et al. conducted a field evaluation of the formalin-inactivated KFD vaccine in more than 200,000 individuals aged 7–65 years residing in 284 villages across three endemic districts of Karnataka (Shimoga, Uttara Kannada, and Chikmagalur). Surveillance for KFD was established in these villages, enabling estimation of vaccine effectiveness.6 Establishing a similar cohort of villages in endemic areas, along with documentation of vaccination status and strengthening surveillance for both KFD cases and adverse events following immunization (AEFI), could generate valuable evidence on vaccine safety and effectiveness under programmatic conditions. In addition, a dedicated AEFI monitoring system in endemic districts, with timely investigation of serious adverse events by district-level AEFI committees, would be essential.

Long-term follow-up of vaccinated populations will be important to assess the durability of protection through assessment of antibody persistence as well as estimation of real-world vaccine effectiveness. Strengthening KFD surveillance after licensure in endemic districts and embedding analytical studies within this surveillance platform could also facilitate post-licensure evaluation of vaccine effectiveness. Inadequate documentation of vaccination status in India has been a challenge,28 usage of U-WIN digital platform29 for recording vaccine receipt could substantially address these limitations. A useful analytical approach in such settings could be a test-negative case–control design, in which vaccination status among laboratory-confirmed cases is compared with individuals presenting with suspected illness who test negative for KFD.30 The validity of test negative studies will depend on standardised case definition, uniform laboratory testing protocol and comparable health seeking behaviour among vaccinated and unvaccinated individuals. Vaccine effectiveness could also be estimated during future KFD outbreaks by comparing the vaccination status of cases and controls.

It is also essential to plan for programmatic deployment of KFD vaccines. Like the earlier KFD vaccine, the candidate vaccine could be offered to individuals aged 7–65 years in endemic areas considering the age distribution of KFD cases. High risk populations including forest-dependent communities, tribal populations, and occupational groups such as forest workers, cattle grazers, and firewood collectors could be prioritised for vaccination.

As new KFD vaccine candidates advance through clinical development, planning for subsequent evaluation will be essential. Given the low incidence of disease and the unpredictable nature of outbreaks, conventional efficacy trials may not be feasible, making immunobridging a pragmatic pathway for vaccine licensure. This approach would involve identifying candidate correlates of protection in KFD patients, validating protective immune thresholds in animal challenge studies, and assessing seroresponse rates against predefined thresholds in Phase 3 trials. Post-licensure effectiveness evaluations would provide additional evidence on real-world vaccine effectiveness. Community engagement with emphasis on vaccination, tick avoidance, and early care-seeking will be essential to complement the vaccination programme.
