Pseudo-Broad Ligament Leiomyoma: A Rare Cause of Diagnostic Uncertainty
Authors: Neelam Singh, Shikha Singh, Rekha Rani, Urvashi Verma, Shikha Arora
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBroad ligament fibroids are rare extrauterine leiomyomas that present diagnostic and surgical challenges due to their atypical location and distortion of pelvic anatomy. Large tumors may closely mimic adnexal masses, making accurate preoperative diagnosis difficult. A 44-year-old multiparous woman presented with progressive abdominal distension and a sensation of heaviness and pain in the lower abdomen for one year. Clinical examination and ultrasonography suggested a uterine fibroid, but the precise site of origin could not be determined. Intraoperatively, a large abdominopelvic mass measuring approximately 121 mm × 117 mm was identified arising from the uterine wall and extending into the broad ligament, consistent with a pseudo-broad ligament fibroid. The mass caused significant pelvic distortion with lateral displacement of the ureter. Tumor excision with total hysterectomy and bilateral salpingectomy was performed. Histopathology confirmed leiomyoma without malignancy. The postoperative course was uneventful. Pseudo-broad ligament fibroids are uncommon and often diagnosed only at surgery. Awareness and meticulous surgical technique are essential to prevent complications.

INTRODUCTION

Uterine fibroids (leiomyomas) are the most common benign tumors of the uterus and represent the most frequent pelvic neoplasm in women, particularly during the late reproductive and perimenopausal years. The prevalence increases with advancing age, affecting nearly 60% of women by 35 years and >80% by 50 years.[1] In midlife women, fibroids often become clinically significant because of progressive growth, degenerative changes, or pressure effects on adjacent pelvic structures.

Although leiomyomas usually arise from the uterine myometrium, they may occasionally occur at extrauterine sites such as the cervix and broad ligament. Broad ligament fibroids are rare, accounting for <1% of all uterine fibroids; however, they represent the most common type of extrauterine leiomyoma.[2] According to the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics classification, these lesions are categorized as type 8 fibroids and may be further classified as true or false broad ligament fibroids.[3]

Accurate preoperative diagnosis of broad ligament fibroids can be challenging due to distortion of pelvic anatomy and their close proximity to vital structures such as the ureters and uterine vessels. True broad ligament fibroids arise independently within the broad ligament, possibly from mesenchymal remnants or smooth muscle elements within the ligament. In contrast, false (pseudo) broad ligament fibroids originate from the lateral uterine wall and extend into the broad ligament while maintaining continuity with the uterus. Consequently, they may mimic adnexal masses on imaging and may be misdiagnosed as ovarian tumors.[3]

Clinically, these tumors are often asymptomatic but may present with pelvic pain, abdominal distension, or pressure symptoms due to compression of adjacent organs such as the bladder, bowel, or ureters, particularly in perimenopausal women.[4] We report a case of a large pseudo-broad ligament leiomyoma presenting in a perimenopausal woman that mimicked an adnexal mass preoperatively, highlighting the diagnostic challenges associated with this rare entity.

CASE REPORT

A 44-year-old woman (para 2, living 2) presented to the gynecology outpatient department with progressive abdominal distension associated with a sensation of heaviness and lower abdominal pain for 1 year. She had not sought medical attention during this period. Menarche occurred at 13 years of age. Her menstrual cycles were regular, occurring every 28–30 days and lasting 4–5 days, with normal flow and no dysmenorrhea. There were no associated bowel or bladder complaints and no history of weight loss.

Her obstetric history included two full-term normal vaginal deliveries. She had undergone tubal sterilization 11 years earlier following her second delivery. There was no significant past medical, surgical, family, or personal history.

On examination, the patient was clinically stable with normal vital signs. There was no pallor, icterus, or lymphadenopathy. Systemic examination was unremarkable. Abdominal examination revealed a firm, nontender mass with smooth margins corresponding to a uterus of approximately 24-week size. The lower border of the mass could not be palpated.

On speculum examination, the cervix could not be visualized, and no abnormal discharge was noted. Bimanual pelvic examination revealed a firm, mobile, nontender uterus of approximately 24-week size with fullness in the left fornix. Per-rectal examination confirmed these findings.

With a provisional diagnosis of uterine fibroid, further investigations were undertaken. Routine laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, blood glucose levels, and urine analysis, were within normal limits. Transabdominal ultrasonography demonstrated a large fundal fibroid measuring 121 mm × 117 mm with heterogeneous echotexture, with an endometrial thickness of 6.7 mm. Both ovaries were normal in size. Cervical cytology showed inflammatory changes, and malignancy was excluded.

After appropriate counseling, the patient was planned for a total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingectomy. Informed consent was obtained, including the possibility of oophorectomy or additional procedures if required.

Intraoperatively, a large abdominopelvic mass measuring approximately 14 cm × 12 cm was identified. The mass was firm in consistency and arose from the left side of the uterus, displacing the ureter laterally [Figure 1]. The left fallopian tube, ovarian ligament, and round ligament were stretched over the mass, whereas both ovaries and the right adnexa appeared normal. The lateral displacement of the ureter suggested a uterine origin of the tumor.

In view of the significant distortion of pelvic anatomy, meticulous dissection was performed to prevent ureteric injury. The mass was excised along with a total abdominal hysterectomy and bilateral salpingectomy [Figure 2]. The postoperative period was uneventful. Histopathological examination confirmed leiomyoma with no evidence of malignancy [Figure 3]. On follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic.

DISCUSSION

Extrauterine leiomyomas are usually asymptomatic until they reach a considerable size, at which point they may present with pelvic pain or symptoms related to compression of adjacent organs, most commonly urinary or bowel disturbances. Abnormal uterine bleeding may also occur when these tumors coexist with intrauterine leiomyomas.[5]

Broad ligament fibroids represent a rare subset of extrauterine leiomyomas and account for <1% of all uterine fibroids. Their unusual location and variable anatomical relationships often lead to diagnostic uncertainty both clinically and radiologically.

Preoperative diagnosis remains challenging, particularly in large tumors. Imaging modalities such as ultrasonography and magnetic resonance imaging may not always accurately determine the site of origin, and these lesions may mimic solid ovarian neoplasms or adnexal masses.[6]

In the present case, neither clinical examination nor ultrasonography accurately identified the pseudo-broad ligament fibroid preoperatively. The diagnosis became evident intraoperatively, where direct visualization revealed the origin of the mass.

Distinguishing between true and false broad ligament fibroids is important surgically. False broad ligament fibroids originate from the uterine wall and extend laterally, often displacing the ureter and surrounding structures. These fibroids are usually located medial to the ureter and uterine vessels.[7]

Identification of the ureter is essential to prevent iatrogenic injury, which remains a recognized complication during surgical management. Preoperative ureteric stenting may be considered in selected cases to reduce this risk.[8]

When these fibroids grow to large sizes, secondary changes such as degeneration, infection, hemorrhage, and necrosis may occur.[9] Degenerative changes are believed to result from compromised blood supply and are often not associated with significant clinical symptoms.[10]

This case highlights the importance of considering pseudo-broad ligament fibroid in the differential diagnosis of large adnexal masses, particularly in perimenopausal women, where ovarian neoplasms are also a concern.

CONCLUSION

Broad ligament fibroids are rare and may pose significant diagnostic and surgical challenges because of their atypical location and distortion of pelvic anatomy. Preoperative imaging may be inconclusive, particularly in large tumors, making intraoperative assessment crucial for determining the exact site of origin. Careful surgical planning and identification of the ureters are essential to minimize operative morbidity.
