Chronic exercise training intensity, immune cells, and cancer outcomes: a scoping review
Authors: Dong-Woo Kang, Kerry S Courneya, Maria C Swartz, Saman Maleki Vareki, Nancy B Gordon, José Cesar Rosa Neto, Richard J Simpson, K Scott Baker, Keri L Schadler, Emily C LaVoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExercise has emerged as a potent, non-pharmacological intervention to enhance immune function in patients with cancer. The effects of exercise are likely influenced by patients’ oncologic characteristics, such as cancer treatment, and intervention variables, such as exercise intensity, which can lead to heterogeneous outcomes. This scoping review aims to identify patterns, trends, and gaps in the literature regarding the relationship between chronic exercise training intensity and immune cell parameters in the context of treatment status.MethodsReports were retrieved from PubMed, MEDLINE, and CINAHL. Eligible reports were controlled clinical trials of an exercise training intervention with more than 1 exercise session, included an objectively defined exercise intensity, and reported cellular immune outcomes.ResultsTwenty-one articles (15 randomized controlled trials) were included. Results suggest a dose-response effect of intensity, where vigorous-intensity exercise (reported in 6 studies) elicited beneficial immunomodulation, such as enhanced natural killer cell cytotoxicity. Light-to-moderate and moderate-intensity exercise (reported in 8 studies) resulted in no significant immunological changes in 5 studies, particularly for patients undergoing active treatment. Comparisons across studies were difficult due to heterogeneity in patients’ clinical characteristics, intervention details, and immune parameters. Few reported cancer clinical outcomes such as disease progression, and none directly examined the relationships between immune and clinical endpoints.ConclusionsWhile direct comparisons of exercise intensities are lacking, these results suggest that vigorous exercise training may exert greater immune modulation than low-to-moderate intensity exercise training. Rigorously designed trials are needed to confirm these findings and establish the role of exercise in oncologic care.

Introduction

Immune cells are critical in orchestrating anti-tumor immunity, and their dysfunction or exhaustion can lead to immune evasion and tumor progression.1 The importance of immune cell interactions within the tumor microenvironment (TME) and systemic immune alterations in patients with cancer underscore the need for strategies that enhance immune function.2 Advances in immunotherapy, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, have also highlighted the clinical importance of modulating immune responses to improve cancer outcomes.3,4 Exercise, as a systemic intervention, has emerged as a potent modulator of immune cell trafficking and activity, offering promise as a non-pharmacological approach that may favorably alter blood immune profiles, immune interactions within the TME, treatment efficacy, and ultimately short- and long-term oncologic prognosis.5,6

Exercise is a dynamic, multifaceted intervention that encompasses diverse modalities, including aerobic, resistance, and combined regimens, and can be prescribed at varying intensities, frequencies, and durations. Exercise provokes transient physiological stress, marked by the systemic release of catecholamines, glucocorticoids, and muscle-derived cytokines (ie, myokines), leading to both acute changes and long-term adaptations in immune cell profiles and functions.7 A single session of aerobic exercise at a moderate or vigorous intensity acutely mobilizes leukocytes into circulation, particularly those with immediate effector functionality.8,9 On the other hand, repeated sessions of exercise or exercise training over an extended period (weeks to months) can promote an anti-inflammatory immune phenotype and reduce the number of senescent T cells, while also enhancing effector immune functions, such as NK cell cytotoxic activity (NKCA) and vaccine responses.10-13 The magnitude of these responses is influenced by multiple exercise variables such as intensity and by individual factors such as age and fitness status.14 Within the oncology setting, exercise immune responses can be further influenced by cancer type, disease stage, and concurrent or sequential treatments. The heterogeneity of exercise interventions and clinical contexts complicates our understanding of how exercise affects immunity in patients with cancer.

A body of existing reviews has provided insights into the broad impacts of exercise on immune function and its potential implication for cancer treatments,6,15-18 but there remains a need to better understand the nuanced effects of distinct exercise training regimens on immune responses, particularly in relation to clinical variables among patients with cancer. Recent work has examined impacts of exercise training characteristics such as modality or minutes per week of activity, yet the potential impact of exercise intensity has received less attention.19-21 In this scoping review, we aim to narrow this gap by focusing on the differential effects of the intensity of exercise training on immune cell parameters (ie, cell phenotype and/or function) in the context of the cancer treatment trajectory. Here, we synthesize the clinical evidence, highlighting how intensity-specific exercise can influence the immune landscape and cancer clinical outcomes in the context of different treatment settings, and identify directions for future research.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines,22 and the review protocol is registered in the Center for Open Science (OSF# AP3HN).

Article selection

Relevant literature was sought through online databases (PubMed, MEDLINE, and CINAHL) from inception to September 24, 2025 (Supplementary Methods Table S1), and through a review of the references within selected articles. Articles were compiled within a review management software (Covidence) and hierarchically filtered for relevance by 2 independent researchers (DK, EL). We included controlled trials involving: patients diagnosed with cancer, a structured exercise training intervention, a non-exercising control group, and changes in immune cell parameters as endpoints. Articles were excluded if they tested only a single session of exercise, did not report exercise intensity data or did not use an objective measure of exercise intensity (eg, intensity solely guided by the rating of perceived exertion [RPE] without objective physiological markers such as % maximum heart rate [HRmax]), used multimodal interventions where the effects of exercise cannot be isolated (eg, exercise with dietary interventions), lacked a non-exercising control group comprising a similar patient population, or examined only non-cellular immune markers (eg, circulating cytokines). The literature search was restricted to peer-reviewed publications written in English reporting original data and excluded study protocols or abstracts. Conflicts in article selection were resolved by conference.

Data extraction, categorization, and reporting

Key data were extracted by 2 researchers (DK, EL) and categorized into study design, patient demographics (age, sex), clinical characteristics (cancer type and stage, treatment status, type of treatment), exercise intervention details (intervention duration, type, frequency, time, intensity, adherence), and immune-related outcomes (ie, leukocyte concentration and/or proportion, phenotype, function). Clinically relevant outcomes, such as treatment response, adverse events, cancer-related biomarkers, disease progression, and physiological outcomes, such as cardiorespiratory fitness (eg, VO2peak) and body composition, were also extracted. Data were summarized primarily according to exercise intensity and treatment status, as evidence suggests that these are strongly associated with immune responses.23,24 Exercise intensity categorization was guided by the American College of Sports Medicine position stand (Table 1).25 Intervention adherence is defined as attendance to prescribed sessions, unless otherwise noted. Studies that employed both aerobic and resistance exercises were categorized based on aerobic intensity as it was reported in a more consistent manner than resistance exercise. Treatment status was categorized according to the timing of the intervention relative to patient primary anti-cancer therapy (eg, surgery, cytotoxic therapy): Treatment-Naïve, On-Treatment, or Post-Treatment. Extracted immune cell parameters included systemic and intra-tumoral immune responses, reported as changes in cell counts and phenotype (eg, cytotoxic NK cells, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes [TILs]), or function (eg, NKCA, T cell proliferation). As our focus was on responses to exercise training rather than acute exercise effects, immune cell parameters were extracted from results of resting samples. Differences between exercise and control groups are presented, unless otherwise noted.

Results

The study inclusion flow is described in the PRISMA diagram (Figure 1). A total of 502 unique articles were retrieved, 79 were retained after title/abstract screening, and 21 articles (15 randomized controlled trials [RCT]; 6 non-RCT) were selected after full-text review. Detailed summaries of individual study characteristics are provided in Table 2, and study results, including immune cell parameters and clinical/physiological outcomes in Table 3. Breast cancer was the most frequently studied (33% of articles). Interventions were administered more often to patients during treatment (48%) followed by posttreatment (33%) and pretreatment (19%). Most articles (71%) employed combined aerobic and resistance exercise training, followed by aerobic exercise only (19%) and resistance exercise only (10%). Intervention intensities varied across studies, including light-to-moderate (19%), moderate (19%), moderate-to-vigorous (33%), and vigorous (29%).

Significant exercise training intervention effects on leukocytes are discussed below. Results are organized by study intensity prescription followed by participant treatment status.

Light-moderate intensity

Four articles reported on light-to-moderate intensity combined aerobic and resistance training. Three articles examined aerobic training at 60 to 70% HRmax in patients undergoing treatment26-28; each also included resistance exercise of either unreported or light-to-moderate intensity. One article investigated aerobic training at 50 to 70% HRmax paired with vigorous (70 to 80% 1RM) resistance exercise in the post-treatment setting.29

On-treatment

Patients with breast cancer receiving adjuvant chemotherapy who performed supervised combined exercise for 18 to 22 weeks had similar pre-to-post intervention changes in the proportions of leukocytes and lymphocyte subsets as those in the usual care group.26 Blood was sampled prior to initiating chemotherapy and exercise, and 1 week after treatment and intervention completion. Both groups also exhibited an increase in tumor markers (CEA, CA) over the course of the study.26. In a separate study of children receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy for solid tumors, the percentage of NK cells expressing an activation marker (NKp46) as well as an inhibitory marker (KIR2DS4) increased in the control group both at treatment completion as and 2-months post-treatment, but not in the group that exercised.27 Leukocyte and lymphocyte concentration and NK cytotoxic activity did not differ at any timepoint. Finally, in children receiving allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), a supervised, 3-week in-hospital intervention yielded no differences in immune reconstitution measured 15- and 30-days post-HSCT relative to historical controls.28

Post-treatment

A 10-week partly supervised combined intervention with a small sample size (N = 6) was conducted among children beginning 30 days post-allogeneic HSCT.29 The light-to-moderate intensity aerobic exercise combined with vigorous resistance exercise led to a greater increase in NKCA (% lysis) and in the proportion of cytotoxic CD56dim NK cells post-intervention than the usual care control group.

Summary

In the 3 reports from patients receiving treatment, light-to-moderate intensity exercise led to few changes in circulating leukocytes. Patients with breast cancer in the exercise group avoided the decline in VO2peak noted among women in the control group.26 And although no statistical analyses were employed, graft-vs-host-disease occurred more frequently in controls than children who exercised (46% vs 29%).28 The study among patients post-treatment suggests a greater benefit of exercise, given the increases in cytotoxic NK cells among the exercise group; however, caution is needed for interpretation due to the small sample size.29

Moderate intensity

Moderate intensity aerobic training effects were reported in 4 articles; each also included a resistance exercise component of moderate intensity,30,31 vigorous intensity,32 or did not report resistance intensity.33 Three articles report results in patients during treatment30-32 and 1, post-treatment (Table 2).33

On-treatment

Among advanced esophageal cancer patients receiving neo-adjuvant chemotherapy prior to surgery, 16-week of partly supervised aerobic and resistance training resulted in a greater number of CD8+ cells within tumor and stroma in the exercise group compared with control; the change in VO2peak post-intervention positively correlated with both CD8+ and granzyme b+ tumor infiltrating cells.30 Further, those in the exercise group had a higher density of cells in peritumor-associated tertiary lymphoid structures as well as structures with more clearly defined germinal centers, indicating cellular maturity. In another study, a 6- to 8-week supervised aerobic and resistance intervention among childhood patients with hematological or solid tumor cancers on adjuvant chemotherapy and/or radiation reduced the systemic immune-inflammation index.31 Last, an 8-week supervised combined aerobic and resistance exercise intervention among men with prostate cancer during radiation and androgen deprivation treatment found that while the intervention improved physical function and fatigue, there were no differences between exercise and control groups in the treatment-related changes in leukocytes and lymphocytes.32

Post-treatment

An 8-week supervised combined aerobic and resistance exercise intervention provided to patients with breast cancer 3-years post-surgery and cytotoxic therapy yielded no change in leukocyte or lymphocyte subset concentration nor any change in NKCA (% lysis).33

Summary

While no intervention-impacts on lymphocytes were reported in the single study examining moderate exercise in the post-treatment setting,33 2 of the 3 studies of moderate exercise training among patients on-treatment report a beneficial effect of exercise on immunity: an increase in intratumoral T cells and increased peritumor lymphoid structures,30 and a decrease in systemic inflammation.31 The clinical importance of such findings are mixed. Although the presence of mature tertiary lymphoid structures has been associated with improved survival in other studies, the control and exercise groups in Rayner et al did not statistically differ in survival.30 Yet, the decrease in systemic inflammation among children undergoing treatment associated with decreased fatigue.31

Moderate-vigorous intensity

Seven trials investigated moderate-to-vigorous exercise intensity; 1 trial included treatment-naïve patients,34 3 included patients on-treatment,35-37 and 3 included patients at post-treatment.38-40 One study examined an aerobic exercise–only intervention,36 another examined a resistance exercise intervention only,40 and the remainder investigated combined aerobic and resistance exercise.34,35,37-39

Treatment-naïve

Brown et al randomized treatment-naïve patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) to partly supervised aerobic exercise that increased from moderate to vigorous intensity over 16 weeks, or to usual care.34 The exercise group also performed resistance exercise of unreported intensity. Despite high adherence to the exercise program (92 ± 8%), no differences between groups were observed in changes cardiorespiratory fitness or physical function, nor did groups differ in changes in leukocyte or lymphocyte subsets.

On-treatment

In a retrospective analysis of women receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy for breast cancer, a combined aerobic and resistance intervention did not yield significant differences in the rate of complete pathological response following chemotherapy.37 In women without a complete pathological response, the number of TIL did not differ between the exercise and control groups, but there was a slightly trend favoring the exercise group (P = .233). Toffoli et al investigated 9-12 weeks of supervised aerobic exercise that straddled the moderate and vigorous intensity domains paired with vigorous intensity resistance exercise among patients with breast or colon cancer receiving chemotherapy.35 Blood was sampled before and after chemotherapy. The exercise group exhibited an increased expression of the activating receptor NKp46 among CD56dim NK cells after the intervention and did not exhibit the same decline in NK cell degranulation noted among controls. Glass et al examined an aerobic-exercise only intervention: patients with solid tumors receiving cytotoxic and supportive therapy performed supervised exercise that varied from continuous cycling at 55%-80% of VO2peak to high intensity interval training (HIIT) at 100% VO2peak across the 12-week intervention.36 Although this approach improved VO2peak, circulating immune cell parameters were not impacted.

Post-treatment

A 3-month aerobic and resistance exercise intervention among patients from several cancer types who had received a peripheral blood stem cell transplant did not impact lymphocyte or T cell numbers, or T cell proliferative function 1-, 2-, or 3-months post-transplant.39 In contrast, Hutnick et al reported that a partly supervised 6-month aerobic and resistance exercise intervention initiated 2-8 weeks following breast cancer treatment (surgery and chemotherapy) increased VO2max and T cell proliferative response to multiple mitogens.38 Further, participants in the control group exhibited a decrease in activated CD4+ T cells, which was not observed in the exercise group. In a supervised 12-week resistance exercise intervention among patients who had been treated at least 2 years earlier for breast cancer with surgery and chemo-radiation (70% with history of hormone therapy), Lee et al reported greater NK activity (measured by IFN-γ secretion following stimulation) post-intervention in those who exercised compared with those in the control group.40 Significant increases in muscle strength and mass were also reported.

Summary

The single study of moderate-vigorous exercise among treatment naïve patients reported no intervention effects on CLL cells or other immune cells, although the study was primarily designed to assess feasibility and safety and was not powered to detect these differences (n = 28).34 Differing results were reported in the 3 trials that investigated moderate-to-vigorous intensity exercise among patients receiving treatment for solid tumors. Whereas no differences in circulating leukocytes due to the intervention were observed among patients with a variety of cancers,36 patients with breast or colon cancer maintained NK cell function across the intervention period,35 and an intervention among patients with breast cancer found an increase in TIL, albeit nonsignificant.37 Finally, 2 of 3 studies of exercise among post-treatment patients reported intervention-related benefit among T cells38 and NK cells.40 Both included a resistance training component, and both were among breast cancer survivors.

Vigorous intensity

Six studies examined vigorous intensity exercise training. Two trials investigated combined aerobic and resistance exercise,41,42 3 trials aerobic exercise only,43-45 and 1 trial resistance exercise only.46 Three were conducted among treatment-naïve patients,42,43,45 1 among patients on-treatment,41 and 2 among post-treatment patients.44,46

Treatment-naïve

Djurhuus et al compared men with prostate cancer randomized to supervised aerobic-only HIIT or usual care prior to radical prostatectomy.43 In the per-protocol analyses (defined as participants who attended at least 75% of training sessions), exercise increased NK cells within tumor tissue. The duration of the intervention varied from 2 to 8 weeks; the number of training sessions positively correlated with the number of NK cells in the tumor. Zhou et al also examined a short (8 sessions) preoperative aerobic-only HIIT program, this among patients with suspected non-small cell lung cancer. The intervention resulted in few blood leukocyte differences 3 days post-surgery.45 The increase in neutrophils and the neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio was significantly less in those who exercised, suggesting a lower inflammatory response, while differences in lymphocytes and monocytes were not observed. A supervised, combined aerobic and resistance HIIT intervention was investigated among treatment-naïve patients with CLL.42 Compared with the control group, the exercise group exhibited less of an increase in B cells (small effect size) and increased NK cell (medium effect) and CD16+ monocyte concentration (large effect) after the 12-week intervention. Moreover, NKCA and NK cell expression of perforin and granzyme b were increased (large effect).

On-treatment

Spiliopoulou et al randomized patients with multiple myeloma on maintenance therapy to a 5-month combined aerobic and resistance exercise intervention.41 The exercise group exhibited a post-intervention increase in blood monocytes and an increase in the proportion of T cells expressing the co-stimulatory marker CD27 within the bone marrow, whereas the control group instead exhibited an increase in bone marrow NK/NKT-like cells.

Post-treatment

Two studies examined vigorous intensity interventions in patients with breast cancer post-treatment.44,46 Patients had received surgery, chemotherapy, and/or radiation and were stratified during randomization based on history or current use of hormone therapy. The earlier of the 2 studies examined a 15-week aerobic exercise-only intervention.44 Women in the exercise group demonstrated increased NKCA and unstimulated lymphocyte proliferation compared with those in the control group; no changes in the proportion of blood mononuclear subsets, neutrophil function, or blood mononuclear cytokine production were found. The second study examined a 16-week resistance exercise-only intervention46; no changes in NK cell expression of functional markers, such as granzyme b, CD107a, perforin, or IFN-γ were found. Instead, those in the exercise group had a lower expression of TNF on NK and NK-like T cells compared with controls at the end of the intervention, suggesting decreased inflammation in the exercise group.46

Summary

Four of six reports from vigorous intensity interventions found enhanced immune activity. Among treatment naïve patients, interventions resulted in increased NK cells within tumors43 and increased circulating NK cell function.42 While no increases in immune activity were noted in Zhou et al, patients in the exercise group exhibited less systemic inflammation (lower neutrophil: lymphocyte ratio) 3 days post-surgery. Importantly, the exercise group experienced significantly fewer complications post-surgery, and a shorter hospital stay.45 Among patients on-treatment, those in the intervention group had a greater proportion of T cells within bone marrow; further, those in the exercise group had lower minimal residual disease at post-intervention (0% positive vs 12.5% positive among controls).41 Both studies of vigorous exercise in the post-treatment setting included breast cancer survivors an average of 12-14 months post-treatment but differed in modality. Whereas the aerobic exercise intervention increased NK function and lymphocyte proliferation,44 the resistance exercise intervention decreased expression of the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF on lymphocytes.46

Discussion

Summary of main findings

There was considerable heterogeneity in clinical characteristics of patients, immune cell parameters, and exercise training intervention details across the 21 articles reviewed here. Nevertheless, we found support for the idea that chronic exercise training interventions can positively modulate the immune system in patients with cancer, as 13 of the articles reported at least 1 change in cellular immunity following the intervention, though these changes varied in cell subtype and magnitude. We also found modest support for the idea that intervention effects relate to the prescribed exercise intensity, where vigorous-intensity exercise more consistently elicits phenotypic and functional changes among immune cells (Figure 2). For example, moderate-to-vigorous and vigorous intensity interventions have been shown to enhance the number of tumor-infiltrating NK cells in prostate cancer patients pre-surgery,43 beneficially remodel the leukocyte profile in treatment-naïve CLL by boosting NK cell populations,42 increase the proportion of activated bone-marrow T cells in multiple myeloma patients on maintenance therapy,41 and increase NKCA and lymphocyte proliferation in post-treatment breast cancer survivors.38,40,44 In contrast, light-to-moderate intensity interventions more frequently resulted in no significant changes to leukocyte phenotype or function, particularly in patients undergoing active treatment.26-28 However, we also uncovered counter-examples to this pattern: increased NKCA following a light-to-moderate intervention29 and no change in leukocyte phenotype following moderate-to-vigorous intensity interventions.34,36 Finally, in the study by Fairey et al, differences were found depending on assay characteristics (stimulated vs unstimulated proliferation) or cell type (neutrophil vs NK cell).44

Summary of immune cell responses to exercise interventions among patients with cancer. The number of articles reporting a response is indicated by the size of the circle.

Among non-cancer populations, there is a dose-response relationship between acute physiological stress and cellular signaling.47 More vigorous intensity exercise elicits a more profound neuroendocrine response than lower intensity exercise, leading to greater surges in hormones such as the catecholamines that are pivotal in modulating immune cell activity and trafficking.9 Furthermore, the greater metabolic stress of high-intensity exercise stimulates a larger release of myokines from contracting muscle, which may induce a systemic anti-inflammatory environment while promoting cytotoxic lymphocyte proliferation and maintenance.48,49 The repetition of the exercise stimulus during exercise training interventions may enhance immune surveillance.50 Thus, it is plausible that repeated and more significant physiological perturbations from more vigorous intensity exercise drive more robust immunological adaptations compared with lower intensity training.51 Understanding the extent to which exercise training intensity also drives immune adaptations in patients with cancer is complicated by the fact that no single study has directly examined exercise intensity as a factor affecting immune responses. Among many potential confounding variables, differences in cancer type and treatment status are likely to have a large effect on immune variables and differences in these parameters complicates comparisons across studies.24 In the next section, we explore the exercise intensity-immune relationship by discussing results across studies that included patients with similar treatment regimens and measured similar immune outcomes.

Cancer type and treatment status can influence intervention response

In the context of clinical oncology, the patient’s underlying immune state is key to understanding their response to exercise as it varies significantly with the cancer itself, treatment status, and individual host factors.52,53 The immune challenge differs between cancer types and thus the desired response to exercise differs in each scenario.54 As examples, the slowed growth in circulating malignant B-cells indicates a beneficial effect of exercise among patients with CLL,42 and the benefit of exercise in patients with solid tumors is exemplified by increased TIL post-intervention.30,43 Oncological treatments also alter the patients’ immune system. As an example, allogeneic-HSCT is an aggressive immunological insult, involving complete ablation and slow reconstitution of the immune system.55 Two articles reporting light-moderate intensity exercise during and shortly after (≤30 days) HSCT are encouraging, as adherence was >90% and no adverse events were reported.28,29 Post-HSCT, the intervention increased cytotoxic NK cells and NKCA suggesting that exercise may help balance NK subpopulations during early immune reconstitution.29 Exercise interventions later post-transplant (100 days) may also facilitate immune recovery, as Spiliopoulou et al showed an increased proportion of CD27+ T cells in bone marrow following vigorous exercise training.41 Other cytotoxic treatments (eg, chemotherapy, radiotherapy), can also induce immune suppression,56 though the extent of its impact on exercise-induced immune changes is unclear. The 8 articles reporting results from exercise interventions among patients receiving chemotherapy and/or radiation generally found few differences between exercise and control groups in the concentrations of circulating leukocyte subsets (Table 3). However, the studies reported by Fiuza-Luces et al and Toffoli et al found indications of decreased immune function over the course of the intervention in control patients (ie, increased inhibitory receptor on NK cells, decreased NK degranulation) whereas these variables remained stable in those who exercised.27,35

It is worth noting that the distribution of studies grouped by intensity differed by participant treatment status. Of the 8 studies that prescribed light-to-moderate or moderate intensity exercise, 6 were among patients on-treatment, whereas just 4 of the 13 moderate-to-vigorous or vigorous intensity exercise interventions were among patients on-treatment. Two of the lower-intensity interventions resulted in differences in immune outcomes: increased CD8+ T cells among tumors30 and decreased systemic inflammation.31 Similarly, 2 of the higher-intensity interventions resulted in immune impact: increased CD27+T cells in bone marrow41 and maintenance of NK cell function.35 While moderate-to-vigorous exercise is generally considered safe for people with cancer,57 adherence to higher-intensity exercise may be lower among patients on-treatment.58 However, this is difficult to assess among the studies included in this review, as only 1 of the higher-intensity interventions reported adherence. Encouragingly, adherence in this study36 was comparable to the 4 lower-intensity studies26-28,30 that reported adherence (84% vs >70%). Adverse events were also less frequently reported among the higher intensity studies. Future work should include these intervention characteristics.

Differences across immune cell parameters

Immune cells of different subtypes coordinate differing responses in the body, and these roles must be considered when interpreting changes that occur with exercise. For example, among cancer patients before or during treatment, an increase in regulatory T cells or myeloid-derived suppressor cells could indicate immune suppression and increased likelihood of tumor escape.59 Importantly, neither of the 2 studies in this review that measured regulatory (FOXP3+) T cells reported an increase with exercise.30,34 Conversely, an increase in NK cells could indicate increased tumor cell killing capacity, as was observed in 4 of the studies in this review.29,40,42,44 Importantly, there is a positive correlation between NKCA and overall survival among patients with cancer and low NKCA associates with relapse.60,61 Immune cells also differ in their acute response to exercise and thus may show different adaptations to exercise training.9 Putting together considerations of immune cell outcome, cancer type, and treatment status, some comparisons of exercise intensity effects on specific immune variables can be made. Two articles report results of interventions among treatment-naïve patients with CLL. Of these, vigorous intensity but not moderate-to-vigorous intensity exercise slowed malignant B-cell growth.34,42 Three articles report intervention related changes in NKCA among post-treatment patients with breast cancer. Whereas moderate intensity exercise yielded no difference in NKCA, both moderate-to-vigorous and vigorous exercise significantly increased NKCA.33,40,44 These comparisons provide some limited support for the idea that training at more vigorous exercise intensities may offer greater immune benefit among patients with cancer both prior to and after treatment.

Further clinical considerations

While changes in circulating immune markers are mechanistically informative, their clinical significance remains uncertain until they can be causally linked to clinical outcomes. Among non-cancer populations, there is debate as to whether prolonged severe intensity exercise (eg, marathon running) can induce a transient period of immune suppression due to extended metabolic and hormonal stress.47,62-64 However, this model has little applicability to the studies reviewed here that included vigorous exercise training, as it does not fully capture the immune response to the often-used HIIT that involves short, intense bursts of effort within a total duration of just 20-40 minutes. In the articles reviewed here, the effects of exercise on immune cells, when present, could generally be interpreted in a positive light (eg, increased NKCA, increased number of TIL) and when absent, were interpreted to indicate that exercise did not have a detrimental effect on the immune system. Thus, the bulk of the evidence suggests that vigorous-intensity exercise may not be immunosuppressive, but rather a potent stimulus for therapeutically desirable immune cell trafficking.

Less than half of the reviewed studies directly reported clinical endpoints such as disease progression or common oncologic adverse events, highlighting a critical gap in the literature. Two studies measured PSA levels in patients with prostate cancer as a surrogate marker of cancer progression, but neither found differences between exercise and control groups.32,43 This may be due to ‘floor effects’ as PSA for those on androgen deprivation therapy is substantially lower. In a breast cancer trial, the tumor marker CA15.3 was significantly increased in the exercise group while no change was seen in the control group.26 The authors concluded that the change in CA15.3 was within the normal range and given inconsistencies with prior publications warrants further investigations.26,65 Rayner et al assessed dose modification, tumor staging, and survival in patients with esophageal cancer undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The exercise and control groups did not differ in these characteristics despite an increase in CD8+ cells in tumors and stroma in the exercise group.30 Some studies did find clinical improvements following exercise. Glass et al observed a trend toward a more rapid hemoglobin response with less drug administration in the exercise group in patients with any solid cancer receiving cytotoxic therapy and synthetic erythropoietin.36 A preoperative HIIT intervention decreased hospital stay and complications among patients with suspected non-small cell lung cancer.45 Finally, 1 patient with multiple myeloma in the exercise group moved from complete response to treatment to stringent complete response, whereas no patients in control had this indicator of improved treatment response following the vigorous intervention.41 Taken together, the clinical outcome evidence remains limited and heterogeneous, with most trials underpowered for clinical endpoints and with mostly exploratory, descriptive, non-prespecified analyses linking immune modulation to clinical outcomes. This gap highlights the need for trials that are prospectively powered for oncologic endpoints (eg, pathological response, tumor markers, and time to oncologic event) and that embed mediation analyses to test whether exercise-induced immune changes translate into improved clinical outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several key strengths. Notably, it is the first review that specifically focused on exercise training intensity as a primary determinant of immunological outcomes among cancer patients. This review summarized evidence across various cancer types and the treatment timelines, considered both the quantity and function of immune cells, and included clinical cancer outcomes to derive clinical relevance. However, the findings must be interpreted within the context of limitations. The included studies differed in exercise modality, which itself may influence immune outcomes.20,21 However, the high prevalence of combined training protocols in the included studies precluded a direct comparison of modalities. Many of the reviewed studies were conducted in small sample sizes, limiting their statistical power, and the clinical status of post-treatment survivors was often ambiguous, which makes it unclear whether they were receiving ongoing therapies such as hormonal therapies. Effect sizes were difficult to extract due to inconsistent reporting, also complicating the comparison of effects across exercise intensity. It should also be noted that none of the trials discussed in this review directly compared 2 (or more) exercise intensities. We chose not to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis, given the included studies are profoundly heterogeneous in terms of cancer diagnoses, treatment regimens, intervention duration, and exercise protocols, which complicates direct comparisons of immune results.

Importantly, the studies we reviewed lacked standardized reporting for exercise intensity. Although we removed studies with exercise prescription that did not use objective measures and systematically categorized based on the ACSM guidelines, it was difficult to compare the dose of exercise administered across trials. As an alternative, we considered whether immune outcomes varied by whether participants exhibited a physiological adaptation to exercise, such as increased VO2peak or an increase in lean or muscle mass. This was based on the report of a positive association between the number of training sessions completed with both change in VO2peak and the number of NK cells in tumors.43 In parallel, Rayner et al found a positive association between change in VO2peak and frequency of CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL).30 It was also rooted in the idea that skeletal muscle is a source of myokines that support lymphocytes.49 Only 8 articles reported aerobic fitness (VO2peak or 6-minute walk test) pre- and post-intervention and 6 found exercise-induced improvements in aerobic fitness. Of these 6, half reported a change in an immune outcome30,38,42 while the other half did not.26,32,36 Six articles reported lean or fat-free mass (largely representing skeletal muscle) pre- and post-intervention; 4 reported improvements in the exercise group vs control group and of these, 3 reported a change in an immune outcome.30,40,43 However, the type of immune outcome investigated also varied across the studies reporting a physiological change; those that found no change in immune cell parameters but did find improved aerobic fitness or increased lean mass only examined the phenotype of circulating immune cells.26,32,34,36 In contrast, those studies that found a change in an immune outcome examined immune function, such as proliferation, NKCA, or cytokine expression.38,40,44 It is important to note that the likely associations among exercise, physiological changes, and immune cell parameters were inferential and not directly tested in most of the reviewed articles.

Future directions

The collective evidence discussed in our review can be applicable in various clinical scenarios. One promising avenue is surrounding immunotherapy, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors or CAR-T cell therapy, which is designed to overcome tumor-induced tolerance, and has been gaining attention in the exercise oncology field. By potentiating an anti-tumor T cell response, immune therapy can fundamentally reshape the immune environment in which exercise exerts its effect.6,16 Specific roles for exercise during immunotherapy have been proposed, such as preserving NK cell cytotoxicity during immunotherapy, mobilizing potent cytotoxic T cells with lowered expression of the exhaustion marker PD-1, and providing a simple, economical strategy to increase cell concentration for adoptive transfer therapies and improve cellular therapies such as monoclonal antibody therapy.6,66 Support for these proposed roles comes from preclinical models that demonstrate that exercise enhances the infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells into tumors, which can improve the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors that rely on these cells to be present in the TME.67,68 The results of Rayner et al, of increased CD8+ T cells in tumors of patients on chemotherapy, are also encouraging. Exercise may also promote a less immunosuppressive TME, potentially overcoming a key mechanism of resistance to immunotherapy.69 Although none of the included articles in our review specifically focused on immunotherapy, several clinical trials are underway to test the roles of exercise during immunotherapy (including NCT04263467, NCT04676009, NCT06497322, NCT06298734, NCT06983899). Moving forward, future trials should further incorporate pre-specified immune endpoints including functional assays such as NKCA, leverage window-of-opportunity biopsies to examine intra-tumoral immune cells, and potentially apply multi-omics to delineate patient- and treatment-specific moderators of exercise and cancer treatment interactions.

Based on the insights and limitations identified, several future directions are crucial for advancing the field of exercise immunology in oncology. Future trials must prioritize standardized reporting of both the exercise interventions, particularly intensity, and the specific details of participants’ oncological treatments to reduce heterogeneity and to allow for more robust evidence syntheses. Only half of the included studies included measures of cardiorespiratory fitness and/or lean mass pre- and post-intervention, which would have clarified physiological impacts of the interventions. Furthermore, most studies reported adherence as attendance rates, which does not necessarily reflect adherence to the prescribed exercise intensity. Moreover, there is a need for more studies designed to directly compare different exercise intensities within the same patient population to map the dose-response curve of immune responses. These trials should also incorporate adequately powered clinical endpoints, such as changes in cancer progression markers, rates of treatment responses, or tumor pathology or microenvironment. Investigating the lingering effects of exercise on immune cells post-intervention would also provide valuable insights for long-term outcomes, in relation to the given training period and patients’ exercise behaviors. Finally, studies that directly compare the effects of exercise on immune function in patients before, during, and after treatment would inform how the positioning of exercise within the treatment continuum may alter its effects.70 Ultimately, by understanding the mechanisms, the long-term goal would be to conduct large-scale trials that can assess the impact of these exercise-induced immune changes on clinical outcomes including disease recurrence, progression-free survival, and overall survival, providing the evidence needed to integrate specific exercise prescriptions as a standard of care in oncology.

In summary, the current clinical evidence, though heterogeneous, suggests a dose-response relationship between exercise intensity and immunomodulation in cancer patients. Whereas fewer studies utilizing lower-intensity exercise reported changes among immune outcomes, vigorous-intensity training interventions more frequently resulted in increases in immune activity. Thus, vigorous intensity exercise may serve as a potential strategy for enhancing anti-tumor cellular immunity. This effect may likely be mediated by robust neuroendocrine and myokine signaling pathways that drive the mobilization and activation of key effector lymphocytes. However, this field needs rigorously designed, adequately powered trials that integrate standardized exercise prescriptions, prespecified immune and clinical endpoints, and mechanistic analyses to clarify causal pathways. Identifying the optimal exercise intensity for positive immune modulation and establishing whether these exercise-induced immune changes can alter outcomes of clinical cancer endpoints would further advance exercise as a therapeutic adjunct in oncologic care.
