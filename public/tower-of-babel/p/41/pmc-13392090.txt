The benchmarking and application of tag-degraders in vivo to validate therapeutic targets
Authors: Charlene M. Magtoto, Stephen Mieruszynski, Hao Dong, Ashley P. Ng, Ladina Di Rago, Andrew J. Kueh, Martin Brzozowski, Christoph Grohmann, Joel R. Walker, Ngee Kiat Chua, Laura F. Dagley, Alessio Ciulli, Guillaume Lessene, Marco J. Herold, Joan K. Heath, John Silke, Rebecca Feltham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transitioning a candidate therapeutic target from bench to bedside requires significant time and financial investment, yet clinical success remains low often due to poor on-target toxicity assessment during preclinical validation. Tag-degraders provide a tool to improve target validation by enabling degradation of any protein of interest via a degron-tag. This drug-based, reversible, and dose-dependent method of protein removal can mimic degrader-based drug treatments and assess the implications of target protein depletion in vivo. However, each degrader has a distinct pharmacokinetic profile that will influence its effectiveness across tissues. To create a resource to enable the most appropriate choice of tag-degrader, we benchmark the dTAG, HaloPROTAC, and NanoTAC systems in vivo by employing a transgenic mouse expressing a reporter protein targetable by these tag-degraders. Through various treatment regimes, we characterise each degrader profile across a panel of 20 tissues and organs, highlighting the superior degradation by dTAG molecules, and identify differences between degradation in whole tissues versus single cell populations. Using an FKBPF36V knock-in mouse expressing 65K-FKBPF36V, we reveal target specific degradation kinetics, and a critical requirement for 65K in mice. Together, this resource will assist researchers in choosing the right degrader and tag for their own applications.

Introduction

Exploitation of the ubiquitin-proteasome system for targeted protein degradation has opened a new avenue in the therapeutic drug development space. This process is centred around a class of drugs known as ‘degraders’ which consist of molecular glues and heterobifunctional small molecules (also described as a PROteolysis Targeting Chimeras, or PROTACs). Molecular glues consist of a single small molecule that can engage both an E3 ubiquitin ligase (usually von Hippel-Lindau disease tumor suppressor (VHL) or Cereblon (CRBN) and a protein of interest (POI), whereas PROTACs consist of two small molecules, one E3 ligase binder and one POI binder, tethered together by a chemical linker. Chemically bridging these two proteins together triggers a cascade to induce the E3 ligase-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of the POI. Different from occupancy-driven therapies like chemical inhibitors and antibodies, targeted degradation drives the removal of a disease-causing protein at sub-stoichiometric concentrations due to the often catalytic and recyclable nature of the drug. Perhaps the most attractive advantage of degrader technology is that the target protein binder does not need to occupy an active site, allowing us to expand the ‘druggable’ proteome to virtually any protein for which a chemical binder can be developed against. Unsurprisingly, there has been an explosion of degrader development in the last 20 years, with some degrader compounds reaching stage III clinical trials1.

Indeed, the trajectory of degrader-based therapies shows promise in the clinic, yet there is still unexplored potential for targeted degradation in the earlier stages of therapeutic target validation. Tag-targeting protein degraders, known as tag-degraders or degron tags, are a subset of PROTAC compounds that recruit an E3 ligase complex to a small degron tag that can be attached to virtually any protein of interest, thereby allowing indirect degradation of the tagged protein. Unlike conventional protein regulation mechanisms like CRISPR knockouts and siRNA, this dynamic and reversible method of protein removal can mimic a drug treatment prior to the development of a binder for a specific POI. Several tag-degrader combinations have been developed, including the AID1/22,3, aTAG4, BromoTag5, CH6/SNIPER6, L-AdPROM7, and SMASh systems8. Our previous study outlined the development of the NanoTAC system which targets the NanoLuc tag9, and benchmarked NanoTACs against two prominent tag-degrader systems, dTAGs and HaloPROTACs which target FKBP12F36V (FKBPF36V) and HaloTag7, respectively10–13. We showed that dTAGs outperformed both NanoTACs and HaloPROTACs, with NanoTAC activity on par with HaloPROTACs, and demonstrated that both dTAGs and NanoTACs can be used concurrently for dual protein regulation. Tag-degrader combinations like AID2 and BromoTag have also been shown to increase target depletion or co-deplete two targets to enhance a specific functional outcome14. Other efforts have been made to identify the most appropriate tag-degrader platform to use, including a comparison of the AID, dTAG, IKZF3d, HaloTag, and SMASh systems in cell lines across a panel of targets, recommending dTAGs as their first choice and highlighting how tag-degrader effectiveness can vary greatly depending on the target itself15.

Despite the superior degradative capacity of the dTAG compounds in cells, there is still broad utility in using the HaloTag and the NanoLuc tag. Given the array of reagents, including antibodies and assay kits, and live imaging capabilities both in cells and in vivo, the use of the HaloPROTAC and NanoTAC systems for target validation garners significant interest, especially where the researcher also wishes to study POI biology in addition to POI degradation. Testing how these tag-degrader systems perform in an animal model is essential for guiding target validation studies. While both dTAGs and HaloPROTACs have been used in vivo in independent studies, these have focused on a specific POI or a specific organ and have not yet been benchmarked alongside each other10,11,16–18. Factors including protein regulation, tissue-specific expression of a target protein, or the disease context used, can all affect tag-degrader activity and how we interpret the degradative potential of a compound.

Here, we present an unbiased head-to-head comparison of dTAG (dTAG13/FC1 and dTAGV−1/FV1), HaloPROTAC (HaloPROTAC-E/HV2), and NanoTAC (NanoTAC4/NC4) activity in vivo across 20 different tissues and organs. We benchmark these tag-degraders using a transgenic mouse strain engineered to express our previously developed reporter protein that can be targeted by these tag-degraders. The reporter, an exogenous protein lacking physiological function, serves as an ideal baseline to assess tag-degrader performance and distinguish intrinsic degrader capabilities from differences arising due to the physiological regulation of specific targets. We highlight the superiority of dTAGs both in efficiency and durability of degradation, and their broad tissue activity. We show that similar degradation kinetics can be observed when targeting an FKBPF36V-tagged POI, 65K, but also note that key differences exist between the degradation of our reporter and this physiological substrate. Finally, we apply a proof-of-concept model to assess the safety of long-term 65K depletion. Our data suggest that degradation of 65K–FKBPF36V primarily results in on-target effects under the dosing regimens used in this study. Together, these findings demonstrate how tag-degrader systems can inform on degrader amenability and tissue-specific degradation, enabling earlier and more informed evaluation of potential therapeutic targets.

Results

Generation of a reporter mouse to analyse in vivo tag-degrader activity

To enable the in vivo comparison of dTAGs, HaloPROTACs and NanoTACs, we took our previously developed Halo-EGFP-NanoLuc-FKBPF36V (H-E-N-F) reporter construct9 and generated a conditional transgenic knock-in (KI) mouse model using CRISPR/Cas9, where the reporter is integrated at the ROSA26 locus. By crossing these mice to a ubiquitous Cre strain, the Cre-mediated LoxP recombination resulted in the removal of an upstream stop cassette to permit reporter protein expression (Fig. 1a). To confirm this recombination event, we first analysed white blood cell (WBC) isolates from H-E-N-F expressing mice. Lysates from WBCs were analysed by Western blotting to confirm whether the reporter protein was both detectable, and at the correct molecular weight (94 kDa). Blotting for Halo and FKBPF36V, we observed a band slightly below 100 kDa that was specific to the KI reporter mice and at the size observed in our previous study (Fig. 1b)9. In crude WBC preparations, there was also an increase in NanoLuc luminescence and a shift in EGFP fluorescence in KI reporter mice that was not present in WT mice (Fig. 1c, d). To compare reporter protein expression levels across the body, a panel of 20 tissues and organs were collected and analysed for NanoLuc luminescence (Fig. 1e). Ubiquitous expression of the reporter protein was observed with varying levels across the selected tissue and organ panel. WBCs showed the lowest expression levels, followed by slightly higher expression in tissues like the eye, skin, lung, liver, kidney, adrenal gland, and bone marrow. The highest expression was observed in the pancreas and in the small and large intestines (colon, duodenum, ileum; Fig. 1e). Considering the transgenic nature of our reporter protein, it was important to ensure that its presence was not immunogenic. Whole blood populations were analysed for WBC counts from the Peroxidase Method (WBCP), red blood cells (RBCs), neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils, and large unstained cells, with no significant changes observed between the WT and KI mice (Supplementary Fig. 1a). dTAGs trigger degradation across an array of tissues and organs

Between dTAGs, HaloPROTACs and NanoTACs, dTAGs are the best characterised tag-degraders in vivo, and have been shown to degrade a luciferase-FKBPF36V protein expressed in mice via engrafted MV4;11 cells at 35 mg/kg10,11. Using this as a benchmark for the other tag-degraders, we performed a dose titration and time course and analysed reporter protein levels in WBC isolates using flow cytometry to determine the treatment concentration and duration where degradation can be detected across all or most tag-degraders (Supplementary Fig. 1b). WBCs were analysed at 0, 4 or 8 h following a single 10, 20 or 30 mg/kg dose of tag-degrader. Reporter levels remained unchanged over the 8 hour period following treatment with a vehicle only control (Ctrl). We observed a 50-60% reduction in reporter levels after treatment with 20 and 30 mg/kg of FC1 at 4 h post-IP, and degradation did not improve over time. FV1 showed a 70% reduction in reporter protein levels at 4 h, which improved to ~80% over time and was independent of concentration (Supplementary Fig. 1b). NC4 showed an increase in reporter degradation in a concentration, but not time, dependent manner resulting in ~60% degradation at 30 mg/kg (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Across all conditions, HV2 was the only degrader unable to reduce reporter protein levels in the WBC population in our hands. Overall, 30 mg/kg injected intraperitoneally (IP) for 8 h was optimal for dTAGs and NanoTACs, and therefore, we maintained these parameters for all tissue and organ degradation comparisons.

To compare tissue and organ degradation profiles for all three degraders, reporter mice were injected IP with 30 mg/kg of tag-degraders for 8 h prior to assessment of reporter protein levels via NanoLuc luminescence. Overall, FC1 and FV1 dTAGs showed superior and widespread degradation profiles in 17/20 and 15/20 of the tested organs, respectively (Fig. 2a). In most organs, dTAGs were able to reduce reporter protein levels by 50-90% (Fig. 2a). The HaloPROTAC showed degradation in 14/20 of the tested organs, however the level of degradation observed was modest and typically less than 50% (Fig. 2a). In addition to promoting the degradation of the NanoLuc tag, NC4 also acts as an inhibitor of NanoLuc luciferase activity9,19, and therefore, luminescence is an inaccurate measure of degrader activity. To reliably assess the effectiveness of NC4, Western blotting was used as an orthogonal approach for all tag-degraders (Supplementary Fig. 2). Notably, Western blot data also paralleled the luminescence assays, corroborating the degradation profiles of dTAGs and HaloPROTACs seen in these assays. Based on the Western blots, NC4 was able to induce degradation in half of the organs tested, however the levels of degradation achieved were higher than those observed in HaloPROTAC treated mice (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 2). Interestingly, in colon, thymus, skin, adrenal glands, and bone marrow, we observed significantly reduced NanoLuc luminescence upon NanoTAC treatment indicating effective tissue penetration but detected no corresponding decrease in reporter protein abundance by Western blot (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 2). This suggests that although the NanoTAC reached these tissues, it did not effectively induce protein degradation. Based on our initial findings that prolonged treatment enhanced degradation efficiency in WBCs (Supplementary Fig. 1b), we treated mice with the same degrader concentration (30 mg/kg) but increased the treatment duration to 24 h. We selected a representative panel of tissues and organs based on their responsiveness to degrader treatment and their relevance for therapeutic target validation. In tissues that had high levels of reporter degradation (liver, spleen, pancreas, kidney and the duodenum), the overall trend was an improvement in degradation, particularly in FV1 treated mice. The HaloPROTAC, HV2, showed mixed results, displaying a reduced level of degradation in the spleen and pancreas, but a maintained or slightly better level of degradation in the liver, kidney and duodenum (Fig. 2b). Almost complete inhibition of the NanoLuc signal was observed across a number of tissues at 24 h by luminescence (Fig. 2b) or Western blotting (Fig. 2c). Additional tissues and organs including skin, brain, heart, adrenal gland and skeletal muscle were selected based on their low degradation at 8 h, with the aim of determining whether extended treatment could enhance degradation in these less responsive tissues. FV1 and NC4 treated mice showed a clear improvement in reporter degradation at 24 h in the skin which at 8 h showed no degradation. FV1 also showed improved degradation in the heart and skeletal muscle by the 24 hour time point (Fig. 2d, e).

HV2 was the least effective degrader, with the highest degradation observed in the pancreas and kidney (~ 50-60% reporter reduction) at 8 h, which was notably reduced at 24 h in the pancreas (Fig. 2a, b). To better understand how the pharmacokinetics (PK) of HV2, as well as how the PK of the other compounds may relate to degradative capacity, we generated a PK profile for HV2 and NC4. When administered intraperitoneally (IP) at 2 or 10 mg/kg, the half-life of HV2 was more than 3 h shorter than that of NC4’s 4+ hour half-life (Supplementary Fig. 3, Tables 2 and 3), and markedly lower than the published half-lives of FC1 and FV1 which were 2.41 and 4.43 h, respectively at 10 mg/kg via IP administration11. To address whether the low activity of HV2 at 8 h was due to its short half-life, we treated mice with HV2 for shorter time points of 2 and 4 h, however no degradation was observed across the 10-tissue panel (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). These data show that dTAGs are the most effective degraders and that tissues with poor initial degradation can be improved over time. VHL and CRBN degrader effectiveness does not correlate with protein expression levels at a tissue level, but may inform on cell-type-specific degradation

Differences in degradation efficiency across each compound in our tissue panel led us to question whether the protein levels of the recruited E3 ligases, VHL and Cereblon, could influence degrader activity. Using publicly available mouse proteome data from Lu et al. 20, we extracted quantitative measurements of VHL and Cereblon expression in 24 tissues and organs that could be matched to those used in our study (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Certain tissues were separated into compartments or regions, e.g. the brain (brainstem, hippocampus, cerebellum) and eye (retina, cornea) (Supplementary Fig. 5a). For tissues that lacked an exact match, we extracted the closest available alternative. For example, to approximate the E3 levels in the axillary and brachial lymph nodes which we collected in our study, we used the mandibular, inguinal, popliteal and mesenteric lymph node data (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Representative heat maps show elevated VHL expression in the spleen, pancreas, duodenum, colon, thymus, lung, stomach, cornea, mandibular lymph node, and popliteal lymph node. Conversely, the kidney, brain, adrenal gland, retina, tongue and inguinal lymph node showed higher levels of Cereblon. Similar expression levels of both E3 ligases were observed in the jejunum, skin, heart, gastrocnemius muscle and mesenteric lymph node (Supplementary Fig. 5a).

Shifting our focus from whole tissues, we next examined whether degradation varied across distinct cell populations. As immune cells are diverse and can be isolated from many tissues, we collected the blood, spleen and bone marrow from reporter animals treated with 30 mg/kg of dTAGs for 24 h. Flow cytometry was used to identify four immune cell populations from these tissues: erythroid cells, B cells, T cells, and myeloid cells. GFP levels were gated based on GFP- (low), GFP+ (medium), and GFP++ (high) (Supplementary Fig. 5b), which varied between each cell type. Strikingly, FC1 showed more effective degradation over FV1 with fewer GFP++ cells following treatment (Fig. 3a). B cells were the most responsive to dTAG treatment, showing almost complete loss of the GFP reporter upon FC1 treatment, which was consistent across all immune cell types regardless of their tissue of origin (Fig. 3a). Using RNA-seq data available through the Immunological Genome Project (ImmGen.org21), we generated a heat map to compare Vhl and Cereblon expression across B cells, αβ T cells, monocyte cells, and granulocyte cells (Fig. 3b). In many of the B cell populations, particularly those from the Spleen (Sp), Cereblon expression is elevated compared to Vhl. The VHL-recruiting degrader is more durable than the CRBN-recruiting degrader in vivo

Since the dTAGs demonstrated the most effective and consistent activity across our tissue panel, we further characterised the durability of FC1 and FV1, specifically assessing how long each compound remained active in selected tissues following a single injection. To test this, reporter mice were intraperitoneally injected with either vehicle control, FC1 or FV1 at 30 mg/kg, and tissues were harvested at 24 h intervals for 4 days. Tissues and organs were extracted, lysed, and analysed for the remaining reporter levels using NanoLuc luminescence as a readout. At 24 h post-treatment, FV1 reduced the reporter to equal or lower levels than those observed with FC1 in all responsive tissues (Fig. 4a). A time-dependent, increase in reporter levels in the FC1-treated mice in tissues including the thymus, lung, liver, spleen, pancreas, kidney, stomach, colon and bone marrow, was visible, with most tissues returning close to untreated levels by the 96 hour time point (Fig. 4a). The reduction in reporter levels in FV1 treated mice was more sustained over 96 h, with most tissues maintaining reporter depletion after a single injection of FV1 (Fig. 4b). In some FV1 treated tissues, there is an increase in reporter levels at 48 h followed by a reduction at 96 h, likely due to variable reporter expression which is common in animal cohorts (Fig. 4b). Some tissues like the brain, eye, and tongue showed minimal to no reduction in luminescence regardless of the dTAG used which is consistent with previous results (Figs. 2a, d, e and 4a, b). In a panel of the least responsive tissues, including the brain, eye, and tongue, we tested whether intravenous (IV) administration of dTAGs could enhance degradation. At 2 mg/kg following an 8 hour treatment, both dTAGs were unable to induce degradation in any of the tissues that were unresponsive via IP administration (Supplementary Fig. 4c). FV1 was able to outperform FC1 in all responsive tissues, however the overall degradation observed following IV administration was either on par or less effective than that seen in IP treated mice at the same time point (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 4c). The dTAG system can effectively degrade the splicing protein, 65K, to identify potential on-target toxicity

Targeting a universal reporter protein was an excellent way to benchmark degrader activity across tissues, however, it was important to test whether the results in this system translated to a natural protein. The minor spliceosome-specific gene, Rnpc3, encodes the 65K protein that is required for the correct splicing of approximately 650 genes (0.35%) in the mouse genome that contain minor introns22. The expression of these minor intron-containing genes (MIGs) is enriched in highly proliferative cells during development and in stem cell compartments in mice, such as the intestinal epithelium23. Recently, inhibition of expression of minor splicing-specific components, including 65K and U6ATAC snRNA has been shown to restrict the growth of cells in several cell line and in vivo models of cancer24,25, including those driven by mutant KRAS in the liver and lung, and constitutive mutant Gp130 signalling in gastric cancer24. With the intention of evaluating the feasibility of developing a degrader against 65K, a 65K-Flag-FKBPF36V -/+ a GS linker construct was introduced into mouse dermal fibroblast (MDF) cells and treated with FV1 or FC1 for 5 h. At 500 nM, both degraders were able to almost completely reduce the levels of tagged-65K compared to a DMSO control, with FV1 slightly more effective than FC1 (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b).

We next generated a 65K-Flag-FKBPF36V knock-in mouse model to test whether 65K-Flag-FKBPF36V could also be degraded in vivo and evaluate the safety of degrading this target. We compared the degradation of 65K-Flag-FKBPF36V following a single dose of FV1 and FC1 treatment for 8 h and collected organs where 65K had either been implicated in disease, or in organs where toxicity is typically observed (Supplementary Fig. 6c). In line with previous results, FV1 was more effective than FC1 and induced almost complete degradation in all tissues except in the brain, where no degradation was observed from either degrader (Figs. 2a, d, e and 4a, b). To further test if FV1 could outperform FC1 more broadly, we generated a second knock-in mouse model that contained TRAF6-V5-FKBPF36V. When treated with 30 mg/kg of dTAGs for 8 h, we observed more robust degradation by FV1 in the liver, kidney, pancreas, colon, and stomach, compared with FC1 (Supplementary Fig. 7). In the lung and heart, similar degradation was observed for both dTAGs, and in skeletal muscle and the brain, minimal to no degradation was achieved (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Having established FV1 as the more potent degrader across two independent knock-in models, we next leveraged the 65K-Flag-FKBPF36V mouse to explore dose optimisation and potential safety liabilities. Since we predominantly used higher doses of degraders, we performed a dose titration of 5, 10, 20, and 35 mg/kg to assess whether any hook effect could occur in vivo with FV1 and if a more effective dose could be identified. Following a 24 h treatment, there was a dose-dependent improvement in 65K degradation in the liver and kidney (Supplementary Fig. 6d). Colon and thymus tissues showed optimal degradation at 20 and 35 mg/kg. In the pancreas, the best degradation was at 20 mg/kg, with 35 mg/kg remaining effective; there was minimal degradation in the stomach and skeletal muscle across all concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 6d). Overall, there was no prominent hook effect observed, so we continued to use the higher (20-35 mg/kg) FV1 doses. Next, we aimed to determine how long 65K reduction could be maintained following a single FV1 dose. In the brain, extended treatment did not result in any detectable 65K degradation (Fig. 5a). Similar outcomes were observed in skeletal muscle, where minimal degradation remained consistent across all time points. In contrast, 65K levels in the thymus and stomach showed peak degradation at 8 h post-treatment, followed by a gradual return at 16–24 h. To test whether 65K degradation could be safely sustained over a longer period, we treated the 65K-Flag-FKBPF36V mice with 20 mg/kg of FV1 or vehicle daily for up to two weeks. By day four, the mice showed signs of illness reaching their humane endpoint (Fig. 5c). To extend tolerability we titrated the dose down to 10, 5, and 2 mg/kg daily, and also trialled 2 mg/kg every second day. Post-mortem inspections identified significant abdominal oedema accompanied by a build-up of lymph fluid within the abdominal cavity of the FV1-treated 65K-Flag-FKBPF36V mice. Spleen weights at humane endpoints were reduced in mice where 65K was degraded compared to control mice (Fig. 5b). The longest survival recorded was 11 days post-treatment in the mice treated every second day with 2 mg/kg of FV1 (Fig. 5c). In contrast, control mice, either 65K-Flag-FKBPF36V mice treated with vehicle daily or wild-type (WT) mice treated with 5 mg/kg of FV1 daily, were able to survive for the 2 week period with no adverse effects (Fig. 5c).

Together, 65K degradation largely mirrors the patterns observed in the H-E-N-F reporter mice and TRAF6-V5-FKBPF36V mice, reinforcing FV1 as the most effective in vivo degrader. The prolonged and almost complete loss of 65K is poorly tolerated. Refined dosing strategies will be required to identify a therapeutic window with minimal on-target toxicity to support the development of 65K-directed degraders.

Discussion

To generate a resource detailing the use of tag-targeting degraders in vivo, we built upon our previous study by benchmarking the activity of dTAGs, HaloPROTACs and NanoTACs in mice. Shifting our universal reporter protein into a knock-in mouse model, we show that consistent with cell line data, dTAGs exhibited the best degradation activity across a panel of 20 tissues and organs, followed by NanoTACs and HaloPROTACs. Unlike our previously published cell line data, where HaloPROTACs and NanoTACs were on par, the outperformance of NanoTACs over HaloPROTACs in vivo demonstrates how the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME), or the PK of a drug, can alter its pharmacodynamics (PD). Notably, the half-life of NC4 is almost eight times longer than that of HV2 which may explain HV2’s minimal activity. Whether this is due to enhanced clearing or compound breakdown is unclear, however there are clear consequences on the body-wide degradation profile of HV2. One potential advantage of the HaloPROTAC system is that it can be used for selective degradation in the tissues where it does show activity (e.g. liver, kidney and pancreas) and may mitigate on-target toxicity. Interestingly, half-life is not always indicative of drug activity as illustrated by the similar half-lives of NC4 and FV1. NC4 is composed of a NanoLuc inhibitor as the target binder19. The conflicting Western blot and luminescence data suggests that while NC4 is an effective degrader in a subset of organs, it also distributes to tissues and organs where no degradation is observed. Cell line time course data showed similar findings, where NanoLuc inhibition occurred without accompanying degradation9. The lack of degradation could be explained by chemical instability due to metabolism or hydrolysis of the CRBN-recruiting glutarimide ring26 present in NC4, leading to a metabolite still able to effectively inhibit NanoLuc activity. If tissue-specific degradation, for example in the liver and kidney, is the desired outcome, both HaloTag and NanoLuc tags remain valuable tools, especially given the availability of tools that extend their utility far beyond degradation. Fewer tools exist for the FKBPF36V tag other than degraders, decreasing the system’s utility outside of the protein degradation space with this also being the case for newer tag-degrader systems. How these newer tag-degraders compare to the dTAG system in vivo would be interesting to test, particularly those with small tags like the 12.8 kDa BromoTag, or the 12 kDa SD40-tag which uses the molecular glue degrader, PT-17927, making them attractive systems that warrant further testing in vivo.

Effectively removing a disease-causing protein in a widespread manner has several advantages within the TPD drug discovery and target validation pipeline. Both dTAGs, FC1 and FV1, are active in most tissues tested, showing efficient degradation of the reporter protein regardless of its variable expression levels across the body, with the one exception being the brain. Yenerall et al17 highlighted similar findings when using FC1 (dTAG13) to degrade CDK2 and CDK5 in vivo, noting the likely inability of dTAGs to pass the blood-brain barrier. Direct delivery of dTAGs to the brain via intracranial injection has shown that dTAGs can degrade FKBPF36V-tagged Nelfb proteins, albeit in a localised manner, indicating that while dTAGs cannot pass the blood brain barrier, both dTAGs are functional in brain tissue18. Proteomic data by the Guo group shows that Cereblon levels are higher than VHL in the mouse brain, yet similar depletion levels of tagged Nelfb were observed regardless of which dTAG was administered18,20. This is consistent with the notion that given the catalytic nature of degrader technology, the engagement of the E3 and the ubiquitination of a target does not require large amounts of available E3 ligase to induce degradation28, however, the increased expression of an E3 ligase can improve degradation efficiency29. At the whole tissue level, higher E3 expression did not correlate with more effective degradation as observed across several tissues where only modest degradation could be achieved by both dTAGs, regardless of the treatment duration. The overall expression of VHL appears to be marginally higher than that of Cereblon and may help fine-tune degrader activity throughout the body. However, as evidenced by the skin, an organ where FV1 was able to facilitate stronger degradation than FC1 at 24 h, whole tissue expression alone does not capture the full picture. Nuances between E3 ligase expression and degrader activity are more pronounced at the single cell level as evidenced by the effectiveness of FC1 over FV1 in immune cell populations from the blood, spleen and bone marrow. What is interesting is how, under the same conditions, FV1 appears to outperform FC1 when looking at the whole tissue. Indeed, immune cells are not the only cell types in these tissues and perhaps the cell type specific degradation by FC1 and FV1 are masked by the degradation observed in other cell populations. Differences in cell-type-specific degradation have been observed in VHL-recruiting BCL-XL degraders that selectively target tumor cells but spare platelets28. Considering that most degraders recruit VHL or Cereblon, our findings give rationale for more diverse E3 characterisation and application. Identifying the expression and degradation profiles of each E3 may provide critical pathways for circumventing on-target toxicity by honing targeted protein degradation towards target cells, creating better degrader specificity.

Several biochemical and biophysical characteristics have been emphasised as important for the degradation capabilities of a compound, including cell permeability, cooperativity and stability of ternary complex formation, and ubiquitination, with ubiquitination acting as a prominent indicator of efficient degradation30. Lysine residue orientation on a neo-substrate in relation to the E2 and donor ubiquitin greatly influences target degradation31, one prominent example being BRD4 and its degrader, MZ132. Physical and biochemical interactions that occur during ternary complex formation are important for creating new interfaces to accommodate the optimal ternary structure33, so how does this occur when targeting a protein indirectly using tag-degraders? Although we did not encounter a target that could not be degraded using dTAG compounds, when using this system to assess how amenable a protein is to targeted protein degradation, it will be important to make this consideration, particularly with targets that show no or little degradation. Comparing the degradation profile of a physiologically relevant target protein and our reporter protein highlights how each protein may respond differently to degrader treatment. When targeting 65K, similar degradation to that of the reporter protein can be observed in the liver, kidney, and pancreas; however, in the skeletal muscle and the stomach, where our reporter protein is almost completely degraded over long periods of time, there is minimal degradation of 65K. In animals, the synthesis and turnover of a specific protein can occur at different rates between tissues and how quickly a specific protein is resynthesised may influence the level of degradation we can achieve34,35. Another factor to consider is tissue-specific deubiquitinase activity which can also have variable tissue expression, or perhaps provide a target protein with protection from degradation depending on its requirement in a specific tissue36. The exogenous nature of our reporter protein is an advantage in this context as it is untethered to any specific biological pathway, other than its natural protein turnover, providing unbiased insight when benchmarking degrader activity.

Between discovery biology and the therapeutic development of a drug, incorporating tag-degraders to bridge these two stages can help us assess 1.) How amenable a disease-causing protein is to TPD, 2.) Any on-target toxicity that may be associated with removing a target protein, and 3.) Whether removing that protein is efficacious in reducing disease burden. Together, this information can provide guidance on whether a protein is worth transitioning into a drug development campaign. By using our dataset as a guide for where tag-degraders have functionality, we can identify degradation characteristics that may be specific to a protein of interest such as turnover or regulatory mechanisms that may be context or tissue specific. As a proof-of-concept, we applied this technology to the target, 65K. While tagged 65K-FKBP was effectively degraded in vivo using dTAGs, the prolonged depletion of 65K led to observed toxicity in our model. These toxicities may reflect the loss of 65K itself, or potential effects of the vehicle or dTAG compounds in the context of 65K depletion. Given that IP administration may result in elevated dTAG concentrations in specific organs, this could disproportionately intensify toxicity due to 65K loss across tissues. How these mice tolerate dTAG treatment through more systemic delivery routes, such as intravenous administration, should be evaluated to better understand the therapeutic window for 65K targeting. Recapitulating disease and drug treatments using the dTAG system have demonstrated success in reducing disease and tumour burden. This is true across several types of disease models, including cell line xenograft models of DCAF5 in SMARCB1-mutant cancers37, GEMM models for KRASG12V-driven lung cancer38, and in patient-derived xenograft models of AML targeting MARCH5 in combination with venetoclax treatment39. Testing dTAGs and 65K depletion in disease-relevant models will be important to thoroughly assess the therapeutic potential of 65K as a degrader target.

Together, this work provides a practical framework for selecting and applying tag-degrader systems in vivo, enabling more informed and efficient therapeutic target validation.

Methods

Animal handling and use

All procedures for this study were approved by the Walter and Eliza Hall Institute (WEHI) Animal Ethics Committee, Australia (Ethics approval #: 2017.010, 2021.032, 2024.015, 2022.021). All research complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal testing and research. C57BL/6 J mice were maintained in-house under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Walter and Eliza Hall Institute of Medical Research (WEHI), Australia. Animal rooms were maintained at approximately 21 °C ± 3 °C at 40–70% humidity with a timed 14/10 hour light-dark cycle. Wild-type C57BL/6 J mice were bred at WEHI and/or obtained from WEHI animal supplies (Kew, Australia). None of the mice used in our experiments had been previously used for other procedures. Female and male mice were at least 6 weeks old at the time of experimentation.

Generation of mouse strains

The HaloTag-EGFP-NanoLuc-FKBPF36V KI/KI reporter mice, the RNPC3-Flag-FKBPF36V mice, and the TRAF6-V5-FKBPF36V mice were generated by the MAGEC laboratory (WEHI) on a C57BL/6 J background. For the reporter mice, the sgRNA sequence CTCCAGTCTTTCTAGAAGAT was used to create double-stranded breaks within the Rosa26 locus to stimulate homologous recombination and allow for the insertion of the reporter sequence (Supplementary Information SI1 for targeting vector sequence). For the RNPC3-Flag-FKBPF36V mice and the TRAF6-V5-FKBPF36V mice, the sgRNA sequences CCAAAACAGGATTCTAAAGA and CATCAGTACTTCGTGGCTGG, were respectively used to create double-stranded breaks within the C-terminal of the Rnpc3 or Traf6 locus to stimulate homologous recombination and allow for the insertion of the FKBPF36V sequence including a Flag or V5 tag (see Supplemental Information 2 and 3 for targeting vector sequences). In both cases, this was done by injecting 20 ng/μl of Cas9 mRNA, 10 ng/μl of the sgRNA, and 40 ng/μl of the targeting vector into the pronucleus of fertilized one-cell-stage embryos generated from wild-type C57BL/6 J breeders. 24 h later, two-cell stage embryos were transferred into the uteri of pseudo-pregnant female mice. Viable offspring were genotyped by next-generation sequencing. Targeted animals were backcrossed twice to wild-type C57BL/6 J to eliminate off-target mutations prior to homozygous strain generation.

Genotyping by PCR

Clips from H-E-N-F and RNPC3-FKBPF36V mice were taken at weaning (3 weeks, ear clips) and lysed in 100 μL DirectPCR lysis reagent (Vivagen Biotech, #102-T) supplemented with Proteinase K (1:1000, Sigma-Aldrich P4850) for 5 h to overnight at 55 °C with shaking. Proteinase K was heat-inactivated at 95 °C for 45 min. PCR reactions were performed using GoTaq Green (Promega, M712) in a master mix of 1 μL lysate, 10 μL 2x GoTaq, and 500 nM of each primer made up to 20 μL. DNA bands were separated using gel electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel containing SYBR safe (1:10,000; Invitrogen, S33102) at 90 V for 40 min and visualised on a ChemiDoc (BioRad).

Cell culture and maintenance

MDF and HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma), 50 U/mL penicillin (Gibco), and 50 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco). All cells were maintained at 37 °C with 10% CO2 in a humidified incubator.

Plasmid constructs and stable cell lines

The cDNA for mouse RNPC3 was cloned using InFusion (Takara Bio) from synthetic Gblock fragments (Integrated DNA Technologies) to generate fusion proteins harbouring a Flag-FKBPF36V tag at the C-terminus of RNPC3, with or without a 9-10 amino acid glycine-serine linker. Stable cell lines were generated using the Lentiviral packaging constructs (VSVg, RSV-Rev, pMDL) and the pTRE3G doxycycline inducible vector and selected with Puromycin (2 μg/mL). HEK293T cells expressing the inducible H-E-N-F reporter were previously generated in the Feltham Laboratory and all cell line degrader treatments using dTAG compounds were performed as previously described9.

Tag degraders

The compounds used in this study represent three distinct tag-degradation platforms - dTAG, HaloPROTAC, and NanoTAC, and target two different Cullin-RING ligase (CRL) complexes by recruiting either Cereblon (CRBN) or VHL. Specifically, FC1 (dTAG13) and FV1 (dTAGV−1) utilise the FKBP12F36V tag, with FC1 engaging the CRBN E3 ligase complex (CUL4CRBN) and FV1 recruiting the VHL complex (CUL2VHL). HV2 (HaloPROTAC-E) targets HaloTag7 and also recruits CUL2VHL. NC4 (NanoTAC4) is a NanoLuc-directed degrader that engages the CRBN E3 ligase via CUL4CRBN. In our previous study9, the dTAGV−1 benzene ring was in the ortho position whereas in this study, we used dTAGV−1 with a meta-positioned benzene ring. These ortho and meta dTAGV−1 compounds were compared in HEK293T cells to show similar degradation efficiency could be achieved prior to in vivo use (Supplementary Fig. 8). All dTAG and HaloPROTAC compounds were synthesised in-house by WEHI chemists or by the Ciulli Lab. NanoTAC compounds were synthesised by Promega.

In vivo degrader treatment

To treat mice with tag-degrader compounds, dTAGs, HaloPROTACs, and NanoTACs were reconstituted in a vehicle of 5% NMP in PEG400 (for H-E-N-F and TRAF6-V5-FKBPF36V mice) or 20% Kolliphor HS 15 (for Rnpc3-FKBPF36V mice) at the appropriate concentration to administer 100 μL of degrader at 2, 5, 10, 20, 30 or 35 mg/kg. Degraders and a vehicle control were administered to H-E-N-F, Rnpc3-FKBPF36V or TRAF6-V5-FKBPF36V mice (minimum 6 weeks old) via intraperitoneal (IP) injection before tissues and organs were harvested and snap frozen at the selected time points. Intravenously (IV) injected animals (minimum 6 weeks old) were injected at 2 mL/kg with either dTAG compounds reconstituted in a 10% Kolliphor HS 15 (Merck, 42966) in saline solution (w/v) or a vehicle-only control. Tissues and organs were snap frozen following collection.

Advia blood analysis

Whole blood was prepared by first separating the blood cells from the plasma through centrifugation at 500 x g for 5 min, then discarding the plasma. The remaining blood was run on the Advia 2120i Haematology System (Bayer HealthCare) and analysed for complete blood cell count (CBC), a WBC differential (DIFF), and a reticulocyte count (RETIC). Data were visualised and analysed using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.6.1).

Isolation of white blood cells from whole blood

Whole blood taken from degrader treated and untreated adult mice (minimum 6 weeks old) was centrifuged for 5 min at 500 x g to separate and remove plasma. The remaining blood cells were lysed using red cell lysis buffer (156 mM NH4Cl, 11.9 mM NaHCO3, 0.097 mM EDTA) at a ratio of 10 mL blood to 1 mL lysis buffer and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. WBCs were pelleted by centrifugation at 500 x g for 5 min and red blood cell lysates in the supernatant were discarded. The WBCs were then used for flow cytometry, Western blotting, or NanoLuc assay analysis.

Flow cytometry of white blood cell isolates

WBCs isolated from whole blood were resuspended in FACS buffer (1% BSA in PBS) and analysed on the CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter) for GFP levels following gating for single cells. All WBC samples were processed and analysed immediately following collection to minimise the chance of any residual degrader activity. Data were analysed using FlowJo software (Version 10.10.0) and visualised using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.6.1).

Flow cytometry analysis of immune cell populations

Single-cell suspensions from bone marrow and spleens were prepared in balanced salt solution (BSS-CS: 0.15 M NaCl, 4 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgSO4, 1 mM KH2PO4, 0.8 mM K2HPO4, and 15 mM HEPES supplemented with 2% [vol/vol] bovine calf serum). Analysis of blood was performed after erythrocyte lysis in buffered 156 mM NH4Cl. Staining was performed using fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies specific for murine antigens Ter119 (Ly-76, BD Biosciences; 1:50), Gr1 (Ly6G and Ly6C, BD Biosciences; 1:333), Mac1 (CD11b, BD Biosciences; 1:333), CD45R/B220 (RA3-6B2, BD Biosciences; 1:333), CD4 (GK1.5, BD Biosciences; 1:333), CD8a (53.6.7, BD Biosciences; 1:333). Cells were analysed using a FACSymphony™ A3 flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson) after antibody staining. Data were collected using BD FACSDiva v9.1 software, analysed through FlowJo software (Version 10.10.0), and visualised using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.6.1).

Protein preparation from tissue samples

Tissues and organs harvested from both degrader treated and untreated adult mice (minimum 6 weeks old) were placed in DISC lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 10% Glycerol, H20) at a ratio of 10 μL buffer per mg of tissue and homogenised using a stainless-steel bead (QIAGEN, #69989) and a TissueLyser II (QIAGEN) and allowed to incubate on ice for 1 hour. Lysates were centrifuged at 21,000 x g for 10 min and protein-containing supernatants were transferred into a fresh tube. Protein quantitation was performed using the Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific, #23225) as per the manufacturer’s instructions and normalised to 1 mg/mL. For SDS-PAGE samples, lysates were normalised in DISC buffer supplemented with 1x NuPAGE LDS sample buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 2% β-mercaptoethanol and heated at 70 °C for 10 min.

SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting

Tissue lysates were separated on 4-12% Bis-Tris gradient gels (Invitrogen) and transferred onto Immobilon-E polyvinyl difluoride membranes (Merck; IEVH85R). The protein loading between samples was visualised using Ponceau staining (Thermo Scientific, A40000279) before membrane blocking for 1 hour at room temperature using 5% skim milk (Devondale) in TBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBS-T). Membranes were washed three times in TBS-T for 5 min and probed using the appropriate primary antibody overnight (diluted 1:1000 in 5% BSA in TBS-T and 0.04% sodium azide, 4 °C). The primary antibodies used for immunoblotting were: HaloTag (Promega, G9211), FKBP12 (Invitrogen, PA1-026A), RNPC3/65K (Santa Cruz, sc-514951), V5 tag (Thermo Fisher, 46-0705), β-Actin-HRP (Santa Cruz, sc-47778) and GAPDH (Cell Signalling Technologies, 2118). The following day, membranes were washed three times in TBS-T for 5 min before an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Peroxidase-AffiniPure Goat anti-Mouse IgG H + L (Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs; Cat#: 115-035-003), Peroxidase-AffiniPure Goat anti-Rabbit IgG H + L (Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs; Cat#: 111-035-003)) was applied for 1 hour at room temperature (diluted in 5% skim milk in TBS-T; 1:10,000; Devondale). Three final 5-minute washes in TBS-T were performed before HRP activation using Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad; 17050661). Protein was visualised using a ChemiDoc (BioRad) and images were processed using the Image Lab software (Version 6.1, Bio-Rad).

NanoLuc assays

NanoLuc luminescence induction was performed by preparing a master mix of Nano-GLO reagent (Promega, N1110) at a substrate to buffer ratio of 1:50. Normalised protein lysates and the Nano-GLO master mix were added to a 384-well white-walled plate (Corning, 3765) at a ratio of 1:1 (10 μL of each), and the luminescence was detected using a CLARIOstar plus plate reader (BMG Labtech). Data were visualised and analysed using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.6.1).

Pharmacokinetic studies

Pharmacokinetic studies for HaloPROTAC-E and NanoTAC4 were conducted in male C57BL/6 mice exposed to either 2 or 10 mg/kg of the drug via intraperitoneal or intravenous injection. All experiments were conducted by the Centre for Drug Candidate Optimisation (CDCO) at the Monash Institute of Pharmaceutical Sciences.

Proteomics data evaluation

Mass spectrometry data were extracted from Lu et al. 20 and the protein intensity values, representative of the mean of summed peptides for VHL and CRBN, were plotted into a heat map. Data interpretation was based on the intensity of the summed peptide means where the protein levels were compared based on low protein levels (purple) versus high protein levels (yellow). Data were visualised using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.6.1).

RNA-sequencing data evaluation

RNA-sequencing data were extracted from Heng et al. 21 and the mean RNA expression values for Vhl and Cereblon, were plotted into a heat map. Data interpretation was based on the intensity of the Log2 values where the RNA expression levels were compared based on low transcript levels (purple) versus high transcript levels (yellow). Data were visualised using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.6.1).
