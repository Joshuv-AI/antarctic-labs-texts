Fibroblast Dysfunction and Immune Dysregulation Impair Palatal Wound Healing in Diabetic Rats
Authors: Li Pan, Hong-Ning Wang, Chao-Ming Hu, Jun Wang, Wen Tong, Jun-Xin Ren, Shu-Fan Zhao, Ping Ji, Yong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate how diabetes mellitus affects palatal wound healing, focusing on inflammatory responses, gene expression dynamics, and fibroblast function.MethodsA standardized full-thickness palatal wound model was established in streptozotocin-induced diabetic and non-diabetic rats. Wound healing progression was assessed via gross observation, histological analysis, and immunohistochemistry (CD3, IBA1) at postoperative days (PID) 0, 3, 7, and 14. Wound-edge tissues were collected at PID 0, 3, and 7 for RNA sequencing. The resulting transcriptomic data were analysed using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). Primary palatal fibroblasts were isolated from both groups and subjected to in vitro assays assessing proliferation, migration, and ATP production.ResultsDiabetic rats exhibited delayed wound closure, defective re-epithelialization, and diminished collagen matrix formation. Immunohistochemistry revealed a biphasic immune response in diabetic wounds, characterized by reduced CD3⁺ and IBA1⁺ cell infiltration at PID 3 and prolonged inflammation at PID 7 and 14. Transcriptomic profiling indicated pre-injury immune activation in diabetic tissues, with elevated inflammatory gene expression at PID 0. Post-injury, diabetes exacerbated tissue repair disruptions, marked by persistent immune activation and suppression of genes involved in extracellular matrix (ECM) organization and metabolic regulation. GSEA revealed marked alterations in oxidative phosphorylation during diabetic wound healing, reflecting mitochondrial dysregulation. WGCNA identified distinct co-expression modules associated with heightened inflammation, impaired ECM remodelling, and perturbed mitochondrial metabolism. Palatal fibroblasts derived from diabetic rats exhibited reduced proliferation, migration, and ATP production, indicating intrinsic functional impairments.ConclusionsDiabetes disrupts the temporal coordination of wound healing, leading to immune dysregulation, metabolic suppression, and fibroblast dysfunction – all of which underpin impaired palatal mucosal repair. These findings provide mechanistic insights and may inform the development of targeted therapies for diabetic wound care.

Introduction

Palatal masticatory mucosa is commonly selected as the donor site for harvesting autologous mucosal grafts, such as free gingival grafts (FGG), due to its favourable tissue thickness and abundant keratinized epithelium.1 This site is widely used not only in periodontal and peri-implant soft tissue reconstruction2 but also in various other medical fields, including otolaryngology, ophthalmology, dermatology, and plastic surgery.3, 4, 5 However, healing at the palatal donor site remains a significant clinical challenge due to delayed re-epithelialization, persistent discomfort, and risks of necrosis or infection.6,7 Optimizing wound healing at this site requires a deeper understanding of its unique biological characteristics, particularly in individuals with systemic conditions such as diabetes.

Diabetes mellitus, a long-standing metabolic disease characterized by chronic hyperglycemia,8 significantly impairs wound healing through multiple pathophysiological mechanisms.9,10 Notably, diabetes is the third most prevalent chronic disease in dentistry, with over 90% of patients with diabetes exhibiting oral manifestations.11,12 Delayed tissue repair poses a major clinical concern in diabetic patients.12, 13, 14 Although previous studies have examined how diabetes affects oral mucosal repair, the specific alterations in the local microenvironment and molecular events associated with palatal mucosal defect healing under diabetic conditions remain poorly understood.

Repair of oral and dermal tissues typically proceeds through well-defined stages: haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and matrix remodeling.15 In the initial inflammatory phase (24-48 hours post-injury), neutrophils are the earliest responders migrating into the damaged site, primarily to phagocytose invading microbes.16,17 Macrophages subsequently participate in tissue regeneration by clearing debris and releasing growth-promoting factors.18,19 This stage is driven by the activation and recruitment of various cell types, including fibroblasts, keratinocytes, and endothelial cells, under the influence of local growth factors.17,20,21

In diabetic conditions, wound healing is severely hindered by a constellation of pathological disturbances. Chronic hyperglycaemia promotes persistent inflammation, which impairs immune signalling, angiogenesis, cell migration and proliferation, collagen synthesis, and accelerates apoptosis.10,22, 23, 24 To clarify the molecular underpinnings of diabetic wound dysfunction, it is essential to dissect the transcriptomic and cellular abnormalities driving this aberrant healing trajectory. Although these mechanisms have been extensively examined in cutaneous models, their applicability to oral mucosal healing – especially within the palatal tissue – remains poorly understood.

To address these knowledge gaps, we established a standardized full-thickness palatal wound model in streptozotocin-induced diabetic and non-diabetic rats. An integrative approach combining histological analysis, immune cell profiling, and transcriptomic sequencing was employed to investigate the temporal dynamics of wound healing. Gene expression data from postoperative days (PID) 0, 3, and 7 were analysed using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to identify dysregulated pathways and regulatory networks. In parallel, primary palatal fibroblasts were isolated from both groups for in vitro assessment of proliferation, migration, and ATP production. This study aimed to elucidate the immune, metabolic, and structural mechanisms underlying delayed palatal wound repair in diabetes and to identify potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

Materials and methods

Diabetic rat model and palatal wounding

Sprague-Dawley rats (6 weeks old, male, 240-280 g) were housed in a certified animal facility. All procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Wenzhou Medical University (Approval No. wydw2022-0245) and conducted in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. To establish the type 1 diabetes model, rats were fasted for 12 hours and then given a single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ, 50 mg/kg; CAS 18883-66-4; Sigma-Aldrich, USA). STZ was chosen over alloxan due to its higher β-cell specificity, more consistent induction of hyperglycaemia, and lower systemic toxicity. Rats with plasma glucose levels > 16.7 mmol/L at 72 hours post-injection were considered diabetic (DM) rats. Control rats (CTRL) received an equivalent volume of buffer solution via the same route.25,26

One month after STZ administration, full-thickness palatal wounds were created under isoflurane anaesthesia. A mouth gag was used to keep the oral cavity open, and the mucosa was disinfected with iodine. A 3 mm circular full-thickness excisional wound was created on the hard palate using a sterile biopsy punch, as previously described.27, 28, 29 At PID 0, 3, 7, and 14, rats were anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized by intraperitoneal sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg; CAS 4390-16-3; Merck, Germany). Euthanasia was confirmed by the absence of heartbeat and respiration following loss of consciousness. Tissue samples were imaged under a stereomicroscope with a stainless steel ruler for scale calibration. Wound dimensions were measured with ImageJ software (NIH, USA), and healing dynamics were evaluated over time based on these measurements.

Histological analysis

Palatal tissue samples were harvested at PID 0, 3, 7, and 14 to evaluate wound healing. Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (pH 7.0) at 4 °C overnight, followed by decalcification in 10% EDTA (pH 7.2; Solarbio, China) for four weeks. After dehydration through a graded ethanol series (60%-100%) and clearing with xylene, tissues were embedded in paraffin. Paraffin blocks were sectioned at 4 μm thickness, with the wound area oriented along the mid-palatal suture. Sections corresponding to the widest part of the wound were selected for analysis. Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E, Beyotime, China) staining was performed to assess general histological architecture, and Masson's trichrome staining kit (G1340, Solarbio, China) was used to evaluate collagen deposition and extracellular matrix organization.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Tissue sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Samples were sectioned at 4 μm thickness. After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked, and sections were incubated in 5% donkey serum for 1 hour at room temperature. Primary antibodies against CD3 (Abcam, ab16669; dilution 1:200) and IBA1 (Abcam, ab178846; dilution 1:200) were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After PBS washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1 hour at room temperature. Staining was visualized with DAB chromogen, followed by haematoxylin counterstaining. Images were acquired using a brightfield microscope. DAB chromogen development was carefully monitored under the microscope to prevent over-staining, and negative controls without primary antibody were included to ensure staining specificity.

Bulk RNA sequencing and data analysis

Tissue samples (5 mm in diameter) were excised from the wound sites of diabetic and control rats for transcriptomic analysis. RNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing were performed by an external service provider (name withheld for peer review). mRNA libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA LT Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) by the same service provider. Read counts were computed using the HTSeq package (v0.9.1), in ‘union’ mode, to quantify gene expression. To normalize expression levels across genes and samples for visualization purposes, the FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) method was applied. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 R package (v1.32.0). Genes with a fold change > 2 and an adjusted P-value (FDR) < .05 were considered differentially expressed (DEGs). The false discovery rate (FDR) was calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to correct for multiple testing. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted using an online gene function annotation platform (name withheld for peer review).

GSEA was conducted in pre-ranked mode using GSEA software (v4.3.2; Broad Institute, USA). Genes were ranked based on log₂ fold change values obtained from DESeq2, and the input .rnk file was analysed against KEGG gene sets (c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt) from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). Pathways with a false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.25 were considered significantly enriched.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed based on DEGs between diabetic and control rats at each time point using the STRING database (https://string-db.org). Minimum interaction scores were set to 0.4 for PID 0, and 0.7 for PID 3 and PID 7 to balance network complexity and confidence. Visualization was performed in Cytoscape (v3.9.1), where node colour represented gene expression change (red: upregulated; blue: downregulated), node size was inversely proportional to the adjusted P-value, and edge thickness corresponded to STRING-derived interaction confidence.

WGCNA was conducted using the WGCNA R package (v1.70-3). Genes with low expression (FPKM < 1 in over 50% of samples) were filtered out prior to analysis. A soft-thresholding power of 18 was selected to achieve scale-free topology (R² > 0.85) while maintaining acceptable mean connectivity (see Supplementary Figure S2A). Gene modules were identified via hierarchical clustering based on the topological overlap matrix (TOM), followed by dynamic tree cutting. Correlations between module eigengenes and experimental traits were calculated to identify gene networks associated with diabetic status and wound healing stages. Functional annotation of key modules was performed through GO and KEGG enrichment analyses.

RNA-seq data were deposited to a public repository and will be made available upon manuscript acceptance.

Isolation of primary palatal fibroblasts

Primary fibroblasts were obtained from the palatal tissue of both diabetic and control rats using a previously described protocol.30, 31, 32 Tissues were rinsed in PBS and finely minced using ophthalmic scissors. The fragments were digested with 0.20% collagenase I at 37 °C for 30 minutes. Digestion was halted by adding DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The resulting cell suspension was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 minutes, the supernatant discarded, and the pellet resuspended in 1 mL of DMEM containing 10% FBS and antibiotics (Gibco, 15140122). Cells were cultured in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator at 37 °C, with medium changed every 3 days. After ∼20 days, fibroblasts were harvested and sub-cultured under identical conditions. Cells from passages 4 to 8 were used for further experiments.

Cell viability assay

Fibroblast viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, China). Cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 1000 cells per well, with 5 replicates per group. At designated time points, the culture medium was replaced with 10% (v/v) CCK-8 solution. Following a 1-hour incubation at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (SpectraMax M5, Molecular Devices, USA). Data were normalized to control values.

Migration assay

To evaluate fibroblast migratory capacity, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/cm2. A scratch was introduced using a 100 μL pipette tip across the centre of the confluent monolayer. After triple washing with PBS to remove debris, cells were incubated in serum-free medium at 37 °C. Images were captured at 0 and 24 hours post-scratch using an inverted fluorescence microscope.33

ATP production analysis

Cellular ATP content was quantified using the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Assay Kit (Promega, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Fibroblast cells were seeded into 96-well plates containing DMEM and cultured to confluence. Then, 100 μL of CellTiter-Glo reagent was added to each well. Plates were shaken gently for 2 minutes and incubated at room temperature for 10 minutes to stabilize the luminescent signal. Luminescence was measured using a microplate reader (SpectraMax M5, Molecular Devices, USA). Protein content was determined using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and ATP levels were normalized accordingly.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from a minimum of 3 independent experiments. Two-way repeated measures ANOVA was applied to evaluate changes in body weight, blood glucose levels, wound area over time, and immune cell populations. When significant interaction effects were observed, least squares post hoc tests were conducted (P < .05). Two-tailed unpaired t-tests were used to assess differences in fibroblast migration and ATP production between groups. A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Wound areas were quantified on postoperative PID 3, 7, and 14 to assess healing progression. On PID 3, no significant difference was observed between diabetic and control rats. However, by PID 7 and 14, wound closure was significantly impaired in diabetic animals compared to controls (Figure 1D-E). Histological evaluation via haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed that control rats exhibited earlier epithelial coverage and complete wound closure by PID 14, while diabetic rats displayed delayed re-epithelialization (Figure 1F).

To assess extracellular matrix remodelling, Masson's trichrome staining was performed. By PID 14, collagen fibres in the control group were densely arranged into thick bundles, indicative of healed tissue. In contrast, diabetic rats still showed visible ulceration and disorganized collagen architecture, suggesting incomplete repair (Figure 1G). These findings support the notion that diabetes substantially impairs palatal wound healing by delaying re-epithelialization and matrix remodelling.

Altered inflammatory cell infiltration in diabetic palatal wounds

To evaluate inflammatory cell infiltration during palatal wound healing, immunohistochemical staining for CD3⁺ T lymphocytes and IBA1⁺ macrophages was performed on wound tissues collected from DM and CTRL rats at PID 3, 7, and 14 (Figure 2A). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed significant differences in CD3⁺ T lymphocyte infiltration between DM and CTRL rats (Figure 2A, left). At PID 3, CTRL wounds exhibited stronger CD3 staining, reflecting higher T cell infiltration than DM wounds, indicating more robust early immune activation. By PID 7, CD3⁺ cells in CTRL wounds decreased substantially (P = .0061), while DM wounds showed a slight increase in T cell presence. For IBA1⁺ macrophages, CTRL wounds showed high infiltration at PID 3, which progressively declined by PID 7 and PID 14, reflecting typical inflammatory resolution. This disrupted immune timeline likely contributes to delayed inflammation resolution and impaired tissue repair in diabetic conditions.

Stage-specific transcriptomic dysregulation in diabetic palatal wound healing

To investigate transcriptomic alterations associated with delayed palatal wound healing under diabetic conditions, we performed RNA sequencing on wound-edge tissues collected from DM and CTRL rats at PID 0, 3, and 7. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering revealed distinct global expression profiles between DM and CTRL groups at each time point, highlighting diabetes-associated transcriptomic shifts (Figure 3A). Differential gene expression analysis showed that the greatest number of transcriptomic changes occurred at PID 3, with a total of 507 DEGs (274 upregulated, 233 downregulated). At PID 0 (uninjured), there were 363 DEGs (249 upregulated, 114 downregulated), reflecting baseline differences in the diabetic tissue. By PID 7, the number of DEGs decreased to 291 (181 upregulated, 110 downregulated), with downregulated gene counts at PID 7 similar to those at PID 0 (Figure 3B). To gain deeper insight into the biological significance of these transcriptional changes, we focused on representative genes involved in immune regulation and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodelling, as these alterations suggest impaired matrix deposition. At PID 0, several immune-related genes,including Cd28, Itgb2, Il18rap, and Cxcl6-were slightly elevated in DM wounds compared to CTRL, suggesting a pre-existing pro-inflammatory state in diabetic tissue prior to injury. By PID 3, CTRL wounds exhibited a more robust upregulation of these inflammatory mediators, indicating timely immune activation under normoglycemic conditions. In contrast, the DM group displayed a blunted response, with most immune-related genes remaining low or only modestly elevated. At PID 7, the expression of nearly all immune-related genes markedly increased in DM wounds, surpassing levels observed in CTRL, reflecting delayed but sustained immune activation and impaired resolution of inflammation (Figure 3C). Conversely, genes associated with ECM organization and tissue integrity, such as Col1a1, Col6a3, and Tnc, exhibited delayed and attenuated upregulation in the DM group (Figure 3D), resulting in consistently lower expression compared to controls. These changes point to insufficient matrix deposition and structural remodelling during healing. Such disruptions in ECM-related gene expression may contribute to the defective granulation and delayed re-epithelialization observed in diabetic wounds.

Volcano plots further illustrated the temporal dynamics of transcriptomic changes over time (Figure 3E). At PID 0, diabetic wounds exhibited prominent upregulation of immune regulators (Il18rap and Selplg) and downregulation of epithelial structural genes (multiple Krt family members). At PID 3, peak transcriptional activity was reflected in the strong upregulation of keratinocyte differentiation genes (Sprr1b and Serpinb2) alongside suppression of ECM components and matrix metalloproteinase regulators, such as Adamts8, Col5a2, and Col6a3. By PID 7, genes involved in leukocyte recruitment and sustained inflammation (Ccl7, Cxcl2, Trem1, and Nlrp12) remained upregulated, while key collagen genes (Col1a1 and Col4a1) continued to exhibit reduced expression. These results further corroborate the notion that diabetic wounds experience sustained inflammatory signalling alongside impaired matrix gene expression.

To identify gene expression signatures consistently altered during wound healing, we performed Venn analysis to detect overlapping DEGs at PID 0, 3, and 7. As illustrated in Supplementary Figure S1A, 9 genes were consistently altered between DM and CTRL wounds across all stages. One unannotated gene was excluded from downstream analyses. The remaining 8 annotated genes (Serpinb3, Nlrp12, Arhgap8, Mt-atp8, RGD1565355, Ssc5d, Tagln3, and Ppp2r2b) were visualized in a heatmap to illustrate their expression dynamics across groups (Supplementary Figure S1B). Notably, 5 genes (Serpinb3, Nlrp12, Arhgap8, Mt-atp8, and RGD1565355) were consistently upregulated in diabetic wounds across all time points. In contrast, Ssc5d was persistently downregulated, while Tagln3 and Ppp2r2b showed variable patterns with late-stage suppression. Functional annotations revealed that these genes are involved in immune and inflammatory regulation, mitochondrial energy metabolism, cytoskeletal remodelling, and protein dephosphorylation. The directional and stage-specific expression of these genes suggests their potential contribution to the delayed and dysregulated wound healing characteristic of diabetic conditions. These findings underscore their potential as candidate biomarkers or therapeutic targets in diabetic wound healing.

Collectively, these transcriptomic findings reveal a diabetes-associated, stage-specific dysregulation of wound healing programs, characterized by persistent immune activation, delayed resolution of inflammation, and compromised ECM remodelling. These alterations likely underlie the observed impairment in palatal wound closure under diabetic conditions.

Enrichment and network analyses identify dysregulated immune and matrix remodelling pathways in diabetic wounds

To elucidate the functional consequences of transcriptomic alterations in diabetic wound healing, enrichment analyses based on GO and KEGG were performed using DEGs identified in palatal wound tissues at PID 0, 3, and 7. At PID 0, diabetic wounds were enriched in pathways associated with immune activity, including leukocyte recruitment and cytokine-mediated signalling, whereas pathways essential for maintaining epithelial structure, such as keratin filament assembly and cytoskeletal organization, were suppressed. By PID 3, DEGs in diabetic wounds revealed a shift toward metabolic dysregulation, characterized by the upregulation of lipid metabolism and protease inhibition genes, and downregulation of pathways involved in ECM organization and tissue development. To identify key gene modules underlying these functional changes, PPI networks of DEGs were constructed at each time point (Supplementary Figure S1D-F). At PID 0, the network was primarily composed of a tightly connected module of immune-metabolic regulators, including Plin1, Cidec, Pck1, and Adipoq, all of which were downregulated and involved in lipid droplet regulation and energy metabolism. Additionally, several keratin and epithelial structural genes, such as Krt79, Krt84, and Hoxc13, were also suppressed, indicating early epithelial disorganization. At PID 3, the network transitioned toward modules enriched in growth factor and immune signalling, with hub genes including Igf1, Tgfa, and Csf1r. Upregulation of Igf1 and Tgfa, combined with the reduced expression of Csf1r and angiogenesis-related genes such as Pgf, Nrp2, and Plxnd1, indicated an imbalanced immune-vascular environment. By PID 7, the network became increasingly centered around ECM components and remodelling enzymes, including Mmp13, Mmp3, Dcn, and Col1a1. Concurrent upregulation of matrix-degrading enzymes and downregulation of collagen isoforms reflected a persistent disruption of tissue remodelling. Notably, pro-inflammatory mediators such as Trem1, Cebpb, and Fcgr2b also remained highly expressed at PID 7, highlighting sustained immune activation that may contribute to delayed repair.

Together, these findings reveal a complex, stage-specific landscape of transcriptomic dysregulation in diabetic palatal wounds. Inflammatory pathways were persistently activated, while structural and metabolic programs required for regeneration were suppressed or delayed. Importantly, the dysregulated temporal pattern of oxidative phosphorylation highlights a potential mitochondrial basis for impaired wound healing in diabetes.

These cellular defects likely impair ECM deposition and wound closure in diabetic palates, underscoring the role of intrinsic cellular dysfunction and mitochondrial dysregulation in the pathogenesis of diabetic wound healing disorders.

Discussion

Wound healing is a highly coordinated biological process encompassing immune activation, extracellular matrix remodelling, and cellular activities such as proliferation, migration, and metabolism.36,37 Diabetes, a chronic metabolic disorder, disrupts these key processes, leading to delayed and compromised wound healing.38 The palatal mucosa, routinely used as a donor site in FGG procedures, serves as a clinically relevant model for investigating the effects of diabetes on oral wound healing. Despite its clinical importance, the precise cellular and molecular mechanisms by which diabetes impairs palatal wound healing remain poorly defined. To address this gap, we characterized both transcriptional and functional alterations in diabetic palatal wounds. Our findings offer mechanistic insights that may guide the development of targeted therapies to enhance wound healing in diabetic patients.

This study employed a multi-modal approach-including histological evaluation, RNA-sequencing, gene network analysis, and cellular assays, to comprehensively investigate the impact of diabetes on palatal wound healing. Our findings reveal that diabetes significantly alters the wound healing trajectory of palatal mucosa, providing mechanistic insights into impaired oral tissue repair under diabetic conditions. Specifically, diabetic wounds exhibited attenuated early immune activation, sustained inflammation, impaired ECM remodelling, and fibroblast dysfunction. These pathological features were consistently identified across histological and immunohistochemical staining, GSEA-based transcriptomic profiling, and functional assays of primary fibroblasts. Collectively, the data suggest that chronic hyperglycaemia fosters an immunologically and metabolically dysregulated microenvironment, ultimately impairing wound resolution across multiple biological levels.

Effective wound healing depends on a timely inflammatory phase that facilitates debris clearance and microbial control, followed by the resolution of inflammation.39,40 In diabetic palatal wounds, however, we observed a dysregulated pattern of immune activation. At day 3 post-injury, diabetic wounds exhibited significantly reduced infiltration of immune cells, particularly CD3⁺ T lymphocytes and IBA1⁺ macrophages, compared to non-diabetic controls. This attenuated response likely reflects impaired immune cell recruitment or activation, potentially resulting from hyperglycaemia-induced disruption of chemokine signalling. Previous studies have similarly reported reduced monocyte and macrophage influx in diabetic wounds as a result of compromised chemotaxis.41,42 Our transcriptomic data corroborated these findings, revealing blunted early activation of innate immune response pathways in diabetic wounds. Such insufficient early inflammation may compromise debris clearance and delay the transition to the proliferative phase.43,44

Paradoxically, diabetic wounds exhibited a protracted inflammatory state during later healing stages. At day 7 and beyond, diabetic palatal wounds exhibited persistent T cell and macrophage infiltration, in contrast to resolved inflammation in controls. Transcriptomic and pathway analyses further revealed prolonged upregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators, including IL-17 and TNF signalling pathways, in diabetic wounds. This failure to terminate the inflammatory phase aligns with the well-documented chronic inflammatory milieu characteristic of diabetic wound pathology.45 This persistent immune activation likely contributes to impaired tissue regeneration and the transition to a chronic wound state.

Together, these findings underscore a temporally dysregulated immune response in diabetes, characterized by insufficient early activation followed by chronic, unresolved inflammation. This dysregulation may initiate a pathological feedback loop that impairs tissue regeneration. Therapeutic strategies may need to both enhance early immune cell recruitment and facilitate timely resolution, for instance, by promoting neutrophil and monocyte chemotaxis in the early phase and implementing pro-resolving or macrophage-reprogramming approaches during later stages.

The healing of the palatal mucosa relies heavily on robust connective tissue remodeling.7,46 Beyond immune dysregulation, our study revealed marked impairments in ECM deposition and fibroblast function under diabetic conditions. Histologically, diabetic palatal wounds exhibited delayed re-epithelialization and disorganized granulation tissue. Masson’s trichrome staining further demonstrated reduced and irregular collagen deposition compared to controls. Transcriptomic analyses corroborated these findings, showing downregulation of key genes involved in ECM structure and remodelling. By day 7, diabetic wounds displayed significantly reduced expression of collagen isoforms (eg, Col1a1, Col3a1, Col6a3) and matrix-associated proteins, along with suppression of cell-ECM interaction pathways, including focal adhesion and integrin signalling. These results align with prior clinical and preclinical evidence that diabetic wounds exhibit a remodelling deficit, where the balance between matrix synthesis and degradation is disrupted, resulting in diminished collagen accumulation within the healing tissue.47,48 This impairment in matrix deposition has been linked to intrinsic fibroblast dysfunction, among other factors. As primary ECM-producing cells, fibroblasts play a pivotal role in orchestrating connective tissue repair,49,50 and in our diabetic model, their compromised function was clearly evident.

To evaluate fibroblast function, we conducted in vitro assays, which demonstrated that fibroblasts isolated under diabetic conditions exhibited markedly reduced migratory capacity and decreased ATP production compared to controls. These deficiencies may directly impair wound closure, as fibroblasts with impaired migration and reduced bioenergetic capacity are less effective at repopulating the wound bed and depositing new matrix. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that fibroblasts derived from diabetic animals or exposed to high-glucose conditions exhibit intrinsic migratory defects and reduced wound-healing capacity.51,52 In our study, gene set enrichment analysis revealed a significant upregulation of oxidative phosphorylation in diabetic wounds by day 7 compared to controls, suggesting altered cellular metabolic states. This enrichment may reflect a compensatory response to bioenergetic stress – diabetic wound cells may attempt to upregulate mitochondrial ATP production to meet healing demands, yet paradoxically, our fibroblast assays revealed reduced actual ATP output. This disparity may indicate inefficient energy utilization or mitochondrial dysfunction despite elevated oxidative metabolism gene expression. High-glucose–induced oxidative stress is known to impair mitochondrial efficiency,53 suggesting that fibroblasts in diabetic wounds likely face an energy production challenge. These metabolic disturbances may further compromise fibroblast-mediated synthesis of collagen and other matrix components, thereby exacerbating defects in tissue repair.

These findings were further corroborated by WGCNA, which identified diabetes-specific disruptions in key co-expression modules. Specifically, an immune-associated module (magenta) encompassed cytokines and chemokines; a structural module (blue) included collagens and ECM regulators; and a metabolic module (turquoise) featured genes involved in mitochondrial function and energy metabolism. The coordinated dysregulation of these modules suggests that diabetes impairs wound healing not through isolated pathways, but through a systems-level failure involving both immune and metabolic networks. This integrative perspective provides a unifying framework for understanding the cellular, molecular, and functional impairments observed in diabetic palatal wounds.

While this study offers a comprehensive perspective on how diabetes alters oral wound healing dynamics, several limitations warrant consideration. First, our analyses were performed at discrete postoperative time points – days 0, 3, and 7 for transcriptomic profiling, and days 3, 7, and 14 for histological and immunohistochemical assessments – which may have missed transient events occurring between intervals. For instance, early neutrophil infiltration or transitional phases of macrophage polarization may not have been fully captured. Second, while we performed qPCR validation for selected genes (Il6, Mmp3, Col1a1, Ndufa9) and confirmed protein-level expression of COL1A1 via immunohistochemistry, further validation (eg, additional protein assays or broader target verification) in wound tissue samples was limited due to sample availability and ethical considerations. Therefore, the expression changes of certain genes should still be interpreted with caution, and further investigation is needed to fully determine their in vivo relevance. Third, although our analyses focused on immune cells and fibroblasts, other key processes-such as angiogenesis-were not directly evaluated, despite their well-established roles in tissue repair. In addition, although the rat model offers a controlled and reproducible platform for studying diabetic wound healing, species-specific differences may limit direct translational applicability to humans. Sample size and inter-individual variability also warrant attention, and larger cohorts would help improve the generalizability of our findings. Despite these limitations, our findings provide valuable mechanistic insights and lay a foundation for future studies incorporating denser time-point sampling, molecular validation, and expanded functional assessments to further elucidate the complex biology of diabetic oral wound healing.

Conclusions

Palatal wound healing is profoundly impaired in diabetes due to multifactorial disruption of immune regulation, ECM remodelling, and fibroblast metabolism. Our integrated findings reveal dysregulated inflammation, marked by delayed early immune activation and sustained IL-17- and TNF-driven chronic inflammation. In parallel, fibroblast dysfunction and mitochondrial inefficiency compromise ECM deposition and cellular energy supply. These interconnected defects highlight key therapeutic targets, including immune modulation, matrix support, and metabolic correction. A deeper understanding of the temporal dynamics of immunometabolic imbalance may enable phase-specific, multi-pronged strategies to promote effective oral wound healing in diabetic patients.
