Concurrence of the Core Mechanistic Tenets of the American Heart Association Statement on the Cardiovascular–Kidney–Metabolic Syndrome and the Adipokine Hypothesis of Heart Failure With Preserved Ejection Fraction
Authors: Milton Packer
Published: 2026

Introduction

Over the past 10–15 years, it has become increasingly recognized that prolonged expansion of visceral fat mass leads to transformative changes in its biology, which promotes the secretion of pro‐inflammatory and pro‐fibrotic molecules—known as adipokines—that can exert deleterious effects on the heart, vasculature, kidney, liver and skeletal muscle. The accumulation of dysfunctional fat underlies many of the critically important risk factors for cardiovascular disease (including hypertension, insulin resistance, diabetes and chronic kidney disease) [1], and it precedes and predicts the development of heart failure (particularly, heart failure with a preserved ejection fraction [HFpEF]) [2]. However, until recently, formal scientific statements have not focused on the central causal role of dysfunctional fat in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular disorders.

The American Heart Association Scientific Statement on the Cardiovascular–Kidney–Metabolic Syndrome

In 2023, the American Heart Association (in a Presidential Advisory and Scientific Statement) defined a new clinical entity—the “cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome”—as a systemic disorder characterized by pathophysiological interactions among metabolic risk factors, the cardiovascular system and the kidney, leading to multiorgan dysfunction and a high rate of adverse cardiovascular outcomes [1].

According to the AHA statements, the CKM syndrome most commonly originates from excess or dysfunctional adipose tissue. Its earliest clinical sign is the presence of abdominal obesity, defined by sex‐specific thresholds for waist circumference or by a waist‐to‐height ratio ≥ 0.5. Patients with central adiposity with no evidence of metabolic abnormalities or organ dysfunction or injury are considered to have Stage 1 CKM [1].

The AHA statements specifically link adiposity with the development of HFpEF, and HFpEF represents the advanced stage (Stage 4) of the CKM syndrome for many patients. Among the range of CKM disorders that may be driven by excess adiposity, obesity is more strongly related to incident heart failure than to incident myocardial infarction or stroke [3, 4]. Bariatric surgery has a greater effect to reduce the development of heart failure than of other cardiovascular disorders [5]. Interestingly, the importance of HFpEF as an outcome in cohorts with excess adiposity has likely been underestimated, since conventional echocardiographic criteria for the identification of HFpEF are insensitive to the presence of heightened left ventricular filling pressures [6].

According to the AHA statements, dysfunctional visceral adipose tissue secretes pro‐inflammatory and pro‐oxidative products (identified as adipokines) that damage arterial, cardiac and kidney tissues [1]. When released into the systemic circulation or acting in a paracrine manner, these maladaptive mediators exacerbate pathophysiological processes involved in atherosclerosis and myocardial injury (including HFpEF); in kidney tubular inflammation and kidney fibrosis; and in the development of metabolic risk factors.

The AHA statements recognize that the common comorbidities of HFpEF are the downstream consequences of excess or dysfunctional adipose tissue [1]. Specifically, they note that most instances of hypertension are related to adiposity or other metabolic risk factors [1]. Furthermore, insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes are described as being almost entirely downstream of excess or dysfunctional adipose tissue, and excess and dysfunctional adipose tissue also contribute to chronic kidney disease [1].

The AHA statements specifically recognize the role of “metabo‐inflammation”—a chronic low‐grade systemic inflammatory response that is triggered by metabolic signals, particularly excess adiposity—in the pathogenesis of HFpEF. Metabolic therapies—such as glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists and sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors—can ameliorate adiposity and reduce the risk of developing heart failure in large‐scale randomized trials [1]. However, the AHA statements do not identify specific molecular signalling mechanisms that are secreted from dysfunctional fat and might lead to HFpEF or accelerate its clinical progression.

The Adipokine Hypothesis of Heart Failure With Preserved Ejection Fraction

In the 1980s, HFpEF was regarded as a myocardial disorder where ventricular hypertrophy (typically related to long‐standing uncontrolled hypertension) leads to an inability of the left ventricular chamber to fill without an excessive increase in left ventricular filling pressure [7]. However, over the past 4 decades, coincident with the global epidemic of obesity, an expansion of fat mass is now the leading cause of HFpEF; 50%–60% of patients enrolled in international trials of HFpEF have a body mass index ≥ 30 kg/m2, and 90%–95% have central adiposity, with a waist‐to‐height ratio ≥ 0.5 [8, 9].

Mendelian randomization studies have confirmed a strong link between an expanded visceral fat mass and the development of HFpEF, with adiposity (rather than hypertension) having a stronger relationship to HFpEF [10, 11, 12, 13]. Visceral adiposity precedes and predicts the development of HFpEF, but not of heart failure with a reduced ejection fraction [2]. The presence of excess adiposity and abdominal obesity is associated with greater hemodynamic and clinical severity of HFpEF, heightened systemic inflammation and a higher risk of cardiovascular death and hospitalizations for heart failure [14]. Visceral adiposity also accounts for the comorbidities commonly associated with HFpEF, including insulin resistance, diabetes, hypertension and chronic kidney disease [13, 15, 16].

Why does excess visceral adiposity promote the development and progression of HFpEF? The adipokine hypothesis of HFpEF proposes that the expansion of visceral fat is accompanied by its biological transformation into a state of dysfunctional fat [17]. In healthy lean people, adipose tissue exerts cytoprotective and nurturing effects, which are mediated by the secretion of cardioprotective and renoprotective signalling molecules (i.e., adipokines) that maintain insulin sensitivity and sodium balance. In contrast, in patients with visceral adiposity and obesity, the biological features of adipose tissue become dysfunctional, and dysfunctional fat secretes adipokines that promote hypertrophy, inflammation and fibrosis in the heart, vasculature and kidney and that lead to insulin resistance and renal sodium retention. At the same time, dysfunctional fat loses its ability to secrete cardioprotective, anti‐inflammatory and natriuretic adipokines [17].

In contrast with the CKM statements, the adipokine hypothesis identifies specific molecular signals that exert beneficial and deleterious functions [17]. Cytoprotective adipokines (e.g., adiponectin) are suppressed in patients with obesity or in those with clinical HFpEF, whereas pro‐inflammatory adipokines (e.g., retinol binding protein 4, lipocalin‐2, autotaxin, activin A) are enhanced in patients with obesity and in those with clinical HFpEF, in proportion to the severity of the disease and presaging a poor prognosis. These findings are not merely epidemiological associations. Experimentally, a high‐fat diet (in concert with other accelerants) causes the phenotypic features of HFpEF together with the selectively enhanced expression of pro‐inflammatory adipokines in adipose tissue. When the expression of an individual pro‐inflammatory adipokine is experimentally silenced only in adipose tissue—but not in the heart or kidney—circulating levels decline, and the phenotypic features of HFpEF (i.e., hypertrophy and fibrosis) are alleviated [17, 18]. The secretion of pro‐inflammatory adipokines can also cause insulin resistance and diabetes, vascular stiffness and hypertension and chronic kidney disease [13, 15, 16].

Importantly, the adipokine hypothesis aligns with current treatments for HFpEF. Large‐scale trials of HFpEF have primarily enrolled patients with central adiposity, and they have specifically excluded HFpEF mimics, that is, cardiac amyloidosis, hypertrophic and infiltrative cardiomyopathies, valvular heart disease and pericardial disorders [19]. Enrolled patients benefitted from treatment with GLP‐1 receptor agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, which can act on adipocytes to reduce their dysfunctional state and restore a healthy balance of secreted adipokines [17]. The contribution of this adipose‐centric effect to the totality of the clinical benefits is unknown, since these drugs also exert direct effects on the heart, vasculature and kidney. Although it is commonly assumed that HFpEF is a heterogeneous disorder, the current treatments for HFpEF exert broad‐based efficacy, without subgroup differences—a finding consistent with mechanistic homogeneity despite phenotypic diversity.

Concurrence of the Core Mechanistic Tenets of the CKM Syndrome and Adipokine Hypothesis Frameworks

There is considerable concurrence of the core mechanistic tenets of the CKM syndrome and the adipokine hypothesis of HFpEF (Table 1). Both conceptual frameworks focus on abdominal adiposity (assessed by waist circumference) and not on obesity (assessed by body mass index), as the earliest clinical evidence of excess visceral adipose tissue mass or dysfunctional fat. Body mass index is influenced by skeletal muscle and bone mass and is not sensitive to the distribution of adipose tissue, and thus, it represents a poor metric of adipose‐centered biological derangements. Both frameworks identify dysfunctional fat (and its secretion of pro‐inflammatory adipokines) as the upstream causal mechanism of both HFpEF as well as its commonly associated comorbidities, including insulin resistance and diabetes; hypertension and arterial stiffness and renal sodium retention and chronic kidney disease [1, 13, 15, 16]. Both frameworks envision HFpEF as representing a common cardiac manifestation of the prolonged effects of dysfunctional fat and adipokine imbalance on the heart, vasculature and kidneys; the combined effects of cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis, vascular stiffness and hypertension, and renal sodium retention lead to HFpEF. Both frameworks consider the use of GLP‐1 receptor agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists as core treatments to prevent the end‐organ consequences of dysfunctional adipose tissue.

Both frameworks propose that dysfunctional fat and its secretory products should be targeted by future drug development [1, 17]. Modulators of specific adipokines have been developed [17] and can be evaluated for the treatment of the downstream consequences of the CKM syndrome, including novel approaches to the management of adiposity‐related hypertension, insulin resistance, chronic kidney disease and HFpEF. Given the global epidemic of obesity and the challenges in sustaining meaningful weight loss, drugs that transform biologically deranged into metabolically healthy adipose tissue deserve a serious commitment to future exploration and development.
