Molecular de-extinction looks to the past to find the molecules of the future
Authors: David Adam
Published: 2026

In a Philadelphia laboratory, a molecule that last circulated through the bloodstream of a woolly mammoth tens of thousands of years ago is killing bacteria again. The molecule, a short chain of amino acids, was resurrected by digital analysis of the mammoth DNA as part of a search for new antibiotics. The work is one example of a fledgling scientific enterprise that looks to extinct animals for a reservoir of biochemical novelty.

The technique is called molecular de-extinction. The researchers involved are looking to reconstruct the lost species’ individual proteins and smaller protein-like molecules called peptides. Encoded in the ancient genomes of legendary lost creatures, including the giant land sloth and extinct hominin lineages, these molecules are being rebuilt and tested for useful biological activity. The approach has moved from idea to research program, propelled by advances in ancient DNA sequencing, synthetic chemistry, and, most crucially, machine learning.

“The beautiful thing about science is that a lot of those data are available in databases and they’re public,” says César de la Fuente, a computational biologist at the University of Pennsylvania and a pioneer of using de-extinction to derive useful molecules. “We’ve taken full advantage of that. We’ve developed algorithms to go in there and mine all that biological data to find these functional molecules.” Several of these recovered ancient antimicrobials have shown promising results in mice. The crux of the idea: Evolution spent eons honing these molecules, and some might be of use, even if they no longer exist naturally. But while no one in the field is trying to resurrect animals, working on engineered molecules raises ethical questions about ownership and our relationship with nature.

Genes Raised from the Dead

Molecular de-extinction, de la Fuente says, is a classic example of a scientific field building on what has gone before. Specifically, it exploits digital repositories of ancient DNA sequences and the genes therein and parallel efforts to predict the proteins these genes would have coded for. This research—itself a revolution in genome analysis and bioinformatics—has flourished in the last decade or so, buoyed by a boost in computing power.

It can’t come soon enough for antibiotic discovery. Given rising levels of antibiotic resistance and replacement drugs being hard to find, there’s a growing movement to seek any and all alternative ways to source antibiotics—from AI and repurposed drugs to antimicrobial peptides such as human defensins.

Hence, analysis of ancient DNA. By the mid-1990s, researchers had shown that they could extract scraps of genetic material from mammoth remains frozen in the Siberian permafrost (1). But it took another decade to develop tools that could assemble and sequence fragments of nuclear DNA and to compare these genome sequences to those of living elephant relatives to identify which regions were unique to the mammoths. The information wasn’t perfect—incomplete and fragmented genomes inevitably introduce errors—but it gave researchers material to work with.

A 2004 review article by Joseph Thornton, a human geneticist now at the University of Chicago, pointed out that these techniques could now “raise a gene from the dead”—by working out its likely sequence from related species, making it, putting it into cells, and having the cells express it (2). Researchers were using this strategy to address evolutionary questions, such as what amino acid changes in the eye’s light-sensitive proteins contributed to development of color vision (3).

De la Fuente’s group was among the first to realize that the strategy could have practical applications (4). “The idea came from a simple question: If evolution has already explored enormous areas of molecular space over millions of years, including in organisms that no longer exist, could some of those lost molecules have useful therapeutic properties today?” he says. “As ancient genomic and proteomic data became more accessible, and AI methods became powerful enough to search them systematically, it became possible to test that idea rigorously.”

The expansion of next-generation sequencing in the 2010s gave researchers more options to do this. Entire genomes from the extinct human relatives Neanderthals and Denisovans, as well as numerous Ice Age mammals, entered public databases. Annotation of these sequences highlighted the coding regions. And by the 2020s, researchers had the digital tools to work out which proteins these sequences would encode. It became possible to take the strings of amino acids corresponding to codon sequences and predict how these would fold into three-dimensional structures. Millions of predicted proteins could now be cataloged. But the only reliable way to work out their function was to compare them to known proteins.

That wasn’t yet possible on a large scale. Even if scientists had the sequence and structure, they still “wouldn’t know if they contained an antimicrobial peptide,” says Jonathan Heddle, a biochemist at Durham University in the United Kingdom.

The final and critical step toward making molecular de-extinction a reality was finding a way to determine likely functions of de-extinct proteins for cases where scientists lacked that basis for comparison. Machine learning offers researchers a way to efficiently mine this vast sequence space for useful properties. Algorithms trained on experimentally characterized proteins can recognize amino acid patterns associated with particular functions, such as antimicrobial activity or structural stability.

So, instead of manually inspecting sequences and corresponding proteins for plausible features, scientists can use AI tools to rank candidates according to predicted biological potential and focus laboratory resources on the most promising examples. “You can find hidden functional peptides within extinct proteins,” Heddle says.

In 2023, de la Fuente’s team used AI to demonstrate that such functional molecules resurrected from extinct human relatives could, at least in principle, retain therapeutic potential. Using a model called panCleave to predict proteolytic cleavage sites, the researchers identified short peptide fragments embedded within proteins from modern humans as well as Neanderthals and Denisovans (5). Some of these fragments no longer exist as functional molecules in living humans.

In vitro tests of selected synthesized peptides showed antimicrobial activity against clinically relevant bacterial pathogens, including multi-drug-resistant strains. The most active peptides disrupted bacterial membranes, a mechanism associated with broad-spectrum activity and a lower propensity for resistance compared with conventional antibiotics. In trials in mice infected with bacteria—under the skin and in thigh muscle—the archaic peptides could reduce bacterial burden in both locations.

A year later, the team repeated the approach on a more ambitious scale. Using a deep-learning pattern-recognition model trained on large datasets of known antimicrobial peptides to learn what “antibiotic-like” sequences look like, the team analyzed predicted proteins from more than 200 extinct species, generating millions of candidate peptide fragments (6). The algorithm identified tens of thousands as likely to possess antimicrobial activity. When a small subset was synthesized and tested, many inhibited bacterial growth in vitro, and some reduced infection in mouse models. Several peptides originated from well-known extinct mammals such as woolly mammoths, giant sloths, and ancient sea cows—but were not in sequences of any cataloged extant species.

“We mined all of the proteomic data from ancient biology”, de la Fuente says. “So that enabled us to really scale up from just ancient hominids to all ancestral biological data.”

Dataset Deep Dive

More recent extinctions are also bearing fruit. In collaboration with University of Brasilia biologist Octávio Luiz Franco, de la Fuente’s group used algorithms to search DNA datasets from extinct and endangered species for those coding for characteristic strings of six cysteine residues. These are motifs of small, antimicrobial peptides called β-defensins produced by animals (including humans), plants, and some fungi as part of the innate immune system. The defensin molecules act as one of the body’s first lines of defense against pathogens such as bacteria, fungi, and some viruses, though they haven’t yet been translated into drugs. The computational study discovered β-defensin molecules from the moa, a large wingless bird that disappeared about 500 years ago, and the Spix’s macaw and the western black rhino, which died out in the last two decades (though the macaw survives in captivity) (7). “I really believe that we can use this knowledge and these tools to understand the past and then maybe try to solve the problems in the future,” Luiz Franco says. “It’s quite exciting.”

Although recent studies generally only involve antimicrobials, molecular de-extinction’s potential applications extend beyond that, de la Fuente says. Ancient proteins may influence immune responses, metabolic pathways, or neural signaling. Because extinct organisms lived in environments unlike those of today, their molecular repertoires may include functions no longer present in modern species.

De la Fuente is aware of the critique that extinct organisms failed at the game of life and died out. Are they really the best models to find life-prolonging molecules? “It’s paradoxical,” de la Fuente admits. “But the conditions back then were very different from now. And one gene is not going to make or break an extinction event.”

The Past Is Prologue

Bringing back long-extinct molecules is less problematic and controversial than trying to resurrect extinct species. But it does carry with it thorny ethical and legal questions. Should those who recreate long-lost proteins be able to patent and profit from them? De la Fuente hopes so. He has set up a company, and his university has filed patent applications.

Naturally occurring genes and molecules, however, can’t usually be patented in countries, including the United States, points out Andrew Torrance, a former biologist and now law professor at the University of Kansas, who has published on this topic with de la Fuente (8). “If you take a molecule that disappeared from the world and you are resurrecting it using human technological methods,” he says, “does that bring it back into the patentable realm?” Torrance suspects that it does—but he stresses that the idea is yet to be tested in court.

Expressing once-naturally occurring proteins from extinct animals through engineered yeast or bacteria also muddies the waters as to how these revived molecules should be labeled and assessed. While drug regulators usually base their decisions on molecular structure, sectors from food to cosmetics to fragrances are pickier about production methods, even if the final product is chemically identical.

Ethicists, researchers, and lawyers will likely have plenty of time to mull over the implications, as commercialization is still many years away and would likely require partnerships to facilitate more advanced testing. Barriers abound to translating a stretch of amino acids into a viable drug. Ancient DNA is often incomplete and chemically altered; the resulting errors in reconstructed sequences might obscure promising peptides. Many extinct species, particularly those from warm climates, are represented by sparse genetic data, limiting the pool of candidates available. Predictions derived from machine learning models depend on the quality and diversity of training datasets, which may bias results toward familiar types of molecules. Finally, the most advanced candidates have been tested only in mice. That leaves many development steps before human testing. Even for compounds derived from present-day organisms with well-understood biology, it’s notoriously difficult to pass every test in the drug development pipeline.

For now, the work proceeds on a much smaller scale. “It’s a way of understanding what was there before,” says Luiz Franco, “by rebuilding just enough of it to see how it worked.”
