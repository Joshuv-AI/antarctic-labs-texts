A γ-radiation-responsive diselenide-core micelle for on-demand delivery of cannabidiol in the mitigation of acute radiation injury
Authors: Caifeng Guo, Xin Shen, Rui Wang, Zhijie Bai, Lin Lin, Jiading Weng, Ningning Wang, Pan Shen, Lei Zhou, Shaoyun Guo, Xianlong Zhang, Wei Zhou, Yue Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the widespread application of nuclear energy, the associated risks of nuclear accidents and radiation leakage have increased considerably. To address potential acute radiation injuries and reduce the toxicity burden posed by conventional radioprotective agents, the development of novel therapeutic strategies is urgently needed. In this study, we designed a γ-radiation-responsive drug delivery system by encapsulating the radioprotective agent cannabidiol (CBD) into amphiphilic polymer micelles (denoted as mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD) incorporating diselenide bonds as radiation-sensitive units. The resulting micelles exhibited a small particle size (≈155 nm), negative surface charge (zeta potential = −14.5 mV), and a stable spherical morphology. In vitro drug release studies demonstrated that the CBD-loaded micelles responded sensitively to low-dose γ-irradiation, achieving over 60% cumulative release within 6 h, with release kinetics governed by Fick diffusion. In vivo evaluations revealed that the polymeric micelles not only promoted body weight recovery in irradiated mice, but also facilitated cellular and tissue uptake, alleviated intracellular oxidative stress, and conferred multi-organ protection. As a result, the 30-day post-irradiation survival rate was enhanced to over 50%. In the absence of radiation, the micelles can remain intact spherical structure, thereby reducing the risk of drug exposure. Collectively, this study presents a radiation-sensitive nanoplatform with radioprotective efficacy, offering a promising new approach for the prevention and treatment of acute radiation injuries.Keywords: Polymeric micelles, γ-irradiation, Diselenide bond, Drug delivery, Radiation injury

Introduction

Ionizing radiation (IR) is mainly caused by X-rays, α-rays, β-rays as well as γ-rays with its detrimental effects being manageable in most cases [[1], [2], [3]]. However, the high penetrability of γ-rays makes it difficult to completely shield them, which can easily create a radiation exposure environment [[4], [5], [6]]. In 1926, American geneticist Muller found that exposing Drosophila melanogaster to high levels of γ radiation could increase their offspring's mutation rate by up to 150 times [7]. Despite increasingly scientific and standardized management of radiation sources, historical radiation incidents, such as the Chernobyl and Fukushima nuclear disasters, continue to evoke significant concern due to human or natural factors [[8], [9], [10]]. Emergency response process after a nuclear accident presents a high risk of acute radiation injury. Therefore, the development of timely, effective strategies for the prevention and mitigation of acute radiation-induced injuries is of paramount importance.

To date, pharmacological intervention represents a main approach for the prevention and attenuation of radiation-induced damage. Despite the development of numerous small-molecule antiradiation agents over the years for radiological and nuclear incidents, only amifostine (WR-2721) has been FDA-approved [[11], [12], [13]]. Previous studies showed that oral amifostine delivery systems significantly prolonged murine survival: 50 mg/kg and 100 mg/kg amifostine microcapsules resulted in mean survival days of 25.5 ± 5.8 and 28.1 ± 3.8, while untreated mice only survived 11-22 days [14]. However, the challenges posed by its metabolic toxicity and related adverse effects have yet to be effectively addressed [15,16]. Furthermore, the antiradiation agents are predominantly limited to post-exposure administration, which inherently results in a certain degree of therapeutic delay. Varghese et al. [17] found that retroductal delivery of amifostine (400 mg/kg) before radiation significantly improved radioprotection and through administration approximately 25 times this dose retroductal injection without significant deleterious effects. But in the non-irradiated stage, the drug itself may induce toxic effects on the body. Beyond the associated health risks, prophylactic administration of drugs also increases economic costs and provokes social panic. Therefore, developing a radiation responsive, least toxic or non-toxic treatment platform to prevent and mitigate radiation-induced damage has significant therapeutic and strategic significance.

Polymeric micelles is a type of nanoparticle formed by amphiphilic polymers, which has good stability and drug loading capacity, and is widely used in drug delivery field [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. Furthermore, the biological toxicity of drug carrier materials used cannot be ignored [[22], [23], [24]]. Compared to synthetic macromolecules, natural macromolecules have lower metabolic toxicity, better biological safety and biodegradability [[25], [26], [27]]. Starch (St), as a common natural polymer material, has abundant availability, excellent biocompatibility and minimal biological toxicity [28]. The mentioned advantages of St make it an attractive material to develop as nanocarriers [29]. To date, various stimuli-responsive drug carriers based on St have been designed for meeting on-demand drug releasing [[30], [31], [32]]. Accordingly, St shows great potential to be modified to prepare radiation-responsive drug release system.

The successful development of a radiation-responsive drug release system directly depends on the responsive unit of the carrier material. Chalcogenide elements have been developed as radiation-responsive units due to their radiation response capabilities [[33], [34], [35], [36], [37]]. Wang et al. [38] constructed a disulfide-bond-containing self-sensitizing radio chromic elastomers, showing a higher radio chromic capability with a 18.3% decrease of a* values after 10 kGy irradiation. In comparison, the diselenide bond, with lower bond energy than disulfide bond (S-S: 240 kJ mol−1 and Se-Se: 172 kJ mol−1), demonstrates greater radiation-responsive sensitivity, and has become a research highlight in radiation-responsive system [39,40]. Xu et al. [41] developed diselenide-bond-bridged nanoparticles that could release drugs when the diselenide bond was interrupted by 5 Gy γ-radiation stimuli. Moreover, materials utilizing the Se-Se bond as a responsive unit, have wide applications in information storage, medicine, tissue engineering, and biomimetic materials [[42], [43], [44]]. Thus, introducing diselenide bond into drug delivery system to endow it sensitive radiation responsiveness is considered a viable strategy.

Previous studies have shown that CBD are the potential protective agent against radiation, which could significantly enhance individual survival rates [45]. In this study, a low-dose γ-irradiation responsive nano-system was developed by loading CBD into micelles self-assembled by amphiphilic polymer mPEG-SeSe-St, where hydrophobic interactions are the driving force for micelle formation. The diselenide bond acts as a radiation sensitive unit and is introduced into the amphiphilic polymer. The synthesis pathway of the mPEG-SeSe-St copolymer is shown in Fig. 1a. The related materials and drug loaded micelles were characterized, the binding force between CBD and mPEG-SeSe-St was considered. In vitro drug release behavior was consistent with the Peppas model, and the drug release mechanism of micelles under irradiation was revealed to be Fick diffusion. More importantly, in vivo pharmacokinetics and anti-radiation effect of mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD were evaluated in rat and mice, indicating the comparable pharmacokinetic behavior and radiation protection effects to those of CBD. Conclusively, a radiation-responsive polymeric micelle, mPEG-SeSe-St, was engineered for the encapsulation of CBD, exhibiting robust radioprotective efficacy, which could be ruptured release drugs at the same time as radiation exposure (Fig. 1b). This γ-ray responsive drug delivery platform may provide a novel and promising direction for the prevention and treatment of potentially acute radiation injury.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of mPEG-SeSe-St

To further elucidate the synthesis process of the polymer, the structures of intermediate mPEG-SeSe and polymer mPEG-SeSe-St were scientifically characterized and analyzed (Fig. 2). Fig. 2a shows the Proton Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (1HNMR) spectra of intermediate mPEG-SeSe and polymer mPEG-SeSe-St, and corresponding peaks can be found for their common characteristic structures. Specifically, the peaks at 2.03 ppm and 6.21-8.47 ppm are attributed to the proton peak in toluene diisocyanate (-C7H8N2-, 2, 3), the proton peak at 3.05 ppm (-CH2CH2SeSe, 4) is attributed to selenocysteine, and the peak at 3.48 ppm is attributed to the methylene unit in mPEG (-CH2-CH2-, 1), indicating that mPEG has been successfully introduced. In addition, a new characteristic peak appeared in the spectrum of mPEG-SeSe-St at 4.49-5.50 ppm, attributed to the characteristic proton peaks on the St sugar unit ring (C2-OH, C3-OH, C1-OH, and C6-OH, 5), confirming the successful preparation of mPEG-SeSe-St.

Characterizations of mPEG-SeSe and mPEG-SeSe-St. (a) 1HNMR spectra; (b) FTIR spectra; (c) XRD spectra; (d) Raman patterns, (e) XPS survey spectra and (f) High-resolution XPS spectra of Se 3d.

The Fourier transforms infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra of intermediate mPEG-SeSe and polymer mPEG-SeSe-St are shown in Fig. 2b. No obvious absorption peak appears at 2230 cm−1, indicating that all the isocyanate groups (-NCO) in the reaction system participated in the reaction [46]. Additionally, characteristic absorption peaks at 3336 cm−1 (O-H stretching vibration), 2920 cm−1 (C-H stretching vibration), and 1631 cm−1 (C=O stretching vibration) are observed in both mPEG-SeSe and mPEG-SeSe-St, confirming the synthesis of the intermediate mPEG-SeSe. Notably, compared to mPEG-SeSe, the infrared spectrum of mPEG-SeSe-St exhibits complex multiple absorption peaks at 1000-1150 cm−1, which is attributed to the various stretching and bending vibrations (C-O-C, C-O-H and hydrogen bond) of St, further indicating the successful synthesis of the polymer mPEG-SeSe-St.

The microstructure of the polymer mPEG-SeSe-St was analyzed by X-ray Diffraction (XRD), and the results are shown in Fig. 2c. The peak positions of mPEG-SeSe and mPEG-SeSe-St were consistent, displaying only a diffraction peak at 2θ = 22.1 with a broad peak shape, indicating a relatively loose arrangement of the polymer segments. Furthermore, no distinct crystalline peaks were observed, further confirming the amorphous structure of the polymer mPEG-SeSe-St. This amorphous structure facilitates the dissolution and dispersion of the polymer in solution. Then, the peak at ∼270-300 cm−1 in Raman spectroscopy is attributed to the bending vibration of Se-Se connected to C, which confirmed the existence of Se-Se bonds (Fig. 2d) [37].

In the X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) survey spectrum of mPEG-SeSe-St (Fig. 2e), four elements (O, N, C, and Se) were successfully detected, demonstrating the successful synthesis of the polymer mPEG-SeSe-St. Fig. 2f presents the high-resolution spectrum of Se 3d, where two peaks correspond to 3d5/2 (54.80 eV) and 3d3/2 (55.61 eV), respectively, further confirming the successful introduction of the diselenide bond. Additionally, in the high-resolution Se 3d spectra of both the intermediate mPEG-SeSe and the polymer mPEG-SeSe-St, the peak positions of the Se element were observed to shift slightly toward higher fields, which is attributed to the presence of C-Se bonds in the polymer segments [37].

Properties of mPEG-SeSe-St

The molecular weight (Mw), HNMR spectra, Raman spectra, FTIR spectra and high-resolution XPS Se 3d spectra of the polymer mPEG-SeSe-St before and after γ-ray irradiation was tested to evaluate the radiation responsiveness of the polymer. As shown in Fig. 3a, the molecular weight of mPEG-SeSe-St exhibited a relatively concentrated unimodal distribution both before and after γ-irradiation, with Mw values of 155,977 and 112,037, respectively. After exposure to 5 Gy γ-irradiation, the test results indicated that the Mw of the prepared polymer mPEG-SeSe-St decreased to 71.8% of its pre-irradiation value, representing a 28.2% reduction in molecular weight. Then, the HNMR spectra of mPEG-SeSe-St after γ-irradiation with different doses confirmed polymers preferentially break at the Se-Se bond under irradiation conditions, causing changes in the proton environment (4.5-5.5 ppm) (Fig. S1, Supporting Information). Additionally, the Raman spectra, high-resolution XPS Se 3d spectra and FTIR spectra directly confirmed the breakage of Se-Se bonds caused by γ-irradiation (Fig. S2–S4). These results preliminarily demonstrate that the polymer mPEG-SeSe-St exhibits a certain sensitivity to γ-rays, which is primarily attributed to the Se-Se bond introduced into its structure [40].

Properties of mPEG-SeSe-St. (a) GPC curves before and after radiation; (b) Dielectric loss ε” as a function of frequency from −60 to 60 °C; (c) Modulus loss M″ as a function of frequency from −60 to 60 °C; (d) TGA curve; (e) 2D graphs of the TG-IR; (f) Temperature dependent FTIR spectra; (g) Micellar formation process and (h) CMC test.

To investigate the molecular dynamics characteristics of mPEG-SeSe-St, broadband dielectric spectroscopy measurements were conducted. Fig. 3b and c respectively display the frequency-dependent curves of the imaginary part of the dielectric constant (ε") and the imaginary part of the dielectric modulus (M″) for mPEG-SeSe-St at different temperatures, with a frequency range of 10°-106 Hz. As shown in Fig. 3b, as the temperature increases, a single relaxation peak gradually emerges in mPEG-SeSe-St, exhibiting thermally activated characteristics. Fig. 3c reveals that the frequency-dependent curves of M″ for mPEG-SeSe-St resemble the shape of the ε" curves, with the relaxation peak frequencies concentrated in the low-frequency region (<103 Hz). These results indicate that the polymer mPEG-SeSe-St shows no electrode polarization phenomenon in the low-frequency region, and the appearance of the single relaxation peak suggests the presence of a β-relaxation process in the polymer system, primarily attributed to the motion occurring in the main chain of mPEG-SeSe-St. The β-relaxation process can induce the reorganization of intermolecular hydrogen bonds, thereby endowing the polymer mPEG-SeSe-St with excellent stability as a drug carrier.

To further investigate the thermal stability of the polymer mPEG-SeSe-St, Thermogravimetry-Infrared Spectroscopy (TG-IR) coupled analysis was conducted. As shown in Fig. 3d, mPEG-SeSe-St rapidly degrades into short-chain molecules near 320 °C, which is attributed to the cleavage of Se-Se bonds in the polymer backbone structure. Subsequently, short-chain molecules continue to degrade, and when the temperature reaches 700 °C, the polymer is almost completely degraded, with carbon dioxide being detected throughout the entire process (Fig. 3e). Moreover, Fig. 3f presents the FTIR spectra of the polymer at 300-700 °C (containing C-H, C-Se, C-N, C-O, N-H, and -OH groups), corresponding to other degraded gaseous products, including H2O and flammable volatiles generated from carbonyl and ether groups. These results indicate that mPEG-SeSe-St is not prone to degradation at room temperature and exhibits excellent stability as drug carrier. However, γ-rays can break Se-Se bonds at room temperature, thereby disrupting the micellar structure and enabling rapid release of radiation injury treatment drugs achieving timely damage treatment and effectively avoiding the problem of “damage first, treatment later” in traditional radiation damage treatment strategies.

Critical micelle concentration (CMC) of mPEG-SeSe-St

The prepared mPEG-SeSe-St is an amphiphilic polymer where the hydrophobic interaction of its intermediate hydrophobic chain serves as the driving force for micelle formation (Fig. 3g). During micellization, the CMC value is a key parameter characterizing the self-assembly behavior of micelles and an important indicator for evaluating their stability in the circulatory system post-administration [18]. A plot was constructed with the ratio of fluorescence intensities at 338 nm and 332 nm (I338/I332) as the ordinate and the concentration of the micellar solution as the abscissa. The I338/I332 ratio exhibits strong dependence on solution polarity. As shown in Fig. 3h, with increasing mPEG-SeSe-St concentration, changes in solution polarity induce an abrupt shift in the I338/I332 curve. Fitting results indicate that the polymer mPEG-SeSe-St possesses a low critical micelle concentration, with a CMC value of 7.26 μg mL−1. These findings demonstrate that mPEG-SeSe-St micelles exhibit excellent dilution stability and can maintain a stable micellar structure in the circulatory system [47], showing promising potential as nanodrug carriers.

Assembly diagram of drug loaded micelles (mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD) is shown in Fig. 4a. Using molecular docking technology to explore the interaction and binding characteristics between drug molecules and the hydrophobic core of micelles, to elucidate the stability mechanism of drug loaded micelles [48]. The Autodock software was employed to simulate the molecular docking reaction, generating a 3D model diagram (Fig. 4b). The molecular docking results further revealed that the polymer mPEG-SeSe-St possesses a large spatial conformation, enabling effective drug encapsulation. The binding between CBD and the polymer mPEG-SeSe-St occurs within the macromolecular spatial configuration formed by the polymer and tends to form intermolecular hydrogen bonds. The pink regions indicate a strong propensity for polar hydrogen atoms, which facilitates hydrogen bond formation. Conversely, the green regions represent areas more receptive to polar hydrogen atoms, also promoting hydrogen bond formation. These results indicate that a rich hydrogen bonding network can be formed between CBD and hydrophobic segments of mPEG-SeSe-St, enhancing the stability of drug loaded micelles. The hydrogen bonding interactions within the micelle nucleus are represented by green dashed lines (Fig. 4c).

Preparation and characterization of mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD micelles

For drug delivery systems, the morphology and size of micelles are also crucial [18]. Therefore, the polymeric micelles were characterized using Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS). TEM images revealed spherical mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD micelles with a diameter of approximately 100 nm (Fig. 4e). Moreover, the responsiveness of mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD micelles to γ-ray irradiation was also investigated, compared to unirradiated drug loaded micelles, the micelles underwent rapid disintegration, both on the surface and internally, upon exposure to 5 Gy γ-rays. This structural transformation primarily stems from the cleavage of the diselenide bonds on the polymer backbone under γ-ray irradiation [39], which leads to the destruction of the hydrophobic core structure within the micelle, thereby causing micelle to be cleaved. TEM-energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS) analysis clearly demonstrated the distribution of C, N, O, Se (Fig. 4f), Se is primarily distributed within the micelle core and contributes to the formation of the hydrophobic region inside the micelle.

The DLS measurement results are shown in Fig. 5. The particle sizes of blank micelles (mPEG-SeSe-St) and drug-loaded micelles (mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD) were 216 nm and 155 nm (Fig. 5a), respectively. The significant reduction in micelle size after drug loading is attributed to the interaction between the drug and the hydrophobic core, which leads to a more compact hydrophobic core structure and consequently a smaller micelle size. Additionally, it was observed that the micelle sizes were larger than those determined by TEM. This discrepancy arises because the micelles are in a dry state during TEM testing, causing the hydrophilic segments on the exterior to contract, resulting in smaller measured micelle sizes compared to those in the solution state during DLS testing. Furthermore, the Polydispersity Index (PDI) values were less than 0.4, indicating good dispersity of the nanomicelle solution [18]. Due to the abundant hydroxyl groups (-OH) on the polyethylene glycol (PEG) and starch molecular chains that form the hydrophilic shell of the micelles, both the blank micelles and the drug loaded micelles exhibit negative surface charges, with zeta potentials of −18.3 mV and −14.5 mV, respectively (Fig. 5b). The slight decrease in surface potential of micelles after drug loading is due to the neutralization of some charges by the interaction between the drug and the polymer. Meanwhile, it can be observed that during the drug loading process, the surface charge properties of mPEG-SeSe-St micelles did not undergo significant changes. More importantly, the mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD micelles showed no significant changes in size and potential within 48 h, further proving excellent stability before γ-radiation exposure (Fig. S5, Supporting Information), surpassing traditional oral CBD nano delivery systems [49].

In vitro drug release behavior

The standard curve of the drug was fitted by high-performance liquid chromatography, with R2 = 0.9994 (Fig. 5c). Based on the standard curve, the drug loading (DL) of the polymeric micelles was calculated to be 10.0% (Fig. S6, Supporting Information). The schematic illustration of in vitro drug release from drug-loaded micelles is presented in Fig. 5d, and the corresponding release profiles are shown in Fig. 5e. After irradiation with 5 Gy of γ-rays, the drug-loaded micelles exhibited rapid release within the first 10 min, with a cumulative release reaching 50%. Under γ-ray irradiation, the breakage of Se-Se bonds leads to the destruction of the micelle structure, thereby triggering the rapid release of drugs within the hydrophobic core. Subsequently, the release rate gradually slows down due to the hindrance of drug diffusion by the outer hydrophilic shell. Within 6 h, the cumulative release of CBD reached over 60%, which is much higher than previous nanoparticles containing diselenide bonds (≈50% drug was released in 24 h) [40]. These results indicated that the mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD micelles have excellent responsive release properties and can be used for the protection and treatment of acute radiation injuries. This responsive release characteristic endows the mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD micelles with significant advantages in radiation injury treatment, enabling rapid drug release at the damaged site to achieve localized high-concentration drug delivery, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy while reducing systemic side effects. In contrast, the drug release rate from micelles was extremely slow where there is no irradiation. In the first 40 min, almost no drug release was detected, followed by a small amount of drug release, and the cumulative release of CBD within 6 h was less than 3%. These results indicate that mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD micelles possess excellent stability, can effectively encapsulate drugs, and prevent premature leakage [18], further confirming their great potential as a radiation-responsive drug delivery system.

To further elucidate the drug release mechanism of drug-loaded polymeric micelles, drug release kinetic analysis was conducted. For spherical nanocarriers, drug release primarily conforms to the Peppas equation [50]:

In the equation, Mt represents the cumulative drug release amount at time t; t denotes the drug release time; M∞ indicates the total drug release amount; k stands for the release constant, which is related to the structure of the nanocarrier; n represents the diffusion exponent, typically used to indicate the drug release mechanism. When n < 0.43, the drug release mechanism follows Fick diffusion; when 0.43 < n < 0.85, the drug release mechanism belongs to anomalous diffusion [51]. The fitting results are shown in Fig. 5f, n = 0.035 (R2 = 0.9946) under γ-ray irradiation, indicating that CBD is released from the micelles through Fick diffusion. However, n = 0.632 (R2 = 0.8491) when without irradiation, suggesting that the release process of CBD follows irregular diffusion.

Pharmacokinetics analysis

The plasma concentration-time (C–T) curves of CBD are illustrated in Fig. 5g. The mass spectrometry conditions, method validation, and pharmacokinetic parameters are summarized in Table S1–S3 of the Supporting Information. Rapid absorption was observed in CBD group, achieving peak plasma concentration (Cmax: 208.8 ng mL−1) at approximately 1 h post-administration (Tmax: 0.94 h). As expected, no CBD were detected in non-irradiated rats administered mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD micelles. Trace amounts of CBD were detected at 5, 15, and 30 min after 3 Gy γ-irradiation, precluding pharmacokinetic parameter calculation. By contrast, the groups following 5 Gy or 8.5 Gy γ-irradiation, all demonstrated rapid achievement of Cmax (Tmax: 0.5 h), indicating radiation-triggered disassembly of the micelles and the rapid release of CBD. For both high-dose irradiation groups, the Cmax of mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD were comparable to that of CBD group, indicating complete structural breakdown of micelles and full release of encapsulated CBD. The elimination half-life (t1/2z) of free CBD group was 3 h, whereas shorter t1/2z of 1.25 h and 1.62 h were obtained for the 5 Gy and 8.5 Gy irradiated micelle groups, respectively. Both Tmax and t1/2z declined in mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD (5 Gy) group and the mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD (8.5 Gy) group relative to free CBD. This phenomenon was speculated to be attributed to pre-irradiation administration, which facilitated the intact CBD loaded micelles that were absorbed into the bloodstream ahead of radiation exposure. When the body received high-dose radiation exposure, micelle rupture released CBD into the plasma, resulting in a more rapid peak plasma concentration followed by accelerated metabolism and excretion.

In vivo γ-irradiation protective efficacy

A thorough evaluation was conducted on the in vivo radiation protection efficacy of mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD micelles. Prior to the experiment, mice underwent adaptive feeding, random grouping, and pretreatment (Fig. 6a). The dose of γ-ray irradiation was set at the lethal dose for mice (8.5 Gy). The weight trajectory of mice is shown in Fig. 6b, the mPEG-SeSe-St group gradually decreased after 7 days, while CBD and mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD group can effectively promote the recovery of weight in mice after γ-irradiation. Fig. 6c shows the survival rate curves of mice after irradiation, both the CBD and mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD groups significantly improved the 30-day survival rate of mice, increasing it from 0% to over 50%. Additionally, the mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD group could also prolong the survival time of irradiated mice. Meanwhile, it was observed that the decrease in White Blood Cell (WBC) and Neutrophil (NEUT) levels in the blood caused by γ-rays was improved under the intervention of CBD and mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD (Fig. 6d and e). However, there were no significant changes in the Red Blood Cell (RBC), Hemoglobin (HGB) and Platelet (PLT) levels in the blood (Fig. S7, Supporting Information). The above results indicate that mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD micelles can not only effectively protect mice from γ-ray damage, but also improve the survival rate of irradiated mice, demonstrating excellent in vivo radioprotective efficacy.

Histopathological analysis

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on multiple tissues of mice at 1, 3, and 5 d post-irradiation to thoroughly investigate the specific sites of radiation damage treatment and its therapeutic effects. On the first day after exposure to a lethal dose (8.5 Gy) of radiation, all groups exhibited a certain degree of radiation damage compared to normal tissues (Fig. 6f, Figs. S8 and S9, Supporting Information). The specific manifestations include: shortening of small intestinal villi, extensive separation of intestinal villous epithelium from the lamina propria, reduction in crypt number and height, with evident radiation damage observed in the small intestine; decreased hematopoietic cell count in the bone marrow cavity of mice, large acellular areas appearing in the marrow cavity, increased bone marrow vascularity with dilated lumens, heightened lymphocyte infiltration, and minor connective tissue proliferation visible around trabeculae; liquefaction, necrosis, and gliosis in the cerebral tissue of mice, cortical atrophy, deepened nuclear staining in hippocampal neurons with irregular shapes, and loosely arranged neuronal cells in small focal areas; markedly reduced thymic tissue volume in mice, indistinct corticomedullary demarcation, scattered lymphocytes visible in small focal areas within the central region of the tissue with loose cellular arrangement, numerous necrotic cells, vascular congestion and dilation. On day 3, the mPEG-SeSe-St group showed no significant changes; whereas in the CBD group and mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD group, the intestinal villus walls gradually regained integrity, areas of cell loss decreased, hematopoietic cell numbers increased, necrotic cell numbers reduced, and cellular arrangement became more organized. These changes indicated mitigation of radiation damage. Until day 5, the mPEG-SeSe-St group still exhibited no significant improvement in radiation damage (Fig. S8, Supporting Information), while the CBD group and mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD group had nearly fully recovered (Fig. 6f and Fig. S9, Supporting Information). These results indicate that the mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD micelles exhibit protective effects on multiple organs in mice after lethal dose (8.5 Gy) γ-irradiation. The mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD micelle with high drug loading capacity and cell uptake could effectively resolve the poor solubility of CBD, improve its bioavailability, and can be administered preventively or in a timely manner after injury, making them suitable for radiation protection and treatment (Fig. 6g).

Tissue distribution

The tissue distribution of CBD in rats at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 h after oral mass spectrometry conditions and method validation are presented in Tables S1 and S4, (Supporting Information). As shown in Fig. 7a, CBD was detected in all tested tissues at 0.25 h, including heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, brain, and intestine. This indicated rapid and widespread distribution in vivo. Across all groups, CBD concentrations were higher in the kidney, spleen, and heart. Lower concentrations were observed in the liver and brain. The kidney may be the main excretion route of CBD, and the heart and spleen may be the primary target organs for its pharmacological effects. For the mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD group, tissue CBD levels were slightly higher than those in the CBD group after 5 Gy and 8.5 Gy irradiation. This suggested that mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD increases the amount of CBD entering tissues due to the lipophilic nature of its encapsulation material. Only trace amounts of CBD were detected in tissues without irradiation or with 3 Gy irradiation. This result showed that the formulation releases CBD effectively only under moderate-to-high radiation doses with favorable release efficiency. In the CBD group, the time to maximum concentration was 0.5 h in the liver and 1 h in all other tissues. In the mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD groups, the time to maximum concentration in all tissues was 0.5 h. This finding demonstrated that the mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD accelerated the time to peak CBD concentration in tissues, which were consistent with the plasma pharmacokinetic results. This further confirmed that, due to pre-administration, intact mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD micelles entered plasma and tissues prematurely. When the body received high-dose radiation exposure, CBD was rapidly released into blood and tissues.

Cellular uptake

As shown in Fig. 7b, in HT22 and mode-k cells treated with mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD for 1 h, mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD micelles were observed in the cytoplasm and maintained a relatively intact morphology, confirming successful cellular uptake of mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD.

Intracellular ROS level

Intracellular ROS levels were detected in HT22 cells (Fig. 7c) and mode-k cells (Fig. 7d). The mPEG-SeSe-St group showed a significant increase in ROS levels compared to the Control group. This confirmed the oxidative stress model was successfully established. Treatment with CBD, mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD and WR-2721 all reduced ROS levels compared to the model group. These results indicate that both free CBD and the mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD nanomaterial can effectively alleviate intracellular oxidative stress. Their protective effects are comparable to that of the positive control WR-2721.

Cytotoxicity evaluation of mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD

The cytotoxicity of mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD before/after γ-radiation was assessed via CCK-8 assay in HT22 cells and mode-k cells (Fig. 7c and d and S10). Across all tested concentrations of mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD (before/after γ-radiation), cell viability remained above 85% in both cell lines. No significant cytotoxicity was observed even at the highest concentration of 10 μg mL−1. These results indicate that mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD has good biocompatibility and biosafety and will not cause significant cell damage even under actual radiation exposure condition.

Conclusion

In summary, this work constructed a nano drug delivery system based on γ-radiation responsive polymeric micelle by introducing diselenide bond, for concurrent radiation prevention and therapy. Molecular docking predicted a strong interaction between CBD and polymer mPEG-SeSe-St, mainly hydrogen bonding, ensuring stable loading and delivery of CBD. Small size (∼155 nm), negative surface charge (zeta potential = −14.5 mV) and uniform spherical shape of designed polymeric micelles contributed to overcoming drug delivery barriers and delivery CBD in vivo, to cope with the radiation environment. The mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD micelles exhibited strong γ-ray responsiveness and protects the body from radiation damage by the delivery of CBD. In vitro drug release behavior stated that the drug release amount can exceed 60% in 6 h under 5 Gy γ-irradiation, which conforms to the Peppas model (R2 = 0.9946), and the drug release mechanism is Fick diffusion. In vivo studies have shown that developed mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD micelles can effectively protective multiple tissues of mice, promoted weight recovery of mice after lethal dose (8.5 Gy) γ-irradiation, improved the 30-day survival rate of mice, and showed excellent radiation protection and therapy effect. The system can be absorbed by cells and tissues, rapidly ruptures to release radiation protection agents under radiation exposure, and reduce intracellular ROS levels. Overall, a novel radiation-sensitive nano-micellar drug system, mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD, was developed, demonstrating pronounced efficacy against radiation-induced injury. The novel radiation responsive drug release system and related antiradiation agents offer a new approach for real-time protection against radiation injury, with its great potential for clinical applications, which could be widely used for protection and treatment during unexpected nuclear radiation exposure.

Methods

Materials

Polyethylene glycol (PEG, Mn = 2000 Da) was purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Selenocystamine was purchased from Yuanye Bio-technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Starch (St, Mw = 8.8 kDa) was obtained from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). CBD was provided by Academy of Military Medical Science. Reference standards of Carbamazepine (IS) with purity (>99.00%) were purchased from the National Institute for the Control of Pharmaceuticals and Biological Products (Beijing, China). All kinds of solvents were supplied by Chengdu Kelon Chemical Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). The water used in the experiment was double distilled. All the chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Synthesis of mPEG-SeSe-St

The copolymer mPEG-SeSe-St was synthesized through chain extension reaction. Specifically, under nitrogen atmosphere, selenocystamine (199.9 mg) and diisocyanate (TDI, 100 μL) were added into the flask, triethylamine (0.2 mL) as catalyst, and the system was stirred at room temperature for 30 min, then heated to 45 °C and reacted for 1.5 h. Meanwhile, PEG (126 mg) was dissolved in anhydrous DMSO (10 mL). Subsequently, the above solution was mixed, and the reaction was continued for 3 h. After the reaction was completed, the sample was transferred into a dialysis bag (MWCO = 3500 Da) and dialyzed against deionized water for 2 days with frequent water renewal, freeze-dried and named as mPEG-SeSe. Next, the St (2 g) was added into DMSO (20 mL), and stirred at room temperature until fully dissolved. With continuous stirring, mPEG-SeSe (molar ratio mPEG-SeSe:St = 1:1) was introduced, followed by 12 h reaction period to guarantee sufficient reaction conversion. Finally, the obtained product was dialyzed, freeze-dried and recorded as mPEG-SeSe-St.

Radiation treatment

In this work, γ-irradiation was used as an irradiation treatment method, Co60 was used as the irradiation source for 5 Gy γ-irradiation. To simulate the real environment, all samples were irradiated in the air at room temperature. The irradiation treatment of samples was completed by Academy of Military Medical Science.

Material characterizations

The functional groups were characterized by NMR (1HNMR, Bruker, AV III HD 400 MHz, German), FTIR (Thermo Nicolet IS-10 spectrometer, USA) in the range of 4000-400 cm−1 at room temperature and Raman spectroscopy (in Via Reflex, Renishaw, U.K.) in the range of 150-400 cm−1 with 532 nm laser excitation and an integration time of 10 s. Crystallization information was measured by XRD (Ultima IV, Rigaku, Japan). The chemical composition of the samples was further analyzed by XPS (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, USA). Dielectric measurements were carried out through Quatro Cryosystem temperature control pattern on a Novo control Concept 50 system. Frequency sweep mode used the frequency range of 100 to 106 Hz at each temperature from −60 °C to 60 °C with 20 °C interval, with temperature stability better than 0.1 K. Thermal stability was performed on a thermogravimetric fourier transform infrared spectrometry (Clarus SQ 8T, PerkinElmer, USA) with a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 at a temperature range of 50 °C to 800 °C under nitrogen atmosphere (flow rate: 25 mL min−1).

Radiation response performance testing

The change in molecular weight of carrier materials before/after radiation can reflect their radiation responsiveness. Gel-permeation chromatography (GPC, Waters 1515 detector, Waters, USA) was utilized to test the weight average molecular weight (Mw) of polymers before/after 5 Gy γ-irradiation with DMF as the mobile phase.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking simulation of CBD and mPEG-SeSe-St was performed using Autodocking, binding activity was predicted and 3D spatial environment images were generated by Discovery Studio 2016.

CMC of mPEG-SeSe-St

The CMC of mPEG-SeSe-St micelles were investigated by fluorescence probe method on the fluorescence spectrometer (FluoroMax-4, HORIBA, Japan) according to the previous research [52], pyrene was selected as the fluorescent probe.

Preparation of CBD loaded micelles and characterizations

The formation of CBD loaded micelles was achieved by the self-assembly process [53]. Briefly, mPEG-SeSe-St (3 mg) and CBD (20 mg) were dissolved into DMSO (2 mL) in a sample bottle. After complete dissolution, the mixed solution (300 μL) was added dropwise to PBS (20 mL) in a glass bottle with slowly stirring. The obtained dispersion of micelles was dialyzed for 2 days to ensure complete removal of DMSO, filtered with needle filter (0.45 μm) and denoted as mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD.

The quantitative drug loading mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD micelle is achieved by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Alliance e2695, Waters, USA). The amount of CBD in the micelles was determined by measuring the absorbance at 211 nm using a standard curve constructed with different concentrations of CBD solutions in PBS. Animal procedures

Sprague–Dawley rats (SD male, 200-220 g) and C57BL/6J mice (male, 20-22 g) were purchased from the Charles River (Beijing, China). Animals were housed internally in flawless animal rooms maintained at constant temperature (23 °C ± 2 °C) and humidity (55% ± 10%), and a 12 h light/dark cycle. Animal studies were carried out in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals as adopted and promulgated by the National Health Ministry of China. Protocols of animal experiments had been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Sichuan University (Animal Experiment Approval No.: KS2022244).

Pharmacokinetics study

Male SD rats were randomly divided into 5 groups (n = 6) after fasting (not water) for 12 h: CBD group, mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD group, γ/mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD (3 Gy) group (treated with 3 Gy γ-irradiation), γ/mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD (5 Gy) group (treated with 5 Gy γ-irradiation) and γ/mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD (8.5 Gy) group (treated with 8.5 Gy γ-irradiation). Single gavage administration (7 mg kg−1) was used to before irradiation. Later, blood of the rat eye sockets was collected at fixed time (5, 15, 30, 60, 120, 240, 360, 480, 640, 720 and 1440 min). The blood was quickly loaded into centrifuge tubes containing heparin and centrifuged at 4 °C, 3000 r min−1 for 10 min. The supernatant plasma was carefully collected and stored at −80 °C. The samples were processed and the content of CBD was detected according to the method (Supporting Information).

In vivo antiradiation effect evaluation

C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into 5 groups with 20 mice in each group: Control group (no treatment), mPEG-SeSe-St group, CBD group, mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD group, and WR-2721 group. The mice were orally administered with CBD (10 mg kg−1), mPEG-SeSe-St and mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD (10 mg kg−1), respectively, at 12 h and 0.5 h before exposure to γ-irradiation. The mice in WR-2721 group were administered WR-2721 (150 mg kg−1) intraperitoneally, at 12 h and 0.5 h before exposure to γ-irradiation. Then, the mice were subjected to lethal dose (8.5 Gy) whole-body irradiation at a dose rate of 186.98 ± 0.5 cGy min−1. Subsequently, the weight and mortality of mice were measured at a fixed time every day.

Blood cell count test

The number of WBC and NEUT inflammatory cells in serum was determined using a blood cell analyzer (sysmex, XN-1000V).

Histopathological observation

At day 1, 3 and 5 after γ-irradiation, mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation, the intestine, bone, hippocampal and thymus were immediately removed and fixed in 4% formalin for 24 h. Tissues were then embedded in paraffin and sectioned into slices (3 μm), and the slices were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E).

Tissue distribution

After oral administration of CBD and mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD to rats tissue concentrations were measured at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 h after 0, 3, 5, and 8.5 Gy γ-irradiation. Rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium, followed by euthanasia via exsanguination from the abdominal aorta. The heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, brain, and intestine tissues were immediately harvested, rinsed with ice-cold normal saline to remove residual blood, and weighed. A 1.0 g aliquot of each tissue was homogenized in 5 mL of pre-cooled normal saline using a tissue homogenizer at 4 °C. The resulting homogenates were stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

TEM analysis of cellular uptake

HT22 and mode-k cells were seeded at a density of 3.5 × 105 cells per well and cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator until reaching 70-80% confluency. The two cells were both incubated with mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD for 1 h. Then, cells were washed three times with pre-cooled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove uninternalized nanoparticles. They were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS at 4 °C for 2 h, then post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h. Fixed cells were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (70-90 nm) were cut using an ultramicrotome, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and observed under a transmission electron microscope.

Intracellular ROS level detection

HT22 and mode-k cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. They were cultured to 70–80% confluency. The cells were divided into five groups: Control group (no treatment), mPEG-SeSeSt group (treated with 5 Gy γ-irradiation), CBD group (treated with 5 Gy γ-irradiation), mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD group (treated with 5 Gy γ-irradiation) and positive control group (WR-2721, treated with 5 Gy γ-irradiation). The cells were treated with CBD, mPEG-SeSe-St, mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD and WR-2721, respectively, at 24 h before exposure to γ-irradiation. After γ-irradiation, the medium was removed. Cells were loaded with 10 μM DCFH-DA probe at 37 °C for 30 min. Cells were washed three times with serum-free medium. This step removed excess probe. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader. The excitation wavelength was 488 nm. The emission wavelength was 525 nm. Relative ROS levels were calculated. The control group was set to 1.0.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8)

HT22 and mode-k cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. They were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 24 h to allow attachment. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD and mPEG-SeSe-St@CBD (treated with 5 Gy γ-irradiation), respectively, and based on the results of previous experiments, the concentrations of two groups both are 50 ng mL−1, 100 ng mL−1, 250 ng mL−1, 500 ng mL−1, 1 μg mL−1, 2.5 μg mL−1, 5 μg mL−1, and 10 μg mL−1. Cells treated with complete medium only served as the negative control (Control). After 24 h of incubation, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. The plates were incubated for another 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated as a percentage relative to the negative control group.
