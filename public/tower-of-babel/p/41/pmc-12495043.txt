Probiotic Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 from baijiu Daqu enhanced γ-aminobutyric acid and antioxidant ability in Cordyceps militaris fruiting body extract and brown rice beverage
Authors: Kaihui Shen, Zhengyan Lei, Shuqi Zhou, Jun Wang, Anjun Li, Jiangjing Gao, Ganggang Cao, Luchan Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstractγ-Aminobutyric acid (GABA) is a functional amino acid with various physiological activities. Increasing the concentration of GABA in foods and enhancing dietary supplementation are important for health benefits. Baijiu, a traditional Chinese liquor, is one of the oldest and most consumed fermented beverages. Daqu, the fermentation starter of Baijiu, contains functional lactic acid bacteria. However, whether these lactic acid bacteria have the potential to prepare functional beverages enriched with GABA is not clear. In this study, Daqu was used as a screening resource, and fruiting body extracts of Cordyceps militaris and germinated brown rice were used as fermentation materials to obtain Levilactobacillus brevis GH1, which can endogenously produce GABA. Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 demonstrated strong probiotic properties and the ability to survive in a simulated stomach and intestinal conditions, as well as tolerate osmotic pressure and bile salts. The optimal fermentation conditions were 30.83 g/L sucrose, 1.04 g/L corn steep liquor powder, and 52.74 % Cordyceps militaris fruiting body extraction solution at pH 4.06 and culture at 34.40 °C. The GABA concentration reached 16.83 g/L. The DPPH scavenging ability, Fe2+ chelating ability, and hydroxyl free radical scavenging ability increased by 1.7, 3.2, and 2.7 times, respectively. Moreover, the beverage is rich in lactic acid, acetic acid, oxalic acid, succinic acid, citric acid, and malic acid. These properties demonstrate that Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 derived from Daqu can be utilized in the development of functional beverages rich in GABA and is suitable for use in functional food products.Keywords: Daqu, Probiotic, Levilactobacillus brevis, γ-aminobutyric acid, Cordyceps militaris, antioxidant activity, Fermented beverage

Introduction

Baijiu, a unique traditional liquor in China, was developed over a long period of time. Chinese Baijiu has attracted many alcoholic beverage drinkers because of its special color, aroma, taste, and characteristics. Among the key elements in Baijiu production, Daqu, the starter of solid-state fermentation for Chinese liquor, plays a crucial and irreplaceable role. Daqu is made from wheat (or raw barley, peas, sorghum, etc.), which is widely regarded as the flavor and microbial source of Chinese Baijiu and plays an important role in Baijiu production as a fermenter, saccharifier, and aroma generator (He et al., 2019). Daqu provides important raw materials, microorganisms, and enzymes for the fermentation of Baijiu, which gives Baijiu many different stylistic characteristics; thus, the quality of Daqu is the key to determining the quality of Baijiu (Tong et al., 2023). Daqu is rich in microorganisms, a natural library of strains. Many of these strains are also related to the production of other foods, such as vinegar, sauce, and juice, which not only shows the wide range of applicability of these strains but also provides additional insights for screening functional strains in the context of Baijiu production. Therefore, the screening of functional strains from Daqu has a unique advantage. Current studies have shown that some strains in Daqu can synthesize high-value metabolites, such as γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), BCAAs (valine, leucine, isoleucine), antimicrobial peptides, esters, tetramethylpyrazine, polyphenols, ergothiocyanines, and B vitamins (Tone et al., 2025). However, it is still unclear whether Daqu contains microbial strains that can be used to develop functional beverages.

GABA, an important functional food additive component, is a central nervous system inhibitory neurotransmitter in animals and humans and is a nonprotein amino acid that widely exists in nature (Roberts and Frankel, 1950). Thirty percent of neurons in the human brain contain GABA, which is involved in the activities of all neurons in the human body (Gladkevich et al., 2006; Wan et al., 2015). Studies have shown that GABA has various physiological functions, such as activating glucose metabolism in the brain, promoting acetylcholine synthesis, exerting antiobesity and anticonvulsant effects, lowering blood pressure, improving brain function, and stabilizing the mind (Hepsomali et al., 2020; Ngo and Vo, 2019). Recent clinical trials further support its efficacy in managing metabolic health. A randomized controlled trial demonstrated that a food supplement containing GABA significantly improved lipid profiles in individuals with mild dyslipidemia (De Lellis et al., 2024). GABA is also found in natural foods such as tea, tomatoes, soybeans, germinated brown rice, and fermented foods (Rashmi et al., 2018; Yılmaz and Gökmen, 2020). However, the content of GABA in these foods is low, which is insufficient to enable the functional activity of GABA. Alternative methods are necessary to increase GABA levels in foods.

GABA in food is produced mainly by enzymes, especially via microbial fermentation (Liu et al., 2023). A variety of microorganisms, including yeasts, fungi, and bacteria, have been confirmed to produce GABA (Luo et al., 2021; Tamés et al., 2023). For GABA-rich foods, lactic acid bacteria fermentation has been recognized as a safe and environmentally friendly method (Evivie et al., 2017). Lactic acid bacteria are generally considered safe (GRAS) microorganisms with good prebiotic effects on humans (Linares et al., 2017). In addition, lactic acid bacteria also exhibit high fermentation ability for GABA production (De Lellis et al., 2024). GABA produced in lactic acid bacteria achieves GABA biosynthesis through the enzymatic reaction of L-glutamate by glutamate decarboxylase with pyridoxal-5′-phosphate (PLP) as a coenzyme (Yogeswara et al., 2020). Although lactic acid bacteria fermentation can increase the yield of GABA, the production of food rich in GABA is achieved mainly by the addition of pure GABA. This approach also has corresponding food safety risks and increases the production cost of functional foods. Therefore, the use of microorganisms to ferment raw food materials directly and supplement them with glutamic acid to obtain endogenous food rich in GABA is the key to preparing functional foods.

Among fermented GABA-producing foods, fermented beverages have become a convenient way to supplement GABA in food. However, the GABA content in these beverages is relatively low (Kittibunchakul et al., 2021). The raw materials for fermentation are usually composed of grains or fruits and have a simple nutritional composition. How to obtain appropriate microorganisms and a favorable cultivation environment is the key to preparing beverages rich in GABA. Ophiocordyceps sinensis, a traditional medicinal fungus, has been widely recognized for its significant health benefits and economic value. (Krishna, Ulhas and Malaviya, 2024) However, owing to enormous market demand and resource scarcity, the price of Ophiocordyceps sinensis has increased dramatically over the past decade (Krishna et al., 2025). Concurrently, it has been listed on the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List of endangered species. Cordyceps (C.) militaris, a model species of Ophiocordyceps sinensis, contains various active metabolites, such as cordycepin, D-mannitol, GABA, adenosine, and polysaccharides (Chou et al., 2024). The cordycepin content in C. militaris is even greater than that in wild Ophiocordyceps sinensis. C. militaris can be easily cultivated on a large scale, making it a cost-effective alternative to Ophiocordyceps sinensis (Olatunji et al., 2018). C. militaris has been confirmed to have anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antidiabetic, antikidney, hypolipidemic, and anticancer functions (Rawat et al., 2024). Hydrolyzed C. militaris extract can regulate the polarization and production of macrophages, inhibit the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines by macrophages, increase the phagocytic activity of macrophages, and increase the differentiation of DCs, Treg cells, and NKTL cells, thereby achieving anti-inflammatory effects (Park et al., 2021). However, the extract of C. militaris, as a nutrient for microorganisms, holds significant value for the development of beverages with specific functional properties. The use of lactic acid bacteria to hydrolyze C. militaris extracts and enrich for GABA is promising for the development of GABA-rich beverages.

Baijiu Daqu is a high-quality resource library for GABA-producing lactic acid bacterial strains, providing a unique advantage for the development of the functional metabolite GABA. In this study, we aimed to obtain a specific lactic acid bacteria by using Daqu as the screening resource. By developing this strain, a functional beverage rich in GABA was established, which was prepared from the fruiting body extract of C. militaris and germinated brown rice. The safety and probiotic characteristics of the screened strain were analyzed in depth, and strategies for the efficient production of GABA by the strains in the fruiting body extracts of C. militaris and germinated brown rice were identified. A novel beverage was developed on the basis of the analysis of the antioxidant function and key metabolites of the fermentation broth. GABA-rich beverages play an important role in the development of diverse functional foods, offering a promising avenue for addressing health-related needs through innovative food formulations.

Materials and methods

Materials

Tryptone and yeast extract were purchased from OXOID (UK). Glucose, sucrose, beef extract, anhydrous sodium acetate, FeSO4, MgSO4, MnSO4 and sodium glutamate were purchased from Shanghai Xinhe Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Fructose, xylose, lactose, maltose, starch, β-cyclodextrin, arabinose, trehalose and whey protein were obtained from Aladdin Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Soy peptone, wheat peptone, corn steep liquor dry powder and cottonseed peptone were purchased from Beijing Hongrun Baoshun Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). GABA and casein were purchased from Sigma‒Aldrich. The reagents used for high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) were of chromatographic grade. In addition, all the above experimental reagents were of analytical grade. The dried C. militaris fruiting bodies were purchased from Xuzhou Hongyu Agricultural Technology Co., Ltd. The fruiting bodies were cultivated on rice media and dried at 60 °C for 2 h.

Daqu, a strongly flavored liquor, was obtained from Anhui Gujing Gongjiu Co., Ltd., China. To prepare germinated brown rice flour, commercially available brown rice was soaked in water (1:1) at room temperature for 12 h, spread flat on wet gauze, and germinated at 37 °C for 24 h. The buds were 3–5 mm in length and then dried at 60 °C for 4 h. Germinated brown rice was ground via a crusher and passed through a 100-mesh sieve to obtain germinated brown rice flour. To prepare the C. militaris fruiting body extract, 40 g of dry C. militaris fruiting body was added to 1 L of ultrapure water, the mixture was placed in a boiling water bath for 2 h, filtered through four layers of gauze, and then replenished with 1 L of water to obtain the C. militaris fruiting body extract solution (CFS solution).

Medium

MRS medium (Leroy and De Vuyst, 2001): 20 g/L glucose, 5 g/L yeast extract powder, 10 g/L beef extract, 10 g/L peptone, 5 g/L sodium acetate, 2 g/L diammonium hydrogen citrate, 1 mL/L Tween 80, 2 g/L K2HPO4, 0.58 g/L MgSO4, and 0.25 g/L MnSO4. The MRS solid medium was MRS medium supplemented with 2 % (w/v) agar.

CFS medium: 50 % (v/v) CFS solution, 60 g/L glucose, 1.2 g/L soy peptone, 6 g/L germinated brown rice flour, 0.1 g/L NaCl, 0.1 g/L MgSO4, 0.2 g/L MnSO4, and 0.1 g/L FeSO4.

Screening and identification of high-yielding GABA-producing strains

The lactic acid bacteria screening method was described by Li et al. (Li et al., 2024; Lim and Im, 2009) The Daqu solution was diluted to 10−5 with physiological saline (0.9 % NaCl), and 100 μL of the diluted solution was spread on MRS plates containing 1 % CaCO3 and 1 % sodium glutamate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The seed solutions were cultured according to Gong et al. (2019). An inoculation loop was used to pick a milky white single colony with a clear circle to isolate the lactic acid bacteria, which was added to 20 mL of MRS medium and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h to prepare the primary seed broth. The secondary seed broth was prepared by pipetting 5 mL of primary seed broth into 50 mL of MRS medium and culturing at 37 °C for 15 h. Then, 2 mL of secondary seed broth was aspirated into 20 mL of CFS medium, 10 g/L monosodium glutamate was added, and the mixture was cultured at 37 °C for 72 h. High-yield GABA-producing strains were screened by measuring the GABA concentration via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The screened high-yielding strain was named GH1.

Genomic DNA was extracted from the strain GH1, and the 16S rDNA gene was amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with the primers 27F (5′-AGGTTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-TACGGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). The PCR mixture included the bacterial genome (10 ng), 1 μL each of 27F (10 mM), 1492R (10 mM), and 12.5 μL of PrimeSTAR Max DNA polymerase and 9.5 μL of ddH2O. The PCR conditions were as follows: predenaturation at 98 °C for 40 s, denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 55 °C for 15 s, extension at 72 °C for 90 s, and 30 cycles of extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The PCR products were subsequently sequenced by China Shanghai Sangon Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The strain GH1 was identified via BLAST analysis. The multiple sequences were aligned via Mega 11 software, and a phylogenetic tree was constructed via the neighbor joining (NJ) algorithm.

Safety and probiotic evaluation of strain GH1

Safety evaluation of strain GH1

A total of 0.1 % precursor amino acids (Lys, Arg, His) were added to the secondary seed broth, which was then streaked (100 μL) on MRS medium and cultured in a 37 °C incubator for 48 h. Then, the plate was covered with an upper layer of medium containing 0.006 % bromocresol violet and 2 % agar. The samples were covered with the upper layer of medium (0.006 % bromocresol violet and 2 % agar), and the color of the colonies was observed to detect the biogenic amines. Yellow indicates negative results. Red or purple indicates positive results. One hundred microliters of secondary seed broth was spread onto a toluidine blue DNase agar plate and incubated for 48 h to observe the growth of the strains and the formation of hydrolysis circles to detect DNase production. A total of 100 μL of the secondary seed broth was applied to a Columbia blood agar plate for 48 h. A hemolytic cycle was observed, and the type of hemolysis was determined.

A 6 mm disc of filter paper was punched with a hole punch and prepared with different concentrations of antibiotics, including 40 mg/mL erythromycin, 30 mg/mL cephalexin, 30 mg/mL vancomycin, 50 mg/mL penicillin, 30 mg/mL tetracycline, 4 mg/mL rifampicin and 30 mg/mL kanamycin. The secondary seed broth (50 μL) was smeared on the plate. Then, sterile filter paper was placed in the center of the plate, and 1 μL of antibiotics was spotted. After 24 h of culture, the formation of an inhibition zone was observed.

Evaluation of the probiotic properties of strain GH1

The cells of strain GH1 were collected and resuspended in normal saline after being cultured for 24 h in MRS. The cell count was controlled at 1.71 ± 0.21∗109 cfu/mL. This constituted the bacterial suspension. The bacterial suspension was inoculated at an inoculation amount of 10 % under different simulated environments to evaluate the cell survival ability. The gastric acid was prepared by adjusting the pH of MRS to 2 or 3 with HCl. The simulated gastric juice was composed of 16.4 mL/L 1 mol/L HCl and 10 g/L pepsin. The composition of the simulated intestinal juice was 13.6 g/L KH2PO4 and 20 g/L trypsin, with a pH of 6.8. Bile salts were added to the MRS medium, and the concentration of bile salts was controlled between 0.3 % and 1 % (w/v). The osmotic pressure of MRS was adjusted with NaCl such that the salt concentration ranged from 1 % to 4 % (w/v). The cells were subsequently cultivated for various durations, followed by cell counting to assess cell tolerance. The cells were incubated statically at 37 °C. The optical density at 600 nm was measured every 0.5 h to monitor autoaggregation. Autoaggregation (%)= (1-OD600(t)/OD600(0))/100.

Response surface optimization

On the basis of the univariate variable method used to assess the effect of each single factor, Box–Benhnken response surface optimization was performed on the basis of the carbon source concentration, nitrogen source concentration, ratio of CFS solution and water, culture temperature, and initial pH of the medium.

The Box‒Benhnken response surface statistical experiment was designed via Design Expert 13 software (Stat-Ease, USA) with five factors and three levels. The Box–Benhnken design was implemented 46 times, and the second-order polynomial model equation was used. Two levels are set for each factor, i.e., low level (−1) and high level (+1), to optimize five variables. In the subsequent response surface analysis, the selection of specific factors as parameters was determined by their statistical significance (P < 0.05), and the model was subjected to statistical analysis to assess the variance and the correlation coefficient R2 to assess the quality of the polynomial model equations. The statistical significance was determined by the F test.

Analysis of antioxidant function

Determination of the Fe2+ chelation ability

Fermentation supernatant (20 μL), 5 mmol/L ferrozine (20 μL) and 2 mmol/L FeCl2 (10 μL) were added sequentially. After mixing, the total volume was adjusted to 500 μL with distilled water and left to stand at 25 °C for 20 min. The absorbance A1 was measured at 562 nm. The sample was replaced with an equal volume of distilled water as blank A0, the FeCl2 was replaced with an equal volume of distilled water as blank A2, and the obtained absorbance was used to evaluate the chelation rate (the calculation method is shown in Formula 1).

Determination of DPPH free radical scavenging ability

A 20 μL fermentation supernatant was taken, 200 μL of 0.04 mg/mL DPPH-ethanol solution was added, the total volume of the solution was increased to 400 μL with 70 % ethanol, and the mixture was shaken. After standing in the dark at 25 °C for 20 min, the absorbance (A1) of the solution was measured at 517 nm. The sample was replaced with an equal volume of absolute ethanol as blank A0, and blank A2 was replaced with an equal volume of absolute ethanol for DPPH. The clearance rate was calculated on this basis (the calculation method is shown in Formula 2).

Determination of the ability to scavenge hydroxyl free radicals

A total of 30 μL of fermentation supernatant, 100 μL of 9 mmol/L FeSO4 solution, 100 μL of 9 mmol/L salicylic acid-ethanol solution, 100 μL of 8.8 mmol/L H2O2 solution, and distilled water were added sequentially. The mixture was adjusted to 500 μL and shaken well, and the absorbance A1 was measured at 510 nm. An equal volume of distilled water was used to replace the sample as blank A0, and H2O was replaced with an equal volume of distilled water as blank A2. The clearance rate was calculated on the basis of the absorbance (the calculation method is shown in Formula 3).

Analytical methods

Biomass measurement

The biomass density was determined at a wavelength of 600 nm via a SpectraMax i3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Santa Clara, California, USA).

GABA content

A Shimadzu liquid chromatographer (LC-16; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) was used to detect GABA production. GABA is derivatized by the reaction of β-thioethanol and o-phthalaldehyde into a substance with strong fluorescence, which can be detected by UV detection(Le et al., 2020; Rea et al., 2005). HPLC mobile phase A: 4.52 g of anhydrous sodium acetate was dissolved in 900 mL of ultrapure water, and the volume was adjusted to a final volume of 1 L with ultrapure water. The pH was adjusted to 7.2 with acetic acid. HPLC mobile phase B: First, 2.26 g of anhydrous sodium acetate was dissolved in 90 mL of ultrapure water to a final volume of 100 mL, and then 400 mL of methanol and 400 mL of acetonitrile were added. The pump flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, the column temperature was 40 °C, and the injection volume was 10 μL. The GABA content was determined via an SPD-16UV/VIS detector (Shimadzu in Kyoto, Japan) with a UV detector at a wavelength of 338 nm. The chromatographic column used was an Agilent HC-C18 (Santa Clara, CA, USA). The measured peak area of GABA was compared with that of the standard GABA sample to quantify the GABA content. The chromatographic software SISC-LAB was used for peak height measurement.

Detection of organic acids

The mobile phase for high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) consisted of 0.005 M sulfuric acid (Wang et al., 2013). The pump flow rate was 0.6 mL/min. The column temperature was 60 °C. The sample injection volume was 10 μL. The organic acid content was determined via a Shimadzu LC-16 pump and a Shimadzu RID-20A differential reactive index detector (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with an AMINEX HPX-87H column (Bio-Rad, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) (Zheng et al., 2013). The concentrations of organic acids were quantified by comparing the peak area with the corresponding standard for organic acids. SISC-LAB chromatography software can measure peak heights. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Metabolomic analysis

Sample preparation

A 50-μL sample was mixed with 200 μl of extraction solution (methanol:acetonitrile = 1:1 (v/v), including internal standards), shaken at 750 rpm for 5 min, allowed to settle for 5 min, and then filtered through a protein precipitation plate, after which the filtrate was collected. The quality control (QC) sample was prepared by mixing an equal aliquot of the filtered liquid of each sample.

LC‒MS analysis

For polar metabolite analysis, a Vanquish (Thermo Fisher Scientific) ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm) was used for chromatographic separation of the target compounds (Wang et al., 2016). Mobile phase A consisted of water containing 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 25 mmol/L ammonia, while mobile phase B consisted of acetonitrile. The sample tray temperature was maintained at 4 °C with an injection volume of 2 μL. An Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer was used for primary and secondary mass spectrometry data acquisition under the control of Xcalibur software (version 4.4, Thermo). The detailed parameters were as follows: sheath gas flow rate: 50 Arb; auxiliary gas flow rate: 15 Arb; capillary temperature: 320 °C; full MS resolution: 60000; MS/MS resolution: 15000; collision energy: SNCE 20/30/40; spray voltage: 3.8 kV (positive) or −3.4 kV (negative).

Data processing

The raw data were converted to mzXML format via ProteoWizard software (V3.0.24054), followed by metabolite identification via a collaboratively developed R package with the BiotreeDB database (V3.0). Visualization analysis was performed via an independently developed R package.

Statistical methods

Three biological replicates were used for each experiment, and the results are expressed as the mean and standard deviation (±SD) of the repeats. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using various media components and culture conditions as the main influencing factors. The significance analysis was performed via SPSS 27 software. The P value was less than 0.05 at the 95 % confidence level. Graphs were created via GraphPad Prism 9 software, and the data are expressed as the means and standard deviations of three biological replicates. The error bars in the graphs represent the standard deviations of the means.

Results and discussion

Screening high-yielding GABA-producing strains in the fruiting body extract of C. militaris

Lactic acid bacteria have significant potential to produce GABA via fermentation in food and are generally recognized as safe (GRAS) for food safety (Nishimura et al., 2016). It is necessary to screen lactic acid bacteria that can yield high amounts of GABA in the fruiting body extract of C. militaris. The organic acid produced by lactic acid bacteria dissolves CaCO3 around the colonies, creating calcium dissolution circles for the identification of lactic acid bacteria (Carr et al., 2002). Using the CaCO3 indicator, we selected 17 strains that produced obvious hydrolysis circles from the enriched culture solution of Daqu from Anhui Gujing Gongjiu Co., Ltd. After being cultured in MRS medium, all the strains were able to produce GABA and lactic acid. These strains were inoculated into CFS medium supplemented with 10 g/L MSG to determine whether the strains could grow and produce GABA. As shown in Fig. 1A, all 17 strains could grow in the fruiting body extract of C. militaris, with a cell concentration (OD600) ranging from 1.52 to 2.45, indicating that the CFS medium provided sufficient nutritional conditions for strain growth. Among them, strain L51B exhibited the best growth. However, this did not correspond to GABA production (Fig. 1B). The GABA production ability of strain L51B was only 1.83 g/L. All the screened strains produced GABA in the extract of the fruiting bodies of C. militaris, with yields ranging from 0.51 to 2.72 g/L. Notably, strain GH1 presented the greatest GABA production ability, at 2.72 ± 0.59 g/L (Fig. 1B). Therefore, strain GH1 was chosen as the focus of further research.

Morphological characteristics and identification of strain GH1

As shown in Fig. 2A, strain GH1 was identified as a gram-positive strain with a cell morphology characterized as short and rod shaped. The cells measured between 10 and 16 μm in length and 5–7 μm in width (Fig. 2B). Next, we confirmed the identity of strain GH1 through 16S rDNA sequencing, which yielded a sequence of 1457 bp. A homology comparison was conducted, and a phylogenetic tree was constructed via MEGA software. The analysis revealed that strains GH1 and Levilactobacillus brevis ATCC 14869T EU194349 belong to the same species, exhibiting 100 % homology. Therefore, strain GH1 was confirmed to be Levilactobacillus brevis (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, studies have indicated that Levilactobacillus brevis possesses two genes encoding glutamate decarboxylase (Lyu et al., 2016; Wu and Shah, 2017). This genetic characteristic may explain the high production of GABA observed in GH1 when it was extracted from the fruiting body of C. militaris.

Safety evaluation and potential prebiotic properties of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1

Safety evaluation of Lactobacillus strain GH1

In the development of functional fermented foods, the safety and probiotic capabilities of lactic acid bacteria are key points, and it is necessary to rule out potential safety risks. Therefore, we explored the safety and probiotic potential of strain GH1. Biogenic amines are a class of harmful substances that may adversely affect human health. Excessive intake of biogenic amines can lead to symptoms such as headache and hypertension, among other health problems (Omer et al., 2021). To assess the safety of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1, experiments were conducted to evaluate its ability to produce biogenic amines. Additionally, a hemolysis test, which detects the ability of lactic acid bacteria to dissolve red blood cells, was performed to assess their pathogenicity and potential harm. The results revealed that Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 did not produce biogenic amines, had no DNase activity, and exhibited no hemolytic activity, indicating that it could not cause lysis of erythrocytes.

To further ensure the safety of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 as a probiotic candidate strain, antibiotic resistance tests were conducted. The results, shown in Table 2, demonstrate that strain GH1 is sensitive to tetracycline, penicillin, erythromycin, rifampin, and cephalexin. However, studies have shown that the majority of lactic acid bacteria strains, including Levilactobacillus brevis, exhibit resistance to vancomycin (Jomehzadeh et al., 2020; Li et al., 2015). The varying drug resistance among different strains is attributed to differences in environmental conditions and the silencing or insufficient expression of drug resistance genes (Pan et al., 2011). These findings collectively indicate that Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 is a safe lactic acid bacterial strain for potential use in probiotic applications.

Prebiotic properties of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1

In addition to their safety, the prebiotic properties of lactic acid bacteria are crucial for determining their functional benefits in the human body. The self-aggregation ability of lactic acid bacteria, for example, plays a significant role in their adhesion to enterocytes and their ability to avoid pathogen colonization (Divisekera et al., 2019; Nikolic et al., 2010). As shown in Fig. 3A, strain GH1 exhibited tolerance to gastric acid under simulated satiety conditions (pH 3.0). Although the number of bacteria decreased slightly after 4 h, the reduction was only 6 %, indicating that strain GH1 can withstand the effects of gastric acid. This finding aligns with the observation that most probiotics decrease in number under conditions of low gastric acid pH (Abdelazez et al., 2022). Moreover, when cultured in simulated gastric juice for 3 h or in simulated intestinal fluid for 8 h, the number of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 cells not only remained stable but also increased by 2.79 % and 3.35 %, respectively (Fig. 3B and C). These results suggest that strain GH1 can successfully colonize the human gastrointestinal tract after oral administration. This finding is consistent with the study by Hojjati (Hojjati et al., 2020), which demonstrated that Levilactobacillus brevis is strongly tolerant to simulated gastrointestinal fluids.

Although Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 showed robust tolerance to gastric acid and intestinal fluids, its viability was tested under various bile salt concentrations. As the bile salt concentration increased, the number of viable Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 colonies significantly decreased (Fig. 3D), indicating that high bile salt concentrations could inhibit cell growth. This observation aligns with previous studies showing that the viability of probiotic organisms decreases as the bile salt concentration increases (Balasingham et al., 2017). Notably, when the bile salt concentration was 0.3 % and the culture time was 4 h, there was no significant difference in the activity of the strain compared with that of the control group, suggesting that a low concentration of bile salt had no adverse effect on the tolerance of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1.

In addition to bile salts, osmotic pressure is another critical factor affecting the survival of probiotic strains in the human intestine. High-salt environments (hyperosmotic pressure) can inhibit the growth of certain bacteria, making it essential for probiotic strains to withstand such conditions (Prabhurajeshwar and Chandrakanth, 2017). As shown in Fig. 3E, the viability of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 was almost unaffected by varying osmotic pressures, indicating that the strain is well adapted to high-osmolarity environments. This finding is consistent with other studies showing that Levilactobacillus brevis exhibits better adaptability under high-osmolarity conditions.

The self-aggregation ability of microorganisms is a key indicator of their ability to colonize and survive in the intestinal tract. This ability is closely related to their adhesion and survival capabilities, which in turn influence their functional effects in the gut. As shown in Fig. 3F, the self-aggregation ability of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 increased significantly with increasing storage time. After 0.5 h of incubation, the self-aggregation ability of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 reached 86.68 ± 0.02 %. By 3.0 h, this ability increased further, reaching 97.20 ± 0.03 %. This study revealed that when the self-aggregation ability was greater than 50 %, the strain demonstrated good probiotic potential. In addition, the self-aggregation rate of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 strain was significantly greater than that of Lactobacillus plantarum B13 strain, at only 85 %, which demonstrates its substantial advantage in terms of intestinal colonization (Rashmi et al., 2018). These findings collectively indicate that Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 is a safe strain with significant potential for use in probiotic applications.

Effects of nutrient components and culture conditions on the growth and GABA production of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1

Effects of individual conditions on the growth and GABA production of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 in CFS medium

Nutrient composition and cultivation conditions significantly influence microbial growth and product synthesis (Sun et al., 2022). To elucidate the growth patterns of Levilactobacillus brevis and its GABA production characteristics from CFS medium, initial studies were conducted under single-condition settings to assess cell growth and GABA production. As depicted in Fig. 4A, the growth of the strain increased with increasing inoculation ratios of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1. The maximum growth was achieved at an inoculation ratio of 8, with an OD600 of 3.92 ± 0.19. However, higher inoculation ratios suppressed GABA production. Across inoculation ratios from 1 to 8, there was no significant difference in GABA production by Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 (Fig. 5A). Therefore, to maximize GABA synthesis in CFS medium, an inoculation ratio of 1 is optimal, resulting in GABA production of 2.44 ± 0.25 g/L.

Having optimized the inoculation ratio, we next examined the influence of different carbon sources on cell growth and GABA production. The type of carbon source markedly affected both the growth of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 and its ability to synthesize GABA (Fig. 4, Fig. 5B). The strain exhibited varying utilization of polysaccharides, disaccharides, and monosaccharides. Growth was most robust when lactose and starch were used as carbon sources, resulting in the highest cell concentration of 5.44 ± 0.14. Polysaccharides and disaccharides had greater effects on GABA enrichment in the CFS medium. Sucrose, in particular, led to the highest GABA production, at 4.69 ± 0.85 g/L (Fig. 5B). Thus, sucrose is the preferred carbon source for Levilactobacillus brevis GH1. However, an increase in sucrose concentration did not increase cell growth, indicating that high sucrose levels may inhibit bacterial growth (Fig. 4C). Conversely, GABA production by Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 increased with increasing sucrose concentration before decreasing; the maximum GABA production was observed between 30 g/L and 90 g/L sucrose, with no significant difference (Fig. 5C). These findings suggest that excessive sucrose concentrations also inhibit GABA synthesis by Levilactobacillus brevis GH1. This observation aligns with findings by Abdel-Rahman, who reported that high glucose concentrations increase the cellular osmotic pressure, leading to cell death (Abdel-Rahman et al., 2013). Therefore, considering both cell growth and GABA production, a sucrose concentration of 30 g/L is recommended.

Following optimization of the carbon source, the effects of nitrogen sources on cell growth and GABA production were investigated. As shown in Fig. 4D, different nitrogen sources did not significantly affect cell growth. However, they had a substantial effect on GABA production by Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 (Fig. 5D). Compared with other sources, nitrogen sources such as silkworm pupae powder, wheat peel powder, soybean powder, corn steep liquor powder, and cottonseed powder led to significantly greater GABA production. Notably, corn steep liquor powder yielded the highest GABA production, at 6.30 ± 0.40 g/L. In contrast, nitrogen sources such as wheat bran and casein inhibited GABA production by Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 (Fig. 5D). Therefore, corn steep liquor powder is the preferred nitrogen source. Increasing the concentration of corn steep liquor powder gradually increased the growth of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 cells (Fig. 4E), but the difference was not significant. Fig. 5E shows that GABA production increased with increasing corn steep liquor powder concentration up to 0.6 g/L, beyond which it started to decrease. At 0.6 g/L, GABA production peaked at 6.01 ± 0.56 g/L (Fig. 3E). Hence, the optimal concentration of corn steep liquor powder is 0.6 g/L.

Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 is a promising strain for GABA production because of its ability to synthesize GABA from sodium glutamate via the enzyme glutamate decarboxylase. The concentration of sodium glutamate in fermentation medium plays a crucial role in GABA production(Iimure et al., 2009). As sodium glutamate enters the cell through the antiporter protein mechanism in the cell membrane, GABA synthesis begins (Kook and Cho, 2013). Research indicates that GABA production is directly influenced by the amount of glutamate decarboxylated by the glutamate decarboxylase enzyme (Alizadeh et al., 2020). Fig. 4F shows that as the concentration of monosodium glutamate (MSG) increased, the growth of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 also increased gradually. Similarly, the GABA-producing ability of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 increased with increasing concentrations of MSG (Fig. 5F). In contrast to some studies suggesting that high concentrations of glutamate inhibit lactic acid bacteria growth (Tajabadi et al., 2015), Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 does not show inhibition at relatively high MSGs. Considering the residual MSG in the CFS medium and the accumulation of GABA, the concentration of MSG for the next stage of fermentation was set at 20 g/L, resulting in a GABA production of 8.53 ± 0.35 g/L. The ability of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 to enrich GABA in CFS medium initially increased but then decreased with increasing concentrations of the extract. These findings suggest that low concentrations of CFS solution can activate GABA production in Levilactobacillus brevis, whereas high concentrations inhibit GABA production, possibly due to the antibacterial properties of the extract (Ahn et al., 2000). As shown in Fig. 4, Fig. 5G, when the addition ratio of CFS solution reached 50 %, the growth of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 was greatest, with an OD600 of 3.92 ± 0.27, and the cumulative GABA content reached 9.39 ± 0.13 g/L.

Regulating fermentation temperature is essential for optimizing biosynthetic efficiency. Our results indicate that the growth of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 increased with increasing fermentation temperature, suggesting that neither high nor low temperatures inhibited its growth (Fig. 4H). However, GABA production varies with temperature. Optimal GABA production was achieved at 37 °C (Fig. 5H). Therefore, 37 °C was selected as the optimal culture temperature for maximizing GABA production. The pH of the fermentation medium significantly influences both bacterial growth and GABA production. Glutamate decarboxylase is active under acidic conditions, with optimal activity typically observed in the pH range of 4.0–6.0 (Rashmi et al., 2018). Our study revealed that Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 grows better at pH values between 5 and 9, with growth inhibition occurring at pH values above 9 (Fig. 4I). GABA production was observed across a pH range of 3–11, with the highest production occurring at pH 5, where it reached 11.91 ± 0.53 g/L (Fig. 5I). This aligns with previous findings where Lactobacillus plantarum B13 exhibited optimal GABA production at pH 5.5 (Rashmi et al., 2018). Similar observations have been made in other Lactobacillus species, such as Lactobacillus fermentum and Lactobacillus futsaii CS3, which produce GABA at an initial pH of 5.0 (Rayavarapu et al., 2021; Thongruck and Maneerat, 2023). However, Lu et al. reported that Lactococcus lactis subsp. lactis had optimal GABA production at pH 7.1 (Lu et al., 2009), highlighting that the optimal pH for GABA production varies among different strains. Overall, Lactobacillus species generally exhibit a strong ability to produce GABA in weakly acidic environments. These findings provide valuable insights for enhancing GABA production in industrial applications and contribute to the understanding of the metabolic capabilities of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1.

These findings suggest that an appropriate ratio of carbon and nitrogen sources plays a critical role in GABA synthesis. The initial pH is particularly crucial, as it interacts with the sodium glutamate concentration, necessitating adjustments on the basis of this concentration. When the optimal parameters for GABA production were applied, we achieved a carbon source concentration of 30.83 g/L, a nitrogen source concentration of 1.04 g/L, an addition amount of CFS solution of 52.74 %, a temperature of 34.40 °C, and a pH of 4.06. This led to a maximum yield of 16.83 ± 0.22 g/L (Fig. 6H), corresponding to a 92.8 % match with the predicted value (Fig. S1). However, shifting the focus from specific food-based fermentation methods to comparisons between different media types, we find that it is also important to consider the difference in GABA production between complex media and these specific matrices. Complex media usually provide a more balanced and abundant nutrient environment, which is more conducive to bacterial growth and GABA production. Thus, the capacity for GABA production in complex media often exceeds that in specific food matrices. Lactobacillus brevis CRL 2013, which was isolated from quinoa-sorghum sourdough, achieved a GABA production of 265 mM after 72 h of fermentation in MRS-GF media supplemented with 267 mM MSG (Cataldo et al., 2020). In addition, Lactobacillus brevis NCL912 can achieve a GABA yield of 205.8 ± 8.0 g/L in 10 L of complex medium (Wang et al., 2018). When L-glutamic acid and MSG were used as substrates to maintain acidity for GABA production, Levilactobacillus brevis CD0817 reached a GABA yield of 353.1 ± 8.3 g/L (Wang et al., 2024). This disparity can be attributed to the fact that GABA production by bacteria is profoundly influenced by the MSG concentration, fermentation duration, and substrate composition. In addition to the differences in media, Lactobacillus brevis is superior to other lactic acid bacterial strains in terms of GABA production. Compared with other lactic acid bacterial strains, such as Lactiplantibacillus plantarum K16, which produces approximately 1000 mg/L GABA after 96 h of fermentation under optimized conditions (Diez-Gutierrez et al., 2022), this advantage stems from its more efficient glutamate decarboxylase system, which typically possesses at least two functional glutamate decarboxylase genes (gadA and gadB). This genetic diversity provides the fundamental basis for both high-efficiency GABA production and acid stress tolerance.

Antioxidant analysis of the GABA-enriched C. militaris fruiting body extract

To elucidate the function of the GABA-rich fruiting body extract of C. militaris, we assessed its antioxidant ability and organic acid metabolites. As shown in Fig. 7A, the DPPH scavenging ability of the solution increased with increasing content of unfermented CFS, both before and after fermentation, indicating that the extract possesses DPPH scavenging ability. After fermentation by Levilactobacillus brevis GH1, the DPPH scavenging capacity of the fermented culture filtrate supernatant (fermented CFS) significantly increased, especially when the CFS content reached 100 %, which was 1.7 times greater than that of the unfermented CFS. The enhancement magnitude observed in this study is significantly greater than that of previously reported natural antioxidants. For example, the maximum DPPH scavenging capacity of tea oil flower polysaccharides was 84.19 %, while the effect achieved in our study was considerably more pronounced (Chen et al., 2020). Similarly, as illustrated in Fig. 7B, the CFS exhibited the ability to chelate Fe2+. Notably, when the fermented CFS content reached 60 %, its ability to chelate Fe2+ significantly increased, with a 3.21-fold increase at 100 % CFS content compared with that of the unfermented CFS. These findings align with those of Yang et al. (2022), who reported similar increases in the DPPH scavenging activity and iron ion-reducing ability of fermentation solutions derived from Porphyra yezoensis. This increase is likely associated with metabolites produced during microbial fermentation, including lactic acid, fructose, phenols, and flavonoids (Sarıtaş et al., 2024).

Moreover, as shown in Fig. 7C, the hydroxyl radical scavenging ability of the fermented CFS after fermentation was significantly greater than that of the unfermented CFS; at a 100 % CFS content, the hydroxyl free radical scavenging ability was 2.72 times greater. Overall, these results indicate that the fermented CFS with Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 has improved DPPH scavenging ability, Fe2+ chelation ability, and hydroxyl free radical scavenging ability. The 2.72-fold improvement in the hydroxyl radical scavenging capacity also surpasses the hydroxyl radical scavenging rates of C. militaris-fermented mung beans 48.13 (Zhu et al., 2018). Additionally, the DPPH radical and hydroxyl radical scavenging capacities of lactic acid bacteria-fermented red bean milk increased by only 20.52 % and 14.17 %, respectively (Jing et al., 2022), making our results significantly more remarkable. This demonstrates that, compared with single-component systems, this research achieves synergistic enhancement between the active ingredients of C. militaris and GABA, indicating the existence of a synergistic antioxidant effect between these two components.

In addition to their antioxidant properties, we measured the major organic acid products of the unfermented CFS after fermentation. As shown in Fig. 7D, the predominant organic acids in the fermented CFS included lactic acid, acetic acid, succinic acid, citric acid, and malic acid. Among these, lactic acid was present at the highest concentration (19.16 ± 0.97 g/L), contributing to acidification of the intestinal tract, improving the intestinal environment, and promoting intestinal peristalsis. Furthermore, lactic acid serves as a natural preservative, effectively enhancing the shelf-life of food. Conversely, oxalic acid, which is also present in a significant amount (11.53 ± 0.16 g/L), can react with metal ions to form stable compounds, thus potentially preventing metal ion-catalyzed oxidation reactions. This may explain the observed increase in the antioxidant capacity of the fermented CFS. Citric acid and malic acid facilitate increased gastric juice secretion and promote gastrointestinal peristalsis while alleviating abdominal distension. In summary, the fruiting body extract of C. militaris, which is derived from nutrient-rich brown rice and fermented with Levilactobacillus brevis GH1, yields a beverage rich in GABA and organic acids with notable antioxidant activity. This is significant for the development of new beverages.

Effects of metabolites on antioxidant activity

To investigate the reasons for the improvement in the antioxidant activity of the beverage, we conducted metabolomic detection on the unfermented CFS medium (CFS Group) and the supernatant of fermented CFS (CFSLF Group). The main components of the three samples in the CFS group and the main components (PC1 and PC2) of the three samples in the CFSLF group are all within the 95 % confidence interval, which shows that there are obvious differences in the metabolic profiles between CFS and CFSLF (Fig. 8A). All the differentially abundant metabolites between the two groups are shown in Fig. 8B and C. There are a total of 1433 differentially abundant metabolites presented in 14 categories, which include organoheterocyclic compounds (381, 26.59 %), organic acids and derivatives (349, 24.35 %), benzenoids (187, 13.05 %), lipids and lipid-like molecules (148, 10.33 %), organic oxygen compounds (117, 8.16 %), phenylpropanoids and polyketides (117, 8.16 %), nucleosides, nucleotides, and analogs (37, 2.58 %), organic nitrogen compounds (36, 2.51 %), alkaloids and derivatives (16, 1.12 %), organosulfur compounds (6, 0.42 %), lignans, neolignans and related compounds (5, 0.35 %), organic 1,3-dipolar compounds (2, 0.14 %), organohalogen compounds (1, 0.07 %), and others (70, 4.88 %). A total of 793 significantly differentially abundant metabolites were identified in the CFSLF group, with 337 upregulated and 456 downregulated metabolites compared with those in the CFS group. The active substances showing significant increases in content included organic acids and their derivatives, organic heterocyclic compounds, lipids and lipid molecules, and benzene ring compounds (Fig. 8D).

As shown in Fig. 8E, the content of cordycepin in the postfermentation solution (CFSLF) was significantly decreased. As a nucleoside analog, the decrease in cordycepin content reflects a significant metabolic redirection in the nucleotide synthesis pathway, which may lead to more precursor substances being reallocated to the GABA synthesis pathway (Polak et al., 2021). This phenomenon is closely related to the competitive relationship between nucleotide metabolism and amino acid metabolism. As a key intermediate metabolite in the degradation pathway of cordycepin (3′-deoxyadenosine), hypoxanthine was significantly upregulated after fermentation, indicating that cordycepin was effectively degraded, thereby releasing abundant purine metabolism precursor substances (van Rensburg & Van den Ende, 2020). The purine metabolism and alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolic pathways were significantly activated, which indicates that the α-ketoglutarate produced by hypoxanthine metabolism may promote the supply of glutamate, thereby improving the GABA synthesis ability of the strain (Cataldo et al., 2020). The significant upregulation of α-ketoglutarate also confirms this metabolic flux and provides the necessary energy and carbon skeleton support for cell metabolism. In addition, the upregulation of pyridoxine plays an important role in the formation of the coenzyme of glutamate decarboxylase and can further promote the production of GABA. This is consistent with the catalytic role of vitamin B6 in amino acid metabolism indicated in the study by Llamas et al. (Llamas et al., 2020) Therefore, the degradation of cordycepin in C. militaris promotes metabolic reconfiguration, releases purine precursors, produces ketoglutarate, promotes the synthesis of glutamate, increases the precursors for GABA synthesis, and at the same time, the additional carbon skeletons and energy produced promote cell metabolism. Metabolic reconfiguration enhances vitamin B6 metabolism, strengthens the increase in the glutamate decarboxylase coenzyme, and provides a guarantee for the catalysis of glutamate decarboxylase. This metabolic redirection mechanism reflects the adaptive utilization of substrates by microorganisms, providing a new theoretical basis for improving GABA yield.

Moreover, sinapinic acid and 6-(acetyloxy)-2-naphthoic acid, which are representative phenolic compounds, play crucial roles (Fig. 8E). These substances can directly trap reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) via the phenolic hydroxyl structure and break the free-radical chain reaction, thereby strengthening the free-radical clearance mechanism (Halliwell, 2024). Moreover, they can simultaneously chelate transition metal ions such as Fe2+ and Cu2+ and inhibit the production of hydroxyl radicals via the Fenton reaction (Leyane et al., 2022). In addition, these phenolic compounds can regulate the enzyme system, activate the Nrf2/ARE signaling pathway, and upregulate endogenous antioxidant enzymes such as glutathione peroxidase (GPx) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) (Morris et al., 2019). Cyclo(his-pro) and cyclo(prolyltyrosyl) are upregulated cyclic dipeptides. The cyclic configuration of these compounds can increase the stability and bioavailability of antioxidant peptides. The phenolic hydroxyl structure of tyrosine also has the ability to scavenge free radicals. Moreover, the significant upregulation of the metabolite Trp-Ala and the significant downregulation of alanine may promote the transformation of amino acid metabolism toward dipeptides with increased antioxidant activity, which is consistent with the findings of Franco et al. (Franco et al., 2019). In addition, during fermentation, the production of lactate and reduction in adenine jointly maintain the intracellular redox balance. The metabolism of lactate results in the production of NADH (Bury et al., 2020). At the same time, the decrease in adenine decreases the cell's requirement for redox-sensitive nucleotides. These two factors work together to contribute to the overall antioxidant network (Leyane et al., 2022). D-Pinitol and L-glycine ether ester are able to stabilize the cell membrane structure, adjust the osmotic pressure equilibrium, and take part in the synthesis of glutathione. By doing so, they build a multilevel cellular protection barrier, which facilitates the generation of antioxidant substances (Bury et al., 2020). Collectively, these metabolites establish a synergistic antioxidant network and a comprehensive cellular protection system, which is highly important for their antioxidant effects.

Considering the relationships between metabolites and metabolic pathways, we analyzed the main metabolites and metabolic pathways involved in carbon utilization, amino acid synthesis, nucleotide metabolism, and general metabolism and transport systems. As shown in Fig. 8E, in the amino acid metabolic pathway, glutamate serves as the direct precursor for the synthesis of glutathione (GSH), and the upregulation of glutamate may promote the synthesis of GSH (Wang et al., 2022). Moreover, the “biosynthesis of amino acids” pathway is activated, providing sufficient precursors for the synthesis of antioxidant substances. During the carbon metabolism process, reducing powers such as NADPH are produced, and large amounts of organic acids are metabolized, which is consistent with the detection results in Fig. 7D. This carbon metabolism, in addition to the amino acid pathway, further contributes to the overall metabolic environment conducive to antioxidant production. In addition, pyrimidine metabolism and purine metabolism can provide energy for gene synthesis and metabolism and enhance anti-inflammatory and antioxidant functions (Wang et al., 2022). These nucleotide metabolic pathways work in harmony with the amino acid and carbon metabolic pathways to create a comprehensive metabolic network. Therefore, the antioxidant-functional beverage produced by Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 is mainly due to the increase in phenolic substances (sinapinic acid and 6-(acetyloxy)-2-naphthoic acid). The activation of the amino acid pathway provides a favorable environment for the production of these phenolic substances, which enhances iron-ion chelation and free-radical scavenging abilities and aids in the production of GPX and SOD. Cyclo (his-pro) and cyclo (prolyltyrosyl), products of the overall metabolic process, stabilize antioxidant peptides. D-Pinitol and L-glycine ether esters, which are also influenced by metabolic pathways, stabilize the cell membrane structure and form a cellular protection barrier.

Conclusion

This study aimed to obtain probiotic lactobacilli and develop a new functional beverage by producing high levels of GABA in a solution of Chinese C. militaris fruiting bodies and germinated brown rice. A strain of Levilactobacillus brevis GH1 was screened from the Chinese Baijiu Daqu. This strain is recognized as safe and possesses prebiotic properties, making it suitable for the development of GABA-containing functional foods. Fermentation resulted in significant GABA enrichment at 16.83 g/L in the final product. This functional beverage contains abundant probiotic Levilactobacillus brevis and various organic acids, demonstrating remarkable antioxidant properties, including DPPH scavenging ability, Fe2chelation capability, and hydroxyl free radical scavenging ability. These findings highlight the potential of this novel lactic acid bacteria-fermented beverage as a distinctive functional food product that contributes to the diversification of health-promoting food options.
