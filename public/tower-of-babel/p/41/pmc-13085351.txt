Sex differences in the associations between illness representations and behavioral intentions to seek early Alzheimer’s detection in Chinese older adults
Authors: Hui Lu, Sixuan Chen, Yiping Tang, Wenke He, Shenhang Xia, Yili Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common form of dementia in older adults with no cure. Early detection and intervention are critical for improving patient outcomes. However, behavioral intentions to seek early detection of AD (BI-SEDAD) remain low. Illness representations, referring to individuals’ beliefs about illness, may influence BI-SEDAD. This study aimed to investigate the associations between illness representations and BI-SEDAD among Chinese older adults, and potential sex differences in these associations.MethodsA survey was conducted among 509 Chinese older adults. Participants completed the assessment of BI-SEDAD and illness representations of AD. Moderation analyses were conducted using SPSS PROCESS.ResultsConsequences (β = 0.26), timeline (β = 0.23), illness identity (β = 0.22), illness coherence (β = 0.21), illness concern (β = 0.22), and emotional representations (β = 0.24) were positively associated with BI-SEDAD, whereas treatment control was negatively associated (β = − 0.10). Moreover, sex significantly moderated the associations between BI-SEDAD and consequences (β = − 0.20), treatment control (β = 0.21), illness identity (β = − 0.17), illness coherence (β = − 0.25), and illness concern (β = − 0.26). Specifically, the associations between BI-SEDAD and consequences, illness identity, illness coherence, and illness concern were stronger in males than in females. The association between BI-SEDAD and treatment control was significant only in males.ConclusionsIllness representations are important predictors of BI-SEDAD, with notable sex differences. Tailored interventions that address key beliefs and consider sex differences may promote early detection in older populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07288-y.Keywords: Illness perception, Behavioral intentions, Health-seeking, Alzheimer’s disease, Sex differences

Background

The global demographic shift toward population aging is accelerating and is expected to drive a substantial increase in dementia cases worldwide [1, 2]. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, the number of people living with dementia worldwide is expected to grow from 57.4 million in 2019 to 152.8 million by 2050 [3]. Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent form of dementia in older adults, accounting for approximately 60–70% of dementia cases worldwide [4]. AD is progressive and incurable, associated with high medical costs and caregiving demands [5]. Although no curative treatment exists, early detection is crucial, providing opportunities for timely intervention, care planning, and lifestyle modifications. Evidence shows that early diagnosis and intervention can delay cognitive decline, improve quality of life, and reduce long-term healthcare expenditures [6]. Consequently, promoting early screening and diagnosis has become a public health priority [7].

Despite these benefits, many older adults do not receive early screening and early diagnosis of AD [8]. Existing evidence indicates that barriers to early AD screening and detection are multifaceted rather than dominated by a single obstacle. Social and healthcare-system barriers, e.g., limited informal support and caregiving resources, suboptimal patient engagement and access to healthcare, cultural and linguistic barriers, workforce constraints, and competing healthcare priorities, have been shown to impede timely diagnosis [9]. Importantly, individual-level and knowledge-related barriers have been highlighted in previous studies, including denial, stigma, fear of diagnosis, symptom normalization, and low perceived need for assessment [10, 11]. To promote older adults seeking early screening and early diagnosis, it is necessary to identify modifiable psychological factors influencing behavioral intentions to seek early detection of AD (BI-SEDAD). In this context, BI-SEDAD refers to an individual’s intentions to seek professional assessment or screening in response to perceived cognitive changes, rather than actual diagnostic behaviors [12]. This construct represents an early stage in the help-seeking process, preceding clinical diagnosis and service utilization [8]. Research on such factors remains scarce, particularly in Chinese populations.

Illness representations, i.e., the cognitive and emotional beliefs individuals hold about illness, have been shown to shape health-related behaviors and intentions [13]. Within the Common-Sense Model of Illness, illness is conceptualized as a stressor that activates cognitive and emotional representations, which in turn guide coping responses, appraisal, and health-related behaviors [14]. Cognitive representations include perceived consequences (assessing the severity of the illness), timeline (considering whether the illness is acute/chronic), control (considering whether the illness is under personal or treatment control), identity (labelling the illness and identifying its symptoms), and coherence (overall comprehensibility of the illness). Emotional representations reflect affective responses such as worry, fear, or distress. These representations interact to influence coping strategies, health service utilization, and behavioral intentions. Importantly, illness representations are malleable and thus suitable intervention targets [15].

Empirical research across multiple health domains supports the predictive role of illness representations. Studies on chronic and infectious diseases have linked cognitive perceptions to health-seeking and adherence [16], while research in mental health contexts highlights their role in treatment utilization [17]. In the context of AD, accurate understanding of disease symptoms has been shown to encourage timely diagnosis [18]. Higher illness coherence and more favorable perceptions of AD consequences are associated with greater BI-SEDAD [8]. Conversely, stigmatizing or fatalistic beliefs may deter individuals from pursuing early detection [19]. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of examining both cognitive and emotional illness representations in predicting BI-SEDAD.

Emotional representations, in particular, may exert dual effects. On one hand, fear and concern about AD can motivate individuals to pursue early screening [20]. On the other, excessive worry may discourage help-seeking due to stigma or avoidance of distressing information [8]. These mixed findings underscore the complexity of emotional responses in shaping BI-SEDAD. Thus, more nuanced investigation is needed.

While illness representations are well established as predictors of health behaviors, their effects may operate differently across sex. Sex differences in AD are well-documented, with females generally exhibiting a higher prevalence, faster progression, and differing symptomatology compared to males [21–23]. Additionally, sex differences are salient in health research, as males and females often vary in health beliefs [24], cognitive appraisals [25], and help-seeking behaviors [26]. Females generally report greater illness concern, stronger emotional responses, and higher perceived consequences than males [27]. They are also more likely to seek cognitive screening and medical advice when noticing memory problems [27]. By contrast, males may underreport concerns due to traditional masculine norms discouraging vulnerability and reliance on healthcare, potentially delaying diagnosis [28]. These well-established sex differences in disease experience, emotional processing, and help-seeking behaviors provide a strong theoretical basis for examining sex as a moderating factor in the relationships between illness representations and BI-SEDAD.

In older adult populations, sex-related differences in caregiving roles, emotional engagement with illness, and attitudes toward medical help-seeking may shape how illness representations translate into BI-SEDAD. Older females, often primary caregivers and with longer life expectancy, may be more vigilant about AD threats and proactive in seeking screening [29]. In contrast, older males may avoid help-seeking due to stigma or concerns about status and independence [30]. Emerging evidence suggests that emotional representations, such as fear or worry, may more strongly influence females’ intentions, whereas cognitive factors like perceived control may be more salient for males [31]. Considering these population characteristics, examining sex as a moderator can help clarify distinct pathways through which illness representations influence BI-SEDAD and inform more targeted intervention strategies [32].

Given the aging population and the rising prevalence of AD, understanding modifiable determinants of BI-SEDAD is vital for fostering healthy aging. The present study investigated the associations between illness representations of AD and BI-SEDAD among Chinese older adults, with a focus on sex differences. We hypothesized that certain illness representations, including consequences, timeline, illness identity, illness coherence, illness concern, and emotional representations, would be positively associated with BI-SEDAD, while personal and treatment control would show negative associations. These factors have not been systematically examined in Chinese older adults, a population particularly vulnerable to late diagnosis [33, 34]. By clarifying the role of modifiable illness beliefs and potential sex differences, this study aims to provide evidence for developing targeted strategies to enhance early detection of AD in older populations.

Methods

Study design and data collection

An anonymous cross-sectional survey was conducted from March to July 2024 in Zhejiang Province, China, targeting the general older adult populations aged 60 years and above. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling approach from two urban communities. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) aged 60 years or older and (b) having adequate cognitive capacity to understand and respond to the questionnaire independently or with minimal help. Individuals with diagnosed severe cognitive impairment or serious physical illnesses that limited their ability to participate were excluded. No formal cognitive assessments or clinical screenings were conducted; eligibility was determined based on functional capacity to complete the questionnaire.

Participants were informed about the purpose of the study, its anonymity and voluntary nature, and the confidentiality of their responses. They were assured that participation or refusal would not affect any of their services or entitlements. Such information was also printed on the cover page of the questionnaire. Informed consent was obtained before administering the questionnaire. Data were collected through face-to-face administration of self-completed questionnaires in community settings. The questionnaire was primarily self-administered. All participants had received at least primary school education and were literate in Chinese. For a small number of participants who experienced difficulties due to reading or vision problems, trained research staff provided neutral assistance limited to reading items aloud or clarifying wording, without suggesting or influencing responses. This approach is commonly used in surveys involving older adult populations to reduce exclusion related to literacy or sensory limitations [35, 36]. It took approximately 10 to 15 min to complete. No personal identifiers were collected.

Due to the community-based and voluntary nature of recruitment, the total number of individuals approached and specific reasons for non-participation were not systematically recorded. Thus, a total of 522 completed questionnaires were collected. 13 were excluded due to poor data quality (i.e., missing more than 20% of responses or showing uniform answering patterns), or not meeting the age criterion. The final effective sample size was 509. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Kangning Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Approval No. YJ-2024-10-01).

Measures

Demographic variables

Demographic variables were collected, including age (years), sex (male/female), marital status (currently married/divorced/unmarried/widowed), educational background (primary school/middle school or equal/high school or equal/college or above), whether they had a medical background (yes/no), perceived family financial situation (very poor/poor/average/good/very good), chronic disease status (none/yes, not well-controlled/yes, well-controlled), belief in AD preventability (yes/no), whether having family members with AD (yes/no), and whether knowing someone has AD (no/yes, one or two/yes, three or four/yes, more than five).

BI-SEDAD

BI-SEDAD was measured using three items adapted from previous studies by Werner et al. and Shinan-Altman and Werner [8, 12]. The three items were “To what extent would you be ready to seek help from a general practitioner (GP) to find out whether you have AD, if you notice you are experiencing serious memory loss? ”, “ To what extent would you be ready to seek help from a GP to check whether you have AD if a family member notices you are experiencing serious memory loss”, and “To what extent would you be ready to seek help from a GP, as part of a routine medical examination, to determine whether you are experiencing a memory decline, even if you did not notice any memory loss?”. In the present study, the items were translated into Chinese using a forward–backward translation procedure and reviewed by bilingual researchers with expertise in gerontology and public health to ensure semantic clarity and cultural relevance. Participants responded to each item on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very frequently), with higher scores indicating greater behavioral intentions to seek early diagnosis for AD, rather than actual help-seeking behaviors. The scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in the current sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.81).

Illness representations of AD

Illness representations of AD were assessed using an adapted version of the 8-item Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire for AD (B-IPQ-AD), with each item rated on an 11-point Likert scale (0–10) [37]. The B-IPQ is grounded in the Common-Sense Model and has been demonstrated good reliability and validity across multiple illness conditions. It has also been psychometrically evaluated and widely applied in Chinese-speaking populations [38, 39]. In the present study, the standard B-IPQ items were adapted to explicitly refer to AD and a hypothetical diagnosis scenario, consistent with prior applications of the B-IPQ to specific illnesses [40]. The domain of consequences was assessed by “If you were diagnosed with Alzheimer’s Disease, how much would AD affect your life?” (0 = does not affect at all to 10 = severely affects my life), timeline by “How long do you think Alzheimer’s Disease condition would last?” (0 = a very short time to 10 = forever), personal control by “If you were diagnosed with Alzheimer’s Disease, how much control do you have over Alzheimer’s Disease conditions?” (0 = absolutely no control to 10 = extreme amount of control), treatment control by “How much do you think treatment can help Alzheimer’s Disease conditions?” (0 = not at all to 10 = extremely helpful), identity by “How many symptoms Alzheimer’s Disease can cause?” (0 = no symptoms at all to 10 = many severe symptoms). Illness coherence was assessed by “How well do you feel you understand Alzheimer’s Disease?” (0 = not understand at all to 10 = understand very well). Illness concern was assessed by “If you were diagnosed with AD, how concerned are you about your illness conditions?” (0 = not at all concerned to 10 = extremely concerned). And emotional representations were assessed by “If you were diagnosed with AD, how much have your illness conditions affected you emotionally (e.g., make you angry, scared, upset, or depressed)?” (0 = not at all to 10 = extremely). The scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in the current sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.87).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics. All primary variables, including BI-SEDAD and illness representations, were treated as continuous variables. Simple linear regression analyses were performed to explore the associations between demographic variables and BI-SEDAD. Subsequently, multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the associations between each illness representation dimension and BI-SEDAD. A forest plot was generated to visually summarize the standardized regression coefficients and 95% confidence intervals from these models, providing a supplementary comparison of effect sizes. To further examine the moderating effect, we conducted moderation analysis using PROCESS macro (Model 1) in SPSS. Prior to analysis, all continuous variables were mean-centered to reduce multicollinearity. Background variables that were significantly associated with the BI-SEDAD were included as covariates in all models. After identifying a significant interaction effect, simple slope analyses were performed. Standardized coefficients (β) were reported. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23.0, except for the forest plot, which was generated using R 4.3.2. Statistical significance was determined based on two-tailed p-values less than 0.05.

The males scored slightly lower on consequences, timeline, illness identity, illness coherence, illness concern, and emotional representations, while females scored slightly lower on personal and treatment control. No sex differences were found on BI-SEDAD.

Associations between demographic variables and BI-SEDAD

Several demographic variables were significantly associated with BI-SEDAD in univariate linear regression analyses. Specifically, higher educational level, better perceived family financial situation, presence of chronic disease, having a family member with AD, and knowing someone with AD were positively associated with BI-SEDAD, whereas having a medical background and belief in AD preventability were negatively associated with BI-SEDAD. Associations between illness representations of AD and BI-SEDAD

As shown in Table 2, in the overall sample, several illness representation dimensions were significantly associated with BI-SEDAD after adjusting for education level, medical background, perceived family financial situation, chronic disease status, belief in AD preventability, having a family member with AD, and knowing someone with AD. Specifically, higher perceived consequences (β = 0.26), longer perceived timeline (β = 0.23), stronger illness identity (β = 0.22), greater illness coherence (β = 0.21), higher illness concern (β = 0.22), and stronger emotional representations (β = 0.24) were all positively associated with BI-SEDAD (all p < .001). Discussion

This study extends existing research by examining whether sex moderates the associations between illness representations of AD and BI-SEDAD among Chinese older adults. This approach is theoretically and empirically warranted, given well-documented sex differences in AD prevalence, health beliefs, emotional responses to illness, and help-seeking behaviors, as well as gendered social roles. The results indicated that consequences, timeline, identity, coherence, concern, and emotional representations were positively, and treatment control was negatively, associated with BI-SEDAD among Chinese older adults. Sex significantly moderated the associations between several illness representations of AD (i.e., consequences, treatment control, illness identity, illness coherence, and illness concern) and BI-SEDAD.

Several illness representations emerged as significant predictors of BI-SEDAD. Older adults who perceived AD as having severe consequences were more likely to report BI-SEDAD, whereas those who underestimated its seriousness or viewed memory decline as a normal part of aging were less proactive. This pattern is consistent with previous evidence that symptom normalization functions as a key barrier to timely help-seeking [41]. When cognitive decline is framed as “normal aging” rather than a pathological condition, the perceived need for medical evaluation is substantially reduced [42]. Perceiving AD as severely disruptive to daily functioning and family life appears to heighten individuals’ perceived threat, thereby increasing motivation to seek early detection [8]. Similarly, individuals who believed the disease progresses slowly tended to adopt a passive “wait-and-see” approach, reducing BI-SEDAD. This temporal framing may reduce perceived urgency, as individuals assume there is sufficient time before professional assessment becomes necessary [43]. Such delay is particularly problematic in AD, where early intervention is crucial for slowing symptom progression and optimizing care planning.

Illness identity, illness coherence, and illness concern also emerged as influential factors in shaping BI-SEDAD. When older adults attributed symptoms like memory decline to AD, they were more likely to seek professional assessment [7]. In contrast, interpreting such symptoms as benign age-related changes delayed action [42]. This tendency may reflect a psychological coping strategy in which individuals downplay symptoms to avoid confronting the emotional distress associated with a potential diagnosis. Similar patterns have been documented in research on illness denial and avoidance, where minimizing symptom significance serves to protect emotional well-being at the cost of delayed care [10, 11]. A coherent understanding of AD, including its symptoms, progression, and management, was positively associated with BI-SEDAD. This finding aligns with evidence that health literacy facilitates timely healthcare utilization by enabling individuals to recognize risk, interpret symptoms accurately, and navigate healthcare systems more effectively [44]. Together, illness identity and coherence reflect the role of cognitive clarity in transforming vague concerns into concrete intentions to seek medical evaluation. Illness concern, in turn, represents the emotional salience of AD; moderate levels of concern appear to motivate early detection, reinforcing emotional engagement in health decision-making [45].

Interestingly, treatment control was negatively associated with BI-SEDAD. Older adults who expressed greater belief in the effectiveness of treatment were less inclined to pursue early detection, perhaps because confidence in treatment created reassurance and diminished urgency [46]. Some may adopt a passive stance, assuming effective care can be sought later. This finding reflects the need to balance public health messages: while effective treatments are important, their benefits depend on timely diagnosis [47]. In contrast, personal control beliefs were not significantly related to BI-SEDAD. This null finding may reflect the widespread perception of AD as a progressive and largely uncontrollable condition at the individual level. When older adults believe that personal actions have limited influence over disease onset or progression, personal control beliefs may be insufficient to motivate early detection [48].

These findings align closely with the Common-Sense Model of Illness [14]. In the present study, cognitive appraisals shaped how older adults interpreted the seriousness, meaning, and urgency of AD, while emotional responses influenced motivational readiness to act. When AD was perceived as severe, identifiable, and well understood, and when concern was sufficiently salient, older adults were more likely to report BI-SEDAD. Conversely, benign interpretations, temporal distancing, or excessive reassurance regarding treatment reduced perceived threat and delayed action. These results underscore the importance of interventions that simultaneously target cognitive understanding and emotional engagement. Public education strategies that clarify the progressive and serious nature of AD, improve symptom recognition, and address maladaptive beliefs about aging and treatment timing may be particularly effective in strengthening BI-SEDAD [49]. By aligning illness representations with clinical realities, such interventions can promote earlier recognition of risk and more timely help-seeking among older adults.

Sex differences provided additional insights into illness representations and BI-SEDAD. The moderating role of sex was evident in several dimensions, suggesting that males and females may translate illness beliefs into help-seeking intentions through partially different pathways. For perceived consequences, the association with BI-SEDAD was stronger among males. One plausible explanation is that perceived severity may be a more action-triggering cue for males, i.e., when AD is appraised as highly consequential, it may more directly activate problem-focused coping rather than delay [50]. Males who perceived greater seriousness and risks of AD reported stronger BI-SEDAD, which is consistent with the evidence that males respond more strongly to perceived disease severity when deciding whether to seek medical evaluation [51]. A similar sex-differentiated pattern was found for treatment control. Among males, lower perceived treatment control was associated with higher BI-SEDAD, whereas this association was nonsignificant among females. For males, perceiving limited treatment effectiveness may heighten urgency (“if treatment is limited, I should act earlier”), functioning as a compensatory response to reduced perceived controllability [14]. In contrast, females’ BI-SEDAD may be less dependent on treatment-control appraisals and more influenced by broader contextual considerations (e.g., caregiving responsibilities, social support, and emotional readiness), which can dilute the control-intentions pathway [26]. These findings suggest that sex-specific decision-making processes in the context of AD may shape early detection intentions, with males translating perceptions of severity and control more directly into action, whereas females integrating a broader range of emotional and contextual considerations.

The interactions among illness identity, coherence, concern, and sex reveal distinct patterns in health-promoting behaviors. Among males, stronger illness identity was significantly related to higher BI-SEDAD, suggesting that males who interpret symptoms as signs of serious illness are more likely to seek professional help, possibly reflecting sex-related differences in health threat appraisal documented in prior AD and aging research [52]. For females, the association was weaker but remained significant, indicating recognition of symptom relevance, though with less urgency to act. One possibility is that females may engage in more extended appraisal and discussion within informal networks (e.g., family) before help-seeking [53], whereas males may be more likely to delay until symptoms are clearly interpreted as disease-signaling, at which point action becomes more immediate [54]. Greater illness coherence also predicted higher BI-SEDAD among males, although the effect was weaker for females, suggesting that males may act more readily on a clearer understanding of the disease, perhaps linked to health literacy or cultural emphasis on proactive management [55]. Illness concern followed the same pattern. It is more likely to translate into concrete action rather than remaining at the level of worry in males when they do experience disease-related concern [45]. For females, concern may still motivate help-seeking but may be partially counterbalanced by competing emotional processes (e.g., worry about burdening family), producing a weaker net association [56].

No significant moderating effects of sex were identified for the associations between timeline acute or chronic, personal control, or emotional representations and BI-SEDAD. This indicates that, unlike other illness dimensions, perceptions of disease progression and the ability to exert control do not differ by sex in shaping health-promoting behaviors. The lack of sex differences for timeline acute or chronic and personal control suggests that both males and females recognize AD as a chronic and debilitating condition, and this shared perception encourages similar health-seeking intentions. Such findings may reflect a broad societal understanding of AD as a progressive disorder in which individuals, regardless of sex, are likely to consider early intervention when aware of its long-term consequences [57]. These results highlight the value of incorporating sex-specific considerations into public health efforts. Designing interventions that address distinctive influences, including emotional concern, perceived severity, and treatment expectations, could enhance engagement in early care. Campaigns should further emphasize emotional aspects of illness concern, strengthen knowledge of early intervention, and promote confidence in managing AD among both males and females [58].

In addition to individuals’ cognitive and emotional representations, broader psychosocial factors such as stigma may further shape BI-SEDAD. Social stigma associated with AD can lead to delayed diagnosis and avoidance of care, as individuals may fear being labeled or socially marginalized [59]. Such stigma may be particularly salient in contexts where aging and cognitive decline are viewed negatively, potentially reinforcing symptom normalization and emotional avoidance [60]. Addressing stigma in public health campaigns is crucial to encouraging BI-SEDAD [61]. Emphasizing that early diagnosis can lead to better management and quality of life, while addressing the fear of stigma, can encourage older adults to seek help more proactively [62, 63]. Although stigma was not directly measured in the present study, it may represent an important contextual factor interacting with illness representations and BI-SEDAD. Future research should explicitly incorporate stigma-related beliefs to examine how they interact with illness representations in influencing BI-SEDAD.

Early detection and timely treatment of AD are critical for delaying progression, reducing caregiver burden, and improving patients’ quality of life [64]. Timely diagnosis allows individuals to benefit from interventions, plan for the future, and maintain autonomy [65]. Consistent with international guidelines emphasizing early identification [66], the present findings indicate that BI-SEDAD is strongly shaped by individuals’ cognitive appraisals, emotional responses, and sex-specific decision-making patterns. Misunderstandings about AD or limited emotional salience may reduce recognition of symptoms and weaken motivation to health-seeking [66]. These results suggest that weaker BI-SEDAD reflects not only structural barriers but also modifiable psychological and social factors. Accordingly, psychoeducational interventions that enhance symptom recognition, correct maladaptive beliefs, address emotional concerns, and reduce stigma may be particularly effective in promoting timely diagnosis [67, 68]. Tailoring such interventions to sex-specific needs may further strengthen their impact and support healthier aging.

This study has several limitations. First, participants were not clinically screened for MCI or early AD, and no formal cognitive assessments were conducted. Although all participants were community-dwelling older adults with sufficient functional capacity to independently complete the questionnaire (or with minimal assistance), unrecognized cognitive impairment cannot be fully ruled out. Second, while the BI-SEDAD items were adapted from previous studies and demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in the present sample, this measure has not undergone full psychometric validation in Chinese older adult populations. Therefore, its measurement properties should be interpreted with caution. Third, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and longitudinal or intervention studies are needed to clarify temporal relationships. Fourth, we assessed detection intentions rather than actual behaviors, and it is possible that intentions do not always translate into actions. The mechanisms that bridge the gap between intentions and behaviors should be further explored in future studies. Fifth, participants’ prior health-seeking behaviors or healthcare experience was not measured in the current study, which may influence BI-SEDAD. The role of past healthcare experiences in BI-SEDAD should be further examined in future studies. Finally, the sample was obtained through convenience sampling, which introduces potential sampling bias. Caution is required when generalizing the findings to other regions or international contexts. Future studies should use larger, more diverse, and representative samples to enhance the robustness and generalizability of the findings.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provides evidence linking illness representations of AD to BI-SEDAD among Chinese older adults. Several dimensions, including consequences, timeline, identity, coherence, concern, and emotional representations, were positively associated with BI-SEDAD, while treatment control was negatively associated. Sex moderated several effects, highlighting gender-specific pathways influencing BI-SEDAD. Identifying modifiable cognitive and emotional factors offers directions for intervention. Public health initiatives should emphasize AD’s seriousness and progressive nature, promote coherent knowledge, and address stigma and misperceptions. Tailoring strategies to the needs of males and females may enhance effectiveness and support early detection, reduce societal burden, and promote healthier aging.
