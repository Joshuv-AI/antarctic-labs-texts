Stereotactic Gamma Knife Radiosurgery for Merkel cell carcinoma brain metastases: case report and systematic review of literature
Authors: Octavian Vatavu, Francesco Maria Crisà, Filippo Leocata, Virginia Arienti, Marco Cenzato, Alessandro La Camera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMerkel cell carcinoma (MCC) is a rare and aggressive neuroendocrine skin malignancy with an increasing incidence. Although it frequently presents with loco-regional metastases, brain metastases (BMs) are exceptionally rare, lacking standardized treatment protocols.Case descriptionThis report details the case of a 75-year-old male diagnosed with MCC following surgical excision of an inguinal mass, with subsequent adjuvant radiotherapy. Four months post-surgery, imaging revealed a pancreatic nodule and two cerebral lesions. The patient underwent Gamma Knife® stereotactic radiosurgery (GK-SRS) followed by adjuvant immunotherapy with avelumab. MRI follow-ups showed a significant reduction of the cerebellar lesion and full remission of the frontal lesion, with stable remission noted at one year. The patient remained asymptomatic and continued immunotherapy without neurological deficits.Discussion and conclusionA systematic review conducted in parallel included 17 papers representing 20 patients with MCC BMs. Treatment strategies varied, including surgery, radiotherapy, and SRS, sometimes combined with immunotherapy. Analysis revealed that SRS provided high rates of local control, highlighting its value as a primary treatment option. Surgical interventions were typically reserved for symptomatic cases or diagnostic uncertainty. Cases involving leptomeningeal spread correlated with poor outcomes, particularly when not coupled with immunotherapy. The results of analysis support the efficacy of SRS in managing MCC brain metastases, recommending its use as a first-line option when feasible. Immunotherapy appears to enhance disease control, especially in preventing leptomeningeal complications. These data suggest to incorporate radiosurgery and immunotherapy into treatment paradigms for improving prognosis in MCC patients with brain metastases.Keywords: Merkel cell carcinoma, Brain metastasis, Gamma Knife, Radiosurgery

Introduction

Merkel cell carcinoma (MCC) is a rare, highly aggressive neuroendocrine cutaneous tumor. The incidence is 0.10–1.5 cases per 100 000 people per year with an increase in the last two decades (Becker et al., 2023). Loco-regional metastases at primary diagnosis are shown in roughly one-third of patients. Distant metastases are frequent, but brain metastases (BMs) are extremely rare with no treatment guidelines available to date. In this article, we report the case of a 75-year-old male with two BMs treated with GK-SRS and we conduct a systematic review about treatment in MCC BMs.

Case description

A 75-year-old male with neither significant nor oncologic medical history was diagnosed with an indolent 6,5 cm diameter inguinal mass after an echo-color Doppler US of the inferior limbs. Abdominal ultrasound, lumbar MRI and chest X-ray were negative, total-body PET-CT scan showed left inguinal adenopathy. A needle aspiration of the lesion showed a small cell neuroendocrine tumor and surgical excision surgery was performed with diagnosis of Merkel cell carcinoma. Sentinel lymph node biopsy showed no infiltration, as it was for obturator and inguinal lymph nodes. Adjuvant RT on surgical site was performed. After 4 months from surgery at the follow-up imaging (total body PET-CT and cerebral CT scan), a new pancreatic nodule, a cerebellar lesion and a frontal lobe lesion were shown. After multidisciplinary discussion, GK-SRS was chosen for the treatment of CNS lesions.

Pre-operative contrast-enhanced brain MRI confirmed the presence of a right cerebellar lesion (15 × 11 mm) and a small right posterior frontal lesion (4 mm diameter) with minimal perilesional edema as shown in Fig. 1. GK-SRS was performed in January 2023. A Vantage© stereotactic frame-based, contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MRI was performed. The radiosurgical procedure was planned on Leksell Gamma Plan© and the treatment was delivered through a Leksell Icon© (Elekta AB Stockholm, Sweden) on a target volume of, respectively, 1,12 cm3 and 0,07 cm3 with a marginal dose of 21 Gy at isodose prescription 50 %.

Pre-operative contrast-enhanced brain MRI. First row: Cerebellar lesion; Second row: frontal lesion.

The patient was discharged 5 hours after the treatment in good clinical conditions and with no neurological deficits.

No immediate, post-treatment side effects were registered, as any periprocedural complication or any neurological/clinical sign due to treatment itself. No late radiosurgical-related side effects were registered, as any radiological sign such as edema or radionecrosis.

The patient underwent adjuvant immunotherapy with avelumab and a contrast - enhanced brain MRI after 3 months which showed a significant dimensional reduction of cerebellar lesion and the complete remission of the supratentorial lesion. The adjuvant immunotherapy with avelumab was carried out for a total 24 months with no changes in immunotherapy strategy. The patient was asymptomatic and at the brain MRI after 6 months, even the cerebellar lesion showed complete response to SRS. At 20-months follow-up, MRI imaging showed complete local control as demonstrated in Fig. 2, without new lesions. At 26-months follow-up, three months after the end of immunotherapy, the radiological picture was stable, with complete local control.

20 months follow-up contrast-enhanced brain MRI (comparison with pre-operative, upper images).

His last total-body enhanced CT was performed in November 2024 with evidence of complete oncological remission. The patient is still on immunotherapy and asymptomatic.

Discussion

MCC is a rare cutaneous malignancy with neuroendocrine differentiation named after the resemblance to Merkel cells, mechanoreceptor cells present in the basal layer of the skin, although these cells are probably not the origin of the tumor (Tello et al., 2018). The incidence of MCC has increased over the last two decades, partially due to better diagnostic tools and improvement in cancer registration. In 2009, the incidence in the USA was 0.6 over 100 000/year with a median age at diagnosis of 75–80 years (Becker et al., 2017). The American Joint Committee on cancer (AJCC) reported a median age at diagnosis of 76 years and only 12 % of patient under 60 years of age (Becker et al., 2017). The etiology of MCC is not well understood yet, but three elements are thought to have an impact: Merkel cell Polyomavirus infection, UV exposure and immunosuppression. Most cases of MCC present with a cutaneous lesion especially in areas with sun exposure, but up to 10 % of cases a primary cutaneous lesion is absent. Skin lesions are usually indolent rapidly growing cutaneous or subcutaneous nodules. Biopsy is needed for diagnosis using immunohistochemical staining analysis, usually positive for CK-20 (but also CK18, CK19, CK8) and neuroendocrine elements (such as synaptophysin, chromogranin-A, etc.) and MCPyV T antigens (Tello et al., 2018; Becker et al., 2017; Xia et al., 2020). Although its indolent presentation, MCC is very aggressive. Metastatic disease correlates with 12 % 5-years survival. Xia et al. analyzed the frequency of metastatic MCC on a court of 3449 patients among which 273 had metastases but only 3 BMs were detected (Xia et al., 2020). The presence of a brain metastasis was identified as a predictor of poor prognosis because all patients died within one year since diagnosis. In order to compare different treatment options for BMs from MCC, we performed a systematic review of the literature (according to PRISMA criteria). We sought for English written papers that presented patients with MCC BMs and explicated number and localization of BMs, proposed treatment, survival in Pubmed and Embase databases in the last 25 years, as showed in flow-chart reported in Fig. 3.

Prisma flow-chart.

We selected 17 papers for a total amount of 20 patients with MCC BMs, with a median age of 64 years old, male-to-female ratio of 2,3:1. Nine patients (45 %) had no primitive skin lesion at the diagnosis of BM, but most of them had other lesions such as lymph node invasion or other metastases. Thirteen patients showed lymph node invasion (65 %), out of these only five (25 %) had other organ metastases. The timing of the BM diagnosis was very heterogeneous with a median valued of 15,8 months after MCC diagnosis. Twelve patients (60 %) were symptomatic at the time of BM. Eighteen had a single metastasis (90 %), while the other two patients had two metastases. Most of BMs were supratentorial (seventeen BMs, 77 %), four BMs were infratentorial (18 %) and one was a pituitary metastasis. Ten patients (50 %) died because of a cerebral metastatic progression at the time of publication of the respective papers. We classified the patients in six groups according to the chosen local brain treatment: none (Group 0); surgery alone (Group 1); surgery and SRS (Group 2); surgery and radiotherapy (Group 3); radiotherapy alone (Group 4) and SRS alone (Group 5), as shown in Table 1.

Conclusion

These case reports highlight the importance of radiotherapy in MCC brain metastases treatment, as they all included RT/SRS as treatment option, alone or in combination with surgery. The rationale relies into the well-known high radiosensitivity of MCC In our opinion, SRS should be - when suitable - the first option treatment due to its high rate of local disease control as shown in our study and in literature (Fife et al., 2021; Jacob et al., 2015). Surgery together with adjuvant SRS showed good outcomes, but due to the high risk of leptomeningeal spread, surgery should be performed in selected clinical findings: intracranial hypertension, unknown primary tumor, life-threatening mass effect.). It is interesting to note that in all cases of leptomeningeal spread, the patients were not on prior immunotherapy, thus immunotherapy may play an important role in MCC brain metastases, as already shown in more common tumor histotypes (like melanoma). Also, some papers (Fife et al., 2021) demonstrated a clinical response to avelumab in recurrent brain metastasis. Nevertheless, the papers have some limitations, due to the rarity of MCC and to the consequent low number of cases in a limited period of research. The findings underscore the efficacy of SRS in managing MCC brain metastases. We recommend its use as a first-line option when feasible. Combining SRS with surgery or employing it as a sole modality showed promising survival outcomes. Immunotherapy appears to enhance disease control, especially in preventing leptomeningeal complications. These insights advocate for incorporating radiosurgery and immunotherapy into treatment paradigms to improve prognosis in MCC patients presenting with brain metastases.
