Polio eradication: A perspective from the UK
Authors: Hilary Simons, Dipti Patel
Published: 2024

The eradication of poliomyelitis (polio) is tantalisingly close. However, the world is now far beyond the original target year of 2000, set by the World Health Assembly in May 1988, by which it was hoped global eradication and transmission of all wild-poliovirus serotypes, and thus a world free of polio, would be achieved [1]. Immunisation targets looked achievable, especially considering the ongoing success of the Expanded Programme on Immunisation. With the programme appearing on course to meet a previously set target to provide immunisation against polio for all children in the world by 1990, the mood was confident [1]. However, it is debatable whether the obstacles to the achievement of these targets that eventually presented, during what would become a long road to elimination, could have been predicted. Social, political, and economic factors have since been identified as disruptors to polio eradication initiatives, including vaccine mistrust, religious objection to or influence over vaccination and the challenges of providing vaccination to hard-to-reach populations [2]. Incredibly, attacks on health professionals delivering polio vaccination forced some programmes to be suspended [3]. Despite a remarkable 99 % reduction in cases between 1988 and 2013 and only three countries remaining that had never interrupted Wild Poliomyelitis Virus (WPV) transmission (Afghanistan, Pakistan, and Nigeria) [3], cases continued to occur. In 2013 the World Health Organization (WHO) acknowledged that most were the result of international spread (although the case numbers were at an all-time low) [3,4] and in May 2014, the WHO Director-General declared that the situation met the conditions for a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) and should be reviewed at three monthly intervals to review the status of poliovirus infected countries, to discuss progress to elimination including strategies to prevent international spread [5]. Eight events have been declared a PHEIC since 2007 (H1N1 influenza pandemic 2009–10, Ebola 2014–16 and separately between 2019 and 2020, polio 2014 to present, Zika 2016, COVID-19 2020–23 and Mpox in 2022–23 and again in 2024) [6]; the PHEIC relating to polio, first declared in May 2014, has endured making it the most protracted WHO declared emergency [5].

Great strides in the eradication by vaccination of WPV have been achieved (WPV 2 and 3 were declared eradicated in 1999 and 2019 respectively); in 2024, WPV1 cases are only reported from two countries (Afghanistan and Pakistan) [7]. The Polio Eradication and Endgame Strategic Plan 2019–2023 [8] called for, as a next step towards polio eradication, the phased removal of the Sabin-strain Oral Polio Vaccine (OPV) replacing this with OPV types 1–3 vaccine assuring that one dose of trivalent Inactivated Polio Vaccine (IPV) was also included in national immunisation schedules; it was hoped that this would mitigate the risk of the emergence of vaccine derived polioviruses during the changeover. This ‘global switch’ started in April 2016 [9]. Practically, the use of OPV (types 1 and 3) remains essential during transition plans and in some areas mop up vaccination campaigns using monovalent OPV type 2 (mOPV2) are necessary to fill immunisation gaps [10]. Unfortunately, although OPV is very effective at stimulating gut immunity, the vaccine virus may over time evolve when excreted into the environment and mutate to become Vaccine Derived Poliovirus (VDPV). VDPV can circulate in under vaccinated communities (cVDPV), behaving like WPV and causing paralysis and death; positive samples in the environment and cases of cVDPV types 1 and 2 continue to be reported; these have occurred predominantly, but not exclusively, in lower income countries and/or where vaccination gaps are problematic [11].

The emergence of cVDPVs, which has been attributed to multiple factors including vaccine refusal and the compromise of immunisation programmes e.g. because of the COVID-19 pandemic or conflict (e.g. in the WHO European Region, Ukraine faced significant challenges in the response to an outbreak of cVDPV2 which began in 2021 and was genetically linked to an outbreak in Tajikistan) [12]. These and other factors have added to the significant challenge to efforts towards a polio free world with the international spread of polio viruses remaining a global concern [5,[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]].

Even for high-income nations, this is not the time to be complacent. Such nations have robust sanitation and surveillance systems, and childhood polio vaccination rates are high enough to protect the population. However, less than optimum vaccination coverage rates in some communities persist, leaving some of the population at risk of polio infection [17]. During 2022 in the United States (US), poliovirus type 2 (VDPV2) was found in the stool of an unvaccinated person who presented with acute flaccid weakness. The case had no history of international travel but resided in the US state and county where the polio vaccination coverage rate for infants and children was historically low and which had further declined during the COVID-19 pandemic to around 60 % at August 2022 [18].

In 2022-23 genetically linked environmental cVDPV2 samples were identified in some high-income countries (i.e. Canada, Israel, the United Kingdom [UK], and the US), with evidence of sustained environmental spread [19,20]. The countries had not used OPV type 2 vaccine in their national immunisation schedules for years; making it likely that the origin of the cVDPV found in environmental samples was from a person vaccinated with OPV2 overseas.

Intense, mass booster vaccination campaigns using inactivated poliovirus vaccine (IPV) took place, targeted at young children and under vaccinated communities proximal to the outbreaks. Attempts to increase population coverage remains a challenge; no further cases of poliovirus infection have occurred to date in the outbreak affected areas and surveillance continues [21,22].

Of interest, between April–May 2022, VDPV1 isolates were identified in Germany; these were not genetically linked to any ongoing outbreak and classified as ‘ambiguous’ (aVDPV1) [12].

It should be remembered that at the time of these outbreaks, the world was just beginning to recover from the COVID-19 pandemic, a health emergency that disrupted the delivery of healthcare, including routine immunisation programmes resulting in a down slide of vaccination coverage worldwide which left the unvaccinated and under-vaccinated vulnerable [23].

The global polio situation is complex and dynamic. National immunisation programmes aim to protect the individual, and the population generally; in the UK for polio, this is through provision of a minimum of five doses of a polio (IPV) - containing vaccine at intervals through childhood and early adolescence [24]. Completion of a five-dose schedule, identifying and closing any dose gaps in adulthood, will give long term protection against polio infection. The travel health consultation provides an opportunity to ensure the primary course of polio vaccination is complete. Where an incomplete schedule is identified, catch up doses should be offered observing the usual intervals, together with adjunct advice on personal and water hygiene, particularly if the optimum number of doses to complete the schedule cannot be achieved pre-travel. For those with a full immunisation record and where the last dose in the primary series was received 10 or more years ago, a single reinforcing dose, to optimise personal protection, may be given prior to travel to countries reporting WPV or cVDPV outbreaks [24].

Whilst the polio vaccination schedule is straightforward in the UK, the policy makers responsible for vaccine recommendations for those travelling abroad must consider the global polio emergency and the measures mandated by the WHO Emergency Committee to prevent the international spread of the disease [5]. WHO measures include the requirement for those exiting a country reporting either WPV1 or cVDPV1 to provide evidence of vaccination (on an International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis – ICVP) with polio vaccine given within 12 months of departure from that country [5]. Where such evidence is not available, vaccination with OPV may be enforced at the port of exit; all travellers should be informed of the potential for this challenge (under International Health Regulations) to occur [5]. Therein lies the conundrum; for a mostly well vaccinated population such as that of the UK, additional vaccine prior to departure is not essential and is not usually recommended. In the worse-case scenario, where a challenge for certification cannot be met, the traveller may be vaccinated with OPV at a border exit point. This should not be a problem for most travellers/fully vaccinated persons but there are important exceptions; namely those for whom OPV is contra-indicated (i.e. the immunocompromised or pregnant traveller). For these travellers a dose of IPV before travel is recommended (if not given and recorded to an ICVP in the preceding 12 months), together with proof of that vaccination, preventing any adverse challenge at ports of exit.

This approach in the UK, a country with high vaccine uptake and good surveillance, is reasonable and worked well when cVDPV2 isolates emerged in several London boroughs; although there cVDPV2 was found in sewage samples, no polio cases were identified. This outbreak did, however, identify a need to improve vaccine uptake nationally in the routine childhood programme [25].

However, within the travel medicine community in the UK, confusion has been identified regarding the rationale for UK polio vaccination recommendations for the global traveller [National Travel Network and Centre, Advice Line Data, unpublished results]. For example, some front-line travel health practitioners, working in a busy clinical set up, may not recognize the nuanced policy, namely the population versus individual benefit.

For the travel medicine practitioner, the recommendation is to firstly ensure the traveller is protected by vaccination (i.e. up to date with the UK vaccination schedule) and to fill any vaccination dose gaps and secondly, to ensure those who cannot have OPV (i.e. on exit from a country) are offered IPV which is documented on an ICVP before they travel.

The international spread of polio, and specifically vaccine derived polio viruses originating from OPV in high income settings where OPV is not used in the national schedules, highlights the vulnerability of unvaccinated or under vaccinated communities. Capacity to minimise onward transmission through responsive immunisation campaigns, effective ongoing surveillance, and other public health measures, wherever in the world polioviruses endure or reemerge, remain key to the interruption of polio virus transmission.
