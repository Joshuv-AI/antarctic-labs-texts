Neurofunctional phenotyping to investigate the role of the orexin system at the intersection of opioid use disorder and insomnia: a protocol for a randomised, placebo-controlled clinical trial of lemborexant in patients with insomnia receiving buprenorphine
Authors: Madison M Marcus, Maha Alattar, Shanshan Chen, Roy Sabo, Tiffany Pignatello, Joyce Ruddley, Amber R Green, Kayleigh Swan, Lori Keyser-Marcus, Frederick Gerard Moeller, Caitlin E Martin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInsomnia is a common complaint among patients with opioid use disorder (OUD) maintained on buprenorphine (BUP). However, people with OUD have historically been excluded from insomnia clinical trials, leaving clinicians without evidence-based treatment options for this patient population. Lemborexant, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved dual orexin receptor antagonist for the treatment of insomnia, was recently shown to be safe and tolerable among a sample of patients with insomnia who were maintained on BUP. We hypothesise that pharmacologically antagonising the orexin system with lemborexant may improve insomnia symptoms in individuals with OUD and also enhance BUP treatment benefits by improving performance in neurofunctional domains identified in the National Institute on Drug Abuse Phenotyping Assessment Battery.Methods and analysisParticipants with insomnia and OUD who have been stabilised on BUP for at least 4 weeks will be randomly assigned to receive either lemborexant (n=50) or placebo (n=50) for 8 weeks. Participants will complete assessments at baseline, during the 8-week intervention, postintervention and at a 2-week follow-up. Primary outcomes are insomnia severity and impulsivity. Secondary measures include objective sleep metrics (total sleep time, sleep efficiency, sleep onset latency and wake after sleep onset) and performance in the neurofunctional domains of negative emotionality and metacognition.Ethics and disseminationThe study was approved by the Virginia Commonwealth University Institutional Review Board in April 2025 (protocol number HM20031777). Data collection began in May 2025 and is expected to be completed by May 2029. The trial is conducted under FDA IND no. 154797 (FGM). The dissemination plan for the trial includes presentations at local and national conferences, submission of primary and secondary outcome manuscripts for publication in peer-reviewed journals and circulation of findings to popular media outlets, as available. Results will also be shared with interested participants and clinical collaborators upon completion of the trial.Trial registration numberNCT06981195.

Introduction

Among patients with opioid use disorder (OUD) receiving buprenorphine (BUP, a medication for OUD), approximately 70% reported insomnia symptoms, with patients reporting in qualitative interviews that treating insomnia was a top priority.1 Insomnia may compromise OUD treatment outcomes via multiple underlying mechanisms, such as increased impulsivity, heightened stress, pain, persistent cravings2 3 and interference with learning during behavioural therapies.46 Even though sleep disturbances among people with OUD have historically been attributed to ongoing substance use, recent literature1710 highlights how insomnia symptoms persist throughout all stages of OUD recovery (on or off medications). Altogether, these findings emphasise how insomnia is a promising target for personalised adjunctive interventions that may have the potential to improve OUD treatment outcomes such as retention and reduce substance use recurrence.11

People with OUD have historically been excluded from insomnia clinical trials due to heightened perceived risks of respiratory depression or concerns of medication dependence, leaving clinicians without evidence to devise tailored care plans for patients with OUD and insomnia. Providers across the USA are prescribing benzodiazepines, which carry risks (eg, overdose) without proven benefit for people with OUD and insomnia.8 The few other medications prescribed for insomnia that have been studied in OUD populations, such as trazodone12 and mirtazapine,13 have only been studied in small samples of patients maintained on methadone and have shown conflicting results. Overall, these findings highlight the need to establish evidence-based medicine for patients maintained on BUP.

The orexin (also known as hypocretin) system acts to regulate homeostasis and plays a role in wakefulness, arousal, reward and stress.14 Given this unique constellation of influence, recent literature indicates that pharmacologically antagonising the orexin system among individuals receiving BUP treatment has high potential to both (a) reduce insomnia symptoms and (b) engage multiple neurofunctional targets associated with improvements in OUD treatment outcomes. A randomised controlled trial was conducted using suvorexant, a dual orexin antagonist approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for insomnia, during an inpatient BUP taper for patients with OUD (n=38). Over 8 days, suvorexant participants demonstrated increased total sleep time (TST) and decreased withdrawal symptoms compared with placebo.15 In exploratory analyses, suvorexant was also associated with lower opioid craving scores independent of sleep improvements.15 More recently, a phase 1 trial of lemborexant, another dual orexin antagonist that is FDA-approved for insomnia, indicated that lemborexant was safe, tolerable and demonstrated low abuse potential among a sample of patients with OUD stabilised on BUP recruited from the outpatient setting.16 Further, a study of patients with OUD on BUP found that clinically significant insomnia symptoms clustered with abnormalities in three neurofunctional domains identified by the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) Phenotyping Battery17: reward, negative emotionality and metacognition.18

Accordingly, this protocol outlines a randomised controlled trial for lemborexant in patients with OUD maintained on BUP. We hypothesise that antagonising the orexin system with lemborexant, relative to placebo, will improve insomnia symptoms and enhance performance in the OUD recovery-associated neurocognitive domains of reward, negative emotionality and metacognition. Placebo was chosen as the control group because there are currently no evidence-based pharmacological treatments for insomnia in this patient population. This study contains two sets of objectives, in addition to exploratory objectives.

Primary objectives

Primary objectives are to examine the efficacy of lemborexant to improve self-reported insomnia severity and performance in the neurofunctional domain of reward. Impulsivity is a core construct within the reward domain. We hypothesise that participants taking lemborexant will report greater improvements in insomnia severity and demonstrate greater improvement in impulsivity relative to placebo.

Secondary objectives

Secondary objectives are to examine the effects of lemborexant on insomnia-related objective daily sleep metrics and performance in the neurofunctional domains of negative emotionality and metacognition. We hypothesise that, relative to placebo, participants taking lemborexant will demonstrate greater improvements in insomnia-related objective sleep metrics and greater improvements in measures probing the neurofunctional domains of negative emotionality and metacognition.

Exploratory objectives

Exploratory objectives include (a) exploring efficacy of lemborexant to reduce non-prescribed substance use, opioid craving, opioid withdrawal and improve patient-reported outcomes related to recovery capital and global health, (b) determining lemborexant treatment acceptability and (c) exploring associations between outcome-based subgroups (changes in sleep and indicators of neurofunctional domains) with biopsychosocial variables.

Methods and analysis

Participants

Individuals will be eligible to participate if they (1) are 18 years of age or older, (2) meet current Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) criteria for OUD of at least moderate severity, (3) are receiving outpatient treatment for OUD with sublingual BUP film/tablets (8–24 mg) or with extended-release injectable BUP, (4) have been stabilised on their current BUP dosage for at least 4 weeks without intention for dose change within the next 3 months, (5) have a screening urine toxicology positive for BUP and an appropriate norBUP level as determined by a study clinician, (6) have a screening urine toxicology negative for non-prescribed substance (except cannabinoids) with a negative breath (or oral fluid) alcohol screen, (7) screen positive for chronic insomnia on the Insomnia Symptom Questionnaire (ISQ), (8) have an Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) score at screening and baseline of 13 or higher, (9) have no clinically significant medical or psychiatric disorder or condition that, in the judgement of the investigator, would prevent participation or heighten safety risks, (10) understand the study procedures and provide written informed consent in English and (11) have access to necessary resources for completing virtual surveys and monitoring.

Individuals will be excluded if they (1) have a current diagnosis of a sleep-related breathing disorder, narcolepsy, somnambulism or sleep paralysis, (2) have a positive screen for sleep apnoea by the Sleep Disorders Screening Battery (STOP-BAG≥5) or home sleep apnoea test (HSAT; 3% drop in oxygen saturation>10%, >50% of respiratory events being central if Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) is between 5 and 10, oxygen desaturation<88% for ≥10 min or oxygen desaturation index using 3% drop in oxygen saturation>10), (3) are currently receiving treatment for insomnia (behavioural or pharmacologic), (4) are currently taking a medication to treat a sleep-related condition or unable to discontinue over-the-counter drug or supplement used to treat a sleep-related condition, (5) are currently taking benzodiazepines or other central nervous system-active medications that may increase risk to the participant, per principal investigator (PI) discretion, (6) have a current DSM-5 diagnosis (any severity) of alcohol or drug use disorder with non-prescribed substance use within the last 3 months (nicotine use disorder is not considered exclusionary), (7) use cannabis >3 days/week, (8) have an uncontrolled serious psychiatric disorder that would make study participation unsafe (such as Bipolar I disorder, attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder, schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorders or major depressive disorder with psychotic features or a neurological disorder), (9) have an uncontrolled neurological, cardiovascular or pulmonary medical condition such as seizure disorder, recent myocardial infarction, stroke, hospitalisation for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, (10) have a baseline ECG with clinically significant abnormal conduction or with QTc>450 ms, (11) report significant current suicidal or homicidal ideation (Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale ‘yes’ answers on questions 4 or 5) or a history of suicide attempt within the past 6 months, (12) have any of the following lab abnormalities: alanine aminotransferase (ALT)/aspartate aminotransferase (AST)≥2 times the upper limit of normal, total bilirubin≥2 times the upper limit of normal, creatinine≥1.5 times the upper limit of normal, (13) are pregnant or breastfeeding; females who are having sex that includes penile penetration must be non-pregnant, non-lactating and either be of non-childbearing potential (eg, sterilised via hysterectomy, bilateral tubal ligation, bilateral oophorectomy or at least 1 year postmenopausal) or of childbearing potential and agree to use an acceptable form of contraception (eg, intrauterine device, hormonal implant, hormonal patch/ring/pill, condoms (male or female), etc), (14) currently taking prescription or over-the-counter drugs or dietary supplements known to significantly inhibit or induce CYP3A4, (15) are currently taking lemborexant or any previous medically adverse reaction to lemborexant or other dual orexin receptor antagonists or (16) are currently incarcerated or pending incarceration.

Recruitment and prescreening

Participants will be primarily recruited from an outpatient addiction treatment clinic affiliated with a large academic medical centre in a southern Medicaid-expanded state. A study staff member will approach patients at the outpatient treatment clinic if they qualify for the study based on a brief electronic medical record review (ie, prescribed BUP, not pregnant, not taking medications for insomnia, no diagnosis of sleep apnoea or narcolepsy). Patients agreeing to learn about the study will undergo a brief prescreen with a study staff member and be scheduled for a subsequent eligibility screening visit if they are eligible based on prescreen criteria.

Eligibility screening

Research staff will review the informed consent with each participant. Participants who provide written informed consent will then undergo an eligibility screening visit. Screening will consist of a series of clinical and psychological assessments to ensure that participants satisfy inclusion/exclusion criteria (see table 1). This eligibility screening includes completing an HSAT with at least 3 hours of valid sleep time (for participants screening negative for sleep apnoea on the STOP-BAG). Eligible participants who wish to continue will then undergo a baseline assessment.

Baseline assessment

Baseline measurements will assess sleep, OUD, neurofunctional, sociocultural and clinical measures (see table 1 for further details). Participants will also be provided with a wrist actigraph (ActiGraph, Pensacola, Florida, USA),19 which they will wear for a total of 2 weeks (approximately 14 days) to objectively monitor their sleep and activity rhythms. Participants will simultaneously complete a total of 2 weeks of daily sleep diaries that establish complementary information to the actigraphy. Baseline visit 2 will occur approximately 1 week after baseline visit 1. This visit will allow the research assistant (RA) to verify and download the first week of actigraphy and sleep diary data and have the participant complete select research assessments. Baseline visit 3 will occur approximately 1 week after baseline visit 2 and will allow the RA to verify and download the second week of actigraphy and sleep diary data (for a total of 2 weeks) and complete research assessments, including the ISI. Participants must still have an ISI score≥13 at the baseline assessment to continue in the study.

Randomisation

Following the study clinician’s review of baseline measures, the study biostatistician will perform participant allocation in accordance with a computer-generated randomisation schedule. Randomisation will be blocked (using random block sizes) and will be stratified by two variables known to impact OUD treatment trajectories20 21: sex (male vs female) and urine toxicology (positive vs negative for non-prescribed substances at baseline visits). The study biostatistician will directly communicate with the research pharmacy regarding the participant’s placement in the lemborexant or placebo group. At a randomisation visit, participants will receive the first week’s supply of the study drug with a tutorial on intervention phase study procedures.

Blinding

Due to the objectives of the study, the identity of the test and control treatments will not be known to investigators, research staff or patients. During the study, the blind may be broken only in emergencies when knowledge of the patient’s treatment group is necessary for further medical management. Unblinding will occur by the PI contacting the research pharmacy to break the blind in case of emergency.

Adverse events (AEs) reporting

The occurrence of AEs will be assessed at baseline visits, at each research visit during the treatment phase, at postfollow-up visits and at extended-follow-up visits. Additionally, the day after initiating the study drug, participants will have a safety check-in visit to assess for AEs, medication side effects and vital signs (table 1). AEs will be reviewed regularly by the study PI (who is also a clinician) and study staff will immediately report all serious AEs (SAEs) to the study PI. AEs will be reported to the Institutional Review Board (IRB), NIDA, the Data Safety and Monitoring Board (DSMB) established for the study and/or the FDA at regular intervals. Any AEs identified as SAEs will be reported in an expedited manner, as outlined in the data safety monitoring plan.

Study monitoring

The DSMB will include approximately four individuals who are completely independent of the investigators and who have no financial, scientific or other conflict of interest with the trial. DSMB members are experts in clinical trials, human subjects research, OUD and/or insomnia treatments. Issues discussed at DSMB sessions will include the conduct and progress of the study, patient accrual, compliance with protocol and problems encountered. As patient-specific data and treatment group data may be presented at DSMB meetings, the discussion will be regarded as completely confidential. The DSMB will furnish the PI with a summary of their findings (including any recommendations), and the PI will communicate the recommendations to the NIDA, programme officer, scientific officer and, if necessary, the FDA. DSMB and FDA reports will be submitted to the IRB annually, as required for IRB continuing review approval.

The trial will be discontinued if the PI, medical monitor, DSMB, IRB or FDA recommend that the study be stopped for any reason. Specifically, the study would be discontinued based on a pattern of AEs that are thought to be related to study procedures or study medications.

Additionally, the study will be monitored by the university’s Clinical Research Regulatory Affairs personnel. Monitoring visits are conducted regularly; the first visit is after the first participant is enrolled, and then approximately every 2–3 months. The Clinical Research Regulatory Affairs Programme is a division of the University’s Human Subjects Protection Programme and operates independently from the study investigators.

Intervention assessments

Each week during the 8-week intervention phase, participants will attend two visits: one for research assessments and urine toxicology and another for urine toxicology only. Research assessments will address sleep, OUD, neurofunctional, sociocultural and clinical measures (see table 1 for further details). Additionally, on Day 2 (ie, the day after initiating the study drug), participants will have a safety check-in visit to assess for AEs, medication side effects, next-day somnolence and vital signs. Participants will complete 2 weeks of actigraphy and home sleep diaries during weeks 3–4 and 7–8 of the intervention phase.

Follow-up assessments

At a postintervention visit, participants will complete assessments of treatment acceptability and a semistructured interview assessing patient experiences with the study drug and procedures to inform the design of future trials. Finally, a 2-week extended follow-up visit will include assessment of safety-related clinical and psychological endpoints.

Recruitment feasibility and retention

Based on a preliminary needs assessment conducted at the addiction medicine clinic associated with the academic medical centre,1 combined with health system TriNetX data, we anticipate being able to randomise at least 100 subjects over the course of the project period. We will also use BuildClinical and community-based recruitment strategies to meet our recruitment goals. By randomising 100 participants, we are conservatively accounting for 25% attrition before study completion, which still enables us to achieve appropriate statistical power (see data analysis plan). The study visits will be coordinated within the existing outpatient OUD treatment programme, when possible, reducing participant burden and facilitating retention. Additionally, we offer monetary incentives for completion of study assessments and reimbursement for transportation costs (see supplemental materials), which will also promote retention.

Intervention

Treatment groups

Lemborexant (active experimental group)

Participants will take 10 mg lemborexant (Eisai Pharmaceutical) each night, approximately 5–30 mins before going to bed, during the 8-week intervention phase. The investigational pharmacy will provide the study drug in gelatin-covered capsules, identical in appearance to placebo pills.

Placebo (control group)

Gelatin placebo capsules identical to the capsules that enclose lemborexant will be used for placebo. Dextrose powder will be used as a filling in the capsules. Participants will be instructed to take a placebo each night before going to bed during the 8-week intervention phase.

Dispensing

The investigational pharmacy will purchase, receive and package the study drug and placebo in tamper-proof containers. The study drug will be dispensed to the participant at weekly research visits during the 8-week intervention phase.

Intervention adherence

To encourage and confirm intervention adherence, participants will submit videos of study drug self-administration via a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA)-compliant platform. Weekly lemborexant pill counts will also be conducted during the 8-week intervention phase. Additionally, BUP treatment adherence will be self-reported via timeline follow-back22 and verified during the two-times-weekly urine drug tests.

Concomitant therapy

All concomitant medications are recorded for this trial and updated at weekly visits. All participants will remain on their prescribed BUP regimen. Prohibited concomitant medications are listed in the exclusion criteria. Participants will continue to receive their standard OUD outpatient care, which typically includes access to counselling, care coordination and recovery support.

Intervention discontinuation

A participant may be discontinued from the study treatment at any time if the participant or the investigator feels that it is not in the participant’s best interest to continue. The decision to discontinue study treatment will be informed by regular AE evaluations, as well as an evaluation for next-day somnolence after starting the study medication by the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale and Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale. Participants are free to withdraw from participation at any time, for any reason, specified or unspecified and without prejudice. Participants who discontinue the study treatment should come in for an early discontinuation visit as soon as possible. Participants will also be encouraged to complete the safety measures associated with the extended follow-up visit approximately 2 weeks after the early discontinuation visit. Finally, participants who elect to withdraw from the study will be asked if they wish to provide further data collection from routine medical care.

Primary outcomes

Insomnia severity

Insomnia severity will be assessed with the ISI,23 a 7-item instrument that measures perceived severity of insomnia symptoms, sleep satisfaction, interference with daily functioning and quality of life and distress about current sleep problems. The ISI results in a numerical score ranging from 0 (no clinically significant insomnia) to 28 (severe clinical insomnia). The ISI was chosen as a primary outcome because (a) self-report is one of the recommended methods of assessment for symptoms of insomnia in adults,24 (b) the ISI is a validated instrument in insomnia research,23 (c) the call for the use of patient-reported outcomes to inform individualised addiction treatments25 and (d) ISI score is a numerical outcome variable. ISI scores collected at baseline, weekly during the intervention, postintervention and at extended follow-up will be used for analysis.

Impulsivity

The construct of impulsivity will be probed using a delay discounting task. Delay discounting will be measured with the 5-trial adjusting delay discounting task.26 This is a brief, a 1 min task in which individuals choose between receiving a small monetary reinforcer ($5) now or a larger monetary reinforcer ($10) following some delay. The task is based on the premise that individuals tend to value rewards less as the amount of time they have to wait increases. This task results in a key measure termed the discounting rate (k), which is a measure of how steeply a person discounts delayed rewards. A higher ln(k) value suggests a greater discount of future rewards (ie, a person with a high ln(k) prefers an immediate reward). Calculation of rate, using ln(k), was chosen based on a systematic review which found that ln(k) was most commonly found to be associated with substance use disorder treatment outcomes, as opposed to other discounting parameters like k and area under the curve.27 Discounting rate (ln(k)) collected at baseline, weeks 4 and 8 of the intervention and postintervention will be used for analysis.

Secondary outcomes

Objective sleep measures

Objective sleep metrics will complement the primary outcome of self-reported insomnia symptoms. During the baseline visit, participants will be provided with a wrist actigraphy device (ActiGraph)19 to wear for 2 weeks to objectively monitor their sleep and activity rhythms while they simultaneously complete a daily sleep diary.28 Using a sleep diary adapted from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine Consensus Sleep Diary,28 participants will record their bedtime, the time it took to fall asleep, wake-up and out-of-bed times, the number of night-time awakenings and sleep quality. Participants will repeat these procedures at weeks 3–4 and 7–8 of the intervention period. Changes in insomnia-related sleep/wake parameters, such as sleep onset latency (SOL), sleep efficiency (SE), TST, wake after sleep onset (WASO) and the number of nocturnal awakenings, will be calculated from the actigraphy and sleep diaries.

Negative emotionality

Negative emotionality will be assessed with three validated measures: the Distress Tolerance Scale (DTS), Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and Generalised Anxiety Disorder Assessment (GAD-7). The DTS29 is a 15-item self-report measure of emotional distress tolerance. Items are loaded across four factors: tolerance, absorption, appraisal and regulation. Numerical scores are calculated for each of these factors, in addition to a total score. The PHQ-930 is composed of nine items for measuring the severity of depressive symptoms over the last 2 weeks. A total numerical score is calculated. The GAD-731 is a 7-item self-report questionnaire for screening and assessing the severity of generalised anxiety disorder symptoms. A total numerical score is calculated. These measures will be assessed at baseline, during weeks 4 and 8 of the intervention and postintervention.

Metacognition

Metacognition, the awareness and understanding of one’s own thought processes, will be assessed with the Metacognition Questionnaire (MCQ-30).32 The MCQ-30 is a 30-item self-report measure of metacognitive beliefs associated with the five-factor metacognitive model of psychological disorders: cognitive confidence, positive beliefs about worry, cognitive self-consciousness, negative beliefs about the uncontrollability of thoughts and danger and beliefs about the need to control thoughts. A total numerical score and scores for each of the five factors are calculated. Metacognition will be assessed at baseline, during weeks 4 and 8 of the intervention and postintervention.

Data analysis plan

Primary endpoints

Primary endpoints include ISI score and discounting rate (ln(k)). We will assess changes in these endpoints from the baseline during the 8-week intervention phase using a mixed-effects linear regression model (with fixed effects for group, time and their interaction and with a patient-level random effect). Comparisons in ISI and ln(k) change at 4 weeks and 8 weeks between lemborexant and control groups will be multiplicity-adjusted using the Tukey-Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) approach.

A sample of 37 participants in the lemborexant and placebo groups (74 total) will achieve 80% power (with a 2.5% multiplicity-adjusted significance level) to detect a 4.4-unit difference in ISI improvement in the lemborexant group relative to the placebo group between baseline and 8-week follow-up, assuming an ISI SD of 6.0 (based on an observed difference in ISI at 8 weeks in a prior lemborexant clinical trial),33 and will achieve 90% power to detect a 5.0-unit difference. A 4.4-unit difference in ISI improvement in the lemborexant relative to the placebo group will be considered a successful improvement in ISI scores. Our goal of recruiting a total of 100 patients will allow for 25% attrition.

A sample of 37 participants in the lemborexant and placebo groups (74 total) will achieve 80% power (with a 2.5% multiplicity-adjusted significance level) to detect a 1.82-unit difference in ln(k) improvement in the lemborexant group (assuming a 2.679-unit improvement) relative to the placebo group (assuming a 0.855-unit improvement and an ln(k) SD of 2.5)34 and will achieve 90% power to detect a 2.30-unit difference (improvements of 3.154 and 0.855, respectively). A 1.82-unit improvement in ln(k) in the lemborexant group relative to placebo will be considered a successful improvement in discounting rate.

Secondary endpoints

The objective daily sleep metrics of SOL, WASO, SE, TST and the number of awakenings will be extracted and calculated from the actigraphy sensor raw data. These same measures will also be extracted from participant reports of sleep and wake times in the daily sleep diary. For the actigraphy and sleep diary data, we will test temporal changes in these sleep measures, as well as the group differences (lemborexant vs placebo) in these temporal changes using linear mixed-effects models with each objective sleep metric as the outcome variable. With our intended sample size (37 completers per group), we will have 80% power to detect clinically meaningful changes over time (Cohen’s d of at least 0.66) for objective sleep measures consistent with the magnitude of changes detected in the prior suvorexant RCT among participants with OUD and comorbid insomnia.15

Neurofunctional outcomes assessing the domains of negative emotionality (assessed using DTS, PHQ-9 and GAD-7) and metacognition (assessed using the MCQ-30) will be collected at baseline and during weeks 4 and 8 of the intervention and postintervention. We will use linear or generalised linear mixed effects models to examine changes across time. Multiplicity due to testing multiple hypotheses over time within each secondary outcome will also be adjusted using the Tukey-HSD approach. Our intended sample size will have 80% power to detect group neurofunctional differences within ranges previously reported (or similar to unpublished preliminary data) between patients with OUD with and without clinically significant insomnia symptoms.3437

To estimate the association between outcome changes over time and clinical variables of interest (eg, BUP duration and type, non-prescribed substance use), ad hoc hierarchical three-level latent variable models will be used to characterise outcome measures and estimate associations with clinical profiles.

Safety and tolerability endpoints

AE rates will be coded by body system and Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) classification term. AEs will be tabulated by treatment group and will include the number of patients for whom the event occurred, the rate of occurrence and the severity and relationship to the study drug.

Data management and confidentiality

Data will be stored in a secure, password-protected, encrypted REDCap database housed on University servers and will be accessible only by designated research staff and used strictly for research purposes. Study personnel will enter data from source documents corresponding to a participant’s visit into the protocol-specific paper/electronic case report form when the information corresponding to that visit is available. Subjects will not be identified by name in the study database or on any study documents but will be identified by a 7-digit subject ID. The enrolment log, which serves as a key, will be destroyed after all contacts with participants have concluded and data analyses are complete.

Missing data

The proposed mixed-effect models do not exclude participants with missing data but rather incorporate what measurements are available. As a sensitivity analysis, multiple imputation through chained equations38 will be conducted to assess the effect of missing data overall on model results. Estimates and covariances from 50 imputed data sets will be combined using bootstrapping techniques to obtain overall coefficients and SEs to compare groups. Participants who do not adhere to self-administration of lemborexant/placebo and/or BUP may also be excluded from analyses, as determined by the study PI and biostatistician.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics

Study procedures were approved by the Virginia Commonwealth University IRB (protocol number HM20031777). Written informed consent will be obtained from each participant prior to study participation. Any amendments to the protocol and/or informed consent form will be approved by the IRB prior to implementation, and participants will be re-consented if necessary.

Dissemination

Since study outcomes have the potential to be influential in the care of people with OUD, the results of this trial will be widely disseminated across numerous avenues. Scientific, peer-reviewed publications will be written by members of the study team and target outlets with a focus on clinical trials, addiction and sleep medicine to ensure dissemination to all interested groups. Study results will also be presented at national and international conferences related to both addiction and sleep medicine. Results will also be shared with local and regional communities of interest, which commonly include patients and clinical providers. Per our data management sharing plan, at the conclusion of the study, participant data will be de-identified and shared via Open Science Framework.

Patient and public involvement

Research questions and outcome measures were informed by the priorities, experiences and preferences of patient participants from our prior Sleep in Opioid use disorder Recovery and Treatment study. Patient participants were invited to refer peers to the study as a part of a peer-referral recruitment strategy.
