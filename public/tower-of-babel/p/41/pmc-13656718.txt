Clinical biomarkers associated with Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia among dermatology patients receiving systemic immunosuppression: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Wenting Hu, Yangyang Ma, Xingang Wu, Ai-e Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) is a rare but potentially fatal complication among dermatology patients receiving systemic immunosuppression. Data on early clinical biomarkers in this population remain limited.ObjectivesTo identify clinical and laboratory biomarkers associated with PJP in patients with severe dermatologic diseases undergoing systemic immunosuppressive therapy.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of hospitalized dermatology patients receiving systemic immunosuppression. Patients who developed PJP were included at the time of diagnosis, whereas non-PJP patients were required to remain free of PJP during at least 6 months of clinical follow-up after initiation of systemic immunosuppressive therapy. Given the limited number of PJP events, multivariable analysis was restricted to three clinically prioritized variables (initial glucocorticoid dose, LDH, and serum albumin) to avoid model overfitting, and Firth’s penalized likelihood correction was applied to mitigate small-sample bias.ResultsAmong 636 patients with severe dermatologic diseases, 18 developed PJP (2.8%). The median interval from the diagnosis of the primary dermatologic disease to PJP onset was 60 days (IQR 30–110 days). Univariate analysis showed that PJP patients had significantly higher initial glucocorticoid doses, lower lymphocyte counts, lower serum albumin, and higher LDH and CRP levels (all P < 0.05). In exploratory multivariable logistic regression using Firth’s penalized likelihood correction, elevated LDH (adjusted OR 1.006, 95% CI 1.003–1.014; P = 0.006) and decreased serum albumin (adjusted OR 0.68, 95% CI 0.43–0.87; P = 0.030) remained independently associated with PJP. ROC curve analysis showed good discriminatory performance for LDH (AUC 0.938; sensitivity 94.4%) and serum albumin (AUC 0.861; sensitivity 73.7%). The optimal internally derived cut-off values were 302.0 U/L for LDH and 28.5 g/L for serum albumin. These thresholds were derived from the same retrospective cohort and require external validation before clinical implementation.ConclusionsDynamic monitoring of LDH and serum albumin, particularly during the early months after initiation of systemic immunosuppression, may help raise clinical suspicion for PJP. Exceeding these thresholds should prompt closer surveillance and timely diagnostic evaluation, rather than automatically triggering prophylactic treatment. The cut-off values derived from this cohort are hypothesis-generating and require external validation; all findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory.

Introduction

Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) is an opportunistic infection in immunocompromised patients, including those with severe dermatologic diseases receiving systemic immunosuppressive therapy. Although historically most common in people living with HIV, the disease now frequently affects non-HIV immunocompromised patients [1], including those with autoimmune and inflammatory dermatologic conditions who receive systemic immunosuppressive therapy [2].

The rapid expansion of therapeutic options in dermatology—particularly high-dose corticosteroids, conventional immunosuppressants, biologics, and Janus kinase inhibitors—has substantially changed infection-risk profiles in this population [3]. Meanwhile, the wider application of bronchoscopy and molecular diagnostic techniques has improved the detection of PJP in these patients. As a result, PJP has become an increasingly recognized and clinically important complication among patients with severe skin diseases [4, 5].

Despite this growing clinical concern, the clinical warning markers of PJP in dermatology patients remain poorly defined. Determining which patients require closer monitoring and when PJP should be suspected remains a major clinical challenge. Because laboratory abnormalities measured at the time of PJP diagnosis cannot be interpreted as pre-onset risk factors, the present study focused on earclinical indicators associated with PJP presentation rather than definitive predictors of future disease [6]. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a single-center retrospective study to characterize patient demographics, clinical features, laboratory markers, and treatment exposures associated with PJP in hospitalized dermatology patients undergoing systemic immunosuppressive therapy.

Method

Study design and population

This single-center retrospective cohort study included hospitalized patients with severe dermatologic diseases treated at Hangzhou Third People’s Hospital between January 2019 and December 2024. Systemic immunosuppression was defined as treatment with systemic corticosteroids (prednisone ≥ 20 mg/day or equivalent), conventional immunosuppressants, biologic agents, or targeted small-molecule therapies, either alone or in combination. No patient in our cohort received TMP-SMX prophylaxis. Patients in the non-PJP group were defined as individuals receiving systemic immunosuppressive therapy who did not develop PJP during the observation period and had at least 6 months of clinical follow-up after treatment initiation. During the study period, routine PJP prophylaxis was not standard practice in our dermatology department, even among patients receiving high-dose immunosuppressive therapy. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted within the context of a prophylaxis-naïve population.

Diagnosis of PJP

PJP was diagnosed based on PCR detection of Pneumocystis jirovecii in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid or induced sputum, together with compatible clinical symptoms (fever, cough, or dyspnea) and radiologic findings, particularly bilateral ground-glass opacities on chest computed tomography. Real-time PCR was performed using a commercial qPCR assay, and a cycle threshold value of < 38 was considered positive. To reduce misclassification, PCR positivity was interpreted in the context of compatible respiratory symptoms and radiologic abnormalities rather than as an isolated microbiological result.

Data collection

The following data were collected: demographic information; dermatologic diagnosis; details of immunosuppressive treatment; laboratory findings; prophylactic use of trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole; diagnostic information related to PJP; and clinical outcomes. For patients who developed PJP, laboratory parameters were collected at the time of PJP diagnosis or symptom onset. For patients who did not develop PJP, laboratory parameters were collected during follow-up at or beyond 6 months after initiation of systemic immunosuppressive therapy. This difference in sampling time points was recognized as a potential source of bias and was considered when interpreting the results.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using the student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate. Categorical variables were analyzed with the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Because only 18 PJP events occurred, multivariable logistic regression was restricted to a small number of clinically justified variables to reduce model instability. The model was considered exploratory. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Multicollinearity was assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients. ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminatory performance of selected laboratory biomarkers. Cut-off values were derived from the same retrospective cohort and were not externally validated. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0.

Ethical statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hangzhou Third People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2024KA143). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 636 patients with severe dermatologic diseases were included, among whom 18 developed confirmed PJP, yielding an incidence of 2.8%. All PJP diagnoses were based on detection of P. jirovecii DNA in lower respiratory tract specimens together with compatible clinical and radiologic findings.

There were no significant differences in sex distribution between the PJP and non-PJP groups (P = 0.37). There was no statistically significant difference in age between patients with and without PJP (P = 0.42).

The median interval from the diagnosis of the primary dermatologic disease to PJP onset was 60 days (IQR 30–110 days; range 8–600 days). Most PJP cases occurred within the first 2–4 months, suggesting that the early phase of systemic immunosuppressive therapy may represent a clinically important window for heightened surveillance.

Among the 18 PJP cases, pemphigus was the most common underlying disease (8/18, 44.4%), followed by connective tissue diseases (6/18, 33.3%), bullous pemphigoid (1/18, 5.6%), and other conditions (3/18, 16.7%). The proportion of patients with blistering diseases did not differ significantly between the PJP and non-PJP groups (50.0% vs. 54.0%, P = 0.93, χ² = 0.005) (Table 1).

Dermatomyositis patients accounted for a higher proportion of PJP cases (22.2%, 5/18) compared with the non-PJP group (3.0%, 19/618), the difference was statistically significant (P = 0.002). However, only one patient in this group was positive for anti-MDA5 antibody. None of the dermatomyositis patients in the entire cohort received pulse-dose corticosteroids; all steroid exposure was in the form of chronic daily oral doses (prednisone or equivalent). Analysis of individual immunosuppressive agents showed that mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) was used in 33.3% (6/18) of PJP patients vs. 15.2% (94/618) of non-PJP patients (P = 0.042), rituximab in 16.7% (3/18) vs. 6.3% (39/618) (P = 0.089), and Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors in 5.6% (1/18) vs. 2.1% (13/618) (P = 0.361); no significant differences in JAK inhibitor or rituximab use were observed between groups. Given the small number of exposed PJP cases, these drug-specific findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered definitive evidence of agent-specific risk.

Clinical manifestations and laboratory characteristics

Fever was the most common presenting symptom among patients with PJP, occurring in all cases (100%, 18/18). Other frequent respiratory manifestations included cough (50.0%, 9/18) and dyspnea (38.9%, 7/18). Impaired oxygenation was common: 12 of 18 patients (66.7%) had PaO₂ < 80 mmHg, and 1 patient (5.6%) met the criterion for clinically significant hypoxemia with PaO₂ < 60 mmHg. Chest CT typically demonstrated diffuse ground-glass opacities, identified in 55.6% (10/18) of PJP cases. Serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and albumin levels—key markers analyzed in this study—were measured at the time of PJP diagnosis (for PJP patients) or at the corresponding follow-up assessment (generally ≥ 6 months after initiation of immunosuppressive therapy); all PJP patients exhibited elevated serum LDH levels at the time of diagnosis.

All PJP patients received guideline-recommended trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole therapy. None of the patients experienced hypersensitivity reactions during treatment. The cure rate was 17 out of 18 patients (94.4%). The ICU admission rate in the PJP group was 5.6% (1/18), compared with 0.16% (1/618) in the non-PJP group (P = 0.19). Mechanical ventilation was required in 1 PJP patient (5.6%), whereas none of the non-PJP patients required ventilation (P = 0.10). The in-hospital mortality among PJP patients was 5.6% (1/18)(Table 1).

Independent clinical biomarkers associated with PJP

To identify clinical biomarkers independently associated with PJP, we performed multivariable logistic regression using Firth’s penalized likelihood correction as the primary analytical approach (Table 3). Given only 18 PJP events and three candidate predictors (events-per-variable ratio ≈ 6), the model was restricted to clinically prioritized variables, and all findings should be interpreted as exploratory.

After Firth correction, elevated LDH (adjusted OR = 1.006, 95% CI: 1.003–1.014, P = 0.006) and lower serum albumin (adjusted OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.43–0.87, P = 0.030) remained independently associated with PJP. Although a higher initial glucocorticoid dose showed a positive trend toward PJP (adjusted OR = 5.13, 95% CI: 0.32–17.31, P = 0.066), statistical significance was not reached. The attenuated effect estimate compared with standard logistic regression suggests that the original model may have been influenced by sparse-data bias; therefore, Firth’s penalized likelihood correction was adopted as the primary analysis.

ROC analyses demonstrated good discriminatory performance of LDH and serum albumin for differentiating patients with PJP from those without PJP. The optimal internally derived cut-off values were 302.0 U/L for LDH and 28.5 g/L for serum albumin (Fig. 2). Because these thresholds were both derived and tested within the same retrospective cohort, they remain hypothesis-generating and require external validation in independent populations before routine clinical implementation.

Discussion

Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) is an important opportunistic infection in dermatology patients receiving systemic immunosuppressive therapy. In recent years, with the widespread application of novel immunosuppressive regimens such as targeted agents and biologics in dermatology, the PJP risk profile in this population needs to be re-evaluated, and clear evidence regarding clinically useful biomarkers for PJP recognition and risk stratification remains limited. In this study, a retrospective analysis of 636 dermatology patients undergoing systemic immunosuppressive therapy was conducted, with 18 confirmed PJP cases yielding an incidence rate of 2.8%. Elevated LDH emerged as one of the strongest markers associated with PJP in the exploratory multivariable model, consistent with its role as a surrogate marker of alveolar epithelial injury [7–9]. PJP-induced lung injury leads to LDH release into the circulation. Because LDH was measured at the time of diagnosis, it should be interpreted as a biomarker associated with active PJP rather than a pre-exposure risk factor.

Hypoalbuminemia was also independently associated with PJP in the exploratory multivariable analysis. This aligns with findings in other immunocompromised populations where hypoalbuminemia reflects systemic inflammation and impaired immune-nutritional status [10, 11]. Because serum albumin is routinely measured in hospitalized patients, it may serve as a practical biomarker during clinical evaluation.

PJP patients received higher initial glucocorticoid doses, and prednisone exposure ≥ 20 mg/day for ≥ 4 weeks showed a trend toward increased risk. Although this association lost statistical significance after adjustment, likely because of the limited number of PJP events, the observed trend is consistent with previous studies demonstrating dose-dependent PJP risk [12–14]. Accordingly, patients receiving high-dose corticosteroids warrant close clinical monitoring.

Although lymphopenia and elevated CRP were significant in univariate analyses, they did not remain significant in the exploratory multivariable model. This finding may reflect overlap among inflammatory and immune-related markers, together with the limited statistical power resulting from the small number of PJP events. Therefore, the independent contribution of these variables requires further investigation in larger cohorts.

In our cohort, dermatomyositis was overrepresented among PJP cases (5 of 18). Although our small sample size precludes definitive conclusions regarding anti-MDA5 positivity, external evidence indicates that anti-MDA5-positive dermatomyositis is one of the highest-risk subgroups due to profound immune dysregulation [15, 16]. Thus, heightened clinical vigilance may be warranted in this subgroup.

The median interval to PJP onset was 60 days, identifying a critical early “vulnerability window” within the first 2–3 months of therapy [17, 18]. Additionally, more than half of our PJP cases had concomitant pulmonary infections, underscoring the necessity of comprehensive microbiological evaluation in dermatology patients presenting with new-onset respiratory symptoms [19].

In summary, elevated LDH and hypoalbuminemia were independently associated with PJP in dermatology patients receiving systemic immunosuppression. Although both biomarkers demonstrated good discriminative performance, the proposed cut-off values were derived from a single retrospective cohort and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating until externally validated. Dynamic assessment of these readily available laboratory markers may assist clinical evaluation but should complement, rather than replace, comprehensive clinical judgment.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single-center design may introduce inherent selection bias and limit the generalizability of our findings to other geographic regions or diverse clinical settings. Second, although the total cohort size was substantial (n = 636), the absolute number of PJP events remained relatively small (n = 18), which may have constrained the statistical power to identify other potential risk factors such as specific immunosuppressant combinations. Third, due to the retrospective nature of the data, some laboratory parameters, such as CD4+ T-cell counts, were not consistently recorded for all patients, leading to potential information bias. Fourth, laboratory measurements in PJP patients were obtained at the time of diagnosis, whereas measurements in non-PJP patients were obtained during follow-up assessments. These non-equivalent sampling time points may have exaggerated differences between groups and limit interpretation of causality. Finally, because laboratory biomarkers were measured at a single time point, we were unable to determine how changes in LDH and serum albumin over time relate to the development of PJP. Future multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings, confirm the proposed cut-off values, and further clarify their clinical utility.
