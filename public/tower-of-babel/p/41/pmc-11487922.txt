Number of involved nodal stations predicts survival in small cell lung cancer
Authors: Han Zhang, Cong Jiang, Dongliang Bian, Jing Zhang, Yuming Zhu, Jie Dai, Gening Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn small cell lung cancer (SCLC), the pathological N category is identical to it in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and remains unchanged over a decade. Here we verified the discriminability of number of involved nodal stations (nS) in SCLC and compared its efficacy in predicting survival with currently used pathological nodal (pN) staging.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed the patients who received operations and were pathologically diagnosed as SCLC at Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital between 2009 and 2019. X-tile software was adopted to determine optimal cut-off values for nS groups. Kaplan–Meier method and Cox regression analysis were used to compare survival between different groups. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was employed to evaluate the standardized net benefit.ResultsA total of 369 patients were included. The median number of sampled stations was 6 (range 3–11), and the median number of positive stations was 1 (range 0–7). The optimal cutoff for nS groups was: nS0 (no station involved), nS1-2 (one or two stations involved), and nS ≥ 3 (three or more stations involved). Overall survival (OS) and relapse-free survival (RFS) were statistically different among all adjacent categories within the nS classification (p < 0.001, for both OS and RFS between each two subgroups), but survival curves for subgroups in pN overlapped (OS, p = 0.067; RFS, p = 0.068, pN2 vs. pN1). After adjusting for other confounders, nS was a prognostic indicator for OS and RFS. The DCA revealed that nS had improved predictive capability than pN.ConclusionsOur cohort study demonstrated that the nS might serve as a superior indicator to predict survival than pN in SCLC and was worth considering in the future definition of the N category.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-024-03313-1.Keywords: Small cell lung cancer, Pathological N stage, Number of involved nodal stations, TNM staging system, Prognostic factor

Background

Lung cancer continues to be the leading cause of morbidity and mortality among all cancers [1]. Especially, small cell lung cancer (SCLC) which accounts for approximately 15% of lung cancers is associated with the poorest prognosis [2, 3]. SCLC is characterized by rapid tumor growth and early dissemination to regional lymph nodes and distant sites. Although chemotherapy and radiotherapy are recommended for SCLC, the role of surgery cannot be omitted [4, 5].

Accurate staging could guide the treatment schemes and predict survival. The staging project for SCLC has transitioned from the Veterans Administration Lung Study Group (VALG) staging system, initially proposed in the 1950s [6, 7], to the tumor, node, and metastasis (TNM) staging system, which was adopted in 2009 [8, 9]. However, since the release of seventh edition of the TNM staging system in 2009, the N parameter has not been changed in the last decade. The latest N parameter recommended by the eighth edition of the TNM staging system (pN) [10] is comprised of N0, N1, N2, and N3 to represent the anatomic range of involved lymph node (LN) stations and is identical in SCLC [11] and NSCLC [12]. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have revealed the heterogeneities in N1 and N2 diseases [13, 14]. Accordingly, several new parameters were put forward to regroup N1 or N2 stages in NSCLC, such as the number of positive LNs (nN) [15–17], the ratio for the number of positive to sampled LNs (LNR) [18–21], single or multiple-station metastasis [22–24], presence of skip metastasis [12, 25], and their combination with pN [26, 27]. Recently, Citak et al. [28] and Xu et al. [29] reported the predictive value of the number of involved nodal stations (nS) in NSCLC, and they concluded that nS was a superior prognostic factor to pN classification.

Moreover, the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) has proposed a new N parameter by a combination of the anatomical location of positive LNs, number of involved stations (single or multiple stations), and presence of skip metastasis: N1a (single N1), N1b (multiple N1), N2a1 (single N2 without N1), N2a2 (single N2 with N1) and N2b (multiple N2) [12]. Subsequent analysis indicated overlap among certain prognostic subgroups, with N2a1 tending to exhibit a more favorable prognosis than N1b. Hence, the N descriptor recommended by IASLC has yet to be adopted, pending further validation [12, 25].

However, limited research has been conducted to investigate the regrouping strategy of pathological N stage in SCLC, probably owing to the scarcity of operated patients. In fact, staging strategies for metastatic lymph nodes employed in SCLC were initially developed for NSCLC and subsequently validated for SCLC by IASLC [9, 11]. Nonetheless, based on the analysis of the IASLC, it appears that pN2 is not clearly distinguished from pN1 in SCLC. Thus, a pathological N category tailored for SCLC is warranted.

The aim of this study was to validate the efficacy of nS in predicting survival in SCLC and compare its discriminability with pN in eighth edition of TNM staging system.

Methods

Study design and data source

From January 2009 to July 2019, 566 patients underwent surgeries and pathologically confirmed as SCLC at Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital. Surgeries intended for curative resection with complete mediastinal LNs dissection or systematic mediastinal LNs sampling were performed. Patients with pathological N1 and N2 diseases were included in this study. Exclusion criteria were as follows: ⑴ Patients with distant metastasis; ⑵ Patients with R1 or R2 resection; ⑶ Patients with other malignant tumor history; ⑷ Patients who died within 1 month after operation; ⑸ Lost contact after being discharged from hospital; ⑹ Preoperative adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy. In addition, patients who had fewer than six lymph nodes or three mediastinal stations resected were also excluded according to TNM guidelines [30, 31].

Postoperative chemotherapy with a platinum-based regimen was routinely administered except for patients unendurable or refused. Postoperative radiotherapy was administered at the discretion of the radiation oncologists and the suggestion of the referring surgeons. Follow-up was executed by our staff regularly since the discharge from hospital. The last follow-up date was June 2023.

The data of SCLC patients in The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database between 1998 and 2015 was also used to evaluate the discriminability among different groups in pN.

Technical definitions

Overall survival (OS) is defined as the time between date of surgery and the date of death [19]. Patients alive at last contact or lose connection were censored at the last contact date. Relapse-free survival (RFS) is defined as the time between date of surgery and the date of cancer recurrence or last follow-up, whichever occurs first [25]. The anatomical classification of LN stations is defined according to international LN map [32]. The LN station which has at least one lymph node pathologically confirmed with invasion of cancer cells is defined as a positive nodal station. The nS means the number of positive nodal stations without considering its anatomic location.

Statistical analysis

OS and RFS are the primary outcome events in this study. Meanwhile, factors such as nS and pN, which may influence the outcomes, function as the independent variables in our analysis. X-tile software was used to determine the optimal cut-off values for nS groups by an internal cross-validation method based on the maximum χ2 value of log-rank test (Version 3.6.1, Yale University). Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were employed to determine factors significantly associated with OS and RFS. Variables identified through univariate analysis as statistically significant (p < 0.05) or previously reported to be associated with survival outcomes were considered for inclusion in the subsequent multivariate analysis (sex, age, smoking, tumor purity, pathological T stage and nS group). Survival curves were plotted by the Kaplan-Meier method and the survival differences among subgroups were evaluated by log-rank test. Analyses above were performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences software (SPSS, version 23, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY).

Decision curve analysis (DCA) was applied to calculate net benefit of predicting survival based on either the nS or pN model. A model with larger area under curve (AUC) is considered to be more clinically useful in the context of this method [33–35]. The decision curve analysis was performed using R software (version 4.3.0, https://www.r-project.org/; package: ggDCA), and the RStudio code used for the analysis is provided in the supplementary file.

Categorical variables were shown as frequencies and percentages, with χ2 tests being used for distribution analyses. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with ranges. All statistical analyses were two-sided tested and a probability value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 369 patients were included (Fig. 1), with a median follow-up period of 40 months. The mean age was 61.8 ± 8.6 years, with males making up 88.1%. Video-assisted thoracoscope surgery (VATS) was performed in 208 patients (56.4%), of whom 83 (39.9%) received uniportal approach (Table 1). The median number of sampled lymph node stations was 6 (range 3–11), and the median number of involved lymph node stations was 1 (range 0–7).

According to the eighth edition of TNM staging system, 178 patients were classified into N0 stage (48.2%), and 55 patients in N1 stage (14.9%), 136 patients in N2 stage (36.9%). As for the IASLC-proposed N parameter, 178 patients were diagnosed as N0 stage (48.2%), 39 patients as N1a stage (10.6%), 16 patients as N1b stage (4.3%), 26 patients as N2a1 stage (7.0%), 43 patients as N2a2 stage (11.7%), and 67 patients as N2b stage (18.2%).

Cutoff determination and prognostic analysis for nS classification

To reveal the prognosis value of nS, the survival curves were plotted, which showed a stepwise deterioration as nS increased (Fig. S1, p < 0.001 for OS & RFS). However, some patients with adjacent nS values shared similar survival, suggesting the necessity to define groups. According to the optimal cutoff values (with greatest χ2 log-rank value of 75.1) determined by X-tile software, patients were classified into three groups as nS0 (no station involved), nS1-2 (one or two stations involved), and nS ≥ 3 (three or more stations involved) (Fig. S2).

Furthermore, the discriminatory ability of nS groups in predicting OS and RFS was verified using Kaplan–Meier method. The 5-year OS rates were 60.2%, 41.7% and 12.1% for nS0, nS 1–2 and nS ≥ 3 categories, respectively. The 5-year RFS rates were 55.3%, 27.5% and 9% for nS0, nS1-2 and nS ≥ 3 categories, respectively. The results showed that the survival in neighboring nS groups could be well separated for both OS (Fig. 2A, nS1-2 vs. nS0, nS ≥ 3 vs. nS1-2, p < 0.001) and RFS (Fig. 2B, nS1-2 vs. nS0, nS ≥ 3 vs. nS1-2, p < 0.001).

In multivariate COX proportional hazards model (Table 2), nS classification was a prognostic factor for both OS and RFS when sex, age, smoking, tumor purity and pathologic T stage were adjusted.

The prognostic significance of nS across different pathological T stages was also validated. The OS and RFS curves stratified by nS classification within each pathological T stage were presented in Fig. S3. While statistical significance in survival differences between adjacent nS categories was not consistently observed, there was a notable trend of decreasing OS and RFS as the nS classification progressed from the nS0 to the nS ≥ 3 subgroups.

Prognostic value of pN and IASLC proposed N parameter

To evaluate the prognostic value of pN in SCLC, we performed Kaplan-Meier curve plot and log-rank test for OS and RFS grouped by pN classification (Fig. 3A-B). The results showed that although N0 stage was separated from other stages (N1 vs. N0, N2 vs. N0, p < 0.001, for both OS & RFS), the survival curve of the N2 stage overlapped with that of the N1 stage (OS, p = 0.067; RFS, p = 0.068). The poor discriminatory power of pN in distinguishing N2 from N1 stage in our cohort was consistent with it in SEER database (Fig. 3C), in which the survival curves overlapped (p = 0.413 for OS).

We also analyzed survival predicted by IASLC-proposed N parameter. The 5-year OS rates were 55.2%, 12.5%, 36.1%, 34.9% and 16.6% for N1a, N1b, N2a1, N2a2, and N2b, respectively (Fig. S4A). The corresponding 5-year RFS rates were 30.4%, 18.8%, 10.1%, 27,6% and 13.3%, for each stage (Fig. S4B). The multivariate Cox analysis revealed that OS for N2a2 was not separated from N1a1, and N1b had a survival similar to N2b. Survival curves for N2a1 vs. N1a, and N2b vs. N2a1/N2a2 tended to be separated, though not statistically significant (Fig. S4A). As for RFS, only N1b and N2b were statistically distinguished from N1a (Fig. S4B).

Cross-validation of the prognostic significance

In Fig. 4, the survival curves for nS classification in pN1 and pN2 patients were presented. Notably, among pN1 patients stratified into nS1-2 and nS ≥ 3 subgroups, prognosis differences were statistically significant for both OS (Fig. 4A, p = 0.010) and RFS (Fig. 4B, p = 0.042) between nS categories. Additionally, within the cohort of pN2 patients, when segregated into nS1-2 and nS ≥ 3 subgroups, statistically significant differences in prognosis were also observed between the nS categories regarding to OS (Fig. 4C, p < 0.001) and RFS (Fig. 4D, p < 0.001). These results underscore the clinical importance of the nS classification in distinguishing prognosis among patients within pN1 or pN2 stage.

In addition, the DCA was conducted to assess the improvement of nS classification in predicting prognosis (Fig. 5E-F). The DCA showed that the nS classification provided a greater standardized net benefit (evidenced by larger area under curve) for survival prediction compared to the pN classification regarding both OS and RFS. This finding suggests that the nS classification may offer enhanced predictive ability for survival outcomes.

Discussion

Nodal status is a key determinant of prognosis and is of particular concern in treatment decisions making. Our study revealed the close relationship between number of involved lymph node stations (nS) and survival in SCLC, which tended to be a better indicator than anatomical location-based pN in eighth edition of TNM staging system [11] and the IASLC proposed N descriptor [12].

Before our study, the heterogeneity of pN1 and pN2 stages has been widely debated [36–38]. In SCLC, The IASLC had launched two studies to evaluate the efficacy of pN in predicting survival. The first was carried out in 2009, which contained 349 surgical cases between 1990 and 2000. It revealed that survival curves were separated from each other, though pN0 overlapped with pN1 in the tail [9]. Differently, the second study in 2015 containing 582 surgical patients in 1999–2009 showed a significant difference between pN0 and pN1 but less difference between pN1 and pN2 [11]. Since the data from the latter study had no overlaps with the former one and was a decade lagging behand, the surgical, pathological and therapeutic improvements may account for the discordance. In our study, we retrospectively analyzed patients who were operated and pathologically diagnosed as SCLC at our hospital between 2009 and 2019, which was very updated in terms of time. Our results showed remarkable difference between pN0 and pN1 groups but no statistical difference between pN1 and pN2 groups with regard to OS and RFS, consistent with findings from the SEER database and the IASLC study in 2015 [11].

Furthermore, the analysis of the IASLC proposed N parameter (N1a, N1b, N2a1, N2a2 and N2b) in our SCLC cohort revealed that some groups tended to be separated but not statistically significant, and N1b had a similar survival to N2b. Our results differ from reports in NSCLC [12, 22, 25, 29] probably because of the difference in pathological subtypes and our relatively small sample capacity. Besides, other studies have recommended the combination of pN with the number of involved lymph node stations [25, 39] or induction of lymph node ration (LNR) [27, 40], which were worth considering, but should be further validated.

The prognostic value of nS has been validated in other cancers, including esophagus cancer [41], gastric cancer [42] and NSCLC [24, 39]. In NSCLC, Citak et al. [28] subdivided nS into four groups as nS0, nS1, nS2-3, and nS ≥ 4, while Xu et al. [29] subdivided nS into four groups as nS0, nS1, nS2, and nS ≥ 3. Both their classifications could predict survival better than location-based pN. Because Xu et al. employed a larger population, their cutoff differed slightly from it defined by Citak et al. Our analysis in SCLC patients subdivided nS into three groups (nS0, nS1-2, and nS ≥ 3), further supporting the notion that nS had improved discriminatory capability compared to pN. The discrepancies in the nS cutoff between our study and NSCLC might derive from the differences in pathological type and sample size. Moreover, our exploratory analysis indicated that the inferior discriminability of pN in predicting survival is probably because a proportion of patients who had survival similar to pN1 group were inappropriately classified into pN2 group in the pN classification. The phenomenon that the number of involved lymph node stations had preferable predicting value in SCLC might be owing to its intrinsic character. Because the SCLC is a histological subtype characterized by rapid local and distal metastasis, the location and the distance of involved lymph nodes in SCLC may not be as important as those in NSCLC. Instead, the number of involved lymph node stations which represent the invasiveness and amount of tumor cells to disseminate distally (tumor burden) may overwhelm other factors.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the prognostic value of nS in SCLC and might pave the way for the modification of N parameter in SCLC and guide the postoperative therapeutic schemes. Nevertheless, some limitations exist in our study. First, it is a retrospective study with patients from a single medical center. Thus, inevitable selection bias, missing data and other unmeasured confounders would lead to inaccuracy of the results. Second, the sample capacity is relatively small and the distribution of patients among different stages is uneven. This will result in bias in this study. Third, the count of sampled LN stations is not enough. Although systematic hilar and mediastinal lymphadenectomy was performed, the inadequate retrieval of peripheral LNs (stations 12 to 14) was unavoidable [43]. Besides, postoperative adjuvant therapy was conducted based on clinical guidelines and surgeon’s decisions, which would affect survival. In the future, more sophisticatedly designed, large-scale, prospective studies are acclaimed to verify the reliability of this N parameter.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated the prognostic significance of nS in SCLC. The nS might be a superior indicator to predict survival than currently used pN in 8th edition of TNM staging system in SCLC and was worth considering in the revision of N category in the future.
