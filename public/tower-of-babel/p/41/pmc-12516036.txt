Cone beam computed tomography navigation reduces impact of poor prognostic factors in osteoid osteoma radiofrequency ablation
Authors: Guy Ben Arie, Assaf Albagli, Amit Benady, Yair Gortzak, Ortal Segal, Ben Efrima
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoid osteoma often requires surgical intervention following conservative treatment failure. While traditional radiofrequency ablation (RFA) is effective, poor prognostic factors such as tumor size, anatomical location, patient age, sex, and previous interventions may negatively influence outcomes. Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT)-navigated RFA could mitigate these negative predictors by enhancing procedural accuracy; however, its specific impact on these factors remains under-investigated.MethodsThis retrospective study included 127 patients (median age 20, IQR: 16–25) undergoing CBCT-navigated RFA for osteoid osteomas from 2015 to 2021. Outcomes assessed were treatment failure, recurrence rates, pain relief (Visual Analog Scale [VAS]), functional improvement (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group [ECOG] status), and postoperative complications classified by the Society of Interventional Radiology (SIR) criteria. Multivariate logistic regression and statistical analyses were conducted to evaluate the influence of previously identified poor prognostic factors on outcomes.ResultsSignificant long-term pain relief occurred in 97 % of patients, with median VAS scores improving from 9 to 0 postoperatively (p < 0.01). Median ECOG scores improved significantly from 1 to 0 (p < 0.01). Overall treatment failure was observed in 11(8.7 %) patients, including symptomatic recurrence (4, 3.1 %), minor peripheral nerve complaints (3, 2.36 %), limited range of motion (3, 2.36 %), and Metatarsal Fracture (1, 0.8 %). Adult age was the only factor significantly associated with treatment failure (OR = 1.117, p = 0.036), whereas tumor size, anatomical location, biological sex, and prior interventions showed no significant associations.ConclusionsCBCT-navigated RFA effectively reduces the negative impact of traditionally poor prognostic factors, achieving high success rates with minimal complications. While older age remains a modest predictor of poorer outcomes, the absence of significant associations with other previously identified risk factors underscores the enhanced precision and clinical value of CBCT navigation.

Introduction

Osteoid osteoma (OO) lesions frequently necessitate intervention after the failure of conservative treatments, as spontaneous resolution occurs in only approximately 50 % of cases.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Historically, open surgical excision has been the primary treatment method, although this approach carries significant disadvantages, including extensive bone resection, prolonged recovery periods, and increased morbidity.6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative that achieves comparable success rates while significantly reducing hospitalization duration, accelerating patient recovery, and minimizing morbidity.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16

RFA is performed percutaneously and relies on intraoperative imaging for precise electrode placement. Effective ablation requires accurate targeting of the lesion's nidus while protecting the surrounding healthy tissues. Factors influencing the efficacy of RFA, particularly in complex cases, include electrode size, ablation temperature, necessity for repeat procedures, and the use of high-resolution CT imaging.17

Several studies have extensively evaluated predictors of OO recurrence following RFA. Tung et al. 8 identified larger tumor size as a significant risk factor for recurrence. Baal et al.14 reported increased recurrence risk independently associated with younger patient age and female sex. Rimondi et al. 9‚17 also found higher recurrence rates in patients who previously underwent interventions. Somma et al.18 and Esteban et al. 19 highlighted that lesions in atypical anatomical sites, particularly the spine, correlate with greater recurrence risk. Typically, recurrences are observed within two years following RFA treatment.6, 8, 10

The advent of intraoperative Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) and intraoperative navigation has improved the precision of RFA procedures for OO. These advanced techniques allow for a more accurate implementation of preoperative planning by enhancing electrode placement accuracy, minimizing damage to adjacent healthy tissue, and reducing patient radiation exposure.18, 19, 20, 21, 22 Despite extensive research identifying tumor size, lesion location, patient age, and history of previous interventions as negative predictors in standard RFA, large-scale studies evaluating the impact of these factors, specifically within CBCT-navigated RFA, are lacking.

We hypothesized that CBCT-navigated RFA could mitigate the negative effects associated with these predictive factors. This study aimed to assess the influence of tumor size and anatomical location, patient sex and age, and previous surgical interventions on patient-reported outcomes (PROMs), complication rates, and recurrence rates in patients undergoing CBCT-navigated RFA.

Materials & methods

Patient selection

In this retrospective, single-institution study we analyzed data from 127 patients who presented with symptomatic osteoid osteoma unresponsive to nonoperative treatment. All patients underwent Cone-Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT)-navigated Radiofrequency Ablation (RFA) using the O-arm® scanner (Medtronic Sofamor Danek, Memphis, TN, USA) between 2015 and 2021. Each case was evaluated preoperatively by a multidisciplinary cancer board comprising radiologists, pathologists, oncologists, and orthopedic surgeons. Interventions were consistently performed by a highly experienced orthopedic surgeon with specialized expertise in orthopedic oncology. Institutional review board approval was obtained for this study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were primary osteoid osteoma treated with CBCT-navigated RFA or revision cases managed by other surgical methods. Exclusion criteria were follow-up periods of less than two years post-intervention.

Evaluation and 3D planning

Preoperative imaging, including CT or MRI, was used to assess tumor anatomical location, size, and proximity to vital structures. Preoperative pain was assessed using the VAS, and functional status was evaluated through physical exam in the outpatient clinic and classified according to the ECOG performance status. Preoperative plan required tumor analysis which was performed using CT and was stratified via a digital 3D model generated in Mimics® software (by Materialise, Leuven, Belgium). Thus the surgeon was able to defining the exact trajectory, depth, and position or the surgical instruments preoperatively. Anatomical locations were divided into five categories: proximal lower limb, distal lower limb, pelvis, upper limbs, and spine. Age groups were defined as children (<18 years) and adults (≥18 years). Biological sex was recorded as male or female, and prior surgical treatments. The categorized data were subsequently reviewed by the senior authers (Author 1 and Author 6). Recurrence was defined as the return of localized pain more than two weeks after RFA treatment, confirmed through imaging. Postoperative Complications were divided into Minor and Major Complications using the Society of Interventional Radiology (SIR) Adverse Event Classification System.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R-Studio® software (Posit PBC, Boston, MA, USA). Data normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Given the non-normal distribution of continuous variables, pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied for comparisons involving more than two groups. For categorical data, the chi-squared test was employed, except when expected frequencies were below 10, in which case Fisher's Exact test was used. Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Treatment failure (including recurrence) and postoperative assessment scores, were compared between groups identified as independent negative predictors factors and analyzed further through multivariate logistic regression.

Intra-operative 3D imaging and navigation

All procedures were performed in the operating room. All ablations were performed under general anesthesia on a fluoroscopy-capable operating table by the senior author (Author 1), a fellowship-trained orthopaedic oncologist. The optical tracking instruments were properly placed and secured. An initial scan was performed using a cone-beam navigation system (o-arm® scanner Medtronic Sofamor, Danek Memphis, TN, USA). A 3D image was then generated using the navigation system (StealthStation ®, Medtronic Sofamor, Danek Memphis, TN, USA). After capturing the initial image, the relevant instruments were calibrated in the system (Suretrack ®, Medtronic Sofamor, Danek Memphis, TN, USA). Once it was confirmed that the generated, calibration, and preoperative images were consistent, the ablation protocol was implemented.

Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) protocol and additional preventive measures

RFA is a thermoablative procedure that causes coagulative necrosis inside the tumor. A 15-cm RF probe needle (Covidien Ltd. USA, cool tip 15-cm long) was inserted into the center of the tumor to generate heat by conducting alternate currents throughout the needle within a range of 350- 500KHZ. This increased the intra-lesion temperature to over 900°0.17 The ablation phase was a total of 16-min. The most common complication is damage to the surrounding structures, mainly the nerves and skin. To minimize damage, a thorough preoperative plan was designed to map the tumor and surrounding structures to calculate the trajectory of the probe. In cases where nerves may have been damaged, neuromonitoring was used to detect signal changes during ablation. In procedures adjacent to the skin, we provided a continuous infusion of normal saline to the subcutaneous layer causing an effective hydro-dissection to protect the skin from thermal damage.

Results

A total of 127 patients were included, comprising 82 adults (64.6 %) aged 18 years or older and 45 children (35.4 %) under 18. There were 88 males (69.3 %) and 39 females (30.7 %). Tumor anatomical distribution included proximal lower limb (n = 57, 44.8 %), distal lower limb (n = 42, 33 %), pelvis (n = 11, 8.7 %), upper limb (n = 13, 10.2 %), and spine (n = 4, 3.1 %). Median tumor size was 0.5 cm (IQR: 0.4–0.7). Median follow up time was 57 (IQR 45–74).

The median preoperative ECOG score improved significantly from 1 (IQR: 1–1) to 0 (IQR: 0–1) postoperatively (p < 0.01), with functional status improvement observed in 89 % of patients. Median preoperative VAS scores significantly decreased from 9 (IQR: 8–10) preoperatively to 0 (IQR: 0–1) postoperatively (p < 0.01), with 97 % of patients reported sustained pain relief.

Treatment failure occurred in 11 patients (8.7 %), including symptomatic recurrence (n = 4, 3.1 %), minor peripheral nerve complaints (n = 3, 2.4 %), and minor range-of-motion limitations (n = 3, 2.4 %). One patient experienced multiple complications. Median postoperative VAS among patients with treatment failure was 2 (IQR: 2–5). Discussion

The principal finding of our study is that Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT)-navigated radiofrequency ablation (RFA) significantly reduces postoperative pain and functional impairment, as demonstrated by marked improvements in VAS and ECOG scores. Specifically, 97 % of patients reported sustained pain relief, with only 11 % experiencing any form of treatment failure. Analysis of potential predictors of poorer outcomes revealed adult age as a modest negative predictor; however, patient sex, tumor size, and anatomical location were not associated with an increased risk of treatment failure. Moreover, significant improvements in median ECOG and VAS scores were consistently observed across all patient groups. Importantly, symptomatic recurrence was rare, occurring in only 3 % of cases, indicating that CBCT-navigated RFA effectively reduces the influence of common risk factors for treatment failure.

The advent of navigation technology has profoundly transformed numerous medical fields, significantly enhancing surgical efficacy, patient safety, and clinical outcomes across various specialties. In spine surgery, navigation systems have become essential, offering real-time anatomical tracking that substantially reduces the risk of injury to critical structures.23, 24, 25 Orthopedic oncology has similarly benefited from these advancements, increasingly utilizing navigation technologies for the precise resection of desmoid tumors and complex bone lesions, thus ensuring comprehensive tumor removal while minimizing functional impairment.26 Additionally, in managing musculoskeletal metastases, navigation-assisted interventions facilitate more accurate tumor ablation and resection, significantly improving local control and preserving surrounding healthy tissue integrity.27,28 In this context, the integration of CBCT-navigated RFA for osteoid osteoma represents a logical progression. This innovative approach leverages precise imaging guidance to optimize lesion localization, decrease procedural duration, and potentially enhance long-term clinical outcomes. By adopting CBCT-navigated RFA, the treatment of osteoid osteoma aligns seamlessly with the broader trend toward precision surgery—characterized by technology-driven, minimally invasive, and highly targeted therapeutic interventions.

Real-time, high-resolution imaging through CBCT navigation facilitates precise tumor nidus localization and optimal needle placement, minimizing collateral damage and ensuring complete ablation. This advanced approach demonstrated superior outcomes compared to conventional CT-guided RFA. Specifically, Lassalle et al. and Tordjman et al. reported recurrence rates of 10.2 % with conventional methods,12,20 whereas our CBCT-navigated cohort exhibited a significantly lower recurrence rate of just 3.14 %. Furthermore, fracture incidence was notably reduced to 0.8 % in our cohort compared to 4.8 % among patients treated without CBCT navigation.29 Our median postoperative VAS score reduction is consistent with established literature.11,12 Notably, long-term pain relief was achieved in 97 % of our patients, marking a success rate unprecedented in cohorts of comparable size and follow-up duration.

Age has been reported as a potential predictor of RFA outcomes, though findings in the literature remain inconsistent. While Baal et al. and Tung et al. identified younger age as a risk factor for symptomatic recurrence,8,14 other studies, including Hage et al. and Tordjman et al., found no such association.15,29 In the present analysis, postoperative VAS and ECOG scores did not differ significantly between age groups. However, all treatment failures occurred in adults, and increasing age was independently associated with higher risk of treatment failure (OR = 1.117, p = 0.036). The superior outcomes in pediatric patients likely reflect biological advantages, including active osteoblast populations, rich vascular supply, open physes, and a responsive periosteum, which together facilitate rapid nidus replacement, rim remodeling, edema resolution, and pain relief after RFA.13, 14, 15 These features align with the observed faster pain resolution and advanced bone healing in children. Conversely, age-related declines in bone remodeling capacity and mechanical adaptability may predispose older patients to recurrence or fracture.30

Tumor size has traditionally been considered a negative predictor of outcomes following RFA. Previous studies have indicated that larger tumors might pose technical challenges; however, our results do not support a significant association between tumor size and treatment failure.8,31 Postoperative VAS and ECOG performance status scores showed no significant differences when comparing smaller and larger tumors. Moreover, tumor size was not significantly associated with increased treatment failure rates. These findings suggest that the technical challenges typically associated with ablating larger tumors may be effectively mitigated by advancements in CBCT-navigated RFA. These advancements enable more precise probe placement and facilitate the creation of overlapping ablation zones, a critical factor in effectively managing larger tumors.32

Mun et al. hypothesized that hormonal differences in females might influence bone remodeling and inflammatory responses following RFA, while Baal et al. investigated sex as a possible predictor of treatment failure.14,33,34 However, our study findings do not corroborate these claims. We observed no significant differences in treatment failure rates between male and female patients. Additionally, sex was not associated with higher postoperative VAS or ECOG scores. The uniform biological behavior of osteoid osteomas across both sexes suggests that precision surgery, such as RFA, effectively mitigates potential sex-related disparities, resulting in comparable clinical outcomes.18

Anatomical location has been proposed as a determinant of RFA success, particularly for lesions in weight-bearing bones or adjacent to neurovascular structures. While prior studies report variable outcomes depending on lesion site, our analysis found no significant differences in treatment failure rates, postoperative pain, or functional outcomes across anatomical regions.18,19 Notably, no treatment failures occurred in spinal osteoid osteomas, underscoring the potential of CBCT navigation to optimize both safety and efficacy in anatomically complex or high-stakes areas. The technology's higher spatial accuracy minimizes the risk of geographic miss—a key driver of recurrence in both spinal and non-spinal locations—by enabling multiplanar trajectory planning, navigated instrument advancement, and iterative confirmation of probe position before ablation. These features are particularly advantageous in constrained anatomical corridors such as the spine.35,36

Prior treatment has previously been suggested as a negative predictive factor for radiofrequency ablation (RFA) success, primarily due to concerns that prior interventions could compromise bone integrity or modify tumor responsiveness.9,17,37 Contrary to this assumption, our results revealed no significant difference in treatment failure rates between patients with and without previous interventions. Furthermore, a history of prior treatment did not correlate with increased postoperative VAS or ECOG scores, nor did it elevate the risk of treatment failure. Notably, most revision cases referred to our institution from external medical facilities were initially treated without CBCT navigation. Among these referrals, there was a significantly higher proportion of large tumors (P < 0.01), underscoring the likelihood that larger lesions treated without precision-guided surgery often necessitate repeat ablation sessions.8 The observed reduction in poor outcomes traditionally associated with known risk factors emphasizes the effectiveness and added value of employing precision-guided surgical techniques such as CBCT navigation.

While the clinical promise of CBCT-guided navigation is clear, broader adoption is influenced by the learning curve, workflow integration, and infrastructure requirements. Evidence from complex musculoskeletal osteotomies indicates that CBCT navigation improves cut accuracy regardless of surgeon experience, underscoring reproducible precision benefits likely transferable to osteoid osteoma targeting and access planning.21 However, early-use penalties in efficiency and radiation exposure have been observed, largely resolving as teams gain proficiency. Required infrastructure includes a hybrid OR or intraoperative CBCT system (e.g., O-arm), an optical tracking/navigation platform, and compatible instruments. Reports in musculoskeletal tumor surgery describe seamless workflows integrating preoperative planning, registration, and intraoperative 3D verification, with further gains in accuracy when using fluoro/CT matching.38 Compared to conventional CT, CBCT platforms offer a smaller footprint and potentially lower capital and operational costs, facilitating placement outside radiology and improving accessibility for extremity procedures. Operative times for CBCT-guided OO curettage average ∼1.5 h in deep locations, and for percutaneous ablation, navigation does not prolong total procedure duration once adopted.21,27,39, 40, 41

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center nature may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader populations. Second, although VAS assessments effectively capture patient-reported outcomes, their inherent subjectivity may introduce reporting bias. Third, the application of ECOG performance scores specifically for osteoid osteoma and RFA is not extensively validated, necessitating caution when interpreting these results. Additionally, the lack of a CT-guided RFA control group prevents direct comparison and limits the ability to definitively attribute observed outcomes to specific treatment modalities. Future research should address these limitations through prospective, multicenter studies involving larger cohorts and incorporating comprehensive clinical and imaging data to enhance the validity and robustness of these findings.

In this series, adult age emerged as a statistically significant predictor of treatment failure, whereas sex, tumor size, anatomical location, and prior treatment history did not. This suggests that age-related factors—possibly biological rather than purely technical—may underlie poorer outcomes in older patients. It was initially hypothesized that CBCT-navigated RFA could mitigate the negative impact of conventional risk factors, particularly where precise lesion localization is challenging (e.g., anatomically constrained regions, prior interventions). This assumption is supported in contexts where the main limitation is targeting accuracy; however, in subgroups where prognosis is influenced by inherent biological behavior (e.g., adult bone remodeling capacity), localization improvements may not fully offset recurrence risk. This could explain why only age—but not other technical variables—remained a significant predictor in this study.

Conclusion

CBCT-navigated RFA demonstrated significant clinical improvement with low rates of complications and recurrence. Precision-guided surgery effectively mitigates the risk of treatment failure, particularly in patients with poor prognostic factors.
