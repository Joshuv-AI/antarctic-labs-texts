Imaging of post-tuberculosis lung disease cases in children and adolescent survivors: a systematic review
Authors: Heda Melinda Nataprawira, Intan MW Dewi, I Gusti Agung Ayu Novi Wiraningrat, Citra Cesilia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionPost-tuberculosis lung disease (PTLD) causes health problems among pulmonary TB (PTB) survivors. Post-TB patients may suffer from chronic respiratory symptoms, declining lung function, and persistent radiological abnormalities. However, studies regarding PTLD in children and adolescents are still scarce. Patterns of radiological abnormalities, including chest X-ray (CXR) imaging, high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in post-TB children, and adolescents are not fully understood.AimIn this study, we aim to review and analyse radiological features in children and adolescent TB survivors of the literature on the differences in imaging findings in drug-resistant (DR) and drug-sensitive tuberculosis (DS TB) children and adolescent TB survivors.MethodWe performed a systematic review to determine imaging patterns of DR and DS TB in children and adolescent survivors. Data collected include study design, number of subjects, age, TB category, treatment duration, time of evaluation, and imaging patterns. We searched MEDLINE/Pubmed, Google Scholar, Science Direct, Wiley Online Library, Cochrane Library, and Proquest and included four studies for data analysis. Study quality was assessed using a modified Newcastle-Ottawa score.ResultStudies included 151 children and adolescents aged 0–17 years. Three out of four studies were conducted on DS-TB patients and one study compared DS- and DR-TB. Radiological abnormalities observed by CXR at TB treatment completion include calcification in the presence or absence of fibrosis, bronchiectasis, and destroyed lung, or lymphoid interstitial pneumonitis. Micronodules are most often seen in HRCT in the acute early stages of TB and were not seen in standard chest radiography. Cavities persisted in almost 50% of patients after TB treatment and fibrotic changes increased after treatment.ConclusionImaging abnormalities after TB treatment are often seen in children and adolescents. Imaging evaluation should be performed in PTB survivors, especially in those with moderate or advanced lesions during active disease and those with severe clinical manifestations.KEYWORDS: Tuberculosis, post-TB lung disease, adolescent, children, imaging

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant global health challenge, particularly affecting children and adolescents. Despite substantial progress in diagnosis, treatment, and prevention, TB continues to be a major cause of morbidity and mortality in these age groups. While the immediate focus often lies on the active phase of the disease, long-term consequences of TB, particularly post-TB lung disease (PTLD), are gaining attention in clinical and public health domains, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where TB burden is high [1,2]. Despite improvements in TB treatment, many patients continue to have health problems. TB may cause permanent lung damage and lung function impairments, even after successful treatment. PTLD is defined as “evidence of chronic respiratory abnormality, with or without symptoms, attributable at least in part to previous pulmonary TB. PTLD may affect airways, lung parenchyma, pulmonary vasculature, and pleura. In children and adolescents, very little is known regarding patterns and characteristics of PTLD [3,4].

Imaging plays a major role in evaluating lung pathology, which may persist beyond TB treatment. Chest X-ray (CXR) may show persistent radiological abnormalities, including the presence of cavity, fibrosis, infiltrates, and calcified nodules [4,5]. CT-scan provides a more detailed characterization of cavitations, bronchiectasis, and presence of aspergilloma. In this study, we aim to review and analyse radiological features in children and adolescent TB survivors. In our analysis, we include studies utilizing CXR, CT imaging, and HRCT. CXR is widely used to diagnose and evaluate TB disease, but CT and HRCT provide specifications and also visualize the extent of lung damage, and HRCT is effective in detecting small foci of parenchymal cavitation and also as imaging at TB treatment completion. PTLD can be assessed using various imaging modalities, such as chest X-ray (CXR), high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT), or chest MRI. Each of these modalities may demonstrate different findings [5,6].

By systematically reviewing available literature, we hope to provide a better understanding of imaging patterns of PTLD in children and adolescents, which could contribute to better diagnostic and management strategies for this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review will follow the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines to ensure a comprehensive and transparent approach.16 This review aims to identify and collate existing literature on PTLD in children and adolescents, with a specific focus on imaging abnormalities.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search will be conducted across several electronic databases, including MEDLINE/Pubmed, Google Scholar, Science Direct, Wiley Online Library, Cochrane Library, and Proquest. The search will cover studies published from the inception of each database until the date of the search. The following keywords are ‘post-tuberculosis lung disease’ OR ‘lung impairment’ OR ‘respiratory disability’ OR ‘lung sequelae’ AND ‘radiology abnormalities’ OR ‘Chest-Xray’ OR ‘HRCT’ OR ‘chest MRI’ AND ‘children’ OR ‘pediatric’ OR ‘paediatrician’ AND ‘adolescent’ (Table 1). These bibliographic electronic databases were selected because they provide comprehensive and precise access to international references published in peer-reviewed journals. They cover a wide range of disciplines relevant to our research topic and are commonly used in systematic reviews for their reliability, accessibility, and inclusion of high-quality scientific literature. Study quality was assessed using a modified Newcastle-Ottawa score. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale is a tool used to assess the quality of studies, particularly in systematic reviews and meta-analyses, and consists of an eight-item instrument.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were 1) Studies involving children and younger children (including infants) (0–18 years) and adolescents (up to 19 years) who were diagnosed with PTB and have completed TB treatment for at least 6 months, 2) Studies reporting radiological findings on CXR, HRCT, or chest MRI performed at the end of treatment and/or serial imaging during treatment 3) Original research articles, including cohort studies, case–control studies, and cross-sectional studies, and 4) Articles published in English.

Reviews, commentaries, editorials, and case reports without original data; studies without radiological examination; and studies in which full text could not be retrieved were excluded.

Selection of studies

Two independent reviewers (CC and N) performed the article screening which included relevant titles and abstracts. Full text of potentially eligible studies will be retrieved and assessed according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Non-eligible studies and reports inaccessible were excluded due to not meeting the methodological criteria or the full text was unavailable. Discrepancies between reviewers will be resolved through discussion, and if necessary, a third reviewer will be consulted (HM). A number of screened and excluded studies can be seen in Figure 1.

●Radiological findings: type of imaging modality (Chest X-ray, HRCT, MRI), specific lung abnormalities or pathology described, and frequency of these abnormalities.

Results

We identified 2445 articles, with 1952 articles removed due to duplicates and irrelevant titles. Abstracts were reviewed for 493 articles, and 433 studies were excluded due to incorrect study design, leaving 60 articles for full-text review. Four articles were excluded because they were inaccessible, and 52 were excluded due to wrong age group, failure in reporting imaging pattern, not written in English, and full articles could not be retrieved, leaving 4 articles for data extraction. The patterns of radiological abnormalities in post-TB children and adolescents are studied, with each study assessing these patterns using either chest X-ray (CXR), HRCT, or MRI.

In the first study, Schaaf et al. enrolled paediatric patients aged 15 years or less with MDR-TB between 1998 and 2001 in South Africa. In this study, PTB occurred in 34 (95%) children, and these children underwent treatment for 6–19 months (Table 2). The authors observed improvement in CXR findings in 15 (44%) patients. At TB treatment completion, calcification occurred in five (15%) of patients, fibrosis in five (15%), calcification and fibrosis in two (6%), bronchiectasis or destroyed lung in six (18%), and lymphoid interstitial pneumonitis in one (3%) patients [4].

HRCT was performed in 52 bacteriologically confirmed paediatric PTB patients in Taiwan at the time of diagnosis and after TB treatment [3]. Patients received 6-month TB treatment regimen, consisting of 2 months initial phase and 4-month continuation phase of isoniazid, rifampicin, and ethambutol. HRCT findings included micronodules, nodules, mass, tree-in-bud-appearance, consolidation, ground glass opacity, cavitation, bronchiectasis, fibrotic changes, parenchymal calcification, bullous formation, lymphadenopathy (> 10 mm), pleural thickening, and pleural effusion. Micronodules were mostly observed in the acute early stages of TB disease and were found all over the lung parenchyma. After TB treatment, these micronodules remained in 39 (75%) of patients. Nodules were also commonly seen before TB treatment and persisted in approximately 56% of patients. Masses, tree-in-bud appearance, and consolidation were observed in some patients before treatment initiation, and decreased dramatically after treatment. Cavities were present in 73% of patients in the early course of the disease and persisted in 35% patients after TB treatment. Fibrotic changes occurred in approximately 40% of patients before treatment initiation and significantly increased in a number of patients after treatment. HRCT is optimal in detecting small foci of parenchymal cavitation both in areas of dense fibrocalcific disease associated with distortion of the underlying lung parenchyma and hence may provide valuable information on the diagnosis of active TB. The disappearance of tree-in-bud appearance, absence of pleural effusions, and the presence of fibrotic changes seemed to be indications of treatment effectiveness [3].

In India, Mukand et al. performed contrast-enhanced CT in paediatric patients (< 17 years) with PTB [5]. An initial chest CT was performed in 91 patients prior to TB treatment and in 45 patients at 6–7 months after treatment initiation. At baseline, lymphadenopathy was commonly observed in the patients, with paratracheal being the most common site. At treatment completion, regression of nodes was seen in 30 (66.7%) patient, in which 6 (13.3%) patients showed complete resolution and the remaining had nodes <1 cm in size. Nodal calcification was observed in 28% of patients. In post-treatment CT, perinodal fat reappeared in all and most patients showed obscuration of perinodal fat. In this study, 15 patients had residual imaging after 6 months of ATT. As many as 52% of patient showed reduction in the size of nodes still showing significant size. Approximately 33.3% patients showed peripheral and inhomogeneous enhancements, and others showed increased number of homogenous nodes. Rates of regression were variable in children, ranging from 6 months to 9 months. In symptomatic patients, nodes of sizes > 1 cm represented active disease. Active or inactive residual nodes should be based on clinical correlation. Disease activity is a major determinant of increasing nodal size, conglomeration, and obscuration of perinodal fat.

In a study by Kiper et al., intermittent administration of isoniazid, rifampin, and streptomycin in young infants aged less than 6 months with TB resulted in radiologic improvement in CXR between 3 and 6 months of therapy [7]. In one infant with miliary tuberculosis, late CXR films showed calcification after treatment. There were marked improvements in the chest roentgenograms in 14 patients at the end of the therapy.

Discussion

Imaging plays a major role in evaluating lung pathology, which may persist beyond TB treatment. Chest X-ray (CXR), chest HRCT, and chest MRI could show persistent radiological abnormalities in patients with post-TB sequelae, such as PTLD. In this study, we discuss previous studies that investigated radiological changes in children and adolescents with pulmonary TB. Studies identified calcification, fibrosis, calcification, bronchiectasis, and lymphoid interstitial pneumonitis as the most common findings in children and adolescent TB survivors. The radiological presentations of drug susceptibility and drug resistance are similar due to disease progression because of the delay in diagnosis [4,5].

In the extracted studies, chest radiograph findings show abnormalities at treatment completion, including calcification in the presence or absence of fibrosis, bronchiectasis, destroyed lung, or lymphoid interstitial pneumonitis. Kwon et al. showed that in approximately 50% of patients with culture-negative PTB, there is minimal improvement in imaging at 1 or 2 months after treatment initiation [8]. In a study of adult PTB patients in India, complete radiological resolution was seen in approximately 60% of patients, and residual pathology include parenchymal, pleural, and mediastinal abnormalities [9].

CT is the gold standard for the detection of the morphological changes of lung parenchyma, but it has a high radiation exposure compared to MRI. MRI as the alternative to CT, but its role for assessing infectious need to be further studied. In recent years HRCT, a specialized CT technique that uses thin-slice acquisition and specific reconstruction algorithms to provide greater spatial resolution and better visualization of fine lung structures has been found to be more superior to chest radiography and used for the assessment of PTB [10]. The study by Lee et al. showed micronodules are most often seen in HRCT as the acute early stages of TB and not seen on standard chest radiography. Micronodules usually 5–6 mm in diameter and generally located peripherally of consolidation or cavities in the upper lung zones (apical or posterior segments of upper lobes and superior segment of lower lobes [3,11]. Micronodules are not visible on standard chest radiography. Micronodules appeared to be a diagnostic sign of TB and these discrete foci are indeed highly suggestive of active TB. Other patterns observed in CT marking active TB disease are the tree-in-bud appearance, which often disappeared at the end of treatment. Absence of pleural effusion and the presence of fibrosis may indicate the effectiveness of the treatment. Meghji et al. showed that participants with weekly or monthly respiratory symptoms had a higher proportion of destroyed lung in more than one lobe on HRCT imaging at TB treatment completion [9].

Micronodules and tree-in-bud appearance were the most common characteristic features in HRCT evaluation, whereas the disappearance of tree-in-bud appearance, absence of pleural effusion, and the presence of fibrotic change seemed to be indications of effectiveness of treatment. HRCT is effective in detecting small foci of parenchymal cavitation both in areas of confluent pneumonia and in areas of dense fibrocalcific-disease associated with distortion of the underlying lung parenchyma [3,12].

The presence of these imaging abnormalities has several clinical implications. First, early detection and intervention by recognizing these radiological findings early can prompt timely interventions, potentially mitigating the progression of chronic respiratory conditions. Second, long-term follow-up for children and adolescents with PTLD who require ongoing monitoring to manage and treat complications as they arise, highlighting the need for structured follow-up protocols. Third, multidisciplinary approach by managing PTLD effectively requires a collaborative approach involving paediatricians, pulmonologists, radiologists, and physiotherapists to address the multifaceted needs of these patients [5,13].

Imaging plays a major role to evaluate outcome of TB treatment [14]. TB-induced inflammation may lead to scar formation and lung tissue damage, which could cause complications such as bronchiectasis, fibrosis, and even respiratory failure in severe cases. Prevention of PTLD primarily involves early detection and effective treatment of active TB infections to minimize lung damage and inflammation. With appropriate management, many individuals can lead relatively normal lives, although some may experience persistent respiratory symptoms. Regular follow-up is required to monitor the occurrence of post-TB sequelae, especially when clinical symptoms are present [3,6,15].

Our study has several limitations. First, the heterogeneity in the study designs, sample sizes, and imaging modalities used across the included studies may affect the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, there may be publication bias towards studies reporting significant findings, potentially overestimating the prevalence of certain abnormalities. Last but not least, many studies included broader age ranges or lacked specific subgroup analyses for children and adolescents, limiting the specificity of the findings to the paediatric population. Despite these limitations, the strength of this review lies in the utilization of multiple databases and a broad range of keywords ensuring a thorough literature search, capturing a wide array of relevant studies. In addition, our focus on imaging findings makes this review provides a detailed synthesis of the radiological sequelae of PTLD in children and adolescents, offering valuable insights for clinical practice and future research.

Conclusion

This systematic review underscores the significant burden of radiological abnormalities in children and adolescents with PTLD, emphasizing the need for early detection, ongoing monitoring, and comprehensive care. By advancing our understanding of the radiological sequelae of TB, this review contributes to improving the long-term health outcomes of paediatric TB survivors. Future research should focus on addressing the identified gaps and enhancing the evidence base to support optimal management of PTLD in this vulnerable population.
