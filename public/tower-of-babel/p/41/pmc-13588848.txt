Hierarchical learning creates invariant schema within plastic neural networks
Authors: James R. Elder, Jie Zheng, Lydia B. Shimelis, Ueli Rutishauser, Milo M. Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cognitive neural circuits must balance the plasticity needed for continual learning with the stability needed to preserve an underlying reasoning framework, called a schema. How circuit learning rules form and protect such schemata from being continually overwritten during learning remains unknown. On a visual boundary detection task, we show that a hierarchical learning algorithm creates an invariant schema circuit whose weights remain fixed following sparse initial training, with additional data serving to refine the upstream representation. In contrast, the end-to-end backpropagation algorithm used to train nearly all current artificial neural networks comprehensively changes its weights throughout training. We show that the hierarchical schema makes an independent mechanistic hypothesis about circuit computation that is consistent with experimental ordering observed in single-neuron measurements in humans. These results suggest that hierarchical learning is sufficient to encode biologically consistent persistent cognitive models within otherwise malleable neural networks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10827-026-00940-x.

Introduction

How neural networks solve cognitive tasks in a way that prioritizes model robustness while allowing plasticity is an open problem (French, 1999; McClelland & Rumelhart, 1985; Mermillod et al., 2013). The brain is capable of continuous learning and model refinement while retaining previously learned core cognitive structures (Grossberg, 2012; Sutton, 1986)–referred to as “schemata" in cognitive psychology (Piaget, 1952). Schemata provide a cognitive framework in which the central logic changes slowly in a discrete or categorical manner consistent with symbolic reasoning, whereas unconstrained degrees of freedom are allowed to vary rapidly and continuously with new information. Schemata therefore increase the likelihood of finding models that generalize well to new data and transfer to new tasks.

How schemata emerge as a neural network property is an open problem. Neural circuits can create subpopulations of stable and plastic neural connectivities. For example, in visual processing in humans, previously plastic sensory thalamic inputs in the visual cortex stabilize in adulthood, while higher-order visual representations remain plastic (Faguet et al., 2009; Pakan et al., 2018; Tan et al., 2020; Yaeger et al., 2023). Similarly, auditory fear conditioning suggests that synapse-specific plasticity encodes memory associations, whereas engram cells form a stable network, allowing specific memory erasure without schema disruption (Abdou et al., 2018). However, the self-organizing principles by which neural network connectivities become sensitive to–but whose cognitive structure is robust to–new inputs are unknown (Alexandrov et al., 2024).

Longstanding limitations in spatial and temporal resolution preclude a purely experimental solution to this problem. Computational modeling can potentially identify general mechanistic principles (Jain et al., 2024; Kay, 2018; Marder & Prinz, 2002; Prinz et al., 2004) and make testable predictions about connectivity, perturbation response, and firing patterns (Storrs and Kriegeskorte 2019; Pulvermüller et al. 2021; Khemlani et al. 2015; Kriegeskorte 2015; Hassabis et al. 2017; Franklin et al. 2020). Such models can also reveal how observed neural representations, such as grid cells, can arise solely from the nature of the network learning dynamics (Cueva & Wei, 2018). Until recently, artificial neural networks (ANNs) capable of learning general-purpose perception tasks at scale have been trained using an end-to-end learning framework in which the error of the network output is minimized by parameter adjustments propagated back through the network (LeCun et al., 2015; Muller et al., 2005; Rumelhart et al., 1986). Initialized with random parameters, the Backpropagation algorithm minimizes the task-specific error via gradient descent in parameter space. In this approach, neural connectivities and couplings are meaningful only insofar that they decrease output error. Consequently, such ANNs do not inherently create protected schemata and are susceptible to “catastrophic forgetting" of previous tasks after training on new related tasks (Mccloskey & Cohen, 1989; Ratcliff, 1990). In backpropagation-trained networks, learning from additional data can overwrite all system parameters. More fundamentally, the lack of robust schemata may explain why existing feedforward neural network-based models, including large language models employing transformers, are incapable of consistent reasoning and world-building despite matching or surpassing humans in many strategy, language, and generative tasks (Vafa et al., 2025, 2024).Fig. 1Hierarchical basis of schema formation in ENNs Downstream schema consolidation enables modular refinement of upstream hyperplanes that improve performance, while retaining the signal interpreted by the hidden layers. Recently, we introduced a general-purpose hierarchical learning model that intrinsically produces neurobiological circuit properties missing from backpropagation trained networks (Bengio et al., 2006; Blazek & Lin, 2021; Hinton et al., 2006). Neurons in these Essence Neural Networks (ENNs) are trained to perform stand-alone pair-wise distinguishing tasks and then hierarchically combined to perform the circuit task (Fig. 1a). This layer-wise learning approach increases network interpretability, enabling ENNs to be automatically distilled into equivalent human-readable code while demonstrating comparable performance to backpropagation across an array of generalization benchmarks (Blazek et al., 2024).

We hypothesize that the hierarchical nature of ENN training can naturally encode robust schemata as follows (See Blazek and Lin (2021); Blazek et al. (2024) for detailed descriptions of ENN training). The first layer of ENN neurons, called differentia neurons, are trained to optimally separate between all pairs of data clusters within the input space. The next layer of neurons, called subconcept neurons take as inputs the outputs of the differentia neurons (Sup. Fig. 1). Each subconcept neuron is trained to approximate a convex region of input space whose boundaries are the hyperplanes defined by the differentia neurons. The weights of the subconcept neuron essentially correspond to which side of each hyperplane the bounded region belongs (Fig. 1a). Therefore, as long as the subconcept region has not been absorbed into another region or split into multiple regions as a consequence of new data, the incoming weights of the subconcept neuron should remain unchanged because the region should remain on the same side of each of the bounding hyperplanes that define the region, even if the hyperplanes themselves (the geometry of the region) are changing (Fig. 1a,b). This is a form of “topological protection," though differing from the topologically protected states that can endow robustness in electronic and mechanical systems (Kane & Lubensky, 2014; Paulose et al., 2015; Ryu & Hatsugai, 2002). In this work, we test this hypothesis by assessing schema formation within neural networks trained on a cognitive vision task by the hierarchical ENN algorithm as well as the end-to-end backpropagation algorithm. We also compare the neural activity patterns of networks trained on these alternative models with high resolution neural recordings of human patients performing the same task. Here, we define a schema in a computational model as a subcircuit whose network parameters remain invariant despite decreasing training loss; this necessitates that other parts of the network must continue to be modified by new training data. We also investigate the implications of schema formation on model performance, generalizability, robustness to noise, and statistical signatures of neuronal firing. Finally, we test if biological circuits display characteristic signatures of either training paradigm. We focus our analysis on feed-forward neural network architectures, the dominant model in modern deep learning.

Human experience is continuous, comprised of diverse internal and external neuronal and sensory inputs. However, memory encoding requires discrete segmentation of beginnings and endings (Brunec et al., 2018; Richmond & Zacks, 2017). Therefore, proper recognition of boundaries separating distinct visual inputs is required for effective memory encoding. In recent work (Zheng et al., 2022), we identified two distinct neuronal cell types, Boundary and Event cells, in single-cell neuron recordings primarily in the medial temporal lobe (MTL, Fig. 2c) implicated in boundary detection. Boundary cells fire shortly after the onset of a boundary (seen in Soft Boundary (SB) and Hard Boundary (HB) clips), whereas Event cells fire only for Hard Boundaries (HB) approximately 100 ms after Boundary cells (Fig. 2f-h). We show that the hierarchical ENN learning algorithm reproducibly converges to a stable schema consistent with the Boundary and Event cells observed in neural recordings, even with sparse training. Further training data refine the input representation into the invariant schema, increasing accuracy. In contrast, backpropagation-based networks are continually reconfigured during learning, with no fixed schema. Consequently, ENNs display increased robustness to parameter noise and are distillable into a discrete circuit that offers a causal explanation for the experimental temporal delay between Boundary and Event cells. Together, these results suggest hierarchical learning can generically create experimentally consistent neural networks with persistent cognitive reasoning structures that mitigate against catastrophic forgetting.

Results

To model the pre-processing in the optic pathway (Fig. 2b), we transformed the pixel inputs into the visual embedding of a convolutional neural network (CNN), a successful model of object recognition in the cortex (Cichy et al., 2016; Wen et al., 2018). For example, CNNs unconstrained by neural data contain layers highly predictive of neural responses in both V4 and inferior temporal cortex when trained on naturalistic images (Yamins et al., 2014). Four representative frames (2 before the boundary, 2 after the boundary) from video clips shown to patients during neural recording (Zheng et al., 2022) were fed through a CNN pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset (VGG-16, Fig. 2d) (Rawat & Wang, 2017; Simonyan & Zisserman, 2015). The concatenated embeddings were used to train the feed-forward boundary classification neural network (Fig. 2e).

The networks consisted of 2 hidden layers, each with 3 interneurons, which are determined by the number of clusters needed to separate the training data into their output labels as determined in the first stage of the ENN’s training algorithm (see Methods section and Refs. Blazek and Lin (2021); Blazek et al. (2024) for details). For consistent comparison, backpropagation networks were trained with identical architecture. Networks were trained using either Backpropagation (end-to-end) or the ENN learning framework (hierarchical) with an 85-15% train-test split on 978 video clips. Across multiple splits, converged networks with 100% training accuracy achieved an average test accuracy of 62.3% (±3.8%) and 56.1% (±4.7%) for ENNs and Backprop, respectively (Sup. Fig. 3), a 1.9 fold improvement over chance, with error primarily coming from distinguishing between soft versus hard boundary cases (Sup. Fig. 4). Alternate embeddings, larger networks, and data augmentation strategies failed to improve performance, likely due to the small dataset available (Sup. Figs. 5, 6). For the purpose of comparing hierarchical versus end-to-end learning, we will show that this level of performance is sufficient due to ENN schema fixation under sparse training (Fig. 3b). Hierarchical networks converge to a stable schema robust to further training

Subsequently, we investigated the structure of trained networks across different learning algorithms and varying training conditions to assess the robustness of network parameters and identify invariant schema. Notably, hierarchical learning converges to a stable schema with limited training data (hidden layers, Fig. 3b). Additional data solely refines input layer weights, optimizing feature representation into the invariant schema and leading to stratification of feature importance (input layer, Fig. 3b). In contrast, networks trained using end-to-end learning (Backprop) display ongoing changes to all network weights during the entire training period (Fig. 3b). Specifically, weights in the “cognitive" layers downstream of the input layer fluctuate without dampening when trained on increasing amounts of data (hidden layers, Fig. 3b). Interestingly, in contrast to the corresponding layer of the ENN, the input layer weight distribution of the Backprop-trained network does not change with increasing training data, indicating a lack of representational sparsification during end-to-end learning (input layer, Fig. 3b). Hierarchical networks are robust to parameter noise and feature perturbation

We next investigated the implications of these differences on robustness to fluctuations in synaptic strength. We directly perturbed the trained networks’ internal weights connecting the first two hidden layers. Upon adding random noise to the weights, the ENN retained its performance even at noise levels of the magnitude of the learned weights (Fig. 4a). Furthermore, the failure modes of these networks were qualitatively different: misclassifications induced by noise in the ENNs primarily occurred in a specific class, whereas Backprop networks displayed varied failure modes, likely due to their distributed computation (Fig. 4c-d). Hierarchical networks’ robustness to parameter noise reflects an additional benefit of the logical consolidation of the schema into the hidden layers.

Similarly, hierarchically trained networks were robust to perturbations to the features of the inputs. Specifically, when features across frames, ranked by importance, were ablated (see Methods for details), ENNs were significantly more resistance to the loss of signal than Backprop networks (Fig. 4b). This finding gives context to the consolidation of input feature importance (Fig. 3b) observed in hierarchical learning.

Hierarchical schema is distillable into a concise interpretable circuit

Inspired by recent work that distilled trained ENNs into human-readable code, we set out to convert the ENN’s learned schema into a binary circuit. Leveraging the stable schema of ENNs and its discrete logic (Fig. 3c), we constructed a simple circuit that utilizes 3 discrete, hierarchically separated neurons with weights from the full ENN to perform the boundary-detection task with an equivalent accuracy to the original network (Fig. 4e). The circuit’s discrete, logical neurons have outputs identical to those observed experimentally. In the distilled circuit, the event-like cell is directly gated by the boundary-like cells, suggesting a mechanism in which the experimentally observed delay is due to the Boundary cells being upstream of Event cells.

To further simplify the model in the input space, after observing the robustness to feature ablation, we generated a sparse circuit that operates on only 50 features per frame (Fig. 4e, example features in Sup. Fig. 8), with only a marginal loss in performance. Additionally, because of the early schema consolidation of hierarchical learning, this distilling can be used to assess the circuit’s performance with varying amounts of sparsified training data. Distinct populations of hidden neurons segregate into sequential layers in hierarchical models

We next characterized the hidden layer neurons in the artificial neural networks to assess if distinct populations of neurons emerged in either training framework. We analyzed the activated output of all interneurons by layer across various test-train splits on a subset of inputs from each class (Fig. 5a). Additionally, we separated the boundary and event cells according to their peak firing into Early and Late firing neurons (Fig. 2h) and analyzed their activated firing alongside the artificial neurons (Fig. 5a, MTL). In the hierarchically trained networks, we observed 2 distinct firing phenotypes on the first layer, and 3 distinct firing phenotypes on the second layer. Across networks, the neurons in the first layer serve to distinguish between NB and SB or HB inputs, while the second layer further separates SB from HB inputs. In contrast, end-to-end learning (Backprop) distributes these distinctions across layers. These findings align with the results of the ENN circuit distilling, suggesting that the first layer detects a boundary (either SB or HB), while the second layer distinguishes between SB and HB inputs.

Subsequently, we quantitatively characterized the neurons average firing by class (Fig. 5b, see Sup. Fig. 9 for standard deviations). These results further demonstrated the presence of distinct, layer-dependent populations in ENNs, while Backprop networks had various firing phenotypes distributed unrelated to layer. To further quantify this separation, we analyzed how many hyperplanes were needed to cleanly separate layer 1 neurons from layer 2 neurons by average firing (Sup. Fig. 10). The ENN neurons needed fewer hyperplanes than the Backprop networks as more neurons were analyzed, reaching a plateau while the Backprop neurons continually needed more hyperplanes, suggesting the emergence of distinct, layer-specific neuron populations in ENNs, but not Backprop networks.

Discussion

Previously, we showed that ENNs intrinsically possess neurobiological properties, such as modular connectivity and localized firing, that are missing in networks trained using backpropagation without additional priors or constraints (Blazek & Lin, 2021). However, how internal logic was consolidated and whether it was stable over varying amounts of data and noise was not investigated. In this work we show that even during the early learning stage, hierarchical learning allows continual refinement by optimizing the mapping from inputs to internal representations while leaving the core schema invariant. In contrast, end-to-end learning leads to continual updating of all network parameters with new training data, thereby preventing schema creation. Additionally, how the neuronal output of either ENN or backpropagation models relates to that of biological neurons implicated in the same cognitive task was not addressed. In this study, for the visual boundary detection task, we demonstrate that only ENN networks consistently produce distinct, layer-dependent populations of neurons. Furthermore, these results suggest a connectionist based explanation of the temporal ordering of biological cell firings, where Boundary cells fire “upstream" of the Event cells, lessening the computational burden of the Event cells by allowing them to primarily distinguish between soft and hard boundaries.

We limit our modeling of cognitive circuits to feed-forward neural network architectures which are dominant in artificial intelligence, as opposed to related work using recurrent models, Bayesian Neural Networks, or neural circuit models (Bonnet et al., 2025; Goudar et al., 2023; Miconi & Kay, 2025). In the context of biological neurons’ continual firing, we additionally have to make assumptions regarding the relationship of temporal separation to layer ordering. Nevertheless, our work identifies a distinct mechanism of schema formation and invariance despite significant overall network plasticity which is qualitatively different from schemata associated with accelerated transfer learning between closely related tasks reported in Recurrent Neural Networks trained on abstracted sensorimotor and serial learning problems (Miconi & Kay, 2025). While our work uses identical video sources to those given to patients with microelecrodes, the four-frame input representation into our network models lacks the temporal continuity of the movie clips which the patients are exposed to, as well as the temporal resolution. Furthermore, these unimodal visual inputs likely represent a subset of the multimodal stimuli relevant to human detection of boundaries in natural contexts, potentially able to be retained by large transformer-based networks (Tang et al., 2023). Nevertheless, these convolution-based visual feature inputs are more representative of stimuli from upstream neuronal pathways than the abstract input embeddings used in related studies (Goudar et al., 2023). We hypothesize that the addition of multimodal inputs in a boundary-detecting hierarchical neural network would serve to reinforce the mapping of salient inputs to the fixed internal schema, which may vary from the schematic circuit presented here, but would nonetheless stabilize as shown in Fig. 1.

In the ENN, schema fixation during early learning arises because each sub-concept neuron computes an effectively digital “AND" operation on its upstream differentia neurons which are continuously refined (Fig. 1). This allows the schema to be mapped to the equivalent of a digital circuit and also makes the ENN network more robust to fluctuations in synaptic strength. Considering the task-agnostic nature of the ENN learning framework, our results suggest that hierarchical learning is a general mechanism that allows invariant, repurposable digital-like cognitive structures to arise from distributed analog neural computation.

Methods

Dataset

The dataset consisted of 888 class balanced clips (298 NB, 293 SB, and 297 HB) selected analogously to and supplemented to those from the original experimental dataset (30 clips for NB and HB, 60 for SB) from Zheng et al. (2022). Clips were labeled as either: No Boundary (a continuous shot with no cuts or transitions), Soft Boundary (a continuous scene with a non-disruptive transition, e.g., cut to another perspective of the same scene), or Hard Boundary (two clips from unrelated scenes artificially spliced together). A 85-15% train-test split was used for all analyses unless stated otherwise.

Video embedding

While the authors expected that pre-trained video transformers might yield the richest representation, after assessing preliminary performance of representations from pre-trained models such as ViViT (Arnab, 2021), we found that the highest accuracy for this task came from using a representation from a VGG-16 trained on ImageNet (Simonyan & Zisserman, 2015).

VGG-16

A modified VGG-16 model pretrained on ImageNet from keras v3.5.0 was used to generate all embeddings for downstream classification. The input to the model is a 1080x1920x3 image, preprocessed by keras’s preprocess_input function, which converts the images from RGB to BGR, zero-centers each color channel with respect to the ImageNet dataset, without scaling. The 3 fully-connected layers at the top of the network used for ImageNet classification were replaced (include_top=False) by a GlobalAveragePooling2D layer, reducing the dimensionality of the embedding to the final 512 features used for the final representation for each frame used in the subsequent top model.

Frames

Four frames were sampled from the video clips: the first frame, the frame prior to the boundary (or at the midpoint for No Boundary clips), the frame subsequent to the boundary (or immediately after the midpoint for No Boundary clips), and the final frame in the clip. Frames were preprocessed using keras’s vgg16.preprocces_input function described above. Each frame was passed independently through the modified VGG-16, yielding a final embedding of dimensionality 4x512 (4 frames, each with 512 features).

Top models

All top models take the same flattened 4x512 embedding from the modified VGG and are tasked with learning to predicting the class of each input (i.e., NB, SB, or HB). Models discussed here are explicitly identical in architecture (i.e., number of hidden layers = 2, number of interneurons on each layer = 3, activation function = tanh) and perform the same classical feed-forward neural network operations during inference (y = tanh(w· x + b)). The final classification is determine by argmax(y), where y is the output of the final layer.

Essence neural networks

Previously, we introduced a class of artificial neural networks called Essence Neural Networks (ENNs), which are trained using a non-gradient-based schema based on neuro-cognitive models. Our implementation follows that outlined in the original paper (Blazek & Lin, 2021). Briefly, training samples are grouped by label, then unsupervised learning divides concepts into subconcepts. Subsequently, individual neurons on the first hidden layer (termed differentia neurons) are learned (i.e., weights and biases) by a linear support vector machine (SVM) that distinguishes between all pairs of subconcepts. These neurons are then used to train the second hidden layer neurons (termed subconcept neurons) through computation of a SVM on the outputs of the differentia neurons for a given subconcept and all outside concepts. Finally, the output of the subconcept neurons are used to compute an SVM between each concept and all training data belonging to other concepts. For all other hyperparameters (e.g., learning_rate_init, regularization strength), defaults were used.

Trial firing

Biological neurons firings were normalized with respect to their background firing (i.e., firing before the boundary). Subsequently, their were activated firings were calculated using a sigmoid of the form (y = a*tanh(b(x-c)) + d), where a is a scaling factor of 0.5, b is the steepness, inversely proportional to the standard deviation of the background firing, c is the xOffset = 1.5, and d is the yOffset = 0.5. Biological neurons were classified as “early” or “late” based on whether their peak firing occurred before or after 220 ms (relative to the boundary), as shown in Fig. 2c.

Artificial neuron firings were taken from the activated firings of hidden neurons from networks trained with a 50-50% split during a forward pass on randomly selected clips from the entire data set (30 trials for each class).

Circuit distilling

The top 50 features with the largest decrease in performance upon perturbation from an ENN trained with access to the entire dataset were used as the input for the distilled circuit. The weights and biases from the fully trained ENN are used to connect the inputs to the discrete neurons. Downstream firing is binarized to all-or-none (y >= 0, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y = {\textbf {w}} \cdot {\textbf {x}} + \textit{b}$$\end{document}), with the “pink” boundary-like neurons operating as an and gate for the “purple” event-like cell (i.e., the event-like cell’s firing is 0 unless both boundary-like cells fire).

Network robustness analysis

Noise injection analysis

Random noise was injected into the internal weights of the top model (specifically, all the weights between the first and second hidden layers). The noise was sampled from a Gaussian distribution and scaled to the magnitude of the weights to which it is added. Retention accuracy was calculated for each noised network by evaluating it on clips correctly classified by the unnoised network.

Feature sparsification analysis

Ranked features were selected and replaced with their average value across the dataset one at at time across all frames. Subsequently, the models evaluated on the sparse dataset until all 512 features had been removed.
