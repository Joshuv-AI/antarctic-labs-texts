MRMQuant: Automated MRM Data Quantitation for Large-Scale Targeted Metabolomics Analysis
Authors: Ke-Shiuan Lynn, Hsiang-Yu Tang, Chi-Jen Lo, Cheng-Hung Yang, Yi-Ting Tseng, Mei-Ling Cheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultiple reaction monitoring (MRM) is a powerful and popular technique used for metabolite quantification in targeted metabolomics. Accurate and consistent quantitation of metabolites from the MRM data is essential for subsequent analyses. Here, we developed an automated tool, MRMQuant, for targeted metabolomic quantitation using high-throughput liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry MRM data to provide users with an easy-to-use tool for accurate MRM data quantitation with minimal human intervention. This tool has many user-friendly functions and features to inspect and correct the quantitation results as required. MRMQuant possesses the following features to ensure accurate quantitation: (1) dynamic signal smoothing, (2) automatic deconvolution of coeluted peaks, (3) absolute quantitation via standard curves and/or internal standards, (4) visualized inspection and correction, (5) corrections applicable to multiple samples, and (6) batch-effect correction.

Quantitative analysis in analytical chemistry is a fundamental aspect of scientific research and industrial applications. Precise measurement of compounds in complex mixtures is essential to understanding biological processes, monitoring environmental contaminants, and ensuring pharmaceutical quality. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM), also known as selected reaction monitoring, is a mass-spectrometry-based approach for the quantitation of targeted analytes with remarkable sensitivity and specificity. It has emerged as a powerful and highly precise analytical technique for quantifying specific molecules in complex samples. Recently, this method has become immensely popular, owing to its versatility and applicability in various fields, such as proteomics, metabolomics, environmental science, clinical diagnostics, and drug development.

To date, many targeted mass spectrometry data processing/analysis software packages capable of performing MRM data quantitation, including MRMPROBS,1 MRMkit,2 OpenMS,3 and Skyline,4 as well as vendor-provided software packages, such as MassHunter (Agilent), MassLynx (Waters), MRMPilot (AB Sciex), and Xcalibur (Thermo Fisher Scientific), have been developed. Despite the availability of these tools, some users still face issues and are actively seeking better solutions. These issues are (1) no graphical user interface (GUI) is available for data input, function selection, parameter assignment, and result inspection and correction; (2) limited correction of the quantitation result, if not impossible; (3) no support for absolute quantitation using multiple internal standards (ISs) or standard curves (SCs); (4) no support for batch-effect correction; (5) no deconvolution for coeluted peaks; (6) no support for multiple compounds in an ion chromatogram; (7) no self-evaluation for individual quantitation result; and (8) not freely available.

To address these issues, we developed a tool, MRMQuant, exclusively for targeted metabolomic quantitation using high-throughput liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry MRM data in this study. This tool aims to provide accurate and consistent compound quantitation from MRM data with key features, such as dynamic signal smoothing, automatic deconvolution of coeluted peaks, and various normalization supports, including IS, SC, and batch-effect correction. However, automatic quantitation is seldom perfect, mainly due to noise, retention time (RT) drifts, contamination, and coelution in the data. Therefore, manual corrections are inevitable. Consequently, efficiency and effectiveness become important issues as the data size increases. To tackle these issues, MRMQuant is equipped with various functions to rapidly inspect the quantitation results, locate the problematic quantitation, and effectively correct it.

Experimental Section

System Overview

Figure 1 illustrates the workflow of MRMQuant. The detailed manipulations of each element are described in the following sections. Briefly, the quantitation procedure starts from selecting a suitable experiment type, followed by loading the MRM sample files and a method file with the information necessary about the targeted compounds into the system. After the quantitation parameters were set, peaks in each sample file are detected, and those corresponding to the targeted compounds are quantitated. Once the targeted compounds are quantitated in all sample files, batch-effect correction is conducted if quality control (QC) samples are included in the sample files. Subsequently, the quantitation results are displayed for inspection, and manual correction of the quantitated peaks can be performed if required. For each quantitated compound, the absolute concentration is converted from the computed abundance via its corresponding IS of known concentration and/or an SC generated by using a series of standard compounds of known concentrations. A similar quantitation procedure is performed on the SC MRM files (termed standard samples) whose computed abundances and known concentrations are used to construct the SCs. Finally, the quantitation results, including the calculated abundances, concentrations, quantitation status, RTs, and SC functions for all of the targeted compounds, are exported to a Microsoft Excel file. In addition, a binary MATLAB MAT-file is generated, which can be loaded into MRMQuant in the future to inspect and correct the existing quantitation results.

System flowchart. Gray boxes on the right side list the detailed functions in the peak quantitation and manual correction modules.

Experiment Types

MRMQuant supports three types of experiments: (1) quantifier ion selection, (2) relative quantitation, (3) absolute quantitation with ISs, and (4) absolute quantitation with SCs. Quantifier ion selection was designed to compute the abundance ratios among multiple product ions so that compounds with consistent ratios can be selected for the subsequent analysis. Relative quantitation means that each targeted compound is quantitated through trapezoidal integration of the most abundant peak area (referred to as abundance) within a specified RT range of an ion chromatogram. However, absolute quantitation requires further conversion from abundance to concentration (e.g., mol/mL). Conversion can be achieved via either ISs of known concentrations in each testing sample or SCs generated from standard samples. The former approach first computes the abundance ratio between a compound and its corresponding IS and then multiplies the concentration of the IS to obtain the concentration of that compound. The latter approach first performs a relative quantitation of standard samples and subsequently uses the computed compound abundances and known concentrations to construct a regression curve (i.e., SC) for each compound. Finally, the concentration of a compound in the testing sample is inferred from its computed abundance by using the SC of the compound.

Input Files

Currently, MRMQuant accepts mzML and txt files as inputs. Both formats can be converted from the raw data of various vendors using msconvert in ProteoWizard.5 Files can be loaded either in the file name or in the acquiring order (“startTimeStamp” in the MRM file, not the file date), which is recommended if batch-effect correction is to be performed. For the standard samples for SC generation, the files are suggested to be named sample name_concentration so that MRMQuant can automatically extract the concentrations from the file names. In addition to the MRM data, information for each targeted compound, such as name, precursor m/z value, product m/z value, expected RT, and corresponding RT tolerance, is also required. These data can be loaded into the system via a comma-separated value (csv) file or a tab- or space-separated (txt) file (referred to as a method file), each row of which lists the information on the targeted compound. For the absolute quantitation of SCs, both testing and standard samples can either share the same method file or use two separate method files. For absolute quantitation with ISs, the method file must contain the name and concentration of the corresponding IS for each targeted compound other than the above-mentioned entities. Notably, MRMQuant allows the quantitation of multiple compounds (isomers) in one ion chromatogram. In this case, compound names should be concatenated with “_” and RTs should be concatenated with “;” in the method file. Example samples and method files are available at https://github.com/kslynn128171/MRMQuant.

Quantitation Parameters

These parameters are crucial to three quantitation stages: peak finding, peak deconvolution, and batch-effect correction. Five parameters are involved in peak identification: background intensity, smoothing window size, signal-to-noise ratio (S/N), minimum peak width, and minimum peak distance. The background intensity specifies a fixed noise level (in counts), whereas the smoothing window size assigns a fixed window size for data smoothing in all ion chromatograms. For both parameters, if no suitable values are known, users can check the “auto” box and let the MRMQuant determine the values (see the Dynamic Signal Smoothing section for details). The S/N value sets the minimum ratio of signals to background intensity for MRMQuant to start the peak-finding process in an ion chromatogram (see the Peak Detection section for details). Finally, the minimum peak width and distance are the minimum values of the compound peak width and distance between two adjacent peaks, respectively. These two values are crucial for the peak-finding process to determine whether a compound peak is coeluted with other peaks, and to trigger the deconvolution process if a coelution is found. Users can determine whether to force the deconvolution of each detected compound peak by choosing auto deconvolution, always deconvolution, or no deconvolution (see the Peak Deconvolution section for details). In contrast to peak deconvolution, which focuses on peaks in a single MRM, batch-effect correction aims to reduce quantitation differences across all sample files caused by the batch effect. It is recommended when sample files from different batches are included in quantitation (see the Batch-Effect Correction section for details). Although MRMQuant provides default values for the parameters mentioned above, they were optimized for our data. The users are strongly advised to provide suitable parameters based on their experimental settings. For easy access for future experiments, MRMQuant allows users to save and load parameters from a file.

Dynamic Signal Smoothing

Raw signals in an ion chromatogram usually contain noise from various sources6 which may form false bumps and valleys that confuse peak detection. Data smoothing provides a solution for eliminating false signals while preserving important trends in the signals. Conventional approaches attempt to replace each signal with a weighted sum of its neighboring signals in a prespecified window. However, owing to the dynamic dwell time in the MRM data, the time intervals between signals can vary in an ion chromatogram. Consequently, conventional algorithms with fixed RT window are inappropriate for MRM data for various numbers of signals in the window. A simple solution is to divide each chromatogram into several sections with uniform intervals and then construct a regression function to determine the suitable smoothing window sizes therein. However, a compound peak can span multiple sections, causing its profile to be composed of inconsistent curves generated by different smoothing windows. To avoid such inconsistent curves, each section is expanded or reduced such that it always starts with and ends at a signal not higher than 1.2 times the background intensity, which is determined using the 30th percentile of the signal intensity in an ion chromatogram. A fixed value is used because of the short RT range during which the background drift is usually small. Nevertheless, user-defined background intensities are possible (see the Peak Correction section for details).

Peak Detection

Peaks are extracted from the smoothed signals based on user-specified RT tolerance, background intensity, S/N, and the minimum peak width and distance. In MRMQuant, the MATLAB built-in function “findpeaks” is used to find possible peak tip locations. If more than one peak is found in the RT tolerance, the highest peak is adopted for subsequent quantitation. A peak-tracing function was developed to trace the left- and right-hand bounds from the peak tip. The bounds are determined by the first local minimum encountered during tracing. If the local minimum is higher than 30% of the peak tip and a nearby bump is detected that is sufficiently high (>5% peak tip), wide (>user-specified peak width), and its tip is sufficiently far (>user-specified peak distance), then the compound peak is said to be coeluted. This function also reports the half-width at half-maximum (HWHM) and whether the peak is coeluted on each side. Each bound is restricted to no larger than 2.5 times the HWHM. Signals between the two bounds and above the background intensity are integrated using the trapezoidal rule to determine the abundance of the compound.

Peak Deconvolution

Once a peak is found to be coeluted, it will undergo a deconvolution process. For example, if a compound peak is left coeluted, the process calls the peak-tracing function to compute the bounds, HWHMs, and coelution conditions of the peak left to the current peak. The same process is performed for the peak on the right side if the current peak is right-co-eluted. MRMQuant can also handle situations in which a peak is coeluted on both sides. The union of the signals of the coeluted peaks is extracted, and the peak tips and their corresponding HWHMs are collected. Assuming that all peaks are Gaussian in shape, a peak deconvolution function was developed to solve for the height and sigma value of each individual Gaussian peak such that their combined signals have a minimum sum of differences with the extracted signal union. The computed Gaussian peak signals (after background removal) corresponding to the targeted compound are integrated into its abundance. For non-Gaussian peaks, users can select the “no deconvolution” option so that signals between two adjacent local minima of the peak tip are integrated for the abundance.

SC Generation

For absolute quantitation with SC, quantitation of the standard samples is first conducted. Based on the quantitation results, SCs for each of the targeted compounds are constructed and shown in an individual window for inspection and manual correction. Each SC of a compound is generated by regression between the computed abundances and their given concentrations via a user-specified constant, linear, or quadratic function. Regression weights of 1, 1/x, and 1/x2, where x denotes the sample concentration, can also be specified for each compound. Two special cases are examined before regression. For each targeted compound, if only two abundances can be obtained from all of the samples, a simple line connecting the two points is used as the SC. In addition, if the standard deviation of the calculated abundances across different samples is less than 0.05, indicating steady abundances, a constant of the averaged abundances is used for the SC. The regression process commences when neither of the above two cases is encountered. However, noise, coelution, and saturation in an ion chromatogram can lead to inaccurate abundance calculations, resulting in a false SC. To resolve this problem, MRMQuant allows the user to correct inaccurate abundances by manually specifying their peak area or even skipping some data points from the SC calculation, e.g., due to saturation. The SC will be recalculated once a correction is made.

Batch-Effect Correction

When the number of samples is large, the samples are typically divided into batches (analytical runs) to generate MRM data. As a result, systematic technical variations become inevitable because these samples are processed at different times, under different environmental conditions, and perhaps by different personnel and machines. For a fair comparison of samples from different batches, the computed compound abundances must be corrected to reduce the batch effects. QC samples, usually composed of all of the samples, are arranged in each batch to provide a baseline for the correction. In MRMQuant, we adopted the QC-based robust locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) signal correction (QC-RLSC) algorithm proposed by Dunn et al. and The Human Serum Metabolome Consortium.7 Briefly, for each compound, the following four steps are performed for batch-effect correction. Step 1. Trend Estimation: a LOESS curve is fitted to its abundance in the QC samples. Step 2. Interpolation: The corrected abundances of the compound in other samples are estimated by using cubic-spline interpolation/extrapolation of the LOESS curve (all samples are arranged in their acquiring order). Step 3. Correction Ratio Calculation: The ratios of abundance computed from the ion chromatograms and those from the LOESS curve are calculated. Step 4: Magnitude Restoration: After trend removal, the LOESS curve becomes a flat horizontal line with an intercept of 1; thus, the abundances are restored by multiplying the ratios by the abundance of the reference sample. In MRMQuant, a GUI is designed to specify the QC samples in the sample list, assign a reference (for magnitude restoration using a QC sample), and indicate the final sample in each batch. If no reference is assigned, the median of all of the QC samples is used as the reference. For absolute quantitation, conversion from abundance to concentration is performed after batch-effect correction using ISs or SCs.

Status Assignment

The quantitation result of each targeted compound is assigned a status, indicating its quality. The possible statuses are (i) successful quantitation, (ii) no qualified peak, (iii) multiple candidate peaks, (iv) saturated signals, (v) defect quantitation, and (vi) unconvertible concentrations. If the quantitation status shows “no qualified peak”, then no peak in the specified RT tolerance satisfies all of the requirements of sufficient S/N, peak width, and distance from other peaks; thus, the peak abundance is zero. The status of “multiple candidate peaks” indicates the presence of multiple peaks in the specified RT tolerance. In the peak-finding section, the highest peak is adopted if multiple peaks are found within the specified RT tolerance. However, the highest peak may not correspond to the targeted compound. Therefore, users are advised to examine the quantitation result if the status shows “multiple candidate peaks.” When the status is “saturated signals,” it indicates that at least 4 signals of the compound exhibit the same (truncated) value exceeding the detect limit of the instrument (the default value is 1.3 × 108, but it can be changed via the parameter file) and thus the calculated abundance is no longer accurate and should be ignored. The status of “defect quantitation” denotes that error occurs during the quantitation process, which may be due to unsuccessful peak tracing or deconvolution, and hence manual inspection/correction is recommended. For absolute quantitation, if the conversion of a compound concentration from its abundance is unsuccessful (for example, the abundance of the IS is 0), the status is noted as “concentration unconvertable.” If none of the above situations is encountered, the quantitation status of the compound is noted as “successful quantitation” meaning the result is trustworthy.

Peak Correction

The peak that represents the targeted compound in an ion chromatogram may not be correctly quantified, mostly due to noise, coelution, multiple candidates, and false background level calculations. In MRMQuant, we developed five functions to address peak correction problems: (i) selecting a region, (ii) selecting a peak, (iii) selecting a reference, (iv) adjusting the background, and (v) adjusting the quantitative status. For noisy ion chromatograms, the peak shapes may not be smoothed effectively, causing multiple bumps along the peak shape, which in turn degenerates the peak-tracing process and hence produces incorrect quantitation. The “select a region” function allows users to specify a rectangle region in the ion chromatogram by mouse. Signals that fell between the left and right bounds of the rectangle are integrated into the abundance of the compound. On the other hand, for the situation when multiple peaks exist in the specified RT tolerance, the “select a peak” function was designed to choose one of them by clicking near tip of the chosen peak. If coelution also occurs among the peaks, the deconvolution process is invoked to compute the peak abundance. Both corrections can be applied to multiple samples of the same compound to reduce the correction effort. To further reduce the effort, users can first inspect all quantitated compounds in a sample with abundant peaks (e.g., a QC), and then use the sample as the reference by choosing the “select a reference” function to correct the duration of all compounds in other samples. If multiple batches exist, then one such sample can be assigned to each batch. In addition to peak shape, if the background intensity is not suitably determined by the program, users can select the “adjust background” function to adjust background intensities by clicking on the suitable intensities in an ascending RT order. Such an adjustment can be applied to multiple ion chromatograms of a sample. Finally, if the quantitation of a compound is examined and no further correction is needed, users can use the “adjust quantitation status” function to change the status to “successful.” This function can be applied to multiple ion chromatograms and samples. Except for the status change, all of the above corrections trigger the recalculation of related compounds (e.g., those related to a corrected IS) and functions (e.g., SC generation or batch-effect correction), and the quantitation result is updated in the related plots.

Result Visualization

MRMQuant displays the quantitation results of m sample files, each with n compounds, in a m-by-n heatmap. Each cell in the heatmap represents a compound in a sample file, and its color reflects the intensity of its concentration (absolute quantitation) or abundance (relative quantitation). For quick inspection, the color of a cell is masked with 20% transparent gray if its quantitation status is “no qualified peak” or “multiple possible peaks” to indicate minor problems and is painted in white if the status is “saturated signals,” “defect quantitation,” or “concentration unconvertable” to denote problems that deserve more attention. Users can use the “heatmap option” button to change the appearance of the heatmap, including (1) show/hide log-scaled values, (2) show/hide grid lines, (3) show/hide a color bar, (4) show/hide masked colors, (5) change the color scheme, (6) show abundance or concentration, and (7) change the display ranges. Moreover, if the “Active Inspection Mode” is pressed, clicking on a heatmap cell will bring up an individual window to illustrate the quantitation details of the corresponding compound in the current ion chromatogram, including raw data, computed background level, the specified RT tolerance, peak area associated with the targeted compound, peak tip location, and the deconvoluted peak if it exists. The user can use the aforementioned peak correction functions to modify the peak area and apply modifications to the same compound in other samples. For easy comparison, users can also see plots of the compound in other samples as well as an overlapping plot of the compound peaks. This function allows users to determine whether a modification is required, especially when multiple peaks are presented in a specified RT tolerance.

Output File

The quantitation results are saved in a Microsoft Excel file in xlsx format. The results include peak abundance, quantitation status, and RT. If absolute quantitation is performed, then the peak concentration is included. In addition, if absolute quantitation is achieved by SC, the peak abundances of the standards and coefficients of the regression formula for SCs are also included. All of the above information is saved on a separate sheet in the Microsoft Excel file. For easy access to existing quantitation results, a binary MATLAB MAT-file with the same name as the Excel file will also be generated for future inspection and modification. Users can load the mat file into MRMQuant to inspect and correct the existing quantitation result and apply different parameter settings to recompute the entire quantitation result.

Results and Discussion

We adopted a data set extracted from a human aging study to demonstrate absolute quantitation using SC in MRMQuant. The other two types of data can be processed using similar steps, except for SC generation. This data set consisted of 47 testing samples (including five QCs) of 74 bile acids in 53 ion chromatograms (18 of which involved 2–3 isomers). In addition, a total of 14 standard samples of various concentrations (0.025–500 mol/mL) were used to generate SCs for absolute quantitation. Owing to the long compound names, pseudonames in the form of BAxxx (xxx denotes sequential numbers) were used in the demonstration. The actual compound names and detailed data preparation procedures are provided in the Supporting Information, whereas detailed experiment settings can be found in Huang et al.8

Before and after Batch-Effect Correction

We adopted the data set issued by MRMkit to demonstrate the effects before and after correction (https://github.com/MRMkit/MRMkit). In short, the data set consists of 3793 samples in 33 batches, each with 295 compounds. A total of 389 batch-pooled QCs (BQCs) roughly evenly arranged in the batches were used for LOESS curve generation during batch-effect correction. We also used the computed concentrations of sphingomyelin (SM) d18:1/C18:0 as in the MRMkit to illustrate the effects before and after the batch-effect correction. As shown in Figure 7, the slope of the regression line of the BQC samples (red dots) was 0.0004 with R2 = 0.3 before the correction but was very close to a flat line (slope ∼0.0) with R2 = 1.44 × 10–4 after the correction, indicating a successful correction. Although the processing time of around 5 h may seem longer than that (4 h) of the MRMKit, the actual computation time should be comparable, disregarding the overhead for visualization. MRMQuant also allows users to examine the batch-effect correction of other compounds, change the vertical axis to a logarithmic scale, and rerun the batch-effect correction with a different set of parameters using the drop-down menu and buttons at the top of the plots.

Comparison with Existing Tools

We compared several existing tools available to our group, including the free tools MRMPROBS, MRMkit, Skyline, and OpenMS, and the commercial tool, MassLynx. A functional comparison of these tools is presented in Table 1. To further compare the quantitation accuracy, a data set consisting of eight technical replicates of a QC sample extracted from a human aging study9 was constructed. Each replicate contained 184 phospholipids from human plasma, some of which were isomers that appeared on the same chromatogram. However, as some tools are incapable of handling multiple compounds in an ion chromatogram, only the first compound was retained in each chromatogram, resulting in 163 of the original 184 phospholipids in the method file. Detailed data preparation procedures are provided in the Supporting Information. We used the averaged standard deviation (STD) of the normalized abundance of the 163 compounds for comparison, as the abundance of each compound should be consistent across the replicates, leading to a small STD among them. Nevertheless, the STD of the calculated abundances provides only a measurement of consistency rather than the correctness of quantitation. Therefore, if a compound is consistently quantitated with the same incorrect peak across replicates, then it may still have a small STD in the quantitation result. We further employed the differences between the expected RTs and the computed ones to investigate the accuracy of the peak locations. Two indices, average and STD of the RT differences, were used in the investigation. For an accurate peak location, both indices should be as small as possible.

Table 2 presents the results of this comparison. Detailed parameter settings and quantitation results for each tool are provided at https://github.com/kslynn128171/MRMQuant. As shown in the table, although TargetLynx achieved the smallest average STD, it also had the highest number of unquantifiable compounds among the tools. The most noticeable issue with TargetLynx in this data set was its capability to deal with coeluted peaks, as shown in Figure 8a. By comparison with Figure 6, it can be seen that the ion chromatogram actually consisted of three isomers of PC 34:3 with RTs at 4.72, 4.84, and 4.92 min, respectively. However, TargetLynx attributed all of the coeluted peaks to PC 34:3, which led to an overestimation of the abundance of PC 34:3. In addition to the incorrect separation of coeluted peaks, TargetLynx may also overestimate the baseline when coelution occurs at the targeted compound. As shown in Figure 8b, TargetLynx estimated the baseline of the compound by connecting its nearby valleys, which underestimated the abundance due to high coelution in the right valley. In addition to TargetLynx, MRMkit and Skyline also exhibited low (<0.1) average STD and RT differences across replicates. However, they had problematic quantitation of the coeluted peaks, noisy peaks, and peaks with unevenly sampled signals (due to dynamic dwell time; see Figure 8c,d). More examples and comparison results are displayed in the Supporting Information. In the OpenMS package, the TOPPAS program was used for comparison. In addition to the problems with the previous two tools, OpenMS was unable to consistently quantify the same peak when multiple peaks existed in the neighborhood of the specified RT, leading to large variations in the computed peak abundances. Finally, even with our best efforts, we could not obtain quantitation results from MRMPROBS. Although we used the required mzML or abf files and a library file, error messages for not finding the specified transition prevented the program from running.

MRMQuant ranked second to fourth compared with other tools. Without peak deconvolution, MRMQuant achieved an average STD of 0.0832. Using the automatic deconvolution option (default settings), MRMQuant improved the value to 0.0828. Compared with the case with no deconvolution, MRMQuant improved the quantitation by fitting Gaussian-shaped peaks to decompose the coeluted ones instead of chopping off the signals at nearby valleys, as observed in the no deconvolution method. Moreover, if the user can inspect all quantitations in one sample and use it as the reference to correct the quantitations in the other samples, the average STD can be further decreased to 0.0813, indicating a consistent quantitation across samples.
