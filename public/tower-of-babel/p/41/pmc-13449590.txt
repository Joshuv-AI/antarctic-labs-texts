Posterior superior alveolar artery in severely atrophic maxillae prior to lateral sinus augmentation: a retrospective cross-sectional CBCT imaging study
Authors: Halil Ibrahim Aygun, Duygu Taskin, Emin Murat Canger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVascular injury to the posterior superior alveolar artery (PSAA) during lateral sinus floor augmentation is a recognized surgical complication, yet data on PSAA anatomy in severely atrophic edentulous maxillae remain scarce. This study aimed to evaluate the prevalence, location patterns, and morphometric characteristics of the PSAA in this specific population using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT).MethodsPreoperative CBCT scans of 121 patients (242 sinuses) with severely atrophic posterior maxillae (residual ridge height ≤ 5 mm) were retrospectively analyzed. PSAA location was classified as intraosseous (Type I), submembranous (Type II), extraosseous (Type III), or transosseous (Type IV). PSAA diameter and vertical distances to the sinus floor (PSAA–SF) and alveolar crest (PSAA–ALV) were measured. Generalized estimating equations accounted for within-patient correlation from bilateral evaluations.ResultsThe PSAA was identified in 99.17% of sinuses, predominantly Type II (56.67%), followed by Type I (37.50%), Type IV (3.33%), and Type III (2.50%). Mean PSAA diameter was 1.02 ± 0.56 mm, mean PSAA–SF distance 8.89 ± 4.61 mm, and mean residual ridge height 3.55 ± 1.37 mm. Type II location was significantly associated with Schneiderian membrane thickening (p = 0.018).ConclusionsIn severely atrophic edentulous maxillae planned for lateral sinus floor augmentation, the PSAA predominantly follows a submembranous course alongside a clinically critical transosseous variant. Preoperative CBCT assessment of the PSAA is essential to inform individualized surgical planning and minimize hemorrhagic risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08695-8.Keywords: Posterior superior alveolar artery, Cone-beam computed tomography, Maxillary sinus, Sinus floor augmentation, Lateral window technique, Atrophic edentulous maxilla

Background

Endosseous implants are widely used for the rehabilitation of edentulism and offer high survival rates and predictable functional and esthetic outcomes [1]. After tooth loss in the posterior maxilla, vertical bone height is frequently reduced due to physiologic alveolar resorption and maxillary sinus pneumatization [2, 3]. Sinus floor augmentation—first described by Boyne and James [4] and later popularized by Tatum [5]—has become a predictable approach to regain sufficient bone height for implant placement [6, 7]. In severely atrophic posterior maxillae with a residual ridge height of ≤ 5 mm, sinus floor augmentation via lateral approach is commonly preferred [8].

The blood supply of the maxillary sinus and Schneiderian membrane is mainly provided by two branches of the maxillary artery: the posterior superior alveolar artery (PSAA) and the infraorbital artery. The PSAA typically courses along the lateral wall of the maxillary sinus in close proximity to the bone and periosteum [9, 10]. Complications involving the PSAA during sinus floor augmentation via lateral approach can cause intraoperative bleeding that obscures the surgical field and increases the risk of iatrogenic membrane perforation [9, 11]. Postoperatively, hematoma formation and compromised local perfusion may affect graft stability and healing [9, 11]. In addition, sinus septa may complicate lateral window preparation and membrane elevation, increasing the technical difficulty and the risk of membrane perforation [5, 12]. Therefore, careful preoperative assessment of maxillary sinus anatomy and PSAA localization using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) is recommended to reduce procedural complications [13, 14].

CBCT provides three-dimensional imaging with high spatial resolution for maxillofacial hard tissues at a substantially lower radiation dose than conventional computed tomography; however, soft tissue detail is limited [15]. The aim of this retrospective cross-sectional study was to characterize PSAA prevalence, location pattern, diameter, and clinically relevant distances (to the sinus floor and alveolar crest) on CBCT in atrophic edentulous posterior maxillae planned for sinus floor augmentation via lateral approach, and to explore associations with age, sex, sinus septa, and Schneiderian membrane thickening.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This retrospective cross-sectional study involved a comprehensive review of the institutional database at the Department of Periodontology of a university dental hospital, covering a 9-year period from 2011 to 2020. To minimize selection bias, a consecutive screening approach was used. The full digital archive of pre-operative CBCT scans of patients who presented to the clinic during this timeframe was initially evaluated for eligibility.

Patients who presented for implant rehabilitation of posterior maxillary edentulism were initially screened by panoramic radiography. When panoramic imaging suggested vertical ridge resorption, preoperative CBCT scans were acquired to assess sinus anatomy and determine the appropriate augmentation approach. The residual ridge height (RRH) was subsequently measured on CBCT cross-sectional images at the planned implant sites, and only patients with a CBCT-verified RRH of ≤ 5 mm — corresponding to severely atrophic posterior maxillae indicated for sinus floor augmentation via the lateral approach [8] — were included in the study.

After screening, 121 patients (comprising 62 females and 59 males, with a mean age of 49.72 ± 8.24 years) met all criteria and were enrolled in the study. Since bilateral sinuses were evaluated for each patient, a total of 242 maxillary sinuses were included for the final radiological and statistical analysis.

Given the retrospective and descriptive nature of the study, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not conducted. However, a post-hoc precision analysis was performed to justify the sample size. For an anatomical variant with an expected maximum-variance prevalence of approximately 50% (similar to the observed Type II distribution), a sample size of 240 sinuses provides a theoretical margin of error of ± 6.3% at a 95% confidence level. Importantly, accounting for the design effect due to within-patient correlation (bilateral sinuses) observed in this dataset, the effective sample size is approximately 219 independent observations, which yields a cluster-adjusted margin of error of ± 6.6%. This precision is considered adequate for a descriptive anatomical imaging study.

CBCT acquisition and image evaluation

All CBCT scans (acquired between 2011 and 2020) were obtained using a NewTom 5G unit (QR, Verona, Italy) with imaging parameters of 110 kV and 3–5 mA, a native isotropic voxel size of 0.25 mm, a field of view of 18 × 16 cm, and an exposure time of 5.4 s. Images were evaluated using NNT Viewer software (version 9.1). Only high-quality images were included. Image orientation was standardized by aligning the Frankfurt plane parallel to the true horizontal plane in the sagittal view and the midsagittal plane parallel to the true vertical plane in the coronal and axial views. Cross-sectional images were systematically reviewed at 1-mm intervals along the antero-posterior axis of the maxillary sinus; however, the native voxel resolution of 0.25 mm provided sufficient spatial resolution for identification and measurement of vascular structures within or adjacent to the lateral sinus wall.

Measurements

For each posterior maxillary edentulous region, when only the maxillary first molar was missing, the CBCT cross-sectional slice corresponding to the center of a virtually planned implant was selected. When both the first and second molars were missing, the slice corresponding to the midpoint between the two virtually planned implant positions was selected. Measurements were performed using the software’s digital ruler.

All parameters were additionally evaluated with respect to sex, age group (30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69 years), side (right/left), presence of septa, and Schneiderian membrane thickening.

Reliability

All measurements were performed by one calibrated examiner and repeated on all scans after a 1-month interval to assess intraexaminer reliability. Intraexaminer agreement was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient for continuous variables and Cohen’s kappa for categorical variables.

Handling of correlated data

Analyses were performed at the sinus level (right and left sinuses analyzed separately). Because each patient contributed bilateral observations (242 sinuses in 121 patients), observations were clustered within patients to account for within-patient correlation.

Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed using histograms, Q–Q plots, and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Inferential analyses were performed using generalized estimating equations (GEE) with robust (sandwich) standard errors, specifying patient identifier as the clustering variable. An independence working correlation structure was specified for GEE models. Correlations were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation. Data are presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range) for continuous variables, as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using TURCOSA (Turcosa Analytics Ltd. Co., Turkey) and R (version 4.0.1).

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Erciyes University, Kayseri, Turkey (No. 2021/43) and was conducted in accordance with the recognized standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective nature of the study evaluating anonymized existing radiographic records, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the institutional ethics committee. This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

Results

A total of 121 patients (242 maxillary sinuses) were evaluated (mean age 49.72 ± 8.24 years; 62 females and 59 males). Intraexaminer reliability was excellent (ICC ≈ 0.99).

Discussion

This retrospective cross-sectional CBCT study characterized the posterior superior alveolar artery (PSAA) in severely atrophic edentulous posterior maxillae (residual ridge height ≤ 5 mm) planned for sinus floor augmentation via lateral approach. The key findings were: (i) PSAA was identified in almost all sinuses, (ii) type II submembranous location predominated over type I intraosseous location, while types III and IV were uncommon, (iii) most arteries had a diameter < 1 mm, and (iv) clinically relevant distances (PSAA–SF and PSAA–ALV) were within ranges reported previously, with limited associations to sex, age, or side, whereas PSAA location was associated with Schneiderian membrane thickening.

PSAA detection rates reported in CT/CBCT studies vary considerably, likely reflecting differences in imaging modality, acquisition parameters (e.g., voxel size/cross-section interval), device settings, and population characteristics [9, 10, 14, 19–21]. The near-universal detectability observed in the present study is in line with evidence that CBCT can provide high detection rates for PSAA-related vascular canals in the lateral sinus wall [15, 19, 22]. Moreover, restricting the sample to edentulous and severely atrophic posterior maxillae reduced heterogeneity related to dentition status and residual ridge morphology, which may influence both detectability and observed distributions [23, 24].

Direct anatomical studies provide essential context for interpreting CBCT-based findings. Cadaveric dissections have consistently demonstrated that the PSAA–infraorbital artery anastomosis is present in virtually all lateral sinus walls [10, 25]. Hur et al. [26] examined 42 Korean cadaver hemifaces and classified the intraosseous PSAA course into straight (78.1%) and U-shaped (21.9%) patterns, reporting that the artery ran closest to the sinus floor in the first premolar region. German et al. [27] confirmed through combined macroscopic dissection and tomographic evaluation that the PSAA bony canal most commonly follows a tunnel-like format in 65% of dry skulls and 85% of dissected specimens. These direct anatomical observations corroborate the high CBCT detection rate reported in the present study (99.17%) and underscore that radiographic identification, while highly sensitive, may still underestimate the full vascular anatomy — particularly for vessels coursing entirely within the Schneiderian membrane without an associated bony canal.

The predominance of a submembranous PSAA course (type II) in severely atrophic edentulous posterior maxillae is clinically relevant. Prior imaging studies have typically used a type I–III classification (intraosseous / below the membrane / superficial) [16–18] and location distributions differ across cohorts. In mixed dentate/edentulous samples, intraosseous canals may be more frequent [18, 20] whereas cohorts focusing on edentulous regions have often shown higher proportions of a submembranous course [14, 23, 24, 28, 29]. This observation is clinically important because a submembranous vessel course may increase the likelihood of encountering vascular structures not only during the lateral wall osteotomy, but also during membrane elevation in the plane between the Schneiderian membrane and the lateral wall [9, 11, 19].

The present study explicitly identifies and reports the transosseous pattern (type IV) as a distinct clinical entity. The widely accepted classification originally proposed by Elian et al. [17] traditionally categorizes the artery into three main topographical relationships: intraosseous, submembranous, and extraosseous. While clinically foundational, this traditional three-tier system, and subsequent studies relying on it, frequently group a traversing course within the broader “intraosseous” category without distinguishing the full-thickness bony trajectory [18]. In our specific cohort of atrophic maxillae, this transosseous variant represents an unavoidable surgical hazard. Unlike a purely intraosseous vessel—which might be bypassed if the osteotomy is shallow—a type IV artery directly intersects the path of the rotary instrument as it penetrates from the outer cortex to the inner aspect of the lateral wall. Preoperative CBCT identification of this pattern alerts the surgeon that the vessel will likely be encountered across the full thickness of the lateral wall during window preparation. The resulting bleeding risk necessitates surgical modifications such as altering the window design [9, 19], or favoring piezoelectric surgery to minimize vascular trauma [19].

In cases where the PSAA follows an intraosseous course within the lateral wall, the transcrestal (osteotome) sinus floor elevation technique may theoretically offer a reduced risk of direct vascular injury, as this approach does not involve manipulation of the lateral sinus wall. However, the transcrestal approach is a blind technique with limited ability to detect or manage membrane perforations, and is generally indicated for moderate residual ridge heights (typically > 5 mm). In the present study population — where all patients had a RRH of ≤ 5 mm — the lateral approach remains the indicated augmentation technique, making preoperative PSAA characterization particularly critical for safe window design.

Most PSAA diameters were < 1 mm, with fewer in the 1–2 mm range and rare cases > 2 mm. This pattern aligns with several CBCT-based reports, though diameter distributions differ across populations and measurement methods [16, 18, 20, 30–32]. Clinically, larger diameters have been associated with greater risk of bleeding that can obscure visibility and increase the risk of secondary complications (e.g., membrane perforation, graft contamination/displacement) [9, 19]. Therefore, diameter assessment should be interpreted alongside location pattern and planned window position rather than in isolation [9, 16, 19].

Reported values for PSAA–SF and PSAA–ALV vary because measurement protocols differ (e.g., vessel center vs. inferior border; standardized planes vs. specific tooth regions) and because patient characteristics differ (dentate vs. edentulous, atrophic vs. non-atrophic) [18, 23, 24, 33–36]. The distances observed here are broadly comparable to prior CBCT-based investigations [18, 23, 24, 33]. In severely atrophic edentulous cohorts, a shorter PSAA–ALV distance is expected because the alveolar crest becomes more apical after resorption [24, 33]. From a clinical standpoint, PSAA–ALV provides a practical cue for window height planning: a smaller crest-to-artery distance reduces the safety margin, particularly when combined with a larger vessel diameter [9, 19].

The septa prevalence observed in this severely atrophic edentulous cohort appears lower than pooled estimates reported in systematic reviews including broader populations; septa prevalence is known to vary with dentition status and sinus morphology [12, 37]. Septa have also been associated with increased technical difficulty during lateral window preparation and membrane elevation, with a higher risk of Schneiderian membrane perforation, supporting the value of preoperative identification and possible modifications in window design [5, 11, 12, 38].

Schneiderian membrane thickening was common in the present dataset. Membrane thickening has been discussed as clinically relevant in sinus augmentation planning, but its interpretation requires caution because CBCT has limited soft-tissue specificity and thickening can represent transient inflammatory changes rather than clinically significant disease [15, 39]. In addition, definitions of “pathologic” thickening are not uniform across studies, as different radiographic thresholds have been used [39]. The observed association between Type II (submembranous) PSAA location and Schneiderian membrane thickening may reflect a localized vascular–inflammatory interaction. A submembranous arterial course could predispose the overlying membrane to chronic vascular congestion and secondary mucosal thickening. Alternatively, pre-existing membrane inflammation or thickening may alter the local tissue environment, influencing the radiographic appearance of the vascular canal. However, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and prospective studies are needed to clarify the directionality of this association. Clinically, membrane thickening should be interpreted alongside symptoms, history, and radiologic pattern; when thickening is substantial or associated with sinus disease, postoperative sinusitis risk and ostium obstruction concerns have been discussed in the literature [39, 40].

Because each patient contributed two sinuses, analyses were conducted at the sinus level while accounting for within-patient correlation [41]. This is important because treating bilateral observations as independent can underestimate standard errors and inflate statistical significance [41]. The clustered analytic approach therefore strengthens the validity of the associations reported.

This study is limited by its retrospective design and single-setting sample, which may affect generalizability. Measurements—although highly reliable—depend on software-based slice selection and CBCT resolution [15]. Rare location patterns (types III and IV) were infrequent, limiting robust multivariable modeling for the full four-category classification; accordingly, these variants were described rather than over-modeled. Despite these limitations, the findings indicate that in severely atrophic edentulous posterior maxillae, submembranous PSAA courses are common and should be anticipated. Preoperative CBCT assessment of PSAA diameter, location pattern, and crest-to-artery distance can inform lateral window design and membrane elevation strategy to reduce vascular complications during sinus floor augmentation via lateral approach [9, 19].

Additionally, although CBCT images were acquired with a native voxel size of 0.25 mm, cross-sectional evaluation was performed at 1-mm intervals. This evaluation protocol may have resulted in some arterial segments being missed between the evaluated slices. However, given the continuous course of the PSAA along the lateral wall, this is unlikely to significantly affect prevalence estimates. Regarding diameter measurements, the native voxel resolution of 0.25 mm is below the mean PSAA diameter reported in this study (1.02 mm), supporting the reliability of the measurements, although partial volume averaging may still influence the accuracy of diameter assessment for the smallest vessels.

Furthermore, CBCT cannot reliably visualize vessels that course entirely within the soft tissue of the Schneiderian membrane without any associated bony canal or groove. The reported PSAA positions and diameters in this study are inferred from the radiographic appearance of vascular canals within or adjacent to the lateral bony wall and may not represent the full anatomical spectrum. Consequently, the true prevalence of submembranous PSAA courses may be higher than radiographically detected.

From a clinical standpoint, the predominance of a submembranous PSAA course in this specific population means that the artery will frequently lie within the dissection plane during membrane elevation, not only within the osteotomy path. The additional identification of a transosseous variant (Type IV) reinforces the need for preoperative CBCT-guided surgical planning, including consideration of modified window positioning or piezoelectric instrumentation to reduce hemorrhagic complications.

Conclusions

In severely atrophic edentulous posterior maxillae (residual ridge height ≤ 5 mm) planned for lateral sinus floor augmentation, the PSAA is almost always identifiable on CBCT, predominantly presenting a submembranous course alongside a clinically critical transosseous (Type IV) variant. Because vascular complications during lateral window preparation depend directly on these specific location patterns, preoperative CBCT assessment of the PSAA is mandatory to inform individualized surgical planning and minimize hemorrhagic risks in this high-risk anatomy.
