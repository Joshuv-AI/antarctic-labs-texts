Penalized Model‐Based Unsupervised Phenomapping Unravels Distinctive HFrEF Phenotypes With Improved Outcomes Discrimination From Sacubitril/Valsartan Treatment Independent of MAGGIC Score
Authors: Kuo‐Tzu Sung, Hung‐Yu Chang, Nai‐Wei Hsu, Wen‐Hung Huang, Yueh‐Hung Lin, Chun‐Ho Yun, Chih‐Chung Hsiao, Chien‐Yi Hsu, Shin‐Yi Tsai, Ying‐Ju Chen, Cheng‐Ting Tsai, Cheng‐Huang Su, Ta‐Chuan Hung, Charles Jia‐Yin Hou, Hung‐I Yeh, Chung‐Lieh Hung
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor (LCZ696) has emerged as a promising pharmacological intervention against renin–angiotensin system inhibitor in reduced ejection fraction heart failure (HFrEF). Whether the therapeutic benefits may vary among heterogeneous HFrEF subgroups remains unknown.Methods and ResultsThis study comprised a pooled 2‐center analysis including 1103 patients with symptomatic HFrEF with LCZ696 use and another 1103 independent HFrEF control cohort (with renin–angiotensin system inhibitor use) matched for age, sex, left ventricular ejection fraction, and comorbidity conditions. Three main distinct phenogroup clusterings were identified from unsupervised machine learning using 29 clinical variables: phenogroup 1 (youngest, relatively lower diabetes prevalence, highest glomerular filtration rate with largest left ventricular size and left ventricular wall stress); phenogroup 2 (oldest, lean, highest diabetes and vascular diseases prevalence, lowest highest glomerular filtration rate with smallest left ventricular size and mass), and phenogroup 3 (lowest clinical comorbidity with largest left ventricular mass and highest hypertrophy prevalence). During the median 1.74‐year follow‐up, phenogroup assignment provided improved prognostic discrimination beyond Meta‐Analysis Global Group in Chronic Heart Failure risk score risk score (all net reclassification index P<0.05) with overall good calibrations. While phenogroup 1 showed overall best clinical outcomes, phenogroup 2 demonstrated highest cardiovascular death and worst renal end point, with phenogroup 3 having the highest all‐cause death rate and HF hospitalization among groups, respectively. These findings were broadly consistent when compared with the renin–angiotensin system inhibitor control as reference group.ConclusionsPhenomapping provided novel insights on unique characteristics and cardiac features among patients with HFrEF with sacubitril/valsartan treatment. These findings further showed potentiality in identifying potential sacubitril/valsartan responders and nonresponders with improved outcome discrimination among patients with HFrEF beyond clinical scoring.Keywords: angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor (LCZ696), guideline‐directed medical therapy, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), MAGGIC score, phenomappingSubject Categories: ACE/Angiotension Receptors/Renin Angiotensin System, Cardiorenal Syndrome, Echocardiography, Treatment

Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) represents a complex clinical scenario with considerable global economic burden with high death rate and multimorbidity. 1 HFrEF is typically featured by a central pathophysiology of diminished cardiac output from diminished left ventricular ejection fraction, thereby eliciting sympathetic and neurohormonal activation. LCZ696 (sacubitril/valsartan; angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor [ARNi]), the new first‐in‐class of dual ARNi, provides a novel therapeutic mechanism of action targeting both renin–angiotensin system and endopeptidase inhibition, emerges as a powerful pharmacological intervention for individuals with HFrEF. 2 In the Prospective Comparison of ARNI With ACEI to Determine Impact on Global Mortality and Morbidity in Heart Failure (PARADIGM) study, LCZ696 outperformed enalapril by demonstrating a 20% reduction in death from cardiovascular causes or hospitalization for heart failure (HF) during the median 27 months of follow‐up. 3

In the real‐world setting, HFrEF encompasses a relative broad spectrum of comorbid demographic features, underlying cause, and cardiac manifestations. For example, prevalent diabetes in HF has shown racial differences and is likely to be higher in certain Asian regions where the risk of diabetic kidney dysfunction may be aggravated. 4 , 5 , 6 On the other hand, LCZ696 treatment efficacy could be geometry dependent, and yet there remain limited data on LCZ696 treatment efficacy across diverse left ventricular (LV) phenotypes. 7 Recently, data‐driven unsupervised machine learning has provided clinical insights into distinct HF phenotypes with prognostic differences. 8 Identification of heterogeneous LCZ696 treatment responses in HFrEF subgroups may aid in tailored pharmacological strategies to optimize classes in clinical scenarios where the use of ARNi can be a concern (eg, borderline hypotension or renal insufficiency). 9 Whether the use of unsupervised clustering use may improve phenotypic classification and more accurately stratify patients with HFrEF with more favorable response or higher risk of adverse events (eg, renal failure) to LCZ696 treatment in a real‐world setting remains unknown.

To this end, we aimed to investigate the diverse end points of both cardiovascular and renal outcomes of patients with HFrEF receiving LCZ696.

Methods

Because of the sensitive nature of the data collected for this study, requests to access the data set from qualified researchers trained in human subject confidentiality protocols may be sent to “MacKay Memorial Hospital” Institutional Data Access/Ethics Committee for researchers (Institutional Review Board contact information: MacKay Memorial Hospital. Address: No. 92, Sec. 2, Zhongshan N. Rd., Taipei City 10 449, Taiwan. TEL: 02–25 433 535#3486–3488, E‐mail: mmhirb82@gmail.com).

Between September 2015 and December 2021, we retrospectively identified 1103 patients with symptomatic HFrEF (defined as an LV ejection fraction <40% using the biplane Simpson's method; average age, 63.6±13.9 years, 75.6% men) with clinical indications for LCZ696 (sacubitril/valsartan) treatment from pooled 2‐center outpatient populations (MacKay Memorial Hospital, Taipei and Tamsui branches). We used unsupervised cluster‐based analysis to regroup patients with HFrEF receiving LCZ696. Selected baseline demographic variables or items for cluster‐based phenotyping and the detailed clustering approach are displayed in Table S1. Information about baseline characteristics, comorbid conditions, NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) level, and biochemical information were all extracted from electronic medical chart review. The number and percentage of ongoing use or prior exposure to RASi (angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin II receptor blockers) and beta blockers were described according to current guidelines and extracted from medical records. 10 During the same study observation period, another RASi‐treated (angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitor or angiotensin II receptor blocker) independent HFrEF population (n=1103) yet without LCZ696 exposure matched for baseline demographics (age, sex, body mass index, systolic blood pressure, heart rate, active smoking), left ventricular ejection fraction, and key comorbid conditions (including hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, atrial fibrillation, stroke, and estimated glomerular filtration rate) served as control. All study patients were free from regular renal replacement therapy at the baseline.

To explore the clinical outcomes, the study index date was set as the date of LCZ696 initiation, with longitudinal follow‐up performed until December 2021 (unless end points or death occurred). Ethical approval was obtained from the local institutional review committee (IRB 21MMHIS052e, MacKay Memorial Hospital, Taipei). Informed consent was waived due to retrospective study design. Our current study conforms to the ethical guidelines as laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cardiac Structure, Diastolic Function, and LV Wall Stress Assessment

All echocardiographic images were obtained according to guidelines recommended protocol. 11 , 12 LV wall thickness (interventricular thickness [IVST] and posterior wall thickness [PWT] as septal and posterior wall thickness [end‐diastolic]), dimensions (left ventricular internal diameter end‐diastole [LVIDd] and LVIDs as end‐diastolic and end‐systolic dimension), and LV mass index were quantified via the linear method, with LV end‐diastolic and end‐systolic volume, related indexed measures and LV ejection fraction measured by the biplane Simpson method. LV hypertrophy was determined as LV mass index >115 and 95 g/m2 for men and women, respectively, with increased wall thickness defined as LV wall (either IVST or PWT) >12 mm. Early (E) and late (A) mitral inflow velocities, deceleration time, and isovolumic relaxation time were all obtained. Tissue Doppler determined peak early diastolic velocity (as e′) on lateral mitral annulus and estimated LV filling using mitral inflow E divided by TDI‐e′ (as E/e′) were also collected, with LV wall stress (as LV circumferential end‐systolic stress [cESS]) and mid‐wall shortening against the operating wall stress [mFS]) (by De Simone et al) calculated using a catheterization‐validated formula, 13 which enabled assessment of these measures using a moderately thick‐walled ellipse highly applicable in dilated cardiomyopathy and also in hypertrophic LV geometry. 14 The formulas for mFS and cESS are listed below:

Statistical Analysis

Normally distributed continuous data were expressed as means±SD, nonnormally distributed continuous variables as medians±interquartile range, and categorical variables as percentage. Comparative analysis for parametric continuous variables was performed using 1‐way analysis of variance or an independent t test. Nonparametric continuous variables were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis or Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using either Fisher's exact test or the χ2 test with a Yates correction, as appropriate. Both uni‐ and multivariate Cox proportional hazard regression models (time‐to‐event) were used to determine the event‐free survival among phenogroups. For survival comparisons among phenogroups with HFrEF, age, sex, and Meta‐Analysis Global Group in Chronic Heart Failure risk score (MAGGIC HF) 15 served as adjustment covariates. The log‐rank test was used for time‐to‐event comparisons, and the Kaplan–Meier method was used to illustrate the survival curves for all groups. Category free net reclassification index was used to examine whether model‐based phenotyping provides incremental prognostic values discrimination beyond the MAGGIC score (as continuous variable) for diverse clinical end points. 16

All analyses were performed using STATA 14.0 software (StataCorp, College Station, TX). All P values were 2‐tailed, with P<0.05 considered statistically significant.

Subgroup Comparisons and Phenotypic Variable Selection for Phenomapping and Hierarchical Clustering

Detailed methods on variables selection, clustering approach, and the results are described in Data S1. In brief, a total of 29 continuous variables from baseline clinical characters and echocardiographic indices within 3 months of the study index date (defined as the initiation of LCZ696 use) were eligible for final model‐based clustering and phenomapping analyses (Table S1). Multiple imputation was used for missing variables. Penalized model complexity from model‐based clustering using Bayesian information criterion (mclust package in R), which determines the final recommended number of phenogroups from our study participants, is shown in Figure S1. Hierarchical clustering procedures were used for visual representation of the resulting heatmap from the selected 29 variables considered as final candidates for analysis (Figure S2).

Outcome Determination

A priori sample size calculation was performed using Power and Sample Size software version 08 (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT). During a median of 1‐ to 2‐year follow‐up duration, we assumed that 500 subjects in any given 2 phenogroups (500 subjects in each) representing best and worst clinical outcomes would provide 90% power (alpha, 5%) for a meaningful detectable survival difference (10%–15%) after LCZ696 treatment, an extent reflecting survival benefit of overall LCZ696 efficacy compared with its comparator with enalapril use from trials. 17 Prespecified primary outcome measures were defined as all‐cause death, cardiovascular death, HF hospitalization, urgent HF visit and composite death, and HF hospitalization starting from the study index date (starting from the initiation of LCZ696 treatment). We further explored renal end point with LCZ696 treatment as end‐stage renal disease requiring replacement therapy in the present study (for at least 3 continuous months).

Results

Among 1103 patients with HFrEF (mean age, 63.6±13.9 years) undergoing LCZ696 treatment, patients classified into phenogroup 1 were youngest; more likely to be men; and had higher body mass, lower New York Heart Association functional class, lower NT‐proBNP, best estimated glomerular filtration rate, and lowest MAGGIC score when compared with other phenogroups (Table 1). Instead, patients in phenogroup 2 were featured by highest percentage of women and smallest body size, had the worst New York Heart Association functional class, and had highest prevalent diabetes and highest vascular diseases history along with worst renal profile when compared with other phenogroups (Figure 1A). Patients in phenogroup 2 also had the highest prevalence of less controlled diabetes. Conversely, phenogroup 3 in general had the lowest prevalence of comorbid conditions (including history of vascular diseases) and the highest glutamic pyruvic transaminase level and blood urea nitrogen/creatinine ratio (all P<0.05).

During a median follow‐up of 1.74 years (25th–75th interquartile range, 0.79–3.29 years), patients in phenogroups 2 and 3 demonstrated higher incidences of all‐cause death, urgent visits due to HF, and the composite HF readmission/death rates compared with phenogroup 1 (as referent group) in fully adjusted models. Further, phenogroups 2 and 3 showed the worst cardiovascular survival and HF hospitalization among groups, respectively (Table S2; Figure 3). Phenogroup assignment provided improved prognostic discrimination beyond MAGGIC score for end points via net reclassification improvement, with overall good calibrations observed for all outcomes (Table S3) (all P<0.05). As outcome comparisons between the ARNi group and matched RASi controls showed comparable baseline clinical characteristics except for medications background (Table S4), we further adjusted baseline treatment disparity. Overall, LCZ696 led to a 27% reduction on all‐cause death (hazard ratio [HR], 0.73 [95% CI, 0.58–0.93]; P=0.01; ARNi versus RASi users). Compared with matched RASi control, phenogroup 1 alone consistently showed better clinical outcomes on all aspects and overall a trend toward better urgent HF visits among all LCZ696 groups (Figure 4; Table S5). Notably, phenogroup 2 demonstrated a significantly higher renal event when compared with RASi control (adjusted HR, 2.96 [95% CI, 1.80–4.87]; P<0.001). By comparing the treatment efficacy of the ARNi versus RASi cohort in each phenogroup, the beneficial effects of ARNi compared with RASi use was also more prominent in phenogroup 1, with phenogroup 3 demonstrating a worse renal outcome (Table 3).

Discussion

By using phenogroups from unsupervised learning from a real‐world data set, our work is the first to describe that LCZ696 may be particularly useful in patients with HFrEF manifesting a larger LV size and higher wall stress cardiac phenotype, with less baseline prevalent diabetes or renal insufficiency. Notably, we identified 2 phenotype‐specific HFrEF groups with less favorable clinical outcomes despite active LCZ696 treatment. These phenogroups exhibited either highly prevalent diabetes and renal insufficiency along with remarkably smaller heart size or as another cardiac phenotype presenting extremely higher LV mass and ventricular hypertrophy. Our phenogroup assignment further provided insightful classification rendering outcome discrimination beyond MAGGIC HF risk score.

LCZ696 as a potent pharmacological class in HFrEF through neprilysin inhibition beyond the renin–angiotensin system axis has shown to further mediate cardioprotective effects through vasodilation, afterload reduction, and myocardial fibrosis inhibition, thereby promoting reverse LV remodeling in HFrEF. 18 Hence, individuals with HFrEF presenting certain geometry featured by higher hemodynamic LV wall tension would perhaps benefit most from LCZ696 treatment. 3 , 7 , 9 , 17 Indeed, our current phenogroup 1 represents a specific HFrEF phenotype manifesting more dilated (or eccentric) LV geometry and higher baseline wall stress accompanied by steeper LV filling pressure in relation to LV expansion. Additionally, patients in this group had a trend toward younger age, less comorbid diabetes, and better renal function compared with other phenotypes. We observed that such population may have the most favorable outcome responses following LCZ696 treatment (as sacubitril/valsartan responders) (Figure 5).

ARNi indicates angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor; CV, cardiovascular; DM, diabetes mellitus; HFrEF, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction; LV, left ventricular; NT‐proBNP, N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide; RWT, relative wall thickness; and sGLT2i, sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor.

In a prospectively enrolled large‐scale study (Asian‐HF [Asian Sudden Cardiac Death in Heart Failure] Registry), patients with HF in Asia had a unique lean diabetic phenotype. 4 In that study cohort, patients with HF coexisted with diabetes, either HFrEF or HF with preserved ejection fraction, were featured by a lesser extent of ventricular remodeling (smaller heart size and more concentric) with nevertheless similarly high LV filling pressure and worse clinical outcomes compared with the nondiabetes HF counterpart. 19 Our phenogroup 2 (with notably high prevalent diabetes) in the current work highly replicated findings from the Asian‐HF study by having a smaller heart, comparably high LV filling yet manifesting the highest cardiovascular death rate compared with other HFrEF phenogroups albeit LCZ696 use (as sacubitril/valsartan nonresponders), and demonstrated worse renal outcomes compared with RASi control (non‐LCZ696 users). The hypothetical mechanism underlying this phenomenon may in part be attributable to aggravated interstitial cardiac fibrosis and advanced glycation end‐product deposition in HF coexisting with diabetes, especially in Asians, thus limiting heart size expansion and enhancing myocardial stiffness. 20 , 21 , 22 With shared microvascular pathophysiology, such a patient population likely experiences a more rapid kidney fibrosis into end‐stage renal disease with HF. 6 , 17 , 23 , 24

Patients in the current phenogroup 3 likely represented a much smaller portion of patients with HFrEF presenting either extremely hypertrophied wall or perhaps underlying hypertrophic cardiomyopathy in the “burned‐out” phase not identified clinically. 25 These patients with HFrEF were featured by the highest NT‐proBNP level, liver enzyme level (signs of more severe congestion) and highest blood urea nitrogen/creatinine ratio subject to highest clinical HF events (highest HF hospitalization or urgent HF visit), and highest all‐cause death rate compared with other HFrEF phenogroups despite active LCZ696 treatment (as sacubitril/valsartan nonresponders). In an era of increasingly recognized infiltrative cardiomyopathy or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, routine genetic screening for highly suspected patients (eg, TTR sequencing) and adequate referral for potential targeted genetic or pathway‐specific approach is highly recommended (class IB) as part of clinical HF management workflow (Figure 5). 26 , 27

Taken collectively, we proposed that our HF phenotyping consideration of comorbid diabetes, renal profiles, and specific cardiac phenotypes may serve as key parameters for the optimal prioritization of a pharmacological approach among patients with HFrEF in real‐world practice (Figure S4).

Limitations

Our present study has several limitations. First, our findings were mainly derived from a real‐world setting and not conducted using a prospective, randomized study design, and thus some bias and imbalanced characteristic differences may exist between LCZ696 and the RASi control group. However, after matching, most baseline characters were comparable between LCZ696 and RASi users. Second, our findings were mainly based on Asian patients with HFrEF from a real‐world setting not generalizable to White individuals. Additionally, these findings may be further strengthened if an external validating cohort with similar clinical settings can be found. Taken collectively, future external validation may be helpful to confirm these findings. Finally, we are not stating that selective or sequential therapy from one to another class of medication use is preferred on the basis of our phenomapping; instead, our data suggested the possible considerations of other guideline‐directed medical therapy candidate use (eg, sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors) in clinical scenarios in which LCZ696 may not be appropriate or when there is a concern in prescription (such as hypotension or lower estimated glomerular filtration rate).

Conclusions

By using model‐based clustering analysis among a large‐scale real‐world group of patients with HFrEF receiving LCZ696, we identified 3 unique clinical phenotypes reflecting insightful yet unique and distinctive clinical and cardiac features. Phenogroup assignment further provided insightful prognostic and treatment outcome discrimination beyond clinical risk scoring. Findings from the current study perhaps underscores the potentiality of precision medicine among patients with HFrEF in discriminating those who may show best clinical responses and benefit most with LCZ696F use as an optimum choice of pharmacological approach and those in whom alternative medications may be more preferable when there is concern for LCZ696 use in clinical settings (such as hypotension or worse renal function).
