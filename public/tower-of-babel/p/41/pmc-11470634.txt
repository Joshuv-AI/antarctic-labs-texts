Indirect estimation of the prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus in the sub-population of Tehran: using non-laboratory risk-score models in Iran
Authors: Yosra Azizpour, Samaneh Asgari, Mehdi Yaseri, Akbar Fotouhi, Samaneh Akbarpour
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in the population covered by the Tehran University of Medical Sciences is unclear but crucial for healthcare programs. This study aims to validate four non-laboratory risk-score models, the American Diabetes Association (ADA) Risk Score, Australian Type 2 Diabetes Risk Assessment Tool (AUSDRISK), Finnish Diabetes Risk Score (FINDRISC), and TOPICS Diabetes Screening Score, for identifying undiagnosed diabetes and indirectly estimate the prevalence of T2DM in a subset of the Tehranian population using the selected model.MethodsThis research consisted of two main parts. In the first part, non-laboratory risk-score models to identify undiagnosed T2DM were validated using Iranian data from STEPs 2016 survey. The model performance was evaluated through the Area Under the Curve (AUC) and calibration via the observed-to-expected (O/E) ratio. Additional independent data from STEPs 2011 survey in Iran were utilized to test the model results by comparing indirect prevalence estimates with observed estimates. Additionally, our findings indicate that the indirect estimates are nearly identical to the observed values in STEPs 2011 survey. Background

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic disease and a significant contributor to microvascular and macrovascular complications [1, 2]. Concerning the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) reports, the global prevalence of diabetes among adults aged 20–79 years was 10.5% (~ 536.6 million) in 2021 and is projected to increase to 12.2% by 2045 (~ 783.2 million) [3]. Respectively, the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region stands out with the highest prevalence (18.1%) among other regions and Iran ranks third in prevalence among other countries in this region recording a prevalence of 9.5% in 2021 and expected to rise to 13% by 2045 [3]. According to the most recent Iranian national survey study in 2021, the prevalence of T2DM was 14.2% and of them, about 27% were unaware of their condition [4], which is lower than the global estimates of unknown diabetes (44.7%) [5].

Accurate population-level epidemiological assessments (such as prevalence, incidence, etc.) are necessary to conduct reliable country-specific evaluations and make impactful public health decisions [6]. This goal is achieved by conducting surveillance studies in every country. Given the significance of managing diabetes resources to control its complications, having access to national-level survey data can prove advantageous. During the past decade, there have been several health surveys focusing on monitoring the main non-communicable disease (NCD) risk factors [7–12], including the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) conducted among the American population [13]. In 2002, the World Health Organization (WHO) developed a standardized but flexible STEPwise surveillance approach for low- and middle-income countries to monitor and report the prevalence of NCD risk factors [14, 15]. Iran is among many countries globally adopting this approach, having conducted STEPs surveys in eight rounds in 2005, 2006, 2007, 2008, 2009, 2011, 2016, and 2021 [16].

Beyond several advantages of the population-household surveys known as the direct estimation method at regional and local levels, there are several key limitations, including high costs [15, 17, 18], the challenge of conducting in-person interviews [15, 18], and collecting biological samples [15], and the absence of access to regular annual trends in the covered community [17]. Indirect methods, such as machine learning-based models or regression-based models [19], have been suggested to estimate prevalence when direct methods are difficult. In this study, our objectives were to (a) identify an appropriate non-laboratory risk score model for indirect estimation based on STEPs 2016 survey data, (b) compare the estimated prevalence with the observed prevalence of T2DM using STEPs survey in 2011 and 2016 data, (c) comparisons of prediction models for undiagnosed T2DM between logistic regression model versus a machine learning model, and (d) estimate the indirect prevalence of T2DM in a subset of the Tehranian population using the selected model.

Methods

For the indirect estimation of the prevalence, we follow the methodology explained by Park et al. which utilized a regression-based model [20]. The present study was structured into two parts:

First part: external validation of a non-laboratory risk-score model for screening undiagnosed T2DM

In this section, we discuss the non-laboratory risk score models selected for external validation among the Iranian population. Additionally, we provide details about the STEPs 2016 survey data utilized for this purpose. The TRIPOD checklist was used for writing this manuscript on the external validation part [21], additional File 1.

Selection of non-laboratory risk-score models for external validation

For model selection, we consider the latest published systematic reviews on T2DM risk score models which are mostly developed among the American or European population [22–24], and from these, three models that had previously been validated on the subset of the Iranian population and had the highest discrimination and calibration were chosen for external validation in the Iranian national data set: American Diabetes Association (ADA) Risk Score [25], Australian Type 2 Diabetes Risk Assessment Tool (AUSDRISK) [26], Finnish Diabetes Risk Score (FINDRISC) [27]. We also include the Japanese model (TOPICS Diabetes Screening Score ) [28] that was developed based on the East-Asian population. More information about selected models is presented in additional File 2: Table S1.

Study population for external validation: National surveillance data of Iran (STEPs 2016 survey)

STEPs 2016 survey data

The WHO STEPwise approach to NCD risk factor surveillance is a straightforward, standardized method for gathering, and analyzing important NCD risk factors across low- and middle-income countries [15]. For the current study, we used data from STEPs 2016 survey which included laboratory measurements on a sub-sample of adults aged ≥ 25 years. Data was gathered through a systematic proportional to size cluster random sampling technique among Iranian adults who were not hospitalized or institutionalized, conducted by skilled interviewers during household visits. Further information regarding STEPs has been previously published [29]. Out of the total 19,503 Population aged ≥ 25 years with available laboratory measurements on fasting plasma glucose (FPG), we excluded pregnant women (N = 237), and those on anti-diabetes medications (N = 1,200). Finally, after removing (N = 1,754) individuals who had insufficient data on risk score variables such as waist circumference (WC), consumption of fruits and vegetables, physical activity, body mass index (BMI), and systolic/diastolic blood pressure (SBP/DBP) measurement,16,312 individuals (49.6% female) were included in the study (Fig. 1). The final samples for each model were obtained by excluding missing data, finally, a smaller sample size was used to validate the models and equalize the sample sizes.

Flow diagram of the study participants for the external validation part (STEPs 2016 survey)

A sample size of at least 100 events and 100 non-events is recommended for the external validation of a prediction model [30, 31]. In this study, 16,312 individuals (716 with undiagnosed diabetes (event) and 15,596 without diabetes (nonevent)) were included.

To verify the accuracy of the results of the valid model in Iran, in addition to the STEPs 2016 survey data, independent STEPs 2011 survey data were used. The indirect prevalence estimation findings were then compared with the observed prevalence. In 2011, Iran conducted its sixth nationwide STEPs survey to assess NCD risk factors among Iranians aged 6 to 70. The survey aimed to include participants from all 31 provinces, with a sample size of 12,000 chosen through multi-stage cluster random sampling [32]. Out of the total 5,332 participants aged 25–70 with laboratory measurements for FPG, pregnant women (N = 52) and those on anti-diabetes medications (N = 459) were excluded. Finally, after removing 262 individuals with insufficient data on variables such as WC, BMI, family history of diabetes mellitus (FH-DM), physical activity, smoking, and SBP/DBP measurement, the study included 4,559 individuals (49.9% female) (Fig. 2).

Flow diagram of the study participants in STEPs 2011 survey

Clinical and laboratory measurements

A trained interviewer collected information on participants ‘medical history, medications, and family history by a standard questionnaire. Weight was measured in kilograms with minimal clothing. Height was measured standing without shoes with a measuring tape, with an error margin of 0.5 cm. WC was measured in centimeters between the lower ribs and the iliac crest.

The SBP and DBP of participants were measured three times by Beurer Medical (Germany), and the averages of the second and third readings were considered in the analysis. After an overnight fast (12–14 h), venous blood samples were collected to measure levels of FPG, triglycerides (TGs), plasma concentrations of total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). The samples were stored at temperatures below 4 °C and promptly transported to the central laboratory within 18 h, and the lab measurement was done using auto-analyzer (Cobas C311, Hitachi, Japan) approved by the reference lab [33].

Variable definitions

BMI was calculated by dividing weight (kilograms) by height (meters squared). BMI was categorized as follows: normal (BMI < 25 kg/m2), overweight (25 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 30 kg/m2), obese (30 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 35 kg/m2), and morbidly obese (BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2). Current smoking was reported by individuals who used tobacco daily (cigarettes, hookah, pipe, etc.). Participants were classified as physically inactive if they engaged in less than 150 min per week of either continuous walking or moderate or vigorous activity. Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg or using anti-hypertensive medications. Undiagnosed T2DM is defined as FPG ≥ 126 mg/dL (≥ 7 mmol/L) without any antidiabetic medication treatment.

Statistical analysis for external validation and model selection approach

To provide nationally and locally representative estimates, post-stratification weights were used to weight the STEPs 2016 survey data based on age groups, sex, and residence (urban/rural) according to the Iran census in 2016. Study participant characteristics were reported as mean (standard error: SE) for continuous variables and percentage for categorical variables based on survey analysis.

For external validation of the selected models, the area under the curve (AUC) was utilized to determine the discrimination ability and the AUC of ≥ 0.5–<0.7, ≥ 0.70–<80, ≥ 0.80–<0.90, and ≥ 0.90 represent poor, acceptable, excellent, and outstanding discrimination, respectively [34]. To report the calibration, the observed risk (O) was plotted versus the mean of expected probabilities (E) over deciles. Moreover, the O/E ratio was calculated; the ratio < 1 indicated overestimation, and > 1 indicated underestimation of the risk. To improve model fitness to the Iranian population, model recalibration was done through re-estimation of intercept. In other words,, to generalize the original AUSDRISK model among the Iranian population, calibration-in-the-large was used to compare the average predicted risk and observed outcome proportion [35].

A series of sensitivity analyses within external validation were done to test the stability of the main result: First, since a FH-DM was not recorded in STEPs 2016 survey, the models were validated by assuming positive FH-DM for all participants. Second, we validated all models by considering the Iranian cut-off point for WC (We defined a WC of 90 cm ≤ WC < 95 cm as indicative of overweight and a WC of ≥ 95 cm as indicative of obesity for both sexes) as recommended by The Iranian National Committee on Obesity [36]. Finally, for the ADA and TOPICS models, external validation was done for different definitions of hypertension using only anti-hypertensive medications or considering the JNC-7 definition (SBP ≥ 140 mm Hg or DBP ≥ 90 mm Hg or anti-hypertensive medications) [37].

Additionally, indirect estimations were compared with an observed prevalence that was reported by previously published articles on STEPs 2011 survey [38] and STEPs 2016 survey [39] data.

Machine learning model

The Random Forest (RF) classification method was utilized to predict undiagnosed T2DM using variables selected from the ASUDRISK model, based on data from the STEPs 2016 survey. The dataset was split into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%). Hyperparameter tuning was conducted using a Grid Search algorithm with five-fold cross-validation. The performance of the model was evaluated using the AUC.

Second part: indirect estimation of T2DM prevalence, utilizing the subset of the Tehranian population achieved by a telephone survey in 2023.

Selected model information in the first part of this study (essential non-laboratory variables) was extracted using data gathering tool from the telephone survey data. Please refer to Additional File 3: Table S2 for details. Stratified random sampling was employed as the sampling method for the telephone survey. Each region (south of Tehran, Eslamshahr, and Shahr-e Rey) was treated as a stratum, and the number of samples specific to each region was chosen in probability proportional to the size (PPS) of that region. Subsequently, the landline numbers of the covered areas were obtained from the telecommunications company to contact households by phone. Households were contacted, and after listing household members based on the criteria, one person was randomly selected as the initial sampling unit for a telephone interview. To participate in this survey, individuals had to meet two inclusion criteria: living with the household for at least the last six months and being at least 18 years old. If the initial call was unsuccessful, it was repeated three times within 24 h at various times of the day and night. If the selected individual could not be contacted, it was classified as a non-response. More details about the monitoring and quality control in the data collection stage can be found in Additional File 2: Box S1. Of a total of 1,176 participants aged ≥ 25 years, after excluding pregnant women (n = 52) and individuals with known T2DM (n = 81), 1,043 participants were included for indirect estimation (Fig. 3). Since 5.34% of the covariates were missing, to have the maximum power, a single imputation with regression method was considered.

Flow diagram of the study participants for indirect estimation in the telephone survey in 2023

The reliability of self-reported height and weight was assessed through telephone and face-to-face interviews. To measure this, we selected 97 participants and measured their height and weight at the entrance of their homes. The reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Based on a 2-way mixed-effects model and absolute agreement, ICC was calculated. For ICC estimates, reliability based on the 95% confidence interval (CI) is classified as poor below 0.5, moderate at 0.5–0.75, good at 0.75–0.9, and excellent above 0.9 [40]. According to ICC statistics, the weight variable showed excellent reliability (ICC = 0.96; 95% CI: 0.95, 0.98), while the height variable displayed good to excellent reliability (ICC = 0.92; 95% CI: 0.88, 0.95). However, self-reported weight and height were corrected using linear regression [41].

Indirect estimation of T2DM prevalence in STEPs 2011 survey and STEPs 2016 survey and to compare with observed prevalence (to verify the accuracy of the selected model)

Out of the total of 4,559 participants in STEPs 2011 survey, 3.72% had undiagnosed T2DM. The mean (SE) values of age, BMI, and WC in STEPs 2016 survey were 38.8 years (0.04), 26.0 kg/m2 (0.08), and 88.6 cm (0.21), respectively (Table 1). The recalibrated AUSDRISK score was used to estimate the probability of undiagnosed T2DM. By applying the bootstrap mean estimation of the probability to equation b, the indirect prevalence of T2DM (95% CI) was estimated at 10.8% (10.4, 11.3) in STEPs 2016 survey, 12.2% (11.3, 13.1) in STEPs 2011 survey. Our findings indicate that the indirect estimates are nearly identical to the observed values. We also presented the observed and indirectly estimated prevalence (95% CI) by sex and residence in Table 3.

Machine learning model

For the classification of undiagnosed T2DM, the RF model demonstrated strong performance, achieving an AUC of 0.81 (95% confidence interval: 0.79, 0.83). The optimal hyperparameters for the RF model were identified as follows: number of trees = 50, maximum tree depth = 4, splitting criterion = ‘gini’, and the number of features considered when searching for the best split = ‘sqrt’.

Second part: Indirect Estimation of Prevalence T2DM using a subset of the Tehranian population by telephone Survey in 2023

The baseline characteristics of study participants are presented in Table 1. A subsample of 1043 individuals from the Tehranian population had mean (SE) values of age and BMI at 42.1 years (0.47) and 27.2 kg/m2 (0.15), respectively. Additionally, 46.1% of the participants were female.

The recalibrated AUSDRISK score was used to estimate the probability of undiagnosed T2DM with a 95% CI of 4.18% (3.87, 4.49). By applying the bootstrap mean estimation of the probability to equation b, the indirect prevalence of T2DM (95% CI) was estimated to be 11.1% (9.34, 13.1) (Table 4). Also, the prevalence of diagnosed T2DM was 7.21% (95% CI: 5.76, 8.88). Additionally, the indirectly estimated prevalence by sex was 10.5% (95% CI: 7.86, 13.7) for males and 11.2% (95% CI: 8.93, 13.9) for females. The estimated prevalence by residence was 10.9% (95% CI: 9.03, 13.0) for urban areas and 12.2% (95% CI: 6.99, 19.3) for rural areas.

Discussion

The present study showed that the best non-laboratory model that can indirectly estimate the prevalence of T2DM in Iran is AUSDRISK. We attempted to indirectly estimate the prevalence of diabetes in a pilot population by conducting a telephone survey and assessing non-laboratory variables.

In Iran, the validation of global models of T2DM for the outcome of undiagnosed diabetes has been limited. In the study of Lotfaliany et al., three models, ASUDRISK, Concise FINDRISC, and ADA, were validated in the prospective cohort setting (Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS)) at the aged ≥ 30 years. In this study, the recalibration model (update the intercept) of AUSDRISK showed the best discriminative ability (AUC = 0.79) and calibration compared with the other two recalibration models (update the intercept and overall calibration slope) [22]. In the study by Mahmoodzadeh et al., two models, Full FINDRISC and ASUDRISK, were investigated in the population of the Kerman Coronary Artery Disease Risk Factors Study (KERCADRS) aged 35–65 years old and the discriminative ability of the original AUSDRISK model was AUC = 0.67, the original FINDRISC model was AUC = 0.62, and both models had good calibration [42]. In the study by Asgari et al., the performance of the ADA risk score was investigated in the setting of the STEPs 2011 survey study [23], and the study of Hasandokht et al., in the Persian cohort of Gilan Study (PCGS) [43]. In the first study, the discriminative power of the ADA risk score was reported (AUC = 0.74), and in the last study, the risk score considered the WC variable (AUC = 0.70) and BMI variable (AUC = 0.69). In comparison to the above studies, our study is the first to compare the four global models for the outcome of undiagnosed T2DM in the setting of a large cross-sectional, and the AUC of the AUSDRISK non-laboratory risk-score model in this study is similar to the AUC obtained in the original study (0.80 vs. 0.78).

Another important point to consider is the use of telephone surveys for gathering model information. Telephone surveys are used worldwide for various health purposes. In the study by Barr et al., conducted to compare the prevalence of non-communicable diseases and their risk factors in a telephone and face-to-face survey, the results indicated no statistically significant difference between these two methods in assessing health indicators such as consumption of vegetables and fruits, obesity or overweight, and history of diabetes [44]. In addition, in Mahfoud et al.‘s study, the interpretation of the Kappa statistic about the variables of alcohol consumption, history of high blood pressure, education level, hookah smoking, history of hyperlipidemia and heart disease, age, insurance status, history of diabetes, current cigarette smoking was reported as substantial or almost perfect [45]. Similarly, in the present study the results of measuring height and weight exhibited a very high correlation with the self-reported values.

Applications of machine learning, data mining techniques [46], and regression models [47] in the field of diabetes research are commonly utilized for prediction and diagnosis. Developing indirect methods to estimate prevalence could offer faster and more cost-effective results than cross-sectional studies, benefiting healthcare applications. Predictive models for managing diabetes and its complications have gained traction in research over the past decade. These models, often constructed using multiple linear or logistic regression. Despite the progress in creating predictive models, there is a lack of implementation and investigation into their clinical impact, highlighting a gap between development and real-world application. Challenges such as variable selection and result validation impede the implementation of these models. Embracing predictive modeling could transform healthcare by optimizing data utilization for clinical decision-making in disease management [48]. According to studies, the discrete-state Markov model or compartmental mathematical model has been utilized to estimate the future prevalence of T2DM in various populations. For instance, in Saudi Arabia [49], Tunisia [50], and Qatar [51], these models have provided relatively accurate estimates of T2DM prevalence. Predictions using models such as the compartmental mathematical model may vary depending on the specific mathematical model and the availability of data for T2DM. Limited input data can restrict the accuracy of predictions [51]. Cichosz and colleagues found that machine learning models can accurately identify undiagnosed diabetes using simple, obtainable variables, suggesting its potential as a valuable prescreening tool in clinical practice [52]. In our study as well, the comparison of the results from the selected AUSDRISK model using the 2016 steps data with those from the traditional approach (logistic regression) and the machine learning model (random forest) demonstrated the superiority and high accuracy of both approaches. Recent research in the form of a systematic review indicates that in a meta-analysis of 71 studies, machine learning (ML) does not demonstrate any performance advantage over conventional logistic regression for clinical risk prediction [53]. Moreover, using regression models for prediction and estimation is much simpler. In this study, the AUSDRISK model, a logistic regression model utilizing non-laboratory variables, was applied to telephone survey data. In the last published report of the STEPs 2021 survey (Iran), the estimated prevalence of T2DM in Iran was 14.2% (95% CI: 13.4,14.9) [4], and in Tehran province was reported at 12.3% (95% CI: 10.2,14.4) [54] were reported. Our estimate at the local level (11.1% (95% CI: 9.34, 13.1) is within the confidence interval of the estimated prevalence for Tehran province and is somewhat close to the national estimate. It is noteworthy that regional estimates may not necessarily match provincial or national estimates. The areas covered by the Tehran University of Medical Sciences are considered underprivileged and differ from other parts of Tehran province in terms of social and economic status. Just like in the national survey of Tehran province, individuals are chosen from all areas within Tehran, therefore, it can be said that these findings were not necessarily unexpected. On the other hand, they highlight the importance of conducting such a study and using a method for regional estimation. This study validated a simple, reliable, and standardized statistical model tailored specifically for Iran. This model facilitates accurate estimates of disease prevalence without requiring in-person interviews or laboratory measurements. Instead, data can be gathered through telephone surveys. By employing these statistical models, it becomes feasible to monitor the annual or biannual prevalence of diseases over time (temporal trend) in a cost-effective manner, without depending on basic laboratory data. This approach allows for identifying temporal trends in disease prevalence, offering valuable public health insights at a reduced cost. This approach is one of the simple methods that can be used to obtain the temporal trends of the disease in the whole world.

Advantages and limitations

Among the advantages of this study are the use of the correction factor to correct self-reported height and weight, the sameness of questions between the STEPs survey and the telephone survey, validating different models that can be used worldwide, instead of developing a regional model. One limitation of this study is that the STEPs survey used only a single fasting glucose measurement to identify undiagnosed diabetes cases. However, in population-based studies, it is common to use a single measurement due to issues with re-measurement, as observed in the Whitehall II prospective cohort and NHANES cross-sectional study.

Conclusion

The lack of an image of the real state of the disease in the covered population (prevalence of the disease) and its change to evaluate the effectiveness of interventions at the regional level is one of the important challenges of experts, but this study offers a cost-effective solution. In this study, we utilized a straightforward and cost-effective method to estimate the local prevalence. While we recognize that modeling may introduce some errors in the estimates, we have taken steps to validate the models with minimal error. We believe that the benefits of using these models, such as simplicity, low cost, and the ability to be repeated annually or biennially, outweigh the potential errors.
