Emerging Roles of Receptor‐Like Proteins in Plant Immunity: Crosstalk, Signalling Networks and Prospects for Disease Resistance Breeding
Authors: Chenfei Gao, Jianping Chen, Zongtao Sun, Hehong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTReceptor‐like proteins (RLPs) are cell‐surface receptors that recognise pathogen‐ or damage‐associated molecular patterns to activate immune responses, and some also participate in plant development and modulation of defence signalling. Recent advances in bioinformatics and high‐throughput technologies have begun to shed light on the functional roles of RLPs, their interactions with other immune receptors, and their involvement in plant defence mechanisms. This review summarises the emerging roles of RLPs in plant immunity, with a particular focus on their dynamic interplay with receptor‐like kinases (RLKs) and intracellular nucleotide‐binding leucine‐rich repeat receptors (NLRs) in forming integrated immune signalling networks. We also compare RLP functions across model species and major crops, revealing both conserved mechanisms and species‐specific adaptations that may reflect evolutionary divergence. Furthermore, we evaluate the potential of RLPs as promising targets for disease resistance breeding. Together, this review provides a comprehensive framework for understanding RLP functions and offers strategic perspectives for leveraging RLPs in translational crop improvement.

Introduction

Plants form the basis of terrestrial ecosystems by converting atmospheric carbon into biomass via photosynthesis, contributing to oxygen production, soil stability and the regulation of water and climate systems (Savary et al. 2019). While plants underpin terrestrial ecosystems and global carbon balance through photosynthesis, they are increasingly threatened by both biotic and abiotic stresses under climate change and human‐driven pressures. Among the most damaging threats to plant health are plant diseases, caused by a wide range of pathogens, including fungi, bacteria and viruses (Strange and Scott 2005; Ma et al. 2025). Pathogen infection not only damages plant tissues, particularly photosynthetic organs and vascular systems, but can also result in crop yield reduction or total failure, exacerbating food security issues.

Plants have evolved complex immune systems that detect and respond to pathogen attack. Central to this system are cell‐surface pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that recognise pathogen‐ and damage‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs and DAMPs, respectively) and activate downstream defence signalling, including antimicrobial chemicals production, cell wall strengthening and localised cell death (Nürnberger et al. 2004; Zhang and Zhou 2010). PRRs are generally classified into two major types: receptor‐like kinases (RLKs) and receptor‐like proteins (RLPs). RLKs contain an extracellular ligand‐binding domain (such as leucine‐rich repeats, LRRs), a transmembrane domain, and a cytoplasmic kinase domain essential for intracellular signal transduction. In contrast, RLPs possess similar extracellular and transmembrane domains but lack the cytoplasmic kinase domain, preventing them from directly transmitting signals via phosphorylation. Instead, RLPs depend on interactions with associated kinases, such as the co‐receptors SOBIR1 (SUPPRESSOR OF BIR1‐1) and BAK1 (BRI1‐ASSOCIATED KINASE 1), to initiate immune responses (Couto and Zipfel 2016; Postel and Kemmerling 2009). Despite their important roles in plant immunity, RLPs remain significantly less characterised than RLKs, resulting in an underestimation of their contributions to immune signalling networks.

The growing interest in RLPs is also driven by their promising applications in crop improvement. Elucidating the functions of RLPs across diverse plant species—including model organisms such as Arabidopsis thaliana and major crops like Oryza sativa , Triticum aestivum and Zea mays —offers valuable opportunities for developing targeted breeding strategies to enhance disease resistance (Wang et al. 2008). Nevertheless, the functional diversity of RLPs among species, their intricate signalling pathways, and their interactions with other immune receptors present both opportunities and challenges for their application in plant breeding. Given these complexities, investigating the emerging roles of RLPs in plant immunity and assessing their potential as novel targets for disease resistance breeding are both timely and essential.

This review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of recent advances in RLP research, with a focus on their roles in immune signalling networks and their potential to enhance disease resistance in crops. By synthesising current findings, we seek to clarify the mechanistic functions of RLPs, examine their species‐specific activities and discuss the challenges and prospects associated with leveraging RLPs in crop improvement.

Conserved Structural Domains and Functions of Plant RLPs

Signal perception and transduction in both plant immunity and developmental regulation frequently involve shared cellular pathways mediated by transmembrane receptor proteins. These transmembrane receptors primarily include RLKs and RLPs containing extracellular domains such as LRR, G‐type lectin, Wall‐associated kinase (WAK), Domain of unknown function 26 (Duf26), L‐type lectin, Lysin motif (LysM) and Malectin. RLPs, a major group of plasma membrane‐localised immune receptors, are broadly conserved across higher plant taxa (Jamieson et al. 2018). Accumulated evidence indicates that LRR‐containing membrane proteins, including RLPs and RLKs, play vital roles in coordinating plant development, hormone signalling, and defence responses by sensing extracellular cues and intercellular signals (Smet et al. 2009; Wang et al. 2010; Wang and Fiers 2009).

A defining characteristic of RLPs is their modular protein organisation. In plant LRR‐RLPs, the extracellular portion typically adopts a superhelical conformation, with β‐sheets lining the inner surface. In the case of Cf‐9, this type of receptor has been classified into seven domains (A–G). Domains B and D function as N‐ and C‐terminal capping units, stabilised by disulphide bridges that shield the hydrophobic core of the LRR scaffold. The central LRR segment (domain C) can be partitioned into three subregions (C1–C3), with C2 forming an island domain (ID) that interrupts the canonical LRR repeat pattern. Domain E, located after domain D, is enriched in acidic residues, whereas the C‐terminal portion consists of a transmembrane helix (domain F) and a positively charged cytoplasmic tail (domain G). Together, these structural modules enable both ligand perception and downstream signalling. Notably, the acidic juxtamembrane domain E and the transmembrane helix (domain F), which carries the GXXXG motif, are critical for interaction with SOBIR1 (Jones and Jones 2010; Van Der Hoorn et al. 2005; Snoeck et al. 2023). These LRR motifs facilitate ligand detection and protein–protein interactions, while the transmembrane region may contribute to intracellular signal relay or complex formation at the membrane interface. Interestingly, the LRR‐containing segments often harbour multiple putative glycosylation sites, implying that RLPs may function as membrane‐anchored glycoprotein receptors with potential roles in both structural stability and ligand interaction specificity (Stulemeijer and Joosten 2008).

From an evolutionary perspective, both LRR‐RLPs and LRR‐RLK‐Xbs share a conserved ‘ID + 4LRR’ unit, suggesting a common ancestor that used this module for ligand recognition and SERK co‐receptor recruitment. While the C‐terminal LRRs are conserved for stable BAK1 binding, the island domain (ID) and N‐terminal LRRs have diversified to recognise distinct ligands, driving functional divergence—RLPs mainly in immunity and RLK‐Xbs in development. Their juxtamembrane and transmembrane regions also evolved differently, enabling RLPs to constitutively interact with SOBIR1 and recruit BAK1 in a ligand‐dependent manner, whereas RLK‐Xbs avoid such immune activation (Ngou et al. 2024). Recent structural and genomic studies further highlight immune‐specific ID motifs (Y‐X8‐KG and K‐X5‐Y) that distinguish defence‐related RLPs from developmental receptors (Snoeck et al. 2023). For example, the immune receptor RXEG1 from Nicotiana benthamiana recognises the pathogen enzyme XEG1 via its ID, both blocking its enzymatic activity and recruiting BAK1 to activate immune signalling (Sun et al. 2022). These findings underscore the ID as a central determinant of RLP functional specialisation in plant immunity. Comparative analyses across lineages further reveal that diversification of the ID and N‐terminal LRRs underpins species‐specific immune adaptation, whereas conserved SOBIR1/BAK1 interfaces maintain core signalling, highlighting a modular yet evolutionarily flexible architecture of RLPs.

RLP‐Mediated Plant Immune Responses

Plants rely on innate immunity to detect and respond to invading pathogens. Upon pathogen recognition, plants rapidly initiate defence responses, including a localised hypersensitive response (HR) that restricts infection and promotes systemic resistance. Innate immunity comprises two interconnected layers: PAMP‐triggered immunity (PTI), activated by conserved PAMPs, and effector‐triggered immunity (ETI), induced by specific pathogen effectors (Yu et al. 2024; Jones et al. 2024). PTI is initiated when plasma membrane‐localised PRRs detect PAMPs such as bacterial flagellin or fungal chitin (Bigeard et al. 2015; Zipfel and Oldroyd 2017). Although physical barriers prevent many pathogens from entering, some penetrate via stomata or wounds, where PRRs activate signalling cascades to limit pathogen proliferation (Boller and Felix 2009; Couto and Zipfel 2016; Zipfel 2014). PAMP recognition triggers diverse defence responses including reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts, phytoalexin biosynthesis, HR and transcriptional activation of defence genes (Lin et al. 2022).

Multiple RLPs have been identified as PRRs critical for PTI. Despite lacking intracellular kinase domains, RLPs form complexes with RLKs, notably SOBIR1 and BAK1, to transduce immune signals (Zhang et al. 2013; Liebrand et al. 2013). Ligand binding promotes the assembly of RLP‐SOBIR1‐BAK1 complexes, leading to transphosphorylation and activation of receptor‐like cytoplasmic kinases (RLCKs), which trigger downstream defence responses (Chinchilla et al. 2007; Li et al. 2014). This modular receptor synergy underpins broad‐spectrum disease resistance. Well‐characterised RLPs include tomato Cf‐9 and Cf‐4, which recognise distinct Cladosporium fulvum effectors and confer race‐specific resistance (Jones et al. 1994; Thomas et al. 1997). Tomato Ve1 and Ve2 encode glycoproteins conferring resistance to Verticillium spp. through receptor‐mediated signalling (Fradin et al. 2009; Fradin et al. 2011; Kawchuk et al. 2001). In Arabidopsis, AtRLP30 mediates resistance to necrotrophic fungi, requiring SOBIR1 and BAK1 (Zhang et al. 2013; Gust and Felix 2014), while AtRLP52 mutants show susceptibility to powdery mildew (Ellendorff et al. 2014). T. aestivum RLP1.1 contributes to resistance against stripe rust via salicylic acid‐dependent pathways (Jiang et al. 2013). These studies highlight the pivotal role of RLPs across species in immune signalling (Figure 1).

Integrated Signalling: Interplay of RLPs, RLKs and Nucleotide‐Binding Leucine‐Rich Repeat Receptors

RLPs are essential immune receptors at the plasma membrane, detecting PAMPs and effectors. SOBIR1 forms stable complexes with RLPs, providing a signalling platform, while BAK1 recruitment upon ligand binding amplifies responses, creating a dynamic surveillance system. Intracellular nucleotide‐binding leucine‐rich repeat receptors (NLRs) form a second layer of immunity, and recent evidence reveals extensive bidirectional crosstalk between PTI and ETI (Thomma et al. 2011). ETI enhances PTI by stabilising PRR complexes and upregulating defence gene expression, while some NLRs monitor RLP/RLK functionality as ‘guardians’, fortifying immune robustness and specificity (Hatsugai et al. 2017; Yuan et al. 2021; Parker et al. 2022). This interplay exemplifies the multilayered architecture of plant immunity, integrating cell surface and intracellular receptors (Figure 2).

Coordinated immune signalling by receptor‐like proteins (RLPs) and receptor‐like kinases (RLKs), and leucine‐rich repeat receptors (NLRs) in pattern‐triggered immunity (PTI) and effector‐triggered immunity (ETI). PTI is initiated at the plasma membrane, where RLPs recognise pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) or effectors. This recognition involves co‐receptor complexes such as SOBIR1‐BAK1, which mediate the initial signal perception. The signal is then relayed through receptor‐like cytoplasmic kinases (RLCKs), activating common defence modules including MAP kinase (MAPK) cascades, Ca2+ influx, reactove oxygen species (ROS) bursts, thereby integrating and amplifying PTI responses. Meanwhile, ETI detects pathogen effectors through intracellular NLR receptors (such as TIR‐NLR and CC‐NLR), leading to the assembly of resistosomes. For example, CC‐NLR forms pentameric Ca2+ channels, while TIR‐NLR assembles into tetrameric NADase complexes. This activation further stimulates EDS1 heterodimers and downstream CCR‐NLRs (e.g., NRG1 and ADR1), which ultimately converge with PTI pathways via Ca2+ signalling to enhance and amplify immune outputs. Notably, PTI and ETI engage in bidirectional signalling: ETI strengthens PTI defence responses through shared downstream components (such as ROS and hormones), while PTI‐associated signalling molecules (e.g., MAPKs) can feedback‐regulate NLR activation thresholds, highlighting a dynamic interplay between these immune layers.

Although PTI and ETI were initially considered independent, accumulating evidence shows they share core defence pathways such as calcium signalling, ROS production, MAP kinase cascades and hormone regulation, differing mainly in intensity and kinetics (Cui et al. 2015; Peng et al. 2018). Importantly, RLPs contribute to this crosstalk by forming receptor complexes that provide essential entry points for PTI and also facilitate ETI potentiation. For example, Arabidopsis RLP23‐SOBIR1‐BAK1 complexes initiate PTI responses, and these are further stabilised and transcriptionally reinforced during ETI, illustrating how surface‐localised RLP signalling feeds into intracellular immune amplification (Albert et al. 2015; Yuan et al. 2021). Moreover, calcium channels regulated by RLK/RLP complexes, such as CNGC2/4, are indispensable for NLR‐triggered HR; mutants lacking CNGC2/4 fail to mount HR but retain basal resistance, underscoring the reliance of ETI on RLP‐associated signalling (Clough et al. 2000; Jurkowski et al. 2004; Ngou et al. 2022). Genetic evidence also shows that Arabidopsis NLRs RPS2 and RPS4/RRS1 require RLK/RLP co‐receptors for antibacterial defence, and ectopic expression of effectors such as AvrRps4 cannot induce HR without prior activation of RLK/RLP pathways (Ngou et al. 2021). Furthermore, PTI initiated by cell‐surface LRR receptors, including RLP23, requires the EDS1‐PAD4‐ADR1 node to connect with helper NLRs, highlighting a specific mechanistic link between RLP‐triggered signalling and ETI (Lapin et al. 2020; Pruitt et al. 2021; Wagner et al. 2013). Thus, RLPs act as critical mediators at the cell surface that not only perceive extracellular ligands but also provide signalling platforms that converge with NLR activity at intracellular hubs, collectively ensuring robust and multilayered resistance.

RLPs in Fungal‐ and Oomycete‐Triggered Immunity

RLPs not only participate in plant immune responses but can also activate distinct immune responses in response to different effectors. In Arabidopsis, AtRLP53 interacts with co‐receptors SOBIR1 and BAK1, and the GPI‐anchored protein LLG1, which regulates its accumulation at the plasma membrane. Functional studies show that AtRLP53 positively contributes to resistance against bacterial, fungal and oomycete pathogens (Chen et al. 2022). The immune functions of RLPs often transcend species boundaries. For example, AtRLP23 and the wild potato RLP SmELR retain functionality when transferred across species, indicating their potential in engineering broad‐spectrum resistance. In Arabidopsis, AtRLP30 recognises SCFE1, an unidentified proteinaceous elicitor from Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, and confers resistance against both S. sclerotiorum and Botrytis cinerea (Zhang et al. 2013). Similarly, AtRLP2 mediates resistance to Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. matthioli (FOM) in the Ty‐0 ecotype. Variations at the RFO2 locus underlie differential susceptibility between ecotypes. Though AtRLP2 and AtRLP3/RFO2 are structurally similar and co‐localised, AtRLP2 alone is insufficient for Fusarium resistance unless its extracellular LRR domain is substituted with that of AtRLP3 (McDowell et al. 2013). Interestingly, the LRR domain of AtRLP3 shows sequence similarity to PSY1R, an LRR‐RLK that perceives the tyrosine‐sulphated peptide PSY1, a negative regulator of immunity (Oehlenschlæger et al. 2017).

Heterologous expression of AtRLP23 in potato enhances resistance to both Phytophthora infestans and S. sclerotiorum (Ono et al. 2020). In wild potato, SmELR detects the conserved oomycete elicitor INF1 from Phytophthora, and its expression in cultivated potato confers resistance to late blight (Du et al. 2015). Additionally, in rice, the BRG8 gene, which encodes a CC‐NBS‐LRR protein, positively regulates resistance to both Magnaporthe oryzae and Xanthomonas oryzae , as demonstrated by complementation analysis (Xu et al. 2024). Together, these findings highlight the central roles of RLPs in mediating defence against fungal and oomycete pathogens via recognition of diverse microbe‐associated molecular patterns (MAMPs).

RLPs in Bacteria‐Triggered Immunity

RLPs are also key players in defence against bacterial pathogens. In Arabidopsis, AtRLP30 has been shown to enhance resistance to non‐host Pseudomonas syringae strains (Wang et al. 2008). SNC2 (AtRLP51), an LRR‐RLP, carries a conserved GXXXG motif within its transmembrane domain, crucial for helix–helix interactions. Mutation of the second glycine to arginine results in constitutive defence activation and enhanced resistance (Zhang et al. 2010; Teese and Langosch 2015; Yang et al. 2012). A similar gain‐of‐function phenotype is observed in its close homologue AtRLP55/SNC3, suggesting potential interaction or functional redundancy between these RLPs and associated membrane proteins (Lv et al. 2016). In Solanum lycopersicum (tomato), the disease resistance genes Cf‐4 and Cf‐9 are located at the same genetic locus. Both encode membrane‐anchored extracellular glycoproteins that mediate resistance to C. fulvum, yet they recognise distinct pathogen‐derived avirulence determinants (AVR4 and AVR9, respectively) (Thomas et al. 1997; Luderder et al. 2001). In Capsicum annuum (pepper), genome editing of CaRLP264, CaRLP277, and CaRLP351 markedly increased susceptibility to Ralstonia solanacearum and Phytophthora capsici, accompanied by reduced HR‐like cell death. These RLPs are therefore implicated in broad‐spectrum immunity and HR‐associated defence (Kang et al. 2022).

RLPs in Virus‐Triggered Immunity

RLPs, traditionally associated with bacterial and fungal defence, have recently emerged as important contributors to antiviral immunity. Unlike intracellular NLR receptors, RLPs are typically localised at the plasma membrane and detect viral signals via extracellular LRR domains, triggering immune responses through PTI or, in some cases, ETI. Compared with their well‐established roles in bacterial and fungal pathogen defence, RLPs have only rarely been implicated in antiviral immunity. To date, the most convincing evidence comes from rice, and the number of experimentally validated cases is still limited. One representative example is OsRLP1, a leucine‐rich repeat RLP that is transcriptionally induced upon infection with rice black‐streaked dwarf virus (RBSDV). Functional and genetic analyses demonstrated that OsRLP1 forms a complex with the adaptor kinase OsSOBIR1, leading to the activation of MAPK cascades and defence‐related gene expression, thereby enhancing resistance to RBSDV (Zhang et al. 2021). This OsRLP1‐OsSOBIR1 module currently represents one of the clearest cases of an RLP directly contributing to antiviral defence in plants. In contrast, the BAK1‐associated receptor gene (OsBAP1) acts as a negative regulator of antiviral immunity. During infection with southern rice black‐streaked dwarf virus (SRBSDV), OsBAP1 expression is induced, and loss‐of‐function mutants display enhanced resistance, indicating that some RLPs can function as brakes on defence activation (Wang et al. 2023). These contrasting roles of OsRLP1 and OsBAP1 highlight the functional diversity of RLPs in antiviral responses, ranging from positive regulators of defence to negative modulators that fine‐tune immunity. It is important to note, however, that examples of RLPs in virus‐triggered immunity remain sparse, and the field still lacks definitive evidence of RLPs acting as bona fide viral pattern‐recognition receptors.

RLPs in Plant Immunity: Comparative Insights Across Model Species and Major Crops

While the core signalling functions of RLPs such as the requirement for co‐receptors like SOBIR1 and BAK1 are conserved across angiosperms, species‐specific variations in RLP domain architecture, ligand recognition, and signalling partners reveal considerable evolutionary plasticity (Capistrano‐Gossmann et al. 2017; Huang and Joosten 2025). This section compares the immune roles of RLPs in model systems ( Arabidopsis thaliana , Solanum lycopersicum ) and major crops ( O. sativa, T. aestivum, Z. mays, Vigna unguiculata ), with emphasis on conserved co‐receptor modules and lineage‐specific diversification (Table 1). A defining feature of RLP‐mediated immunity is their strict dependence on kinase‐active co‐receptors (Figure 3). In Arabidopsis, RLP23 detects necrosis‐ and ethylene‐inducing peptide 1‐like proteins (NLPs) from microbial pathogens and initiates canonical PTI through the formation of an RLP23‐SOBIR1‐BAK1 complex. This leads to downstream activation of MAPKs, ROS bursts, and transcriptional reprogramming (Albert et al. 2015; Ono et al. 2020). Similar signalling logic is observed in Solanaceae species, where tomato Cf‐type RLPs (e.g., Cf‐4, Cf‐9) detect C. fulvum effectors and activate defence responses through the same SOBIR1‐BAK1, illustrating functional conservation across dicots (Van Der Burgh et al. 2019). However, lineage‐specific demands have shaped RLP evolution. In S. lycopersicum , expanded Cf‐family members such as Cf‐2 and Cf‐5 mediate effector‐triggered cell death responses, and some RLPs, like Cf‐4, also interact with intracellular NLR helper proteins (e.g., NRCs), thereby increasing complexity to downstream signalling (Qi et al. 2022). These modular interactions suggest that Solanaceae RLPs have evolved enhanced immunological flexibility, likely driven by co‐evolution with specialised pathogens.

Structural and functional comparison of RLPs involved in immune signalling across representative dicots (Arabidopsis, Solanum lycopersicum [tomato]) and monocot crops (Oryza sativa [rice], Triticum aestivum [wheat]). Despite the conservation of key architectural elements—including extracellular leucine‐rich repeat (LRR) motifs, a single transmembrane helix, and essential partnerships with co‐receptors such as SOBIR1 and BAK1—significant diversification is observed in ligand recognition domains. In Arabidopsis, RLPs such as RLP23 and RLP30 contribute to the recognition of pathogen molecules, initiating immune responses characteristic of pathogen‐associated molecular pattern (PAMP)‐triggered immunity (PTI). Tomato RLPs, including Cf‐4 and Cf‐9, further engage intracellular helper nucleotide‐binding leucine‐rich repeat receptor (NLR) proteins to mount effector‐triggered immunity (ETI) and hypersensitive responses. In monocots, RLPs like OsRLP1 and TaRLP1.1 are implicated in defence against viruses and fungi, frequently interacting with monocot‐specific SOBIR1 variants. In legumes, the LRR‐RLP INR perceives the herbivore‐associated molecular pattern (HAMP) peptide inceptin from caterpillar oral secretions, enabling crops like Vigna unguiculata (cowpea) and common bean to mount defence responses. Together, these cases underscore the functional versatility of RLPs across plant families. The divergence in extracellular regions likely underpins species‐specific adaptation to diverse pathogen challenges, whereas intracellular signalling components maintain functional consistency.

In monocots, RLPs' evolution is more divergent. While rice immunity is dominated by RLKs such as XA21, the identification of OsRLP1, which contributes to bacterial defence, suggests that functional diversification of RLPs is emerging in monocotyledonous systems. In T. aestivum , multiple TaRLP genes are transcriptionally induced upon Puccinia striiformis infection and TaRLP1.1 overexpression enhances resistance, although its specific ligand remains unknown (Jiang et al. 2013). In Z. mays , stress‐responsive expression patterns of ZmRLPs under fungal and herbivore challenge hint at potential roles in integrating defence signals across biotic stress axes, though functional data are limited (Block et al. 2021). In certain legume species, there exists a receptor protein named INR, belonging to the LRR‐RLP family, that specifically recognises a well‐defined herbivore‐associated molecular pattern (HAMP) in caterpillar oral secretions, the peptide inceptin derived from proteolytic fragments of chloroplastic ATP synthase. In major legume crops such as cowpea ( Vigna unguiculata ) and common bean ( Phaseolus vulgaris ), INR mediates the perception of inceptin and subsequent defence responses. The existence of INR provides another exemplary case of RLP activity in an additional plant family (Steinbrenner et al. 2020).

Evolutionarily, RLPs exemplify a modular defence strategy combining structural conservation with selective diversification. Core components like SOBIR1 and BAK1 are highly conserved across taxa, serving as universal signalling platforms (Wei et al. 2022). In contrast, the extracellular LRR domains undergo rapid divergence, enabling recognition of a broad and shifting repertoire of pathogen‐derived molecules. Phylogenetic studies show lineage‐specific RLP expansions in Solanaceae and Poaceae, consistent with arms race‐driven diversification and potential subfunctionalisation or neofunctionalisation of paralogues (Chen et al. 2023). Taken together, RLPs illustrate the plant immune system's capacity for modular evolution: while conserved intracellular signalling partners ensure stability, flexible ligand‐binding domains allow adaptive fine‐tuning. Notably, several well‐characterised RLPs illustrate their direct applications in crop disease resistance breeding. For instance, tomato Cf‐9 and Cf‐4 confer resistance to C. fulvum (Jones et al. 1994; Thomas et al. 1997), and Ve1 mediates resistance to Verticillium spp. (Fradin et al. 2009; Fradin et al. 2011). In wild potato, SmELR recognises the conserved oomycete elicitor INF1, conferring resistance to P. infestans (Du et al. 2015). In monocots, OsRLP1 in rice contributes to defence against RBSDV (Zhang et al. 2021), while TaRLP1.1 in wheat enhances resistance to stripe rust (Jiang et al. 2013). In pepper ( Capsicum annuum ), functional studies of CaRLPs have shown that several family members are required for broad‐spectrum resistance against diverse pathogens, including R. solanacearum and P. capsici (Kang et al. 2022). Continued characterisation of RLPs in crops will be critical not only for understanding plant–pathogen co‐evolution but also for developing precision breeding strategies targeting durable disease resistance.

Potential Applications of RLPs in Disease‐Resistance Breeding

Climate change has significantly increased the frequency and severity of crop disease outbreaks. In pursuit of stable yields, the overuse of chemical pesticides has not only degraded agroecosystems but also raised food safety concerns, posing urgent challenges for sustainable agriculture, particularly in China. RLPs play critical roles in the early detection of pathogens and the initiation of immune responses. Advances in genomics and molecular biotechnology have facilitated the functional characterisation and application of RLPs in crop improvement programmes aimed at enhancing disease resistance. Efforts to enhance plant immunity have increasingly focused on engineering immune receptor modules, including RLPs and NLR proteins, to boost both PTI and ETI. For instance, in rice, the immune regulatory protein PICI1 promotes methionine synthesis and ethylene biosynthesis by stabilising methionine synthase, thereby strengthening basal immune responses. Conserved broad‐spectrum NLRs can protect PICI1 from pathogen effector interference, thus amplifying its function and contributing to a methionine–ethylene‐dependent resistance pathway (Zhai et al. 2021). Similarly, the conserved glycoside hydrolase XEG1, secreted by bacterial, fungal and oomycete pathogens, is recognised by several plant species including Nicotiana benthamiana, soybean and tomato. The receptor‐like protein RXEG1 mediates recognition of XEG1, functioning both as a sensor and a negative regulator of virulence. Understanding the RXEG1‐XEG1 interaction mechanism provides new molecular targets for engineering RLP‐based resistance and supports the development of ecofriendly biopesticides, such as immune‐activating compounds tailored to RLP pathways (Ma et al. 2015; Sun et al. 2022).

In addition to classical PRR signalling, recent advances in NLR resistosome biology offer novel opportunities for immune engineering. For example, calcium‐permeable CNL‐type resistosomes such as ZAR1, and downstream signalling molecules derived from TNL‐type resistosomes (e.g., ADP‐ribose and ATP), have been shown to activate defence pathways even in the absence of pathogen effectors. Some atypical TIR domain proteins can undergo NAD+/ATP‐induced self‐condensation, triggering immunity in a pathogen‐independent manner. These properties expand their utility across both PTI and ETI, as well as responses to abiotic stress, providing promising strategies for engineering broad‐spectrum resistance in cereals such as rice, wheat and maize (Song et al. 2024). In Arabidopsis, the functional dissection of RLPs such as RLP23, RLP30 and RLP52 has provided mechanistic insights into PRR‐co‐receptor complex formation, signal transduction, and downstream defence activation. These discoveries offer a genetic and molecular blueprint that can be translated into crops. Moreover, reverse‐genetic approaches in Arabidopsis have helped define conserved structural motifs within RLPs and their interaction interfaces with SOBIR1/BAK1, which can be exploited for synthetic receptor design. Combining RLP gene editing with inducible promoters or tissue‐specific expression systems may enable the development of crop varieties with fine‐tuned, durable resistance while minimising potential trade‐offs in growth or yield.

Conclusion

RLPs are indispensable components of the plant immune system, acting as extracellular sensors that link pathogen recognition to intracellular defence activation. Their functional reliance on co‐receptors such as SOBIR1 and BAK1 enables kinase‐independent signal transduction and positions them as modular hubs within broader immune networks. While comparative studies highlight their structural diversification and pathogen‐specific adaptations, much remains unknown about how RLPs discriminate ligands, assemble signalling complexes, and coordinate with intracellular receptors such as NLRs. Future efforts should focus on resolving these mechanistic questions and on translating RLP‐based immunity into crop improvement strategies. In particular, island domain reconfiguration, conserved island motifs, and PSKR1 homology represent emerging themes that redefine our understanding of RLP diversification and their potential in durable resistance breeding. Targeted manipulation of RLP pathways offers a compelling route to engineer broad‐spectrum, durable disease resistance under rapidly changing agricultural conditions.
