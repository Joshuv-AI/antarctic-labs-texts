Introduction

Four decades of war and armed conflict have created a complex and prolonged humanitarian crisis in Afghanistan. The country, with a population of approximately 41 million, is grappling with rapid economic decline, pervasive poverty, and climate shocks, driving emergency levels of food insecurity and unprecedented hunger.1 An estimated 15 million Afghans face acute food insecurity, and 13 million lack access to healthcare due to inadequate infrastructure and high costs.1 2 Healthcare facilities are chronically understaffed and under-resourced, with many consistently short of medicines and supplies.2 The Taliban’s takeover in August 2021 triggered an economic collapse, exacerbated by the withdrawal of foreign investments and restrictions on international banking transactions.1 The Taliban’s oppressive gender policies towards women and girls, including bans on work and education, have drawn widespread media attention and condemnation from the international community. In response, major foreign donors suspended billions of dollars in aid to Afghanistan.1 Over $9 billion in Afghan assets remains inaccessible, and humanitarian funding, which once accounted for 75% of public expenditures, has been severely restricted.1 Little attention, however, has been given to the human rights violations and health inequities facing ethnic and religious minoritised groups, especially the historically persecuted Hazara population.

The withdrawal of the US forces and the drastic reduction of foreign aid have disproportionately affected the Hazaras, who once again are facing widespread systemic discrimination and persecution under the Taliban regime.3 4 Ethno-religious motivated violence and discrimination against Hazaras, perpetuated by the Taliban regime, threaten Hazara health equity. The global health community must recognise ethnicity and religion as important social determinants of health and consider Hazara health equity a public health priority in Afghanistan.

Structural violence and systemic discrimination against Hazaras

The structural violence against Hazaras is rooted in longstanding patterns of intersecting ethnic and anti-Shia discrimination in a Sunni-majority country that has historically othered Hazaras as infidels, resulting in their political, social, and economic marginalisation.4,6 Hazaras are a minoritised ethnic and religious group in Afghanistan who predominantly practise Shia Islam and have distinct cultural and linguistic characteristics.5 They constitute Afghanistan’s third-largest ethnic group, comprising 10%–15% of the population.5 Hazaras have endured a long history of persecution and systemic discrimination, from the 19th-century genocide under ethnonationalist Pashtun ruler Abdur Rahman Khan, in which reports indicate over 60% of the Hazara population was exterminated, to the Taliban’s massacres in the 1990s.3,5

Following the Taliban’s return to power in 2021, the situation for Hazaras has deteriorated significantly.5 6 The Taliban’s persecution of Hazaras stems from ethnic and religious prejudice, reinforced by a broader ethnonationalist agenda to establish Pashtun nationalism.6 The de facto authorities have dismantled diversity and inclusion initiatives established under the previous government. The Taliban have systematically excluded Hazaras from government and leadership roles.6 The exclusion of Hazaras from the government is evident in the Taliban’s 33-member cabinet, which lacks Hazara representation.3 6 The current situation in Afghanistan’s judiciary system further illustrates the systemic exclusion of Hazaras. By 2022, all Shia Hazara judges and judiciary staff were replaced with Taliban appointees.3 6 Additionally, Hazaras are once again facing land confiscations and forced displacement, driven by the Taliban’s discriminatory policies on land dispute resolution.3 6 According to evidence gathered by the Canadian House of Commons Standing Committee on Foreign Affairs and International Development, thousands of Hazaras have been forcibly evicted from their ancestral homes and have become internally displaced since the regime change.3

The Taliban regime has not only failed to prevent but has also enabled deadly attacks by armed groups, including Islamic State-Khorasan, whose targeted attacks on predominantly Hazara schools, mosques and neighbourhoods have intensified since the Taliban’s return to power.3 6 The 2020 bombing of a Médecins Sans Frontières maternity ward located in a Hazara neighbourhood, which killed 16 Hazara mothers and injured several healthcare workers, highlights the disproportionate barriers to healthcare access faced by Hazara women.3 7 While the Taliban claim to protect minoritised groups to obtain international legitimacy, they have not taken meaningful actions to prevent violence against Hazaras. The political and economic disenfranchisement of Hazaras is further compounded by the inequitable distribution of international aid, with reports indicating aid is being diverted by the Taliban away from Hazara communities both by exerting pressure on the ground and through negotiations between the Taliban and international organisations.3

Hazara health inequities

Medical literature on the social determinants of health in Afghanistan and Hazara health inequities remains scarce. Ethnic and racial discrimination have a well-established link to poor health outcomes.8 The ongoing structural violence and systemic discrimination against Hazaras is likely to contribute to adverse health outcomes, including heightened risks of post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety and depression. Research involving Hazaras in the diaspora has documented collective trauma and poor mental health resulting from decades of targeted violence and institutionalised discrimination experienced in Afghanistan.9 Another pathway linking discrimination to poor health outcomes is the potential for experiences of discrimination to lead to reduced healthcare-seeking and adherence to medical care.8 A 2021 survey found that after the US withdrawal, Hazaras were three times more likely to avoid routine healthcare appointments and two times more likely to face difficulties accessing healthcare compared with other ethnic groups.10

Furthermore, as shown in other settings, land dispossession and displacement are assoicated with increased burden of communicable and non-communicable diseases.11 12 Forced displacement has well-documented long-term health consequences. Studies from other conflict-affected settings show that internally displaced persons experience poor physical and mental health outcomes decades after displacement.12 13 The ongoing displacement of Hazaras could lead to a disproportionate burden of infectious and chronic diseases among the Hazara population. Many Hazaras live in rural and remote regions with limited access to basic services.14 15 Over 50% of Hazara-majority provinces (Central Highlands) report unmet basic healthcare needs and have one of the lowest levels of primary care provision.14 15 The cumulative impact of displacement, socioeconomic marginalisation and targeted violence could exacerbate health vulnerabilities, reinforcing cycles of poverty and worsened health outcomes among Hazaras.

Hazaras as a health equity priority

There is a need to recognise ethnicity and religion as key determinants of health in Afghanistan to ensure that global health and humanitarian efforts meaningfully address the systemic violence and discrimination faced by Hazaras. Human rights-based approaches offer a critical pathway to addressing Hazara health inequities.16 International accountability mechanisms, such as treaty bodies and courts, can be leveraged to hold the Taliban accountable for their ongoing abuses and human rights violations against Afghanistan’s ethnic and religious minoritised groups.16 The international community must press the Taliban for taking actions to protect the Hazara community, safeguard their land rights, and mandate Hazara representation in Afghanistan’s governance structures. Historically, diplomatic efforts have focused on broad national security; future negotiations must explicitly include guarantees for Hazara security, political representation and equitable access to healthcare resources and economic opportunities. The international community, through the United Nations and the International Criminal Court, must document, investigate and prosecute crimes against Hazaras.

Sustained humanitarian support is also essential. International aid organisations must recalibrate their strategies to ensure aid reaches Hazara communities without reinforcing the Taliban’s discriminatory policies. Several strategies can be implemented to avoid reinforcing marginalisation of Hazaras through policy negotiations that fail to acknowledge their health equity and human rights. Aid could be channelled through local non-governmental and community-based organisations, as this approach allows resources to reach those in need while bypassing the Taliban’s corrupt national bureaucracies that divert aid away from its intended beneficiaries.17 18 Including Hazara stakeholders and ensuring diverse ethnic representation in the planning and dissemination of aid is essential to prevent bias in resource allocation.18 Moreover, monitoring and evaluation frameworks must be implemented to track the impact of aid distribution and prevent misallocation of resources.18 19 By implementing these strategies, aid organisations can help ensure international support advances equity and avoids perpetuating marginalisation of Hazaras.

Conclusion

Drawing on the evidence that ethnic discrimination exacerbates health inequities, this commentary underscores the need to recognise ethnicity and religion as important social determinants of health in Afghanistan. Structural violence creates social conditions that lead to a disproportionate burden of adverse health outcomes among Hazaras, reinforcing cycles of vulnerability and poor health. An equity framework that integrates ethnicity and religion as critical determinants of health is essential to ensure health and socioeconomic progress for all. Additionally, the international community must prioritise Hazaras as a health equity priority in Afghanistan, recognising the historical and ongoing structural violence that threatens their health and well-being.
