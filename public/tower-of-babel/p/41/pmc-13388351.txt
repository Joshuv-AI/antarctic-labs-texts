Predictive modeling correlates flow rate and residence time with measurement precision in two-detector Taylor dispersion analysis
Authors: Hillary D Bourger, Christopher A Baker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTaylor dispersion analysis (TDA) provides calibration-free measurements of particle or molecular size by characterizing band broadening induced by the interaction of diffusion and pressure-driven laminar flow. Appropriate flow for TDA analyses is conventionally defined by Taylor’s conditions, which set thresholds related to flow velocity (Pe ≥ 69) and residence time (τ > 2.5). Optimizing these parameters is complicated by the fact that they are covariant when detector position is a fixed instrumental constraint. Previous reports have reconsidered these conditions, but consensus is lacking on appropriate threshold values and on their relative contributions to measurement performance, especially in the context of capillary TDA with two detection points. Here, we describe a statistically driven experimental study of the interaction between residence time and flow velocity and the impact of this interaction on measurement precision in two-detector TDA. A six-detector array achieving 15 unique τ values per analysis was used to generate a dataset of 1200 TDA measurements across three volumetric flow rates. Two analytes, Alexafluor 532 (AF532) and R-phycoerythrin (RPE), were selected as models to bracket a pragmatic range of hydrodynamic radii (Rh) for biomolecular sizing. A strategy of iterative regression modeling with random subsampling was applied to generate a predictive model describing the effects of flow velocity and residence time on relative measurement precision. Experimental conditions based on the predictive model showed improved measurement precision for AF532 from 9 to 6% RSD, and for RPE from 24 to 12% RSD. External validation using non-training data showed precision improvements of 9.4% to 8.5% RSD for size measurements of a synthetic ssDNA oligonucleotide. Introduction

Taylor dispersion analysis (TDA) provides calibration-free measurements of particle or molecular size by characterizing band broadening induced by the interaction of diffusion and pressure-driven laminar flow. Performing TDA in fused silica capillaries allows analysis of nL sample volumes with short analysis times using the same selective and sensitive detection modes available for capillary electrophoresis (CE) [1–10], making TDA an attractive alternative to more common sizing techniques like dynamic light scattering or size-exclusion chromatography. Several important considerations for capillary TDA are discussed below, but a more thorough discussion is provided via a tutorial review authored by our group in 2021 [11].

Precise control of flow conditions is essential for measurement accuracy and precision in TDA. The conventional framework for defining appropriate flow conditions is based on two threshold criteria called the Taylor conditions. The first Taylor condition states that the rate of radial diffusion must be substantially greater than that of longitudinal diffusion such that the latter is negligible as compared to the former. This is accomplished with flow at sufficiently high Péclet number (Pe):

where u is the linear flow velocity, D is the analyte’s diffusion coefficient, and Rc is the radius of the cylindrical flow channel. Pe quantifies the ratio of advective transport to diffusive transport and is controlled experimentally via u. Taylor showed that Pe ≥ 69 gives a relative measurement error of 1% [12], while Cottet et al. reduced the threshold to Pe ≥ 40 for relative measurement errors of 3% [13].

The second Taylor condition aims to ensure that an analyte resides in the appropriate flow conditions long enough for measurable Taylor dispersion to occur. The dimensionless time ratio (τ) is used to quantify this threshold condition:

where tR is residence time. τ quantifies the ratio of residence time to the analyte’s characteristic radial diffusion time and is controlled experimentally by the interaction between u and the length of the flow system over which Taylor dispersion is observed. τ and Pe are, therefore, covariant because they are inversely proportional to one another with respect to changes in u when the length of the flow system is constrained. This covariance represents a complication for optimizing TDA measurement conditions.

Taylor set a threshold of τ > 2.5 [12], while Cottet al. defined a “Taylor regime” with onset at the reduced threshold τ > 1.25, below which they described a convective transport regime resulting in non-Gaussian peak shapes [13]. Harada et al. suggested that Gaussian peak deformation occurs at τ < 0.37 [14], and Latunde-Dada et al. have shown successful TDA sizing in the convective regime [15]. These works give important insights on the dynamics of mass transport under various flow conditions, and they illustrate a lack of consensus on the precise role τ plays in influencing TDA measurement performance.

Capillary TDA is often performed using CE instrumentation. Although there are reports of modifying CE instrumentation for multi-point detection [16, 17], CE instruments typically have a single detection point, and capillary TDA measurements are commonly made via single-point detection [18–22]. In these cases, residence time is defined by the distance between the capillary inlet and the detector, and band broadening is characterized under the assumptions of continuous, stable flow rates and an infinitely narrow injection band. These assumptions do not accurately represent experimental conditions and can therefore result in measurement errors. While mathematical corrections have been presented for addressing these inaccuracies [23], a more robust approach is to perform TDA on the basis of band broadening observed between two detection points [1, 15, 16, 24–27]. TDA with two-point detection allows continuous, stable flow to be established prior to peak characterization at the first detector and eliminates the assumption of infinitely narrow injection plugs by measuring the change in peak width between the first and second detection points. In this case, tR, and subsequently τ, is defined by the interaction of u and the distance between detection points. This subtle reframing of the τ definition presents further ambiguity on the role of τ in defining TDA performance because it does not consider effects of band broadening occurring prior to the first detector.

The lack of consensus on the appropriate τ threshold, the complexities associated with τ Pe covariance, and the reframing of τ in the context of two-detector TDA motivated the current work. Here, we describe a statistically driven experimental investigation of the interaction of residence time and flow velocity and its impact on measurement precision in two-detector TDA. A six-detector array achieving 15 unique two-detector τ values per analysis was used to generate a dataset of 1200 TDA measurements across three volumetric flow rates. Two model analytes, Alexafluor 532 (AF532) and R-phycoerythrin (RPE), were selected to bracket a range of hydrodynamic radii (Rh) of practical value for biomolecular size characterization. A strategy of iterative regression modeling with random subsampling was applied to generate a single predictive model describing the effects of flow velocity and residence time on relative measurement precision.

Materials and methods

Reagents and materials

AF532, RPE, and the ssDNA oligonucleotide (5′HEX-ACAGGTTTGCGT-3′) were purchased from Thermo-Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA). Phosphate buffered saline (PBS) was purchased from Fisher Scientific (Suwannee, GA). Fused silica capillaries were from Molex (Lisle, IL). All aqueous solutions were prepared using ultrapure DI water.

Detector optical design

Laser excitation was provided by a 532 nm collimated laser diode module (CPS532, Thorlabs, Newton, NJ) focused by a plano convex lens (LA1472, Thorlabs Inc., Newton, NJ). Fluorescence emission was collected via two 6.0 mm N-BK7 ball lenses (Edmund Optics Inc., Barrington, NJ) and a 10 mm diameter mirror (Edmund Optics Inc., Barrington, NJ). To enable 6 detectors to share 3 photomultiplier tubes (PMTs), a strategy of beam coupling by 3D printed periscope was utilized as previously described [24]. Briefly, fluorescence emission from each pair of adjacent detectors was combined as side-by-side beams via a 3D printed periscope, and the time domain signal from the detector pair was recorded by a single PMT. For each pair of detectors, one laser was pulsed at 73 Hz while the other was pulsed at 127 Hz. After digitization of the time domain PMT signal, frequency filtering by Fourier transform was performed to resolve the recorded signal originating from each detector. To accommodate increased detector spacing as compared to previous work, periscopes were modified with biconvex lenses to improve optical transfer efficiency. Three Hamamatsu H7828-50 PMTs were each fitted with a 533/17 nm OD 6 notch filter (NF533/17, Thorlabs Inc., Newton, NJ), a 580/10 nm OD 6 bandpass filter (FB580-10, Thorlabs Inc., Newton, NJ), a 550 nm OD 6 long-pass filter (FELH0550, Thorlabs Inc., Newton, NJ), a 50 mm focusing lens (LA1131, Thorlabs Inc., Newton, NJ), and a 532 nm OD 5 notch filter. One PMT was coupled to each periscope coupling adjacent detectors, and frequency modulation-demodulation was used to resolve two detection channels per PMT. Signal was acquired using a USB-6341 DAQ (National Instruments, Austin, TX). The optical detection system is described further and illustrated in the supporting information (Figure S-1).

TDA measurements

Capillary conditioning and sample preparation

Prior to each experimental session, the capillary was conditioned by flushing for 5 min each with 0.1 M NaOH, deionized water, and the running TDA buffer (PBS, pH 7.5). Between replicate injections, the capillary was flushed with 0.1 M NaOH and TDA buffer for 1 min each. Training analytes were 20 nM AF532 or 5 nM RPE in PBS. The external validation analyte was 125 nM ssDNA oligonucleotide prepared in TE buffer (10 mM Tris and 1 mM EDTA, pH = 8.0).

Experimental design and data acquisition

Training set measurements of AF532 and RPE were conducted at three target linear flow velocities (0.45, 1.85, 4.7 mm s−1). Flow rates were controlled by calibrating the pressure applied at the outlet eductor against the resulting linear flow rate. Minor fluctuations in flow velocity were observed in the experimental data due to uncontrolled variations in the pressure system. Each individual datapoint was analyzed on the basis of its observed flow velocity rather than the nominal target velocity for that experimental condition. Flow rate and sample injection were controlled via a 3D-printed eductor as described previously [27]. To generate a dataset spanning a broad range of residence times, a fixed injection duration (2.5 s) was applied across all flow rates, resulting in injection volumes that scaled proportionally with flow velocity (9, 37, and 92 nL). Training data was collected with n = 8 replicates per combination of experimental conditions (ntotal = 134 injections). While this work develops a statistical model of measurement precision as a function, in part, of flow rate, ANOVA of the training data revealed no statistically significant relationship between mean size and flow rate conditions (p > 0.05).

External validation measurements were performed at four target linear flow velocities (1.5, 2.5, 2.8, and 4.5 mm s−1) with corresponding injection volumes of 29, 50, 54, and 58 nL, respectively (ntotal = 56 injections). Peak variance and diffusion coefficients were calculated from the resulting taylorgrams using OriginPro Version 2026 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA).

Regression modeling

Regression modeling and statistical analyses were performed in R using the RStudio IDE (Posit Software, Boston, MA). Linear regression utilized the base R lm() method, while partial least squares (PLS) regression utilized the plsr() method from the pls library. The full training data set (n = 1200 TDA measurements) was divided into 80% used for model fitting and 20% reserved for model validation using the base R sample() method for random sampling. This 80/20 split was performed uniquely for each iteration of regression, thus no two regression models in this study were trained nor validated on identical subsamples. Each regression strategy was iterated 500 times; each of the resulting 500 models per regression strategy was used to predict measurement precision for the reserved 20% subsample, and the regression models were evaluated on the basis of the R2 resulting from the linear regression of predicted vs actual measurement precision. Code was generated with partial assistance from the Gemini LLM web interface (https://gemini.google.com/app, Alphabet, Inc., Mountain View, CA), with all code reviewed and validated for accuracy by the corresponding author.

Results and discussion

Heteroscedasticity of measurement precision

To study the relationship between TDA measurement performance and analyte residence time, we designed a unique 6-detector array of 3D printed LIF detectors positioned irregularly along the length of a fused silica capillary (see supporting information, Figure S-1). Irregular detector spacing allowed 6 detectors to provide 15 unique 2-detector spacings (see Table 1). This approach allowed each analyte injection to produce 15 two-detector TDA measurements each with unique residence times (See supporting information, Figure S-2) while minimizing measurement variation associated with injection-to-injection irreproducibility. Replicate measurements of two analytes (AF532 and RPE) were measured at 3 flow rates and 3 injection volumes, generating a total dataset of 1200 two-detector TDA measurements.

Figure 1 shows Rh vs. τ for two-detector TDA measurements at three flow rates for AF532 and RPE (Fig. 1 A and B, respectively). Improved measurement precision was observed with increased τ when comparing measurements of the same analyte performed at a specific flow rate. When comparing these heteroscedastic trends across various flow rates, however, measurement precision was not independently influenced by τ. The heteroscedasticity suggested that relative measurement precision was appropriately modeled as the natural logarithm of the squared residuals, which we refer to here as relative precision. Plotting this metric of relative precision as a function of Pe and τ (Fig. 1C and D for AF532 and RPE, respectively) showed a high degree of structure in the data, suggesting systematic interaction between Pe and τ, but the underlying relationship was not evident. Importantly, ANOVA of relative precision as a function of detector pair found no significant relationship between detector identity and relative precision, indicating that flow rate and residence time were more important than potential systematic errors associated with specific detectors in the array.

Regression modeling

Initially, we aimed to model relative precision by multiple linear regression against τ, Pe and their first-order interaction. 500 regression models were generated using unique random subsamples of 80% of the total dataset (n = 960). The predictive performance of each regression model was quantified as the R2 of the predicted vs. actual plot when the model was used to predict measurement precision for the reserved 20% of data not used in the regression model fit. Figure 2A (gray bars) shows the histogram of R2 values from all multiple linear regression models, with mean predictive performance of R2 = 0.18 ± 0.04. This poor predictive performance was not surprising, since Pe and τ are both influenced by flow rate and therefore demonstrate a high degree of covariance.

To improve regression in the presence of covariance, we pursued PLS regression of relative precision as a function of the four experimentally controlled factors which influenced Pe, τ, and peak variance: Analyte identity quantified as the nominal diffusion coefficient (D.nom), injection volume (inj.vol), residence time between detectors (time), and linear flow velocity (velocity). 500 PLS regression models were generated and evaluated in the same way described above for multiple linear regression. Figure 2A (turquoise bars) shows the histogram of R2 value for all PLS models that regressed relative precision against D.nom, inj.vol, time, velocity, their second-order effects, and all possible two- and three-factor interactions of first-order variables, totaling 18 factors. This modeling approach improved predictive performance to R2 = 0.68 ± 0.04. Figure 2B shows the variable’s importance in prediction (VIP) score for the 18 factors (turquoise bars) averaged across 500 PLS models. To refine the PLS modeling strategy, terms with VIP scores below 0.9 were eliminated which reduced the number of model factors from 18 to 11. A further 500 PLS regression models were generated using 11 model terms with no impact on predictive performance (Fig. 2A, red bars), while showing moderate stability of VIP scores for the retained model terms (Fig. 2B, red bars).

Mean predictive model generation

To reduce 500 PLS models to a single consensus model, each of the 500 11-factor PLS models was linearized and the coefficients of 500 linear equations were geometrically averaged (see supporting information, Figures S-3 through S-5). The resulting linear model was used to predict measurement precision for 500 random subsamples of 20% of the full dataset. The mean PLS model showed substantially reduced predictive performance of R2 = 0.51 ± 0.05 (Fig. 2A, beige bars) compared to the population of 500 11-term PLS models. Thus, a single mean model representing the 500-model population was not feasible, and a strategy for refining the PLS model population to favor models with high predictive performance was pursued.

Of the initial 500 PLS models, 76 models gave R2 more than 1 standard deviation above the mean of the population. Each of those 76 models was validated via 500 additional rounds of random 20% subsampling, with the distribution of resulting R2 values shown in Fig. 2C (beige bars). ANOVA on the full set of R2 values (n = 3.8 × 104) revealed that 23 models returned R2 with a small but statistically significant (p < 0.05) improvement over the 76 model population. The distribution of R2 values from the 23 best performing models is shown in Fig. 2C (red bars, n = 1.15 × 104).

All 23 best performing models were linearized, and the model coefficients of the resulting linear equations were tabulated (see supporting information, Table S-1). A mean predictive model was generated by linear averaging of the model coefficients across these 23 models. While the model coefficients showed various degrees of stability (see RSD% in Table S-1), we interpreted instability as a direct result of high covariance inherent to the model, and therefore all model coefficients were preserved in the final predictive model. Model validation

To simplify model predictions, we interpreted the model as response surfaces. Figure 3 shows predicted relative precision on a color axis from blue (low residuals, high precision) to red (high residuals, low precision) as a function of flow velocity and residence time. For clarity of interpretation, the D.nom model term has been translated to Rh via the Stokes-Einstein equation. Predicted measurement precision as a function of operating parameters is shown across the Rh range of 0.5–6 nm, corresponding to the interpolated Rh range between the model analytes used to generate the predictive model. Injection volume is not shown in Fig. 3 because it has no discernible effect on the relative shape of the response surfaces, despite a small but statistically significant effect on the absolute magnitude of predicted residuals. We therefore validated the predictive model performance on the basis of optimal velocity and residence time, which closely aligns with the conventional approach that considers τ and Pe as determinants of measurement performance.

Response surfaces as a function of Rh. The prediction model (see Table 2) yields response surfaces showing relative measurement precision (color map) as a function of flow velocity, residence time, and Rh which can be used to predict operating conditions for two-detector TDA with improved precision. Effects of injection volume are not shown, having only a small impact on absolute predicted precision and no discernible effect on the relative shape of the response surfaces. The trend suggests that increased flow rates benefit measurement precision across the full interpolated range of Rh

While size-dependent changes in optimal operating conditions are subtle, in general the model predicts a broader range of near-optimal velocity-residence time combinations for smaller analytes, with flow velocity becoming a more dominant affector of measurement precision as analyte Rh increases.

To internally validate the predictive model, experimental data points from the training data were overlaid onto their respective predicted response surfaces, and subsets were visually clustered on the basis of their proximity to the predicted optimal operating conditions (Fig. 4). For AF532 (Fig. 4A), a precision improvement from 9 to 6% RSD was observed for measurements made in the sub-optimal versus near-optimal operating conditions, respectively. In the case of RPE (Fig. 4B), a more dramatic improvement of 24% to 12% RSD was observed. Importantly, this visual clustering approach was not statistically driven, but clusters were pragmatically selected by the following criteria: (1) The near-optimum cluster was defined as data nearest to the predicted optimum bounded by inherent gaps in the experimental parameter space separating those points from others; (2) sub-optimal data was selected from outside the near-optimal cluster to closely match the number of data points in the near-optimal cluster, again following inherent gaps in the experimental parameter space; (3) sub-optimal data was selected to closely match residence time of the near-optimal cluster to (a) illustrate the relative importance of flow velocity in the prediction results, (b) highlight performance improvement in the context of similar analysis times, and (c) sample data from regions of the response surface nearest to the predicted optimum, where we anticipated higher model accuracy. Within these pragmatic guidelines, the internal validation results indicate the predictive utility of the model toward improving experimental design for two-detector TDA measurements.

The model was further externally validated with measurements not derived from the training dataset and having Rh intermediate to the two training analytes. A synthetic 12-base ssDNA oligonucleotide labeled at the 5′ terminus with Hexachlorofluorescein was sized under various flow rate conditions, and the results overlaid on the appropriate response surface (Fig. 5). In this case, the improvement from sub-optimal to near-optimal clusters was more modest than with the internal validation, improving from 9.4% to 8.5% RSD. While this more modest improvement may indicate a degree of overfitting in the predictive model, it also suggests that there is utility to interpolating the model to Rh values bracketed by the training data. Furthermore, the data appears to indicate decreased precision with increasing Rh, although the response surface model has insufficient degrees of freedom to statistically evaluate this observation. We hypothesize that this effect captures the relative cost of reduced τ with increasing Rh, but more work is needed to investigate this hypothesis. In all three validation cases, we see a strong dependence on flow rate and evidence that τ does not independently influence measurement precision.

Conclusion

Two-detector TDA improves molecular size determinations by eliminating inaccurate assumptions inherent to single-detector TDA. Two-detector TDA measurements that violate Taylor’s conditions of residence time are found in the literature showing little or no apparent cost in terms of measurement performance. This motivated us to look more closely at the interaction of flow velocity and residence time as determinants of relative measurement precision, which we accomplished by combining a unique fluorescence detector array with a statistically driven empirical model of operating conditions and their effects on relative measurement precision. We observed a complex relationship between flow velocity and residence time, the dynamics of which change subtly as a function of the measured Rh. The resulting response surfaces suggested that experimental designs for two-detector TDA may benefit from prioritizing flow velocity over residence time, offering the compound benefit of improved sizing precision at reduced analysis times.

While this empirical model was internally and externally validated, there are caveats to our work that prevent extrapolating these findings to generalizable rules or best practices for two-detector TDA. First, capillary radius plays an important role in the mass transport dynamics of TDA, but this work used only a single capillary dimension (100 μm i.d.). A robust integration of these findings with the established framework of Taylor’s conditions will require more extensive study on the effects of capillary radius, and more extensive modeling at more intermediate Rh values. Second, the empirical model was built for molecular sizing in the range Rh = 0.5–6 nm. One of the key strengths of TDA is its wide dynamic range, extending to nano- and microparticle analysis. We do not anticipate that the models described here extrapolate to those size regimes. Despite these caveats, this work has demonstrated a statistically driven empirical approach to improved measurement precision in two-detector TDA of molecular analytes in a size range practical for biomolecular analyses. The findings suggest that we reconsider the utility of strict threshold values of τ and Pe, and indicate that further work is needed to understand the role and interactions of flow velocity and residence time in molecular sizing by TDA.
