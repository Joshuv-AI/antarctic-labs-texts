For which entrustable professional activities must medical students be prepared if unsupervised patient care without further training is an expectation? An international Global South study
Authors: Olle ten Cate, Daniel Nel, Marije P Hennus, Susanne Peters, Gustavo Salata Romao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTraining medical students requires objectives that are often translated into frameworks of competencies. Since the introduction of entrustable professional activities (EPAs), these tasks or ‘units of professional practice’, originally conceived for postgraduate training, define what residents must be prepared to do unsupervised. Nowadays, EPAs are also applied to undergraduate training, with the prospect of entering residency. However, in many countries, especially in the Global South, a substantial number of medical graduates will be working in healthcare with little or no supervision and no further training soon or ever. EPAs for these conditions cannot be copied from undergraduate medical education EPA frameworks in Global North countries.MethodsWe conducted a generative investigation to identify and elaborate EPAs for Global South countries who must train students for unsupervised general practice. We included 39 medical educators from 13 Global South countries and 17 specialties in either one of two online focus group sessions using a nominal group technique (NGT) or as Delphi panel member. Results from the two NGT sessions were merged and fed into the two-round Delphi investigation.ResultsA framework of 11 EPAs resulted, each with an elaborate description (specification, setting and limitations).ConclusionThis framework of undergraduate medical education Global South EPAs differs in its nature and specifications from existing Global North EPAs. The authors do not pretend universality for all Global South countries with graduates who face expectations of unsupervised practice but present it to support countries that consider introducing the model of entrustable professional activities.Keywords: Health policy, Health education and promotion

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Entrustable professional activities (EPAs), a new core focus of competency-based medical training, are rapidly being introduced worldwide. However, EPAs for medical school graduates assume a continuation with postgraduate residency. EPAs for schools delivering graduates expected to start clinical work unsupervised, in many countries of the Global South, have not been defined and described.

Introduction

Competency-based medical education proposes a training model of fixed standards and flexible pathways1 and aims to train professionals at a recognised level of competence.2 Entrustable professional activities (EPAs) were introduced in 2005 to bridge the gap between competency frameworks and daily clinical practice.3 Since then, EPAs have been applied in a variety of health professions in several countries.4,7 An EPA has been defined as ‘a unit of professional practice or profession-specific task that can be entrusted to a learner, once he or she has demonstrated the acquisition of the required competencies, knowledge, skills, and attitudes’.8 As EPAs represent the typical activities of a sector, unit or health service that contribute to providing care, a clear and precise description of an EPA is essential for its use as a reference for training and assessment.9 The acquisition of competencies occurs progressively, and the further a student develops in performing an EPA, the lower the level of required supervision can be.10 The use of supervision scales for EPAs can organise training and assessment and should provide a safer environment for patients and learners.

EPAs were initially conceived for postgraduate medical education, marking the transition to unsupervised practice, and subsequently also introduced in undergraduate medical education (UME), supported by various guidelines.8 9 11 12 The first national UME-EPA project was conducted in the USA and focused on Core EPAs for Entering Residency,5 thus defined EPAs not to prepare for unsupervised practice, but for ‘day 1’ in a postgraduate training programme. This ‘CEPAER’ project inspired other countries, such as Canada, Switzerland and Ireland, in their definition of undergraduate EPAs.6 13 14 However, this development typically reflects the ‘Global North’[Global North and Global South countries are not unequivocally defined. We adhere to the UNCTAD definition (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_North_and_Global_South)]. In many other countries, medical graduates do not directly start postgraduate medical training or attend such training at all. Furthermore, while licensed physicians in Global North countries do not or cannot provide patient care without supervision, in many less affluent countries, particularly in the Global South, physicians are often expected to be ready to practice without supervision directly after medical school. This may be in primary care with limited medical facilities and infrastructure, or in hospital settings.

While across all countries the populations speak of ‘doctors’ or ‘physicians’, the work these professionals are expected to be ready to perform directly after undergraduate medical education, that is, when students become doctors, differs. In terms of EPAs and general daily work, doctors are defined differently in different countries. This should have implications for medical curricula. The EPAs for which students are being trained must be geared to local healthcare needs. Simply copying EPAs from earlier projects in some countries to different countries appears inadequate, specifically if these are countries of the Global North and the Global South, respectively. Mainstream literature is scarce regarding EPAs for graduation in low-income and middle-income countries15 as is the case with medical education in general.16 In many countries in Latin America, Africa, the Middle East and part of Asia, postgraduate medical training is not mandatory, and supervision is not required for physicians without postgraduate degrees.17 In these regions, medical students must be prepared for unsupervised clinical practice and ready to perform all medical activities expected of a general practitioner without advanced training, including attendance in rural areas, hospitals and emergency rooms.17 However, there is generally no clear description of the essential professional activities with which newly trained physicians should be entrusted. Consequently, defining EPAs in these contexts requires a distinct approach. In addition, the extent to which EPAs in these countries differ from those in more affluent nations, or even among less economically developed countries themselves remains unclear. This study aims to define UME-EPAs for countries where the expectation is that graduates from medical school are ready to practice without supervision and without postgraduate medical training. In addition, the study aims to explore variations between such countries.

Methods

Overview

We employed a multistage procedure to identify relevant EPAs and seek international consensus, by combining focus group sessions using a nominal group technique with a modified Delphi procedure.18 [We use the word focus group. While this use is sometimes discouraged in NGT techniques, suggesting FG uses group dynamics, and the nominal group technique (NGT) works with independent opinions, we believe that both overlap. NGT includes a phase of discussion and has a setting similar to FG. We prefer to call this a focus group with a NGT procedure embedded.] Given the complexity of the process, we created figure 1 explaining the steps in this procedure and the roles of the research team, the focus group panels 1 and 2, and the Delphi panel.

Steps in the research process of the project.

Focus group participants

Participants in the focus groups were selected to be knowledgeable about EPAs, sufficiently fluent in English and well acquainted with the demands of recent medical graduates working in unsupervised conditions.11 We recruited among alumni of the English language International online course ‘Ins and Outs of EPAs’ (about 350). While the vast majority is from the Global North, from each of 17 Global South countries, two alumni were approached to participate. All those interested were sent a brief biographical questionnaire to understand their involvement with recent graduates in unsupervised clinical roles, and information about the concept of EPA (including AMEE Guide 1409) and the project’s purpose.

Focus group procedure and data analysis

Two independent online focus groups were held using an NGT to generate an initial set of EPAs. The NGT is a structured brainstorming method to generate ideas and come to an agreement on priority, that is, relative importance of items. The NGT procedure consists of four stages: (i) individual idea generation, (ii) sharing of ideas, (iii) discussion/clarification of ideas, and (iv) individual voting, usually anonymously.19 The procedure was carried out online in two synchronous virtual meetings, 2 months apart, using Zoom Video Communications Inc. software. Zoom’s recording option was used during the group sessions to capture subtleties in the discussion. The 2-hour virtual meetings were led by a facilitator with international experience in developing EPAs (OtC). The trigger question was ‘identify and list EPAs that medical graduates without further training be asked to perform in your country without supervision’. Microsoft Word was used to visualise EPA ideas and suggestions with the support of a research assistant (SP), as a virtual NGT flip chart with screen sharing.

After each meeting, the research team convened to evaluate and edit the EPA list to arrive at EPAs with a specification and limitations.9 This was subsequently sent to all participants of the session for a member check and to apply the EQual Rubric Tool to all listed EPAs. This tool is built in Excel, based on a method created by Taylor et al.20 The tool allows to rate the EPA for quality and structure and to add detailed comments. The tool asks for a quality rating (using a 5-point Likert scale) on 14 features of each EPA and leads to aggregated average scores in three dimensions (EPAs as discrete unit of practice; Entrustable, Essential and Important professional tasks; EPAs’ curricula roles) and an overall score. A quality benchmark for overall score is >4.0. This procedure was conducted separately for each focus group.

The two sets of resulting EPAs with evaluation information were merged and processed by the research team to create a new list of EPAs with specifications and limitations as the input for the modified Delphi study phase.

Delphi participants

All focus group panellists were asked, through snowballing, to invite experts from their own country with substantial experience as clinical educators, and preferably already knowledgeable about EPAs. In addition, a few others were selected from the list of the international online EPA course alumni.

Delphi procedure and data analysis

A modified Delphi technique was applied to obtain consensus among experts on EPAs derived from the focus groups, through iterative consultation without interaction among participants.18 The communication with the participants happened through emails and SurveyMonkey was used as a survey tool.

The Delphi round 1 questionnaire included (a) ethics questions related to informed consent to participate, (b) biographical questions (age, gender, specialty, country, email address and focused expertise). Next, we asked for (c) a prevalence question for each EPA (‘Please estimate how frequently, in your country, doctors without specialist training and certification perform the following activities unsupervised (ie, a supervisor or specialist not present at healthcare facility)’ with anchor options Never-Seldom-Regularly-Always). Then, we asked (d) for each EPA to support two statements: (i) ‘This EPA is an essential task for medical graduates in my country (ie every medical graduate should be ready to do this without a supervisor or specialist present in the healthcare facility; this EPA must be included in the list)’; (ii) ‘The description of this EPA is clear’, both with Likert anchor options Strongly Agree-Agree-Neutral-Disagree-Strongly Disagree. Finally, we added space for additional comments per EPA.

For Delphi round 2, the anonymised results of the first Delphi round were sent to the panellists to re-rate the EPAs for a prevalence score as well as re-rate them for essentialness and clarity using the same scales. In this round, we explicitly asked panellists to comment on any discrepancies between prevalence score and essentialness score.

In both rounds, a Content Validity Index (CVI) was calculated for essentialness and a Clarity Index (CI) for clarity and all comments were reviewed. Both indices are measures of agreement among panellists. A CVI (number of ‘Agree’ + ‘Strongly Agree’ divided by total) lower than 0.8 for essentialness was considered a reason to discuss the retention of the EPA, and lower than 0.8 CI for clarity a reason to edit the EPA based on comments received.21 Likewise, we calculated a Prevalence Index (PI) (number of ‘Agree’ + ‘Strongly Agree’ divided by total) related to prevalence as estimated by the participant.

Considering the potential medical culture and practice differences between participating countries, EPAs with low CVIs we considered removing from the standard list, but keeping them as additional EPAs for countries where these activities are relevant.

Ethics

Ethical review was sought with the Ethical Review Board of the Netherlands Association for Medical Education. The NVMO-ERB reviewed the study but reported not to be competent to provide ethical approval as none of the study participants was from the Netherlands. The research protocol guidelines of NMVO-ERB were followed and the study adhered to the Helsinki Declaration principles.

Participants were invited by email, received an information letter about the EPAs concept and structure, the project proposal, aims, methods, and the tasks assigned to the participants, followed by a link to an electronic informed consent form. All participants consented with the study, knowing that it would not be anonymous (both during the focus groups session, and in the Delphi study, to enable follow-up). Time investments were about 2 hours+preparation for the focus group and about 3 hours per Delphi round. Note that these regard different groups of study participants. No monetary or other benefits were provided.

Patient and public involvement: there were no patients involved in this study and public involvement would regard those responsible for, or consumers of, medical training. The results of this study should pave the way for such involvement in next curricular steps, wherever the results of this study are being used, be it locally, regionally or nationally.

Reflexivity

The research team covered expertise that was considered essential for the conduction of data collection and analysis. OtC is a medical education specialist and expert in all EPA-related matters, DN is a general surgeon involved in EPA implementation in South Africa, MPH is a paediatric intensivist and educator in the Netherlands with expertise in national EPA development and implementation, SP is a recent medical graduate in the Netherlands and GSR is ObGyn educator involved in EPA implementation in Brazil. For more details, see online supplemental file ‘Structured reflexivity statements for BMJ-GH submission’.

Results

Composition of panels

The focus groups consisted of 8 and 5 panel members respectively (6 females, 7 males), from Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, India, Iran, Mexico, Pakistan, Peru, South Africa and Sudan, with backgrounds in Paediatrics, Dentistry, Family Medicine, Internal Medicine and Emergency Medicine, including medical education specialists. All had attended the International Ins and Outs of EPAs course.22

The Delphi panel consisted of 26 (round 1) and 21 (round 2) members (9 females, 17 males). Five members were lost to follow-up in round 2. The panel members were from Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Cuba, Iran, Mexico, Peru, Saudi Arabia and South Africa, with backgrounds in anaesthesiology, cardiology, endocrinology, family medicine, intensive care, internal medicine, nephrology, neonatology, neurology, neurosurgery, ObGyn, ophthalmology, otolaryngology, paediatrics and public health. Their experience and knowledge related to EPAs were self-rated as extensive (n=12) or moderate (n=14). Their experience and knowledge related to working in general practice unsupervised without further training were self-rated as extensive (n=16), moderate (n=6) or little (n=4).

Results of the nominal group technique sessions

Focus group 1 yielded a raw list of 40 EPAs and focus group 2 a list of 30 EPAs. The procedures to restructure the lists of EPAs by lumping and splitting were not done by the groups but within the research team. Many EPAs were small and related to other EPAs, enabling merging, and leading to shorter lists, each with smaller activities that were incorporated into the specifications of the EPAs. This led to 10 and 9 EPAs for focus group 1 and focus group 2, respectively (online supplemental tables A1 and A2), each with a title, specification and limitations.9 These lists were first fed back for member check and EQual quality evaluation to the focus group members. Six of the focus group 1 members (75%) and two of the focus group 2 members (40%) responded. All EPAs in both groups show mean EQual scores higher than the recommended norm (>4.0) (individual range: 3.43–5.00, across 78 scores) (online supplemental tables B1 and B2).20

Results of the Delphi study

Next, the research team processed all narrative comments of all respondents, merged the two lists and created 11 EPAs described with specifications and limitations as input for the first round of the Delphi study. Online supplemental table C shows how EPAs 1, 2, 4, 9 and 10 scored high on prevalence (>0.7), 3, 5 and 6 scored medium (0.5–0.7), and 7, 8 and 11 scored low (<0.5) on prevalence. All EPAs except 8 and 11 were considered essential (CVI>0.8), and all descriptions were rated as clear. Even EPAs 8 and 11 score still relatively high on essentialness (both 0.76).

The research team processed comments from all EPAs to improve clarity. All EPAs were left in for round 2, considering the diversity among respondents and countries and that none had an essentialness score below 0.76. As we were curious to understand the seeming disparity between low prevalence scores and high essentialness CVI scores for some EPAs (online supplemental table A2), we asked respondents to explain such disparities in the free space provided. We adapted the order of EPAs based on the CVI score in round 1. We moved the ‘setting’ of the EPA execution from the title to the specification of the EPA, and we adapted specifications of the EPA only in cases where respondent suggestions apparently seemed to improve the description (CI scores did not force us to do so).

After this round, we found (table 1) no low, and four medium prevalence scores (EPAs 6, 9, 10 and 11). All EPAs, except for two (EPAs 8 and 9), showed CVIs at or above the cut-off value of 0.8, and all had high CI scores. Discrepancies between essentialness and prevalence remained for EPAs 6 and, to some extent, 10 and 11. Explanations provided for these discrepancies (see online supplemental tables D1–11) included: (EPA 6:) ‘some procedures listed are prevalent, others not’; ‘students are not adequately trained now, but there is a great need’, (EPA 10:) ‘physicians are not expected to do child deliveries but need to assist’; ‘they need to take care of newborns, remove retained placenta if needed and refer to specialised care if needed’ and (EPA 11:) ‘graduates must have more knowledge than currently, even if they work under supervision’.

Discussion

Worldwide, medical students must be trained to meet the expectations of competent practitioners ready for license. Medical practice for licensed physicians, however, varies significantly across regions and countries based on differences in educational trajectories.23 The objectives of undergraduate medical education have traditionally been dominated by the Global North, where unsupervised practice directly after medical school is exceptional.23 However, this is not the case in many other parts of the world.

Using the concept of EPAs, we sought to define what medical students, in countries of the Global South, should be prepared for, anticipating that they may become employed to work with little or no supervision, either before commencing with postgraduate training or without a prospect of postgraduate training at all. With the help of almost 40 selected educators acquainted with this practice from a large variety of Global South countries (Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Cuba, India, Iran, Mexico, Pakistan, Peru, Saudi Arabia, South Africa and Sudan), and 17 medical specialties using different panels, and combining various procedures (nominal group technique, EQual Rubric evaluation, modified Delphi method), we arrived at 11 EPAs that may serve as a basis for curriculum development in the Global South (table 1, figure 1 and online supplemental material). These Global South EPAs (GS-EPAs) are generally more comprehensive than EPAs formulated for western countries. Whereas ‘Gather a history and perform a physical examination’ or ‘Prioritise a differential diagnosis following a clinical encounter’ are typical EPAs for UME in the USA, Canada and Switzerland,5 24 25 the GS-EPAs, such as ‘Providing care to non-hospitalised adult patients presenting with a new complaint’, reflect a broader responsibility. The GS-EPAs for physicians align with the recently published WHO competency framework for universal health coverage,26 which describes the ‘practice activities’ of all levels of healthcare workers.

We do not intend to present a universal framework of EPAs for the Global South.

Instead, our goal is to provide support for countries considering the adoption of the EPA model. Our study was limited to educators who had some background training (focus groups) or otherwise informed (Delphi panel) about EPAs, in a limited number of countries and acquainted with their own regional practices, but who cannot be regarded to speak for other regions or countries. In addition, we found differences among Global South (GS) countries, based on local prevalence, structure of healthcare, economic conditions, and legal and regulatory restrictions.

There may be debate whether even the concept of ‘Global South EPAs’ should be used as a terminology, because of the diversity between GS countries, and also because this may perpetuate the suggestion of systemic differences with Global North (GN) countries for education, while rather, each country may also be regarded as unique. However, we could not find better comprehensive terminology to qualify countries as a group that we explored and that are familiar with training doctors to be prepared for unsupervised clinical practice. Certainly, there may be GS countries which differ in this respect, and GN countries that actually predominantly meet our criteria. Moreover, within countries, some schools and regions may see the majority of medical graduates go directly into unsupervised practice while other schools, for example, in urban areas, see their graduates predominantly enter postgraduate medical training. After debates among the author team, we considered using ‘Global South EPAs’ the best compromise but acknowledge its limitations.

The GS-EPAs should, therefore, be interpreted with a local lens and adapted to national and regional needs for physician practices. Next, we must stress that EPA titles (table 1) are insufficient for understanding and using these EPAs. They can only be well understood with their descriptions, specifications and limitations as provided in online supplemental tables E1–11. EPAs serve to qualify trainees for units of clinical practice (with ‘indirect supervision’ or ‘unsupervised practice’27), and it must be fully clear for all parties what this qualification includes and what not. See figure 2 for an exmple.

Lessons learnt during this study include the importance of a clear conception among panel members of (a) what EPAs are and (b) what it means to work unsupervised as a medical graduate. EPAs are the activities for which competencies, and thus competent professionals are needed; they are not competencies in themselves.28 Next, the research team, in a study like this, is not a neutral observer but an active interpreter and editor of findings. This requires a clear justification of the team composition, which goes beyond a reflexivity paragraph, and a feedback loop to panel members. This is built into Delphi procedures, which require follow-up surveys of interpreted results but should also be applied with focus groups that use a nominal group technique. The research team must make sure their interpretations and rewrites of EPAs still reflect the opinions of the original panels. A list of EPAs resulting from a nominal group procedure cannot simply be created within a group session but requires editorial work, often by grouping detailed EPAs under the heading or broader EPAs. The breadth of an EPA to enable formal qualification and how to elaborate its specification requires a thoughtful balance.9

The full nominal group technique requires panel members to vote on priorities within a list of EPAs.29 30 While we included this in our procedures, we found little additional value for compiling a list of EPAs; we did not exclude any propositions because of a low voting score. To maximise the focus group members’ contributions to the quality of the EPAs, we asked them to evaluate the redacted EPAs of their own group by applying the EQual rubric procedure. This procedure invites respondents to score rubric items and online supplemental comments for each EPA. These comments, even if the response in this phase was somewhat disappointing, were helpful to further optimise the EPAs as the two sets were merged into one input set for the Delphi phase. A set with high quality EPAs is strongly recommended before a Delphi procedure is started.11 We could have initiated another EQual procedure for the merged EPAs but felt that would add little additional quality.

We noticed quite some discrepancies between what Delphi panel members considered prevalent practices in their regions or countries and what they considered essential EPAs, especially in round 1. This was likely caused by wishes (‘graduates are not equipped for this now, but our need is high, and we must prepare them for this EPA’) or by the inclusion of sub-activities within the EPA that were not a common practice of a panel member’s particular region.

Our study has significant limitations. While we have not seen such broad transnational investigation into EPAs for medical graduates in countries of the Global South, with the exception of the WHO work to prepare for its competency framework for universal health coverage,26 our proposal for an EPA framework may have sounded different with a different research team and different panel members from different countries. For example, adult patient care was elaborated in three EPAs, and the care for children in but one EPA. We felt tempted to adapt but that would deviate too far from the results of our investigation.

Nevertheless, we believe the GS-EPA framework is a useful starting point for discussing what requirements can be expected for recent graduates in settings where they are employed in healthcare facilities with little or no supervision and prospect of postgraduate training any time soon.
