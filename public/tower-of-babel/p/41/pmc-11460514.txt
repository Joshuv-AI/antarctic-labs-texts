Dataset on recovered waste post-earthquake 2016 in Manabi Province, Ecuador for recycling and reuse
Authors: Elvira Rodríguez-Ríos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe earthquake in Ecuador on 16 April 2016 generated large volumes of debris and waste. This dataset contains data on recovered and reused disaster materials. Data were collected through a census survey of the scrap dealers of earthquake 2016 debris and waste (n = 62). This dataset was compiled to demonstrate how earthquake waste was generated during the 2016 earthquake and compare it with the pre-disaster period 2015 and the 2019 current when the data were collected. The recovered disaster materials include plastic, metal, cardboard, paper, glass, other recyclable materials, and reused materials. Likewise, the database allows us to observe the time response of medium- and small-sized scrap businesses as scrap dealers engage in the commercial transaction of disaster materials, and this dataset shows the process phases of recovering disaster waste. In addition, the dataset includes profit perceptions and factual earnings from scrap businesses after an earthquake. Considering the significant volume of waste and debris generated, this database can provide useful data for evaluating disaster waste management as an important task in post-disaster recovery.Keywords: Disaster waste survey, Material recycling, Material recovered, Questionnaire, Material reused, Ecuador earthquake 2016

Background

One effect of the 2016 earthquake in Ecuador was the generation of large volumes of debris and waste. This earthquake generated 7,561,219.81 m3 of debris in Manabi. To study the increase in solid waste under disaster conditions, a comparison was made with the volume of waste generated without an earthquake and the amount of material recovered for recycling and reuse was emphasized.

The dataset was founded on the microeconomic definition of production expressed as the quantity of recovered material in kilograms (Q) and the price in a competitive market is represented as P. The scrap dealers of earthquake 2016 debris and waste participated both as buyers and sellers. They bought material from individual recuperators and then they sold these materials to both the recycling industry and individual buyers for reuse. Therefore, the scrap dealers participated in two markets, the first as a buyer and the other as a supplier, hence, they assumed two different market prices, one price buying, and the other selling price.

The demand is defined as the quantities that scrap dealers were willing and able to buy at various prices in each period, ceteris paribus. By contrast, supply explains different quantities of disaster material that scrap dealers were willing to bring to the market at various prices. The price of recyclable materials in a perfectly competitive market is determined by demand and supply. The price is the value that the seller is paid by a material kilogram.

Providing data on disaster waste generation is critical because it is an acceptable method for avoiding waste disposal at unauthorized sites and open dumps during a post-disaster emergency. In addition, the recovery rate of recyclable materials was low. The data described are from Manabi province, which was the most affected by the earthquake in 2016. This province comprises 1,592,840 people [2], more than 9 % of the countryʼs population.

Manabi is located on the Ecuadorian coast and generates a provincial gross production of USD 10,693,480,000 and a Gross Value Added (GVA) of USD 5,383,568,000 in 2022 [3]. Products such as cocoa, wood, seafood, shrimp, and others are produced in Manabi. The industry of Manabi province is founded on the production of tuna.

Data Description

Between the end of 2019 and the beginning of 2020, 62 scrap dealers operated in Manabi. Most (93 %) were small. Their storage capacity was variable and depended on the behaviour of the waste market. For example, after the 2016 earthquake, some chose to increase their capacity by renting other premises for storage due to the increased waste market supply. The medium-sized scrap businesses are branch offices of the main recycling industries in cities such as Quito and Guayaquil. They aim to purchase and transport waste to the main offices.

The scrap dealers provided data on unit price per kilogram of the recyclable material, both buy and sell. For case recyclable materials for direct reuse, the price of an item was divided by the total kilograms to determine the price per kilogram of gas stoves, washing machines, smoothing irons, blender machines, toasters, doors, windows, zinc sheets, cell phone plates, computer motherboards, sinks, and clothes irons. Scrap dealers provided the price, divided by the standard weight of the sold piece (e.g. considering the model and mark of the item usually sold).

Raw and calculated data were provided as table-type data in an Excel file, separated into six Excel spreadsheets. The spreadsheet includes data on three variables: the monthly quantity of recyclable material, the buying price, and the selling price of the recovered material group. The buying price is part of the production cost, and it constitutes the income for the individual recoverer of materials. Therefore, the buying price does not include the transport costs and the cost of the storage facilities.

The recovered materials were classified as recyclable materials and reusing materials. The recycling materials, in turn, were classified by their primary composition as plastic, metal, Cardboard and paper, glass, and other recyclable materials (Thermoplastic polymers such as shoes and boots). Each of these groups has its components. For example, plastic is classified as polyethylene terephthalate (PET plastic), high-density polyethylene (HDPE), polyvinyl chloride (PVC), low-density polyethylene (PEBD), and home plastic waste (HP). The price and quantity of each component are input data provided by the scrap dealers. Based on this input data, the prices and quantities of waste are given as monthly averages over a year. Three variables are provided for 2015, 2016, and 2019 (Table 1).

Furthermore, Excel spreadsheets 1–5 provide the number of months post-earthquake when the scrap dealers purchased recycled material and data on the advantages of medium- and small-sized scrap businesses after the earthquake. In the business advantages column, the response is recorded under the following numeric codes to indicate yes (1), no (2), and neutral (3).

For the data collection, small and medium-sized scrap businesses (62) that received letter of face-to-face applications to interview were reminded by phone calls. According to the acceptance to interview for providing information required the scrap dealers were categorised into three groups: 1) scrap dealers accepting the application before a telephone reminder was performed (48.4 %); 2) scrap dealers that communicated their acceptance to the interview after the first telephone reminder (32.4 %), and 3) scrap dealers responding after two telephone reminders (19.4 %). The acceptance application included an agreement day and time for the interview. The telephone reminders had no consequence on responses

In addition, 2019 was investigated to facilitate memory by implementing a memory strategy from the present to previous years. This refers to the presentation of the same set of questions to Scrap dealers in three periods: 2019 (i.e., the data collection period), 2016 (the year in which the earthquake occurred), and 2015 (i.e., before the occurrence of the disaster) to activate the memory and encourage retrieval of the stored information, which mediates their remember process. Recall responses were confirmed on the inventory records of the entry and exit of recyclable and reused material during the years analysed in the current study. Therefore, recall bias was avoided.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

In today's market, the limited availability of natural resources, low environmental awareness, and widespread poverty have encouraged people from some countries to attempt recovery and reuse. Examples of this tendency are seen in devices such as gas stoves, washing machines, smoothing irons, blender machines, toasters, doors, windows, zinc sheets, cell phone plates, computer motherboards, sinks, and clothes irons.

The census method of data collection was used. This process of statistical list allowed all small and medium-sized scrap businesses to be interviewed which functioned in the study area at survey time. The municipalities and scrap dealers' associations provided the register of the population of scrap businesses.

The survey format is based on microeconomic definitions and market forces. This theory includes market demand and supply. The law of a competitive market is that more quantity is supplied by the minor market price and the inverse [4]. The behaviour of the quantity (Q) of recovered disaster material and the price (P) variable are shown in the dataset for demand (buying price) and supply (selling price). In addition, the definitions of protocols for managing waste in the post-disaster scenario [5] were considered.

This survey was based on the following microeconomic model: IT=P*Q, where IT=total income, P=price, and Q=quantity which allowed to definite the variables for analysis. Using this input data, such as waste quantity and selling price, researchers could calculate the total income. Similarly, the operating costs of purchasing the materials for recycling and reuse could be calculated based on the buying price and the quantity.

This study adopted panel data that combined time series features and cross-sectional data. Time-series data were collected over three periods (2015, 2016, and 2019), and the cross-sectional data for each period were the selling price, buying price, quantity of waste purchased, and sell quantity of disaster waste. In addition, the seven phases of waste treatment, advantages of the business after the earthquake, and the number of months post-earthquake when scrap dealers purchased recyclable materials were included

To obtain the database, an interviewer conducted a face-to-face paper survey. The survey protocol comprised three sections: survey respondent's identification, technical, and closing information. The survey respondent information included name and contact information, title, function/team, and location of the scrap business. However, this information was excluded from the dataset to ensure respondents’ anonymity as above indicated. The measures of economic and technical information in the different questions were derived from the variables of the microeconomic model IT=P*Q. The questions in this section provide the information needed to support the study's goals. The closing information consisted of the date, time, and name of the interviewer. In addition, open-ended questions for example on general remarks included in the closing information section of the survey form (refer to survey form) helped us see things from the interviewees' perspective for feedback in their own words on survey issues.

The questionnaire survey comprised a set of questions asked to administrators or owners of medium and small scrap businesses. The questions were closed-, semi-closed-, or open-ended. To minimize any possible hypothetical recall bias, the interview began with questions on the price (P) and quantity (Q) of different disaster-recovered materials in 2019 and then asked them about the previous years, 2016 and 2015. Survey questions were asked in such a way that respondents could think more deeply, topic by topic (buying, selling, cost, and protocols), lowering their cognitive load.

Procedures of Quality Control and Quality Assurance were implemented in the field information-gathering phase. The crucial first step was the comprehensive training and orientation of the interviewers in the data collection and recording process, which included induction, development, and closure of the interview, emphasizing accurate data recording. This was followed by establishing a supervision process in the data collection phase, with three quality controls implemented.

1.Completeness quality criterion: verification of the records by the interviewer immediately after the interview to identify possible recording errors (non-recording, incorrect, or incomplete). Also, logical errors (for example, the scrap metal business could not have sold a larger quantity of waste than it bought) and errors of inadvertent omission (checking for blank spaces on the form). All cells had to be full; if the data was not generated, you had to enter zero.

Ethics Statement

The authors have read and followed the ethical requirements for publication in Data in Brief and have confirmed that the current work does not involve human subjects, animal experiments, or any data collected from social media platforms. However, the respondents’ identities were removed before the dataset was made public.

According to the institutional research ethics regulations, this survey study does not require Ethical approval by the ethical committee. In this survey research, no intervention was delivered to research participants, therefore, there was no risk of physical and psychological harm to individuals who participated. However, institutional communication was sent to the scrap dealers indicating the aims of the survey study and requesting acceptance to provide the information. This allowed all the small and medium-sized scrap businesses to agree to the interview.
