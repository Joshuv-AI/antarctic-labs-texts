Comparative study on the detection efficacy of KU-F50 centrifugal method and routine detection methods for gastrointestinal parasites
Authors: Rongjiang Liu, Kaili Qin, Shaoheng Huang, Qian Sun, Chu Huang, Lin Liao, Faquan Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare the detection performance of three automated fecal methods for common gastrointestinal parasites and assess the analytical validity of the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method.MethodsA total of 1,290 fecal samples submitted to a hospital from December 10, 2025, to January 15, 2026, were collected. Parallel testing was performed using the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method, the KU-F50 routine detection method, and the KU-F40 routine detection method. Cochran’s Q test was used to compare the overall detection rates and the detection rates of various parasite eggs among the three methods.Pairwise comparisons were performed using the McNemar test and Bonferroni correction.ResultsThe total detection rates for the three methods were 15.81% (204/1290), 14.50% (187/1290), and 13.02% (168/1290), respectively, with a statistically significant difference (Q = 8.763, P = 0.013). Pairwise comparisons were performed using the McNemar test, and the Bonferroni method was adopted to adjust the significance level (=0.0167). The results indicated that the overall detection rate of the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method was significantly higher than that of the KU-F40 routine detection method (P = 0.008).Subgroup analysis of individual parasite eggs resealed a statistically significant difference in the detection rate of Clonorchis sinensis eggs among the three methods (Q = 12.365,P = 0.002). Further pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction (=0.0167) showed that the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method achieved a significantly higher detection rate for Clonorchis sinensis eggs than the KU-F40 routine detection method (P = 0.001). Gradient dilution experiments with Entamoeba samples showed that the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method exhibited a positive deviation at low dilution factors (74.00%~122.00%) and a reduced deviation at high dilution factors (-10.00%~-20.00%). Both the KU-F50 routine detection method and the KU-F40 routine detection method showed negative deviations across the entire gradient. In simulated samples with 5, 10, and 15 pcs/mL, the detection rates of the KU-F50 centrifugal method reached 50%, 80%, and 80%, respectively, which were significantly higher than those of the other two methods (0%~20%). The automatic recognition precision and recall rates of the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method for Clonorchis sinensis eggs were 98.08% and 98.46%, respectively.ConclusionThe KU-F50 Centrifugal sedimentation method yields relatively higher detection rates for digestive tract parasites. Its testing results are in good consistency with manual interpretation, and it shows remarkable advantages in detecting Clonorchis sinensis eggs, possessing certain clinical application potential.Keywords: Gastrointestinal parasites, Feces analyzer, Centrifugal sedimentation method, Detection rate

Introduction

Parasitic diseases are a major public health issue threatening the health of the population and are also a key factor restricting socioeconomic development [1]. Among these, gastrointestinal parasites, due to their wide transmission routes and large infected population, have become a core focus of public health prevention and control [2].

Currently, manual morphological microscopy based on optical microscopy remains the recognized “gold standard” for the diagnosis of parasitic diseases [3]. This method, relying on the intuitive ability to identify parasite morphology, offers the advantages of simple operation and relatively low cost, holding an irreplaceable position in clinical diagnosis [4]. However, in the clinical practice of fecal parasite detection, the accuracy of manual microscopy is susceptible to various interfering factors, making it difficult to maintain stable diagnostic performance. These interfering factors are mainly reflected in the following three aspects. First, result interpretation is highly subjective. Microscopy results heavily depend on the professional experience and morphological knowledge of the laboratory personnel, leading to significant differences in judgment criteria among individuals. This subjectivity is difficult to completely eliminate through standardized operating procedures, directly affecting the consistency and reproducibility of results [5]. Second, morphological identification is challenging. Parasites have complex life cycles, and the morphology of the same species can vary dramatically across different developmental stages (e.g., eggs, larvae, adults). Additionally, some species share similar and indistinguishable features, significantly increasing the difficulty of identification under the microscope. Traditional morphological diagnosis is therefore prone to missed detection and misjudgment [6]. Third, low-concentration samples are prone to missed detection. When the egg concentration in a sample is low, the distribution of parasites within the fecal matrix is highly random. Errors during routine sampling and slide preparation can further amplify this randomness, significantly reducing detection efficiency and making it difficult to ensure effective capture of sparse eggs [7]. These multiple issues stemming from subjective judgment, morphological identification, and sampling randomness superimpose, collectively leading to the difficulty of achieving ideal clinical diagnostic sensitivity and specificity with manual optical microscopy [8]. This limitation readily causes missed diagnosis and misdiagnosis, thereby delaying early intervention and precise treatment for patients.

With the automated and standardized development of clinical laboratory technology, fully automatic feces analyzers are gradually replacing traditional manual operations and have become an important tool for fecal parasite detection [9]. However, most mainstream automated detection systems employ only simple filtration for sample pretreatment and lack a standardized enrichment mechanism equivalent to that of routine concentration methods. Consequently, their detection performance remains substantially compromised for samples with low parasite burden [10].

To improve the efficiency and accuracy of gastrointestinal parasite detection, our hospital introduced the KU-F40 and KU-F50 Feces analyzers developed by Zhuhai Keyu Biological Engineering Co., Ltd. (Zhuhai, Guangdong, China). The KU-F50 adds a centrifugal enrichment adapter based on the routine detection technology, theoretically enabling physical enrichment of parasite eggs through centrifugation, thereby increasing the detection rate in low-concentration samples.

Existing studies have confirmed the standardization advantages of the KU series analyzers in detecting formed elements in feces [4]. However, head-to-head comparative studies between the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method and the KU-F40 routine detection method are still lacking. The actual detection efficacy of the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method for gastrointestinal parasites, its limit of detection for low-concentration eggs, and its automatic recognition accuracy have not been systematically verified [8]. Therefore, this study conducted parallel testing on the same fecal samples using the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method, KU-F50 routine detection method, and KU-F40 routine detection method to compare the differences in gastrointestinal parasite detection rates among the three methods. Meanwhile, gradient dilution tests of Entamoeba specimens were conducted to evaluate the low-concentration detection capacity of the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method. The precision rate (PR) and recall rate (RE) of the automatic identification model were calculated to comprehensively assess its practical detection performance. This study preliminarily compared the detection differences of three methods and analyzed the application potential of KU-F50 assay in fecal parasite detection, offering basic references for the selection and optimization of clinical detection strategies.

Materials and methods

Study samples

A total of 1,290 fecal samples submitted to a hospital from December 10, 2025, to January 15, 2026, were included (All specimens included in this study were archived clinical samples from our hospital. This was a retrospective single-center study, and the sample size was determined by the number of available archived specimens). All samples were submitted for testing within 1 h of collection to ensure freshness and absence of contamination, meeting the testing requirements. Sample exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) contaminated samples; (2) samples with incomplete patient diagnostic information; (3) for patients with multiple submissions, the second and subsequent samples were excluded.

Testing equipment and reagents

The KU-F40 fully automatic feces analyzer and KU-F50 fully automatic feces analyzer manufactured by Zhuhai Keyu Biological Engineering Co., Ltd. (Zhuhai, Guangdong, China) were used, along with reagents, sample collection cups, sample diluent, pipettes, disposable tips, counting chambers, and other consumables. The detection volume of the KU-F50 counting chamber was 55 µL, and that of the KU-F40 counting chamber was 45 µL.

Testing methods

Three different parasite detection methods were compared in this study. Fresh fecal samples from inpatients and outpatients were collected using disposable clean stool collectors. Each fecal sample was divided into three portions for testing by the KU-F40 routine detection method, the KU-F50 routine detection method, and the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method. Manual review was performed by technicians with extensive experience in fecal morphology according to the requirements of the “National Clinical Laboratory Operating Procedures” [11].

Sample preparation

A total of 1,290 clinically submitted fecal samples were collected. Each sample was divided into 3 equal portions for the three testing methods. All samples were tested within 1 h of collection to ensure sample quality.

Testing procedures

KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method

One level spoon of fecal sample was added to a centrifuge tube pre-filled with 3 mL of sample diluent and manually shaken up and down. The tube was centrifuged at 2000–2500 rpm for 2 min. After removing the filter screen, the tube was loaded for testing. The instrument captured 432 images under low magnification and 30 images under high magnification. The AI function automatically identified formed elements and recorded the type and quantity of parasite eggs.

KU-F50 routine detection method

One level spoon of fecal sample was added to a sample collection cup. The instrument captured 432 images under low magnification and 30 images under high magnification. The dilution ratio function was activated, and the instrument’s AI automatically added diluent based on the sample volume. The AI automatically identified formed elements and recorded the type and quantity of parasite eggs.

KU-F40 routine detection method

One level spoon of fecal sample was added to a sample collection cup. The instrument captured 768 images under low magnification and 30 images under high magnification. The dilution ratio function was activated, and the instrument’s AI automatically added diluent based on the sample volume. The AI automatically identified formed elements and recorded the type and quantity of parasite eggs.

Quality control

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the test results, the following quality control measures were implemented in this study.

Personnel and operational quality control

All testing personnel received professional training and worked with certification, strictly adhering to standardized operating procedures. A double-blind, double-operator testing model was adopted, in which two technicians independently examined the specimens without knowing each other’s results or the sample grouping information, in order to reduce subjective human error.

Serial dilution experiment

High-concentration clinical Entamoeba specimens were used as experimental materials. Initial cyst concentrations were determined by manual counting under an optical microscope. Specimens were serially diluted with normal saline at ratios of 1:1, 1:3, 1:5, 1:9 and 1:19, and simulated reference samples with graded concentrations were prepared for subsequent dilution tests. All samples were detected by three methods: the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method, the KU-F50 routine method and the KU-F40 routine method. Detected cyst counts at each dilution ratio were recorded. Percentage deviations of measured values from theoretical concentrations were calculated, and the quantitative accuracy of the three detection methods was evaluated accordingly.

Analysis of detection capacity for serially diluted samples

A parasite egg standard with a concentration of 5 pcs/mL was used. Each of the three detection methods was repeated 10 times. The number of positive detections for each method was recorded, and the detection rate was calculated to evaluate the sensitivity of each method for samples with low parasite egg concentrations.

Statistical methods

Detection data were compiled using Excel software, and statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 23.0 software. Detection rates were expressed as percentages (%). The Cochran’s Q test was used for comparison of categorical data, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of total detection rates of digestive tract parasites among three detection methods

A total of 1,290 specimens were included, all of which were valid. The KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method detected 204 positive specimens, with a detection rate of 15.81%; the KU-F50 routine detection method detected 187 positive specimens, with a detection rate of 14.50%; and the KU-F40 routine detection method detected 168 positive specimens, with a detection rate of 13.02%. The difference in detection rates among the three methods was statistically significant (Q = 8.763, P = 0.013). Pairwise comparisons showed that the detection rate of the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method was significantly higher than that of the KU-F40 routine detection method (P = 0.008). No statistically significant differences were found between the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method and the KU-F50 routine detection method, nor between the KU-F50 routine detection method and the KU-F40 routine detection method (P > 0.05). Discussion

Parasitic infections represent a significant global public health issue. Over 300 parasite species have been identified that can be transmitted to humans [12], including the eggs of Clonorchis sinensis, hookworms, Taenia, Ascaris lumbricoides, Trichuris trichiura, Entamoeba, and Giardia lamblia [13]. These parasites can harm the human body by absorbing host nutrients, producing toxins, causing immune damage, and inducing mechanical disruption, leading to malnutrition, anemia, impaired growth and cognitive function, and other adverse health effects [14, 15].

Stool parasite examination is the gold standard for diagnosing digestive tract parasitic infections [3]. With the advancement of automation technology, fully automatic KU-series stool analyzers have been applied in more than 50 countries worldwide. Most existing automated instruments lack centrifugal enrichment procedures, failing to concentrate parasite eggs in specimens and prone to missed detection of low-abundance eggs, which restricts overall detection performance [16]. The KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation detection method provides a novel option for parasite examination.

This study found that the total detection rate of the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method (15.81%) was higher than that of the KU-F50 routine detection method (14.50%) and the KU-F40 routine detection method (13.02%), with a statistically significant difference compared to the KU-F40 routine detection method. This merit is largely derived from centrifugation: centrifugation promotes rapid sedimentation and enrichment of formed elements such as parasite eggs, effectively increasing egg recovery rates. This is particularly beneficial for samples with low egg concentrations, helping to avoid missed detections. In contrast, routine detection methods without centrifugation enrichment face increased difficulty in detecting low-concentration samples, resulting in relatively lower detection rates.

Regarding the detection of specific parasites, the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation mode demonstrated significantly higher detection rates for the eggs of Clonorchis sinensis, compared to the KU-F40 routine detection method. Clonorchis sinensis is a clinically common digestive tract parasite with relatively high infection rates and significant harm to humans [17]. The KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method leverages the principle of centrifugal enrichment to effectively concentrate parasitic ova, thereby improving specimen detection rates and offering a potential reference strategy for early clinical diagnosis.

For other parasites such as Strongyloides stercoralis, Entamoeba, and Giardia lamblia, although the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method showed slightly higher detection rates than the other two methods, the differences were not statistically significant. This may be related to the low infection rates of these parasites and the small number of eggs in the samples, warranting further verification with expanded sample sizes.

The main advantages of the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method include: (1) High detection efficiency – centrifugation enrichment technology significantly improves egg recovery and overall detection rates, with clear advantages in detecting common parasites such as Clonorchis sinensis and hookworms, effectively reducing missed detections; (2) High biosafety – the detection process is carried out in a fully enclosed system equipped with pipeline cleaning functions, effectively reducing biosafety risks and cross-contamination risks; (3) Image traceability and convenient data management – the instrument can store detection images long-term, facilitating manual re-examination and result traceability, while also supporting categorized data storage and database management, enhancing the level of laboratory information management.

This study has certain limitations. Firstly, it is a single-center retrospective analysis, with all samples derived from the same diagnostic and treatment institution, resulting in a relatively limited sample source. Consequently, the generalizability of the findings across different endemic regions and populations is restricted, necessitating further validation through multicenter studies. Secondly, this study primarily compared the egg detection efficacy of three testing systems—the KU-F50 centrifugal sedimentation method, the KU-F50 routine method, and the KU-F40 routine method—but did not conduct comparative analyses with traditional methods such as direct smear microscopy or saturated saline oviposition collection. Lastly, the study solely used the egg detection rate as the primary evaluation metric without analyzing the detection stability or methodological precision across varying infection loads. Future research should enhance methodological validation and evaluate detection performance across multiple dimensions to ensure more comprehensive and reliable conclusions.

Conclusion

In summary, the KU-F50 centrifugal detection method exhibits a higher positive detection rate and detection consistency for fecal parasites, with the advantages of operational simplicity and favorable biosafety. Its overall detection performance is superior to that of the KU-F50 routine method and the KU-F40 routine method. As revealed by this single-center preliminary validation, the KU-F50 centrifugal detection method demonstrates certain clinical application potential and can serve as an optional alternative for automated fecal parasite detection in clinical practice. Further efforts are still needed to optimize the detection workflow, refine the artificial intelligence recognition model, and conduct multi-center large-sample validations to further improve its detection efficacy and provide more reliable technical support for the screening, prevention and control of parasitic diseases.
