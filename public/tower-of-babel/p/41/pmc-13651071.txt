Restoring premorbid occlusal relationship with 3D-printed occlusal splints in treatment of extensive comminuted mandibular fractures: an exploratory technical report
Authors: Qing-Quan Dong, Hao-Ke Zhao, Kun Lv, Zhi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtensive comminuted mandible (ECM) fractures with dentition defects pose challenges for occlusal restoration. In selected severe cases with extensive comminution and compromised dentition, conventional MMF may be difficult to apply effectively. This study aimed to evaluate the applicability and outcomes of using three-dimensional (3D) printed occlusal splints for occlusal restoration in ECM fractures.MethodsThis retrospective, non-randomized cohort study with a small sample size was conducted involving 22 patients with ECM fractures. Twelve patients were treated with 3D-printed occlusal splints for occlusal restoration (Group A). The other 10 patients who received traditional maxillomandibular fixation (MMF) served as the control group (Group B). After confirming baseline comparability between groups, outcomes were quantitatively assessed using linear measurement deviation and 3D analysis, with fracture healing as the primary outcome measure.ResultsFracture healing was achieved in all patients. According to radiographic and clinical assessment (no validated occlusal index was used), all patients in Group A achieved the planned occlusal restoration. Compared to Group B, Group A demonstrated better reduction accuracy, reflected by significantly smaller deviations in both linear measurement deviation and 3D analysis (p < 0.05).ConclusionIn this small retrospective cohort, 3D-printed occlusal splints provide a technical solution for occlusal restoration in selected severe cases of ECM fractures. This technique represents a promising adjunct but requires further validation in larger prospective studies.Keywords: Extensive comminuted mandibular fractures, Premorbid Occlusal Restoration, Virtual surgical planning, 3D-printed occlusal splint

Introduction

Comminuted mandibular fractures are typically caused by high-impact trauma. They are characterized by multiple fracture lines, resulting in numerous small fragments within a single region of the mandible (i.e., the angle, body, ramus, or symphysis) [1, 2]. Extensive comminuted mandibular (ECM) fractures extend from one anatomical region of the mandible to another. They involve multiple anatomical zones [3–5] and are invariably associated with significant soft tissue injury, bone fragmentation, and dental defects secondary to tooth loss or mobility [6, 7].

The inherent complexity of 3D fragment displacement, compounded by concomitant dental injuries, presents unique challenges in treatment. Crucially, tooth loss or loosening often obscures essential occlusal reference points. Conventional methods include maxillomandibular fixation (MMF) with arch bars or intermaxillary fixation (IMF) screws. These methods may be difficult to apply effectively for achieving premorbid occlusal restoration in ECM fractures with compromised dentition [8–10]. Consequently, in ECM fractures with compromised dentition, the lack of reliable occlusal landmarks necessitates alternative strategies.

Maxillofacial surgery makes extensive use of digital technology. 3D printing has become an integral element of the workflow in several areas of oral and maxillofacial surgery [11]. The advent of computer-aided design (CAD) and 3D printing technologies has revolutionized the management of mandibular fractures. These technologies enable precise virtual surgical planning (VSP) and personalized solutions [12, 13]. With VSP and 3D-printed guiding templates, surgeons can perform fracture reduction and fixation more accurately and efficiently. This improves treatment outcomes [14]. Previous studies on the use of splints or 3D printing techniques in mandibular fracture management have largely been limited to case reports or technical descriptions. They lack quantitative validation of occlusal accuracy, particularly in extensive comminuted fractures with compromised dentition. Moreover, few studies have directly compared digitally designed 3D-printed occlusal splints with conventional MMF in terms of radiographic precision. The present study therefore aims to provide a quantitative 3D analysis to clarify the value of this approach in such complex fractures. In this study, we present a method for restoring the occlusal relationship in ECM fractures with dentition defect. This method uses a digitally designed and 3D-printed occlusal splint as an exploratory technical report. The purpose of this study was to investigate the clinical applicability and therapeutic outcomes of this method in the management of ECM fractures.

Methods

A retrospective cohort study was conducted involving patients who underwent surgical management of ECM fractures in our department between June 2018 and December 2024. For the purpose of this study, ECM fractures were defined as mandibular fractures that met all of the following criteria: (1) The fracture lines extend continuously across at least two anatomical regions of the mandible; (2) The fractures result in multiple small, fragmented bone pieces within the fracture zone.

The inclusion criteria for the study sample were as follows: (1) radiologically confirmed ECM fractures via preoperative spiral multislice computed tomography (CT); (2) compromised dentition due to multiple tooth loss or loosening that hindered the restoration of occlusal relationship; and (3) a minimum follow-up duration of 3 months. Patients were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) comminuted mandibular fractures with sufficient residual dentition to restore the occlusal relationship (2) incomplete follow-up records; (3) lack of complete preoperative and postoperative CT data; and (4) patients under 16 years of age.

According to the definition of ECM described in the Methods section, 22 patients were enrolled and divided into two groups according to whether 3D-printed splints were used for occlusal restoration, as confirmed by reviewing their medical and consumption records. Group A (n = 12): Patients who underwent intraoperative restoration of the occlusal relationship using 3D-printed occlusal splints. Group B (n = 10): Patients in whom the occlusal relationship was restored using traditional MMF alone (without the adjunctive splint), serving as the control group.

Virtual planning

For patients in Group A, preoperative CT data in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format were imported into Mimics Research software for VSP. Segmentation of anatomical structures was standardized using the software’s built-in bone thresholding algorithm to generate active masks highlighting bone and dental hard tissues (Fig. 1A). Subsequent manual refinement of these masks was performed to accurately segment all individual bone fragments, enabling 3D reconstruction. The data were then imported into 3-matic Research software. On this platform, optimal virtual reduction of the bony fragments was achieved by ensuring precise alignment in the axial, coronal, and sagittal CT planes (Fig. 1B).

Splint design and fabrication

With the mandible in this virtually reduced position, a digital occlusal splint was designed by referencing the residual occlusal landmarks on both the maxillary and mandibular arches (Fig. 1C). For patients with associated maxillary fractures, the maxilla was first reduced in VSP to restore the integrity of the maxillary arch. The maxilla was confirmed as a stable reference in all patients before splint design. The final outputs—including the original craniofacial model, the VSP reduction model, and the digital occlusal splint—were fabricated using 3D printing technology. The 3D printing workflow was as follows: a multi-material 3D printer (JOYE600) was used with medical-grade photopolymer resin (8111X) at a layer thickness of 0.1 mm. After printing, post-curing was performed in an ultraviolet curing chamber, followed by sterilization using low-temperature plasma. Finally, reconstruction plates were pre-contoured to precisely fit the geometry of the VSP mandibular model (Fig. 1F).

No standardized occlusal index or validated occlusal assessment tool (e.g., T-Scan, OccluSense, or a clinical occlusal index) was used in this retrospective study. The premorbid occlusal relationship was inferred based on a combination of the following information: preoperative CT morphology and spatial positions of residual dentition, the contralateral healthy dentition (if present) as a reference, the stable structure of the maxillary arch (in the absence of maxillary fractures or after reduction and fixation), and the surgeon’s clinical judgment. The splint design for all Group A patients was reviewed and confirmed by two senior surgeons to ensure consistency with the occlusal relationship, although this does not replace a validated occlusal endpoint.

In contrast, for patients in Group B, no 3D-printed occlusal splints were used. Fracture reduction and internal fixation were guided solely by the surgeon’s intraoperative assessment using traditional MMF. The VSP data, although generated, were reserved for postoperative analytical comparison and were not employed for preoperative planning or surgical guidance.

Surgical procedure – group A

For patients in Group A, surgery was performed via a submandibular incision or through existing soft tissue lacerations to expose the fractures. Non-viable teeth within the fracture lines were extracted. An initial manual reduction was performed to approximate the mandible contour and restore gross anatomical continuity. After the insertion of the sterilized 3D-printed occlusal splint, which served to guide the precise anatomical repositioning of the fracture fragments and the occlusal restoration (Fig. 1G), MMF was then implemented using arch bars or IMF screws. The reduced fragments were initially stabilized with miniplates or microplates to the non-fractured proximal and distal segments. After the establishment of a stable framework along the superior border, the reduction and fixation process was extended to the fragments along the inferior border using additional miniplates or microplates. Finally, a pre-contoured reconstruction plate was secured along the inferior mandibular border to provide rigid internal fixation (Fig. 1H). After confirming proper occlusal restoration, the MMF and splint were removed.

Surgical procedure – group B

Patients in Group B underwent the same surgical approach and fixation principles, but without the use of a 3D-printed occlusal splint. Reconstruction plates were manually contoured intraoperatively rather than pre-contoured based on virtual planning. All procedures were performed by the same surgical team to minimize technical variability. Preoperative baseline data collected for analysis included patient age, gender, etiology of injury, presence of concomitant fractures (condylar and maxillary), number of bone fragments, status of dentition defects and the types and quantities of titanium plates.

Outcome assessment

The Eichner classification system was utilized to categorize the extent of dentition defects. Clinical functional data (e.g., pain, masticatory function, TMJ symptoms) and patient-reported outcome measures were not collected in this retrospective analysis. Data from both groups were analyzed to assess their baseline comparability (Fig. 2).

Distribution of Eichner classification between Group A and Group B. The horizontal 100% stacked bar chart presents the proportion of patients in each Eichner class. The distribution of Eichner classification was comparable between the two groups (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.733)

Primary outcomes were fracture healing (evaluated by clinical and radiological assessment) and quantitative reduction accuracy, including linear measurement deviations (coronoid-pogonion distances, inter-mental foramen distance), mean deviation, and root mean square (RMS) from 3D superimposition. Secondary outcomes included maximal mouth opening (MMO), operative time, and deviations at five cephalometric landmarks (supramentale, left/right gonion, pogonion, menton). Operative time was analyzed using a generalized linear model to control for covariates, but it was not a primary endpoint.

Fracture healing was evaluated through both clinical examination and radiological assessment during follow-up. Satisfactory fracture healing was defined by the following criteria: (1) restoration of normal mouth opening and the premorbid occlusal relationship; (2) no postoperative complications; and (3) radiographic evidence of fracture union on postoperative CT scans, without signs of abnormal reaction or significant bone resorption.

Postoperative CT data and VSP data were used for the quantitative measurement and evaluation of treatment outcomes. The efficacy of 3D-printed occlusal splints was quantitatively assessed through two primary methods in the comparative analysis between Groups A and B. First, linear measurement deviation was calculated as the absolute difference between postoperative achieved values and preoperative planned values for specific anatomical distances: from the tip of the coronoid process to the pogonion (bilaterally) and the inter-mental foramen distance. Secondly, 3D analysis was conducted by superimposing the VSP model onto the postoperative CT-derived model using 3-matic software. Three-dimensional overlay and heatmap analysis quantified the spatial differences (in millimeters) between the preoperative/VSP models and the postoperative CT data, with a uniform green color in the heatmap representing minimal deviations. The mean deviation and root mean square (RMS) were calculated. Additionally, deviations at five cephalometric landmarks including supramentale, left and right gonion, pogonion, and menton were measured manually. Two experienced observers independently performed all measurements in a blinded manner. In instances of discrepancy, measurements were repeated until a consensus was reached.

No validated clinical occlusal or functional outcome measure was used. During follow-up, clinical occlusal assessment was performed by two independent surgeons. They checked: (1) occlusal contacts in the intercuspal position; (2) deviation of the dental midline from the facial midline (≤ 2 mm accepted as normal); (3) patient-reported functional comfort during light clenching and swallowing. Restoration of the premorbid occlusal relationship was considered achieved when the occlusal contacts matched the preoperative virtual plan and the midline deviation was within 2 mm, with no patient complaint of occlusal discomfort.

All patient data were anonymized to ensure privacy. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 20. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared between groups using Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables with normal distribution and equal variances were compared using the independent samples t-test; those with normal distribution but unequal variances used Welch’s t-test; non-normally distributed variables used the Mann-Whitney U test. The specific test applied to each variable is indicated in the Results section. To control for the influence of covariates (e.g., number of fragments, surgical sites), operative time was compared between groups using a generalized linear model (GLM), with non-parametric analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) also used for verification. For all statistical tests, a two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. It should be noted that Group A received multiple digital workflow advantages (3D-printed splint, pre-contoured reconstruction plates, and full VSP), whereas Group B did not. Therefore, any observed differences cannot be attributed solely to the splint; the entire digital workflow likely contributed.

Results

The mean age was 47.83 years (range: 25–67 years) in Group A (12 males) and 41.50 years (range: 16–71 years) in Group B (7 males and 3 females). Falls and traffic accidents were the most common causes of injury. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of age, gender, or etiology of injury (p > 0.05). Condylar fractures, either unilateral or bilateral, were present in the majority of patients (8 in Group A and 8 in Group B), with a subset of cases also involving maxillary fractures. Fisher’s exact test confirmed no significant differences in the incidence of condylar or maxillary fractures between the two groups (p > 0.05). The number of bone fragments and the severity of dentition defects, classified using the Eichner index, were also comparable at baseline. All cases fell within the Eichner index range from B1 (three occlusal support areas) to C2 (no occlusal support area), with B3 (one occlusal support area) being the most frequent in Group A (41.7%) and B2 (two occlusal support areas) in Group B (30%). Statistical results showed that all patients in both groups were treated with reconstruction plates. The Mann-Whitney U test demonstrated that there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in the total number of titanium plates and the number of other types of titanium plates except reconstruction plates (Table 1).

According to radiographic evaluation and clinical judgment, planned occlusal restoration was achieved in all patients in Group A. The assessment was based on CT superimposition, occlusal contact check, and surgeon confirmation. No instances of occlusal deviation were observed. Postoperative maximal mouth opening (MMO) improved significantly compared to preoperative values, from 1.500 ± 0.674 cm preoperatively to 3.608 ± 0.215 cm at follow-up. In Group B, one patient exhibited limited mouth opening and occlusal deviation during follow-up. The MMO in Group B increased from 1.460 ± 0.778 cm preoperatively to 3.170 ± 1.022 cm postoperatively. All patients achieved satisfactory wound healing, with no cases of infection, wound dehiscence, or delayed wound healing. Compared with preoperative CT scans, the postoperative CT imaging confirmed satisfactory fracture reduction and stable fixation with titanium plates and screws (Fig. 1D-E), with no signs of nonunion or bone resorption.

Analysis of linear measurement deviations revealed statistically significant intergroup differences in the following parameters: the distance from the left coronoid process to pogonion (p = 0.003), the right coronoid process to pogonion (p = 0.037), and the inter-mental foramen distance (p = 0.030). The absolute differences between postoperative and VSP values are presented in Table 2, with Group A exhibiting significantly smaller mean values (standard deviation) and median values across these three parameters compared to Group B. This result indicates that the postoperative data in Group A had smaller deviations from the VSP models (Fig. 3A-C).

Heatmap analysis showed more homogeneous green coloration in Group A, indicating lower deviation and better reduction accuracy (Fig. 4). Statistical analysis of the five cephalometric landmarks (supramentale, left and right gonion, pogonion, and menton) revealed a mixed pattern. Significant differences were identified at supramentale and both gonion points (all p < 0.05; Table 2; Fig. 3F-H). In contrast, the differences at pogonion and menton did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.069 for both), despite Group A exhibiting lower median values at these landmarks (Fig. 3D-E). The mixed results for cephalometric landmarks should be interpreted cautiously, and the non-significant differences may be related to the limited sample size, suggesting that future studies with larger cohorts are warranted to further clarify this finding. Following adjustment, the generalized linear model showed that Group A had a shorter estimated marginal mean operative time than Group B (5.316 vs. 5.806 h), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.469; Fig. 3I). The result was consistent with the non-parametric ANCOVA (p = 0.923). Because operative time was not a primary outcome and the difference did not reach significance, no further emphasis is placed on this finding.

Discussion

The management of ECM fractures poses considerable challenges due to complex 3D fragment displacement and frequent dentition defects. Inaccurate treatment often leads to a series of complications such as occlusal deformities and limited mouth opening [15, 16]. The goal of treating comminuted mandibular fractures is to achieve anatomical bone healing with minimal complications. Restoration of the occlusal relationship is particularly crucial. Conventional treatment of mandibular fractures typically involves MMF with dental arch bars or IMF screws to assist in restoring the occlusal relationship. However, ECM fractures are often accompanied by multiple tooth loss and loosening. Furthermore, teeth deemed non-viable—such as those with root fractures or pre-existing poor structure—are often extracted during surgery. Consequently, the residual dentition may not provide sufficient reference points, making it difficult to restore the occlusal relationship using conventional MMF alone. In such scenarios, occlusal restoration relies heavily on the surgeon’s subjective clinical judgment.

Main findings

In this study, we used a digitally designed and 3D-printed occlusal splint to restore the occlusal relationship in patients with ECM fractures, and assessed its efficacy. Patients in Group A (3D-printed splint) demonstrated favorable outcomes, achieving satisfactory fracture healing and restoration of the occlusal relationship. Superimposition analysis of VSP data with postoperative CT data showed that mean deviation and RMS values were significantly lower in Group A than in Group B. Linear measurements and most cephalometric landmarks confirmed better reduction accuracy in Group A. These findings suggest that the 3D-printed occlusal splint serves as a promising technical aid for maintaining precise fracture reduction.

Linear measurements and the analysis of most cephalometric landmarks similarly confirmed better reduction accuracy in Group A. However, no statistically significant differences were observed for the pogonion and menton, although Group A demonstrated lower median values. From an anatomical perspective, both landmarks are located in the mandibular symphysis region, which provides favorable surgical exposure. This accessibility allows surgeons to achieve relatively accurate reduction in this area even without the use of a 3D-printed occlusal splint, thereby minimizing the final discrepancy between the two techniques at this specific location. Additionally, the limited sample size may have contributed to the statistical findings. Future research would benefit from expanded cohort sizes and more efficient analytical approaches.

These results suggest that 3D-printed splints facilitate more precise restoration of the occlusal relationship. Restoration of the occlusal relationship significantly contributes to fracture reduction and reestablishment of mandibular continuity. 3D-printed occlusal splints may achieve reduction precision comparable to conventional MMF techniques.

Comparison with previous literature

CAD and 3D printing technologies have been widely applied in mandibular fracture treatment [17]. The 3D-printed “simulated” mandibular fragments address challenges caused by insufficient visualization of the overall 3D structure of fractured fragments [18]. Previous reports on splint assisted reduction and 3D printing have largely been limited to case reports or technical notes [12, 13, 19], lacking controlled comparisons and quantitative accuracy data. Our study adds to the literature by providing, for the first time in ECM fractures with compromised dentition, a retrospective cohort design (n = 22) with baseline comparability and direct quantitative comparison between 3D-printed occlusal splints and conventional MMF. The results demonstrate that 3D-printed splints improve radiographic reduction accuracy, filling an evidence gap for this challenging patient population. Most previous reports are case reports or technical notes, and our study, despite being a retrospective cohort, remains preliminary due to its small sample size and lack of functional endpoints.

Surgeons have been utilizing intraoperative splints to repair complicated fractures [19, 20]. Although traditional splint materials offer greater flexibility in design and functional diversity [21], preoperative intraoral impression is often impractical for patients with limited mouth opening. Computer-aided design and additive manufacturing technology enable the fabrication of patient-specific occlusal splints without the need for physical impressions, thereby overcoming the limitations of conventional manual methods in achieving precise occlusal relationships. Patients with comminuted mandibular fractures frequently present with concomitant condylar fractures. Whether surgical or conservative treatment is employed, the use of a postoperative occlusal splint is essential. This approach not only helps reduce occlusal stress but also stabilizes the occlusal relationship, thereby preventing occlusal disorders and the onset of temporomandibular disorders [22].

Mechanistic and clinical implications

In Group A, the anatomical reduction of the comminuted mandibular fractures was digitally simulated, and the digital occlusal splint was designed by referencing the residual occlusal landmarks on both the maxillary and mandibular arches. Subsequently, 3D printing technology was used to fabricate the patient’s original craniofacial models, the VSP models, and the 3D-printed occlusal splints. Surgeons could use the 3D-printed models to understand the spatial relationships between fragments and determine the sequence and critical steps of fracture reduction. The digitally designed and 3D-printed occlusal splints provide a more accurate occlusal relationship than traditional arch bars, offering reliable support for the reduction and fixation of fractures.

As a secondary outcome, we also examined operative time. Some studies have reported that 3D printing technology may reduce operative time for complex fractures [23–25]. In our study, after adjusting for covariates, the estimated marginal mean operative time was lower in Group A than in Group B, but the difference was not statistically significant. Because operative time was not a primary endpoint and the difference was non-significant, no further emphasis is placed on this finding.

The application of 3D-printed occlusal splints resolves the difficulty of having insufficient remaining teeth to restore the occlusal relationships, providing a reliable reference for fractures reduction. Particularly for patients with limited mouth opening, this method eliminates the need for preoperative intraoral impression. Overall, digitally designed and 3D-printed occlusal splints can serve as potentially useful guide tools, enhance procedural accuracy.

Limitations and future directions

The primary limitation of this technique lies in its dependence on preoperative CAD and 3D model fabrication. For critically ill patients requiring urgent intervention, clinicians must carefully evaluate the clinical advantages against the extended preparatory phase through comprehensive cost-benefit analysis. Moreover, the additional preoperative time, cost, and technical requirements of the digital workflow should be carefully evaluated before routine clinical application.

In this study, Group A received multiple digital workflow advantages (3D-printed splint, pre-contoured reconstruction plates, virtual surgical planning), whereas Group B did not. The observed differences cannot be attributed solely to the splint; the entire digital workflow likely contributed. Although virtual surgical planning data were generated for Group B patients, they were not used intraoperatively and were reserved only for postoperative analytical comparison. This difference may introduce performance bias, as the lack of uniform intraoperative guidance could mean that differences in surgical outcomes are partly attributable to variations in the surgical approach rather than solely to the technical advantage of the splint.

This study is a retrospective cohort study, not an interventional or randomized trial, and the small sample size (n = 22) limits generalizability and precludes direct causal inference. The assessment of occlusal restoration relied on CT-based measurements and surgeon judgment rather than a validated occlusal scoring system. This limits the objectivity and reproducibility of the occlusal outcome. No validated clinical outcome measures (e.g., occlusal stability, quality of life) were used, and TMJ function, pain, chewing performance, and patient satisfaction were not evaluated. Improved radiographic reduction accuracy does not necessarily equal meaningful patient-centered benefit, and this remains to be investigated. The absence of functional outcome measures and the relatively short follow-up (minimum three months) restrict comprehensive assessment of clinical outcomes. Specifically, we note that a substantial proportion of patients had condylar fractures, and the absence of condylar position or condyle–fossa evaluation in either group limits the complete interpretation of occlusal and functional outcomes. The minimum follow-up of 3 months is only sufficient for short-term radiographic and healing assessment, but insufficient to assess long-term occlusal stability, TMJ adaptation, or late complications. Future studies should incorporate these measures to further validate the clinical advantages of 3D-printed occlusal splints. Therefore, the findings should be considered as an exploratory technical report, and the technique warrants further validation in larger prospective studies.

Conclusion

Digitally designed and 3D-printed occlusal splints offer a technical solution for restoring the premorbid occlusal relationship in ECM fractures with compromised dentition. This technique is not intended for all mandibular fractures but may offer benefit in selected severe cases, such as: (1) ECM fractures with extensive tooth loss or loosening that leave insufficient occlusal reference points for conventional MMF; (2) patients with preoperative limited mouth opening who cannot undergo conventional intraoral impression taking. The added preoperative workflow and cost should be weighed against the clinical necessity. This technique represents a possible adjunct. Further validation in larger prospective studies incorporating functional and patient-reported outcome measures is needed.
