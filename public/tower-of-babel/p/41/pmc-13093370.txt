Cutaneous Leishmaniasis in High‐Altitude: A Case Report From Nepal
Authors: Eliz Aryal, Rohan Sapkota, Sunny Shrestha, Arika Baral, Santosh Sapkota, Mandish Prasad Phuyal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLeishmaniasis is a parasitic infection caused by Leishmania and spread by the bite of a sandfly, most commonly Phlebotomus. Among the three subtypes of Leishmaniasis, cutaneous leishmaniasis is the most common form. Visceral Leishmaniasis is common in Nepal whereas few cases of cutaneous leishmaniasis have been reported in migrant Nepalese population. The most common lesion seen in cutaneous leishmaniasis is papulo‐nodular although other morphological lesions can also be seen. A 30 years female currently residing in Humla presented with a history of multiple non‐tender nodules with central necrosis with size approximately 3 × 4 cm and 5 × 4 cm present at left lower chin and right lower eyelid respectively. Punch biopsy was done from the site of lesion which showed the presence of amastigote form of Donovan bodies, consistent with the diagnosis of Cutaneous Leishmaniasis. The patient was treated with Oral Miltefosine for 4 weeks and advised for follow‐up. This case report highlights the importance of considering Cutaneous Leishmaniasis as one of the differential diagnoses with papulo‐nodular lesions in non‐endemic areas like Nepal.Keywords: altitude, cutaneous leishmaniasis, Nepal, Phlebotomus, skin

Introduction

Leishmaniasis is an infectious disease caused by the flagellated parasite Leishmania which is transmitted by the bite of an infected sandfly. The two common genera of sandfly vectors are Phlebotomus and Lutzomyia. Clinically, Leishmaniasis can be divided into three subtypes: cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL), visceral leishmaniasis (VL), and mucosal leishmaniasis (ML).

Cutaneous leishmaniasis is the most common form of a Leishmania infection that affects approximately 0.7 million to 1.2 million human beings [1]. It impacts the poorest of the poor and is linked to immune system weakness, lack of resources, gender discrimination, illiteracy, inadequate housing, displacement, and hunger [2]. It is most commonly seen in tropical and subtropical areas around the globe. Few cases of leishmaniasis have been reported in Nepal as well. The lesions of cutaneous leishmaniasis are painless, non‐pruritic, and frequently seen in the exposed areas of the body, initially appearing as papules and progressing to form nodulo‐ulcerative lesions with seropurulent discharge. The other forms of lesions include hypodermic, verrucous, and hemorrhagic [3].

Case Presentation

We present a case of a 30 years female currently residing in Humla presented with a history of multiple erythematous, well defined, elevated lesions present in the right lower eyelid and left chin for 6 months which started as a small, painless lesion and gradually increasing in size over next few weeks, eventually leading to ulcerated lesion with raised and indurated margins along with central necrosis (Figure 1). The lesion was not associated with pain, surrounding skin changes, itching, discharge, bleeding, and regional lymphadenopathy. There was no history of fever, abdominal pain, cough, night sweats, or weight loss.

Multiple erythematous nodular lesions with central necrosis present over right lower eyelid and chin.

She gives no h/o recent travel, trauma, and insect bites. No history of chronic illness like diabetes mellitus or hypertension. No history of contact with Tuberculosis in the family.

On general examination, the patient was conscious and well oriented. Vitals were within normal limits. There was no associated lymphadenopathy present.

On examination of lesions, there was the presence of multiple non‐tender nodules with central necrosis with a size approximately 5 × 4 cm and 3 × 4 cm present at the right lower eyelid and left lower chin, respectively. Systemic examinations were within normal limits.

Dermoscopic examination revealed the orange‐yellow background representing dermal granulomas accompanied by “yellow tears” (tadpole‐shaped follicular plugs). The vascular pattern was characteristically polymorphous, featuring comma‐shaped, dotted, and linear‐irregular vessels with a central crust or erosion surrounded by a white starburst pattern indicating peripheral fibrosis.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Based on the clinical history and examination, the differential diagnosis considered included cutaneous leishmaniasis, lupus vulgaris, cutaneous sarcoidosis, deep fungal infections. Due to financial constraints, only essential investigations were performed. Mantoux test was done, which was negative. Tests like rk39 serology or PCR can be useful in supporting the diagnosis; however, this was not feasible due to financial constraints. A chest X‐ray was done, which was within normal findings. A punch biopsy was done from the site of the lesion, which showed presence of numerous epitheloid cell granuloma along with macrophages containing tiny uniform round intracytoplasmic organisms known as Leishman donovan (LD) bodies (Figure 2). Inflammatory cell infiltrate of lymphocytes, plasma cells, eosinophils and neutrophils were seen which were consistent with features of cutaneous leishmaniasis and the diagnosis of cutaneous leishmaniasis was established. The patient was started on Oral Miltefosine 100 mg two times a day for 28 days. The patient was counseled regarding the side effects of the drug. Baseline investigations including LFT could not be obtained prior to treatment due to financial constraints. However, the patient was evaluated clinically and had no history or findings suggestive of hepatic dysfunction and was counseled regarding the potential side effects of the drug. The patient was advised for regular follow up but did not return after the initial visit and was lost to follow up due to geographical and socioeconomic limitations.

Discussion

The classical lesion of Cutaneous Leishmaniasis is the presence of a non‐pruritic lesion in the exposed part of the body, which initially starts as papulo‐nodular and progresses into ulcers with indurated borders [4]. This gradually heals over months to years, leaving a depressed scar with altered pigmentation.

Diagnosis of cutaneous leishmaniasis can be done by history and clinical examination, and confirmed by demonstration of amastigotes in Giemsa‐stained smears from infected skin by direct microscopy, intracellular amastigotes in the dermis of H&E (Hematoxylin and Eosin)‐stained sections from biopsy specimens, presence of leishmanial granulomas in the dermis in H specimens, growth of promastigotes in Nicolle‐Novy‐MacNeal (NNN) culture medium from lesional specimens, and demonstration of leishmanial DNA by PCR. Also, rapid tests like rk39 are available. In places where leishmaniasis is endemic, the clinical diagnosis is straightforward due to its characteristic symptoms [2]. However, it is easy to overlook cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) in non‐endemic nations where it is uncommon, like Nepal.

Nepal is non‐endemic to cutaneous leishmaniasis, but its cases have been increasing gradually. From the year 2016–2019, 42 CL cases were reported from 26 different hospitals and were diagnosed on the basis of clinical presentation and laboratory findings, with the facial region being the most common exposed site to be affected [5]. The first CL case was documented in Nepal in 1998, and there have since been a few intermittent cases [6]. To the best of our knowledge, the patient lived in a higher elevation area of Nepal above 2500 m before becoming ill and had never visited any places where CL was endemic or recorded. The maximum cases of cutaneous leishmaniasis have been reported from hilly and mountainous regions of Nepal, highlighting the need for active surveillance in such areas. Because of non‐endemicity and heterogeneous clinical presentation, the diagnosis of cutaneous leishmaniasis can easily be missed. The increased number of cases in non‐endemic areas like Nepal raises epidemiological concerns, suggesting the possibility of change in transmission pattern requiring further studies to understand the vector surveillance and adaptability in higher altitude areas.

The common complications of cutaneous leishmaniasis include scarring and disfigurement due to atrophic or hypertrophic scar, secondary bacterial infection leading to cellulitis or abscesses [7]. Less commonly it can even progress to involve mucosal areas leading to mucocutaneous leishmaniasis [8]. Other complications include systemic involvement, immunological reactions and affect quality of life [9]. To minimize cases of CL, prevention should focus on use of insect repellants, insecticide containing nets and curtains and maintain environmental hygiene by reducing sandfly breeding areas [10].

Management of cutaneous leishmaniasis in low‐resource settings may be limited by lack of investigations and follow‐up, highlighting the diagnostic challenges and assessment of the treatment outcomes. Furthermore, genotypic analysis and entomological surveys can be done in future to understand the transmission dynamics of Leishmaniasis in non‐endemic areas.

Conclusion

Cutaneous leishmaniasis remains a diagnostic challenge in non‐endemic regions such as Nepal, especially in high‐altitude areas, where reported cases are limited. Clinicians should maintain a high suspicion and include cutaneous leishmaniasis in the differential diagnosis, even in areas considered as non‐endemic. Strengthening national surveillance and case reporting is essential to ensure timely diagnosis and appropriate management, thereby preventing complications. This case highlights the need for genotypic analysis and entomological surveys to understand the transmission dynamics of Leishmaniasis in non‐endemic areas like Nepal.
