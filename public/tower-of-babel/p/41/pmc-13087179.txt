Survival prediction for bladder cancer using multimodal data with quantum neural networks and transformer architectures
Authors: Zhouyuan Qin, Hui Zhou, Yangsheng Hu, Xin Xiong, Jiang Lu, Heng Su, Jianfeng He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the challenges of cross-modal information fusion in high-dimensional multimodal medical data for cancer prognosis, this study presents a hybrid diagnostic accuracy model for cancer survival prediction, integrating quantum computing with classical deep learning in a retrospective analysis of bladder cancer patients. We propose QTMPN (Quantum-Transformer Multimodal Prognostic Network), a novel framework integrating quantum neural networks (QNNs), Transformers, and graph neural networks (GNNs). For high-dimensional whole-slide pathological images (WSIs), a quantum feature extractor (QFE) is designed using parallel quantum encoding and a hybrid quantum network to capture long-range dependencies. Multimodal data—including clinical and image features—are fused via a Transformer-GNN Collaborative Fusion (TCF) module employing attention-guided dynamic graphs. Evaluated on the TCGA-BLCA dataset, QTMPN attained a survival prediction accuracy of 76.1%, outperforming baseline models such as PARADIGM and CMTA (up to 70.0%). This improvement suggests the model’s enhanced capability to capture cross-modal prognostic features. Further ablation experiment validated the effectiveness of the hybrid QNNs feature extract part (QFE) in QTMPN. QTMPN presents a promising quantum-classical framework for survival risk prediction in bladder cancer, effectively modeling complex multimodal interactions. The approach contributes to improving prognostic accuracy in oncology and supporting precision medicine.

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BCa), a common type of urothelial carcinoma in the urinary system, ranks as the tenth most prevalent malignancy worldwide and remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality1. It is associated with poor prognosis and a relatively low five-year survival rate2.

In clinical diagnosis of BCa, multiple data modalities are typically integrated, including biochemical test results, pathological records, radiological imaging data (e.g., CT, PET-CT, MRI), histopathological slides, and genomic information. Radiological imaging is usually employed for preliminary diagnosis, followed by pathology and genomic sequencing for further assessment. Notably, studies focusing on the relationship between genomic data and BCa pathogenesis and prognosis have yielded significant insights1–5.

Survival analysis plays a pivotal role in cancer prognosis assessment and is crucial for evaluating diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic efficacy. Due to the inherent complexity of cancer, reliable survival prediction often necessitates the integration of heterogeneous, multimodal data sources. A key challenge is whether the survival prediction model can effectively capture multimodal features, assign appropriate weights to each modality, and achieve meaningful fusion to produce accurate prognostic outcomes6.

With advancements in computational technology, computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) systems have been increasingly introduced into BCa prognosis prediction and overall survival estimation. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been widely used to construct diagnostic models and platforms. However, most existing approaches fail to incorporate sufficiently diverse data modalities for model training and evaluation, and often lack effective strategies for feature extraction and fusion. As a result, current BCa prognosis models fall short of achieving satisfactory predictive performance.

A review of existing research reveals two primary limitations that require further investigation:

(1) Inefficiency of feature extraction methods.

Current feature extraction techniques can be further optimized for various data modalities. For textual data, natural language processing (NLP) methods are commonly employed7,8; however, more effective approaches are needed to handle clinical records that contain mixed textual and numerical formats. For genomic sequencing data, constructing expression matrices is essential to avoid isolated analysis of individual sequencing sites, which may lead to information loss. Although convolutional neural networks (CNNs), such as ResNet and InceptionNet, have been widely applied to pathological image analysis9, there remains a need to design more efficient algorithms capable of capturing subtle cellular morphological features in high-dimensional histopathological data.

(2) Ineffective fusion of multimodal features.

Most existing studies on survival prediction for bladder cancer rely solely on traditional network architectures10–14, which are inadequate for effectively fusing multimodal data features. As a result, the highest reported prediction accuracy remains limited to approximately 70%. In contrast, the integration of Transformer and graph neural network (GNN) architectures offers improved capability for cross-modal feature learning. This hybrid design enhances the model’s ability to capture complex inter-modal interactions and enables more effective representation learning across heterogeneous modalities.

To address the aforementioned challenges, this study proposes a multimodal survival prediction model for bladder cancer based on adaptive graph neural networks and quantum computing. The model is designed to enhance both modality-specific feature extraction and cross-modal feature fusion, thereby mitigating the limitations of existing approaches. Clinical multimodal data feature fusion

Cancer clinical diagnosis requires integration of heterogeneous multimodal clinical representations from diverse sources, including electronic health records, pathological and radiological images, laboratory test results, and genomic data. Cross-modal feature fusion is typically achieved via attention mechanisms that capture inter-modality dependencies15, thereby supporting multimodal-assisted diagnostic decision-making. Existing cross-modal fusion methods can be broadly categorized into five types: direct concatenation of modality-specific features, attention-based fusion across modalities, subspace-based fusion, Transformer-based fusion, and graph neural network (GNN)-based fusion16. Prior studies have leveraged multimodal fusion techniques to integrate histopathological images and genomic data for cancer survival outcome prediction. For instance17, employed the Kronecker product to model cross-modal feature interactions and introduced GNNs combined with gated attention mechanisms to regulate feature expression. Other approaches, such as the weighted multimodal U-Net (W-MM-U-Net), enhance multimodal feature learning by optimizing skip connection weights18. To address feature conflict issues, the DeAF framework applies modality-adaptive alignment techniques coupled with supervised learning to effectively fuse medical imaging and clinical data19.

Recent advances in deep learning have led to the development of specialized architectures for various medical image analysis tasks. For diabetic retinopathy detection, a systematic evaluation of CNN models on the ODIR dataset demonstrated that a ResNet50-LSTM hybrid achieved the highest accuracy of 99.74%, with the Adam optimizer providing greater stability than RMSprop20. In skin cancer classification, the BCB–CSPA network, which integrates bipartite convolutional blocks with a condensed semantic perceptual attention mechanism, attained an accuracy of 95.92% and employed Grad-CAM for interpretability, though limitations regarding dataset diversity and clinical deployment were noted21. For thyroid nodule segmentation in ultrasound, the hybrid TATHA model, incorporating attention mechanisms and dilated convolutions, outperformed benchmarks such as U-Net and Vision Transformers on the DDTI dataset, showing strong capability in handling noise and low contrast22. In liver tumor segmentation, the unified Inception-U-Net hybrid gravitational optimization model (UIGO) achieved a segmentation accuracy of 99.93% and a Dice coefficient of 0.997 on multi-source datasets, demonstrating high precision and computational efficiency23. For breast cancer segmentation, the CICADA (UCX) model combined U-Net with a ConvNeXt-inspired architecture and a cheetah-inspired optimization algorithm, reaching a Dice coefficient of 99.65% and an AUC of 99.88%, indicating superior performance in malignancy delineation24. In brain tumor classification, the PABT-Net model, which integrates a pyramidal attention mechanism with a bi-partitioned T-network and NASNetMobile, achieved an overall accuracy of 99.12% across multiple MRI datasets, though challenges in explainability and dataset diversity were acknowledged25. For lung cancer detection in histopathological images, the DY-FSPAN framework utilized dilated Y-blocks and a feature-summarized pyramidal attention mechanism to attain 98.5% classification accuracy, with Grad-CAM visualizations employed to enhance interpretability26.

These methods employ deep neural networks to achieve complementary integration of multimodal features. However, their effectiveness in cross-modal data fusion remains limited. To address this issue, improvements can be made to the inter-modality attention mechanisms to enhance the correlation between cross-modal features, thereby ensuring effective fusion of multimodal information.

Prognostic survival prediction of bladder cancer based on multimodal data

Cancer survival prediction is a weakly supervised ordinal regression task that requires complex modeling of the tumor microenvironment within whole-slide images (WSIs)10. Multimodal graph neural networks (MGNNs) integrate gene expression, copy number variation, and clinical data by constructing a patient-data bipartite graph structure. Graph neural networks are then employed to learn embedding representations, and a fusion layer is used to achieve effective feature integration27.

Research on multimodal survival prediction of bladder cancer based on TCGA-BLCA primarily focuses on the fusion of two or three modalities10–14,28–33. In bimodal studies10, employs cross-modal attention mapping to associate WSI pathological images with genomic features;11 utilizes a self-normalizing network to process gene expression and clinical data;12 optimizes pathological feature representation through hypergraph construction and tumor microenvironment analysis;28 introduces Vision Transformer to extract global pathological image features and dynamically adjusts multimodal fusion weights using Dempster–Shafer evidence theory;13,29 propose the BPE-MoME framework and a cross-modal attention-enhanced bidirectional encoder-decoder architecture to strengthen modality interaction. However, these six studies10–13,28,29 utilize only two modalities, limiting their ability to comprehensively capture the heterogeneity of cross-modal associations, which weakens the predictive power of the models for patient prognosis. Consequently, the accuracy of prognosis prediction in these models remains relatively low. In contrast, trimodal studies further integrate pathological images, clinical records, and genomic data:14 extracts redundant, synergistic, and modality-specific information via multimodal knowledge decomposition;30 generates multiresolution aggregated representations based on graph neural networks;31 combines random forests with gene expression data to identify metastatic biomarkers. Additionally32,33, incorporate CT imaging from the TCIA database, employing structural equation modeling to analyze multimodal associations and utilizing 3D U-Net for molecular subtype classification, revealing differential features between high- and low-grade bladder cancers. Studies34–36, benefiting from increased data modalities, enhance the representation capacity of complex clinical data through the correlations among the three modalities, resulting in improved prognostic prediction accuracy compared to bimodal models.

These studies leverage deep learning methods to achieve complementary integration of multimodal data, progressively improving survival prediction accuracy. However, in current survival prediction tasks based on the TCGA-BLCA database, there remains significant room for improvement in the effectiveness of multimodal feature fusion and the overall accuracy of survival prediction. Future efforts can focus on two key aspects for enhancement: effective feature extraction and efficient feature fusion. However, the performance of these deep learning models is fundamentally constrained by the classical computing paradigm. Key limitations persist in handling the high-dimensionality and inherent heterogeneity of multimodal clinical data. These include computational inefficiency in modeling complex, non-linear interactions across modalities (e.g., images, genomics, clinical variables), suboptimal fusion that may not fully capture synergistic information, and a heightened risk of overfitting when training complex networks on limited, noisy biomedical datasets, which ultimately caps prognostic accuracy and generalizability.

Multimodal tasks based on quantum neural networks

In recent years, an increasing number of image-based tasks have begun to explore the use of quantum neural networks (QNNs) for high-dimensional feature extraction, demonstrating promising performance. Quantum computing leverages principles of quantum mechanics, such as superposition and entanglement, for information processing. Its fundamental unit, the quantum bit (qubit), can exist in a superposition of both 0 and 1 states simultaneously, endowing quantum computers with a theoretical computational advantage for certain problems37. Quantum convolutional neural networks (QCNNs), as a representative application of quantum computing in neural network architectures, have shown superior feature extraction capabilities compared to classical CNNs in image recognition tasks, particularly excelling in preserving high-dimensional features38,39.

Quantum convolutional neural networks (QCNNs) have demonstrated significant potential in medical image analysis, offering efficient processing of high-dimensional medical data and improving diagnostic accuracy. Numerous studies have validated the superior noise robustness and feature extraction efficiency of QCNNs compared to conventional neural network models across various clinical tasks, including cardiac disease prediction40, brain disorder analysis41, Alzheimer’s disease classification42, and breast cancer detection43–45. Furthermore, quantum fusion models integrating multimodal medical data, such as computed tomography (CT), whole slide images (WSI), and clinical records, have further confirmed the advantages of QCNNs in cross-modal data integration and analysis46,47.

The parallel processing capabilities and inherent robustness of quantum computing offer promising improvements for traditional medical multimodal data analysis. Quantum neural networks (QNNs) have the potential to directly address the core limitations of classical models in survival prediction. By leveraging quantum principles such as superposition and entanglement, QNNs can natively process and represent high-dimensional data in exponentially large state spaces, enabling more efficient encoding of complex features from histopathology or genomics. Furthermore, quantum entanglement provides a powerful, innate mechanism for modeling deep, non-linear correlations between different data modalities, potentially leading to more effective and integrative fusion. This foundational capability may enhance model robustness to noise and improve generalization from smaller datasets, paving the way for more accurate and reliable prognostic systems.

Methods

In this study, we propose a multimodal survival prediction model, termed Quantum-Transformer Multimodal Prediction Network (QTMPN), which integrates quantum networks and Transformer architectures. The model comprises three key modules: Gene Optimization & Denoising Operation (GODO) for gene selection and noise reduction, Quantum Feature Extraction (QFE) for parallel quantum-based representation learning, and Transformer-GNN Co-Fusion (TCF) for dual-stream collaborative fusion. The QTMPN is applied to multimodal data from the TCGA-BLCA dataset for bladder cancer survival prediction.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the proposed framework consists of three main stages. First, during the Gene Optimization & Denoising Operation (GODO) data preprocessing phase, the three modalities for each patient (electronic health records (EHR), genomic data, and whole slide images (WSI)) are individually subjected to modality-specific data cleaning and preprocessing. Next, in the Quantum Feature Extraction (QFE) stage, corresponding feature extraction methods are applied to each modality to generate modality-specific feature matrices. Finally, in the Transformer-GNN Co-Fusion (TCF) cross-modal feature fusion stage, the extracted features from all three modalities are integrated, followed by survival prediction based on a graph neural network (GNN), yielding the final prognostic outcome.

Feature extraction based on quantum feature extraction (QFE)

Feature extraction for electronic health records and genomic data

Categorical textual data, such as pathological T staging, were transformed into numerical vectors via one-hot encoding. This conversion facilitates effective integration and processing of these features within the subsequent modeling pipeline.

Numerical features within the electronic health records and genomic data were standardized using StandardScaler to eliminate scale differences among features. This normalization improves model training stability and convergence by ensuring consistent feature distributions.

After the aforementioned preprocessing steps, feature matrices representing patient-specific electronic health records and genomic data were obtained, serving as inputs for subsequent quantum feature extraction.

Feature extraction for pathological image data

Leveraging the superposition and entanglement properties of qubits, a hybrid quantum-classical network architecture is constructed. Qubits encode multidimensional features in parallel through amplitude encoding of superposition states, while entanglement is utilized to model cross-domain feature correlations. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the proposed hybrid framework first employs ResNet-101 to extract preliminary image features. Subsequently, these features are further processed by quantum computing units implementing nonlinear transformations via parameterized quantum gate sequences. Through feature matrix learning and downsampling, patient-specific pathological image feature matrices are generated for downstream analysis.

For example, if a qubit is initially in state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left|0\right\rangle=\left[\begin{array}{c}1\\0\end{array}\right]$$\end{document}, after applying the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{R}}_{\mathrm{y}}\left({\uptheta}\right)$$\end{document} operation, its state becomes:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {R_y}(\theta )\left| 0 \right\rangle = & \left[ {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} {\cos \left( {\frac{\theta }{2}} \right)}&{ - \sin \left( {\frac{\theta }{2}} \right)} \\ {\sin \left( {\frac{\theta }{2}} \right)}&{\cos \left( {\frac{\theta }{2}} \right)} \end{array}} \right]\left[ {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} 1 \\ 0 \end{array}} \right]=\left[ {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} {\cos \left( {\frac{\theta }{2}} \right)} \\ {\sin \left( {\frac{\theta }{2}} \right)} \end{array}} \right] \\ & =\cos \left( {\frac{\theta }{2}} \right)\left| 0 \right\rangle +\sin \left( {\frac{\theta }{2}} \right)\left| 1 \right\rangle \\ \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Subsequently, entanglement among qubits is introduced via Basic Entangler Layers. This step utilizes two-qubit entangling gates such as the CNOT gate.

When the control qubit is in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left|1\right\rangle$$\end{document} state, the target qubit’s state is flipped, thereby generating entanglement among multiple qubits. The entangled state of multiple qubits can be expressed as (taking two qubits as an example):5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left| \psi \right\rangle = \alpha _{{00}} \left| {00} \right\rangle + \alpha _{{01}} \left| {01} \right\rangle + \alpha _{{10}} \left| {10} \right\rangle + \alpha _{{11}} \left| {11} \right.$$\end{document}

Finally, each qubit is measured using the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{i}-\mathrm{Z}$$\end{document} operator, and the expectation values are returned.The matrix representation of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{i}-\mathrm{Z}$$\end{document} operator is given by:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z=\left[\begin{array}{cc}1&0\\0&-1\end{array}\right]$$\end{document}

For a quantum state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left|{\uppsi}\right\rangle={\upalpha}\left|0\right\rangle+{\upbeta}\left|1\right\rangle$$\end{document}, the measurement yields an expectation value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\langle {\psi \left| Z \right|\psi } \right\rangle ={\left|{\upalpha}\right|}^{2}-{\left|{\upbeta}\right|}^{2}$$\end{document}, which is subsequently utilized for downstream data processing.

During the construction of the quantum layer, the features extracted by ResNet are reshaped and partitioned into 16 segments, corresponding to 16 qubits. Each segment is processed using the previously defined quantum circuit, and the measurement results for all segments are concatenated and averaged to form the output of the quantum layer. By integrating quantum computation into the model in this manner, the intrinsic properties of quantum systems, such as superposition and entanglement, are leveraged to enable more efficient and expressive feature extraction. This enhances the representational capacity of the model and provides more informative features for subsequent multimodal data fusion and survival prediction tasks, thereby improving overall model performance.

Cross-modal feature fusion based on transformer-GNN Co-fusion (TCF)

With the recent advancements of Transformer-based models in multimodal medical tasks36, the attention mechanism offers a novel approach for cross-modal interaction. Inspired by the MCAT model10, which performs pathology–genomics co-attention mapping, and Graph Transformer Networks (GTNs)48, this study incorporates a pre-Transformer layer to construct an adaptive graph structure for cross-modal fusion of QFE-processed features. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the modality-specific features obtained from the QFE module are first concatenated. Subsequently, cross-modal feature fusion is performed via a Transformer network equipped with scaled dot-product attention and multi-head attention mechanisms. Finally, an adaptive graph neural network is constructed to facilitate structured representation learning, enabling enhanced integration of heterogeneous modalities for downstream survival prediction tasks.

Weighted cross-entropy loss function

The class distribution in our cohort (221 alive vs. 173 deceased) indeed presents an imbalance. To mitigate its potential influence on model training, we employed a weighted cross-entropy loss function. This assigns a higher penalty to misclassifications of the minority class (deceased), thereby directing the optimization process to pay more attention to correctly identifying these cases. No oversampling or undersampling techniques were applied, ensuring that all models were evaluated under the same, realistic data distribution to facilitate a fair comparison.

Results

Dataset and data preprocessing

(1) Dataset description The database used in this study is the TCGA-BLCA bladder cancer database. This database contains data from 412 bladder cancer patients, primarily comprising three modalities: genomic data, clinical text data, and pathological slide data. By effectively utilizing these three modalities, this study aims to provide a more accurate assessment of the overall condition of bladder cancer and offer scientific evidence to support survival prediction tasks.

Based on the completeness of the three modalities in the database, 394 patients with all three modal data were selected to construct the experimental dataset in this study. According to patient survival status, there are three categories: Alive, Dead, and Not Reported. The Not Reported category is included in Alive, resulting in 221 surviving patients and 173 deceased patients. According to tumor staging, patients are classified into four stages: stage i–iv. For both classification types, patient gender and age were compared and analyzed. (3 Feature extraction and dimensionality reduction of small lesion patches: Preliminary feature extraction was performed using ResNet-101 as in50, extracting features from 1000 lesion patches (224 × 224 pixels) per patient, resulting in a [1000 × 1000] feature matrix. Dimensionality reduction was achieved via a quantum autoencoder constructed with a 16-qubit QCNN layer. Using amplitude encoding and quantum entanglement, the autoencoder compressed the high-dimensional features nonlinearly into a [1000]-dimensional latent space.

Experimental environment

The experiments were conducted on a device equipped with an Intel Core i9-12900 K processor, an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 24GB GPU, and 128GB of RAM. The proposed model was trained for 500 epochs to sufficiently learn the data features. To enhance the model’s generalization capability and evaluation accuracy, a 10-fold cross-validation was performed for each training session, repeatedly partitioning the dataset into training and validation sets for iterative training and validation.

Experimental results

Through hyperparameter tuning process, the final optimal settings were determined as dropout = 0.7 and lr = 0.007. Under these settings, the model achieved approximately 76.1% accuracy on bladder cancer survival prediction. The AUC score (0.758 ± 0.058) and F1-score (0.725 ± 0.070) further indicate good comprehensive discriminative capability of the model.

Discussion

QTMPN survival prediction model

As shown in Table 4, QTMPN demonstrates strong prognostic performance via 10-fold cross-validation, achieving an accuracy of 0.761 ± 0.055 and AUC of 0.758 ± 0.058 on the TCGA-BLCA dataset, indicating effective multimodal learning and reliable class discrimination. Specificity was higher (0.788 ± 0.074) than sensitivity (0.727 ± 0.113), suggesting more consistent identification of negative samples.

The confusion matrix (Fig. 5) reveals a balanced distribution of errors (FP = 47, FN = 47), indicating an unbiased decision boundary and stable generalization. These results indicate that QTMPN effectively captures key discriminative features between outcome classes, maintaining both accuracy and robustness across modalities.

Limitations

Scalability with respect to data volume

The scalability of the quantum feature extraction (QFE) module was evaluated on a cohort of 394 patients. This data scale is representative and directly comparable to that used in several recent and influential studies within the domain of computational pathology and hybrid quantum-classical learning (e.g., CMTA, PARADIGM). While extending the model to future, larger multi-center cohorts is a natural direction for validation, we note that the associated increase in computational cost is expected to scale in a manageable manner. With established techniques such as efficient mini-batching and distributed data loading, training times for expanded datasets can be maintained within practical bounds, ensuring the framework’s adaptability for broader application.

Hardware and computational overhead

The computational experiments in this study were conducted on a single consumer-grade GPU (NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090). It is noteworthy that many contemporary studies in machine learning for healthcare utilize dedicated computational servers or multiple high-end GPUs, representing a significantly greater resource baseline. This context highlights that the performance gains achieved by our QFE module were obtained under relatively constrained hardware conditions. The computational overhead, primarily from circuit simulation, is a known factor in hybrid quantum-classical research. We anticipate that this cost will remain contained even with larger data volumes, and future work will focus on algorithmic optimizations (e.g., circuit compression) and leveraging cloud-based quantum simulation services to further improve efficiency for eventual translational deployment.

Multi-dataset generalization performance

The model was developed and validated exclusively on the TCGA-BLCA dataset. While this public cohort serves as a valuable benchmark, performance on independent, multi-center cohorts remains unestablished, representing a key limitation for assessing broad clinical generalizability. Importantly, this reliance on a single dataset primarily stems from the significant practical constraints inherent in acquiring real-world, multi-modal clinical data from hospital settings.

This limitation encompasses not only potential demographic biases—where even a diverse cohort like TCGA may not fully represent broader populations—but also, and more critically, the systematic challenges of multi-omics data integration. Constructing a sizable external validation set with matched histopathological, genomic, and clinical profiles faces multiple difficulties. First, the longitudinal nature of clinical outcome data implies that the timeline from patient enrollment to obtaining analyzable survival data often spans several years. Second, comprehensive genomic profiling is cost-prohibitive and often falls outside standard care. Furthermore, multi-institutional ethics approvals and data-sharing agreements involve complex and lengthy processes. These time- and resource-intensive practical barriers fundamentally restrict the scale of available data and may introduce selection bias, as the patient population with ultimately complete multi-modal data may have limited representativeness.

Consequently, many studies in this field, including the present methodological exploration, initially validate models on curated public datasets like TCGA as a necessary and common step given these constraints. To advance clinical translation, our future work will prioritize establishing collaborative partnerships to compile a multi-center validation cohort, with explicit attention to demographic diversity, to prospectively evaluate the model’s robustness and mitigate potential biases.

Conclusion

This study presents QTMPN, a quantum Transformer based multimodal graph learning framework for survival prediction using TCGA-BLCA bladder cancer data. The model integrates genomic, clinical, and pathological image modalities through modality-specific preprocessing strategies: correlation based gene selection, clinical feature cleaning via GODO, and a quantum feature extraction (QFE) method for whole-slide images that combines quantum computation with classical networks to improve high-dimensional representation.

A cross-modal fusion module (TCF) is introduced, leveraging multi-head attention and adaptive GNNs to capture inter-sample associations and facilitate modality integration. On the TCGA-BLCA dataset, QTMPN exhibits improved survival prediction accuracy compared to state-of-the-art models (e.g., PARADIGM, CMTA), suggesting that the integration of quantum encoding, Transformer attention, and graph learning enhances the capture of shared and complementary features across modalities. These findings highlight the potential of hybrid quantum-classical frameworks for medical multimodal analysis, though prospective validation in independent cohorts and broader clinical datasets is warranted to further assess generalizability.

The clinical translation of the QTMPN framework faces practical challenges that must be addressed for real-world deployment. While the model demonstrates high analytical accuracy in a controlled research setting, its current computational footprint, reliance on high-resolution whole-slide images (WSIs), and inference speed may strain typical hospital IT infrastructure and workflow timeliness. Seamless integration with existing hospital information systems and regulatory pathways also presents non-trivial hurdles.

However, this study confirms the core technical viability and diagnostic value of the approach. Future work will prioritize engineering optimizations for clinical feasibility, including the development of a streamlined, lightweight inference pipeline, exploration of cloud-based or hybrid computing solutions to manage resource demands, and collaboration with clinical partners to pilot the system in operational environments, thereby bridging the gap between proof-of-concept and point-of-care utility.
