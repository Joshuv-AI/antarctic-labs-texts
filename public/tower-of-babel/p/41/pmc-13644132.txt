Societal worries and mental disorders in the general population: insights from the Netherlands Mental Health Survey and Incidence Study (NEMESIS)
Authors: Margreet Ten Have, Marlous Tuithof, Saskia Van Dorsselaer, Simone Korteling, Laura Shields-Zeeman, Annemarie I. Luik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWorries about societal issues such as climate change, pandemics, geopolitical tensions, and economic instability may negatively impact mental health. However, few studies have assessed a broad range of worries within a single study, examining both individual and cumulative associations with mental disorders.MethodsIn this cross-sectional analysis of wave 2 of NEMESIS-3, a population-based study conducted in 2023–2024 (n = 4,688; age 21–78) worries about climate, COVID-19, international tensions, energy supply/prices, daily living costs, and the housing market were each assessed with a single item and dichotomized (1 = "a lot" or "very much"; 0 = "not” or “a little"). Mental disorders were measured using the Composite International Diagnostic Interview 3.0. Logistic regression models estimated associations between worries and 12-month prevalence of mood, anxiety, and substance use disorders, adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics.Results38.3% of respondents were worried about international tensions and 34.0% about housing; 25.9%-28.3% about climate, energy, and daily costs; 1.4% about COVID-19. Worries were modestly intercorrelated. Worries about COVID-19, international tensions, energy, and housing were associated with higher odds of mood (range adjusted odds ratios: 1.54–5.61), anxiety (1.27–2.68), and substance use disorder (1.44–4.27). Climate worries were related to anxiety and substance use disorder (1.47–1.83); daily cost worries to mood and anxiety disorder (1.72–1.81). Each additional worry increased the odds of any disorder by 24%.ConclusionsWorries about societal issues are common and associated with mental disorders. These findings suggest that societal worries may be relevant to consider in public mental health practice and future longitudinal research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28377-y.

Background

People around the world are increasingly confronted with a range of societal issues that have far-reaching global implications, such as climate change, pandemics, escalating geopolitical tensions, and economic turmoil characterized by rising consumer prices, increased living costs and housing shortages [13, 14]. Most of these societal issues have been shown to influence the mental health of those most impacted (for example [4, 5, 28, 29, 33, 36]. However, these issues can also generate substantial worry among the broader population, not just those directly affected [12, 14]. Such widespread worries have been hypothesized to negatively influence mental health, often referred to as an indirect effect of societal issues (e.g., [5, 28, 37, 38, 40]). Possible mechanisms linking societal worries to poorer mental health include intolerance of uncertainty, repetitive negative thinking, and heightened stress responses, particularly when societal threats are perceived as severe or difficult to control [2, 15]. Alternatively, the effect may be in the reverse direction, whereby individuals with poorer mental health may be more likely to experience heightened societal worries due to negative attentional or interpretative biases and greater difficulty tolerating uncertainty [49]. A third possibility is that shared vulnerabilities or contextual stressors contribute to both heightened societal worries and poorer mental health. Given mounting evidence of a substantial decline in adult mental health over the past two decades [18, 22, 42, 46, 52], further investigation into the relationship between societal worries and mental health is warranted.

Worries about climate change have received increasing attention recently. A recent systematic review concluded that climate change worry, even in the absence of direct, first-hand experiences is associated with poorer mental health outcomes [8]. Four studies reported that higher levels of climate change worry in the general population were linked to increased symptoms of depression and/or anxiety, with small to medium effect sizes [3, 9, 35, 45]. Conversely, a relatively small study found no significant association between climate change worry and symptoms of depression or anxiety [23]. Notably, studies examining related constructs, such as climate anxiety or eco-anxiety which are defined as a chronic fear of environmental doom [7], have reported stronger associations with symptoms of depression and/or anxiety [8].

Though the mental health impact of the COVID-19 pandemic has been extensively studied, most of these studies focused on the prevalence of symptoms during the pandemic [33], and fewer examined pandemic-related worries. In a quota-based sample of adults in the United Kingdom, pandemic-related worries were associated with symptoms of depression and anxiety [32]. Similarly, a population-based study in the Netherlands among middle-aged and older adults reported a significant association between COVID-19-related worries and anxiety symptoms, although no such association was found for depressive symptoms [11]. However, most population-based studies conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic did not use diagnostic instruments to assess mental disorders [24, 33, 42], which makes it unknown to which extent COVID-19-related worries are associated with clinically defined mood and/or anxiety disorders.

Worries about geopolitical tensions among people not directly affected by conflict have received relatively little attention, despite their potential association with poorer mental health outcomes. Direct exposure to geopolitical tension or war can clearly impact mental health [6, 27], but also continuous exposure to media coverage of wars, conflicts, economic crises, and humanitarian disasters may increase anxiety levels [1, 34]. International tensions – similar to the COVID-19 pandemic and climate change – can also contribute to greater social and political polarization [41, 47, 50], whereby individuals may feel alienated from others holding different views.

Globally there has also been an increase in daily living costs, especially related to energy, essential goods, and housing, which may be associated with increased financial strain and heightened worries regarding the affordability of daily life [12]. For financial strain and related worries, it is well-known that these are associated with poorer mental health outcomes [31]. Financial stress – reflected in indicators such as material deprivation, unsecured and/or personal debt, financial hardship, and subjective perceptions of financial stress – is consistently associated with increased depressive symptoms, particularly among low-income populations [19]. Similarly, a significant increase in depressive symptoms was reported during and after periods of economic recession, such as the global financial crisis of 2008 [20]. However, evidence regarding the association between financial stress and anxiety symptoms remains limited and inconclusive. Furthermore, specific worries related to energy supply/prices, other daily living costs, and the housing market have rarely been examined in isolation within this context. Although prior research on the 2008 financial crisis has demonstrated persistent mental health effects [17, 20, 39], the specific contribution of distinct worries on clinically relevant mental disorders remain underexplored.

To narrow this knowledge gap, this study examines the cross-sectional association between societal worries and three commonly occurring mental disorders (mood, anxiety and substance use disorders) in a representative Dutch cohort study (Netherlands Mental Health Survey and Incidence Study, NEMESIS). Specifically, we (1) assessed to what extent adults are worried about societal issues across six domains (climate, COVID-19, international tensions, energy supply/prices, other daily living costs, and housing market) and (2) investigated to what extent these worries are associated with three common mental disorders.

Methods

Study design

For this study we analyzed cross-sectional data from those participating in the second wave of NEMESIS-3 between January 2023 and November 2024, when societal worries were first measured. NEMESIS‐3 [42, 43] is a population-based longitudinal cohort study with a multistage, stratified random sampling procedure. Between November 2019 and March 2022 respondents were recruited for the baseline wave of NEMESIS-3. A random sample of municipalities was drawn, of which in turn a random sample of individuals aged 18–75 years was drawn from the Dutch population register for these municipalities. Individuals with insufficient command of the Dutch language as well as institutionalized individuals (i.e., those living in hostels, hospices or prisons) were excluded. Individuals temporarily living in institutions were contacted for an interview upon returning home if applicable. The interviews were almost all held at the respondent's home.

Three years later these respondents were followed up in the second wave between January 2023 and November 2024 to be able to address longitudinal questions, which was also when societal worries were first measured. Of the 6,135 respondents invited for the second wave, 4,688 participated (76.4%). Respondents of the baseline wave (n = 6,194) reflected the Dutch population reasonably well, but younger people, higher secondary educated people, people not living with a partner, people living in more urbanized areas, and people of non‐Dutch origin were somewhat underrepresented. Attrition between both waves was significantly associated with any mental disorder and any mood disorder in the 12 months prior to baseline [44]. After weighting the data for selective nonresponse at baseline and attrition at follow-up, the distribution of the sociodemographic characteristics of those who participated at the second wave was very similar to that of the Dutch population.

The study was reviewed and positively assessed by a local ethical review committee. As the study was considered outside the scope of the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO; reference number: WAG/mb/19/017126; May 15, 2019) no further official ethical approval was required. All respondents provided written informed consent before participation at each wave, after receiving written and verbal information about the study.

Measures

Societal worries

In this study, worry refers to respondents’ self-reported level of worry about a given societal issue. This operationalization does not distinguish between cognitive, emotional, or behavioral components of worry. Societal worries were assessed with six domain-specific single items. These items were included to capture worry about several currently salient societal issues and were not formally validated. These items were introduced with a general statement indicating that the following questions concerned worries that people may have. No additional definitions or explanations were provided for the individual domains; respondents therefore rated each item based on their own interpretation. To capture worry about currently salient societal issues, respondents were asked about six specific societal worries commonly reported in the Netherlands. Specifically, respondents were asked: “How worried are you today about 1) the climate, 2) the Corona virus or the measures against it, 3) international tensions, 4) energy supply and energy prices, 5) the costs of other things in daily life, and 6) the housing market?”. For the main analyses, the 4-point worry items were dichotomized to distinguish respondents with elevated worry (“a lot” or “very much”) from those with absent or limited worry (“not” or “a little”). This allowed for a straightforward interpretation of elevated worries across domains, increased power, and enabled us to construct a cumulative count of worries variable.

Mental disorders

A slightly modified CIDI version 3.0 was used to assess mood (major depressive disorder, persistent depressive disorder, bipolar disorder), anxiety (panic disorder, agoraphobia, social anxiety disorder or social phobia, specific phobia, generalized anxiety disorder) and substance use disorders (alcohol use disorder, drug use disorder) according to the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria [43] in the previous 12 months. The CIDI 3.0 is a fully structured diagnostic interview, developed for use in the World Mental Health Survey Initiative [25]. As described elsewhere [43], the modifications included removal of some questions because previous analyses indicated that they rarely affected diagnostic classification, adjustment of a limited number of skips/referrals, and addition of questions needed to derive DSM-5 diagnoses. Most DSM-5 diagnoses could be derived from information already present in the original CIDI 3.0 through relatively minor algorithmic adaptations, although more extensive changes were required to determine alcohol and drug use disorders. Before omitting questions or changing skips/referrals, the impact of these adaptations on DSM-IV prevalence estimates was examined in NEMESIS-2 and found to be small and non-significant for the broad categories of mood, anxiety, and substance use disorders.

Sociodemographic variables

Information on sex, age, education (primary or lower secondary, higher secondary, higher vocational or university), living situation (living with partner and children, with partner without children, single parent, alone, with other(s)), employment (paid job, homemaker, student, unemployed/disabled, retired), and urbanicity (rural, city) was collected during the interview.

Statistical analyses

First, descriptives of the total population were calculated. Second, the correlations between the different types of worries were estimated using pairwise correlation coefficients. Third, the associations between sociodemographic characteristics and societal worries were assessed using separate univariable logistic regression models for each sociodemographic characteristic-worry combination, and associations with the number of societal worries were assessed using separate univariable poisson regression models for each sociodemographic characteristic. Fourth, the associations between societal worries and common mental disorders were assessed using separate multivariable logistic regression models for each societal worry-mental disorder combination. Each model included one societal worry variable as the main predictor, first adjusted for sex and age, and then adjusted for all sociodemographic variables. As a sensitivity analysis, the main association models were re-estimated using the original 4-point worry variables as categorical predictors instead of the dichotomized worry variables. As an additional exploratory analysis, we examined associations for five specific disorders with sufficient occurrence in our dataset (n > 100): major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, social phobia, specific phobia, and alcohol use disorder.

Analyses were performed using STATA 16.1. Two-tailed tests were used with statistical significance set a p < 0.05. Analyses accounted for the complex survey design by incorporating sampling weights, stratification, and clustering at the municipality level. Missing data were minimal, worry about COVID-19 and worry about the housing market each had one missing value; all other variables were complete. Observations with missing values were excluded only from the respective analyses (i.e., variable-specific complete-case analysis). No imputation was performed because the amount of missing data was negligible.

Results

Sample characteristics

The weighted sample consisted of 50.1% female, with a mean age of 49.0 years (standard error (SE) = 0.32). Most respondents had higher vocational or university education (77.2%), lived with a partner (66.0%) and were employed (65.2%). Over a third of the respondents were worried about international tensions (38.3%) and the housing market (34.0%). About a quarter were worried about the climate (28.3%), energy supply/prices (25.9%) and costs of daily living (27.5%). Only 1.4% was worried about COVID-19 or measures against it (Table 1). Overall, individuals who expressed worries about a societal issue were more likely to experience any mental disorder compared to those who did not express such worries (Table 5: range aORs 1.38—4.63). Discussion

In this study, we found that worries about climate change, international tensions, energy, daily living costs, and the housing market are common among Dutch adults, whereas worries about COVID-19 were minimal in 2023–2024. Correlations between these worries were modest. Most worries showed similar associations with the presence of any common mental disorder after adjusting for sociodemographic factors, with the exception of COVID-19-related worries, which were markedly more strongly associated with mental disorders. Each additional worry was associated with a 24% increase in the likelihood of experiencing any common mental disorder.

Worries about climate were significantly associated with anxiety disorders, consistent with global findings on eco-anxiety and symptoms of anxiety [8], which is not surprising as these may in part be overlapping constructs. Similar to the systematic review by [8], we saw stronger associations with anxiety disorders than with mood disorders, though the latter was not significant in our study. The systematic review did not examine the association with substance use disorders, which showed the strongest association in our sample. As this association has not been systematically investigated to date [48], further research is needed to confirm whether those with climate-related worries are more likely to have substance abuse or substance use disorders.

The minimal worries about COVID-19 likely reflect both the currently lower infection rates and the termination of infection control measures since 2022. The relatively strong associations between these worries and all three common mental disorders may suggest that individuals who continued to report COVID-19-related worries during the past-pandemic phase may constitute a particularly vulnerable subgroup. Potentially this may in part reflect persons who suffer from long COVID, which has an estimated prevalence of 6.0% in the general population although estimates vary widely [16, 51]. Experiencing long COVID may naturally worsen worries about COVID-19, but it also has a high co-morbidity and partially overlapping symptoms with common mental disorders [10]. As we did not have any data on long COVID we were not able to test this hypothesis. On the other hand, these could also be persons potentially characterized by underlying psychological distress or heightened health anxiety per se. Another possible explanation is that, for some individuals, COVID-19-related worries may reflect a broader sensitivity to invisible and unpredictable threats, which may be particularly salient in anxiety-related psychopathology. However, the weak correlations between worries about COVID-19 and worries about the other societal issues suggest that this may not fully explain the observed associations.

Worries about international tensions were significantly associated with mood, anxiety, and substance use disorders. To our knowledge, these associations have not previously been examined in studies employing a diagnostic interview. However, they suggest that the impact of geopolitical tensions on mental health may be widespread. Whether this association can be partly explained by increasing social and political polarization [30, 47], potentially exacerbating feelings of isolation and disconnection, remains to be determined.

Worries related to energy supply/prices and the housing market were significantly associated with mood, anxiety, and substance use disorders, while worries regarding the costs of daily living showed significant associations with mood disorders and anxiety disorders. These findings align with previous research linking financial stress and periods of economic recession to depressive symptoms [20], as well as with existing literature on financial stress more broadly [19], and underscore the importance of financial stability and access to economic security as key determinants of mental health.

Although the worries were not highly correlated, most showed a similar association with common mental disorders, except for a much stronger association of worries about COVID-19 with mental disorders. Having multiple worries was associated with a higher likelihood of a common mental disorder. Exploratory analyses of major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, social phobia, specific phobia, and alcohol use disorder were broadly consistent with the main findings, while also suggesting that the use of broad diagnostic categories may have obscured some variation across specific diagnoses. Future research should examine whether different societal worries are differentially associated with specific disorders where sufficient numbers are available.

Taken together, these findings are consistent with several possible explanations: (1) having these worries may be associated with a higher likelihood of mental disorders, (2) having mental disorders may heighten worries about societal issues, (3) shared causes may contribute to both, or (4) a combination of these mechanisms may be at play. Although further research is needed to clarify the temporality and bidirectionality of these associations, the present findings may be useful for generating hypotheses about possible public mental health responses that should be examined in future longitudinal and intervention research.

First, if future longitudinal research were to show that worries about societal issues contribute to the onset or persistence of mental disorders, initiatives at both the individual and community level aimed at reducing these worries or mitigating their impact could be relevant to examine [31]. Examples that could be explored in further research include neighborhood-based outreach – such as multidisciplinary teams providing integrated psychosocial and practical support – peer-led support groups for those affected by financial hardship, housing insecurity, or climate-related anxiety, and community-building initiatives that strengthen social cohesion and reduce loneliness, such as community rooms or communal meals [26].

Second, if future research were to indicate that mental disorders heighten worries about societal issues, it would be relevant to examine whether psychological interventions could help individuals cope with these worries or address unhelpful or catastrophizing thoughts. Cognitive behavioral therapy, for example, may be one approach worth exploring in this context. Importantly, community-level interventions, as described above, may also be relevant to examine for those with mental disorders.

Third, if shared underlying causes explain the associations between societal worries and mental disorders, future research could examine whether broader social or policy-level responses are relevant for reducing both elevated worry and mental disorders.

These considerations may be especially relevant when linked to specific population groups who experience elevated levels of worry about societal issues. Based on the data, different sociodemographic groups reported distinct patterns of worry – each of which may be relevant when considering possible responses at the individual, community, and policy levels. However, the present findings do not allow firm conclusions about which clinical, community, or policy responses would be most appropriate.

A major strength of this study is the assessment of common mental disorders using a structured clinical interview in a large, population-based sample, representative of the Dutch adult population. Nonetheless, several limitations warrant consideration. First, although the sample is broadly representative of the Dutch population across key demographic characteristics, certain groups were not included – namely, individuals with insufficient proficiency in Dutch, those without a permanent residential address, and institutionalized populations. Consequently, the findings cannot be generalized to these subgroups, while the prevalence of worries may be particularly different in these subgroups. Second, the societal worry variables were assessed using single-item, self-reported measures and were not part of a formally validated psychometric instrument, nor were they meant to represent an exhaustive list of societal worries. The cumulative count variable should therefore be interpreted as an indicator of the number of domains in which elevated worry was reported, rather than as a unidimensional scale. Third, while the CIDI 3.0 is a validated instrument for assessing DSM-IV mood, anxiety, and substance use disorders [21], we used a slightly modified version to approximate DSM-5 diagnoses. The psychometric properties of this adapted version have not yet been formally evaluated, which may affect the precision of diagnostic classification. Fourth, due to the current availability of only two waves of data, the present analysis is cross-sectional. As such, we cannot draw conclusions about the directionality of the observed associations between societal worries and mental disorders. The findings may reflect that societal worries contribute to poorer mental health, that existing mental disorders shape the perception or reporting of societal worries, that shared vulnerabilities underlie both, or a combination of these mechanisms. Longitudinal research is needed to disentangle these possibilities and to examine whether societal worries predict the onset or persistence of mental disorders, or whether existing mental health problems shape individuals’ worries about societal issues. Such research could also distinguish between worries related to direct personal exposure and more indirect worries about broader societal issues.

Conclusions

Worries about societal issues, particularly about international tensions, energy, and housing, are common and associated with a higher likelihood of having a mental disorder. Although the direction of these associations remains unclear, the findings suggest that societal worries may be relevant to consider in public mental health practice and in future longitudinal research.
