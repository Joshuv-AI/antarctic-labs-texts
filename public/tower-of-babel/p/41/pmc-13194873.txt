Failure mechanism of surrounding rock and synergistic control of strong support-strong pressure relief for lower-seam roadways in close-distance coal seam groups
Authors: Shui Yu, Yonglu Suo, Chiheng Cai, Jian Feng, Xiaoquan Huo, Jian Hu, Jianxi Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address the problem of asymmetric dynamic failure and superposed stress concentration in lower-seam roadways during stratified mining of deeply buried close-distance coal seam groups in the Jiaoping mining area of Shaanxi Province, where strong mining disturbance, eccentric loading from residual coal pillars, and weak rock-burst effects are coupled, a surrounding rock stability control system based on a “strong support–strong pressure relief” synergistic strategy is proposed. By integrating theoretical analysis, numerical simulation, and field monitoring, the asymmetric failure mechanism of roadway surrounding rock induced by the coupling of overlying residual coal pillars and mining-induced stress is revealed. On the basis of an internally staggered roadway layout and a high-preload cable bolt support system, directional hydraulic fracturing pressure-relief technology is further introduced. The dynamic response characteristics of roadway surrounding rock under different impact locations are systematically investigated, and a method for determining hydraulic fracturing parameters for arbitrarily oriented boreholes is established based on elastic theory. Comparative analyses of roof impact and roof-rib impact loading conditions show that, under static loading, roadway stress and deformation exhibit pronounced asymmetry but remain controllable due to the high-preload cable bolt support. Under dynamic loading, roof impact mainly induces tensile failure of the roof, whereas roof-rib impact tends to produce a composite failure mode characterized by severe rib extrusion and overall roof subsidence. Directional hydraulic fracturing effectively weakens stress concentration in the surrounding rock and overlying coal pillars, thereby reducing the intensity of dynamic roadway response. Borehole transient electromagnetic detection results indicate significant fracture development after fracturing, confirming an evident pressure-relief effect. Field monitoring further demonstrates that bolt and cable forces remain within a safe range, and the asymmetric expansion of the loosening zone is effectively controlled. The results verify the applicability and effectiveness of the proposed “strong support–strong pressure relief” synergistic control technology for lower-seam roadways in close-distance coal seam groups, providing a reliable theoretical basis and engineering reference for surrounding rock stability control and safe, efficient mining under similar geological and mining conditions.Keywords: Close-distance coal seam groups, Roadway surrounding rock, Dynamic failure, Numerical simulation, Support, Pressure reliefSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Close-distance coal seam groups are widely distributed in China and are typically characterized by small interlayer spacing and pronounced mining-induced interactions between adjacent seams. Under such geological conditions, the evaluation and control of surrounding rock stability in roadways serving fully mechanized top-coal caving faces remain one of the critical challenges for the safe extraction of deep coal seam groups1–5. The stability of roadways excavated in lower seams is influenced by multiple factors, including overlying goafs formed in upper seams. Dynamic failure of the surrounding rock under these conditions may lead to serious safety accidents during coal production6–9. Zhang et al.10 employed theoretical analysis and numerical simulation to construct a cooperative bearing structure model involving residual coal pillars and hinged rock blocks in upper seams, as well as a floor stress transfer calculation model, thereby proposing a method for determining the optimal staggered distance between roadways and coal pillars. Zhu et al.11, based on the structural characteristics of roof strata above lower seams, established a roof structural model and revealed the failure mechanism of roof collapse in stratified roadways. Meng12 analyzed the stress distribution in the floor beneath residual coal pillars, the stability of sectional coal pillars, and the deformation and failure characteristics of roadway surrounding rock, and determined the optimal layout position of lower-seam roadways under downward mining in close-distance coal seam groups. Li et al.13 conducted physical similarity simulation experiments combined with theoretical analysis to establish a mathematical model describing the spatial distribution of elliptic–parabolic fracture zones induced by repeated mining in close-distance coal seams. Ding et al.14 focused on the asymmetric deformation and failure as well as the difficulty of support encountered during roadway excavation in coal seam mining, and proposed reasonable support parameters and construction techniques to improve the surrounding rock conditions.

Numerical simulation has achieved remarkable progress in the study of roadway surrounding rock stress and failure mechanisms. Through simulation analyses, previous studies have investigated stress distribution and deformation–failure characteristics of stratified roadways with and without overlying goafs, failure modes of roadways with different lithologies under combined static and dynamic loading, as well as the distribution, peak values, and superposition evolution mechanisms of abutment pressure in thick coal seams beneath goafs15–17. Li Yang et al.18 systematically studied the typical morphological characteristics and formation mechanisms of collapsed roofs in goafs by integrating material mechanics, mine pressure theory, and numerical simulation, and proposed the concept of a “positive-inverted triangular” roof collapse structure with its dynamic evolution characteristics. Li et al.19 employed FLAC3D numerical simulations and demonstrated that increasing prestressing force and replacing rock bolts with short anchor cables can significantly improve roadway support performance. Furthermore, extensive studies have been carried out on roof collapse during upward mining of close-distance coal seams, fracture development in overlying strata above lower seams, and unloading-induced deformation of surrounding rock in deep roadways, providing important insights into instability mechanisms and safe mining practices for close-distance coal seam groups20,21.

In summary, substantial progress has been made by domestic and international researchers in the control of surrounding rock in close-distance coal seams, establishing a solid theoretical foundation. However, several limitations remain. First, many existing studies rely on single research methods, which are insufficient to fully represent complex engineering environments where strong mining-induced stress, residual coal pillars, goaf water accumulation, and dynamic disturbances coexist. Second, although roadway stability under static loading has been extensively investigated, the coupled mechanisms of static and dynamic loading-particularly the differential failure behaviors induced by impacts occurring at different locations-have not been systematically and comparatively analyzed. These shortcomings significantly restrict the further development of safe and efficient mining technologies for close-distance coal seam groups.

To address the above issues, this study takes the 224 fully mechanized top-coal caving face in the 4−2 coal seam beneath a close-distance coal seam group in the Jiaoping mining area of Shaanxi Province as the engineering background. By integrating theoretical analysis, numerical simulation, and field monitoring, the asymmetric failure mechanism of roadways induced by the combined effects of mining activities, residual coal pillars, and water accumulation is revealed. Moreover, the dynamic responses of roadway surrounding rock under impact loading at different locations are systematically analyzed. The results provide theoretical support and practical guidance for the safe and efficient extraction of coal seams under similar complex geological conditions.

Engineering background

Based on the 224 fully mechanized top-coal caving (FMTC) face in the lower seam of a deeply buried close-distance coal seam group in the Jiaoping mining area, Shaanxi Province, this study focuses on a mine located in the northwestern part of Tongchuan City with an initial designed annual production capacity of 0.6 Mt. The primary coal seams are the 3−2 and 4−2 seams, with an average interlayer spacing of 27.9 m. At present, extraction of the 3−2 seam has been completed, and the 224 FMTC face is being mined in the 4−2 seam by downward mining, at a burial depth of 508.1–647.7 m. The panel has a recoverable strike length of 1550 m and a dip length of 170 m.

According to the mine’s rock-burst risk assessment, the studied panel is classified under a weak rock-burst hazard level (none/weak/medium/strong). In this paper, this condition is referred to as a “weak rock-burst effect.” Following the comprehensive-index criterion, the weak-hazard level corresponds to 0.25 < Wt ≤ 0.5. The 224 panel was evaluated as weak hazard (Wt = 0.32) in the assessment report. The 224 panel is a thick coal seam with a hard roof. Although the seam occurrence is relatively stable, endogenous fractures are well developed, making the coal mass prone to rib spalling and roof caving. Directly above the panel, the 2302 goaf in the 3−2 seam is present. Adjacent panels include the 222 panel (already mined out) and the 226 panel, with residual coal pillars of 70 m and 20 m, respectively. During extraction of the lower seam, these residual pillars in the overlying goaf induce pronounced stress redistribution and the formation of stress-concentration zones. When the coal–rock mass within such zones can no longer maintain equilibrium under the accumulated stress and energy, instantaneous energy release may occur, potentially triggering dynamic hazards. As a result, the roadways serving the lower-seam FMTC panel are highly susceptible to instability and damage, leading to severe maintenance difficulties and posing a serious threat to safe mine production.

The pronounced differences in hydromechanical properties between the coal seam and the surrounding rock indicate that, when subjected to infiltration from accumulated water in the overlying goaf, the coal seam is more susceptible to softening and strength degradation. Support and pressure-relief technologies for lower-seam roadways based on surrounding rock stability

Main factors affecting the stability of surrounding rock in lower-seam roadways

Numerous factors influence the stability of surrounding rock and the support design of roadways in lower-seam working faces within closely spaced coal seam groups. Increasing mining depth leads to higher in-situ stress and elastic energy accumulation in the coal seam. The average burial depth of the No. 224 longwall top-coal caving face reaches 577.9 m, approaching the critical depth for rock burst, which significantly increases the risk of dynamic instability.

In terms of lithology and bedding inclination, plastic surrounding rock exhibits low strength and is prone to water-induced softening, while inclined strata tend to cause asymmetric deformation and failure of roadways. Under a stress regime dominated by horizontal stress, roadway surrounding rock is more susceptible to dynamic instability. Field measurements indicate that the maximum principal stress in the original stress field of the 224 working face is horizontal (Fig. 1).

Schematic diagram of the spatial relationship of the longwall top-coal caving face.

With downward mining, the extraction of the upper coal seam results in roof damage and a reduction in surrounding rock strength in the 224 working face. Regarding roadway geometry and layout, rectangular roadways generally exhibit poorer stability, and different layout schemes have a significant impact on mining safety and efficiency. In terms of mining-induced stress, residual coal pillars left in the upper seam can cause stress concentration and redistribution, thereby increasing the risk of rock bursts.

From a hydrogeological perspective, inadequate treatment of water accumulation in upper-seam goafs can induce rock bursts. Water accumulation in the 2302 goaf adversely affects both roadway excavation and coal extraction in the 224 working face.

Upper-seam extraction causes stress transfer and floor damage that may affect the lower-seam roadways. The elastic analysis yields a maximum floor failure depth of 14.71 m beneath the upper-seam goaf (Eq. 11) and 12.30 m after extraction considering abutment pressure (Eq. 18), indicating a conservative vertical influence range of about 10–15 m. Meanwhile, both simulation and field observations show that the residual coal pillar dominates the asymmetric stress concentration on the coal-pillar side. Therefore, the roadway layout should avoid the pillar-controlled high-stress zone and be placed in a relatively pressure-relieved region. Based on the above criterion, three typical layout schemes can be compared: (i) an overlapped layout tends to intersect the abutment-pressure-affected zone near the upper-seam goaf boundary; (ii) an externally staggered layout shifts roadways toward the coal-pillar side and therefore has the highest risk of entering the pillar-controlled HSZ; (iii) an internally staggered layout shifts roadways toward the goaf side and is most likely to place them in a relatively pressure-relieved region, thereby reducing asymmetric loading and improving supportability. This is consistent with the asymmetric stress mechanism identified later in the numerical/field results. This layout can effectively avoid stress concentration zones formed by upper-seam retreat mining, making the gate road of the lower seam easier to support and resulting in relatively better surrounding rock stability24,25.

Support scheme and parameters for the lower-seam longwall top-coal caving roadways

The stability of roadways is closely related to the adopted support scheme. A rational support design can significantly enhance the stability of the surrounding rock and enable the roadway to withstand repeated variations in surrounding rock stress26–28. In the No. 224 transportation roadway, both the roof and ribs are supported by Φ22 mm × 2400 mm left-handed high-strength threaded steel bolts without longitudinal ribs. The bolt spacing and row spacing for the roof are both 900 mm, while the rib bolts are installed with a spacing of 800 mm and a row spacing of 900 mm. One bolt is installed at each position, with a designed anchoring force of 100 kN, and the installation torque of the bolts exceeds 400 N·m.

The ribs are reinforced with diamond-shaped metal mesh woven from No. 8 iron wire, with a specification of 1000 mm × 3900 mm. The overlap between adjacent mesh panels is 50 mm. Q235 steel bearing plates are used for both roof and rib bolts, with dimensions of 150 mm × 150 mm × 10 mm (length × width × thickness). W-shaped steel strips are adopted as combined support components.

Roof cable bolts are made of Φ21.8 mm × 6300 mm steel strands, arranged between the roof bolts with a spacing of 2400 mm and a row spacing of 1800 mm. The pretension force of the cable bolts is 250 kN, and the bearing plates have dimensions of 300 mm × 300 mm × 16 mm.

Numerical analysis of dynamic failure of surrounding rock in lower-seam roadways

Model establishment

FLAC3D numerical simulation software was employed to analyze the surrounding rock failure characteristics of the No. 224 longwall top-coal caving roadways and to verify the rationality of the support parameter design. The numerical model dimensions are 858 m × 32 m × 94.8 m (length × width × height). The length of the longwall working face is 170 m, and the average thickness of the coal seam is 8.2 m. The cross-sectional dimensions of both the No. 224 return airway and transportation roadway are 5.0 m × 3.8 m.

A section coal pillar with a width of 70 m is reserved between the No. 224 return roadway and the No. 222 goaf, while a 20 m-wide section coal pillar is arranged between the No. 224 transportation roadway and the No. 226 return airway. The model is composed of solid elements and contains 181,890 elements and 196,992 nodes. The numerical calculation model is shown in Fig. 8.

Simulation of Static Loading Conditions and Determination of Parameters

Simulation of Static Loading Conditions and Determination of Parameters

Zero-displacement boundary conditions are applied to the left and right boundaries of the model, that is, horizontal constraints are imposed on both sides of the model. This setting is intended to simulate the horizontal confinement of an infinite medium, while allowing free movement in the vertical direction, thereby more realistically reflecting the mechanical behavior of the overlying strata under self-weight loading and avoiding artificial boundary effects caused by fully fixed constraints. The upper boundary is defined as a free boundary and is loaded by the overburden pressure, whereas the bottom boundary is set as a fully constrained boundary.

Considering the geological conditions of the closely spaced coal seam group in this mine, the simulation procedure is conducted as follows. First, the upper-seam working faces 2301, 2302, and 2303 are excavated sequentially, followed by the excavation of the adjacent 222 working face. The goafs are treated using the caving method. Subsequently, the roadways of the 224 longwall top-coal caving face and the return airway of the 226 working face are excavated. The coal seam thickness of the 224 longwall top-coal caving face is 8.2 m, and the heights of both the transportation roadway and the return airway are 3.8 m. Stepwise excavation is adopted with a step length of 0.9 m.

Prior to excavation calculations, initial stress equilibrium is performed to ensure that the model is in the original in-situ stress state. The roadways are excavated with simultaneous support installation. Cable structural elements are used to simulate bolt and cable support systems. Each rock bolt is modeled using 15 structural elements and 16 nodes, while each cable bolt is modeled using 18 structural elements and 19 nodes. Corresponding pretension forces are applied to the bolts and cables. In addition, bolt and cable bearing plates as well as anchorage devices are simulated using nodal constraints.

This asymmetry mainly originates from the downward transfer of concentrated stress induced by the residual coal pillar in the 2302 upper-seam goaf, which acts on the side of the 224 transportation roadway close to the coal pillar, as well as from the superposition of secondary mining-induced stresses caused by the excavation of the return airway of the 226 working face. At a position 4.4 m above the roadway roof, a relatively high concentration of horizontal stress is observed in the surrounding rock. However, owing to the high-strength roadway support system, the surrounding rock is predominantly subjected to compressive stress, which effectively ensures the stability of the surrounding rock in the lower-seam longwall top-coal caving roadways.

As shown in Fig. 10, with the advance of the longwall top-coal caving face, the deformation of the surrounding rock of the roadway is significantly intensified compared with that during the excavation stage. The maximum roof deformation reaches 36.3 mm, while the maximum deformation of the ribs is 35.3 mm. Both the roof–floor convergence and the convergence of the two ribs are far lower than the critical values specified in the stability classification criteria for coal mine roadway surrounding rock. This indicates that, under static loading conditions, the existing support system effectively controls the surrounding rock deformation within a range dominated by elastic deformation.

Dynamic failure characteristics of surrounding rock under impact loading

Figures 11 and 12 present the stress contour distributions of the roadway subjected to roof impact loading and roof–rib impact loading, respectively. As shown in the figures, under roof impact loading, the roof and ribs of the roadway exhibit pronounced dynamic response characteristics. The dynamic load is first transmitted to the roadway roof, which acts as the “wave-facing surface” and initially bears the compressive stress wave, resulting in intense compressive deformation. When the wave reaches the free surface and reflects, a tensile wave is generated, which is highly likely to induce tensile failure in the central part of the roof. Under this condition, the maximum vertical stress in the roof is significantly lower than that in the ribs.

Stress contours of the roadway under roof impact loading (unit: Pa).

Stress contours of the transportation roadway under roof–rib impact loading (unit: Pa).

The maximum vertical stress in the roof is 8.32 MPa, occurring at the shoulder of the roadway. In contrast, the maximum vertical stress in the ribs reaches 19.61 MPa and is located at the bottom corner of the rib adjacent to the coal pillar. The maximum horizontal stress is 18.30 MPa and appears at the bottom corner of the floor near the coal pillar.

Compared with roof impact loading, the ribs directly subjected to impact loading exhibit a much more intense dynamic response. Impact energy is rapidly transmitted from the ribs to the roof and floor, inducing shear slip in the roof and leading to a composite failure mode characterized by “large rib deformation and extrusion combined with overall roof subsidence.” Under roof–rib impact loading, both the maximum vertical stress and the maximum horizontal stress increase significantly. The maximum vertical stress reaches 17.32 MPa and occurs at the bottom corner of the rib adjacent to the coal pillar, while the maximum horizontal stress reaches 21.27 MPa and appears at the end of the roof.

The displacement contour maps of the roadway under different impact loading locations are shown in Figs. 13 and 14. It can be observed that under roof impact loading, the maximum roof subsidence reaches 129.6 mm. The maximum displacement of the main rib is 3.0 mm, while that of the auxiliary rib is 13.6 mm. The “main rib” refers to the roadway rib adjacent to the goaf of the 224 working face, whereas the “auxiliary rib” refers to the rib adjacent to the return airway of the 226 working face. This side is significantly affected by stress concentration induced by the overlying coal pillar, and its deformation and failure are generally more severe.

Displacement contours of the roadway under roof impact loading (unit: m).

Displacement contours of the roadway under roof–rib impact loading (unit: m).

Under roof–rib impact loading, the maximum roof subsidence is significantly smaller than that under roof impact loading, with a value of 97.9 mm. In contrast, the rib displacements are much larger than those observed under roof impact loading, with the maximum rib displacement reaching 103.9 mm. The relatively small rib displacement under roof impact loading can be attributed to energy dissipation during stress wave propagation and the fact that the ribs are not directly subjected to impact. However, under roof-rib impact loading, the ribs act as the direct load-bearing medium, resulting in a substantial increase in displacement. This clearly demonstrates that the impact location directly determines the point of energy input and the region of maximum damage.

Field measurements of surrounding rock deformation in lower-seam longwall top-coal caving roadways

Monitoring of surrounding rock stress

Stress meters were installed in the rib on the coal pillar side of the transportation roadway of the No. 224 longwall top-coal caving face. The monitoring points were arranged starting at a distance of 100 m from the cut-through, with a spacing of 30 m between adjacent monitoring sections. At each monitoring section, stress meters were installed using a combination of deep and shallow boreholes. The installation depths of the deep and shallow boreholes were 13 m and 7 m, respectively.

The stress variation curves measured at different monitoring sections are shown in Fig. 15. During the mining process, Stress Meter No. 1 was located 100 m from the cut-through. When the working face advanced to 108 m, no obvious change in stress was observed. As the face advanced from 108 to 170 m, the stress increased continuously, with the amplitude reaching 6.5 MPa at the deep measuring point and 6.1 MPa at the shallow measuring point.

Stress variation curves at the monitoring points.

Stress Meter No. 2 was located 130 m from the cut-through. When the working face advanced to 36 m, the stress at the deep measuring point decreased slightly from 5.4 to 5.3 MPa and then remained essentially unchanged. As the face advanced to 72 m, the stress at the shallow measuring point increased, with an amplitude of 8.9 MPa. At 72 m, the stress dropped to 4.6 MPa. During further advance to 170 m, the stress increased again from 4.6 to 5.9 MPa.

Stress Meter No. 3 was located 160 m from the cut-through. During the advance to 170 m, no significant changes were observed in either the deep or shallow measuring points, with stress values fluctuating within the range of 5.3–5.4 MPa.

The stresses at Monitoring Points No. 1 and No. 2 exhibited significant increases after the working face advanced to a certain distance, which is fully consistent with the conclusion revealed by the numerical simulation that face advance induces pronounced abutment pressure ahead of the working face. The monitoring data verify the accuracy of the numerical model in simulating the propagation and evolution of mining-induced stress and also provide direct evidence for determining a reasonable advance support distance.

Although the loosened zone expands during mining, no roof collapse occurs, indicating that the combined support system consisting of “rock bolts-steel strips-cable bolts-mesh” successfully anchors the fractured rock mass into an effective load-bearing structure, enabling it to withstand intense mining-induced disturbances.

Discussion

The combined results from numerical simulations and field monitoring demonstrate that the stability of lower-seam roadways in closely spaced coal seam groups is governed by a persistent asymmetric loading environment. This asymmetry originates from the coupling between eccentric loading induced by overlying residual coal pillars and a stress regime dominated by high horizontal tectonic stress, so that the coal-pillar side consistently exhibits higher stress levels, larger deformation, and greater bolt forces. On this basis, the roadway instability can be interpreted as a coupled static–dynamic mechanism: the pillar-controlled biased static stress field is further intensified by mining-stress superposition, and impact disturbances amplify the asymmetric response, with failure modes depending on the impact location (roof impact promoting roof tensile cracking due to tensile-wave reflection, whereas impact near the roof–rib intersection leading to rib extrusion accompanied by overall roof subsidence). These findings confirm that biased loading is the primary driver of asymmetric damage and explain why instability is difficult to eliminate through support measures alone; accordingly, the proposed “strong support–strong pressure relief” strategy controls shallow deformation via high-preload support while reducing deep stress concentration through directional hydraulic fracturing, achieving synergistic stability control.

Clear distinctions are observed between static and dynamic responses of the roadway. Under static loading, deformation remains limited and predominantly elastic, indicating that the high-preload support system provides effective confinement to the shallow surrounding rock. Under dynamic loading, however, failure characteristics are strongly dependent on the impact location. Roof impact loading mainly induces tensile damage in the roof due to stress-wave reflection at the free surface, whereas roof–rib impact loading leads to a composite failure mode characterized by severe rib extrusion and overall roof subsidence. These differences highlight that impact location directly controls energy input paths and damage localization, thereby amplifying the asymmetric response under strong mining disturbances. Field monitoring further confirms the effectiveness of the high-preload “bolt–steel strip–cable bolt–mesh” support system in maintaining near-field stability. Roof separation develops primarily beyond the bolt anchorage zone and stabilizes without continuous growth, indicating that high-preload cable bolts provide effective deep reinforcement and promote coordinated load-bearing behavior. The consistently higher bolt forces on the coal-pillar side quantitatively validate the asymmetric stress distribution revealed by numerical simulations, while the maximum measured cable force remains far below the breaking load, demonstrating a sufficient safety margin under coupled static and dynamic disturbances. Nevertheless, monitoring results also indicate that deep stress concentration on the coal-pillar side persists, suggesting that strong support alone cannot fundamentally eliminate the source of asymmetric loading.

In this context, hydraulic fracturing serves as an effective pressure-relief measure that complements strong support by actively modifying the stress environment of the surrounding rock. Theoretical analysis of fracture initiation pressure for arbitrarily oriented boreholes shows that appropriate selection of borehole inclination and azimuth can significantly reduce fracture initiation pressure and promote controlled fracture propagation. Field implementation and borehole transient electromagnetic detection confirm that hydraulic fracturing induces extensive fracture development and fluid-filled fracture networks, forming pronounced low-resistivity anomalous zones. These observations indicate that hydraulic fracturing successfully redistributes concentrated stresses away from the coal-pillar–roadway system, thereby reducing the driving force of asymmetric deformation and dynamic failure.

Roadway stability under closely spaced coal seam conditions can be effectively achieved through a synergistic “strong support–strong pressure relief” strategy. High-preload anchorage systems provide immediate confinement and load-bearing capacity for shallow surrounding rock, while hydraulic fracturing alleviates deep stress concentration and weakens the source of dynamic disturbance. This integrated control approach establishes a complete stability-control chain from stress redistribution to structural reinforcement, offering a reliable technical reference for similar mining scenarios. This study adopts the Mohr–Coulomb constitutive model and a simplified sinusoidal dynamic load, which adequately capture the dominant stress redistribution trends and comparative responses between different impact locations but may not fully represent rate-dependent damage evolution or strain-softening behavior. Future work should incorporate constitutive models that account for damage accumulation and dynamic strength effects, calibrate impact sources using microseismic waveform data, and extend validation to multiple panels with varying pillar geometries, seam spacing, and water-weakening conditions to further generalize the proposed design framework.

Conclusions

The mechanism of asymmetric dynamic instability is clarified and verified. The persistent asymmetry in stress redistribution, deformation, and support loading is attributed to the coupled effect of eccentric loading induced by residual coal pillars and a strong horizontal tectonic-stress regime, explaining why conventional support-only strategies may fail to fundamentally eliminate instability.
