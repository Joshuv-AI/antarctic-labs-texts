Kinematics of different vault techniques in elite male gymnasts
Authors: Haowen Wang, Jiayu Liu, Xiaomei Wei, Zhenke Tan, Yiman Qin, Lei Hu, Zhengyang Zhao, Jing Guo, Yichuan Dai, Dongdong Ren, Laicong Song, Yanrong Jin, Jinting Duan, Yingjun Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vaulting requires the transformation of horizontal momentum into vertical displacement and angular momentum through the run-up, springboard contact, table support, second flight, and landing phases. This study aimed to compare key kinematic parameters among different vault technique types in elite male gymnasts and to explore phase-specific kinematic factors associated with landing angle. Landing angle was defined as a kinematic descriptor of body posture at initial mat contact, not as a direct measure of landing quality, landing stability, or E-score. Kinematic data from 16 vault trials performed by eight elite male gymnasts during the finals of the 2022 Chinese Gymnastics Championships were collected with a markerless motion capture system at 200 Hz. Seven core kinematic parameters were extracted: approach velocity, table contact angle, table contact time, table release angle, maximum height of the second flight, trajectory angle, and landing angle. Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and a theory-driven hierarchical linear regression model. The most robust kinematic difference between direct-entry and Yurchenko vaults was approach velocity, which was significantly greater in direct-entry vaults. Other variables, including table release angle, maximum height of the second flight, and landing angle, showed only trend-level effects and should be interpreted cautiously. When Tsukahara and handspring vaults were compared, table contact angle and maximum height of the second flight showed exploratory between-group differences, but these findings did not remain stable across all sensitivity analyses. Landing angle was not significantly correlated with E-score (Spearman’s ρ = -0.303, p = 0.255). In the hierarchical regression model, maximum height of the second flight was positively associated with landing angle after controlling for D-score, approach velocity, and table contact time (β = 0.717, p = 0.029). Approach velocity and table contact time were not independently associated with landing angle in the main linear model. An exploratory quadratic analysis suggested a possible nonlinear relationship between table contact time and landing angle, but this result should be interpreted as preliminary. However, the overall model fit was not statistically significant; therefore, the regression findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Different vault technique types showed exploratory differences in kinematic profiles, and approach velocity was the most stable difference between direct-entry and Yurchenko vaults. Landing angle was not significantly associated with the official E-score and should therefore be interpreted only as a kinematic descriptor of landing posture, not as a proxy for landing quality or execution performance. Maximum height of the second flight was positively associated with landing angle, whereas the roles of approach velocity and table contact time appear to be more complex. Given the small and unbalanced sample and the competition-based observational design, all findings should be interpreted as exploratory associations rather than causal evidence.

Introduction

The evolution of modern men’s vaulting techniques is increasingly characterized by multi-rotational flips combined with complex twists. Achieving greater Difficulty (D) scores primarily relies on modifying body postures (tucked, piked, or layout) and increasing the number of rotations about the longitudinal and transverse axes during the second flight phase1. Consequently, these intricate maneuvers place substantial demands on gymnasts’ aerial postural control and landing preparation2.

A gymnastics vault comprises seven sequential phases: (1) the approach run; (2) the hurdle to the springboard; (3) springboard contact; (4) the first flight; (5) table support; (6) the second flight; and (7) landing3. It is widely acknowledged that the approach run is critical for generating the requisite kinetic energy for the vault4–6. This energy is then transformed during the springboard take-off and table support phases into rotational (angular momentum), vertical (flight height and time), and translational components required for the execution of complex saltos and twists in the second flight phase7. Given that the springboard and table contact phases serve as key phases in this energy conversion8, optimizing key kinematic parameters—specifically approach velocity, table contact angle, and table contact time—is of paramount importance.

In elite vaulting competitions, landing posture is often a key factor in determining performance outcomes. The chosen vault technique may affect energy transfer efficiency and the maximum height of the second flight, which may in turn be related to landing posture and overall execution quality7,9,10. To date, kinematic research has predominantly focused on isolated phases, such as measuring approach velocity11, or comparing approach runs and springboard ground reaction forces across different movements4,12. Consequently, there is limited empirical evidence providing comprehensive kinematic comparisons of different vault techniques across the full movement sequence. Furthermore, although differences in landing stability among vault styles have been reported, the underlying kinematic mechanisms remain unclear9. It also remains unclear how specific kinematic parameters during the springboard take-off, table support, and second flight phases are associated with landing posture at initial mat contact.

Based on the existing literature, this study tested two hypotheses: (1) kinematic differences would exist among vault techniques across the approach run, springboard take-off, table support, and second flight phases; and (2) kinematic parameters from these phases would be associated with landing posture at initial mat contact. To test these hypotheses, kinematic data from the men’s vault finals at the 2022 Chinese Gymnastics Championships were analyzed. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate differences among vault techniques, followed by a theory-driven hierarchical linear regression model to examine the combined effects of preceding phases on landing outcomes. This study aimed to provide exploratory evidence on phase-specific kinematic factors associated with landing posture in elite male gymnasts.

Methods

Participants

This study included 16 vault trials performed by eight elite male gymnasts during the vault finals of the 2022 Chinese Gymnastics Championships. All participants were first-tier members of their respective provincial teams. To ensure data quality, participants were required to be free of lower-extremity injuries during the preceding three months and to have no history of neurological disorders affecting motor control. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. We conducted all procedures in accordance with the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national regulations in China. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhan Sports University (Approval No.: 202416044).

The competition was held using Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique (FIG)-certified apparatus at the Dong’an Lake Sports Park Multipurpose Gymnasium in Chengdu, Sichuan Province, and was officially sanctioned by the Gymnastics Management Center of the General Administration of Sport of China and the Chinese Gymnastics Association.

To account for potential confounding by vault difficulty, FIG code, D-score, and E-score for each trial were extracted from the official competition records. By vault family, the 16 trials were classified as Tsukahara vaults (n = 9), handspring vaults (n = 3), and Yurchenko vaults (n = 4). The D-scores ranged from 4.8 to 6.0, and the E-scores ranged from 7.666 to 9.166. The FIG code, D-score, E-score, and final score for each trial are reported in Table 1.

Study design

This study used a markerless motion capture system (SPROT) to collect kinematic data during the men’s vault finals at the 2022 Chinese Gymnastics Championships. Markerless motion capture does not require reflective markers on athletes’ bodies, making it particularly suitable for official competition settings in which athletes’ technical performance cannot be disrupted. Previous studies have shown that multi-camera markerless motion capture systems are increasingly used in human movement biomechanics and can provide acceptable three-dimensional kinematic estimates under appropriate conditions12,13. The data acquisition system consisted of eight high-definition cameras and a synchronized server. The cameras were positioned around the run-up, springboard contact, table support, second flight, and landing areas of the vault runway. Each vault was recorded at 200 Hz with an image resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels. Using multi-view image reconstruction, the system generated a three-dimensional skeletal model of the athlete and extracted the center-of-mass coordinates, key event frames, and relevant angular variables without interfering with the athletes’ competitive performances (Fig. 1). The extracted core kinematic parameters were approach velocity, table contact angle, table contact time, table release angle, maximum height of the second flight, trajectory angle, and landing angle.

Because the data were collected during an official competition, it was not feasible to attach reflective markers to the athletes or to use a laboratory-based optical motion capture system, force platform, or other gold-standard measurement devices simultaneously. Therefore, the SPROT system could not be directly validated against a gold-standard system under the present competition conditions. To improve methodological transparency, we contextualized the measurement uncertainty associated with the extracted kinematic parameters using methodological studies of the SPROT system and published validation studies of comparable multi-camera markerless motion capture systems.

For angular variables, including table contact angle, table release angle, trajectory angle, and landing angle, previous SPROT-related vision-based markerless kinematic studies have reported measurement errors for joint-angle and joint-position estimates. Validation studies comparing comparable multi-camera markerless motion capture systems with marker-based motion capture systems have also shown that differences in angular variables are typically within a few degrees, although the magnitude of these differences varies across joints, movement planes, and task types14–16. In addition, the magnitude of measurement error in markerless motion capture systems may be influenced by movement speed, occlusion, camera placement, calibration procedures, the underlying body model, and key-event identification14,16,17. Therefore, when interpreting angular variables such as table contact angle, table release angle, trajectory angle, and landing angle, this study considered measurement uncertainty of several degrees as an important contextual factor.

For table contact time, the sampling frequency of the system was 200 Hz, corresponding to 0.005 s per frame. Given that the identification of hand contact and hand release from the table may involve an uncertainty of approximately 1–2 frames, the uncertainty in table contact time was interpreted as approximately ± 0.005–0.010 s. For variables derived from the center-of-mass trajectory, such as approach velocity and maximum height of the second flight, the results were interpreted cautiously with reference to published validation studies of markerless motion capture systems in three-dimensional kinematics, spatiotemporal parameters, and functional movement analysis14–16,18.

It should be emphasized that the error ranges reported in the above studies were used only to provide context for the measurement uncertainty associated with markerless motion capture systems and were not treated as dataset-specific measurement errors in the present competition dataset. Accordingly, dataset-specific RMSE, MAE, ICC, or CV estimates could not be obtained from the present data. Therefore, results with differences close to the expected range of measurement uncertainty were interpreted cautiously throughout the manuscript.

Data collection

Seven kinematic parameters were extracted from the vault trials: approach velocity, table contact angle, table contact time, table release angle, maximum height of the second flight, trajectory angle, and landing angle. Differences in these variables across vault techniques were analyzed, and a theory-driven hierarchical linear regression model was used to examine the associations of selected kinematic variables with landing angle. Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 and R. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Before formal analyses, normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots, homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test, and potential outliers were examined using boxplots and standardized residuals.

Group differences in the seven kinematic parameters were examined using ANOVA. Given the small sample size and unequal group sizes, conventional ANOVA and Welch’s robust test were both reported. When the homogeneity-of-variance assumption was not satisfied, Welch’s test was used as the primary reference. Effect sizes were reported using η², ω², and Hedges’ g. Holm correction and Mann–Whitney U tests were used as sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of the between-group findings.

The association between landing angle and official E-score was examined using Spearman’s rank correlation. This analysis was conducted only to assess whether landing angle was statistically related to E-score, not to treat landing angle as a direct measure of landing quality, landing stability, or execution performance.

A theory-driven hierarchical linear regression model was used to examine kinematic factors associated with landing angle. Landing angle was entered as the dependent variable. D-score was entered in the first block to account for vault difficulty, followed by approach velocity and table contact time in the second block, and maximum height of the second flight in the third block. For each model, R², adjusted R², ΔR², ΔF, standardized regression coefficients, 95% confidence intervals, and VIF were reported. Model stability and influential observations were assessed using Cook’s distance, leverage values, standardized residuals, and VIF.

An exploratory quadratic regression analysis was also conducted to examine a possible nonlinear relationship between table contact time and landing angle. Table contact time was mean-centered before the squared term was generated, and the model controlled for D-score, approach velocity, and maximum height of the second flight. Robust standard errors clustered at the athlete level were used. Given the small and unbalanced sample, all regression results and subgroup comparisons were interpreted as exploratory associations rather than causal evidence.

Results

Descriptive statistics of kinematic parameters

Table 3 presents descriptive statistics for the core kinematic parameters across Tsukahara, Handspring, and Yurchenko vaults. During the approach phase, Handspring vaults showed the fastest approach velocity (7.80 ± 0.20 m/s), followed by Tsukahara vaults (7.45 ± 0.37 m/s), while Yurchenko vaults had the slowest velocity (5.61 ± 0.17 m/s). In the table support phase, Yurchenko vaults exhibited the largest table contact angle (41.57 ± 11.14°), followed by Tsukahara vaults (39.80 ± 8.89°), with Handspring vaults showing the smallest angle (27.79 ± 3.72°). Table contact time was similar across all vault types, with mean values ranging from 0.15 to 0.16 s (overall range: 0.12–0.22 s). At table release, Tsukahara and Handspring vaults showed larger release angles (82.17 ± 3.58° and 81.76 ± 4.38°, respectively) than Yurchenko vaults (76.82 ± 6.36°). During the second flight phase, Handspring vaults achieved the greatest maximum height of the second flight (3.09 ± 0.09 m) and the largest trajectory angle (49.81 ± 4.53°). Tsukahara vaults followed with a maximum height of 2.95 ± 0.09 m and a trajectory angle of 44.84 ± 5.85°, while Yurchenko vaults recorded the lowest values for both flight parameters (2.86 ± 0.09 m and 40.46 ± 8.02°). Landing angle was greatest for Tsukahara vaults (59.41 ± 17.46°), followed by Handspring vaults (56.96 ± 11.00°), and lowest for Yurchenko vaults (47.04 ± 6.27°).

Kinematic comparison of distinct vaulting techniques

Before conducting the one-way ANOVA, the normality of each kinematic variable within each group was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots. No clear evidence of marked departures from normality was observed. However, because of the small sample size, particularly in the Yurchenko and handspring-type groups, the normality tests were used only as supporting diagnostic checks. Therefore, the results were interpreted using a combination of Welch’s robust test, Mann–Whitney U sensitivity analyses, Holm-corrected p values, and effect size estimates.

Kinematic differences among vault entry techniques

Based on springboard entry strategy, vault techniques were categorized into direct-entry vaults (Handspring and Tsukahara) and Yurchenko vaults7. ANOVA was used to assess kinematic differences between these groups (Fig. 2; Table 4).

Approach velocity was significantly greater in direct-entry vaults than in Yurchenko vaults. Although Levene’s test suggested possible heterogeneity of variance, this difference remained significant in both conventional ANOVA and Welch’s robust test, with large effect sizes. Table release angle, maximum height of the second flight, and landing angle showed directionally greater values in direct-entry vaults, but these differences were not consistently significant across tests and should therefore be interpreted as trend-level findings. No stable between-group differences were observed for table contact angle, table contact time, or trajectory angle. Overall, approach velocity represented the most robust kinematic difference between direct-entry and Yurchenko vaults.

Kinematic differences among table support techniques

Kinematic differences between Tsukahara-type and handspring vaults are presented in Table 5. Because the handspring group included only three trials, this comparison was treated as an exploratory subgroup analysis. Accordingly, the results were interpreted mainly using Welch’s robust test, effect sizes, and sensitivity analyses.

Table contact angle was greater in Tsukahara-type vaults than in handspring vaults. Although the conventional ANOVA showed only a trend, Welch’s robust test reached statistical significance, with a large effect size. Given the very small handspring sample, this finding was interpreted as exploratory. Maximum height of the second flight was greater in handspring vaults than in Tsukahara-type vaults, but this result was less stable because Welch’s test showed only a marginal trend. No stable between-group differences were observed for approach velocity, table contact time, table release angle, trajectory angle, or landing angle. (Figs. 3 and 4).

To assess the robustness of the group comparisons under the small and unequal sample sizes, Mann–Whitney U tests were used as non-parametric sensitivity analyses. For the comparison between entry types, the difference in approach velocity remained significant (U = 0.000, p = 0.001), consistent with Welch’s robust test. Maximum height of the second flight showed only a marginal trend (U = 8.500, p = 0.058), whereas table release angle, landing angle, and the other variables did not show stable differences.

For the comparison between table support types, Mann–Whitney U tests showed marginal trends for table contact angle and maximum height of the second flight (both p = 0.064). These results were directionally consistent with the parametric analyses but did not reach statistical significance, indicating that these differences should be interpreted as exploratory.

Holm correction was also applied to the Welch p values to control for multiple testing within each group comparison. After Holm correction, only the difference in approach velocity between direct-entry and Yurchenko vaults remained significant. The Welch-significant difference in table contact angle between Tsukahara-type and handspring vaults did not remain significant after correction and was therefore interpreted as exploratory.

Correlation analysis between landing angle and E-score

To examine whether landing angle was statistically related to official E-score, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used because of the small sample size and the non-normal distribution of the variables. No significant correlation was found between landing angle and E-score (Spearman’s ρ = −0.303, p = 0.255, n = 16). This result indicates that, in the present sample, a larger landing angle did not necessarily correspond to a greater E-score. Therefore, landing angle was interpreted only as a kinematic descriptor of body posture at initial mat contact, rather than as a direct proxy for landing quality, landing stability, or execution performance.

Hierarchical regression analysis of kinematic factors associated with landing angle

Table 6 presents the final model coefficients of the hierarchical linear regression. To avoid the model instability and overfitting associated with stepwise regression in small samples, a theory-driven hierarchical linear regression model was used to examine the associations of D-score, approach velocity, table contact time, and maximum height of the second flight with landing angle.

In Model 1, D-score explained little variance in landing angle (R² = 0.001, adjusted R² = −0.070, p = 0.893). Adding approach velocity in Model 2 slightly increased the explained variance (R² = 0.060), but the change was not significant (ΔR² = 0.059, p = 0.384). Adding table contact time in Model 3 further increased R² to 0.114, but this change was also not significant (ΔR² = 0.054, p = 0.411). In Model 4, adding maximum height of the second flight increased R² to 0.437, with an adjusted R² of 0.232, and the additional explained variance reached statistical significance (ΔR² = 0.323, p = 0.029).

In the final model, maximum height of the second flight was positively associated with landing angle (B = 90.568, β = 0.717, p = 0.029), whereas D-score, approach velocity, and table contact time were not significant predictors. This suggests that, after controlling for vault difficulty, approach velocity, and table contact time, greater second-flight height was associated with a greater landing angle. However, the overall fit of the final model was not statistically significant (F = 2.135, p = 0.144), and the sample size was small. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as exploratory associations rather than evidence of causal effects.

To assess the stability of the regression findings, two sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, the model was re-estimated after excluding the observation with the largest Cook’s distance. The positive association between maximum height of the second flight and landing angle remained statistically significant and in the same direction (B = 70.077, β = 0.610, p = 0.049). In this sensitivity model, table contact time was also positively associated with landing angle (B = 313.202, β = 0.597, p = 0.036), whereas D-score and approach velocity remained non-significant. These results suggest that the association between second-flight height and landing angle was not driven solely by the most influential observation. However, the coefficient for table contact time appeared sensitive to the exclusion of this observation and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Second, because each athlete could contribute two vault trials, the model was re-estimated using robust standard errors clustered at the athlete level. Maximum height of the second flight remained positively associated with landing angle (B = 90.568, robust SE = 20.897, p = 0.003, 95% CI: 41.154–139.981), whereas D-score, approach velocity, and table contact time were not statistically significant. Overall, both sensitivity analyses supported a directionally consistent positive association between maximum height of the second flight and landing angle. However, given the small sample size, the limited number of athlete-level clusters, and the observational competition-based design, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory associations rather than causal evidence.

Exploratory nonlinear extension analysis

Given the possibility of a nonlinear relationship between table contact time and landing angle, an exploratory quadratic regression analysis was conducted. The squared term of mean-centered table contact time was negatively associated with landing angle (B = −4740.104, p = 0.014), suggesting a possible inverted U-shaped association. Based on the model estimate, the vertex of the curve was approximately 0.178 s; however, this value should be treated only as a preliminary reference.

After the quadratic term was included, maximum height of the second flight remained positively associated with landing angle (B = 130.577, p = 0.001), and the model explained 67.68% of the variance (R² = 0.677). Given the small sample size, the quadratic finding should be interpreted only as exploratory.

Discussion

Vault performance depends on the coordinated transformation of horizontal momentum into vertical displacement and angular momentum through the run-up, springboard contact, table support, second flight, and landing phases. Although previous studies have examined specific vault techniques or isolated movement phases, competition-based kinematic evidence comparing different vault technique types in elite male gymnasts remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to compare key kinematic parameters among vault technique types and to explore phase-specific kinematic factors associated with landing angle. The first hypothesis was partially supported. Approach velocity differed robustly between direct-entry and Yurchenko vaults, whereas other between-group differences were less stable and should be interpreted as exploratory. The second hypothesis was also partially supported. Maximum height of the second flight was associated with landing angle in the hierarchical model, whereas approach velocity and table contact time were not independently associated with landing angle in the main linear model. Given the small sample size and the lack of a significant correlation between landing angle and E-score, all findings were interpreted as exploratory, and landing angle was treated only as a kinematic descriptor of body posture at initial mat contact.

Kinematic differences among vault entry techniques

The most consistent difference between entry types was approach velocity, which was greater in direct-entry vaults than in Yurchenko vaults. This finding remained stable across sensitivity analyses, suggesting that approach velocity was the clearest kinematic distinction between the two entry strategies. This difference may be explained by the distinct biomechanical characteristics of each entry method19. In direct-entry vaults, gymnasts perform a linear sprint followed by a forward hurdle and take-off, which may help retain approach velocity. This process provides the initial translational kinetic energy (TKE) for subsequent energy conversion. In contrast, Yurchenko vaults involve a round-off prior to springboard contact. During this phase, part of the accumulated TKE may be converted into angular kinetic energy (AKE) to initiate backward rotation about the transverse axis7. Consequently, the horizontal velocity of the center of mass at springboard contact tends to be lower than in direct-entry techniques.

The mean table release angle was larger in direct-entry vaults (82.07 ± 3.58°) than in Yurchenko vaults (76.82 ± 6.36°). In direct-entry vaults, the CM may follow a steeper trajectory during the first flight phase, which may be associated with a larger release angle and more favorable conditions for subsequent energy transfer. In contrast, Yurchenko vaults may exhibit a flatter CM trajectory. At table repulsion, the gymnast’s torso may need to rotate past the vertical plane, facilitating the conversion of horizontal momentum into angular momentum20. During the table support phase, changes in shoulder and trunk posture may contribute to differences in table release mechanics; however, shoulder flexion angle was not directly measured in the present study. Therefore, this explanation should be regarded as a technical interpretation rather than direct evidence for the observed trend.

The mean maximum height of the second flight was lower in Yurchenko vaults (2.86 ± 0.09 m) than in direct-entry vaults (2.98 ± 0.11 m). Previous studies have suggested that the combination of the round-off and back handspring in Yurchenko techniques may elevate the center of mass in a step-like manner, potentially contributing to greater second flight height10,21. However, the present results show a different pattern. In elite competitions, gymnasts often perform Yurchenko vaults combined with multiple somersaults and longitudinal twists to increase the D-score. These additional twisting demands may reduce vertical displacement. Biomechanical models suggest that each additional 180° twist around the longitudinal axis requires increased angular momentum7. As a result, a greater proportion of mechanical energy may be allocated to rotational motion during the table support phase, potentially limiting vertical height during the second flight. These findings suggest that training may benefit from focusing on core stability and efficient force application during table contact to improve energy transfer.

Landing angle was greater in direct-entry vaults (58.80 ± 15.65°) than in Yurchenko vaults (47.04 ± 6.27°). This pattern may suggest a relatively more extended body posture at the instant of landing in direct-entry vaults. However, the present study found no significant correlation between landing angle and E-score, indicating that landing angle should not be interpreted as a direct proxy for landing quality, landing stability, or execution score. According to the Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique (FIG) Code of Points, landing evaluation is multidimensional and includes posture, steps, balance adjustments, falls, and other execution deductions; therefore, hip and knee flexion during landing represents only one possible component of execution evaluation rather than a determinant of E-score22.

From a motor control perspective, direct-entry vaults may allow more favorable visual information for spatial orientation before landing compared with the backward-oriented trajectory in Yurchenko vaults19. Previous research highlights that enhanced visual feedback during aerial somersaults may significantly improve spatial orientation and subsequent landing preparation23. In this sample, Yurchenko vaults were associated with a backward-facing landing relative to the vaulting table. At initial ground contact, gymnasts may have retained backward horizontal momentum. This may lead to increased hip and knee flexion during landing to absorb impact, adjust the center of mass, and maintain balance24. However, because visual contact, head orientation, joint moments, landing steps, and time to stabilization were not directly measured in this study, this explanation should be regarded as a technical interpretation rather than direct evidence of landing stability.

These findings suggest that training should not focus solely on maximizing aerial rotation efficiency. Instead, incorporating neuromuscular training, such as depth jumps and eccentric plyometric exercises, may help improve lower-limb joint stiffness regulation25,26. Such adaptations may support more controlled landing mechanics, although the present study did not directly assess performance deductions or landing stability.

Biomechanical variations in table repulsion techniques

Table contact angle showed a group-level difference in the exploratory subgroup analysis. Tsukahara vaults showed a larger table contact angle (39.80 ± 8.89°) than Handspring vaults (27.79 ± 3.72°). Although Welch’s robust test reached statistical significance, this result did not remain significant after Holm correction, and the handspring group included only three trials. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory rather than as a stable confirmatory difference. This difference may reflect the biomechanical demands of the executed skills. In the present sample, the handspring vaults included double front somersaults in tucked or piked positions, with or without a half twist, with D-scores ranging from 5.2 to 5.6. To achieve sufficient vertical height and angular momentum for these forward rotations, gymnasts may contact the table at a smaller angle. This contact posture may facilitate effective blocking and support the conversion of horizontal kinetic energy into vertical displacement7. In contrast, Tsukahara vaults often incorporate longitudinal twists, such as Kasamatsu and Tsukahara with 1½ twists. During the table support phase, a larger (steeper) contact angle may be advantageous compared with Handspring vaults, as it may help accommodate the trunk rotation and asymmetrical blocking required to initiate aerial twisting27. However, because joint moments, ground reaction forces, and energy transfer were not directly measured in the present study, this biomechanical explanation should be regarded as a technical interpretation rather than direct causal evidence.

The maximum height of the second flight was higher on average in Handspring vaults (3.09 ± 0.09 m) than in Tsukahara vaults (2.95 ± 0.09 m). Although the conventional ANOVA indicated a statistically significant difference, Welch’s robust test showed only a marginal trend; therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously as an exploratory result rather than a stable between-group difference. From a mechanical perspective, this trend toward lower vertical displacement in Tsukahara vaults may be related to differences in energy transfer during table support. One potential mechanism is the staggered, asymmetrical hand placement characteristic of the Tsukahara entry, which may lead to a sequential blocking action. This asymmetrical repulsion pattern may redistribute part of the upward momentum, thereby potentially reducing the efficiency of vertical momentum conversion28. Second, when Tsukahara vaults are combined with longitudinal twists (e.g., Kasamatsu), gymnasts may need to convert translational kinetic energy (TKE) into angular kinetic energy (AKE) around the longitudinal axis during table contact7. Previous energetic analyses have demonstrated that during the Tsukahara entry, the system’s AKE reserves increase by approximately 70%, most of which must be preserved through the repulsion phase to support subsequent aerial twisting7. During the brief table contact phase, gymnasts may allocate a greater proportion of mechanical energy to preserving sufficient AKE to support rotation. This may limit the energy available for conversion into potential energy (PE), resulting in a lower maximum height of the second flight. However, because the present study did not directly measure hand reaction forces, joint moments, mechanical energy transfer, or angular momentum, this explanation should be regarded as a biomechanical interpretation based on previous literature rather than direct causal evidence.

Trajectory angle was retained as a descriptive kinematic variable because it reflects the direction of the center-of-mass velocity vector at table release and therefore describes the initial direction of the second-flight trajectory. In the present dataset, trajectory angle showed directional differences among vault categories but did not show stable between-group differences across sensitivity analyses. Therefore, it was interpreted as a descriptive indicator of second-flight direction rather than a robust differentiating variable. Given the small sample size and the need to keep the regression model parsimonious, trajectory angle was not entered into the hierarchical regression model; instead, maximum height of the second flight was selected as the primary second-flight variable because it more directly represented the vertical outcome of the preceding energy-transfer phases.

Kinematic factors associated with landing angle

This study used a theory-driven hierarchical linear regression model to further examine the relationships of D-score, approach velocity, table contact time, and maximum height of the second flight with landing angle. It should first be clarified that landing angle in this study represents only a kinematic descriptor of body posture at the instant of landing, rather than a direct proxy for landing quality, landing stability, or E-score.

In the hierarchical regression model, D-score was entered first to control for the potential effect of vault difficulty on landing angle. The results showed no significant association between D-score and landing angle, suggesting that, in the present sample, vault difficulty itself did not directly explain the variation in landing angle. This result does not imply that vault difficulty has no influence on landing performance. Rather, it indicates that D-score, as a composite indicator of difficulty, may not by itself capture the complex technical demands of specific vaults, including aerial twisting, somersaulting, body posture adjustment, and landing preparation. The biomechanical demands associated with different technique categories and FIG codes are not identical. Therefore, D-score is more appropriately treated as a potential confounding factor to be controlled for, rather than as a direct determinant of landing angle.

Approach velocity was also not significantly associated with landing angle in the final model. Approach velocity is an important initial kinematic condition in vaulting, as it provides the horizontal momentum base for the subsequent springboard take-off, table support, and second flight phases29–31. However, the present results suggest that a faster approach velocity does not necessarily translate into a larger landing angle. One possible explanation is that the effect of approach velocity on body posture at the instant of landing is not a direct linear relationship. Instead, it may be mediated through several subsequent phases, including springboard take-off, table repulsion, angular momentum generation, and postural adjustment during the second flight. In other words, approach velocity provides the initial energy condition for completing the vault, but its effect depends on whether the gymnast can effectively redirect energy and control body posture in the subsequent phases. Therefore, training should not focus solely on achieving faster run-up or approach velocity; rather, it should emphasize the efficiency of technical coordination across the run-up, board contact, and table support phases.

The table support phase is a key stage for mechanical energy transfer and angular momentum regulation in vaulting, during which the characteristics of the blocking action influence the overall efficiency of the vault. Traditional perspectives on upper-body explosive strength often suggest that a shorter table contact time reflects greater repulsion efficiency and may contribute to greater second-flight height and momentum32–35. The table contact times observed in the present sample ranged from 0.12 to 0.22 s, which is broadly consistent with the range reported in elite gymnasts19.

However, in the main hierarchical regression model, table contact time was not significantly associated with landing angle. This suggests that there was no stable simple linear relationship between table contact time and body posture at the instant of landing. A very short table contact time may indicate insufficient time for effective blocking, force production, and postural adjustment, whereas an excessively long contact time may reflect reduced support efficiency or loss of horizontal momentum. Therefore, the relationship between table contact time and landing posture may not follow a simple “shorter is better” or “longer is better” pattern. The exploratory quadratic analysis further suggested a possible inverted U-shaped relationship between table contact time and landing angle. Within a certain range, a moderate increase in table contact time may be associated with a larger landing angle, whereas beyond that range, the association may weaken or even reverse. This interpretation is consistent with the view that moderate extension of key technical phases may support high-quality execution in Yurchenko vaults36. Nevertheless, this quadratic analysis was post hoc and exploratory, and the sample size was limited. Therefore, the estimated peak should not be interpreted as an “optimal” table contact time. Rather, the present finding suggests that future studies should examine whether a functional range of table contact time exists in elite vaulting, instead of treating table contact time only as a linear indicator. From a practical perspective, these findings suggest that athletes may benefit from optimizing, rather than simply minimizing, the duration and quality of force production during table contact. Upper-body conditioning that combines explosive exercises with isometric and high-load resistance training may help improve the capacity to sustain effective force production during the repulsion phase. However, because landing angle was not significantly correlated with E-score in the present study, these training implications should be interpreted as considerations for improving table support mechanics and pre-landing body posture, rather than as direct evidence for improving landing quality or execution score.

The positive association between maximum height of the second flight and landing angle is consistent with theoretical expectations. Greater vertical displacement prolongs flight time, providing additional time to complete rotations about the transverse and longitudinal axes. This extended flight duration may allow more time for processing visual and vestibular information. Such mechanisms may theoretically facilitate neuromuscular pre-activation (feedforward control) of the lower-extremity musculature, supporting appropriate joint stiffness prior to impact37. From a biomechanical perspective, insufficient second flight height may disrupt spatiotemporal preparation for landing. If extension occurs before rotation is fully completed, the center of mass (CM) may shift rearward relative to the base of support at touchdown, increasing the likelihood of compensatory deep squatting or posterior instability. Conversely, if extension is delayed to complete rotation under limited height, residual forward momentum may result in an under-rotated landing. These patterns suggest that adequate vertical displacement during the second flight supports a more controlled and upright landing posture.

Overall, the hierarchical regression results suggest that maximum height of the second flight was the kinematic variable most consistently associated with landing angle. Although approach velocity and table contact time remain important components of the vaulting technique chain, their relationships with landing angle appeared more complex and were not independently significant in the main linear model. From a practical training perspective, these findings suggest that coaches may need to consider not only approach velocity and force production during table support, but also whether these factors are effectively translated into adequate second-flight height and appropriate pre-landing postural adjustment. Training goals should therefore not focus on a single isolated variable. Instead, they should be framed within the complete technical sequence, including the integrated optimization of approach velocity, springboard contact rhythm, table repulsion efficiency, aerial posture control, and pre-landing preparation. Because landing angle was not significantly correlated with E-score, the training implications proposed here relate only to the kinematic optimization of body posture at initial mat contact. They should not be interpreted as direct evidence for improving E-score or overall landing quality.

Limitations

While this study provides a kinematic analysis of key vaulting phases, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was small and specific to elite male gymnasts. Because the participants were finalists at the 2022 Chinese Gymnastics Championships, the dataset was limited to 16 vault trials performed by eight athletes. Although these data reflect real elite competition performance, the small and unequal group sizes, particularly for handspring-type and Yurchenko-type vaults, reduced statistical power and limited generalizability. Therefore, all group comparisons and regression results should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory. The small sample also limited the feasibility of D-score-matched subgroup analyses and more complex mixed-effects models. Second, measurement uncertainty should be considered. The data were collected using a markerless motion capture system in an official competition setting. Because reflective markers, force platforms, or other gold-standard measurement devices could not be used simultaneously, dataset-specific measurement errors for the SPROT system could not be directly estimated. Therefore, the measurement uncertainty discussed in this study was based on previous SPROT-related methodological studies and validation studies of comparable multi-camera markerless motion capture systems, rather than on direct validation under the present competition conditions. Findings with differences close to the expected range of measurement uncertainty should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Third, this study did not include kinetic or neuromuscular measurements. The analysis was based on spatiotemporal and kinematic variables derived from markerless motion capture. Without force plates or surface electromyography, ground reaction forces, joint torques, joint power, angular momentum, mechanical energy transfer, and muscle activation patterns could not be directly assessed. As a result, mechanistic explanations related to energy transfer and postural control remain limited. Fourth, landing angle was only a kinematic descriptor. The correlation analysis showed no significant association between landing angle and the official E-score. Therefore, landing angle should not be interpreted as a direct proxy for landing quality, landing stability, or execution score. Instead, it should be understood only as a descriptor of body posture at initial mat contact. The FIG E-score is a holistic evaluation that includes aerial posture, completion of twists and rotations, landing steps, balance adjustments, falls, and other execution deductions, and therefore cannot be represented by a single kinematic variable. Fifth, vault difficulty may have confounded the observed technique-category differences. Although the FIG code, D-score, E-score, and final score were reported for each trial, and D-score was included as a control variable in the hierarchical regression model, the small sample size and unequal distribution of difficulty across vault families prevented a full separation of technique effects from difficulty-composition effects. Therefore, the observed differences among vault categories may reflect both vault family and difficulty composition. Finally, the regression and nonlinear analyses were exploratory. The hierarchical regression model and sensitivity analyses were used to examine associations, not causal relationships. In particular, the quadratic analysis of table contact time was exploratory and post hoc, and should not be used to define an optimal table contact time. Future studies with larger samples and multimodal biomechanical measurements are needed to verify these findings and better characterize the underlying energy transfer mechanisms.

As a result, the mechanistic interpretations remain primarily kinematic. Future studies incorporating multimodal biomechanical measurements are needed to better characterize energy transfer processes.

Conclusion

Based on a quantitative analysis of the biomechanical characteristics of elite male finalists at the 2022 Chinese Gymnastics Championships, this study draws the following conclusions. First, different vault technique types showed distinct kinematic profiles. The most robust difference was observed in approach velocity, which was faster in direct-entry vaults than in Yurchenko vaults. Other differences, including table release angle, maximum height of the second flight, and landing angle, showed only trend-level patterns and should be interpreted cautiously. Second, landing angle was not significantly correlated with the official E-score and should therefore be interpreted only as a kinematic descriptor of body posture at the instant of landing, rather than as a direct proxy for landing quality, landing stability, or execution score. Third, hierarchical regression showed that maximum height of the second flight was positively associated with landing angle after controlling for D-score, approach velocity, and table contact time. Approach velocity and table contact time were not independently associated with landing angle in the main linear model. The exploratory quadratic analysis suggested a possible nonlinear relationship between table contact time and landing angle, but it should not be used to define an optimal contact time. Overall, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory associations based on real competition data, rather than causal evidence.

Based on these biomechanical findings, several practical implications for elite gymnastics training can be proposed. (1) Optimization of approach kinematics. Training may consider sport-specific explosive power exercises aimed at supporting approach-speed development. In addition, technical drills targeting coordination during the hurdle and springboard phases may help improve the transition and support effective momentum generation. (2) Management of aerial energy transfer. For vaults involving longitudinal twists and multiple somersaults, training may emphasize core stability and effective asymmetrical blocking mechanics. This may support balanced force production and reduce energy loss during the flight phase. (3) Enhancement of landing neuromuscular control. Training may incorporate vestibular-based spatial awareness exercises together with eccentric plyometric training (e.g., depth jumps). Such approaches may improve lower-limb stiffness regulation and support feedforward neuromuscular control prior to landing. (4) Improvement of repulsion efficiency. Optimizing, rather than minimizing, table contact time within an effective range may support more efficient blocking. Upper-body conditioning that includes isometric and high-load resistance training may further improve force production during the repulsion phase.
