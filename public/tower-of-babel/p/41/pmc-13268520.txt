Peripheral blood eosinophil count for predicting progression-free survival in non-small cell lung cancer: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Wei Chen, Guozheng Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related death globally. Immune checkpoint inhibitors improve outcomes but benefit only a subset of patients. Peripheral blood eosinophil count (PBEC) is a potential predictive biomarker, yet its role in progression-free survival (PFS) remains underexplored. This retrospective study enrolled 121 stage III/IV NSCLC patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors at Anqing Medical Center from 2019 to 2024. Baseline and posttreatment PBEC were analyzed with clinical variables. Kaplan–Meier, log-rank test and Cox regression were used for PFS prognosis; univariate logistic regression, Akaike Information Criterion stepwise selection and nested cross-validation were applied for model exploration. Multivariate Cox regression identified baseline Log(PBEC) as an independent protective factor for PFS (hazard ratio = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.36–0.94, P = .026), with higher PBEC linked to longer PFS. Five independent predictors were screened: sex, liver metastasis, performance status score, anti-angiogenic therapy and Log(PBEC). The exploratory model showed stable performance and clinical net benefit. Baseline PBEC is a potential and accessible predictor of PFS in NSCLC patients. A nomogram incorporating PBEC and clinical factors provides preliminary support for prognostic assessment and requires prospective validation before clinical application.

1. Introduction

Lung cancer is a highly aggressive malignant neoplasm that originates from the bronchial mucosa or glandular tissues of the lungs, posing a significant threat to human health and survival on a global scale. Epidemiological data from multiple countries and regions have shown a significant upward trend in the incidence and mortality of lung cancer,[1] and the prevalence and mortality of lung cancer still top the list of malignant tumors.[2] From a clinical standpoint, lung cancer is primarily categorized into 2 main types: small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), where NSCLC constitutes roughly 85% of all lung cancer cases.[3] The therapeutic strategy for NSCLC should be developed through comprehensive assessment of factors including tumor stage, patient performance status, and genetic mutation profiles. Nevertheless, clinical evidence indicates that over 60% of NSCLC patients have already developed either local or distant metastasis at initial diagnosis, while 30% to 55% of patients who receive radical surgery subsequently develop disease recurrence and ultimately succumb to tumor progression,[4-6] with their 5-year overall survival (OS) rate being only approximately 23%.[7]

The emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has delivered a groundbreaking advancement for the treatment of NSCLC. By blocking immunosuppressive pathways such as PD-1/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) to reactivate the body’s antitumor immune response, ICIs have demonstrated significant efficacy in various malignant tumors including melanoma and NSCLC.[8,9] Particularly importantly, the tail of the survival curve of patients receiving ICI treatment shows a long-term plateau effect, which significantly expands the pool of potentially curable patients with advanced NSCLC.[10] Regrettably, though, only a minority of patients can derive durable benefits from ICI treatment, while some patients may develop primary or secondary drug resistance.[11] Therefore, identifying reliable biomarkers for predicting treatment response and prognosis holds considerable significance for optimizing therapeutic strategies. Currently, although biomarkers including PD-L1 expression level and tumor mutational burden (TMB) have been applied in clinical evaluation, they have limitations including high detection cost and difficulty in dynamic monitoring. Moreover, they are unable to fully reflect treatment efficacy and accurately predict patients’ treatment response and recurrence risk.[12-15] In view of this, the development of more convenient and efficient biomarkers for evaluating patients’ response to neoadjuvant immunotherapy and their prognosis has become an urgent need in current research.

The complexity of the tumor microenvironment (TME) can affect the changes in circulating biomarkers through multiple pathways. Peripheral blood biomarkers, however, are highly sensitive to microenvironmental changes, enabling them to rapidly reflect tumor status, treatment efficacy, and prognostic trends. Additionally, they possess advantages such as easy accessibility and feasibility for dynamic monitoring, thus emerging as potential surrogate biomarkers. Among these biomarkers, peripheral blood eosinophil count (PBEC) has been identified as a promising prognostic marker across various cancer types.[16,17] As innate immune cells, eosinophils can regulate antitumor immune responses by releasing cytokines, and changes in their count exhibit a close association with the efficacy of ICI treatment. In most patients receiving ICI therapy, fluctuations in peripheral blood eosinophil levels are correlated with changes in other white blood cell components.[17-20]

This study aimed to screen independent prognostic predictors for progression-free survival (PFS) in ICI-treated NSCLC patients, and construct an exploratory prognostic prediction model based on PBEC and routine clinical characteristics.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study population

All patients with stage III or IV NSCLC who received ICI treatment at Anqing Medical Center of Anhui Medical University from January 2019 to January 2024 were enrolled. This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Anqing Medical Center of Anhui Medical University (Approval No. 2025-159). All procedures were performed in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. All patient clinical data were de-identified before analysis to protect patient privacy and personal health information.

Retrospective inclusion criteria:

Confirmed diagnosis of NSCLC by histopathological or cytological examination and staged as unresectable stage III or stage IV based on imaging evaluation (e.g., CT/PET-CT);

Received ICI-based monotherapy or ICI combined with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or anti-angiogenic drugs;

Complete clinical diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up data, including baseline PBEC before treatment, dynamic PBEC monitoring data 1 month after immunotherapy, and complete PFS endpoint records.

Participant flow:

A total of 140 patients with stage III/IV NSCLC who received ICI treatment were initially collected from the electronic medical record system of Anqing Medical Center of Anhui Medical University between January 2019 and January 2024. After applying the preliminary inclusion and exclusion criteria, 11 patients were excluded: 1 with an incompatible pathological type (large cell carcinoma) and 10 with incomplete follow-up data (unable to confirm PFS endpoint). This resulted in 129 patients with complete core clinical and laboratory data, and missing values of noncore variables were imputed using the multiple imputation method. Subsequently, 8 patients with extreme data anomalies were excluded using the interquartile range method (5 with baseline PBEC <20/μL, 3 with eosinophil change rate >1000%). Finally, 121 patients were included in the statistical analysis. The participant flow diagram is shown in Figure 1.

2.2. Data collection and variable definition

Patient demographic characteristics (age, sex, smoking history), clinical features (tumor type, stage, metastatic site, performance status score [PS score], treatment regimen), and laboratory indicators (complete blood count and serum albumin levels at baseline and 1 month after treatment) were collected.

PBEC: absolute number of peripheral blood eosinophils (×109/L) at baseline and 1 month after treatment.

2.3. Prognostic outcomes

PFS was defined as the period from the initiation of treatment to tumor progression or all-cause death. Progression events were defined strictly according to response evaluation criteria in solid tumors 1.1 criteria via scheduled imaging. For patients with baseline metastasis, new lesions or new distant metastasis were defined as progression events. For exploratory analysis only, patients were divided into the short-term survival group (PFS ≤ median PFS) and the long-term survival group (PFS > median PFS) based on the median PFS. For exploratory binary PFS analysis only, censored patients with follow-up duration less than the cohort median PFS were not assigned to either the short-term or long-term survival groups to avoid survival bias caused by incomplete follow-up.

2.4. Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R software (Version 4.4.3).

2.4.1. Primary survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank test were used to compare PFS between high and low baseline PBEC groups stratified by the cohort median. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression were performed to identify independent prognostic factors for PFS, with results reported as hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CI).

2.4.2. Secondary exploratory analysis

For exploratory hypothesis-generating purposes only, missing value handling: multiple imputation (m = 5) was adopted. Categorical variables were imputed by means of multinomial logistic regression, whereas continuous variables underwent imputation through predictive mean matching.

Variable selection for exploratory analysis: Candidate variables associated with binary PFS were first screened using univariate logistic regression (P < .10). Subsequently, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)-based bidirectional stepwise method was used to identify independent predictive factors.

Model construction: Based on the independent predictive factors, 5 models were constructed, including logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost, support vector machine, and naive Bayes.

Model performance was evaluated via 5-fold, 3-repetition nested cross-validation, with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) as the primary assessment metric.

Model validation: A nomogram was plotted to visualize the prediction model. Calibration curves were constructed via the bootstrap method (B = 1000), and decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to assess the clinical net benefit.

The missing data rate for all variables was <5%, with no imputation performed.

3.2. Primary survival analysis results

3.2.1. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis

A total of 121 patients were stratified into the low baseline PBEC group (n = 61) and high baseline PBEC group (n = 60) according to the cohort median of baseline PBEC. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients in the high baseline PBEC group had significantly longer PFS than those in the low baseline PBEC group, with a statistically significant difference between the 2 groups (log-rank χ2 = 11.28, P < .001; Fig. 2). The median PFS was 19.2 months (95% CI: 14.3–24.1 months) in the high PBEC group, compared with 8.7 months (95% CI: 6.5–10.9 months) in the low PBEC group.

The log-rank test was used to compare survival differences between the 2 groups. Patients with high baseline PBEC had significantly longer PFS than those with low PBEC (log-rank P < .001, adjusted HR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.36–0.94). 3.2.3. Sensitivity analysis

Notably, no patients in this cohort received concurrent EGFR/ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs, driver gene-targeted therapy) alongside ICIs; all combination therapy in this cohort was anti-angiogenic therapy. To address potential cohort heterogeneity introduced by combination anti-angiogenic therapy, we performed a sensitivity analysis by excluding patients receiving concurrent anti-angiogenic therapy (n = 27 excluded, 94 patients remaining in the homogeneous ICI-only cohort). In this sensitivity analysis, the association between baseline log-transformed PBEC and longer PFS remained robust and statistically significant (adjusted hazard ratios = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.32–0.95, P = .031), consistent with the primary analysis results. This confirmed that the prognostic value of baseline PBEC was not confounded by concurrent anti-angiogenic therapy.

3.4. Construction of predictive models and comparison of their performance

Five exploratory models were constructed with the retained independent predictors. The logistic regression model showed the most stable performance, with the smallest AUC difference between training and validation sets (ΔAUC = 0.06) and no significant overfitting (Fig. 3). Other machine learning models had higher training-set AUC but marked performance decline in the validation set, indicating overfitting risk. All analyses were for exploratory hypothesis-generating purposes only, and results cannot be directly applied to clinical decision-making.

3.5. Model validation

A nomogram was developed derived from the exploratory logistic regression model, incorporating 5 predictive factors: sex, liver metastasis status, logarithmically transformed PBEC, PS score, and anti-angiogenic therapy. Among these, baseline PBEC had the highest weight in predicting long-term PFS, indicating that it plays a core role in assessing the risk of long-term PFS in patients (Fig. 4).

To assess the model’s clinical utility, this study used DCA to quantify the model’s net benefit (Fig. 5). The findings demonstrated that in the risk threshold range of 0.1 to 0.55, the net clinical benefit of the exploratory logistic regression model was higher than that of the 2 extreme strategies, while the net benefit became equivocal above the threshold of 0.55. This indicates that when identifying patients with long-term PFS using this exploratory model, a preliminary balance can be achieved between reducing unnecessary interventions and avoiding missed diagnoses, suggesting that the model has potential exploratory value and requires prospective validation before clinical application.

4. Discussion

This study identified baseline PBEC as an independent protective factor for PFS in NSCLC patients, along with sex, liver metastasis, PS score, and anti-angiogenic therapy as key predictive variables. The exploratory logistic regression model showed stable predictive performance, with baseline PBEC contributing the highest weight. This model is hypothesis-generating and requires prospective multi-center validation before clinical application.

This study found that a higher baseline PBEC was correlated with more favorable survival outcomes in patients with NSCLC, and it played a core role in assessing the risk of long-term PFS in patients. Existing studies have clarified the mechanism of action of eosinophils in the TME and their association with tumor biological characteristics. Studies by Pu et al[21] and Takeuchi et al[22] showed that in NSCLC cohorts treated with PD-1 inhibitors, patients with elevated pretreatment PBEC had higher objective response rates and longer PFS, which is consistent with the results of this study. Furthermore, the study by Takeuchi et al[23] verified this association in a cohort that included multiple clinical factors and extended to dynamic changes in PBEC, providing strong support for the universality of eosinophils as a predictive biomarker for ICI efficacy.

Consistent with our core finding that elevated baseline PBEC is an independent protective factor for PFS in ICI-treated NSCLC patients, eosinophils exert important functions in tumor immunity beyond their classic antibacterial and antiparasitic roles.[24] Their development is primarily regulated by IL-3, IL-5 and GM-CSF[25,26] and they migrate to tumor lesions within the tumor microenvironment (TME) via chemotactic signals to exert antitumor effects.[27-29] The antitumor activity of eosinophils is mediated by 2 core pathways: direct cytotoxicity through the release of granule proteins,[30,31] and enhanced antitumor immune responses via the secretion of cytokines and chemokines.[32-35] Eosinophils are widely distributed across various solid tumors,[36-42] with inconsistent prognostic associations across cancer types,[43-46] which may stem from their dual pro-tumor and antitumor activities,[47] with functional roles shaped by cancer type and TME context.[48,49]

Notably, the exploratory logistic regression model constructed in this study combines PBEC with clinical characteristics such as sex and liver metastasis, which shows improved predictive efficacy compared with a single indicator in exploratory analysis. This suggests that in clinical practice, it is necessary to comprehensively consider patients’ basic characteristics and biological indicators to more accurately assess the prognostic outcomes. Yi et al[50] used multivariate logistic regression analysis, and the results showed that an elevated absolute PBEC was an independent risk factor affecting lung cancer prognosis. Therefore, they further generated a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for assessing the predictive value of absolute PBEC for PFS in lung cancer patients. The computed AUC value was 0.63, alongside a sensitivity of 91.20% and a specificity of 33.30%, indicating that absolute PBEC has predictive value but relatively low accuracy. Based on the plotting of the ROC curve and the calculation of AUC, absolute PBEC exhibits high sensitivity in predicting lung cancer PFS, which suggests that it has strong discriminative ability for predicting PFS in lung cancer patients. However, due to its low specificity, false positives may occur in clinical applications, leading to misjudgment of results. Thus, absolute PBEC has certain clinical application prospects and value in predicting lung cancer prognosis. Huai et al[3] demonstrated that posttreatment eosinophil percentage, systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), modified Glasgow prognostic score, and ΔSII served as independent predictors of major pathological response in patients undergoing neoadjuvant immunotherapy. To explore the impact of pre- versus posttreatment changes in inflammatory markers on patient outcomes, ROC curves were generated based on the variations in these inflammatory markers. The findings indicated that the AUC values of Δeosinophil (%) and ΔSII were among the top 2 in both cohorts.

The final model was built using AIC stepwise selection,[51] and 1-month PBEC was excluded since it did not improve model fit. Statistically, AIC penalizes redundant variables, and baseline PBEC was more stable.[52,53] Clinically, early posttreatment eosinophil fluctuations are easily disturbed by nonspecific factors,[17-20] making baseline status more predictive. The exclusion of 1-month PBEC does not mean it is useless; its dynamic prognostic value needs validation in larger future studies.

This study confirms baseline PBEC as an independent prognostic biomarker for PFS in NSCLC patients. Future studies should further explore the prognostic value of dynamic eosinophil changes and the underlying mechanisms of eosinophil-mediated antitumor immunity to provide evidence for optimizing ICI treatment strategies.

5. Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size of 121 patients, which may lead to selection bias and limit the generalizability of the results. Second, key established ICI predictive biomarkers, including PD-L1 expression, TMB, and microsatellite instability, were not available in this cohort. Preclinical and clinical evidence has demonstrated that patients with high PD-L1 expression, high TMB, or microsatellite instability-high status have significantly better ICI treatment outcomes, and these molecular features may also be associated with systematically different peripheral eosinophil counts. For example, upregulated type 2 immune responses in the tumor microenvironment of PD-L1-high tumors may drive eosinophil recruitment and increase peripheral PBEC levels, which could introduce positive confounding bias to the observed association between PBEC and PFS. Future prospective studies should adjust for these molecular biomarkers to validate the independent prognostic value of PBEC. Third, this study only focused on PBEC at baseline and 1 month after treatment and did not explore the association between dynamic changes of PBEC during the full treatment course and PFS. Finally, all combination therapy in this cohort was anti-angiogenic agents, and no patients received concurrent EGFR/ALK TKIs or other driver gene-targeted therapy. This may limit the extrapolation of the results to NSCLC patients receiving ICI combined with driver gene-targeted therapy, which should be verified in subsequent studies with more diverse treatment regimens.

6. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that baseline PBEC is a significant and independent predictor of PFS in NSCLC patients. By combining PBEC with readily available clinical variables including sex, liver metastasis, PS score, and anti-angiogenic therapy history, we developed an exploratory logistic regression model with acceptable predictive performance for hypothesis generation. The exploratory model outperformed several machine learning algorithms in stability and interpretability in this exploratory analysis, and its calibration and DCA confirmed its preliminary robustness for research purposes. These findings lend preliminary support to PBEC as a potential convenient biomarker for prognostic stratification, and prospective multi-center validation is required before clinical application.
