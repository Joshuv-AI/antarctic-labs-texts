Updating Clinical Management of Cystic and Alveolar Echinococcosis: Insights and Challenges
Authors: Beate Gruener, Marko Kornmann
Published: 2025

Introduction

Cystic echinococcosis (CE) and alveolar echinococcosis (AE) remain rare but clinically demanding parasitic diseases with substantial diagnostic and therapeutic challenges [1, 2]. This editorial introduces the present special issue, which brings together new insights on imaging, clinical management, interventional approaches, and pathology of both CE and AE. Together, the contributions highlight recent advances, address persisting uncertainties, and point toward future developments in guidelines and treatment strategies.

Echinococcosis is often regarded as a rarity in daily clinical practice. Yet for those of us working in referral centers, the disease is a constant reminder of how parasitic infections can challenge modern medicine. CE, caused by Echinococcus granulosus sensu lato, and AE, caused by Echinococcus multilocularis, present with distinct biological behavior but share a common reality: patients frequently encounter delays in diagnosis, their management requires long-term follow-up, and therapeutic decisions are rarely straightforward.

CE has a worldwide distribution and is closely associated with livestock farming and sheepdog husbandry. In Central and Western Europe, most cases of CE are imported, reflecting the region of origin of the patient. AE is endemic only in the Northern Hemisphere and ranks among the most threatening parasitic diseases in this region.

Yet, because of their rarity, diagnostic and therapeutic uncertainties remain common, and it must be assumed that clinical management is often suboptimal. Insufficient dissemination of knowledge adds to this challenge: clinicians and radiologists frequently fail to distinguish reliably between AE and CE. This differentiation, however, is crucial for establishing an adequate therapeutic strategy [3, 4].

Highlights of This Issue

The contributions in this special issue of Visceral Medicine reflect recent advances in the understanding and management of echinococcosis. One study reports a contemporary cohort of patients newly diagnosed with AE between 2019 and 2023 at a major referral center, comparing their characteristics with earlier and other European cohorts. These data highlight shifting patterns in disease presentation and enhance our understanding of its natural history in the era of improved imaging and heightened awareness [5, 6].

Further articles illustrate the wide morphological spectrum of AE in ultrasound and cross-sectional imaging. While typical features can be identified, no pathognomonic patterns exist, in contrast to CE. The distinct macroscopic and microscopic appearances of AE and CE in pathology are also presented.

Surgical innovation is showcased in a manuscript on minimally invasive and robotic approaches to AE, demonstrating that even complex hepatic resections can increasingly be performed with reduced morbidity. Complementing this, a review of non-operative interventions in both CE and AE underscores that not all patients are surgical candidates and that interventional strategies remain essential components of individualized management.

Personal Perspective

As both clinicians and researchers who have worked with patients affected by echinococcosis for many years, we remain convinced that expertise and multidisciplinary cooperation are decisive. Optimal management of CE and AE cannot be achieved by isolated specialists – it requires hepatobiliary surgeons, infectious disease physicians, radiologists, pathologists, and parasitologists to collaborate seamlessly. Equally important is the recognition that echinococcosis is not a static disease: both its epidemiology and its therapeutic possibilities are changing.

Conclusion and Outlook

With this special issue, we aim to provide both an update and an outlook. This special issue reflects an important step in keeping the management of echinococcosis at the forefront of visceral medicine. By bringing together updated cohort data, refined histo-/pathological and imaging knowledge, and innovative surgical and interventional strategies, it highlights both progress and persisting gaps.

Echinococcosis remains a rare disease, but with high clinical impact. By integrating expertise across disciplines and countries, and by continuously updating guidelines considering new evidence, we can further improve diagnosis, management, and outcomes for patients affected by this challenging parasitic infection.

Reference centers and networks for rare diseases play an increasingly important role in ensuring high-quality care. International collaborations and registries must, therefore, be strengthened to generate robust data and guide evidence-based practice. In our personal experience, the most successful outcomes are achieved when patient care is not protocol-driven alone, but also tailored to the individual – balancing innovation with caution, and surgical boldness with medical prudence.
