Trends and Causes of Hospitalization Among People With HIV in Korea: A 20-Year Observational Study
Authors: Young Rong Kim, Ji Hyun Park, Eun Jin Kim, Hakjun Hyun, Young Hwa Choi, Jung Yeon Heo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe widespread use of antiretroviral therapy (ART) has shifted a change in hospitalization patterns among people with HIV. However, long term trends and causes of hospitalization among people with HIV (PWH) in Korea remain underexplored. This study aimed to investigate 20-year trends in hospitalization rates and causes among PWH.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed hospitalization data from 803 PWH aged ≥ 18 years who received care at the university-affiliated hospital from 2001 to 2020. Hospitalization data were examined across five 4-year intervals. Hospitalization rates per 100 person-years (PY) were estimated using generalized estimating equation models with a Poisson distribution, adjusting first for sex and age and then baseline CD4 T cell count and HIV viral load. Cox regression identified predictors of first hospitalization.ResultsOver 3,998 PY, 593 hospitalizations occurred among 322 patients. Unadjusted all-cause hospitalization rates declined from 92.3 to 8.6 per 100 PY, driven by significant reductions in AIDS-defining illnesses (−79.0% per period; 95% confidence interval [CI], −94.8 to −63.1) and non−AIDS infections (−48.4% per period; 95% CI, −63.3 to −33.5). Maternal conditions increased from 0 to 0.67 per 100 PY, with a mean change of 91.3.% per period (95% CI, 41.3–141.2), whereas most of the other chronic conditions and trauma/injury remained stable. Fully adjusted models demonstrated similar trends in most of the categories. Anal condyloma emerged as a leading causes of hospitalization in the later periods. Multivariate analysis revealed that age ≥ 50 years (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.42; 95% CI, 1.05–1.92), CD4 counts < 100 cells/mm3 (aHR, 2.48; 95% CI, 1.74–3.53), and HIV RNA > 100,000 copies/mL (aHR, 1.61; 95% CI, 1.13–2.30) at enrollment were associated with a higher hospitalization risk.ConclusionHospitalization rates among Korean PWH have declined substantially over two decades, primarily driven by a decline in opportunistic infections, attributable to earlier diagnosis and expanded ART access. However, persistent AIDS-related illnesses and the high proportion of non-AIDS-defining cancers diagnosed at advanced stage highlight the ongoing need for earlier HIV detection and a stigma-free healthcare environment that promotes care engagement for PWH.Keywords: Hospitalization, HIV, Anti-Retroviral Agents, Korea

INTRODUCTION

HIV infection is now considered a chronic disease. The development and adoption of antiretroviral therapy (ART) since the late 1990s has significantly reduced morbidity and mortality among people with HIV (PWH), largely attributable to a reduction in AIDS-related hospitalizations.1,2,3,4 With access to ART, the life expectancy of PWH is now comparable to that of the general population. As a result, the proportion of PWH aged > 50 years has reached nearly 40% since 2018, with a corresponding increase in the proportion of individuals aged ≥ 60 years in Korea.5,6,7

Despite these advances, PWH are grappling with a high burden of non-AIDS related conditions, including cardiovascular, renal, and liver diseases as well as malignancies.1,8,9,10,11 Persistent immune activation and chronic inflammation associated with HIV infection may contribute to the increased incidence of chronic end-organ diseases.12 In addition, while modern ART has become less toxic, cumulative ART exposure, compounded by a high prevalence of traditional risk factors, including smoking and substance abuse, may further elevate the risk of comorbidities in PWH compared to the general population.13,14

While numerous studies have examined hospitalization trends in the United States and European countries, research on hospitalization patterns among PWH in Korea remains scarce. Understanding hospitalization patterns over time in the context of ART advancements may provide valuable insights into the evolving burden of comorbidities among PWH. Therefore, we aimed to investigate hospitalization trends and causes among PWH receiving care at a university-affiliated hospital from 2001 to 2020. Additionally, we analyzed changes in the clinical characteristics of PWH over time and identified risk factors associated with hospitalization.

METHODS

Study population and follow-up

We retrospectively reviewed all hospitalization records of PWH who were diagnosed with HIV between January 2001 and December 2020 at Ajou University Hospital, Korea. PWH aged > 18 years old with at least one outpatient or emergency room visit were included. Hospitalization data were reviewed across five 4-year intervals: period 1 (2001–2004), period 2 (2005–2008), period 3 (2009–2012), period 4 (2013–2016), and period 5 (2017–2020). Person-years (PY) were calculated from either the date of cohort entry or January 1, 2001, whichever occurred later, to the earliest of death, loss to follow-up, or December 31, 2020. Person-years were censored at loss to follow-up, defined as 12 months with no outpatient CD4 T cell count or viral load. Inpatient days were excluded from the count of person-year.

Categorization of discharge diagnoses

We classified primary hospital discharge diagnoses into distinct categories to analyze hospitalization trends over a 20-year period. AIDs-defining illnesses were identified according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention criteria.15 Non-communicable diseases, defined according to World Health Organization classification, were also examined as causes of hospitalization (Supplementary Table 1). Within the non-communicable disease category, more common diagnoses, such as non-AIDS-defining cancer (NADC), cardiovascular diseases, and liver/gastrointestinal diseases, were analyzed individually, whereas less frequent diagnoses (< 10) were grouped under the category of “Other non-communicable diseases.” Hospitalizations due to trauma, injuries, and maternal conditions were also analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Hospitalization rate was calculated as the number of hospitalization episodes per 100 PY in each of the period. PWHs experiencing multiple hospitalizations were likely included across multiple time periods or contributed more than one hospitalization within each period. To account for potential overestimation due to planned readmissions, multiple admissions for NADCs within a 6-month window and for anal condyloma within a 3-month window were consolidated into single episodes. For all other diagnoses, each admission was treated as a separate episode. We used general estimating equations models with an independent correlation matrix to evaluate the temporal trends of hospitalization, assuming a Poisson distribution. Incidence rate ratio is reported as a mean percent change per period. The unadjusted model included only the hospital period as a linear variable to examine crude trends in hospitalization incidence rates (IRs). Partially adjusted model additionally included age (categorized as < 40, 40–49, 50–59, and ≥ 60 years) and sex as covariates. Fully adjusted models further incorporated the initial CD4 T-cell count recordedt within each period (≤ 200 vs. > 200 cells/µL) and HIV viral load (≤ 400 vs. > 400 copies/mL). We conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding hospitalizations that occurred on the day of cohort entry to assess potential overestimation of IRs. Cox regression analysis was used to assess the risk factors for first hospitalization. P-values were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Ajou University School of Medicine (IRB No. AJOUIRB-DB-2025-066), which waived the requirement for informed consent as the participants’ data are fully anonymized.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

The study included 803 PWH, who were followed up for a mean duration of 4.98 ± 4.62 years, contributing to 3998.9 PY. Among the 803 PWH, 19 (2.4%) patients died, and 154 (19.2%) were censored for loss to follow-up. A total of 523 patients (65.1%) were followed until the end of the study, of whom 90.4% were male and the median age was 37 (interquartile range [IQR], 28–47) years. Between the earliest (2001–2004) and the latest (2017–2020) study periods, the mean age of patients decreased over the study periods, from 41.15 ± 11.05 years in the first period to 37.24 ± 11.57 years in the last (P = 0.002). The median CD4 T cell count increased from 180 (IQR, 40–345) cells/μL to 340 (IQR, 150–510) cells/μL throughout the period (P < 0.001). HIV RNA levels at enrollment varied across periods with median viral load ranging from 18,979 (IQR, 1,009–76,630) in 2017–2020, to 57,000 (IQR, 6,878–130,000) in 2001–2004 (P = 0.015). The proportion of patients receiving ART increased from 74.5% to 94.8% (P < 0.001), and the median time to ART initiation shortened from 11 (IQR, 3–146) weeks to 3 (IQR, 2–6) weeks (P < 0.001). Concurrently, the proportion of PWH experiencing hospitalization decreased from 55.3% to 21.4% (P < 0.001), with the multiple hospitalization rate also decreasing (Table 1).

Characteristics of hospitalized PWH

Among the 803 PWH, 322 were hospitalized at least once, contributing to 593 cases of hospitalization. The median age of hospitalized patients was 40 (IQR, 31–50) years, median CD4 T cell count was 350 (IQR, 220–550) cells/μL, and median HIV RNA at admission was 26,003 (IQR, 0–190,581). As the proportion of ART experience before admission increased from 37.8% to 75.4%, the median CD4 T cell count at admission increased from 90 to 370 cells/μL (P < 0.001), alongside improved viral suppression and a decline in-hospital mortality over time (Supplementary Table 2).

Distribution of categories for primary discharge diagnosis

The most common category of hospital discharge diagnosis was AIDS-defining illness (36.6%), followed by non-AIDS-defining infection (29.8%) and liver/gastrointestinal diseases (7.4%). The proportion of AIDS-defining illness declined over time, whereas maternal conditions and other non-infectious, chronic conditions gradually emerged (Fig. 1). Pneumocystis pneumonia ranked highest in most periods but its proportion declined over time; in contrast, anal condyloma increased steadily and became the most frequent discharge diagnosis in 2017–2020 (Table 2).

Rates and causes of hospitalization over time

Unadjusted all-cause hospitalization rates decreased from 92.3 to 8.6 per 100 PY between period 1 and period 5, with a mean change of -50.9% per period (95% confidence interval [CI], −59.8% to −42.0%). The reductions were observed in AIDS-defining illness (−79.0% per period; 95% CI, −94.8% to −63.1%), non-AIDS-defining infection (−48.4% per period; 95% CI, −63.3% to −33.5%), NADC (−36.7% per period; 95% CI, −71.2% to −2.1%), liver/gastrointestinal diseases (−29.0% per period; 95% CI, −58.1% to 0.0%), and non-communicable diseases (−29.3% per period; 95% CI, −56.2% to −2.4%). However, maternal conditions increased from 0 to 0.67 per 100 PY, with a mean change of 91.3% per period (95% CI, 41.3% to 141.2%). Unadjusted hospitalization from most of the other categories showed stable trends (Table 3, Fig. 2). These trends remained consistent after adjusting for sex and age. Further, the fully adjusted model showed similar trends, but the percent change was attenuated in all-cause, AIDS-defining illness, and non-AIDS infection, highlighting the significance of CD4 T cell count and viral load. The sensitivity analysis results showed similar trends to the main findings, but the incidence rate ratio was lower due to the exclusion of patients with immediate hospitalization after cohort enrollment (Supplementary Table 3).

Risk factors for first hospitalization of PWH

Cox regression analysis results showed that age ≥ 50 years (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.42; 95% CI, 1.05–1.92), CD4 T cell count < 100 cells/mm3 (aHR, 2.48; 95% CI, 1.74–3.53), and HIV RNA >100,000 copies/mL (aHR, 1.61; 95% CI, 1.13–2.30) at enrollment were associated with the first hospitalization episode. Early ART initiation within 8 weeks of enrollment showed no association with reduced risk of hospitalization (aHR, 1.16; 95% CI, 0.89–1.51) (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Our study of PWH in care from 2001 to 2020 showed an improvement in immunologic profile and viral suppression over time, following widespread adoption of ART. All-cause hospitalization rate decreased from 94.8 to 11.5 per 100 PY, mainly due to the reductions in AIDS-defining illness and non-AIDS-defining infections. In contrast, the hospitalization rate for maternal conditions increased significantly (91.3% per period; 95% CI, 41.3% to 141.2%). The risk of hospital admission was associated with markers of advanced HIV infection and age > 50 years.

The “Treat all” policy and revised 2013 Korean guidelines recommending ART initiation regardless of CD4 T cell count led to earlier treatment initiation and improved immune recovery.16,17,18 This change was reflected in our cohort, with higher CD4 T cell counts and better viral suppression among hospitalized PWH contributing to reductions in AIDS-defining illnesses and non-AIDS infection. After adjusting for changes in age, CD4 T cell count, and viral load, we still observed decreases in all-cause hospitalization, AIDS-defining illness, and non-AIDS infection, suggesting that factors beyond immunologic status may affect hospitalization. These findings may be attributable to the expansion of voluntary testing in South Korea, which likely facilitated earlier diagnosis and reduced the frequency of late presentation, thereby lowering hospitalization rates.7,19,20 This interpretation is supported by changes in the baseline characteristics of our study population over time, including a younger median age and improved immunologic status at enrollment. Furthermore, the provision of financial support under the national health insurance since 2009 and structured counseling by HIV specialists may have enhanced treatment engagement, thereby reducing the burden of illness requiring hospitalization.20 In addition, expanded use of prophylaxis for opportunistic infections may have further contributed to the reduction in hospitalizations.

Despite these advances, AIDS-defining illnesses remained the leading cause of hospitalizations throughout the study period. Multiple hospitalizations and in-care deaths persisted, largely attributable to late presenters with severe immunosuppression. Nearly 30% of PWH enrolled in the later period had CD4 T cell count ≤ 200 cells/µL, emphasizing the ongoing challenge of late detection. Similar findings were observed in a Chinese cohort, comprising 70% late presenters, where AIDS-defining illnesses remained the leading cause of hospitalization.21 These findings emphasize the need for efforts to detect new HIV infection earlier to avoid deleterious outcomes that lead to hospitalization of PWH. Our sensitivity analysis excluding hospitalizations at cohort enrollment showed consistent declines in all-cause, AIDS-defining illnesses, and non-AIDS-defining infections. While IRs were slightly attenuated after exclusion of severely ill patients hospitalized immediately after cohort enrollment—many of whom presented with AIDS-defining conditions—the overall direction remained unchanged, supporting the robustness of our findings.

Compared with previous research, our study showed higher IRs across most diagnostic categories. Notably, 13.4% (108/803) of patients were hospitalized immediately after cohort enrollment, owing to severe illness, resulting in shorter follow-up duration and potential overestimation of IRs. In addition, Korea’s high accessibility to healthcare—where hospital visits occur 2.6 times more frequently than the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development countries’ average—facilitates rapid admission of PWH.22 These factors likely contributed to the elevated hospitalization rates observed in our study relative to other cohorts.

Consistent with our findings, most studies reported a decline in AIDS-defining illnesses over time, although the degree of decline varies. This trend has coincided with substantial immunologic restoration.1,8,9,21 Hospitalizations due to non-AIDS-defining infection also declined in our study, contrasting with some western studies that reported stable or rising rates.8,23,24 Previous randomized controlled studies have demonstrated that ART reduces bacterial infections by directly restoring CD4 T cell counts and activating specific immunologic pathways, regardless of initial CD4 T cell count.25,26 Differences across studies may reflect persistently low CD4 counts in certain populations, variations in categorization of non-AIDS-defining infections, and the higher prevalence of injecting drug users in western cohorts, which increases the risk of sepsis, hepatitis C, and soft tissue infections.8,23,24,27 Unlike prior studies, we did not report an increase in hospitalizations related to chronic end organ diseases but rather demonstrated stable or even declining trends in hospitalization rates for liver/gastrointestinal diseases and other non-communicable diseases.1,4,8,9,28 However, these trends were not observed in the fully adjusted model, suggesting that improved immunologic status partially contributed to reductions in chronic comorbidities requiring inpatient care.

Effective ART may reduce hepatic decompensation in patients with chronic viral hepatitis, although it can contribute to metabolic liver disease.29,30 The SMART (Strategies for Management of Antiretroviral Therapy) trial also demonstrated that CD4 count-guided ART increased the risk of cardiovascular and metabolic complication due to chronic inflammation.31,32 However, despite effective viral suppression, persistent immune activation may still predispose PWH to chronic comorbidities.33,34 Although most studies have documented increasing cardiovascular-related hospitalizations, others noted declines, possibly reflecting reduced use of cardiotoxic ART and improved management of metabolic comorbidities.8,9,23 Some cohorts even reported increases in non-HIV related admissions that offset declines in AIDs-related hospitalization, driven by aging populations and behavioral risks such as substance abuse.4 Differences in population demographics and healthcare systems likely contribute to these heterogeneous findings.

We observed a rise in maternal conditions related to delivery in the late 2010s, aligning with trends reported in previous studies in the United States. This finding likely reflects improved intrapartum care and proactive efforts to prevent perinatal transmission.35,36,37Anal condyloma became increasingly prominent over time in the later years, highlighting the burden of HPV-related diseases among PWH. Anal cancer was the most frequent NADC in this cohort. Nationwide studies in Korea similarly report elevated risks of virus-related cancers among PWH, while overall NADC incidence appears comparable to that of the general population.38,39,40 Nearly 50% of cancer cases in our study presented at an advanced stage, and nationwide data demonstrated lower 5-year survival among Korean PWH.40 These findings suggest the presence of persistent barriers to participation in national cancer screening programs.41 Efforts to reduce hospitalizations among PWH should address barriers to care engagement, including cancer surveillance. Furthermore, HPV vaccination to prevent anal condyloma should be strongly promoted, particularly among high-risk groups, such as men who have sex with men.

From the risk factor analysis, early initiation of ART did not significantly reduce the hospitalization rate. This finding likely reflects treatment protocols in the early 2000s, which prioritized rapid ART initiation for individuals with advanced HIV infection (CD4 T cell count < 200 cells/µL). Consequently, the lack of association may reflect the advanced disease burden of patients eligible for early ART during this period, potentially overshadowing benefits of rapid treatment initiation. Although early ART improves long-term outcomes, its immediate effect on hospitalization risk appears limited among PWH presenting with advanced disease at diagnosis. These findings highlight the importance of early detection of HIV to reduce hospitalization rate and improve the health outcomes of PWH.

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center design involving a relatively small number of patients limits the generalizability of our findings to all PWH in Korea. Second, our dataset did not include behavioral risk factors for chronic diseases and HIV, such as smoking, alcohol use, obesity, sexual orientation, and injection drug use. Therefore, we could not thoroughly assess the behavioral or demographic risk factors for disease burden and hospitalization trends. Consequently, modifiable risk factors for hospitalizations among PWH could not be fully elucidated. Nonetheless, to our knowledge, this study is the first to examine long term trends in hospitalization rates and causes among PWH in Korea over a 20-year period. The sensitivity analyses further supported the robustness of our results.

In conclusion, we observed a sustained decline in hospitalization for all-cause, AIDS-defining illnesses, and non-AIDS-defining infections in fully adjusted models, suggesting an improvement in early diagnosis, healthcare support systems, and prophylaxis for opportunistic infections. Although chronic inflammation persists despite effective ART and has been linked to non-HIV-related comorbidities in prior studies, we did not observe an increase in chronic end-organ diseases. The increased prominence of anal condyloma-related hospitalizations in later period and a high proportion of advanced-stage diseases among NADCs at diagnosis highlight the need for a stigma-free healthcare environment that promotes timely cancer screening and HPV vaccination. Meanwhile, the continued burden of AIDS-defining illnesses highlights the need for early HIV detection. Strengthening access to care and reducing stigma can help reduce both HIV-related and non-HIV-related morbidity among PWH. Future studies should focus on barriers to HIV care engagement and cancer surveillance among PWH to guide long-term strategies for PWH.
