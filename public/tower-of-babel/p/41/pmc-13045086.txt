Anxiety, life-space mobility and quality of life among frail older adults in Enugu, South-East Nigeria
Authors: Ilo Ijeoma Judith, Edeh Ikenna David, Osiri Emmanuel Jonah, Uchenwoke Chigozie Ikenna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty is an increasing public health concern in ageing populations and is frequently accompanied by psychological distress, mobility limitations, and reduced quality of life. Anxiety is common among frail older adults and may influence functional mobility and well-being. However, evidence on the relationships between anxiety, life-space mobility, and quality of life among frail older adults in low- and middle-income countries, particularly Nigeria, is limited.ObjectiveThis study assessed the prevalence of anxiety, life-space mobility, and quality of life, and examined their relationships with selected socio-demographic variables among frail older adults in Enugu, south-east Nigeria.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 119 frail older adults aged ≥ 60 years residing in Uwani community, Enugu State. Frailty was assessed using the FRAIL Scale. Anxiety, life-space mobility, and quality of life were measured using the Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), Life-Space Mobility Questionnaire (LSM), and World Health Organization Quality of Life Questionnaire (WHOQOL-BREF), respectively. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participants’ characteristics. Spearman’s rank correlation examined associations among anxiety, life-space mobility, and quality of life, while Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis H tests assessed differences across gender, marital status, and employment status. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsClinically relevant anxiety symptoms were present in 36.1% of participants. Most participants (95.8%) demonstrated larger life-space mobility, while 4.2% had restricted life-space. Anxiety showed a weak but significant positive correlation with life-space mobility (ρ = 0.286, p = 0.002), but was not significantly associated with quality of life. Life-space mobility was also not significantly related to quality of life. Females, married participants, and those who were employed reported significantly higher quality of life scores.ConclusionFrail older adults exhibited a high prevalence of anxiety despite relatively preserved mobility. Anxiety was associated with mobility patterns but not overall quality of life. Socio-demographic factors remain important determinants of quality of life, highlighting the need for integrated geriatric interventions targeting mental health, mobility, and social support.

Introduction

Worldwide, frailty has become a subject of increasing concern in healthcare due to the growing population of older adults [1]. There are several reasons why people are living longer. Healthcare advancements, better nutrition, sanitation, and medical treatments, as well as greater access to family planning and education, are a few examples [2]. The progress made towards gender equality and public health programmes that promote an active and healthy lifestyle from an early age is also noteworthy [2]. The number of people aged 65 years or older is expected to double over the next three decades, according to the United Nations [2]. This means that by 2050, there will be 1.6 billion of them, making up more than 16% of the world’s population. In 2020, approximately 9.4 million Nigerians were aged 60 and above, representing about 4.5% of the total population. This number is projected to surpass 16 million by 2030, signalling a significant shift in the country’s population structure [3], and indicates that the world’s population is ageing rapidly.

An ageing population, as the term implies, is a population whose age distribution has shifted so that the proportion of older individuals is increasing. While longer life expectancies are positive, an ageing population has drawbacks such as frailty. Healthcare systems will continue to bear a significant and growing burden due to frailty, a multifaceted geriatric syndrome linked to poor health outcomes. It reflects a decline in physiological reserve and function, which is more common as people age, and is typified by heightened susceptibility to physiological stresses [4]. Furthermore, clinicians have used frailty to describe the physiological reserve of older patients [1].

According to Rohrmann and Sieber [5–7], up to 59% of persons aged 65 and older may be affected by frailty. One in five men and one in three women in the EU who are 65 years of age or older require long-term care [2]. Multiple physiological systems are affected by frailty, which leads to aberrant energy metabolism, immunological dysfunction, endocrine dysregulation, neuromuscular dysfunction, and degeneration of the central nervous system. It is of concern in the elderly population because it is linked to unfavourable health outcomes such as anxiety, falls, hospitalisations, impairment, and mortality [8].

As people age, their health both physical and mental circumstances change. Context-specific factors such as cultural attitudes, inadequate health infrastructure, and lax policy enforcement exacerbate mental health issues among older persons in Nigeria [9, 10]. According to recent estimates, about 25% of Nigerians suffer from a mental illness each year, yet fewer than 10% of them receive appropriate care [11]. The situation is particularly concerning among older adults. A community-based survey conducted in Ogun State reported that 35.8% of people aged 60 years and older had a diagnosable mental illness,the most prevalent conditions were substance use disorders, anxiety, and depression [12].

Anxiety is one of the many psychological issues that older persons commonly experience, in addition to major shifts in their social roles. Research indicates that the prevalence of anxiety symptoms among older persons in Nigeria ranges from 15% to over 27% for anxiety disorders, and is even higher for general anxiety symptoms, which frequently co-occur with depression [13]. Significant attention should be paid to the potential harm that anxiety poses to the physical and mental well-being of older adults. Studies have shown that 65% of older persons experience anxiety symptoms, and over 25% have anxiety levels comparable to those observed in people with generalized anxiety disorder [14].

Anxiety describes feelings of unease or tension that can be debilitating and interfere with day-to-day functioning [15]. It can also lead to mood disturbances. One of the most common symptoms among the elderly is anxiety [16]. Compared to depression and cognitive impairments, sub-syndromal anxiety is more prevalent [17]. With an estimated point prevalence of 17% for any anxiety disorder [17], anxiety is a frequent mental health issue in later life [18, 19]. According to Subramanyam et al. [17], the most common anxiety disorders among the oldest-old population are specific phobias (10%), generalized anxiety disorder (4%), and social phobia (4%).

Later-life anxiety has numerous negative consequences, including reduced quality of life, difficulty with everyday tasks, increased risk of dementia [15], and higher healthcare costs [20, 21], report that anxiety is strongly associated with earlier onset of frailty and poorer health outcomes. Given its significant prevalence among older persons, anxiety has been described as the “silent geriatric giant” [22].

Anxiety has been linked to frailty in elderly surgical patients [23]. Research also suggests that the impact of anxiety on frailty may be reversible and preventable, indicating that anxiety may represent a modifiable risk factor for frailty [24]. According to Clarke and Currie [25], older persons experiencing anxiety are more likely to report and encounter health problems such as falls, pain, and chronic illnesses. These issues can further exacerbate frailty and impair the mobility of older adults.

Mobility can be broadly defined as the ability to move between locations using a mode of transportation or mobility aids [26, 27].

As people age, their mobility and physical function may change. Traditional assessments of mobility and physical function focus on self-reported physical performance, but these measures often fail to capture older persons’ usual daily movements. Consequently, life-space mobility (LSM) assessments were developed to evaluate the everyday, enacted mobility of older adults [28].

Lower life-space scores are associated with adverse outcomes in older adults, including frailty, nursing home admission, increased subsequent healthcare utilisation, and mortality [29].

It is important to differentiate between mobility performance (real-life mobility) and mobility capability (what a person can achieve under laboratory or controlled testing). Evidence indicates that mobility capability does not accurately reflect actual mobility [30]. Real-life mobility remains strongly influenced by an individual’s level of physical activity. Life-space mobility captures additional important aspects of a person’s movement in daily life [31].

The physical and social environments traversed by individuals in daily life are referred to as life-space mobility. This can be categorised as in-home or out-of-home mobility, depending on the environment in which an individual moves (e.g., home, neighbourhood, city, or beyond). Consequently, life-space mobility, as defined by Webber et al. [27], encompasses any movement supported by mobility aids and modes of transportation, as well as an individual’s independent mobility, which requires mobility-related physical activity, such as walking.

All aspects of daily life are influenced by physical mobility, which is a crucial component of independent living and significantly affects the quality of life of older adults. Maintaining mobility becomes increasingly important with age, impacting both health and overall life satisfaction [32]. According to Taylor et al. [33], mobility is essential for older adults to age well, as it helps preserve their independence and autonomy. Previous studies have shown that mobility is influenced by environmental factors, transportation, psychological variables, and cognitive and physical function [32]. These findings have highlighted the need to evaluate mobility in older adults beyond conventional assessments of walking ability, activities of daily living (ADLs), and instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs), which focus on specific activities rather than capturing the full spectrum of mobility in older adults [27, 32]. Life-space mobility (LSM) enables the assessment of social, environmental, and functional factors that shape individuals’ daily lives [34].

Reduced physical activity, a higher prevalence of depressive symptoms, cognitive decline, poor physical capacity, obesity, and an increased risk of frailty are anticipated consequences of restricted life-space mobility among older adults in low- and middle-income countries, as noted in previous research [35, 36]. In particular, inactivity related to deconditioning [37, 38] increases the risk of health deterioration associated with chronic non-communicable diseases [38, 39] and may accelerate the accumulation of body fat and loss of muscle mass and strength. According to Kirwan et al. [40], inactivity ultimately results in poorer general health. Furthermore, social inequality may amplify the detrimental effects of restricted mobility on life-space, particularly for older individuals living in low-resource settings [41].

Life-space mobility is vital for social participation, particularly in older age [42]. Research also indicates that life-space mobility is strongly associated with quality of life [43], physical functioning [44], mental function [45], and overall health status. According to Portegijs et al. [46], limited life-space mobility is considered an early warning sign of physical disability and may indicate an increased risk of mortality. An individual’s life space is dynamic and may change due to both acute conditions (such as following a stroke or surgery) and chronic disorders (such as dementia or osteoarthritis) [47]. Smaller life-space is linked to lower quality of life,however, it remains unclear how these changes impact quality of life among frail older adults [48].

Despite the important understanding of the negative psychosocial sequelae of frailty among older adults, there is a scarcity of studies on life-space mobility among frail older adults in low- and middle-income countries, particularly Nigeria. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of anxiety, life-space mobility profiles, and quality of life, as well as to examine their relationships with each other and with selected demographic variables among older adults in Enugu, southeast Nigeria. It is hoped that the findings of this study will provide evidence to inform health theory, policy, and practice regarding the variables investigated in the care of older adults.

Methods

Design

This study adopted a cross-sectional quantitative design to explore the relationships among anxiety, life-space mobility, and quality of life in older adults.

Setting

The study was conducted across two communities in Uwani, Enugu South Local Government Area of Enugu State, Nigeria. Enugu, the capital city of Enugu State, is located in the southeastern geopolitical zone of Nigeria. Uwani serves as the headquarters of Enugu South Local Government Area, which constitutes the lower tier of government in the state. It falls within the Eastern Senatorial Districts of Enugu, covering an area of 67 km2 and a population of 198,723 at the 2006 census, which is projected to be 267,300 in 2016. Thus, Uwani was selected because it adequately represents the population of this study, being the largest town in Enugu South LGA and likely to have a greater proportion of older adults.

Participants

The participants for this study were frail older adults residing in Uwani community, Enugu South Local Government Area, Enugu State, South-East Nigeria. The target population comprised men and women aged 60 years and above living within the study area at the time of data collection. Older adults aged 60 years and above are commonly used as the threshold for defining older age in low- and middle-income countries, including Nigeria [49].

As recommended for descriptive and analytical cross-sectional studies [50, 51]. A 95% confidence level was applied, with an absolute precision (d) of between 4 and 5%, and an estimated proportion of older adults of approximately 5%, consistent with demographic estimates in Nigeria [52]. Based on these parameters, a minimum sample size ranging from 73 to 114 participants was projected. To improve statistical power and account for potential non-response, a total of 119 frail older adults were recruited and included in the study.

Participants were recruited using a community-based approach with the assistance of community leaders and primary health workers, a method shown to be effective for recruiting older adults in community settings in sub-Saharan Africa [53]. Eligible participants were identified through community listings and household visits within the Uwani neighbourhood. All eligible individuals were informed about the objectives and procedures of the study prior to participation.

Inclusion criteria were frail older adults aged 60 years and above who were residents of Uwani community and were able to communicate effectively to respond to the study instruments. Frailty was considered appropriate for this population given its established association with anxiety, restricted life-space mobility, and reduced quality of life among older adults [34, 54]. Participants were excluded if they had a history of diagnosed psychiatric disorders or significant mobility disorders before the age of 60, as such conditions could independently influence anxiety levels, mobility patterns, and perceived quality of life [55, 56].

Prior to data collection, the study procedures were thoroughly explained to all potential participants. Informed consent was obtained either verbally or in writing, depending on the participant’s level of literacy, in line with ethical recommendations for research involving older adults in low-literacy settings [57]. Participants were assured of the confidentiality and anonymity of their responses and informed that participation was voluntary, with the freedom to withdraw at any stage without consequences.

Instruments for data collection

Frailty was assessed using the FRAIL Scale, a simple and validated screening tool designed to identify frailty among older adults in both community and clinical settings [58]. The FRAIL Scale comprises five self-reported components, represented by the acronym FRAIL: Fatigue, Resistance, Ambulation, Illnesses, and Loss of weight.

Fatigue assesses whether the individual experiences frequent tiredness or exhaustion. Resistance evaluates difficulty in climbing a flight of stairs, reflecting reduced muscle strength. Ambulation assesses difficulty in walking a specified distance, indicating impaired mobility. Illnesses refer to the presence of five or more chronic medical conditions, representing cumulative health burden. Loss of weight evaluates unintentional weight loss over a defined period, reflecting nutritional and metabolic decline.

Each component is scored dichotomously as 0 (absence of the condition) or 1 (presence of the condition), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 5. Based on the total score, participants are classified as robust (0 points), pre-frail (1–2 points), or frail (3–5 points) [58]. In the present study, only participants classified as frail were included.

The FRAIL Scale has demonstrated good validity and reliability across diverse populations and settings. It correlates well with other established frailty models, including the Fried frailty phenotype and the Frailty Index, while remaining practical for use in large community-based studies and low-resource settings [58, 59]. The scale has also been shown to predict adverse outcomes such as functional decline, hospitalisation, disability, and mortality among older adults [58, 60]. Due to its brevity, ease of administration, and suitability for populations with varying levels of literacy, the FRAIL Scale was considered appropriate for identifying frailty among older adults in this community-based study.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item (GAD-7) was used to measure anxiety [61]. The GAD-7 comprises seven items, each asking the participant to rate the severity of symptoms experienced over the past two weeks. Response options include: not at all, several days, more than half the days, and nearly every day. The GAD-7 has been validated in the general population, primary care patients, and adolescents with GAD, and has been shown to reliably assess anxiety symptoms and differentiate mild and moderate GAD [62].

To determine the GAD-7 score, the response categories not at all, several days, more than half the days, and nearly every day are assigned scores of 0, 1, 2, and 3, respectively. These scores are then summed across all seven items. The total GAD-7 score ranges from 0 to 21. Cut-off points for mild, moderate, and severe anxiety are 5, 10, and 15, respectively. A score of 10 or higher, when used as a screening tool, indicates the need for further assessment. Normative data for the GAD-7 have been produced for boys and girls across various age ranges [63]. Excellent internal consistency and acceptable test–retest reliability have been reported for the GAD-7 [61].

Life-Space Mobility Questionnaire (LSM) was used to measure life-space mobility [32]. The LSM assesses mobility across five domains: outside the bedroom, outside the house, within the neighbourhood, outside the neighbourhood but within the town, and beyond the town over the preceding four weeks. A sub-score is assigned to each life-space level based on independence and average weekly frequency. Higher scores indicate greater mobility. The composite score ranges from 0 to 120. The LSM was validated using a random sample of 306 Medicare recipients aged 65 and above from central Alabama, with a 2-week test–retest reliability of 0.96 (95% CI = 0.95–0.97). Following validation and translation into multiple languages, the LSM has become widely used both domestically and internationally [64–69]. Nearly all research conducted in the last decade has measured older adults’ mobility using the LSM instrument [32].

Quality of life was assessed using the World Health Organization Quality of Life Questionnaire (WHOQOL-BREF) [70]. The WHOQOL-BREF is a condensed form of the WHOQOL-100, both developed by the World Health Organization (WHO) and released in 1995. The questionnaire was developed over several years across fifteen global centres [70]. Items were derived from statements regarding health, well-being, and quality of life provided by health experts and individuals with and without illnesses [71]. The self-administered WHOQOL-BREF consists of 26 items assessing an individual’s perception of their health and well-being over the preceding two weeks. Responses are recorded on a Likert scale of 1 to 5, where 1 denotes disagree or not at all and 5 denotes completely agree or extremely. The four domains included are environment, social relationships, psychological health, and physical health. The WHOQOL-BREF has been extensively tested across diverse disease populations, ethnic groups, and linguistic backgrounds. It has demonstrated good test–retest reliability, internal consistency, discriminant validity, and content validity, and has been described as “a sound, cross-culturally valid assessment of quality of life” [70, 71].

Socio-demographic information, including marital status, gender, and employment status, was obtained through oral interview.

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human participants. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Nigeria Health Research Ethics Committee (Approval Number: NHREC/05/01/2008B-FWA00002458-1RB00002323) prior to the commencement of data collection.

Participant recruitment was carried out using a community-based outreach approach within Uwani community, Enugu South Local Government Area. Three trained research assistants were deployed across the selected neighbourhoods to facilitate recruitment and data collection. Recruitment strategies included door-to-door canvassing, participation in community meetings, and visits to churches and primary healthcare centres to disseminate information about the study and invite eligible older adults to participate.

Older adults encountered during these outreach activities were approached directly and provided with detailed information regarding the study objectives, procedures, potential benefits, and minimal risks involved. Contact information was obtained from individuals who expressed interest in participating, and follow-up reminders were conducted via telephone calls to encourage attendance at scheduled screening and data collection sessions.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. For participants requiring assistance due to literacy limitations or physical challenges, trained research assistants provided support during questionnaire administration, ensuring that responses reflected the participants’ own views. Participants were informed that their participation was entirely voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any time, for any reason, without any consequences for their medical care, legal rights, or access to services.

To ensure uniformity and minimise respondent burden, data collection for each participant was completed in a single session. Immediately following completion of the questionnaires, research assistants reviewed all responses to ensure accuracy and completeness. Any missing or unclear responses were addressed on-site through clarification with the participant. This process ensured high data quality, resulting in a final dataset with no missing values.

All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 23 for Windows (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarise participants’ socio-demographic characteristics.

Given the nature of the data and the distribution of study variables, non-parametric statistical tests were employed. Spearman’s rank-order correlation was used to examine the relationships between anxiety and life-space mobility, anxiety and quality of life, and life-space mobility and quality of life. This method was considered appropriate for assessing associations between continuous or ordinal variables that did not meet parametric assumptions.

The Mann–Whitney U test was used to assess differences in anxiety, life-space mobility, and quality of life based on participants’ gender. The Kruskal–Wallis H test was applied to examine differences in anxiety, life-space mobility, and quality of life across categories of marital status and employment status. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and the level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study explored the prevalence of anxiety, life-space mobility levels, and quality of life, as well as their interconnections and relationships with gender, marital status, and employment status among frail older adults.

Findings of this study revealed a high prevalence of anxiety (36.1%) among frail older adults. This prevalence is notably higher than that reported in previous studies conducted among apparently healthy older adults. Globally, anxiety disorders are among the most prevalent mental health conditions, affecting approximately 4% of the general population [72, 73] and ranking as the sixth leading cause of disability worldwide [74]. Among older adults, earlier studies have estimated the prevalence of anxiety to be about 25%, indicating that nearly one in four older individuals may experience anxiety [75]. The higher prevalence observed in the present study emphasises the substantial burden of anxiety among frail older adults and highlights the need for focused mental health support in this vulnerable group.

Evidence from Nigeria and sub-Saharan Africa similarly indicates that anxiety is a significant mental health concern in later life. A community-based study of retirees in North-central Nigeria reported anxiety symptoms in 27.7% of older adults, with 20.5% experiencing anxious depression, suggesting a considerable burden of anxiety in this population [76]. Likewise, a community survey conducted in south-western Nigeria found that 19.9% of older adults had anxiety-only symptoms, while 6.0% experienced comorbid anxiety and depression [77]. Although large-scale epidemiological studies across sub-Saharan Africa remain limited, meta-analytic evidence suggests wide variability in anxiety prevalence among older adults in low- and middle-income countries, with some studies reporting the prevalence of generalized anxiety disorder as high as 32.2%, including African settings [78]. These findings underscore that anxiety disorders are not confined to high-income countries but affect a substantial proportion of older adults in Nigeria and the broader sub-Saharan African region, often co-occurring with depression and other psychosocial stressors.

Globally, recent meta-analytic evidence continues to demonstrate that anxiety remains a common mental health problem in older adults. A systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the global prevalence of anxiety symptoms among older adults to be approximately 28% [75], while another meta-analysis of 47 studies reported an overall prevalence of 16.5% (95% CI: 11.1–22.8%), with the highest rates observed in Africa [79]. Population-based data from China also revealed that 8.0% of adults aged 60 years and above reported anxiety symptoms in a recent national survey [80]. These findings reinforce the global relevance of anxiety in older adults and emphasise the need for targeted mental health interventions for ageing populations.

Furthermore, evidence suggests that anxiety prevalence varies according to frailty status. A review by Tan et al. [81] demonstrated that prefrail older adults are more likely to experience anxiety than robust individuals, while frail older adults exhibit even higher levels of anxiety symptoms compared to prefrail groups. Indicators of frailty, such as exhaustion and reduced mobility, may significantly influence mood and psychological well-being, thereby contributing to the elevated prevalence of anxiety observed among frail older adults [81].

The 4.2% prevalence of restricted life-space mobility observed in this study is substantially lower than estimates reported in other low- and middle-income settings. For instance, a nationally representative study of older adults in Ghana, using data from the World Health Organization’s Study on Global AGEing and Adult Health (SAGE), reported that 40.3% of participants experienced mobility difficulties [82]. Similarly, a community-based study among older adults in rural Nigeria found that 22.5% had functional limitations, indicating a considerably higher burden of mobility-related problems than observed in the present study [83]. These disparities may be partly explained by differences in mobility constructs and measurement approaches, including the assessment of self-reported functional difficulty versus life-space mobility, variations in survey instruments, and contextual environmental factors.

Previous research has shown that the earliest signs of mobility decline typically manifest in more demanding tasks, such as walking long distances or running, with perceived running difficulties already common by midlife [84]. During the early stages of functional decline, older adults may compensate for underlying impairments by modifying how tasks are performed, such as altering speed, frequency, or method, thereby maintaining function without perceiving overt difficulty. This transitional stage, characterised by changes in task performance or increased fatigue prior to observable task difficulty, has been described as preclinical disability [85, 86]. Overall, these findings highlight that mobility declines progressively with age, affecting the most complex and demanding activities first. Older adults may adapt by reducing exposure to challenging environments or modifying activity patterns, which can mask emerging disability [86]. This underscores the importance of interventions aimed at enhancing environmental mobility and supporting active engagement in broader life-spaces among older adults.

Findings of this study revealed a higher level of quality of life compared with the outcomes of previous studies. Generally, the process of ageing and frailty causes a decline in self-efficacy, quality of life (QoL), and the ability to sustain healthy exercise levels in older persons [87, 88]. QoL of community-dwelling older persons refers to the subjective evaluation of their general function. According to Topinková [89], the state of impairment, reliance on family members, and social burden of older persons are all impacted by the changes brought about by age, illness, and frailty. However, research has indicated that quality of life is considered better in those aged 50 to 68 years, and it tends to drastically deteriorate beyond the age of 86 [90]. Additionally, independent living, functionality, and physical health are more crucial to an older person’s quality of life than they are to a younger one [90]. This may primarily be caused by factors such as cognitive impairments, which have a detrimental impact on bodily function and, thus, quality of life.

Anxiety was significantly associated with life-space mobility in the present study. According to previous research, older persons who experience high levels of anxiety have lower functioning [91]. Feldman and colleagues [92] found that older adults who experience anxiety are more likely than those without anxiety to experience balance impairments and mobility difficulties. The fear of having anxiety episodes when operating a vehicle, riding a bicycle, walking, taking public transport, or driving might significantly limit the mobility of people with anxiety disorders [93]. As evidenced by other studies, these issues may have significant negative effects on day-to-day functioning, including the inability to visit friends and family, apply for jobs, or attend medical appointments. Long-term effects may also include social isolation and unemployment [94, 95]. Therefore, it is crucial to comprehend how anxiety disorders and (anticipated) anxiety episodes may influence mobility options and behaviour. This understanding will benefit healthcare institutions, affected individuals, and the goal of creating an inclusive mobility system.

The outcome of this study showed no significant relationship between anxiety and quality of life among frail older adults. However, a previous study demonstrated a moderate-to-strong correlation between anxiety and quality of life. Dangerfield et al. [96] reported an indirect relationship between anxiety and each of: current health-related quality of life, perceived change in health-related quality of life, and medication-related quality of life. The disparity in outcomes can be attributed to the use of different outcome measures in the assessment of quality of life. Whereas the present study assessed quality of life using the WHOQOL-BREF questionnaire as a measure of health-related quality of life, Dangerfield et al. [96] measured quality of life using two global questions from the RAND-36 and the Medication-Related Quality of Life Scale. Furthermore, over time, anxiety significantly influences how individuals’ quality of life develops [97].

Furthermore, findings of this study demonstrate a significant relationship between gender and quality of life among frail older adults. This is in line with the outcomes of previous studies which have observed a significant association between gender and quality of life among the general older population. According to Lee et al. [98], prior exploratory research revealed that the factors influencing QoL varied depending on gender. The observed gender differences in QoL may reflect longstanding sociocultural norms in which men are expected to engage in socioeconomic activities while women assume responsibilities for family cohesion and caregiving. These roles shape how older adults perceive social support, purpose, and well-being, influencing their overall quality of life.

The participants’ quality of life was also associated with their marital status. Empirical studies have shown that marital status significantly influences quality of life in older adults, with married individuals often reporting higher psychological well-being and stronger social relationships compared to those who are widowed, divorced, or single [99, 100]. This likely reflects the emotional support and companionship provided by spousal relationships. In the current study, married participants exhibited higher average quality of life scores than those in other marital status categories. However, Zueras et al. [101] found that family relationships, such as marital status or living arrangements, were not strongly associated with higher quality of life among older men. This suggests that social interactions may be more important than family ties for male older adults. Historically, men have been more involved in financial and socioeconomic pursuits, while women often take on the role of family “kin-keepers,” making family ties potentially more significant for women [101]. The higher quality of life observed among married participants in this study may also reflect the cultural importance of spousal support and family cohesion. In societies where intergenerational support and strong family structures are highly valued, married older adults may have greater access to emotional and practical resources, enhancing psychological well-being.

Additionally, findings of this study demonstrate a significant association between employment status and quality of life among the participants. Frail older adults who were employed reported better quality of life. Employment in older adulthood has many advantages beyond increased productivity and financial gain [102, 103]. Growth curve models evaluating the impact of productive activities on the trajectories of mental health in over 8,000 mid- to older-age individuals identified protective benefits [104]. In particular, full-time work and light volunteerism alone had a protective impact against a reduction in psychological well-being, and combined participation had an even stronger effect [104]. According to Maimaris et al. [105], research on employment and well-being in older adults indicates that working past the conventional retirement age may have positive effects on mental health. Diverse findings in the literature, however, highlight the need for a cautious approach in understanding how older adults’ employment statuses relate to their well-being and overall quality of life. The positive association between employment and QoL can be understood in light of sociocultural values that associate continued work with productivity, social recognition, and autonomy. In societies where older adults are respected for their contributions, employment may serve as a key mechanism for maintaining psychological well-being and life satisfaction.

Conclusion

Frail older adults in Enugu exhibited a high prevalence of anxiety despite relatively preserved mobility, with anxiety significantly associated with life-space mobility restrictions. Women, married, and employed individuals reported better quality of life, highlighting the need for targeted support for widowed, unemployed, and male older adults. Future research should investigate the role of healthcare access in moderating these relationships.

Limitations

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. Furthermore, frailty was assessed using the FRAIL Scale, which may limit comparability with studies employing Fried’s phenotype. Finally, the GAD-7 and LSM instruments were not cross-culturally adapted for Nigerian older adults, and such validation may be necessary to confirm their cultural appropriateness.

Policy and practice implications

The findings of this study have important implications for policy and practice in geriatric care. The high prevalence of anxiety observed among frail older adults underscores the need to integrate routine mental health screening into primary and geriatric healthcare services. Policies supporting the early identification and management of anxiety, particularly among socially vulnerable and unmarried older adults, may help prevent further functional decline and deterioration in quality of life. Training healthcare providers in geriatric mental health and promoting multidisciplinary care models that include mental health professionals are therefore essential.

The observed associations between quality of life, marital status, and mobility highlight the importance of community mobility programmes tailored to the needs of frail older adults. Community-based interventions that promote safe walking environments, accessible transportation, and participation in social and physical activities may enhance life-space mobility and reduce anxiety. Such programmes should be designed to encourage social interaction, particularly for older adults who lack spousal or family support.

Furthermore, the findings support the development of social support interventions that strengthen social networks beyond the immediate family. Policies and programmes that foster peer support groups, community engagement initiatives, and age-friendly social spaces may be especially beneficial for older adults who rely more on social relationships than family ties for emotional well-being. Strengthening social connectedness at the community level may therefore play a critical role in improving mental health and overall quality of life among frail older adults.
