A large-scale Wi-Fi channel state information dataset for contactless human speech recognition
Authors: Rami Alazrai, Hashem F. Qaryouti, Mahmoud Al-Sarayreh, Sahar Qaadan, Baha A. Alsaify
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wi-Fi sensing has emerged as a promising contactless technology for understanding human activities and interactions by analyzing variations in wireless signals. In this vein, Wi-Fi-based spoken-word recognition from speech-associated movements offers a non-intrusive alternative for capturing speech-related information without microphones, supporting applications in privacy-preserving human–computer interaction, assistive technologies, and smart indoor environments.It is important to note that the Wi-Fi signals do not directly capture acoustic speech waves. Instead, the Channel State Information (CSI) and Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI) measurements reflect wireless-channel perturbations caused by speech-associated physical movements, including articulatory, facial, jaw, lip, and small head movements that occur during word pronunciation.This paper presents a dataset developed for Wi-Fi-based human speech recognition. The dataset consists of recordings of 30 distinct spoken English utterances, including single words and short phrases, collected from 25 subjects in an indoor environment. Each subject performed 30 trials for each utterance, resulting in a total of 22,500 trials across all participants, calculated as 25 subjects × 30 utterances × 30 trials.The data collection process used the publicly available CSI tool to capture Wi-Fi signals transmitted from a commercial off-the-shelf access point, the Sagemcom 2704, to a desktop computer equipped with an Intel 5300 network interface card. The recorded wireless measurements include both RSSI values and CSI values, providing complementary signal representations for speech-related analysis.The presented dataset provides a valuable resource for advancing research in Wi-Fi-based human speech recognition. It can support the development, evaluation, and comparison of machine learning and signal processing approaches for recognizing spoken English utterances from Wi-Fi signal variations. By providing a large and structured collection of trials from multiple subjects, the dataset enables further investigation into contactless speech sensing and contributes to the growing field of Wi-Fi-based human sensing.

Background

Recent advances in CSI-based sensing have demonstrated significant potential for contactless human activity recognition (HAR) [2,3,4] and environmental monitoring [5]. Unlike conventional sensors, CSI leverages Wi-Fi signal distortions caused by human movement to detect activities with high accuracy [6]. While CSI signals have been extensively explored for body motion analysis, their application in speech recognition remains largely unexplored [7].

This gap presents a significant opportunity: spoken words and phrases (i.e., utterances) produce subtle yet consistent perturbations in wireless signals, which can be captured and classified using CSI. Traditional microphone-based speech recognition faces challenges in noisy environments and raises privacy concerns. A CSI-based system offers a privacy-preserving, device-free alternative that operates without dedicated audio sensors [8].

Previous foundational efforts, such as WiHear [9], have demonstrated the potential of Wi-Fi CSI for recognizing syllables and words by capturing micro-movements of human lips. However, these studies often utilized limited subject pools and were conducted in highly controlled settings. More recently, Lip-Siri [10] introduced a silent speech recognition framework using frequency-shifted backscatter; however, it requires specialized hardware tags, limiting its application in ubiquitous device-free scenarios.

A significant barrier to progress in this field remains the lack of publicly accessible, large-scale raw datasets for benchmarking [11]. This work addresses this gap by providing a comprehensive, device-free dataset consisting of 22,500 trials from 25 subjects, representing the first large-scale public resource of its kind for Wi-Fi-based speech recognition. Specifically, we present a novel CSI dataset capturing 30 distinct spoken English utterances, including single words and short phrases, investigating how vocal articulations modulate Wi-Fi signal propagation. The sensing mechanism in this dataset is based on the interaction between Wi-Fi signal propagation and subtle physical movements associated with speech production. While the task is framed as spoken-word recognition, the measured CSI/RSSI variations are not acoustic recordings. Rather, they reflect changes in the wireless channel caused by articulatory and orofacial movements, such as lip, jaw, facial, and small head motions, that accompany the pronunciation of each word or phrase. This dataset enables comprehensive analysis of speech-induced CSI variations, establishing a benchmark for future CSI-based word-level speech recognition and paving the way for privacy-aware, microphone-free voice interaction systems in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) applications.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Subjects

A total of 25 healthy volunteers (9 females, 16 males) with a mean age of 21.96 ± 0.6 years participated in the experiments. Among the 25 participants, two participants self-reported as native English speakers, while the remaining participants were non-native English speakers who were able to pronounce the selected English utterances used in the experiment. Before the study, all participants received a detailed explanation of the experimental protocols and provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received formal approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the German Jordanian University. Experimental procedure

Each subject was instructed to perform thirty distinct trials for each of the 30 spoken English utterances, including single words and short phrases, listed in Table 1. Each trial was structured into two primary intervals: a steady-state interval and an utterance interval. During the steady-state interval, the subject remained silent, maintaining a consistent posture. Subsequently, during the utterance interval, the subject articulated one of the designated English utterances.

To ensure consistent and accurate data acquisition across all utterance recordings, a standardized timing diagram was implemented for every trial. This diagram, depicted in Fig. 4, precisely outlines the sequence of events and interval transitions. A system of pre-programmed auditory cues (beep sounds) was developed to guide subjects through the experimental procedure. Beep 1 marked the start of the trial and the beginning of the fixed 2-second silent interval. During this interval, the subject remained silent and maintained a consistent posture. Beep 2 marked the end of the silent interval and the start of the speech/utterance interval. The subject then pronounced the target spoken utterance, and the end of speech was marked at the completion of the utterance. The duration of the speech/utterance interval was not fixed; rather, it varied depending on the spoken utterance and the participant’s articulation rate. This variability is reflected in the number of packets captured during the speech interval, as summarized in the packet-count analysis shown in Fig. 2. The corresponding timestamps were stored in the Events field of each MATLAB file, and their textual descriptions were stored in the EventsDescription field. Prior to data collection, all subjects received a comprehensive explanation of this timing diagram and were instructed to adhere strictly to its protocol throughout the recording sessions.Fig. 4Standardized timing diagram for each experimental trial. Software and equipment

The experimental setup utilized the publicly available CSI tool [1] for recording Wi-Fi signals. This involved a commercial off-the-shelf Sagemcom 2704 access point (AP) transmitting signals to a desktop computer equipped with an Intel 5300 Network Interface Card (NIC). The AP was configured to operate at a 2.4 GHz frequency band, utilizing wireless channel 6 with a 20 MHz channel bandwidth and a Modulation Coding Scheme (MCS) index of 14. Both the AP and the NIC adhere to the IEEE 802.11n standard, which supports Multiple Input Multiple Output (MIMO) technology with Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing (OFDM) for high-throughput wireless communication [12,13].

The effective CSI packet sampling rate was computed from the packet timestamps recorded in each trial. Specifically, for each trial, the sampling rate was estimated as the number of captured CSI packets divided by the trial duration, using the timestamp information stored in the MATLAB data files. Across the dataset, the average effective CSI packet sampling rate was approximately 460 packets/s.

The Sagemcom 2704 AP incorporates two internal transmit antennas (Ntx=2), while the Intel 5300 NIC features three external receive antennas (Nrx=3). This configuration establishes a 2 × 3 MIMO system, resulting in six unique Wi-Fi streams, each formed between a distinct transmit-receive antenna pair. For each OFDM-modulated MIMO stream, the CSI tool can capture CSI across 30 subcarriers (Nsc=30), which are uniformly distributed over the 20 MHz channel bandwidth. Consequently, the entire MIMO system captures CSI data across a total of 6 × 30 = 180 subcarriers. Data quality control and preprocessing

The dataset was released as a raw CSI/RSSI dataset to allow researchers to apply preprocessing and feature-extraction methods suited to their specific models and research objectives. Therefore, no advanced post-processing methods, such as denoising filters, smoothing filters, phase correction, or automatic outlier-removal algorithms, were applied to the released CSI/RSSI measurements.

Nevertheless, basic acquisition-level quality-control checks were performed to ensure data integrity. During recording, packets with missing or null CSI values, incomplete RSSI/CSI-related fields, unexpected CSI dimensions, or non-finite numerical values were discarded. In addition, packets showing unstable acquisition behavior, including abnormal or inconsistent AGC/noise-related values, were excluded when detected. These checks ensured that the retained packets contained complete and structurally valid CSI/RSSI information.

Following data collection, trial-level quality checking was performed. Each trial was inspected to verify that the recorded signal contained the expected silent interval and speech/utterance interval according to the stored event markers. Trials with corrupted recordings, missing event information, abnormal noise behavior, or no distinguishable speech-associated signal perturbation were re-recorded or excluded from the final dataset.

Exploratory signal-diversity analysis

To further characterize the diversity of the recorded CSI signals, we conducted an exploratory analysis using a randomly selected subset of the dataset. Eight subjects were randomly selected from the 25 participants, while maintaining a balanced gender distribution of four male and four female subjects. Five utterance recordings were also randomly selected from the vocabulary, and one representative trial was analyzed for each selected subject–utterance pair. This resulted in 40 recordings for the exploratory analysis.

Before feature extraction, the packets associated with the silent interval of each trial were excluded to reduce the influence of the initial steady-state interval and acquisition transients. The remaining CSI amplitude samples were then extracted from the 30 subcarriers, three receive antennas, and two transmit antennas, resulting in 180 CSI streams per recording.

Three levels of signal diversity were evaluated. First, utterance-level diversity was assessed by comparing the selected spoken utterances while blocking by subject. Features used for this analysis included amplitude kurtosis and spatial antenna-link features such as Rx3–Tx1 energy, Tx1/Tx2 ratio, Tx1 mean amplitude, and Tx2 mean amplitude. These features were selected because different spoken utterances can induce different articulatory movements, which may affect both the amplitude distribution and the spatial multipath structure of CSI signals. The strongest utterance-level result was obtained for Rx3–Tx1 energy, with Friedman p = 0.0073. Combined utterance-fusion Principal Component Analysis (PCA) scores also showed word-related variation, with p = 0.0255 for PC3 and p = 0.0302 for PC1.

Second, participant-level diversity was assessed by comparing the selected subjects while blocking by utterance. The tested features included median amplitude, amplitude coefficient of variation, RMS amplitude, mean-square amplitude, energy per packet, envelope median absolute deviation, Rx1/Rx2 ratio, Rx1–Tx1 mean amplitude, Rx1 mean amplitude, and Rx2 mean amplitude. These features were selected to capture individual differences in signal strength, variability, energy, and antenna-link spatial response. The participant-level results showed strong variability across subjects. For example, Rx1/Rx2 ratio produced Friedman p = 0.000066, while Rx1–Tx1 mean amplitude produced p = 0.000092. The participant-fusion PCA scores also showed strong subject-related variation, with participant-fusion PC2 producing p = 0.

Third, gender-related variation was examined as an exploratory participant-trait analysis. The gender-sensitive feature set included link covariance eigen-entropy, envelope standard deviation, envelope coefficient of variation, envelope interquartile range, envelope RMS difference, envelope spectral entropy, Rx1–Tx2 energy, and Tx2 mean amplitude. These features were selected because gender-related or body-geometry-related differences may be reflected in the spatial covariance structure and temporal-envelope behavior of CSI signals. Using subject-level mean features, the gender-fusion feature set showed an exploratory separation between male and female participants, with an exact centroid-distance permutation p-value of 0.0286 and a Mann–Whitney U p-value of 0.0286 for the first gender-fusion PCA score. However, because this analysis was performed on a randomly selected subset of eight subjects, the gender-related result is interpreted cautiously and should be considered exploratory rather than conclusive.

Overall, this exploratory analysis provides additional evidence that the dataset contains diverse CSI signal patterns across spoken utterances and participants. The strongest effects were observed at the participant level, while word-level and gender-related analyses provided complementary evidence of speech-content and participant-trait variability.

Baseline classification analysis using standard evaluation workflow

To provide an initial benchmark for using the dataset in Wi-Fi-based spoken-utterance recognition, we conducted baseline experiments using a Random Forest classifier. The baseline was designed as a subject-specific closed-set classification task, where a separate classifier was trained and evaluated for each subject.

For each subject, the .mat files corresponding to the 30 spoken utterances and 30 trials were loaded. The speech/utterance segment was extracted using the event timestamps, while the silent interval was excluded from the classification task. Each extracted speech segment was divided into overlapping windows of 128 samples with an overlap of 64 samples. If the final window exceeded the end of the segment, zero-padding was applied to obtain a complete window.

For each window, five statistical features were extracted from each of the 180 CSI channels: mean, standard deviation, maximum, minimum, and energy. This resulted in a 900-dimensional feature vector per window. Each feature vector was assigned the corresponding spoken-utterance label, resulting in a 30-class classification problem.

The evaluation was performed using repeated stratified cross-validation. Specifically, 10 repetitions of 10-fold cross-validation were used. In each repetition, 90% of the data were used for training and 10% were held out for testing. The training features were z-score normalized, and the same normalization parameters were applied to the test fold. A Random Forest classifier was trained using bagging with 10 trees, a maximum of 50 splits per tree, a minimum leaf size of 5, and the Gini diversity index as the split criterion. These parameters were experimentally selected to reduce classification error.

Performance was evaluated using overall accuracy, macro-averaged precision, macro-averaged recall, macro-averaged F1 score, and Cohen’s Kappa. The metrics were reported as the mean ± standard deviation across the 10 repetitions. Specifically, across the 25 subject-specific classifiers, the Random Forest baseline achieved an average accuracy of 74.12 ± 5.97%, macro-averaged precision of 72.35 ± 6.90%, macro-averaged recall of 68.73 ± 6.97%, macro-averaged F1 score of 68.15 ± 7.36%, and Cohen’s Kappa of 0.730 ± 0.062. These results provide an initial benchmark for closed-set subject-specific spoken-utterance recognition using the proposed Wi-Fi CSI dataset.

Limitations

The current dataset was collected in a single furnished indoor environment with a fixed transmitter–receiver configuration and a fixed subject position. Consequently, recognition models trained exclusively on this dataset may learn environment-specific multipath characteristics, room-layout effects, and device-placement patterns. Such models may therefore experience reduced performance when deployed in different rooms, under non-line-of-sight conditions, with different transmitter-receiver distances, or with different subject positions. Future work should include multi-environment data collection and cross-environment evaluation protocols to assess the robustness of Wi-Fi-based spoken-utterance recognition models. Domain adaptation and transfer learning may also be useful for adapting models trained on this dataset as a source domain to new target environments with limited additional data.

Another limitation is that data acquisition was limited to the 2.4 GHz band with 20 MHz bandwidth. Although this configuration reflects a common commodity Wi-Fi setup, models trained using this dataset may not directly generalize to 5 GHz or 6 GHz bands, wider bandwidths, or newer Wi-Fi standards. Different frequency bands and bandwidths may produce different propagation behavior, subcarrier resolution, interference levels, and sensitivity to speech-associated articulatory, facial, and head movements. Future datasets should therefore investigate additional frequency bands and wider bandwidths to improve generalization across Wi-Fi configurations.

The participant pool also presents demographic limitations. The dataset was collected from 25 healthy young adult volunteers from a university environment, with a relatively narrow age range and limited demographic and linguistic diversity. In addition, most participants were non-native English speakers. Therefore, future studies should include larger and more diverse participant groups, including broader age ranges, different native-language backgrounds, varied accents, and different speaking styles, to further evaluate the generalizability of Wi-Fi-based spoken-utterance recognition systems.

Finally, the dataset contains 30 spoken utterances and is therefore most suitable for closed-set spoken-utterance recognition. Models trained on this dataset should not be interpreted as supporting open-vocabulary or continuous conversational speech recognition. Such models may have limited ability to recognize out-of-vocabulary utterances unless additional training, adaptation, or few-shot learning strategies are used. Expanding the vocabulary to include more words, phrases, sentence-level utterances, and spontaneous speech would be an important direction for future work.

By framing these limitations as research gaps, this dataset serves as a foundation for subsequent studies aimed at developing more generalized, real-world wireless speech sensing systems.
