Automated interpretation of fetal cardiac function evaluation from the echocardiogram
Authors: Caixin Huang, Lihe Zhang, Baihong Xie, Yuting Jiang, Yunxiao Zhu, Xiaozhen Liu, Ting Lei, Miao He, Yafei Yan, Nan Wang, Hongning Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prenatal assessment of fetal cardiac function is crucial for predicting neonatal outcomes, yet manual echocardiographic measurements are labor-intensive and subjective. We developed a fully automated artificial intelligence (AI) workflow for estimating fetal cardiac function parameters from echocardiograms. The workflow integrates a deep learning model for real-time detection and segmentation of cardiac structures, followed by quality control and geometric calculation. It was developed and validated using an internal dataset of 52,942 annotated images from 1940 normal fetal echocardiograms, with further testing on two external normal datasets (245 echocardiograms) and one internal abnormal dataset (83 echocardiograms). Performance was compared against manual and Fetal Heart Quantification (Fetal HQ) measurements, and a dynamic Z-score model referencing gestational age and fetal biometrics was established. The AI achieved accurate segmentation, with mean Dice similarity coefficients >92% and mean intersection-over-union >85% across all test datasets. It exhibited higher intraclass correction coefficients and R-values relative to experts than inter-observer variability, alongside smaller mean absolute error and limits of agreement. The mean individual equivalence coefficients of all cardiac function parameters were below zero, indicating lower variability than manual or Fetal HQ. These results demonstrate that our fully automated AI workflow enables accurate, efficient, and reproducible quantification of fetal cardiac function, supporting its potential for standardized clinical application.

Introduction

Assessment of fetal cardiac function is an essential component of prenatal diagnosis, as impaired cardiac function has been closely associated with adverse neonatal and long-term outcomes1–4. Prenatal ultrasound examination is a vital tool for evaluating fetal cardiac function due to its non-invasive, safe, and highly acceptable characteristics5,6. However, manual measurement often requires the operator to have proficient skills and extensive experience. The measurement process is cumbersome, time-consuming, and inevitably susceptible to human factors. Although semi-automatic measurement tools like Fetal Heart Quantification (Fetal HQ) have been developed, experienced physicians still need several minutes to measure a single case in clinical practice7–11. The substantial time cost limits the widespread adoption of cardiac function measurements, leading operators to rely more heavily on visual features for diagnosis. Therefore, it is an urgent need to improve clinical efficiency and provide standardized and repeatable measurements.

However, automated measurement faces the challenges of dealing with various positions and irregular movements of the fetus in utero. Reliable measurements depend on the analysis of the intrauterine scene as depicted by the entire echocardiogram. Advances in artificial intelligence (AI), represented by deep learning, have made automated analysis of medical images possible12–14. Previous efforts to interpret the fetal echocardiography content have focused on the image acquisition and plane identification15–17, quality control optimization18–21, and congenital heart disease diagnosis22,23. Several studies have shown the potential of AI method for automating cardiac biometric measurements24–26. However, a comprehensive workflow that fully automates cardiac function measurement directly from echocardiograms is still lacking. These approaches typically split the analysis into several independent sub-tasks and validated performance using only still images sampled from echocardiograms. Most existing methods are limited to a few fetal cardiac function parameters, such as the cardio-thoracic ratio, cardiac chamber area, and left ventricular volume27–30. More importantly, there is a lack of evidence demonstrating that AI is interchangeable with expert manual measurements for the interpretation of echocardiography31.

Therefore, this study aimed to develop and validate an AI workflow that fully automates the analysis of fetal echocardiographic data for quantitative assessment of cardiac function.

Results

Study Overview

We developed and validated the AI workflow following the flowchart depicted in Fig. 3. The study included a total of 2,282 singleton fetuses with gestational ages ranging from 18+0 to 37+6 weeks, who were recruited from the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (FAH-SYSU), the Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (SAH-SYSU), and Zhongshan City People’s Hospital (ZCPH). Further details regarding the data characteristics are provided in Study Design and Datasets We developed the AI model using the internal normal dataset, which was randomly partitioned into a training set (1358 fetuses), a parameter selection set (291 fetuses), and an internal testing set (291 fetuses). The architectural details of the AI model are described in Develop AI-based Workflow for Video Analysis. We evaluated the segmentation performance across three separate and independent validation datasets. All annotations were performed and subsequently double-checked by a panel of sonographers using a standardized computer, as detailed in Data Acquisition and Annotation. We then compared the AI measurements against those obtained by two independent sonographers who manually remeasured the fetal cardiac function parameters using ultrasound instruments. The workflow for the manual human measurements is detailed in Comparison with Human-based Workflow. Finally, to quantify fetal cardiac development, we developed a Z-score model that integrated AI-automated measurements with both gestational age and routine biometric data. The details of the Z-score model are provided in Comparison with Human-based Workflow.

Quantification on Fetal Cardiac Development

Based on the results of AI automated measurements, we constructed a Z-score model to characterize the reference ranges of normal cardiac function parameters that vary with fetal biomarkers. We considered 44 candidate regression equations, as listed in Supplementary Table S9, to establish relationships between seven routinely acquired fetal growth biometrics and cardiac function parameters. Table 4 presents the percentile and mean of R2 values for cardiac function parameters fitted by the Z-score model using various growth biometrics as independent variables. We found that estimated fetal weight (EFW) was highly correlated with most cardiac function parameters, as it comprehensively reflected the overall developmental level of the fetus. Figure 2 displays the regression results for three cardiac function parameters, with subplots a and b showing scatter plots of normal and abnormal data respectively. The vast majority of normal samples fell within the 95% CI, demonstrating significant correlations between EFW and left ventricle end-systolic basal-apical length (LVBALs), cardiac area, and left ventricle end-diastolic area (LVEAd). In Fig. 2b, three abnormal cases with lowest z-score that exceeded the 95% CI were diagnosed with coarctation of the aorta, intrauterine growth restriction, and persistent left superior vena cava with ventricular septal defect, respectively. This demonstrates the model’s utility in identifying fetuses with cardiac compromise, providing valuable evidence to guide clinical management. Discussion

The fetal echocardiogram analysis is often compromised by fetal position and fetal movement, leading to variable image quality, inter-observer discrepancies, and subjective measurements. To solve this problem, the AI workflow directly detects cardiac structures in echocardiograms without relying on single-frame selection. Three key strategies were implemented: (1) training on diverse fetal positions and probe directions with augmented data to encompass acoustic variations; (2) utilizing precise polygon and bounding box annotations for anatomical feature learning; and (3) integrating RT-DETR with a Mask-DINO-like head in a unified Transformer framework for simultaneous detection and segmentation. These strategies distinguish our AI workflow from previous attempts to automate fetal echocardiogram analysis by focusing on individual tasks, such as standard view classification27,32,33, cardiac cycle detection34,35, or key structure segmentation30,36,37. Furthermore, prior to this study, there was a lack of the validation for the final measurement results derived from an integrated analysis of these individual tasks. In summary, our AI workflow addresses the challenges of fetal echocardiogram analysis and directly provides clinical measurements by its unified framework design.

In our prospective study, AI-based measurements exhibited lower variability than manual measurements by expert sonographers. While prior studies have shown deep learning algorithms’ proficiency in segmenting cardiac chambers in ultrasound images, few have validated performance on abnormal or external datasets24–26,30,31. We evaluated our RT-DETR segmentation model across three cohorts: internal normal/abnormal datasets and an external dataset. The model achieved DICE scores above 0.85 across all cohorts (Table 1), confirming its robust generalization to unseen pathologies and clinical environments. In Table 2, AI demonstrated superior agreement with reference standards: higher ICC and correlation coefficients, alongside lower MAE and variance. The mean IECs were consistently negative (Fig. 1), indicating greater inter-observer variability among sonographers than between either sonographer and AI. These results confirmed the interchangeability of AI with human workflows, demonstrating superior repeatability and smaller deviations. Therefore, our AI workflow effectively addresses operator dependency and time inefficiency in cardiac function assessment, providing an objective, efficient, and reproducible tool.

This study developed an AI-based dynamic Z-score model to quantify fetal cardiac function. The Z-score model extends the application value of automated measurement by eliminating the subjective influence of operators and the heavy physical labor in the large-scale cohort analysis. The model was the first to adapted to variations in gestational age and biometric parameters, and used fractional polynomial regression to capture nonlinear relationships between cardiac parameters and fetal measurements. For example, the Z-score model for left ventricular end-systolic basal-apical length achieved an R2 of 0.785, outperforming a traditional linear model (R2 = 0.721). The dynamic adaptability allowed precise quantification of individual fetal growth patterns, especially in abnormal cases such as fetal growth restriction or macrosomia38. By incorporating regressions for both the standard deviation and the residual, the Z-score improved the accuracy of reference intervals across gestational ages. The AI workflow enabled efficient, automated measurement of multiple cardiac parameters within 10 seconds per case, reducing subjectivity and enhancing reproducibility. This facilitates continuous data integration and model updates, thereby mitigating the obsolescence inherent in traditional methods. This dynamic Z-score tool enables sensitive detection of functional deviations (e.g., Z-score >1.7 predicts neonatal aortic stenosis with 94.9% sensitivity39) and tracks longitudinal changes of fetal growth restriction40, supporting risk stratification and personalized perinatal management41.

The present study is part of a broader paradigm shift in fetal ultrasound screening42,43. New deep learning techniques have been shown to be effective substitutes or supplements for medical practitioners in repetitive tasks such as quality control44, biometrics45,46, education47,48, and even disease diagnosis31,49,50. This study presents an important step forward by verifying the agreement between deep learning and human experts in measuring fetal cardiac function parameters. The AI workflow lies in its dual capability for real-time intra-examination assistance and retrospective batch analysis, which enhances clinical efficiency while serving as a powerful research tool. Therefore, it have the potential to improve the prenatal detection and prognostic assessment of cardiac defects, such as hypoplastic left heart syndrome and valvular dysplasia51. The study’s limitations highlight important future directions. First, the model was trained on single-center data and validated within Guangdong province, which is the most populous province in China. Although the dataset represents a large-scale and demographically diverse population, future work should include broader multi-center validation. Also, the exclusive use of GE ultrasound systems was necessitated by our reliance on the vendor-specific Fetal HQ software as the reference standard. Although our cohort included patients with varying BMIs, fetal positions, and machine models, the performance across differing imaging protocols, and equipment from other manufacturers remains unconfirmed. Furthermore, the repeatability and reproducibility of the AI measurements require further investigation52. Second, while focusing on the standard four-chamber view, expanding to other views like the three-vessel view would enhance clinical utility28. Besides, the application of the AI workflow needs to be extended to explore a hybrid human-AI collaborative model to balance efficiency with clinical trust. Finally, the application to the abnormal fetus require further development where normal cardiac features are disrupted53.

In conclusion, this study established an automated AI workflow that accurately quantified fetal cardiac function from echocardiograms. The model demonstrated high segmentation precision and exhibited lower variability compared to manual measurements. By enabling robust, automated parameter extraction, this tool supported the construction of objective Z-score models for assessing fetal heart development. These results demonstrated that the AI workflow holds significant clinical value, offering an accurate, simple, efficient, reproducible, and referential tool for the quantification of fetal cardiac function.

Study Design and Datasets

This study is a non-registration clinical trial, which is not for the purpose of drug or medical device registration, and does not involve clinical intervention for the diagnosis and treatment. All participants provided written informed consent for the use of routinely collected data in future research before the ultrasound examination. The ethics approval was provided by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University on October 29, 2019 (approval no.: [2019]421). As shown in Fig. 3, we included 1,940 singleton pregnancies attending routine antenatal care between January 2021 and December 2023. The inclusion criteria required videos to contain a standardized four-chamber view and an anatomically normal fetal heart. This dataset contained 83 echocardiogram videos from patients attending routine antenatal care between January 2021 and November 2023. Diseases that cause severe anatomical distortion were excluded because, in such cases, global cardiac function is fundamentally compromised and conventional cardiac function parameters are not applicable. Data Acquisition and Annotation

All echocardiogram videos, from FAH-SYSU, SAH-SYSU, and ZCPH, were captured by GE Voluson E10, E8, and E6. The instruments were equipped with 3D volume probes (RM7C, RM6C) and provided Fetal HQ software, which supported semi-automatic measurements. The ultrasound examination followed the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology (ISUOG) guidelines54,55. The echocardiograms spanned a variety of fetal positions, ultrasound scanning directions and a wide range of gestational ages, thereby capturing features with rich angle semantics and supporting good generalization ability. The protocol required routine acquisition of the standard fetal four-chamber view during periods of stable maternal breathing and fetal quiescence, with the prode maintained in a stable position. The probe was positioned to avoid shadows from the spine and ribs that could obscure the cardiac structures. Each video was acquired with a frame rate of at least 80 Hz and encompassed 3 to 10 cardiac cycles. We sampled these videos into images, which retained their original resolution, for further annotation. Six ultrasound physicians with 5 to 8 years of clinical experience annotated the anatomical structures in the preprocessed images. Two ultrasound physicians with over 10 years of clinical experience reviewed the annotations. Note that, none of the physicians responsible for image annotation and training data preparation participated in the subsequent measurement validation process. These double-checked annotations were used for training the deep learning model and validating its segmentation performance in the AI-based workflow. The annotation details are provided in Supplementary Section 1.

Develop AI-based Workflow for Video Analysis

The common human-based workflow involved a series of lengthy, sequential steps for performing fetal cardiac measurements, such as identifying the standard fetal four-chamber view, determining the systolic and diastolic phases, outlining the boundaries and selecting the measurement points. The selection of frames and measurement endpoints involved significant subjectivity, leading to poor repeatability of measurements and even failure to replicate results in regions lacking expert resources.

To solve these problems, we developed an AI workflow comprising identification and judging processes to automate fetal heart measurements, as shown in Fig. 4a. The motivation was to shift the analysis of fetal echocardiography from the selection of key frames to the direct selection of key cardiac structures. In the identification process, we trained well-established deep-learning-based detector named RT-DETR56 to detect and segment anatomical structures in real-time. Figure 4b shows the architecture of the detector, which employs a Transformer only on the smallest-scale feature map to extract global context. Then, this global information is propagated across all scales by a lightweight feature pyramid based on convolutional neural network (CNN). As the result, RT-DETR effectively combines CNN and Transformer advantages within a compact architecture while significantly reducing the computation. Motivated by the Mask-DINO57, we designed a segmentation head to extend RT-DETR, which enables the unified Transformer-based decoding for both the object detection and segmentation tasks. The detailed designs of RT-DETR and Mask-DINO are introduced in Supplementary Sections 4 and 5. We trained the model on 52,264 frames annotated with 553,998 boxes and 313,584 outlines from 1649 individual echocardiogram videos. We designed a specific processing pipeline to augment the image data and enhance the visual representation, as described in Supplementary Section 2. The training hyperparameters for the detection and segmentation networks are shown in Supplementary Table S1. Figure 4c illustrates the segmentation of six key cardiac structures and the detection of eleven other structures on the video frame. In the judging process, we adopted a variety of strategies to ensure the quality and accuracy of the detected structures, as shown in Fig. 4d. First, the AI workflow eliminated low-quality and redundant detections using a Non-Maximum Suppression algorithm based on their confidence scores. Second, we retained the most robust sequential detections by identifying the connection path with the largest IoU between structures in adjacent frames. Finally, the AI workflow followed the ISUOG guidelines54,55 to filter unqualified frames that lacked the main chamber structures or more than three auxiliary structures. The cardiac function parameters were calculated using geometric, mathematical, and image processing methods as described in Supplementary Section 6. The final measurement was obtained by combining measurements from multiple qualified cardiac cycles at the video level. Comparison with Human-based Workflow

We invited two independent sonographers with over five years of experience to remeasure the fetal cardiac function parameters on the normal and abnormal internal validation datasets. Blind to the patient information, routine examination findings, AI measurements and the data annotations, each sonographer performed both manual and semi-automatic measurements using Fetal HQ software53 on the ultrasound instruments. These sessions were separated by a two-month washout period to prevent recall bias. Manual measurements of left ventricular end-systolic volume, end-diastolic volume, and ejection fraction were performed using the Simpson’s method58. All 70 fetal cardiac function parameters were classified into three categories: full heart (17 parameters), right heart (24 parameters), and left heart (29 parameters), as listed in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3. Consistency tests were performed by comparing the AI measurements against the manual or Fetal HQ measurements. The individual equivalence coefficient (IEC) was used for the comparison of the variability between the two manual measurements, the two Fetal HQ measurements, and the AI measurements.

Quantify fetal cardiac development

Standardized dynamic assessment of fetal cardiac function is essential for prenatal risk stratification, perinatal management, and the quantification of cardiac development. Currently, clinical practice primarily relied on gestational age-based percentile references, ignoring that cardiac parameter variability changes with gestation. Consequently, reference intervals were too wide early in pregnancy, reducing sensitivity, and too narrow later, lowering specificity, which can mask abnormalities or misinterpreting normal variations59. In contrast, the Z-score—representing the number of standard deviations a measurement is from the mean—dynamically adapted to biological variations across gestational ages. Therefore, we developed a Z-score model that integrates AI automated measurements with both gestational age and routine biometric data.

In this study, we included a consecutive cohort of 1385 singleton pregnancies with gestational ages at scan ranging from 18w+0d to 37w+6d from the internal normal dataset. Table 7 summarizes the distribution ranges of seven common growth parameters, and Figure 5 shows the histogram of gestational age. Significant positive correlations were observed between end-of-pregnancy gestational weeks and growth parameters (Pearson r = 0.966 ~ 0.978, p < 0.001), consistent with established principles of fetal growth and development. We constructed the Z-scroce model based on the mean and standard deviation of the automated measurements, reflecting the relationship by both linear and Royston-Altman fractional polynomials. Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or as median with interquartile range (IQR). Table 8 lists the metrics used for the statistical analysis, as well as their definitions and interpretation thresholds. The Dice coefficient, IoU, and HD were used to evaluate segmentation performence and were culculated using the Python package scikit-learn. The Pearson or Spearman correlation, ICC, LoA, and MAE were used to assess the agreement between automated and human measurements. The IEC was used to analyze the interchangeability between automated and human measurements, with a prespecified non-inferiority margin of Δ = 0.25. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test and P >0.05 (two-tailed) was interpreted as evidence of a normal distribution. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 27.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and MedCalc 19.5.6 software (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium).
