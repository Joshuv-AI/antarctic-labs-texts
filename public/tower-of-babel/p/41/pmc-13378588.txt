ALR couples IMS redox and heme biosynthesis beyond the disulfide relay
Authors: Julia Racho, Dylan Stobbe, Jan Jirschitzka, Marijana Croon, Carmelina Petrungaro, Muna Ali, Dario Wernet, Markus Habich, Nele Sophie Gawellek, Julian Strippel, Finn Dicke, Jörn Dengjel, Stephanie Kath-Schorr, Bent Brachvogel, Thomas Langer, Aleksandra Trifunovic, Ines Neundorf, Jan Riemer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proteins in the mitochondrial intermembrane space (IMS) play essential roles in respiratory chain assembly, metabolism, signaling, and organelle dynamics. Their stability and functionality often depend on structural disulfide bonds introduced by the mitochondrial disulfide relay, mediated by MIA40 and ALR. In this system, the sulfhydryl oxidase ALR reoxidizes MIA40, which in turn oxidizes incoming substrate proteins. Although evidence has suggested that ALR can also act independently of MIA40, its endogenous substrates have remained unknown. In this study, we captured proteins directly oxidized by ALR. Among these, we found coproporphyrinogen III oxidase (CPOX), a key enzyme in heme biosynthesis. We show that ALR-mediated disulfide bond formation is crucial for maintaining CPOX stability in the IMS, thereby ensuring effective heme biosynthesis and mitochondrial functionality. Notably, while disulfide-deficient CPOX failed to rescue CPOX loss when localized to the IMS, it retained functionality when redirected to the cytosol. However, this bypass compromised pathway efficiency, leading to the accumulation of protoporphyrinogen IX, a highly hydrophobic and redox-active intermediate that sensitized cells to cell death. Together, our findings reveal that ALR has functions beyond the MIA pathway and highlight that oxidative protein folding in the IMS relies not only on a relay mechanism but also on a broader disulfide-introducing network of enzymes.

INTRODUCTION

Proteins of the mitochondrial intermembrane space (IMS) fulfil important roles in assembling and maintaining respiratory chain complexes, facilitating mitochondrial morphology and dynamics, catalyzing metabolic reactions and mediating metabolite exchange, and facilitating diverse signaling modalities (1–6). Many IMS proteins contain structural disulfide bonds that are crucial for their import into the compartment, enable multimerization, or facilitate stability (2, 3, 7, 8). For most IMS proteins, these disulfide bonds are formed by a dedicated machinery—the mitochondrial disulfide relay (MDR) (2, 9–12). MDR consists of the oxidoreductase and import receptor MIA40 (in humans also CHCHD4) and the homodimeric sulfhydryl oxidase ALR (also called GFER, Erv1, or hepatopoietin) (13–19). MIA40 interacts with and oxidizes newly synthesized reduced substrate precursors during their import from the cytosol. ALR reoxidizes MIA40 and relays electrons to cytochrome c or molecular oxygen directly (14–16, 18, 20–26). The oxidation follows a cascade of thiol-disulfide exchange reactions that proceed through mixed disulfide intermediates containing intermolecular disulfide bonds between, e.g., MIA40 and its substrates as well as MIA40 and ALR (27). Trapping mixed disulfide intermediates can be used to identify native targets, which has been successfully done for MIA40 substrate identification (17, 25).

Mutations in ALR are associated with a rare autosomal recessive mitochondrial myopathy and combined respiratory chain deficiency (28–30). Beyond its role in the MDR, human ALR has been implicated in several additional processes including antiapoptotic and antioxidative functions, modulation of inflammation and metabolism, biogenesis of iron-sulfur clusters, and promotion of hepatic regeneration (31–33). None of these processes were so far linked to MIA40, thus suggesting that MIA40 might not be the sole ALR substrate.

We thus set out to identify MIA40-independent endogenous ALR substrates (Fig. 1A) and characterize the functional consequences of their interactions with ALR. Among these, we identified coproporphyrinogen III oxidase (CPOX), an IMS-localized enzyme of the heme biogenesis pathway (34–44). We demonstrated that its stability in the IMS and thus its function relied on disulfide bonds introduced by ALR. Consequently, a CPOX variant lacking these disulfide bonds could not complement CPOX loss. However, when we targeted the very same variant to the cytosol, it could do so, in line with the full in vitro activity of purified disulfide-less CPOX and the different requirements for protein stability in IMS and cytosol. However, expressing cytosolic CPOX was at the expense of efficient heme biosynthesis resulting in lowered cellular heme levels that, under conditions of increased heme demand, might be critical. Moreover, the product of the CPOX reaction, protoporphyrinogen IX (PPgenIX), accumulated, thereby sensitizing cells to cell death.

RESULTS

Interaction partners of ALR beyond the disulfide relay

To identify the interactome of human ALR, we constructed stable inducible human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 cell lines expressing C-terminally hemagglutinin (HA)– or Strep-tagged ALR at low, homogeneous levels. After confirmation of the correct localization of tagged ALR (fig. S1A), we enriched ALR-substrate complexes in a proof-of-concept experiment (Fig. 1A). Before cell lysis, free cysteine residues were alkylated with N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) to prevent unspecific postlysis thiol-disulfide exchange (Fig. 1B). Precipitation of ALR-Strep but not the mock control yielded a number of high–molecular weight bands that migrated slower than the ALR-Strep dimer under nonreducing conditions (Fig. 1B, black arrowheads). Upon dithiothreitol (DTT) treatment, these bands disappeared and were replaced by faster-migrating bands (white arrowheads), indicating that the associated proteins formed mixed disulfide bonds with ALR (Fig. 1B). To identify the interaction partners, we combined native and denaturing precipitation approaches of ALR-HA or ALR-Strep with quantitative mass spectrometry approaches for a total of six approaches with two to four biological replicates each (Fig. 1, C to E, and fig. S1B). ALR was identified in all experiments. To count a given hit as interaction partner, we set the threshold to significant enrichment above log2 ~1.5 in four of six experiments, which led to the identification of seven potential ALR targets (Fig. 1E). All interactors are either IMS proteins or are membrane proteins that partially face the IMS (fig. S1C). As expected, one of the hits was MIA40, the partner of ALR in the disulfide relay. In addition, we identified adenylate kinase 2 (AK2), CPOX, mitochondrial carrier 2 (MTCH2), apoptosis inducing factor 1 (AIFM1), translocase of the inner mitochondrial membrane 50 (TIMM50), and voltage-dependent anion channel 2 (VDAC2). All hits contain IMS-exposed conserved cysteine residues of which some are in proximity to each other (fig. S1C).

The coprecipitation of all potential targets with ALR also under denaturing conditions implied that ALR interacts with its targets in a covalent manner via disulfide bonds (Fig. 1D). In line, we found that breaking disulfide bonds by using DTT before the denaturing ALR immunoprecipitation (IP) prevented the enrichment of these partners indicating a functional redox activity of ALR toward its partners (Fig. 1F).

Together, we identified various endogenous targets of ALR linking the redox function of ALR to diverse IMS processes. Given the physiological importance of the interplay between redox processes and heme synthesis, as well as different indications pointing to a link between the MDR and heme synthesis (35, 37, 42), we decided to focus in the following on understanding the link between ALR and CPOX.

ALR introduces disulfides into CPOX

To further test the covalent interaction between CPOX and ALR, we tagged CPOX and, upon coimmunoprecipitation, analyzed its interacting partners using quantitative mass spectrometry. Among these, ALR (but not MIA40) was detected as one of the significantly enriched, a result further confirmed by Western blotting (fig. S2, A and B). The presence of a covalent ALR-CPOX interaction indicated that ALR introduces disulfide bonds into CPOX. Of the 10 cysteines in mature CPOX, 6 are conserved, with two cysteine pairs spaced only 4.2 Å (C164, C192) and 4.5 Å (C304, C357) apart, respectively (Fig. 2A). However, none of them is present in the oxidized state in the crystal structure of human CPOX that was expressed and purified from the highly reducing cytosol of Escherichia coli [Fig. 2A; (45)]. To assess whether ALR oxidizes CPOX, we generated HEK293 cell lines stably and inducibly expressing ALR-Strep together with different CPOX-HA cysteine variants. These CPOX variants lacked either all 10 cysteines (CPOX10CA), the 4 cysteine residues that are in proximity to each other (CPOX4CA), or the cysteine pairs potentially forming the individual disulfides (CPOXC164,192A; CPOXC304,357A). All cysteine variants exhibit mitochondrial localization (fig. S2B). We performed denaturing ALR-Strep precipitation followed by immunoblotting against Strep (ALR), HA (CPOX variants), and as positive and negative controls MIA40 and the IMS protein HAX1 (46), respectively. When comparing CPOXWT-HA with CPOX10CA-HA or CPOX4CA-HA, we found that ALR-Strep only precipitated CPOXWT underlining the requirement for specific CPOX cysteines (Fig. 2, B and C). Analysis of the CPOX variants that lack putative disulfides revealed that the absence of C304/C357 but not C164/C192 resulted in loss of the ALR interaction (fig. S2D), indicating that the cysteines 304 and 357 of CPOX play a particularly important role in mediating its interaction with ALR. In line with a role of ALR in CPOX oxidation, we also found that the interaction was transient and only occurred during CPOX biogenesis as interaction was reduced upon cycloheximide (CHX) treatment, which stops the synthesis of new proteins (Fig. 2D).

Using an inverse redox shift experiment (Fig. 2E), we next tested the cysteine redox state of CPOX. In both HEK293 and HepG2 cells, endogenous CPOX migrated in a single band at an intermediate position, indicating that a fraction of cysteines was completely oxidized (Fig. 2F and fig. S2E). Likewise, CPOXWT-HA was present in this partially oxidized state (Fig. 2G), while CPOX4CA-HA and CPOX10CA-HA presented reduced redox states indicating that these variants lack the disulfide-forming cysteines (Fig. 2H and fig. S2F). Of the two CPOX double-cysteine variants, C164A/C192A still seemed to shift, unlike C304A/C357A, which migrated similarly to the reduced control (fig. S2G). This indicates that in cells, CPOX contains at least one disulfide bond.

To assess whether ALR could introduce those disulfide bonds directly, we in vitro reconstituted the oxidation of CPOX by ALR. To this end, we used purified ALR and in vitro–translated radioactive CPOX that is present in an unfolded and fully reduced state (Fig. 2I). While CPOX alone remained in the reduced state, addition of ALR led to partial CPOX oxidation, indicating that ALR was capable of forming at least one disulfide bond in CPOX although very inefficiently (Fig. 2, J and K).

Collectively, we demonstrate that ALR and CPOX transiently interact via a mixed disulfide bond leading to formation of at least one intramolecular disulfide bond in CPOX likely connecting cysteines 304 with 357 (Fig. 2L).

Loss of CPOX leads to decreased heme biosynthesis and impaired growth on carbon sources requiring respiration

To understand the role of the disulfide bonds for CPOX function, we first established a CPOX knockout (KO) in HEK293 cells (fig. S3A). CPOX serves as antepenultimate enzyme in the heme biosynthesis pathway (Fig. 3A, left) that starts with the rate-limiting conversion of succinyl–coenzyme A and glycine to δ-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) by the enzyme δ-aminolevulinic acid synthase (ubiquitously expressed ALAS1 and erythroid-expressed ALAS2) in the mitochondrial matrix. Defects in heme biosynthesis usually result in strong increases of ALAS1 levels and integrated stress response (ISR) induction (47–49), both of which we also observed in CPOX KO cells (Fig. 3, B and C). 5-ALA leaves mitochondria and continues to react in a series of cytosolic reactions to coproporphyrinogen III (COPgenIII), which enters the IMS. There, it encounters CPOX, which converts COPgenIII to PPgenIX (Fig. 3A, right) (50, 51). Thereafter, conversion to protoporphyrin IX (PPIX) and iron insertion take place by the enzymes protoporphyrinogen oxidase (PPOX) and ferrochelatase (FECH), respectively. As expected for a loss of CPOX, we found in CPOX KO cells strongly decreased levels of total cellular heme after the induction of heme synthesis by 5-ALA treatment, while the levels of COPIII, which served as a proxy for the CPOX substrate, COPgenIII, were significantly increased (Fig. 3, D and E). In line, we also observed a proliferation defect upon CPOX loss when we shifted cells to galactose-containing medium (Fig. 3E). For growth on galactose, cells require a functional respiratory chain, the biogenesis of which depends on the heme cofactor. This growth defect was not observed when cells were grown in glucose-containing medium complemented with uridine and was restored upon reexpression of CPOXWT-HA (Fig. 3F). On glucose-containing medium, the residual heme present in the fetal calf serum (FCS) of the medium was sufficient to sustain growth. Collectively, loss of CPOX results in defects in heme biosynthesis, which is accompanied by increased ALAS1 levels and induction of the ISR. As a consequence, proliferation of respiratory carbon sources is impaired.

Lack of disulfide-forming cysteines decreases CPOX stability in the IMS and impairs heme biosynthesis

When we complemented CPOX KO cells with different CPOX cysteine variants, we found that CPOXWT-HA and CPOXC164A/C192A-HA restored the growth on galactose-containing medium (Fig. 4A and fig. S4A). Conversely, CPOX4CA-HA, CPOXC164A/C304A-HA, and CPOXC304/C357A-HA could only do so to a minor extent, indicating that whenever the C304-C357 disulfide in CPOX was lacking, CPOX was unfunctional in the IMS. In line, CPOX4CA-HA expression did not restore total heme levels and instead resulted in even higher COPIII levels compared with the CPOX KO (Fig. 4, B and C). Conversely, CPOXWT-HA expression lowered cellular COPIII levels and increased total heme levels (Fig. 4, B and C). Last, expression of CPOXWT-HA but not CPOX4CA-HA led to lowered ALAS1 levels (Fig. 4D). Moreover, we also found that CPOX4CA-HA protein levels were much lower compared with CPOXWT-HA (Fig. 4D).

Next, we wondered whether the absence of disulfide bonds in CPOX would affect its enzymatic activity. To this end, we purified the protein and established an in vitro activity assay. While we succeeded in purifying wild-type (WT) CPOX, we did not manage to express and purify the 4CA variant. Instead, we purified the CPOXC164,304A variant (Fig. 4E), which we deem a valid proxy as it can also not form the disulfide bonds and fails to complement growth of the CPOX KO (fig. S4A). Unexpectedly, the activities of purified CPOXWT and CPOXC164,304A in an in vitro assay were very similar (Fig. 4E), indicating that disulfide bond loss does not affect the enzymatic activity but potentially the maturation or stability of CPOX.

First, we studied whether ALR would affect cellular CPOX levels. Using small interfering RNA (siRNA), we depleted ALR and as control MIA40 in cells inducibly expressing CPOX-HA. In those cells, loss of ALR but not MIA40 led to a reduction of CPOX-HA levels at steady state (fig. S4, B to D). We then assessed whether dynamic accumulation of CPOX-HA was affected by ALR loss. To this end, we depleted ALR and MIA40 and initiated CPOX-HA expression only after the knockdown was complete (Fig. 4F). We thereby observed that CPOX-HA levels only increased in control- and MIA40-siRNA–treated cells but not in cells treated with ALR-siRNA (Fig. 4, G and H), indicating that ALR-mediated disulfide formation in CPOX is important during CPOX biogenesis.

We next analyzed the CPOX life cycle by radioactive pulse-chase approaches. Using a pulse-only experiment, we found that directly after translation, levels of newly synthesized CPOXWT-HA and CPOX4CA-HA were very similar (Fig. 5A). We then turned to pulse-chase experiments using different longer and shorter chase times to assess biogenesis and stability of CPOX variants, respectively. CPOX is expressed with an unusual long bipartite mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS) (52), which we expected to be cleaved upon import in multiple steps. Endogenous CPOX revealed five bands that occurred in the course of the chase with the fastest migrating band at the height of endogenous, fully processed CPOX in immunoblot experiments (Fig. 5B). Processing occurred relatively fast as about 20%, and 40% of mature CPOX was already present after 5- and 15-min pulse, respectively (Fig. 5C). CPOXWT-HA in CPOX KO cells behaved very similarly as endogenous CPOX and allowed assignment of a critical cleavage step during CPOX maturation to the IMMP1/2 complex (Fig. 5D and fig. S5, B and C). The IMS proteases HTRA2, PARL, and YME1L did not affect CPOX biogenesis, leaving later processing steps unclear (fig. S5, D to G). CPOX4CA-HA exhibited a delayed maturation (Fig. 5, E and F). In a pulse-chase experiment with extended chase times up to 8 hours, we confirmed that total amounts of CPOX4CA-HA were rapidly declining, and mature CPOX4CA-HA was at the end hardly detectable. Concomitantly, total and mature CPOXWT-HA levels stayed constant and increased, respectively (Fig. 5, G and H, and fig. S5H).

In conclusion, the disulfide-forming cysteines of CPOX are essential for proper cellular functioning of the enzyme in the heme biosynthesis pathway. The absence of the disulfide bonds impairs cellular enzymatic activity leading to the accumulation of the CPOX substrate COPgenIII and the depletion of heme. As a consequence, ALAS1 levels increased, and cells were unable to sustain growth on a carbon source requiring a functional respiratory chain. This is not caused by differences in enzymatic activity of CPOX lacking the disulfide bonds but by a delayed maturation of CPOX and strongly decreased cellular stability of this variant compared with the WT protein (Fig. 5I).

Cytosol-targeted CPOX is active without disulfide bonds

The IMS is considered a relatively “harsh” proteostasis environment potentially with increased temperatures and the absence of adenosine triphosphate (ATP)–dependent chaperones (3, 53) which might explain the requirement for stabilizing disulfide bonds in many proteins. In baker’s yeast, the CPOX homolog Hem13 localizes to the cytosol, and the heme biosynthesis intermediate PPgenIX enters the IMS (Fig. 6A, left). Hem13 shares the very same fold with CPOX but lacks the disulfide-forming conserved cysteines [Fig. 6A; middle and right (54)].

To test whether heme biosynthesis in human cells could be spatially reorganized to resemble the situation in yeast and thus lower the requirement for disulfides in CPOX, we complemented CPOX KO cells with either Hem13-HA or with CPOXWT-HA, CPOX10CA-HA, or CPOX4CA-HA lacking their MTS (Δ88 variants; Fig. 6B). All variants were cytosolically localized (fig. S6A), and Δ88-CPOXWT and CPOX4CA variants exhibited comparable stability (fig. S6B). Unexpectedly, all variants were capable of suppressing ALAS1 accumulation (Fig. 6C), they restored growth on galactose-containing medium (Fig. 6D), and they lowered cellular COPgenIII amounts to WT levels (Fig. 6E). Despite this, expression of Δ88-CPOXWT-HA compared with IMS-localized CPOXWT-HA resulted in lower total cellular heme levels (Fig. 6, F and G) although its protein levels were higher in cells (Fig. 6C).

Cytosol-targeted CPOX leads to cellular accumulation of its product PPgenIX and increases sensitivity to cell death

Why does cytosolic CPOX not sustain heme biosynthesis with the same efficiency as IMS-CPOX? The final intermediates in heme biosynthesis are highly hydrophobic and poorly water soluble. As product of its activity, CPOX generates the most hydrophobic intermediate, PPgenIX (Fig. 7A). Previous work had demonstrated that metabolite shuttling from CPOX to PPOX possibly via the transmembrane protein TMEM14C is a crucial step in the heme biosynthesis pathway (42, 55, 56). Placing CPOX in the cytosol does not only prevent this shuttling, but PPgenIX even requires additional mitochondrial outer membrane (OMM) transport. In line, we observed increased cellular levels of PPIX (as proxy for the CPOX product PPgenIX) (Fig. 7B and fig. S7A). This might indicate either inefficient OMM transfer of the intermediate and/or its slow release from CPOX. As evidence that the latter might indeed be relevant, we found copurified PPIX bound to a fraction of CPOX heterologously expressed and purified from E. coli (Fig. 7C). An additional loading of this purified CPOX with either PPIX or COPIII led to the stable binding of these metabolites despite attempts to remove them (Fig. 7D). Thus, although cytosolic CPOX complements the CPOX KO, it does this not very efficiently likely because both PPgenIX release from CPOX and the subsequent transfer across the OMM are slow and would require substrate shuttling.

Accumulation of PPgenIX/PPIX has also been observed in some cancer cells, where it induced metabolic stress and increased sensitivity to ferroptosis, a cell death modality associated with diminished activity of endogenous antioxidant defense systems that protect against membrane lipid peroxidation (57). We thus tested whether our conditions with increased cytosolic PPgenIX levels would exert as similar effect. To this end, we treated CPOX KO cells complemented with Δ88-CPOXWT-HA or CPOXWT-HA with a range of concentrations of 5-ALA to induce increased heme biogenesis. We observed an increased susceptibility to cell death of Δ88-CPOXWT-HA–expressing cells (Fig. 7E). In our cellular model, this seemed to be a so far unidentified and most likely protoporphyrin-specific type of cell death as neither inhibitors of ferroptosis, apoptosis, or necroptosis could prevent cells from dying (fig. S7B). Collectively, this indicates that evolutionary transfer of CPOX to the IMS enabled efficient substrate shuttling of highly hydrophobic intermediates and prevented sensitization of cells toward cell death. This came at the price of a complex CPOX biogenesis pathway involving ALR-mediated disulfide formation.

DISCUSSION

CPOX links ALR-mediated disulfide bond formation to heme biosynthesis

The sulfhydryl oxidase ALR has been originally described as the homolog of yeast Erv1 in the human MDR pathway. Although several studies suggest that ALR homologs introduce structural disulfide bonds into various target proteins in heterologous expression systems, endogenous substrates beyond MIA40 remained elusive so far (21, 22, 58–60). In this study, we identified a set of endogenous, disulfide-dependent interaction partners of ALR. In addition to MIA40, these include AK2, CPOX, VDAC2, MTCH2, AIFM1, and TIMM50, which all interacted covalently with ALR. The fact that the identified interactors contain conserved cysteines that are exposed to the IMS, with some cysteines positioned in close proximity to each other (fig. S1C), links ALR oxidase activity to redox regulation of key mitochondrial pathways such as adenine nucleotide homeostasis, heme biosynthesis, and import of proteins into the OMM, matrix, and IMS and might at least in part explain its roles beyond the disulfide relay and the consequences of patient mutations (28–30). Notably, AK2 and AIFM1 are also known MIA40 interaction partners (61–65), raising the possibility that multiple redox enzymes cooperate on the same targets.

For one interaction partner, CPOX, we demonstrated that ALR introduces disulfide bonds during its maturation. This is in line with data from the Oximouse database that report the equivalent cysteines in mouse CPOX to be oxidized (66). These disulfide bonds are essential for maintaining CPOX stability within the IMS and consequently for its function in heme biosynthesis (Fig. 5I). Previous studies using pharmacological inhibition already linked mitochondrial iron homeostasis and heme biosynthesis to redox regulation within the IMS without providing mechanistic explanation (67). We confirmed that inhibiting ALR oxidase activity with MitoBloCK-6 or reduction of ALR levels significantly reduced heme levels in HEK293 and HepG2 cells (fig. S7, C and D) fitting to the role of ALR in CPOX maturation. The reduction in total cellular heme levels by pharmacological inhibition of ALR has been shown to impair cell viability and trigger transcriptional changes typically associated with defects in heme production. Notably, these phenotypes could be largely rescued by exogenous heme supplementation, though the underlying mechanism remained unclear. Our findings showing that ALR-derived disulfide bonds stabilize CPOX and enable efficient heme biosynthesis provide now a mechanistic explanation for these observations.

CPOX can be stably expressed without its disulfide-forming cysteines in the cytosol and remains catalytically active, as also shown for the recombinant protein. This observation aligns with the evolutionary placement of the yeast homolog Hem13, which is localized in the cytosol and lacks the disulfide-forming cysteine residues (54). In mammalian cells, the transport of the CPOX educt CPgenIII from the cytosol across the OMM is mediated by ABCB6 (68). This transporter has been shown to efficiently shuttle also other tetrapyrrole species, including the CPOX product PPgenIX, explaining why we can rescue CPOX loss by expression of cytosolic CPOX variants.

This finding raises the question of why CPOX in higher eukaryotes became compartmentalized into the IMS, making the ALR-mediated introduction of stabilizing disulfide bonds necessary in the first place. It appears that IMS localization of CPOX homologs is likely advantageous for heme biosynthesis. It may enable formation of a CPOX-TMEM14C-PPOX metabolon (56, 69, 70) and facilitate the direct substrate shuttling of highly hydrophobic late-stage intermediates, thereby enhancing pathway efficiency (Fig. 7F). This localization becomes particularly important under conditions of elevated heme demand, where the CPOX-catalyzed reaction may become a rate-limiting step downstream of ALAS in the regulation of heme biosynthesis (71, 72). Efficient substrate shuttling also suppresses accumulation of cytotoxic pathway intermediates including the product of the CPOX reaction, PPgenIX (36, 57, 73), which sensitizes cells to cell death. Conversely to free heme, which can be continuously degraded by HMOX, PPgenIX cannot be degraded by cellular machineries (47, 74), and instead has to be exported from cells by a plasma membrane ATP-binding cassette transporter (75).

From the disulfide relay to a disulfide-forming network of IMS enzymes

The MDR has long been viewed as a linear pathway, in which substrates are first oxidized by MIA40, followed by reoxidation of MIA40 by ALR, which transfers electrons to cytochrome c and the respiratory chain. In such a model, loss of MIA40 and ALR would be expected to produce similar phenotypes. However, our data and previous observations argue against this simple view. Depletion of MIA40 and ALR resulted in markedly different effects on protein homeostasis, proliferation, and cellular physiology (fig. S7, E to G). Consistently, MIA40 is nonessential in Arabidopsis thaliana (76), and several protists such as Trypanosoma brucei and Plasmodium falciparum lack MIA40 homologs altogether, instead expressing ALR/Erv1 orthologs with additional domains predicted to mediate redox and chaperone functions (77–79). Together, these observations support a broader role for ALR beyond the classical disulfide relay.

In contrast to MIA40, ALR showed low coprecipitation with its identified substrates, suggesting transient interactions. This is consistent with the distinct functional properties of the two enzymes: MIA40 acts as an oxidoreductase and import receptor with prolonged substrate-binding times, whereas ALR likely acts later during protein maturation (80, 81).

Supporting this idea, most ALR interactors are imported via pathways independent of the MIA40-dependent disulfide relay. For example, mitochondrial import of CPOX, assessed by MTS processing, was unaffected by the absence of disulfide-forming cysteines. ALR may therefore introduce disulfide bonds into partially folded substrates after import, potentially facilitated by its flexible N-terminal shuttle domain (18, 23).

Together with the identification of additional IMS oxidoreductases such as Dbi1, Gpx3, and Trx1 (82–85), our findings suggest that oxidative protein folding in the IMS is not restricted to the MIA40 relay. Instead, the IMS likely contains a broader network of redox enzymes with specialized roles, including MIA40 in import-coupled folding of small proteins and ALR in postimport oxidation of larger substrates.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plasmids, cell lines, and chemical treatments

For plasmids, cell lines, antibodies and further tools used in this study, see tables S1 to S3. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 8% FCS at 37°C under 5% CO2. For CHX chase experiments, cells were treated with CHX (100 μg/ml) dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). For the generation of stable, inducible HEK293 cell lines, the Flp-In T-REx-293 cell line was used with the Flp-In T-REx system (Invitrogen). For generation of double stable, inducible cell lines, the PiggyBac Transposon system (System Biosciences, BioCat) was used on top of the Flp-In T-REx system. Expression of constructs was induced using doxycycline (DOX) (0.0001 to 1 μg/ml) (Flp-In T-Rex) or cumate (30 μg/ml) (PiggyBac Transposon) for the indicated time points. The amount of DOX used for induction of CPOX-HA variants varied as it was adjusted to obtain protein levels comparable to endogenous protein levels.

Generation of HEK293 KO cells

For the generation of CPOX KO cells, guide RNA sequences targeting human CPOX were cloned into the pSpCas9(BB)-2A-GFP (PX458) vector, which was a gift from F. Zhang [Broad Institute of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Harvard, Cambridge, MA, USA; plasmid no. 48138; Addgene; (86)]. The guide RNA sequence 5′-AGCTCGCCCAGGTCGGTCACAGG-3′ was used. HEK Flp-In T-REx-293 cells were transfected with the plasmid using polyethylenimine (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 24 hours, green fluorescent protein (GFP)–positive cells were collected via fluorescence-activated cell sorting, and single-cell clones were seeded onto 96-well plates (one cell per well). Clonal cell lines were screened for CPOX protein expression by immunoblotting and sequenced to verify indel mutations (by Eurofins GATC Biotech).

Immunofluorescence

Cells were seeded onto poly-l-lysine–coated coverslips in 12-well plates, and protein expression was induced with DOX (1 μg/ml). After 48 hours, cells were stained with MitoTracker Deep Red (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in a dilution of 1:100,000 for 1 hour at 37°C. Afterward, fixation was done with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Coverslips were washed 3× with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated in fresh blocking buffer [10 mM Hepes-KOH (pH 7.4), 3% bovine serum albumin, and 0.3% Triton X-100] for 1 hour. Coverslips were incubated in blocking buffer containing primary antibody (1:400) for 1 hour at room temperature (RT), washed 3× with PBS, and transferred to blocking buffer containing fluorescent secondary antibody (1:400) for 1 hour at RT in the dark. After three additional washing steps with PBS, nuclei of the samples were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). DAPI (0.5 ml) (1 μg/ml) was added onto the samples for 15 min at RT in the dark. Samples were washed with PBS one last time and placed onto drops of prewarmed ProLong Gold antifade mounting medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) onto coverslips. Samples were dried overnight at 4°C in the dark and sealed with nail polish the next day. Samples were imaged with a Leica LSM980 Airyscan 2 confocal laser scanning microscope and a Plan-Apochromat 63×/1.4 oil objective.

Galactose shift

To shift cells to galactose, they were resuspended in galactose medium [DMEM without glucose, supplemented with 8% FCS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1× minimum essential medium nonessential amino acids (Sigma-Aldrich), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Sigma-Aldrich), uridine (50 μg/ml), and galactose (4.5 g/l)] after trypsinizing for seeding.

Cell growth assay

Expression of the gene of interest in stable cell lines was induced by addition of DOX 24 hours before seeding. DOX amounts were titrated previously to reach expression levels of the protein of interest similar to endogenous levels. A total of 15,000 cells were seeded on a poly-l–coated 48-well dish and incubated at 37°C. In case of galactose shift experiments, a galactose shift was performed during seeding as described before. Medium was exchanged every day. For medium exchange, 250 μl of old medium was aspired from the well, and 250 μl of fresh medium was added carefully. For stable cell lines, medium always contained the respective amount of DOX to ensure constant protein expression. Every 6 hours, the coverage of each well was scanned using the cytosmart omni (Axion Biosystems).

IP and affinity purification

To display protein-protein interactions in HEK293 cell lines, native IP or affinity purification (AP) experiments were performed. The experimental procedure of these two only differs in the matrix used for pulldown of proteins of interest. For IP experiments, an antibody-coupled agarose matrix is used, whereas a Strep-Tactin–coupled Sepharose matrix is used of APs. HA-tagged proteins were pulled down via monoclonal anti-HA agarose from mouse (Sigma-Aldrich), endogenous CPOX was enriched via protein-A–agarose matrix (Expedeon) loaded with anti-CPOX antibody (St. John’s Laboratory), and Strep-tagged proteins were purified over Strep-Tactin Sepharose (IBA).

For the experiment, 3,000,000 cells were seeded onto 15-cm dishes, induced with DOX or cumate depending on the cell line and grown for 96 hours until reaching around 80 to 90% confluency. Cells were placed on ice and incubated in situ with 20 mM NEM for 10 min. Afterward, cells were detached from plates using a rubber policeman and pelleted at 700g at 4°C for 3 min.

For native lysis, cell pellets were resuspended and lysed for 1 hour on ice in native IP buffer containing 1% Triton X-100 [100 mM Na-Pi (pH 8.1), 100 mM NaCl, and 1% Triton X-100]. For denaturing lysis, cells were resuspended in denaturing IP buffer without Triton x-100 [30 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.1), 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM EDTA], and SDS was added to a final concentration of 1.6%. Samples were incubated at RT for 10 min and then heated up to 95°C for 5 to 20 min depending on the sample volume. Afterward, samples were sonified with two times 15 cycles at an amplitude of 80% to disrupt remaining DNA and improve lysis. Then, 2.5× the amount of denaturing IP buffer containing 2.5% Triton X-100 was added to the lysates to replace SDS, which would interfere with binding of proteins to antibody or Strep-Tactin–coupled beads. Lysates were incubated for 1 hour on ice.

Totals were taken after the 1-hour incubation and prepared for SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) by adding 4× reducing Laemmli buffer to a final concentration of 1× [60 mM tris (pH 6.8), 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 0.0025% bromophenol blue, and 50 mM DTT] and sonifying them in 10 cycles at an amplitude of 80%. Simultaneously, remaining cell lysate was distributed to 1.5-ml tubes and cleared at 20,000g at 4°C for 1 hour. Beads were equilibrated in native or denaturing IP buffer containing 1% Triton X-100 by washing them 3× for 1 min at 2000g at 4°C. Cleared lysate was pooled and incubated with beads tumbling overnight at 4°C to allow for binding of prey protein to the beads. After that, beads were washed 5× for 1 min at 2000g at 4°C with native or denaturing IP buffer containing 1% Triton X-100. A last washing step was performed with native or denaturing IP buffer without Triton X-100. Then, beads were eluted in 1× Laemmli buffer (with 50 mM DTT for reducing conditions) at 95°C two times 10 min. Totals were heated up to 95°C for 10 min. Samples were analyzed via SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting.

Proteomics analysis

Samples were analyzed by the Cologne Excellence Cluster on Cellular Stress Responses in Aging-Associated Diseases (CECAD) Proteomics Facility on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, granted by the German Research Foundation under INST 216/1163-1 FUGG) mass spectrometer that was coupled to an Vanquish neo in trap-and-elute setup (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were loaded onto a precolumn (Acclaim 5 μm PepMap 300 μ Cartridge) with a flow of 60 μl/min before reverse flushed onto an in-house packed analytical column (30-cm length, 75-μm inner diameter, filled with 2.7-μm Poroshell EC120 C18, Agilent). Peptides were chromatographically separated with an initial flow rate of 400 nl/min and the following gradient: initial 2% B (0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile), up to 6% in 3 min. Then, flow was reduced to 300 nl/min and B increased to 20% B in 26 min, up to 35% B within 15 min and up to 98% solvent B within 1.0 min while again increasing the flow to 400 nl/min, followed by column wash with 95% solvent B and reequilibration to initial condition. The mass spectrometer was operated in data-dependent acquisition with a cycle time of 1 s with MS1 scans acquired from 350 mass/charge ratio (m/z) to 1400 m/z at 60k resolution and an AGC (Automatic Gain Control) target of 300%. MS2 scans were acquired at 15k resolution with a maximum injection time of 118 ms and a normalized AGC target of 50% in a 2-Th window and a fixed first mass of 110 m/z. All MS1 scans were stored as profile, all MS2 scans as centroid.

All mass spectrometric raw data were processed with Maxquant [version 2.6, (87)] using default parameters against the Uniprot canonical human database (UP5640, downloaded 15.01.2025) with the match-between-runs option enabled between replicates. Follow-up analysis was done in Perseus 1.6.15 (87). Protein groups were filtered for potential contaminants and insecure identifications. Remaining IDs were filtered for data completeness in at least one group and missing values imputed by sigma downshift (0.3-σ width, 1.8 σ downshift). Afterward, false discovery rate–controlled two-sided t tests were performed.

Immunoblot and image acquisition

Samples were prepared in Laemmli buffer containing 50 mM DTT and heat denatured for 10 min at 96°C. Protein samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE using Tris-Glycine acrylamide gels and immunoblot, followed by immunological detection of the protein of interest. The addition of 2,2,2-trichloroethanol to the Tris-Glycine acrylamide gels allowed for visualization of proteins and as a loading control. The immunoblotting images were detected using the ChemiDoc Touch imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Protein purification

Recombinant proteins were expressed from the indicated plasmids (table S1) in Rosetta2 E. coli strains. Bacterial growth was conducted in LB media (for ALR supplemented with riboflavin) shaking at 25°C and 180 rpm. Protein expression was induced with 0.1 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside and incubated for further 20 hours before harvesting. Cells were harvested on ice in PBS and stored at −20°C. The 6×His-tagged constructs (Δ110 CPOX WT, Δ110 CPOX C164,304A, and Δ80 ALR) were purified by immobilized metal affinity chromatography using Ni Sepharose (6 Fast Flow, GE). The bacterial lysate was bound to beads in binding buffer supplemented with 10 mM imidazole at 4°C. Beads were washed with binding buffer supplemented with 50 mM imidazole before elution by an imidazole gradient up to 500 mM imidazole. The protein was eluted from the column by adding 20 mM reduced glutathione (GSH). GSH was removed from the purified sample using a PD-10 desalting column, after which the GST-tagged protein was incubated with the previously purified GST-PreScission Protease for 2 hours at 4°C to remove the GST tag. Protease and cleaved GST tag were removed by a GSTrap 4B column.

In vitro translation and in vitro oxidation kinetics

Full-length ALR was purified the day previous to the experiment as described above, stored overnight at 4°C; 30 min before the oxidation was performed, 25 μM purified ALR, 30 μM horse heart cytochrome c (Merck), and 10 mM EDTA were preincubated on ice in reaction buffer [50 mM NaPi and 300 mM NaCl (pH 8.0)]. For the control condition, ALR was exchanged with reaction buffer. The [35S]-labeled precursor was prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol and (88). The oxidation reaction was performed at 30°C and hypoxic conditions (O2 < 1%), to prevent autoxidation of the precursor protein. Reaction was started by adding radioactively labeled protein precursor lysate to the reaction mix (2 μl per time point) and stopped by adding reaction mixture directly into Laemmli buffer containing 4 mM mmPEG24 (final concentration). The samples incubated in the dark for 1 hour, were heat denatured, and analyzed by SDS-PAGE and autoradiography. To determine the oxidation state of the newly synthesized precursor, one control sample was immediately shifted at the beginning of the experiment without any additions. A maximal shift sample was generated by heat-denaturing the precursor lysate in Laemmli buffer in the presence of 5 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP), followed by performing the shift.

Radioactive pulse and pulse-chase experiments

Cells were seeded onto poly-l-lysine–coated dishes and induced with DOX (1 μg/ml) for 24 hours in the case of the comparative AP of ALR-Strep (Fig. 1B) or 1 hour before the experiment in the case of the radioactive pulse and pulse-chase experiments precipitating CPOX. On the day of the experiment, cells were starved of cysteine and methionine in starvation medium [DMEM without cysteine and methionine (Sigma-Aldrich) and 100 mM Hepes-KOH (pH 7.4); for stable cell lines, DOX (1 μg/ml)] for 15 min at 37°C. Then, newly synthesized protein was labeled by incubating cells in pulse medium, starvation medium supplemented with Easytag Express 35S Protein Labeling Mix (Revvity) at 200 μCi/ml (ALR-Strep and endogenous CPOX) or 100 μCi/ml (CPOX-HA constructs), for 15 min at 37°C. Pulse labeling was stopped by removing the medium and adding chase medium [high-glucose DMEM, 20 mM l-methionine, and 100 mM Hepes-KOH (pH 7.4)] at 37°C for variable times (no chase in case of pulse assays). The chase was stopped by placing cells on ice and washing with ice-cold PBS. Cells were detached from the plate using a rubber policeman and pelleted at 600g for 5 min. An IP or AP was performed as described in the respective section. Samples were analyzed via Tris-Tricine SDS-PAGE, immunoblot, and autoradiography.

Autoradiography and image acquisition

Samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE using Tris-Tricine acrylamide gels and immunoblot. After blotting, membranes were dried, and molecular weight marker bands were marked with ink containing small amounts of radioactive sulfur 35S. Membranes were exposed to radioactivity-sensitive Storage Phosphor Screens BAS-IP (GE Healthcare) for 3 to 28 days. Signal was detected at the Typhoon FLA9500 Imaging System (GE Healthcare). Screens could be reused after being blanked with high-intensity light for 1 hour with the FLA Image Eraser (GE Healthcare).

Inverse shift assay

The inverse shift assay helps assess the cysteine redox state of proteins. Thiol-active molecules, in this case mm(PEG)12 or mm(PEG)24, are used to modify free cysteines. These modifications alter the protein’s running behavior on SDS-PAGE because of added molecular mass and different charge to mass ratio, allowing redox state determination. For the inverse shift assay, cells were seeded onto poly-l-lysine–coated six-well plates and let grow until 50% confluency. Then, cells were put on ice, and the sample for steady-state redox state determination was washed and incubated with PBS containing 20 mM NEM for 10 min. NEM modifies and therefore blocks free thiols to prevent further modification later during the experiment. Unmodified, minimal, and maximum shift controls did not require this pretreatment and were only washed with cold PBS. Then, PBS was aspired and 8% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) was added to rapidly acidify samples to reversibly protonate reactive thiols and trap the redox state while precipitating the protein. Samples were frozen at −80°C for 1 hour and thawed again at RT afterward. Precipitated protein was pelleted at 13,000g at 4°C for 15 min and washed with 5% TCA at the same conditions. TCA solution was completely removed, and protein pellets were dissolved in 1× Laemmli buffer containing 10 mM of the nonthiol reductant TCEP to reduce previously oxidized cysteines and incubated at 95°C for 15 min. Afterward, these previously oxidized cysteines were modified with the larger mm(PEG) and incubated at RT in the dark for 1 hour. Then, samples were analyzed via SDS-PAGE and immunoblot.

Cellular metabolite extraction

All cell lines were induced with DOX 2 days before seeding. For each cell line, six replicates of 500,000 cells were transferred to a six-well plate, induced again with DOX, and incubated for 24 hours. The next day, the cells were transferred to ice and washed with 2 ml of ice-cold PBS. For each cell line, two replicates were harvested in 1 ml of PBS for the determination of total protein levels by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. For porphyrin extraction, 1 ml of extraction buffer [80% MeOH and 5% formic acid (v/v)], previously stored at −80°C, was added, and all wells were incubated at −80°C for 30 to 60 min. Cell lysates were scraped into 1.5-ml reaction tubes, cell debris was pelleted (20,000g for 20 min at 4°C), and 700 μl of cell extraction was transferred to a fresh reaction tube and stored at −80°C until analyzed. On the day of the high-performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) analysis, all samples were dried using a SpeedVac manufactured by Thermo Fisher Scientific, after which they were dissolved in a 50:50 methanol:acetonitrile solution (v/v) at a volume of 60 μl, and 10 μl of the solution was loaded into the system. To normalize the detected porphyrin levels to protein levels, a BCA assay was performed on two replicates of each cell line, after which the absolute MS intensity of each porphyrin was normalized to the detected protein levels. To detect cellular PPIX levels, the cells were treated as described above. However, 20 hours before harvesting, the cells were washed once and transferred to FCS-free medium to prevent PPIX efflux (75).

Cellular heme measurement

All cell lines were induced with DOX 2 days before seeding. For each cell line, six replicates of 1,000,000 cells were transferred to a six-well plate and induced again with DOX. After 6 hours, the cells were treated with 1 mM 5-ALA (solved in PBS) to induce heme production. The next day, the cells were transferred to ice, washed with 2 ml of ice-cold PBS, and harvested in 1 ml of PBS. To normalize the measurements, 25 μl of each cell suspension was taken and analyzed by BCA assay for total protein levels. The PBS was removed completely, and the cell pellet was solved in 300 μl of oxalic acid (2 M). To release all the heme from the cells and convert it into detectable PPIX, the cells were sonicated and heated to 100°C for 1 hour. For the fluorescence measurements, lysates were transferred to a black-bottomed 96-well plate. Porphyrin fluorescence was excited at 405 nm, and the emission was detected at 603 nm using a CLARIOstar fluorescent plate reader (BMG LABTECH). For HPLC-MS analysis, the lysates were mixed 1:1 with a 50:50 methanol:acetonitrile solution (v/v), and 20 μl was loaded into the system.

MB-6 treatment

To determine the effect of MB-6 treatment on the total cellular heme levels, 1,000,000 WT HEK293 Flp-InTM TREx or WT HEPG2 cells were seeded into six-well plates (for the HEK293 cells previously coated with poly-l-lysine) treated with 20 and 40 μM MB-6 (solved in DMSO) or DMSO for 20 hours, respectively. Heme levels were measured as described above.

CPOX in vitro enzyme activity

Δ110 CPOX WT and Δ110 CPOX C164,304A were purified as described above. Previously, COPIII-tetramethyl ester (Merck) was converted to COPIII by acid methyl ester hydrolysis using 8 M HCl, and the aqueous solvent was removed by evaporation. The remaining COPIII was solved in 50 mM KOH and stored at −80°C. The complete conversion to COPIII was monitored using HPLC-MS. On the day of the experiment, COPIII was reduced to COPgenIII by treatment with sodium amalgam (1 mg/μl). After complete reduction (visible in a color change from pink to colorless), the COPgenIII solution was adjusted to a neutral pH using 1 M HCl (89). All solutions (with the exception of the purified proteins) were flushed with N2 to remove all oxygen to prevent the autoxidation of COPgenIII to COPIII, what would prevent catalytic turnover by CPOX. In addition, the reaction was performed at 37°C under hypoxic conditions (O2 < 0.5%). To start the reaction, 30 μM COPgenIII was added to different concentrations of purified protein (0, 0.5, 2, and 5 μM). The reaction was stopped after 15 min by mixing the reaction mix 1:1 (v/v) with ice-cold methanol, and the samples were incubated for 1 hour at RT with light exposure to induce complete autoxidation of COPgenIII and PPgenIX to their detectable forms, COPIII and PPIX. Afterward, the precipitated enzymes were removed by centrifugation (20,000g for 20 min at 4°C), and the samples were analyzed by HPLC-MS. To determine CPOX activity, a dilution series of COPIII and PPIX standards was added to the analysis to determine total COPIII and PPIX concentrations in the samples. For the final analysis, the concentration of detected PPIX was divided by the residual COPIII concentration.

HPLC-MS analysis

Chromatography was performed on a Nexera XR 40 series HPLC (Shimadzu) using a Nucleodur Sphinx RP (3 μm, 150 mm by 2 mm) (Macherey Nagel). The column temperature was maintained at 40°C and the sample tray at 4°C. Samples (10 μl) were injected at a flow rate of 0.4 ml/min using 0.1% formic acid and acetonitrile as mobile phases A and B, respectively. Metabolites were eluted using the 20-min gradient profile 0 min, 2% B; 0 to 2 min, 2% B; 2 to 9 min, 100% B; 9 to 13 min 100% B; 13 to 13.1 min, 2% B. The LCMS-8060 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer with electro spray ionization (Shimadzu) was operated in positive mode. Scheduled multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) was used to monitor analyte parent ion to product ion formation. MRM conditions were optimized using authentic standard chemicals including the following: Coproporphyrin III [(M + H) 655.10 > 596.20, 655.10 > 537.25, 655.10 > 523.20], Coproporphyrin III tetramethyl ester [(M + H) 711.20 > 565.30, 711.20 > 551.25, 711.20 > 638.20], and PPIX [(M + H) 563.10 > 504.15, 563.10 > 445.15, 563.10 > 431.25]. Both Q1 and Q3 quadrupoles were maintained in unit resolution. LabSolutions LCMS v5.118 software was used for data acquisition and LabSolutions Postrun for processing (both Shimadzu). Metabolites were quantified by scheduled MRM peak integration.

Porphyrin loading of purified proteins

After successful purification of the heterologously expressed proteins, a ultraviolet (UV)/visible (vis) spectrum was measured using a UV/vis spectrophotometer (JASCO). After a typical porphyrin spectrum was found inside the measured protein spectrum of CPOX, the porphyrin was released from the enzyme by 1:1 (v/v) methanol treatment and identified by HPLC-MS analysis. In addition, Δ110 CPOX WT was incubated for 20 hours in the presence of twice the molar amount of either COPIII or PPIX (previously solved in 50 mM KOH) or protein storage buffer [200 mM NaCl and 20 mM tris (pH = 7.4)], and as control, a control Δ80 ALR was incubated with PPIX or buffer. After incubation, all unbound porphyrins were removed from the solution using a PD-10 desalting column. To make sure that no unbound porphyrins were collected in the protein fraction of the size exclusion chromatography, all samples were loaded onto a 10-kDa molecular weight cutoff spinning filter (Amicon), and the flow-through was checked for the presence of any porphyrins by UV/vis spectroscopy (no porphyrin absorbance spectra were observed). All protein samples were then analyzed by UV/vis spectroscopy. The absorbance value of the maximum of the protein peak at around 280 nm was set to 1.0 for all the spectra.

Cell death assay

All cell lines were induced with DOX 2 days before seeding. For each cell line, six replicates of 20,000 cells were transferred to a 96-well plate (previously coated with poly-l-lysine) and induced again with DOX. The next day, the FCS-containing medium was removed, and 100 μl of FCS-free medium was added with the respective amounts of 5-ALA and DOX. DRAQ7 dye was added according to the manufacturer’s instructions to measure cell death. Fluorescence emission was detected every hour for 48 hours in an InCucyte live-cell imager. DRAQ7-positive cells were quantified relative to the total cell count in each condition. Results were normalized by defining the total cell number per condition as 100%.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The intensity of immunoblot signals was quantified using Image Lab (Bio-Rad). The intensity of autoradiography signals was quantified using Fiji/ImageJ. To statistically compare levels of different CPOX variants or CPOX level at different time points, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post hoc Tukey test was performed, setting the level of significance to P < 0.05. To statistically compare heme levels between two cell lines or treated and untreated cells, an unpaired t test was performed, setting the level of significance to P < 0.05. The number of experiments is reported in the figure legend.
