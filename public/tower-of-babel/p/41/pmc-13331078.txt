Work engagement profiles of rural nurses: a multicentre cross-sectional study
Authors: You Liu, Huayong Huang, Ershan Xu, Yanhui Zhou, Wen Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundExisting research on work engagement among rural nurses is relatively limited. Based on the job demands-resources (JD-R) model, this study aims to identify profiles of work engagement among rural nurses, explore associated factors and derive actionable policy implications.MethodsA multicentre cross-sectional study was conducted on 848 rural nurses in Hunan from June to August 2024. The survey used self-administered general information questionnaires and work engagement scales. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was used to identify latent profiles of work engagement. Multinomial logistic regression analysis was used to analyse the associated factors on the different profiles.ResultsAmong the respondents, 50.9% had an educational attainment of college degree or below, 16.0% received a monthly income of less than 3000 CNY and 26.7% engaged in more than five night shifts per month. Work engagement of rural nurses could be classified into three profiles: low (n=99, 15.8%), moderate (n=410, 66.6%) and high (n=116, 18.6%). Multinomial logistic regression revealed that marital status, number of children, night shifts and leadership position were significantly associated factors of work engagement across different profiles.ConclusionUsing latent profile analysis, this study identified three distinct work engagement profiles among rural nurses—low, moderate and high—with the moderate group being the most prevalent. It confirms the applicability of the JD-R model in explaining work engagement among rural nurses while identifying context-specific associated factors—particularly marital status, family structure, leadership role occupancy and night shift intensity. These findings suggest three actionable policies: adjusting night shift allocation based on family structure, offering leadership resilience training for nurses and incorporating marital/family status into retention planning.Keywords: China, Health Workforce, Nurses, Organisation and Administration

Introduction

In China, nurses working in rural healthcare institutions serve as essential components of the rural health service system. The seventh national census revealed that the permanent population at the county-level and below amounts to approximately 748 million people, with an average county population of 399,000, representing over 51% of the total national population.1 This indicates that hospitals at the county level and below must oversee a substantial network of grassroots medical and health institutions and provide medical treatment and health management for more than half of the population. Although medical service resources have expanded continuously, the supply remains relatively insufficient compared with escalating demands and the substantial population size.2,5 Rural nurses are operationally defined as nursing professionals employed in healthcare institutions at or below the county level in China, including county hospitals, township health centres and village clinics, consistent with the National Bureau of Statistics’ classification of rural areas. Owing to the challenging working conditions prevalent in rural hospitals—including staffing shortages, inadequate equipment, restricted training opportunities and comparatively lower salaries—the nursing workforce currently faces significant challenges, such as diminished work motivation, unmet professional needs and severe personnel turnover, all of which critically undermine the stability and development of rural nursing teams.6,8 Work engagement denotes an individual’s demonstrated dedication, vigour and commitment within their occupational role, representing a pivotal metric for assessing employee attitudes and productivity.9 Research has established a robust correlation between nurses’ work engagement and both patient satisfaction and overall hospital service quality.10 Elevated levels of work engagement have been shown to enhance nurses’ well-being and job satisfaction, reduce turnover intentions and improve the quality of nursing care delivered.11 12

However, existing research on work engagement has predominantly concentrated on nurses in urban hospitals, where resources are relatively abundant,13 14 very few specialised investigations targeting rural nurses specifically. Work engagement has traditionally been assessed using variable-centred approaches, wherein total scores are used to reflect individuals’ overall engagement status. For instance, Tian et al15 and Maung et al16 employed the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES) to evaluate nurses’ work engagement, with higher scores indicating more favourable engagement profiles. Although this method enables multidimensional assessment of overall work engagement, it fails to capture individual heterogeneity in how nurses experience and manifest engagement. In contrast, latent profile analysis (LPA) represents a person-centred methodological approach that identifies homogeneous subgroups of individuals based on their response patterns across measured indicators.17 By focusing on latent subgroup characteristics, LPA can uncover the diverse manifestations of work engagement among nurses. The identification of these heterogeneous profiles provides robust empirical evidence for developing tailored intervention strategies and targeted management policies, thereby facilitating precision in human resource management within rural healthcare settings.

The job demands-resources (JD-R) model posits that work characteristics can be classified into two distinct dimensions: job demands (aspects requiring sustained physical, psychological or organisational effort) and job resources (functional means facilitating goal attainment and personal growth).18 These dimensions operate through divergent mechanisms: excessive job demands initiate health-impairment processes culminating in exhaustion, whereas abundant job resources activate motivational processes fostering work engagement. The present study applies the JD-R model to this understudied population, using work engagement—a positive, fulfilling state characterised by vigour, dedication and absorption—as the central outcome. Employing LPA, we identify heterogeneous demand-resource configurations among rural nurses, which manifest as distinct latent profiles of work engagement. We then examine the characteristics and associated factors of these profiles while controlling for demographic and occupational covariates (hospital level, professional title, shift frequency, leadership role and income). This analytical approach provides empirical evidence for tailored interventions aimed at enhancing rural nurses’ work engagement and care quality, thereby strengthening the sustainability of the rural nursing workforce.

Methods

Design and participants

A multicentre cross-sectional investigation was conducted using convenience sampling to recruit nursing professionals from three rural hospitals in Hunan Province, China, between June and August 2024. The sample comprised one primary-level hospital, one secondary-level hospital and one tertiary-level hospital, ensuring representation across the rural hospital hierarchy.

Inclusion criteria specified registered nurses with at least 1 year of continuous employment who voluntarily consented to participate. Exclusion criteria encompassed intern nurses, participants enrolled in hospital-sponsored advanced training programmes and individuals absent during the data collection period due to medical leave, maternity leave or retirement.

Samples

Sample size was determined based on methodological guidelines for LPA. Simulation studies suggest that n≥300 represents the minimum threshold for ensuring basic stability of parameter estimates, whereas n≥500 is recommended for exploratory analyses aiming to identify between two and four latent classes.19 In the present study, 848 questionnaires were distributed, yielding 625 valid responses (effective response rate: 73.7%). This sample size exceeds recommended thresholds and is therefore considered adequate for robust LPA estimation. The survey comprised 28 items (13 from the Demographic and occupational characteristics questionnaire and 15 from the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale), but sample size justification followed LPA-specific guidelines rather than item-based rules.

Data collection

We created an electronic questionnaire using the Wenjuanxing platform (Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Co., Ltd., Changsha, China). The first page of the questionnaire explained the survey’s purpose, significance and instructions for completion; participants provided informed consent before proceeding. The questionnaire contained no personally identifiable information. The principal investigator communicated with nursing administrators and department managers at the three hospitals to explain the study objectives and procedures on obtaining institutional approval, and we distributed the anonymous online questionnaire via WeChat (Tencent Holdings Ltd., Shenzhen, China). Survey links were initially sent to head nurses, who disseminated them to rural nurses during non-working hours. Technical safeguards restricted responses to one submission per device and IP address, and mandatory completion of all items was required before final submission. Quality control measures excluded questionnaires with completion times under 3 min or those exhibiting patterned response tendencies (eg, straight-lining or zigzag patterns). Between June and August 2024, 848 questionnaires were disseminated, yielding 625 valid responses.

Variables and measures

Demographic and occupational characteristics questionnaire

This study employed a self-designed general information questionnaire, which was developed following a review of pertinent literature.20,25 This instrument captured demographic and occupational characteristics, including hospital level, gender, age, education, years of nursing experience, professional title, marital status, department, monthly night shift frequency, number of children, employment status, leadership roles and monthly income.

Utrecht work engagement scale (UWES）

Work engagement was assessed using the UWES, developed by Schaufeli et al26 and validated in Chinese populations by Zhang et al,27 demonstrating robust psychometric properties. The 15-item scale comprises three dimensions: vigour (six items), dedication (four items) and absorption (five items). Responses are recorded on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 7 (“always”), with higher scores indicating greater work engagement. In the present study, the UWES demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency among rural nurses, with a Cronbach’s coefficient of 0.870.

Data analysis

Prior to conducting LPA, no standardisation (eg, conversion to z-scores) or normalisation transformations were applied to the continuous variables under investigation. All analyses were performed using variables in their original metric. LPA was implemented using Mplus V. 8.3 (Muthén & Muthén, Los Angeles, CA, USA) to identify latent profiles of work engagement among rural nurses, with model fit indices guiding the determination of the optimal number of classes.28 29 Evaluative metrics included the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample-size-adjusted BIC (ssaBIC), entropy, Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT) and bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT). Model selection criteria were operationalised as follows: superior model fit is indicated by lower values of AIC, BIC and ssaBIC coupled with higher entropy approaching 1.0; statistically significant LMR-LRT and BLRT results (p<0.05) indicate that the k-class model provides improved fit compared with the (k-1)-class alternative. Based on these parameters, the optimal model was selected to categorise rural nurses into distinct work engagement profiles. The theoretical foundation of LPA posits that heterogeneity in response distributions across observed indicators can be attributed to a finite mixture of mutually exclusive latent classes, each characterised by distinct within-class response patterns on the measured variables.30

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, V. 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were characterised by means and SD. The χ2 test was employed to examine differences in work engagement profiles across demographic and occupational characteristics. Multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed to identify associated factors of latent class membership. Variables demonstrating statistical significance in univariate analyses were entered as independent variables, with LPA-derived work engagement profiles serving as the nominal dependent variable. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Given the reliance on self-reported data, we assessed the potential for common method bias using Harman’s single-factor test. Exploratory factor analysis without rotation extracted six factors with eigenvalues exceeding 1.0; the first factor accounted for 32.551% of the total variance, below the 40% threshold commonly used to indicate problematic method bias. These results suggest that common method variance is unlikely to threaten the validity of our findings.

LPA of work engagement

Table 2 presents model fit comparisons for LPA solutions specifying one through five classes. Based on comprehensive evaluation of statistical and substantive criteria, the three-profile solution was selected as optimal. Statistically, this model demonstrated substantially lower AIC, BIC and aBIC values compared with the two-profile solution, accompanied by significant LMR-LRT and BLRT results (p<0.05) and high classification accuracy (entropy=0.950). Although the four-profile and five-profile solutions yielded marginally improved information criteria, the LMR-LRT indicated non-significant improvement over the three-class model, and both solutions produced small auxiliary classes (eg, 3.7% of the sample in the five-profile solution), compromising statistical stability and substantive interpretability. The three-profile solution demonstrated balanced class proportions (15.8%, 66.6% and 18.6%), with no class comprising less than 5% of the sample, thereby ensuring adequate statistical power and theoretical meaningfulness while adhering to the principle of parsimony. Collectively, these considerations—encompassing model fit, classification clarity and parsimony—supported the selection of the three-profile solution.

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression results for associated factors on the three-class pattern of work engagement

Online supplemental table S1 presents the results of univariate analyses examining associations between demographic and occupational characteristics and work engagement profiles. To minimise the risk of omitting important confounders and to enhance model accuracy and robustness, variables demonstrating statistical significance at the p < 0.10 level in univariate analyses were entered as independent variables in the multinomial logistic regression model, with latent class membership serving as the dependent variable. Table 5 presents the results of the multinomial logistic regression analysis associated with profile membership. Marital status emerged as a significant associated factor of work engagement profiles among rural nurses. Compared with their single counterparts, married nurses demonstrated significantly higher odds of belonging to the high engagement profile (OR=4.849, 95% CI 1.410 to 16.674, p=0.012). The number of children was also identified as a significant associated factor. Nurses with one to two children exhibited significantly higher odds of low work engagement relative to those with more than two children (OR=0.182, 95% CI 0.038 to 0.875, p=0.033). Night shift frequency was additionally associated with profile membership. Nurses working ≤4 night shifts per month demonstrated higher odds of high engagement compared with those working ≥5 night shifts monthly (OR=2.105, 95% CI 1.038 to 4.270, p=0.039). Finally, holding a leadership position was significantly associated with membership in the high engagement profile (OR = 5.185, 95% CI 1.045 to 25.744, p = 0.044).

Discussion

This study revealed that among the 625 surveyed rural nurses, 50.9% had educational attainment at the college degree level or below, 16.0% reported monthly incomes below 3,000 RMB (approximately 420 USD), 26.7% worked more than five night shifts monthly and only 30.1% held formal employment contracts. These findings are not merely descriptive; they identify specific job resource deficits (low education and informal contracts) and elevated job demands (night shifts and low income) that, according to the JD-R model, jointly threaten work engagement. For instance, low educational attainment limits career development resources, while informal employment status erodes job security—both representing deficiencies in organisational resources that can activate the health-impairment pathway. Accordingly, we propose a tiered intervention strategy. First, collaborative partnerships between rural hospitals and higher education or continuing education institutions should establish accessible pathways for academic advancement and skills training. Targeted programmes addressing the specific competency gaps of nurses with lower educational attainment—particularly in emergency care, chronic disease management and digital health technologies—could enhance both individual career trajectories and service quality. Second, nursing administrators must optimise human resource allocation to reduce night shift frequency. The interventions should include structured stress management programmes, circadian rhythm optimisation guidance and regular health monitoring. Third, institutional reforms should prioritise standardised contract management to ensure legal protection of nurses’ rights and benefits. Concurrently, team-building initiatives and transparent career ladder programmes—emphasising progression from informal to formal employment status—can foster organisational belonging. Establishing performance-based compensation frameworks that narrow the remuneration gap between contract and permanent staff represents a critical equity intervention.31 32 Fourth, and fundamentally, sustained governmental fiscal commitment specifically earmarked for county-level and below healthcare facilities is imperative. Directed investments in competitive salaries and improved working conditions constitute the structural foundation for attracting and retaining nursing talent in rural China, thereby ensuring the sustainability of primary healthcare delivery for over 700 million rural residents.

This study represents the first systematic investigation of work engagement profiles among rural Chinese nurses using LPA. Our findings reveal a heterogeneous distribution of work engagement characterised by three distinct profiles: low work engagement (15.8%), moderate work engagement (66.6%), and high work engagement (18.6%). The identification of distinct engagement profiles supports the JD-R proposition that the balance between job demands and resources determines differential motivational outcomes. A comparative analysis across the three profiles reveals meaningful patterns: the low-engagement profile (15.8%) is disproportionately characterised by nurses with one to two children, frequent night shifts (>5/month) and non-leadership positions—suggesting a demand-rich, resource-poor configuration. In contrast, the high-engagement profile (18.6%) is more likely to include married nurses, those in leadership roles and those with ≤4 night shifts monthly—indicating a resource-rich configuration that buffers against demands. The moderate-engagement profile (66.6%), while demographically intermediate, may represent a resource-constrained equilibrium where demands and resources are roughly balanced but insufficient to achieve vigour and absorption. These comparative findings inform a tiered management strategy. First, the 15.8% low-engagement subgroup constitutes a high-priority population requiring immediate targeted support. Second, the predominance of moderate work engagement (66.6%) suggests that while most rural nurses maintain baseline professional commitment despite systemic challenges, they fall short of the optimal state characterised by simultaneous vigour, dedication and absorption. This moderate engagement profile, though preferable to disengagement, may represent a subclinical risk state vulnerable to deterioration under sustained job demands.33 For this majority group, boosting interventions—such as peer support groups, digital health competency training and flexible scheduling—can provide additional job resources to transition them into the high-engagement profile. The 18.6% prevalence of high work engagement is notably lower than the 25.9% reported in urban Chinese hospital samples,34 corroborating concerns regarding rural healthcare workforce sustainability. For this high-engagement minority, sustainability measures (eg, regular off-duty periods, recognition programmes, leadership development) are essential to prevent burnout and retain this valuable human resource.

The present findings should be interpreted within the ongoing rural healthcare reform context in China, particularly the ‘Thousand County Project’ aimed at improving comprehensive service capacity at county-level hospitals. If effectively implemented, this policy could directly address several job resource deficits identified in our study. For example, investments in medical equipment, digital health infrastructure and specialised training programmes would enhance professional development resources, potentially shifting nurses from moderate to high engagement profiles. Conversely, if policy implementation is uneven or adds administrative burdens (eg, increased documentation requirements), it could inadvertently raise job demands. Our identified profiles—15.8% low, 66.6% moderate and 18.6% high—serve as a baseline for future longitudinal evaluations of policy impacts on nurse work engagement. Policy makers should consider complementing infrastructure investments with workforce-specific interventions, such as night shift caps (≤4 per month) and family-supportive policies, to maximise the motivational benefits of the ‘Thousand County Project’ and ensure the sustainability of rural primary care delivery.

Our results provide partial validation of the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model within an understudied population. The protective effect of marriage on high engagement (OR=4.849) aligns with the JD-R model’s emphasis on non-work resources as buffers against job demands,35 demonstrating that familial support structures may compensate for organisational resource deficits prevalent in rural hospitals. Conversely, the deleterious effect of having one to two children (vs>2) on engagement appears counterintuitive. To empirically ground this finding using our own data, we conducted post-hoc exploratory comparisons between nurses with one to two children (n=504) and those with >2 children (n=38) on marital status. We observed that nurses with >2 children were all married, were significantly more likely to the rural nurses of one to two children (100% vs 96.2%, p<0.05). Future research should adopt a mixed-methods approach to further investigate this paradox. First, longitudinal cohort studies are needed to examine whether the birth of a third child (transitioning from 1 to 2 to>2 children) is temporally associated with changes in work engagement levels. Second, qualitative interviews with rural nurses in different family contexts could elucidate the specific economic, social, and psychological mechanisms (eg, perceived family support, financial strain and childcare burden) underlying this counterintuitive association. Therefore, nursing administrators should recognise and leverage familial support systems in workforce management. For unmarried nurses, customised interventions should be designed to create more supportive work environments that acknowledge and reward their contributions.36 Establishing regular feedback mechanisms enables administrators to understand subordinates’ needs and concerns, facilitating timely adjustments and improvements to enhance rural nurses’ work engagement.37

Night shift frequency emerged as a modifiable demand factor, with ≤4 night shifts per month being associated with high engagement (OR=2.105). This threshold effect suggests that the JD-R health-impairment process may activate beyond a specific intensity of circadian disruption, consistent with occupational health research on shift work tolerance.38 Frequent night shift work results in insufficient rest, chronic fatigue and compromised work performance.39 40 Nursing administrators must alleviate workload burdens through optimised scheduling and adequate staffing.41 Additionally, sleep quality improvement initiatives should be implemented for rural nurses.42 Given the modifiable nature of night shift scheduling, administrators should consider implementing a maximum threshold of ≤4 night shifts monthly as a cost-effective organisational intervention.

The significant association between leadership roles and high engagement (OR=5.185) supports the JD-R proposition that job resources—including autonomy, professional development opportunities, and social status—foster motivational processes. This finding is consistent with Chen et al.’s43 investigation of 585 hemodialysis nurses.

Therefore, nursing administrators should prioritise role empowerment by involving rural nurses in departmental decision-making processes, thereby stimulating initiative and professional accountability to enhance engagement.44 Furthermore, targeted career development support should be provided, including specialised training programmes, mentorship opportunities, and established advancement pathways within the rural healthcare system.45

Limitations

This study has several limitations warranting consideration.

First, the study sample was drawn exclusively from three hospitals in Hunan Province, which may not represent the full socioeconomic and healthcare diversity of rural China (eg, coastal regions with higher resource allocation vs underdeveloped western regions). Therefore, the generalisability of our findings to other rural Chinese regions with different economic development levels or ethnic compositions is limited. More importantly, because LPA relies on the sample’s heterogeneity to identify distinct engagement subgroups, convenience sampling may introduce selection bias that either underestimates or overestimates certain profiles, potentially affecting the stability and replicability of the profile solution. Specifically, the convenience sampling method may have led to over-representation of hospitals and nurses with higher cooperation levels while under-representing those with extremely low work engagement or resistance to participating in surveys. This selection bias could further affect the generalisability of the identified profiles. Future studies should employ stratified random sampling across diverse rural settings across multiple provinces or consider quota sampling to ensure representativeness across different engagement levels and validate our results.

Second, the response rate was 73.7% (625 valid questionnaires out of 848 distributed). While acceptable, this rate is below 80%, raising the possibility of non-response bias. Nurses who declined to participate may have different work engagement levels or demand-resource configurations, potentially influencing the number and characteristics of the identified latent profiles.

Third, due to limited sample sizes within certain subgroups (eg, specific hospital levels, gender groups), we did not conduct formal multi-group LPA to test measurement or structural invariance. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples should assess profile stability across different demographic and occupational subgroups.

Fourth, while LPA provides robust classification, the method assumes population homogeneity in response patterns; unobserved heterogeneity may exist within the identified profiles. Future research should employ longitudinal designs to examine profile transitions over time, particularly in response to policy interventions.

Fifth, the exclusive reliance on self-reported measures (eg, UWES) may introduce common method bias and social desirability bias, potentially inflating observed associations. Moreover, confirmatory factor analysis of the UWES in our sample revealed suboptimal fit (RMSEA=0.122, CFI=0.886, TLI=0.862), which may be attributable to the unique rural Chinese context (eg, high workload, resource scarcity and cultural factors) altering inter-item relationships. Nevertheless, the UWES total score has been extensively validated as a reliable unidimensional measure of work engagement across diverse populations, and our primary objective was to use the total score as a composite indicator for LPA rather than to validate the factor structure. Therefore, we retained the total score without further modifications to improve fit. Future studies should consider contextual adaptation of the UWES for rural Chinese nurses and incorporate objective indicators (eg, supervisor ratings, patient satisfaction scores or actual turnover records).

Sixth, although we controlled for several demographic and occupational covariates (hospital level, professional title, shift frequency, leadership role and income), we cannot rule out residual confounding from unmeasured variables such as personality traits (eg, resilience), family support quality or chronic health conditions.

Seventh, our sample comprised nurses clustered within three hospitals, violating the independence assumption of conventional regression models. However, due to the small number of clusters (k=3), multilevel modelling for LPA was not feasible. Future studies with larger samples of hospitals should employ multilevel LPA to account for hospital-level variance (eg, management styles and resource allocation policies) that may influence profile membership.

Conclusion

Using LPA, this study identified three distinct work engagement profiles among rural nurses—low, moderate and high—with the moderate group being the most prevalent. It confirms the applicability of the JD-R model in explaining work engagement among rural nurses while identifying context-specific associated factors—particularly marital status, family structure, leadership role occupancy and night shift intensity. These findings suggest three actionable policies: adjusting night shift allocation based on family structure, offering leadership resilience training for nurses and incorporating marital/family status into retention planning.
