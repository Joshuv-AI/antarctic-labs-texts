Assessing the feasibility of collective licensing of in-copyright works as training data for generative AI systems
Authors: Pamela Samuelson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Copyright owners have sued several developers of large-scale generative AI systems for copyright infringement because of their uses of massive quantities of in-copyright works as training data for building AI models. Fair use will be the main defense against these charges. If fair use defenses succeed, developers will be free to continue to commercially exploit models already built on copyrighted data as well as to use these data to train new models or fine-tune existing ones. If copyright owners prevail, developers may be liable for billions of dollars of damages. Developers could also be enjoined from further model development on in-copyright works and even ordered to destroy models trained on infringing works. Numerous commentators have proposed collective licensing as a compromise solution to the copyright-training-data dilemma. Other commentators have questioned the feasibility of such a compromise. This article discusses several proposals for collective licensing to enable development of generative AI systems while providing some compensation to copyright owners. It assesses the complex normative, economic, and practical problems that must be addressed if such a regime is to become feasible. It discusses the implications of a licensing mandate not only for the large firms whose models are widely used today, but also for start-ups, research centers, and higher education developers of generative AI systems, as well as the general public.

One of the most pressing legal questions regarding generative AI is whether, and to what extent, the use of in-copyright works as training data for developing large-scale AI models does or does not require copyright owners’ consent or compensation. This is the subject of multiple ongoing lawsuits between copyright owners and AI developers. While a definitive ruling in the courts may be some years off, various proposals for the establishment of a collective licensing system for training data have attracted some attention. The feasibility of such a system, and its possible ramifications, should be carefully considered before proceeding.

Background on Copyright and the Generative AI Lawsuits

Many developers of generative AI systems have trained foundation models on datasets that include massive quantities of Internet content (1).* While some may use their own webcrawlers to gather Internet data, AI developers often get copies of Common Crawl’s datasets of an estimated 9.5 petabytes which this nonprofit organization makes available to all comers without charge (2).

While some content widely available on the Internet is in the public domain (that is, free from copyright constraints), most such content is in-copyright and will be in-copyright for decades to come. This is true not only for commercially disseminated works, but also for user-generated and open source content widely available on the Internet. Copyright protection attaches to original works of authorship from the moment of their creation and generally lasts for seven decades after the deaths of their authors. While most works posted on the Internet are there with right-holders’ permission, it is well known that large quantities of Internet contents were posted without permission and infringe copyrights.

The first lawsuit to charge a generative AI developer with copyright infringement based on the developer’s use of in-copyright works as training data was brought against Stability AI in January 2023 by Sarah Andersen, on behalf of a class of visual artists whose works were used to train a model for Stability’s generative AI system Stable Diffusion (3). Since then, more than four dozen other lawsuits have challenged the legality of using in-copyright works as training data for generative AI models (4).† OpenAI, Meta, Google, and Anthropic are among the generative AI model developers who have raised fair use defenses for using in-copyright works as training data.‡

Fair uses of in-copyright works are not infringements. The U.S. copyright statute (6) directs consideration of four factors in assessing whether a second comer’s use of a first author’s work is fair or unfair: 1) the purpose and character of the challenged use; 2) the nature of the copyrighted work; 3) the amount and substantiality of the taking; and 4) the effect of the challenged use on the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work (7). Several purposes tend to favor fair use defenses: criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, and research. Whether the use is commercial or noncommercial is also considered in fair use cases.

Since the U.S. Supreme Court’s 1994 ruling in Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc. (8), in which the Court considered whether a rap parody of a popular song was a fair use or infringement, it has mattered whether the putative fair user’s purpose in using the first author’s work was “transformative,” which is tested based mainly on the similarity or difference in the two works’ purposes. The more transformative (or different) the second comer’s purpose is from the first author’s purpose, the less likely it is that the second author’s use of the first author’s work will serve as a market substitute for that author’s work.

Plaintiffs in the generative AI copyright cases argue (e.g., ref. 9) that training data uses of their works are unfair for the following reasons. First, the developers’ purposes are commercial and outputs of generative AI unfairly compete with the plaintiffs’ works. Second, the developers’ use of high quality inputs, such as the plaintiffs’ works, is the reason that the defendants’ generative AI models can produce high quality outputs. Third, the entirety of works in the dataset are copied in the training process, which disfavors fair use defenses. Fourth, the outputs of generative AI systems may have substitutive effects on markets for the plaintiffs’ works and harm the plaintiffs by depriving them of license revenues.

AI developers, by contrast, argue (e.g., ref. 10) that their uses of works as training data is fair. First, their uses of plaintiffs’ works are for very different purposes than the authors’ original purposes in creating them, hence the defendants’ purposes are transformative. Second, training uses the works as data, not as works. That is, training does not consume the works for their expressiveness, but only processes and analyzes their parts (such as words) in relation to one another. Moreover, the public availability of works on the Internet tips in favor of fairness. Third, whole works may be copied, but this is an intermediate step in building models, which do not embody the works’ expression in any recognizable form. Fourth, developers contend that transaction costs of negotiating individual permissions for uses of the plaintiffs’ works cannot feasibly be obtained. There is consequently no cognizable harm to the plaintiffs’ markets.

Some commentators are persuaded by the generative AI developers’ fair use defenses (e.g., refs. 11 and 12), while others think the training-data uses of works are unfair (e.g., refs. 13 and 14). A definitive ruling on the generative AI training-data fair use defenses in the United States is likely some years off.

The next part reviews three ways that European scholars have suggested that collective licensing of in-copyright works for generative AI systems may be established. It then discusses the perceived feasibility of collective licensing of in-copyright works as training data in the United States. Finally, it discusses some considerations that courts and policymakers should bear in mind as they address generative AI copyright issues.

European Scholars on Collective Licensing

Paying some attention to European proposals about collective licensing to address generative AI-related uses of in-copyright works is worthwhile for three reasons. First, their proponents aim to make American and other non-EU model developers pay these license fees. Second, EU policymakers aim to influence policies of other nations to follow EU AI-related initiatives. Third, the European proposals reflect a different perspective on collective licensing than U.S. proposals.

To set the stage for analyzing European scholars’ collective licensing proposals, some background about relevant EU laws is necessary. One such law is the European Directive on Copyright and Related Rights in the Digital Single Market (CDSMD) (15) which became effective in 2019. That directive contains two provisions requiring EU member states to adopt new exceptions in their copyright laws to authorize copying of in-copyright works for purposes of text- and data-mining (TDM), which EU officials recognize would be socially beneficial (15, Recital 5).

Article 3(1) of the CDSMD authorizes nonprofit scientific research and cultural heritage institutions to engage in TDM on lawfully acquired copies of in-copyright works for scientific and research purposes. Article 4(1) directs member states to create a second copyright exception to authorize other entities to engage in TDM, although Article 4(3) gives copyright owners the right to opt-out of the Article 4(1) TDM privilege. In contrast, Article 7(1) of the CDSMD provides that any attempt to override the scientific and cultural heritage TDM privilege by contract will be unenforceable.

A second relevant EU law is the AI Act (16). While the AI Act focuses primarily on regulation of AI risks and safety, it includes important rules about copyright obligations of developers of generative AI models (which the AI Act calls “general purpose AI” or GPAI) (see e.g., ref. 17). Article 53(1)(c) of the AI Act requires GPAI developers to adopt policies about their compliance with EU copyright laws and to articulate how they intend to enable copyright owners to opt out through state-of-the-art technologies of the TDM privilege established by Article 4(1) of the CDSDM.

Article 53(1)(d) of the AI Act further requires GPAI model developers to prepare and disclose a summary of the data on which their model was trained in accordance with a template published by the EU’s AI Office (18). This information may be useful when assessing whether the GPAI developer’s access to training data was lawful. It may also help copyright owners determine whether their works were used as training data which, in turn, may inform their decisions about whether to opt out of Article 4(1)’s TDM privilege.

Recital 106 of the AI Act states that any commercial GPAI provider who markets a model in the EU must have complied with EU copyright law and the CDSMD’s TDM opt-out obligation “regardless of the jurisdiction in which the copyright-relevant acts underpinning the training of those general-purpose AI models take place.” It explains that this measure is “necessary to ensure a level playing field among providers of general-purpose AI models where no provider should be able to gain a competitive advantage in the Union market by applying lower copyright standards than those provided in the Union.”

Although recitals of EU regulations do not set forth binding rules, they may inform interpretations of the AI Act. Recital 106 signals that its drafters think that GPAI developers should ensure their models’ development processes comply with EU copyright law, even if the model’s development was lawful under the national law where the models were trained. In this way, the AI Act aims to achieve a “Brussels effect,” that is, to persuade AI developers to comply with EU copyright standards and to thwart regulatory arbitrage (see ref. 19). There is a lively debate in the United States and EU about whether the EU can achieve this kind of extraterritorial reach of its laws through a recital in the AI Act (see e.g., refs. 20 and 21).

Neither the CDSMD nor the AI Act mentions collective licensing. This has not dissuaded European copyright scholars from endorsing the idea of a collective licensing regime under which generative AI developers would be obliged to provide right-holders with remuneration for AI-related uses of in-copyright materials (22; see also refs. 23 and 24). These scholars recognize the societal value of generative AI systems, but are concerned that generative AI is going to displace job opportunities for authors, artists, and other creators. These collective licensing proposals also reflect a sense of fairness to creators for the uses that generative AI model developers are making of their works. The next three sections discuss ways that a collective licensing regime might arise. A fourth section considers some pros and cons of these proposals and their feasibility in the EU and potential implications for U.S. developers.

The Article 4(3) Opt-Out Privilege May Lead to Collective Licensing.

Even before the AI Act became effective, some AI developers were negotiating licensing deals with major European copyright industry players to use their content as training data. OpenAI, for instance, announced a deal in 2023 with Axel Springer to use this publishers’ works as training data (25). Other voluntary licensing agreements between AI developers and EU copyright owners have also been occurring (26, at 95–98).

Some EU collective management organizations (CMOs), which grant licenses on behalf of their members’ copyrights in specific types of works for uses within their mandates from their member right-holders, have announced their intent to exercise opt-out rights under Article 4(3) as to all works in their repertoires for TDM uses (27). The opt-out rule gives CMOs heightened bargaining power with AI developers to negotiate terms that would benefit their members. Voluntary collective licensing for training-data uses can occur through direct negotiations between AI developers and CMOs.

Spain proposed an extended collective licensing (ECL) regime to enable remuneration of copyright owners for generative AI training uses of in-copyright works (28). CMOs are generally authorized to grant licenses only as to copyright owner members’ works. An ECL regime would empower CMOs to grant licenses for uses of works of the types they administer, including works of nonmembers, as long as CMOs treat nonmembers fairly in the allocation of revenues collected by the CMO for right-holders (see e.g., ref. 29). Right-holders would need to opt-out of the CDSMD Article 4(1) TDM privilege to be eligible for compensation under the Spanish ECL proposal.

Statutory Licensing as an Alternative to CDSMD’s Opt-Out Rule.

Some European commentators who favor collective licensing for generative AI-related uses of in-copyright works think that the Commission should reopen the CDSMD to repeal the opt-out rule of Article 4(3) because it will deprive developers of AI models of access to the highest quality and quantity of inputs (30, 31). Large numbers of right-holder opt-outs would cause AI models to be more limited in capabilities than is socially desirable. These commentators endorse a “permitted-but-paid” (32) model for CMOs’ grants of licenses for uses of works as generative AI training data.

The biggest disadvantage of the CDSMD opt-out approach is that it may produce a lose-lose situation. When right-holders exercise opt-out rights under Article 4(3), commentators worry that licensing may not occur as to a great many works that would be excellent training data because negotiations for licensing after opt-outs would either not happen or fail (30, at 6; 33). The result would be that AI developers would lose the opportunity to train on large volumes of high-quality data and copyright owners would lose the opportunity to get some compensation for the AI developers’ new uses of their works.

Professors Christophe Geiger and Vincenzo Iaia suggest that the Commission should replace Article 4(3) with a limitation-based remuneration right provision. This new provision would allow commercial developers of generative AI systems to use all manner of works as training data if they provide adequate remuneration for the licensed uses (30, at 6).

Geiger and Iaia regard their proposal as a workable compromise: AI developers would have the right to use as much high-quality data as they need to develop high quality models. And copyright owners would benefit from compensation for the generative AI uses of their works. This proposal is in keeping with numerous other statutory license regimes adopted in the EU for socially beneficial uses of in-copyright works coupled with remuneration obligations, as well as being consistent with fundamental rights to science and culture and freedom of expression (30, at 6–7).

Professor Giancarlo Frosio has made a similar proposal (31). He favors a levy regime in which “statutory remunerations could be linked to specific categories of works or right-holders, based on an empirical review of the creative markets most significantly disrupted by AI-generated creativity” (31, at 83). This levy system should take account of generative AI’s market substitution effects on human authorship, consider the unique combinatorial nature of AI outputs, lower transaction costs compared to traditional copyright models, and support human author creativity which could then provide more data for further training of AI models. Yet, Frosio recommends that only those creators whose works are “significantly affected by AI could be entitled to these remunerations” (31, at 83). An assessment of impacts should be revisited over time because any generative AI impacts on creative markets are likely to evolve.

A Levy on Generative AI Commercial Products or Services.

Most European proponents of collective licensing for AI uses of in-copyright works focus on the training-data phase of generative AI development and anticipate that CMOs would provide fair remuneration to individuals for training uses of their data. Professor Martin Senftleben’s collective licensing proposal is markedly different than the training-data-oriented remuneration regimes because it would allow training to proceed without advance compensation, but would impose a levy on generative AI developers’ commercialization of their products and services (22, 34).

Senftleben agrees with Geiger, Iaia, and Frosio that Article 4(3)’s opt-out rule should be abandoned because of the likelihood of the lose-lose scenario (33). He argues that requiring remuneration at the training stage would be at odds with the societal goal of fostering the highest quality and unbiased AI systems trained on massive quantities of high quality works and promoting innovation in AI industries. Transaction costs of negotiating with copyright owners who opt-out would be very large and the opt-out rule would disadvantage small and medium-sized enterprises as well as individual authors because it would likely be only very large right-holders with whom AI developers would make deals. Senftleben also thinks a training-data-focused remuneration scheme may result in AI developers deciding against training their models on EU works, which would marginalize EU culture. He proposes instead a levy on commercialization of generative AI products and services in the EU to support human authorship (22, at 1549).

Because human authorship is so valuable to society, as well as to AI model developers who need new quantities of creative works on which to train models, Senftleben argues that a collective licensing regime is needed to support authors and their creative work. He conceptualizes this levy on commercial developers of generative AI products and services as a delayed way to compensate creators for uses of their works as training data (34, at 47–48).

Senftleben regards his proposal as a copyright-based solution, under which revenues generated by the levy would be administered by CMOs to support creators and their works. He characterizes CMOs as providing a “well-established infrastructure for the appropriate distribution of the monies collected,” and a more stable solution than a tax measure (22, at 1540). He theorizes that “AI remuneration could even be set at a level that would counterbalance lower production costs and enhance human authors’ chances of competing with generative AI systems” (22, at 1550). Senftleben expects that commercial AI model developers would pay the same lumpsum levy set by the relevant CMOs annually (35).

Assessing the European Collective Licensing Proposals.

European critics of the CDSMD opt-out rule under Article 4(3) are probably right that this privilege will likely be exercised by large right-holders who can use such leverage to negotiate deals with large commercial AI model developers. Individual authors are unlikely to benefit from such deals unless the European Commission decides to impose an obligation on right-holders to share with authors some of the revenues they receive from AI model developers for training-data uses of their works. The European Copyright Society, whose members are academics from various countries in Europe, has recently urged the Commission to ensure that authors receive a fair share of revenues that large right-holders have obtained through agreements with AI model developers (36).

Small and medium-sized right-holders are less likely than large right-holder groups to benefit from the opt-out privilege. If small or medium-sized right-holders opt out, AI developers may not seek them out for licensing deals, and even if they do, smaller players may not have as much leverage for negotiating deals. Startups are also unlikely to be able to negotiate comparable deals with large right-holders, which means the opt-out rule will have created a significant entry barrier at least for commercial model developers.

Professor João Pedro Quintais has succinctly observed that the opt-out rule “limits data quality and availability, increases costs, reduces competition, and slows innovation and productivity in generative AI, ultimately hampering economic growth” (20, at 13). The European scholars’ predictions that the opt-out privilege will impede access to the widest array of European works seem sound.

While a collective license solution to AI developers’ copyright problems is intuitively attractive, it would be daunting to devise a fair remuneration scheme, as Professor Frosio recognizes, given that all types of copyrighted work capable of being digitized are fodder for generative AI training (31, at 83). His suggestion that CMOs could tailor payouts based on empirical data about which sectors of creative workers are being displaced by generative AI may be a step in the right direction, but it too would be costly to administer and adjust over time. It would likely also lead to considerable disagreements about whether this approach would be an equitable resolution to copyright owner claims.

Another complication for collective licensing in the EU is, as Professor Senftleben has pointed out, the rights clearance infrastructure in the EU is highly fragmented (22, at 1546). One-stop-shopping for rights clearances for large volumes of EU works is virtually impossible (except for uses of musical compositions). Each of the 27 EU member states has its own national copyright law, only some parts of which are harmonized across the Union. EU-wide copyright directives have achieved only partial harmonization. Its member states sometimes enact variations even when implementing copyright rules required by directives. On many issues, the European Commission has not tried to achieve harmonization of copyright rules. The only way that generative AI developers can get EU-wide licenses is to negotiate with individual right-holders, but this must be done on a right-holder by right-holder basis.

A further complication with CMO collective licensing for training data is that CMOs are territorial to member states in which they are incorporated, and they typically represent only certain types of creators whose works are in the same sector (e.g., authors of music, literature, or photography) as to specified uses. AI model developers would have to negotiate with multiple CMOs to get blanket licenses to their repertoires. CMOs also typically grant licenses only for certain types of uses of those works. Professor Quintais has observed that it is far from clear whether CMOs currently have authorization to license TDM or AI training uses of works in their repertoires (20, at 14). If they do not have training-data rights at present, it may be difficult and costly to acquire those rights. Although CMOs may acquire rights, they generally do not have copies of works to license to AI developers.

Quintais suggests that CMOs may “underestimate the monumental scale, complexity and cost of the right clearance endeavor” for training-data uses (20, at 14). Each CMO’s dataset, however large, is “but a drop” in the “ocean of data” used to train models (20, at 14). Quintais notes that while CMOs often grant blanket licenses for uses of works in their repertoires, they may not know which of their members have opted out of the Article 4 TDM privilege, given that the terms of negotiated agreements between CMO members and generative AI developers may not be publicly available. This would obviously complicate assessment of the value of their repertoires for licensing TDM or AI training uses. He adds that because “EU law contains no compulsory mechanism of collective rights management for ‘training rights,’” it may be that “only voluntary forms of collective management are possible,” although Quintais recognizes that ECL might be possible (20, at 14).

Given these complexities, it is understandable that some European scholars propose trading the Article 4(3) opt-out right for a fair remuneration regime as a better solution to the training-data dilemma. Yet details are sparse about how they would design a fair remuneration regime, and those details matter. Would, for example, EU member state remuneration regimes cover only those in-copyright works created in their jurisdictions? Could EU works be segregated from works more broadly available on the Internet by the locations of data on European servers?

Professor Senftleben’s proposed levy on generative AI products and services has some advantages over training-data-based collective licensing options. It would support model developers’ uses of the widest range of inputs for training models, keep entry barriers lower than the opt-out regime, provide financial support for human creators and their works of authorship, and avoid expending the bulk of collected levies on CMO administration and payouts to individual creators.

Yet it too has some problematic features. Professor Quintais questions what distinct harm that Senftleben’s proposed levy would address, which “raises both conceptual problems (how to align the levy with infringement rules?) and practical ones (how to fairly calculate a levy for harm that cannot be determined?)” (20, at 16). That proposal “challenges the central normative justification for the AI levy: if the copyright harm cannot be identified, how can we establish the substitution effect of AI outputs on human-authored works?” (20, at 16) The levies recommended by Senftleben would be paid to CMOs, which he assumes they would use to support authors, artists, and other creators presumably through grants or other revenue-sharing arrangements (22, at 1550–1551). But how would the CMOs identify who was worthy of being supported and what process would CMOs undertake to ensure that the revenues were properly allocated? Senftleben does not address these questions.

Expectations among European collective licensing proponents seem to be high that authors and other right-holders would be able to get substantial revenues from training-data-based or AI-product-and-service-based remuneration schemes. At present, it is impossible to assess how much value particular inputs have contributed to models on which they were trained. Because of the massive size of training datasets and the inability to pinpoint value-added by particular inputs, it seems likely that the costs of administering the licenses may be very high in relation to payouts to millions of individual right-holders (20, at 14; 5, at 1565–1567).

The European Commission has not initiated a consultation about whether the EU should require GPAI developers to collectively license their uses of in-copyright works under Article 4 of the CDSMD. It is unclear whether it will do so, although Professor Alexander Peukert has stated that the AI Act’s “general copyright compliance obligation can function as the basis for additional duties concerning the training phase” and “is also flexible enough to cover future changes of EU copyright law, for example a codification of a mandatory AI output levy” (17, at 507).

For now, the most troubling feature of the EU AI-related copyright landscape is the AI Act’s Recital 106 which aims to require GPAI developers to comply with EU copyright rules, even if their uses of works as training data are lawful in the jurisdictions where models were trained. Leading developers of generative AI systems hope that U.S. courts will rule in favor of their fair use defenses, even if those uses would not be lawful under EU law.

U.S. Collective Licensing Proposals

Collective licensing has seldom been discussed in the United States as a solution to the AI training-data problem. The first section discusses several published statements that have endorsed collective licensing of in-copyright works as training data for AI models. The second section considers whether the class action lawsuits against generative AI developers for copyright infringement based on the unauthorized uses of works as training data may precipitate collective licensing as a solution. The third section reviews reasons to be skeptical about collective licensing as a compromise solution to the AI training-data dilemma.

Published Endorsements of AI Training-Data Collective Licensing.

U.S. copyright scholars have thus far expressed very little interest in collective licensing for AI-related uses of in-copyright works compared with European colleagues.§ A rare exception is an article by Benjamin Sobel, a scholar then-affiliated with Harvard’s Berkman Klein Center for Internet and Society, who in 2018 endorsed collective licensing as a reasonable compromise for the training-data copyright problem, after finding the arguments for and against fair use defenses unsatisfactory (37, at 79–89).

Sobel pointed to the compulsory collective license created by the Audio Home Recording Act (AHRA) (38) as a model for an AI training collective license regime,¶ although he acknowledged that the “number and variety of the works implicated in expressive machine learning could make for an accounting regime even more convoluted than that of the AHRA” (37, at 91–93). Sobel regarded levies on machine learning as a “bargain” if it averted years of litigation, but demurred about “[c]alculating the appropriate levy, and prescribing its disbursement” as a “more ambitious task than this Article can fulfill” (37, at 91–93).

If a disbursement-based levy proved infeasible “because individual uses are difficult to value and to track,” Sobel suggested that “machine learning levies … might be remitted to appropriate charities” or “put toward government programs specifically designed to support individuals who seek to make a living from creative, intellectual work” (37, at 93). This presages Professor Senftleben’s proposed levy on generative AI products and services (22).

The most ardent public supporter of collective licensing of in-copyright works for uses as training data has been the Authors Guild, a nonprofit organization that represents about 16,000 professional authors. In August of 2023, the Guild, along with eleven other professional creator organizations, published a statement proposing several legal and policy initiatives to address creators’ concerns about generative AI (39). The statement supported voluntary collective licensing as a way to “solve the problem of how to license a mass number of works to AI developers for AI training on behalf of individual creators and small business on an industry-by-industry basis” (39).

To enable collective licensing, the Authors Guild has suggested that professional creator groups would need an antitrust exemption so they could collaborate to develop the kind of regime that would adequately protect their interests (39). The Guild wants collective licenses to be negotiated with AI model developers on an industry-by-industry basis, appropriate terms for which should be decided by each industry (39).

Matthew Stratton, Deputy General Counsel of the Association of American Publishers (AAP), has also expressed support for voluntary collective licensing as a solution to the generative AI training-data problem (40). While recognizing difficulties of getting licenses for all copyrighted content available on the Internet, Stratton thinks that generative AI model developers could feasibly get licenses from copyright professionals in the publishing and news/media industries (40, at 439–440). AAP, he notes, has about 110 members and some have already made deals with generative AI developers (40, at 440, 446). Stratton points out that the Copyright Clearance Center (CCC) has developed a license for uses of works as training data (40, at 445–446). AAP will oppose any proposal that would call for compulsory licensing of copyrighted works as training data for generative AI models (40, at 448).

In late August 2023, the U.S. Copyright Office asked for public comment on numerous generative AI copyright-related issues, including several on training data uses of in-copyright works (41). It also asked for comments on the feasibility and desirability of voluntary licensing, compulsory licensing, and ECL (41, Questions 10.1–5, 11).

On May 9, 2025, the Office took the unusual step of issuing a “pre-publication” report on the copyright implications of generative AI training on in-copyright works (42). Much of this report analyzed the four fair use factors (7) as applied to uses of works as training data, with a particular attention to the purpose and market effects factors. It acknowledged the vast range of training-data uses of works that, while transformative, might still fall somewhere along the fair-use-or-infringing spectrum (42, at 45–52). It considered three types of potential market effects: possible lost sales if outputs were exact or near-exact copies of particular inputs, lost licensing opportunities, and the potential for market dilution posed by outputs that compete with the plaintiffs’ works, even if not substantially similar to particular inputs (42, at 61–71). It stated that where licensing was feasible, the market effects factor would cut against fair use. Its overall conclusion was that some uses of works as training data may be fair, while others may be unfair (42, at 74).

The report also addressed licensing of training data (42, at 85–106). It noted that a voluntary licensing market for uses of works for training AI models is emerging and should be allowed to continue to develop. Although collective licensing “presents its own logistical and organizational challenges,” the report opined that “it affords copyright owners and licensees flexibility to tailor agreements to their needs” (42, at 104). It suggested that “[m]ultiple CMOs can each license different types of copyrighted works on terms that make sense for that particular creative industry and AI model” (42, at 104). The report stated that voluntary collective licensing or, in some cases, an ECL would be a more beneficial approach than compulsory licensing because the latter type of regime is costly and contentious (42, at 96, 104–105). Yet, the report acknowledged that instituting successful licensing regimes across all works at scale might prove difficult (42, at 103).

Class Action Lawsuits as a Catalyst to Collective Licensing?

Courts may not have the power to establish collective licensing regimes through which generative AI model developers could obtain permission to use massive quantities of in-copyright works as training data. Judges can only order remedies as to the litigants in the cases before them. However, a ruling against fair use defenses in one or more of the class action lawsuits may give some urgency to collective licensing as a solution to the training-data dilemma.

The economic feasibility of this solution has been assessed in Bartz v. Anthropic PBC, one of several class action cases in which book authors claim that generative AI developers infringed copyrights by using the plaintiffs’ works as training data. Anthropic asked a trial court to rule that its uses of the plaintiffs’ books were fair and noninfringing. Anthropic contended that cutting edge AI models require such large quantities of works on which to train models that licensing is infeasible because transaction costs of negotiating with right-holders would dwarf the ability to achieve licensing at scale. Bartz, by contrast, claimed that licensing markets for training-data uses of copyrighted works exist, are emerging, and/or are likely to develop.

To aid the court’s assessment of the fair use market effects factor, Anthropic and Bartz submitted competing economic expert reports to support their arguments about the feasibility of licensing to train a large language model (LLM) such as Anthropic’s Claude. Although the court ruled in June 2025 that training-data uses of in-copyright works is fair use (43, at 1019), it did not address the economic experts’ opinions.# Yet, their divergent views on training-data licensing is relevant to the larger feasibility question.

Steven Peterson, Anthropic’s economic expert, declared that there is no incipient market for getting the necessary licenses to use texts for large-scale LLM training; nor is a competitive market for licensing text to train LLMs practicable (45, ¶7). He explained that AI firms would need to enter into millions of licenses just to clear rights for books owned by plaintiffs’ proposed class and a great many more if seeking licenses from individual authors who might own training-data rights in blogs and other texts in subdomains on the Internet.

Peterson opined that a serious impediment to efficient licensing of texts is the incompleteness of information on copyright ownership for works on which Claude’s model was trained. Some works are “orphans,” that is, works for which right-holders cannot be found through a reasonably diligent search; moreover, ownership information is poorly documented in texts found on the Internet (45, ¶¶60–62). Nor is good information available about the fair market value of training-data licenses, in part because individual books represent only a “tiny fraction of the overall data required to train an LLM,” and transaction costs in relation to the cost of the license would be very high, so efficient markets cannot form (45, ¶¶7, 68–70).

Peterson asserted that the existence of some training-data licenses between right-holders and generative AI companies does not undercut his analysis about the impracticality of a text training-data license market. The “hypothetical market to license AI training data has none of the characteristics needed for it to function” (45, ¶49). Peterson observed that LLMs such as Claude are useful products that consumers value, and that extending copyright as the plaintiffs have requested would be an “unwarranted expansion of the copyright monopoly [that would] harm[] the public” (45, ¶52).

Peterson was skeptical of suggestions that collective licensing can overcome the transaction cost problem with AI training-data licensing for AI models. He regarded such suggestions as “effectively admissions that the licensing market AI firms face would not allow them to license the texts they need under existing conditions” (45, ¶54). Although some have suggested that CCC could serve as CMO for training-data licenses, Peterson viewed CCC as incapable of granting licenses to sufficiently large volumes of data for training large-scale LLMs, in part because training rights are highly dispersed among authors; moreover, CMOs such as CCC would also face high transaction costs to acquire and then license training-data rights (45, ¶¶72–73).

Bartz’ economic expert, James E. Malackowski,|| asserted that a market for licensing books for LLM training is emerging, despite some initial “confounding factors” and “inefficiencies” which can be overcome in time (46, ¶39). Malackowski‘s declaration gave numerous examples of training-data licenses between generative AI developers and right-holders to counter Peterson’s claims. He also provided a list of many entities that are willing to license training-data rights (46, ¶¶40–48, App. 4) to support his claim that “[t]here is an emerging market for books for LLM training and Anthropic’s conduct, especially if prevalent, harms this likely-to-develop market” (46, ¶¶14, 68). That this market is still emerging is not a reason to discount its plausibility (46, ¶50). Malackowski’s declaration did not discuss collective licensing.

The economists’ arguments did not play an important role in the eventual fair use ruling in the Bartz case. The court decided that training-data use of in-copyright works was simply a licensing market that Bartz had no right to control (43, at 1031–1032). It is possible, even if unlikely, that other class action cases will result in settlements that provide seed funding for a collective licensing regime to enable training-data uses. Such a settlement is unlikely to be as far-reaching as was proposed, but ultimately rejected, in the Authors Guild v. Google case (47). That settlement would have created a new CMO to collect revenues and administer rights in books that Google would have been authorized to commercialize. Training-data revenues under a collective license would be much less likely to generate significant revenues to authors. After all, Google planned to make money from giving customers access to the books’ expressions, not just using the books to train a model.

Other Reasons to Question a Collective Licensing Solution for Training Data in the United States.

There is a much less well-developed CMO infrastructure in the United States as compared with the EU. Music publishing, recorded music, and cable and satellite retransmission of broadcast television are the only sectors of U.S. copyright industries in which collective licensing has been a common way for copyright owners to be compensated for exploitations of their works. These sectors largely operate under copyright compulsory licensing regimes and consent decrees (see ref. 48).

Efforts to expand collective licensing in the United States have met with little success. The music and sound recording industries had high hopes for the AHRA, which imposed a compulsory collective license levy on digital audio tape (DAT) recorders and tapes for use in DAT machines (38). The AHRA (§§1004–1007) established elaborate procedures for collecting funds that would be administered by Copyright Royalty judges from which right-holders could draw. But the last payout from the AHRA fund was only $56.43 in 2020 (49).

During the early 2000s, some scholars and civil society groups endorsed proposals for a collective compulsory license to enable Internet users to engage in peer-to-peer filesharing of in-copyright works subject to a levy to be collected by broadband providers to be paid to copyright owners (see e.g., refs. 50 and 51). The motion picture and recording industries did not support these proposals, so Congress never adopted legislation to authorize this licensing regime.

A more recent effort to expand collective licensing in the United States arose after the failure of a proposed settlement of the Authors Guild v. Google lawsuit (47). The Copyright Office recommended adoption of a pilot program to allow mass digitization of books on terms similar to those in the failed settlement, including an ECL regime to be administered by an unnamed CMO (52). The Office’s report on the pilot project recognized that “the vast number of rightsholders from whom permission may be required in order to digitally reproduce and offer access to a large collection of works renders such a model impracticable in this context,” so the Office endorsed an ECL regime to address the transaction cost problem with right clearances (52, at 72). It asked for public comments on the proposal, but concluded it was infeasible because of strong opposition from copyright holders and prospective users of the proposed corpus of mass digitized books.

Notwithstanding the Authors Guild’s and AAP’s endorsements of voluntary collective licensing (39, 40), a significant impediment to achieving this goal is unclarity about who (if anyone) owns training-data rights in published works. Publishers may have gotten copyright assignments from authors, but courts may find that authors retained rights that were not in contemplation when book contracts were signed. In Random House, Inc. v. Rosetta Books, for instance, a court upheld authors’ rights to license publication of ebooks (53). A similar problem bedeviled the proposed GBS settlement in the late 2000s (47). The proposed settlement included provisions for settling disputes between publishers and authors over their competing ownership claims for rights to create electronic books (ebooks) or a subscription service with contents of the GBS corpus (54, at 498–501).

Uses of commercially exploited works as training data for generative AI models would similarly seem beyond what publishers and authors contemplated when contracting years before. In any event, one would have to examine the terms of book contracts to determine who might own what rights to license training-data uses of published books. Given how unexpected were training-data uses of works to build AI models, any rights in such uses should generally be with authors, who collectively are going to find it difficult to negotiate with AI model developers. Moreover, large quantities of data used as training consists of open source and user-generated content whose authors likewise cannot negotiate licenses with AI model developers.

The Authors Guild and AAP may look to CCC as a CMO to grant collective licenses for training data. It is, however, unclear how many training-data rights CCC currently has available for licensing. It certainly does not have the scale of rights that leading AI model developers would need to train models. CCC’s willingness to license uses of works as training data is not meaningful without a mandate from the appropriate right-holders to represent their interests. If publishers are providing CCC with training-data rights, how can CCC be sure that the publishers actually own the rights? The Authors Guild sometimes points to the Authors Registry (55) as a CMO that works with authors,** but that registry cannot hope to collect enough rights from authors to support a viable training-data collective license market.

An ad hoc committee of independent economists, convened by the Copyright Office in 2024, shares Peterson’s doubts about the practicability of licensing of the massive quantities of data needed to train large-scale LLMs (56). This committee issued a report in February 2025 on its deliberations about the economic implications of generative AI for copyright policy. The report recognized that licensing is feasible for developers of “bounded” generative AI models insofar as the works on which the models were trained and their owners can easily be identified (such as Adobe’s Firefly model) (56, at 5–6). However, the committee questioned whether licensing was practicable for “unbounded” generative models, that is, those trained on data whose right-holders cannot easily be identified (such as models trained on Common Crawl datasets) (56, at 6).

The economists’ report emphasized the importance of low transaction costs as a key factor when assessing the viability of licensing as a solution for the training-data dilemma (56, at 37). It also emphasized the difficulty (and perhaps impossibility) of identifying the contribution of any one data source to the model’s performance, and noted that given the massive amounts of data necessary to train general purpose AI models, the contribution of any one data source was likely to be very small (56, at 34–35, 50). The report observed that markets can fail if transaction costs outstrip the payments for right clearances (56, at 45–47). Curiously, the Copyright Office’s pre-publication report on generative AI training does not mention the economic scholars’ report, even though it specifically convened the committee to advise it on the economics of AI and copyright policy.

Collective licensing seems infeasible as a compromise in the U.S. class action cases which seek class-wide compensation for training-data uses. Those classes do not exist as entities capable of granting licenses until and unless a court certifies the classes, and it is unclear whether courts will find enough commonality among the highly fragmented authorial community about the extent to which they care about whether generative AI models have trained on their works.

Concluding Thoughts

As courts consider generative AI fair use defenses in the U.S. cases, they should be mindful of the U.S. Supreme Court’s fair use ruling in Google v. Oracle (57) which directed courts to consider not only possible harms from putative fair uses, but also public benefits of challenged uses, as well as the extent to which the challenged uses promote ongoing creativity. Google’s uses of parts of the Java Application Program Interface (API) not only enabled the development of the innovative Android smartphone platform; it also enabled millions of Java programmers to take advantage of their investments in having learned those declarations to build millions of apps capable of running on the Android platform. The Court thought these considerations weighed in Google’s favor when assessing actual and potential market effects in fair use cases. Courts should also keep in mind whether rulings for or against the fair use training-data defenses are most consistent with the constitutional purpose of copyright to promote the progress of knowledge (58).

The development of generative AI technologies has promoted consumer welfare. Developers have made very substantial investments in creating generative AI systems and supplied both public and private sectors with products and services for which there is exceptionally high demand. A 2024 study reported that nearly 40% of the U.S. adult population already uses generative AI for work or personal uses, across a broad range of occupations and tasks and at an adoption rate faster than PCs and the internet (59). Google, Meta, and Microsoft are not the only firms that have been able to enter generative AI markets. The existence of some open source models and weights means that even less well-resourced entities, such as university researchers, can participate in ongoing innovation in this field. Enterprises, academic and other research institutions, as well as members of the general public have adopted these productivity tools with amazing alacrity. Expectations that these technologies will have transformative impacts on the economy and society are very high, although the technologies also pose safety and other risks which will continue to pose challenging policy questions.

It is understandable that many individual artists and authors are upset about the unauthorized uses of their works as training data and have concerns about the new form of competition they face from outputs of generative AI. Yet many uses of generative AI technologies are beneficial for authors as well as for other members of the public. It seems likely that high quality human authorship will continue to be more valuable to the general public than AI-generated works that are capable only of simulating human authorship (e.g., ref. 60). Generative AI may displace some opportunities and jobs, but society has adapted to many challenges arising from automation of previously human-skilled tasks and jobs. Revenues to off-set some job displacement can come about through taxing generative AI products and services.

It would be beneficial to the growth of the generative AI technology industry and economies that depend on it if an international consensus emerged about the legal implications of using in-copyright works as training data for AI models. Professors Matthew Sag and Peter Yu believe that such a consensus is emerging (61). In addition to the plausibility of fair use defenses in the U.S. and the EU’s adoption of TDM exceptions that enable training-data uses, Israel’s Ministry of Justice has issued an opinion that uses of in-copyright works as training data are lawful under the fair use doctrine of its laws (61, at 1179–1180). Japan and Singapore have adopted broad exceptions to copyright rules so these countries can grow their AI industries (61, at 1185–1187, 1190–1192). China’s ambition to dominate global AI industries may cause it to decide to allow uses of in-copyright works as training data too.

Sag and Yu suggest that countries are recognizing “that text and data mining, computational data analysis, and AI training can be socially valuable and may not inherently prejudice the copyright holders’ legitimate interests” (61, at 1163). This equilibrium could be upset by unfair use rulings in the U.S. court cases or by legislative or regulatory measures that seek to limit the use of in-copyright works to train generative models. The equilibrium could also be upset if every country imposed collective licensing fees on generative AI developers for uses of in-copyright works as training data. The risk of regulatory arbitrage is real. Countries with the least favorable copyright exceptions and development environments are unlikely to attract investment and talent. Developers are likely to move to places where the most favorable rules, talent, and development environments can be found.
