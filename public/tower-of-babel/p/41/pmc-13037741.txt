Clinical and Genetic Characteristics of a Chinese Occult Maculopathy Cohort
Authors: Zixuan Jiang, Jiamin Ouyang, Zhen Yi, Wenmin Sun, Xueshan Xiao, Xiaoyun Jia, Shiqiang Li, Panfeng Wang, Qingjiong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo analyze the genetic basis and phenotypic features of occult maculopathy in the Chinese population.MethodsClinical data and venous blood from probands with occult maculopathy and available family members were collected. Variants were detected using next-generation sequencing (NGS), analyzed bioinformatically, and confirmed through Sanger sequencing.ResultsHeterozygous pathogenic variants in RP1L1 were detected in 17 of 24 families, with c.133C>T and c.3599G>T identified in 15 and 2 families, respectively. In patients with the RP1L1 variants, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) showed an overall declining trend despite sporadic fluctuations, stabilizing between 0.70 and 1.0 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution in most patients. No potential pathogenic variants were detected in the remaining seven families (7/24, 29.17%) after systematic analysis of NGS data. Comparative assessment revealed that patients lacking RP1L1 variants had significantly earlier onset and better BCVA at their first visit. A distinctive finding was universally normal optical coherence tomography (OCT) profiles in this non-RP1L1 group. Other atypical changes were detected in subsets of patients on other examinations, including OCT, fundus autofluorescence, microperimetry, and adaptive optics retinal imaging.ConclusionsHeterozygous pathogenic variants in RP1L1 contributed to 70.83% (17/24) of Chinese families with occult maculopathy in our cohort. Multifocal electroretinogram (mfERG) remains the most sensitive detection modality for occult maculopathy, while bilateral OCT abnormalities might indicate a genetic etiology related to heterozygous RP1L1 variants. Other modalities, including fundus autofluorescence, visual field, and adaptive optics imaging, have been explored for diagnosis and differentiation.Translational RelevanceThis study emphasizes the importance of genetic diagnosis and mfERG for the accurate diagnosis and management of occult maculopathy.

Introduction

Occult maculopathy refers to a spectrum of macular disorders characterized by progressive visual loss in the absence of significant fundus abnormalities.1 A prominent form within this spectrum is occult macular dystrophy (OMD; OMIM #613587), which is caused by variants in RP1L1 and inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern.2 Dysfunction in OMD is limited to the macular region without apparent abnormalities on routine fundus examination, making diagnosis difficult.3 The development of ophthalmic functional and imaging techniques has facilitated the diagnosis of OMD. In particular, the multifocal electroretinogram (mfERG), capable of objectively detecting macular dysfunction, has become indispensable for diagnosis.4–6 With the advancement of optical coherence tomography (OCT),7 subtle structural abnormalities, such as disruption of the photoreceptor inner segment–outer segment (IS-OS) junction and loss of the Verhoeff membrane/cone outer segment tip (COST) line, became detectable in individuals diagnosed with OMD despite a normal-appearing macula on funduscopy.8 These zones of disruption, visible on OCT, correspond to the bands now widely referred to in clinical literature as the ellipsoid zone (EZ) and the interdigitation zone (IZ), respectively.9–11 In addition to mfERG and OCT, other ophthalmic examinations, such as fundus autofluorescence (FAF), visual field (VF), and adaptive optics (AO) retinal imaging, have also been investigated for diagnosis and differential diagnosis of OMD.12–14 However, most evidence remains limited to a small number of studies and lacks confirmation across diverse cohorts.

In addition to clinical examinations, genetic diagnosis also provides important evidence for the diagnosis of OMD. The initial implication of heterozygous variants in the RP1L1 gene in the pathogenesis of autosomal dominant OMD was reported in 2010 through genetic analysis of two affected families.2,15 The role of RP1L1 was further supported by immunohistochemistry in monkeys, which demonstrated the protein's expression in photoreceptor cells and indicated its importance in their morphology and function.2,16 Subsequent to its discovery, several RP1L1 variants have been associated with autosomal dominant OMD in a number of families (RP1L1-OMD).17–20 Moreover, the ability of next-generation sequencing to analyze a panel of genes or all genes, including variants in ABCA4, CRX, EYS, GUCY2D, KCNV2, and SCA7, has been reported to be associated with OMD or OMD-like phenotypes in several cases.19,21–28 However, a subset of patients with subtle macular dystrophy with a normal fundus, in whom no causative variants are identified, is considered as having nonhereditary occult maculopathy.29 This study collectively refers to these conditions, which include multiple underlying pathologies that affect the macula without an abnormal fundus appearance, as occult maculopathy.

In this study, the genetic variants in 24 Chinese families with occult maculopathy were screened by exome sequencing. Clinical features of the patients were evaluated by multimodal imaging, including fundus photography, FAF, OCT, visual field, microperimetry, AO retinal imaging, and ocular electrophysiological examination. The results showed that mfERG remains the most valuable examination for occult maculopathy. The OCT scan showed a minor difference between patients with occult maculopathy with and without RP1L1 variants.

Methods

Participants

This study was approved by the institutional review board of the Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center. Twenty-four probands from 24 families clinically diagnosed with occult maculopathy between March 1, 2017, and January 1, 2024, along with their available family members, were recruited from our Pediatric and Genetics Clinic, Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, Guangzhou, China. Based on the initial complaint and routine ophthalmic examination, the preliminary diagnosis of occult maculopathy was defined as follows12,30: (1) presence of unexplained vision loss, (2) fundus photography or fundoscopy demonstrating a normal appearance of the fundus, and (3) electrophysiological testing or OCT scan confirming the presence of macular dysfunction. Individuals exhibiting any comorbidities with other ocular diseases or systemic diseases with ocular manifestations were excluded from the study. Written informed consent consistent with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki was obtained from the participants or their guardians before the collection of peripheral venous blood samples and clinical data. Genomic DNA was obtained using a previously described method.31,32

Variant Identification and Evaluation

Targeted exome sequencing (TES) or whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed on genomic DNA from peripheral venous blood samples of 24 probands from the 24 families (Supplementary Table S1). The procedures were described in our previous studies.32–34 The Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon Enrichment Kit (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used to extract the exome from the genomic DNA of the probands. Next, enriched DNA fragments were sequenced at an average depth of 125× using the HiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Variants were found by utilizing the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA, http://bio-bwa.sourceforge.net/) to align the sequencing reads with the hg19 reference genome. SAMTOOLS (http://samtools.sourceforge.net/), ANNOVAR (http://annovar.openbioinformatics.org/en/latest/), and dbNSFP (http://varianttools.sourceforge.net/Annotation/DbNSFP) handled the tasks of variant calling, annotation, and functional prediction, respectively.

As previously mentioned,35 several stages of bioinformatic analysis were used to filter the discovered variants. First, variants in the gnomAD database (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/) with a minor allele frequency (MAF) of more than 1% or low sequencing quality (coverage depth <5) were eliminated. Five in silico prediction tools were then used to assess the possible impact of missense variants: SIFT (http://sift.jcvi.org/), Polyphen-2 (http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/), PROVEAN (http://provean.jcvi.org/seq_submit.php), CADD (http://cadd.gs.washington.edu), and REVEL (https://sites.google.com/site/revelgenomics/). Next, using the Human Splicing Finder Program (HSF, https://hsf.genomnis.com/mutation/analysis), an online splicing prediction tool, the impact of variants on splicing signals was computationally investigated. These variants were also assessed by a comparative analysis between the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD) and the gnomAD database. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) criteria were used to evaluate the variants.36 Sanger sequencing was used to verify the pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants. The sequences of the forward and reverse primers were RP1L1-c.133-F-5′-GCACCTCTAGAAAGACGGGA-3′, RP1L1-c.133-R-5′-TCCATCTTCCAGCTGCTCC-3′, RP1L1-c.3599-F-5′- GATGTGACGTTGGGGAAGAC-3′, and RP1L1-c.3599-R-5′- CTGGTTGGAGGTTTTCAGGG-3′.

Clinical Data Collection

A thorough clinical examination was conducted on a total of 31 individuals from the 24 families, including 24 probands as well as 4 additional family members with OMD and 3 asymptomatic members validated with pathogenic variants in RP1L1. A comprehensive medical history was documented, along with visual symptoms, gender, age, disease onset (the time the patient started exhibiting symptoms or received a diagnosis), and disease duration (the period from onset to examination). Routine ophthalmological examinations included best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), funduscopic observation, axial length (IOL Master 500; Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany), refractive error (KR-8900; Topcon Corp., Tokyo, Japan), fundus photographs (VISUCAM 224; Carl Zeiss), FAF (Canon CR2 plus AF; Canon, Tokyo, Japan), OCT (Heidelberg Spectralis OCT; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany), and visual fields (Humphrey HFA 3; Carl Zeiss). Visual electrophysiological examinations included pattern visual evoked potentials (p-VEPs), full-field electroretinogram (ffERG), and mfERG, which were performed according to the international standard guidelines of the International Society for Clinical Electrophysiology of Vision (ISCEV) protocol.37–39 Visual evoked potential (VEP) testing was performed using the RETI-port/scan 21 (Roland Consult, Brandenburg, Germany). The ffERG was conducted with the RETeval ffERG device (LKC Technologies, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) or the RETI-port/scan 21 or Espion E2 (Diagnosys LLC, Lowell, MA, USA), while mfERG was carried out using the VERIS Clinic 5.0.9 system (Electro-Diagnostic Imaging, San Mateo, CA, USA) or the RETIscan system (Roland Consult). Both mfERG systems were configured with a stimulus array consisting of 61 hexagons arranged across five eccentric rings, enabling consistent topographic assessment of retinal function across the central retina. Additional examinations, including macular integrity assessment microperimetry (MAIA microperimetry [iCare Finland Oy, Vantaa, Finland] or MP-3 microperimeter [Nidek, Gamagori, Japan]), which allows precise assessment of retinal sensitivities at specific retinal locations, and AO fundus imaging (rtx1; Imagine Eyes, Orsay, France), enabling photoreceptor degeneration detection at a cellular level, were also carried out in available patients.

Classification of Clinical Parameters (FAF, OCT, and mfERG)

Twenty-one individuals (18 patients and 3 asymptomatic carriers) underwent FAF testing, including 13 men and 8 women. Their ages ranged from 5 to 60 years, with the age at exam of 24.71 ± 17.38 years (mean ± SD). The following examinations were conducted after fully dilating the pupils. Based on FAF findings, patients were classified into three patterns: pattern 1, increased autofluorescence at the fovea; pattern 2, ringlike hyperfluorescence around the macula; and pattern 3, normal fundus fluorescence.

Thirty participants (27 patients and 3 asymptomatic carriers) underwent OCT examination, including 18 men and 12 women, with the age at exam of 23.30 ± 15.82 years (range, 5–60 years). The digital caliper tool built into the OCT system was used to measure the thickness of the outer nuclear layer (ONL) (between the internal limiting membrane and the external limiting membrane) at the central fovea.40 OCT findings were characterized by the structure of the EZ, the IZ, and the thickness of the ONL: stage 1, EZ is intact, but the IZ is blurred; stage 2, EZ is blurred, while the IZ is blurred, or focally disrupted; stage 3, EZ demonstrates blurring or focal disruption, accompanied by extensive disruption or loss of the IZ; and stage 4, loss of EZ and IZ, with a significant reduction in foveal ONL thickness.3,29,41,42

A total of 22 individuals (13 men and 9 women, including 20 patients and 2 asymptomatic carriers) underwent mfERG examination. Their age at exam was 23.86 ± 18.72 years with a range of 5.5 to 65 years. The mfERG recordings were performed using two systems: the Visual Evoked Response Imaging System (VERIS Clinic 5.0.9; Electro-Diagnostic Imaging) and the RETIscan system (Roland Consult). It is recommended that patients undergo light adaptation under standard room illumination for a minimum of 15 minutes preceding the mfERG test. Particular attention should be paid to extending this period to 30 minutes if the patient has recently been subjected to intense illumination, as from fundus photography or ophthalmoscopy.39 It is essential that the electrodes maintain contact with the corneal or nearby conjunctival surface. Adequate retinal image quality and proper refraction are also prerequisites for optimal test conditions.43

Using a combination of classifications reported in the literature, patients were classified into three functional groups according to the mfERG findings: group 1, central/paracentral dysfunction with relatively preserved peripheral function; group 2, extensive dysfunction across the entire recorded area of the retina; and group 3, normal mfERG.44 Based on the results of the TES and WES, patients were divided into two groups. Group A comprised patients with occult maculopathy who had identified pathogenic variants in the RP1L1 gene; group B consisted of patients with occult maculopathy but no detectable pathogenic variant in RP1L1.

Adaptive Optics Retinal Imaging

The condition of the cone photoreceptors located in the foveal and parafoveal areas was obtained and analyzed using an AO retinal camera through undilated pupils.45 Random samples were taken within 1° from the foveal area (approximately 270–300 µm) using a standardized 80-µm × 80-µm sampling window, with the instrument selecting five windows for analysis at a time, ensuring that the sampling area was evenly distributed around the foveal area (including temporal, nasal, superior, and inferior sides) three times in each eye for each patient (six eyes in three patients). The patients comprised three men (F3-I:1, F3-II:2, and F12-II:1), with an average age of 39.06 ± 20.09 years, while the control group comprised four volunteers (four men, four eyes; mean age: 34.68 ± 9.92 years). All control volunteers received a comprehensive ophthalmic examination to exclude ocular pathology or media opacities and to confirm a BCVA of 0 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR). Images obtained for each participant were analyzed for the number, density, regularity, dispersion, and spacing of cone photoreceptors using AOdetect, an application designed for the supervised segmentation of ultrahigh-resolution images acquired with the rtx1 AO imaging instrument.

Statistical Analysis

The Mann–Whitney U test was performed to compare the age of onset, age of first visit, BCVA at first visit, refractive errors, and axial length between group A and group B, as well as AO parameters of cone cells between patients and controls. Fisher's exact test was employed on clinical categorical data, including FAF patterns, OCT stages, and mfERG groupings. The normality test of foveal ONL thickness and mfERG parameters (R1–R5) was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test (sample size <50). For the normally distributed foveal ONL thickness data, an unpaired t-test was conducted for the comparisons between group A and group B. For mfERG parameters (R1–R5), which violated the normality assumption, a two-stage nonparametric strategy was adopted. The Kruskal–Wallis H test was first utilized for global comparisons among the three study groups. When parameters showed significant global differences (Kruskal–Wallis P < 0.05), post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons.

The correlation between age of onset and initial BCVA was assessed using simple linear regression. The linear mixed-effects model (LMM) was used to analyze the correlation between disease duration and duration-related BCVA. The residuals were normally distributed.

All statistical analyses and calculations were performed using R software (version 4.5.2; R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A P value (or adjusted P value, Padj = P × n, where n is the number of pairwise comparisons) less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical Profile and Genetic Findings

The profile of 28 patients and 3 asymptomatic carriers from 24 families (17 men and 14 women) is summarized in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S2. Their mean age of onset was 15.81 years (age range, 5–50 years). The BCVA in affected eyes ranged from 0.0 to 1.10 logMAR. All patients denied any history of amblyopia or trauma, and two claimed a cold before the onset of the disease. Pathogenic variants in the RP1L1 gene were found in 17 of 24 (70.83%) families (Table 1; Supplementary Fig. S1). A c.133C>T in exon 2 with a substitution of tryptophan for arginine at amino acid position 45 (p.R45W), previously identified as the dominant variant in a Japanese OMD study,2 was also the most common variant (15/17, 88.24%) in our cohort. The other two families were detected to have a missense variant of c.3599G>T/p.(G1200V) (Table 1; Supplementary Fig. S1). In the meantime, no potential pathogenic variants were detected in RP1L1 in the remaining 7 of 24 (29.17%) families. To investigate possible pathogenic variants in other genes associated with occult maculopathy, systematic analysis was performed on the available exome sequencing data from the seven probands, with a particular focus on six previously reported genes: ABCA4, CRX, EYS, GUCY2D, KCNV2, and SCA7. A total of two rare variants were detected in seven probands, including rs4147886 in ABCA4 and rs140222628 in EYS. However, neither of these variants was predicted to be damaging through multistep bioinformatic analyses (Supplementary Table S3). Patients were divided into two groups based on WES findings: group A comprised patients with OMD who had a pathogenic variant in the RP1L1 gene, and group B comprised patients with occult maculopathy who had no pathogenic variant in RP1L1 or other genes. Patients from group B had an earlier mean onset age of 8.07 ± 2.68 years (range, 6–14 years) compared to the mean onset age of group A, 18.52 ± 13.45 years (range, 5–50 years). In the 21 patients in group A, the mean spherical equivalent refraction was −3.31 ± 2.59 diopters (range, −9.50 to +1.00 diopters), while the patients in group B had a mean spherical equivalent refraction of −0.98 ± 2.47 diopters (range, −6.25 to +2.00 diopters). Consistent with this, the mean axial length of the eyes of group A was 25.46 ± 1.95 mm (range, 22.67–27.72 mm), while that of the eyes of group B was 22.96 ± 2.16 mm (range, 20.30–26.45 mm) (Table 2).

Classification of Clinical Parameters (FAF, OCT, and mfERG)

FAF, OCT, and mfERG classifications were performed as described above, and detailed information is provided in Figures 1, 2; Supplementary Figure S3; and Table 2. FAF images of 11 patients from group A are shown in Figure 1, whereas the images of the remaining 3 from a Zeiss CLARUS 500 are not shown. Visual Field and Microperimetry

We also classified the visual fields according to the patterns observed from the pattern deviation maps of the Humphrey visual field (HVF). Based on the HVF analyzer 30-2 or 24-2 test, VF examination was performed in eight patients (F1-II:1, F4-IV:1, F8-II:1, F10-I:2, F11-II:5, F12-II:1, F16-II:1, and F17-I:1) from group A and two patients (F20-II:1 and F24-II:1) from group B. All 10 patients, with a mean age of 24.75 ± 16.11 years (age range, 6–53 years; six males, four females), showed paracentral scotoma.46

A total of five patients (F1-II:1, F3-II:2, F12-II:1, F14-II:1, and F15-II:1) and two asymptomatic carriers (F3-III:1 and F3-III:2) underwent microperimetry. Four patients (F1-II:1, F3-II:2, F12-II:1, and F14-II:1) from group A (4/5, 80.00%) showed decreased sensitivity in the macular region and unstable fixation on this examination (Supplementary Figs. S2A, S2B, S2E, S2G),47 while the remaining patient (F15-II:1) and two asymptomatic carriers showed no obvious abnormalities (Supplementary Figs. S2C, S2D).

Visual Electrophysiological Analyses

VEP was conducted in seven patients (F9-II:1, F10-I:2, F12-II:1, F14-II:1, F16-II:1, F20-II:1, and F22-II:1) aged 5.5 to 34 years (mean age, 18.21 ± 11.72 years). Abnormalities on p-VEP were observed in two (F9-II:1 and F10-I:2) of the seven patients, both with mildly reduced P100 amplitude under low-frequency (1°) checkerboard stimuli and one (F9-II:1) also with moderately reduced amplitude under high-frequency (15′) stimuli. Results for the remaining patients were within the normal reference ranges established for the respective devices used. In total, abnormal p-VEP results were observed in 4 of the 14 eyes examined (28.57%).

Sixteen participants underwent ffERG testing, including 15 patients (aged 5.5–65 years, mean 27.90 ± 19.13 years) and 1 asymptomatic carrier (Supplementary Fig. S5, Supplementary Table S4). Of the 15 patients, only 2 (2/15, 13.33%) showed specific ffERG abnormalities: F10-I:2 exhibited mildly reduced amplitude of the light‑adapted cone a‑wave, and F12‑II:1 showed mild to moderately reduced amplitude of the dark‑adapted rod b‑wave, along with mildly reduced mixed rod‑cone b‑wave and a 30-Hz flicker response. The remaining participants had normal ffERG findings.

A total of 20 patients underwent mfERG examination, and all patients visualized a decreased macular central recess response on the three-dimensional response map of the mfERG (Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. S3). Parameters including amplitude density, P1 amplitude, N1 amplitude, P1–N1 amplitude difference, and implicit period of P1 of the five rings, as well as the ratio of the R1-R4 ring to the R5 ring, were compared among three groups: group A, group B, and normal controls (NC) (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S5). Significant global differences were observed in several mfERG parameters. In R1, P1 amplitude density, P1 amplitude, and △P1–N1 were significantly reduced in both group A and group B compared to NC (all Padj < 0.05). Although both patient groups showed significant deficits in R1 compared to normal controls, the spatial distribution of impairment differed between them. Group A exhibited lower P1 amplitude density and a more extensive impairment, reaching R2 to R4 (all Padj < 0.05), whereas the deficit in group B remained localized to R1. This pattern suggests that cone cell dysfunction in patients carrying pathogenic RP1L1 variants is more spatially widespread, characterized by damage extending into the paracentral macula, compared to those without such variants.

Assessment of Cone Mosaic Status

The mean values of the cone features were calculated and compared for cases (six eyes in three patients, namely, F3-I:1, F3-II:2, and F12-II1, with the c.133C>T/p.(R45W) variant in RP1L1) and controls (four eyes in four normal controls). On a magnified AO image of the macula area, the cone cells of normal controls showed regular hexagonal shapes, whereas the patients’ cone cells were disorganized (Figs. 5A–D). Follow-Up of BCVA

Follow-up visit on BCVA of 19 patients (16 in group A and 3 in group B) was available (Table 1). BCVA at serial follow-up visits of patients harboring RP1L1 variants was analyzed. By setting the BCVA at the first visit as the baseline visual acuity, the follow-up BCVA in patients with RP1L1 variants exhibited fluctuation with a generally decreasing trend (Fig. 6A), with 75.00% of patients (12/16) having a BCVA in the better eye worse than 0.52 logMAR, which corresponds to a decimal BCVA lower than 0.3 at the first visit. At the final visit, the BCVA of 68.75% of patients (11/16) was stable within the range of 0.70 to 1.0 logMAR, or between 0.1 and 0.2 in decimal units. Furthermore, the relationships between age at onset and initial BCVA, as well as between disease duration and final BCVA, were assessed separately (Figs. 6B, 6C). In patients with RP1L1 variants, age at onset showed a significant correlation with initial BCVA (R2 = 0.144, P = 0.0225), indicating that patients with an earlier onset tended to present with poorer baseline vision. Furthermore, longitudinal analysis demonstrated a significant association between disease duration and BCVA, with an estimated progression rate of 0.0116 logMAR/year (P = 0.0059). These results indicate a significant progressive decline in visual acuity as the disease duration increased. Review and Summary of Literature

A total of 281 cases from 173 families were reported in the previous literature, of which 276 cases were genetically tested, and 232 individuals were found to carry the RP1L1 variant. Twenty-eight variants of RP1L1 have been identified in the 232 individuals from 130 unrelated families. In this study, all previously reported OMD-associated RP1L1 variants were summarized and scored by a variety of bioinformatics prediction tools (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7). The 28 variants included 27 (96.43%) missense variants and 1 (3.57%) frameshift variant. The most frequent variant was c.133C>T/p.(R45W) identified in 94 families (94/130, 72.31%), followed by c.3596C>G/p.(S1199C) in 13 families (13/130, 10.00%) and c.3593C>T/p.(S1198F) in 3 families (3/130, 2.31%), located within two previously reported variant hotspots.17 An allele count schematic diagram showing the family numbers and location of RP1L1 variants from our in-house dataset, gnomAD database, and published sources is presented in Figure 7, indicating that missense variants with low predictive scores as well as truncating variants of RP1L1 lack pathogenic potential according to the distributions of these variants.

To get a bird's-eye view of variant effects across the entire RP1L1 gene, possible changes of every residue (NM_178857.6; UniProt: Q8IWN7) were sequentially predicted by the nine in silico prediction tools. The tolerance of mutation at each amino acid was calculated by summing the scores of all potential changes, then ranking them to construct a tolerance landscape.

According to the previously reported literature, 26 of the 232 individuals with RP1L1 variants did not develop symptoms aged 9 to 66 years. Of these 26 individuals, 24 carried the c.133C>T/p.(R45W) variant of RP1L1, suggesting incomplete penetrance, although the penetrance is likely to increase with age. Furthermore, of the 180 patients from 94 families with the c.133C>T/p.(R45W) variant, 156 had OMD with a penetrance of 86.67% (156/180), while the remaining 24 were asymptomatic carriers.

Among the 281 cases, 253 patients from 171 unrelated families were diagnosed with occult maculopathy genetically or on the basis of clinical features, including 137 males and 116 females. No disease-causing variants were detected in 43 patients (43/253, 17.00%). Of the 253 patients, 187 (73.91%) were East Asian, including 31 Chinese,26,53 103 Japanese,2,14,29 25 Korean,20 and 28 without a country specified16; the remaining countries or ethnicities included 45 German (one Ashkenazi Jewish),50,54 4 American,55–57 1 Swiss,58 6 Italian,49,51,59 3 South Asian,48 and 7 White European (Supplementary Fig. S4).48

Discussion

In this work, two previously described pathogenic variants in RP1L1 were detected in 17 of the 24 families with occult maculopathy, including the c.133C>T/p.(R45W) variant in 15 families and the c.3599G>T/p.(G1200V) variant in 2 families. No potential pathogenic variants in RP1L1, as well as other genes, were detected in the remaining seven families through a systematic analysis of the next-generation sequencing data.

The RP1L1 genotype is associated with the morphology and distribution of photoreceptor damage, and the genotypes differed in terms of visual function as well. Among our patients, there were significant differences in age at onset, BCVA at first visit, OCT classification, and mfERG results between individuals with and without RP1L1 variants. Patients carrying RP1L1 variants had an earlier age of onset and worse BCVA at first visit compared with those without a variant. The morphology and distribution of photoreceptor disruption were also associated with genotype: 80.00% (16/20) of patients with RP1L1 variants had abnormal OCT results, whereas all patients without RP1L1 variants had normal OCT results. Moreover, among the four patients carrying RP1L1 variants with normal OCT, three exhibited abnormal FAF findings. Visual function was also associated with genotype, specifically with cone cell dysfunction, in which reduced mfERG P1 amplitude density was more severe and extensive in patients harboring RP1L1 pathogenic variants than in those without variants. Based on a literature review, pathogenic variants were detected in 82.30% (186/226) of occult maculopathy cases with abnormal OCT scans, whereas they were found in 50.00% (3/6) of cases with normal OCT results. Therefore, an abnormal OCT in patients with occult maculopathy implies a high association of pathogenic variants in RP1L1. In contrast, when only the mfERG is abnormal with a normal OCT result, genetic testing is less likely to yield relevant variants.

Phenotypic features of patients with occult maculopathy were comprehensively analyzed based on multiple imaging modalities and literature review. In our cohort, there were no significant abnormalities in fundus color photographs in any patient. Similarly, about 82.50% of the cases reported in the literature (132/160) had completely normal fundus color photograph results, while some patients had fundus changes associated with high myopia-associated or other ophthalmic disease, and the rest of the patients had mild ocular changes. Furthermore, more than half of the patients in our cohort had no significant abnormality in FAF (9/17), while 59.26% (16/27) had detectable abnormalities in the EZ and/or IZ on OCT. In previous reports, approximately 48.28% of patients (42/87) had normal FAF results, and the rest had abnormal fluorescence in the macula or perimacular areas. Among all patients with occult maculopathy, 97.40% (225/231) exhibited abnormalities in the EZ and/or IZ on OCT, while among those with RP1L1-associated OMD, 98.37% (181/184) showed such changes. This demonstrates that the detection of abnormalities is highly dependent on the imaging modality. While fundus color photography shows low sensitivity, OCT demonstrates high sensitivity and strong concordance with molecular diagnosis.

While VF of all 10 patients in our study demonstrated consistent paracentral scotoma changes, a distinct phenotypic heterogeneity was present in previous studies, including 83.85% of patients with central scotoma (135/161), approximately 5.59% with paracentral scotoma (9/161), and approximately 10.56% with normal visual fields (17/161). Although the patients with paracentral scotomas in our cohort had a shorter disease duration (4.20 ± 3.43 vs. 12.51 ± 13.21 years) and younger mean age (24.75 ± 16.11, range 6–53 years vs. 45.55 ± 19.92 years, range 14–91 years) compared to previously reported populations, the reasons for the differences in VF changes remain unclear and require further studies to be clarified. Separately, microperimetry examination revealed abnormal findings in four of the five patients assessed (80.00%), consistent with previous reports of reduced macular sensitivity.49,58

Based on electrophysiologic examination, 86.67% of the patients in this cohort (13/15) had cone and rod cell responses in the normal range on ffERG examination (Supplementary Fig. S5, Supplementary Table S4), and all had abnormal alterations on mfERG examination. This strongly aligns with the aggregate literature data; 90.37% of the patients (122/135) had completely normal ERG examinations, and all had abnormal alterations on mfERG examination. In contrast to the 96.43% abnormality rate (27/28 eyes, manifested as either prolonged P100 latency or reduced amplitude) reported in literature (mean age 41.72 years),59–62 only 28.57% of our cohort exhibited p-VEP abnormalities. The lower rate of p-VEP abnormalities observed in our study (28.57%) compared to the literature (96.43%) likely relates to the significantly younger average age of our cohort (18.21 years vs. 41.72 years). However, the observed abnormalities similarly presented as reduced P100 amplitude, consistent with prior findings.

Research regarding AO imaging in OMD is currently limited to five published studies, which include three single-patient case reports and two small-scale observational studies involving 13 participants.14,49,63–65 The AO imaging from these five studies consistently revealed decreased density and irregular mosaic of cone cells in the macular region, as observed in the three patients in our study. These findings suggest that AO imaging has potential diagnostic value for OMD. Notably, even in asymptomatic carriers, AO imaging can detect significant cone deficits within the central retina before abnormalities become apparent on conventional examinations,49 highlighting its potential value for identifying early pathological changes. Further studies are expected to assess the specificity and long-term monitoring of AO imaging for OMD in more cases.

Based on long-term follow-up of the BCVA status of patients in our cohort, a total of 88.00% of patients (22/25) had a BCVA of worse than 0.52 logMAR (the criterion for low vision) in the better eye at the final examination, and in the literature approximately 62.96% of patients (153/243) were reported to have a BCVA of worse than 0.52 logMAR in the better eye. Both this study and existing reports confirm that progressive visual decline is a central feature of the natural history of occult maculopathy.

There is currently no treatment for occult maculopathy, and the only options for management are supportive care and genetic counseling. Previous research revealed that the RP1L1 protein is localized to the axoneme of outer segments and connecting cilia in rod photoreceptors, and RP1L1 knockout mice exhibit disorganized photoreceptor outer segments, reduced electroretinogram amplitude, and progressive photoreceptor degeneration.66 The lesions in patients with occult maculopathy are mainly concentrated in the macular region, but the mouse retina lacks macular structures, so there are some limitations in studying occult maculopathy disease in the mouse animal model, and future research may be able to focus on stem cells to construct retinal organoids with defective RP1L1 genes and explore possible therapeutic options on this basis.67

Conclusions

A normal-appearing eye on routine examination with a characteristic change on mfERG remains the key feature of occult maculopathy, although other atypical changes may be detected in subsets of patients on OCT, FAF, microperimetry, and AO imaging. These additional examinations are helpful in the differential diagnosis, but their clinical value remains to be verified in future studies.
