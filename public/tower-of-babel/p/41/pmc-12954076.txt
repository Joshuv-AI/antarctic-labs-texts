Prolonged dysregulation and pathological changes in the upper respiratory tract of SARS-CoV-2 infected hamsters
Authors: Feifei Liu, Yao Xia, Andrew Chak-Yiu Lee, Yanxia Chen, Zhe Chen, Yixin He, Celia Hoi-Ching Chan, Jianpiao Cai, Terrence Tsz-Tai Yuen, Zi-Wei Ye, Jinxia Zhang, Zhaohui Qian, Kwok-Yung Yuen, Jasper Fuk-Woo Chan, Hin Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRespiratory long COVID symptoms, including dyspnea and breathing pattern disorders, are frequently reported in convalescent COVID-19 patients. Yet the mechanisms driving these persistent respiratory manifestations remain poorly elucidated. In this study, we characterized persistent upper respiratory tract pathology in SARS-CoV-2-infected hamsters. Strikingly, we observed persistent expression of SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (N) protein and subgenomic RNA (sgRNA) gene, which resulted in sustained tissue pathologies, dysregulation of pro-inflammatory markers, pro-apoptotic genes, as well as the altered expression of viral entry receptors, in the nasal turbinate of infected hamsters up to 120 days post-infection. These findings indicate that residual viral components may contribute to dysregulation of tissue repair and remodeling, chronic tissue pathology, and potentially enhanced susceptibility to secondary respiratory infections upon SARS-CoV-2 infection even upon recovery of acute infection. Collectively, our study provides insights into potential drivers of post-acute COVID-19 sequelae in the upper respiratory tract.Subject terms: Diseases, Immunology, Microbiology, Pathogenesis

Introduction

Long COVID is defined as the persistence or emergence of symptoms three months or more after the initial SARS-CoV-2 infection1,2. These symptoms encompass respiratory, extra-pulmonary, immune-related, and neurocognitive sequelae3–7 and are estimated to impact at least 65 million individuals worldwide8. To date, more than 200 symptoms of long COVID have been identified that might impact different human organ systems9. Previous studies have reported that SARS-CoV-2 may persist in multiple tissues of infected patients, including the nasal mucosa, lungs, gut epithelium, and olfactory neuroepithelium10–14. Earlier research on post-COVID-19 condition indicated that a substantial proportion of convalescent patients (5–40%) experience persistent respiratory symptoms15–17. Despite these findings, the long-term pathological alterations in the upper respiratory tract including the nasal turbinate tissues remain largely unexplored.

Previous animal models for SARS-CoV-2 studies, including mice and hamsters, have primarily been utilized to investigate acute infections and transmission, with samples typically collected between 2 and 7 days post-infection (dpi)18–26. Due to the differences in lifespan and viral kinetics between human and laboratory animals, most animal studies on long COVID harvested samples before 60 dpi and have been focused predominantly on neutralizing antibody response, pulmonary function, or neuropathological outcomes27–30. Currently, there is limited information on the long-term viral clearance and pathology in the upper respiratory tract of infected animals upon SARS-CoV-2 infection. In this study, we employed the Syrian hamster model to investigate dynamic pathological changes in the upper respiratory tract following wildtype SARS-CoV-2 infection at 4, 7, 42, 84, and 120 dpi, as well as the effects of heterologous and homologous reinfection on respiratory tract pathology. We observed persistent expression of SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (N) protein and viral subgenomic RNA (sgRNA) in the nasal turbinate of infected hamsters, accompanied by sustained dysregulation of pro-apoptotic and pro-inflammatory responses until at least 120 dpi. These molecular alterations correlated with long-term histopathological changes in the upper respiratory tract. Furthermore, primary SARS-CoV-2 infection triggered lasting modifications in the expression of viral entry factors within the nasal turbinate, which may alter susceptibility to secondary respiratory viral infections. Together, these findings underscore the importance of proactive management of SARS-CoV-2 infections, particularly among elderly COVID-19 patients, even under the current Omicron era.

Results

Persistent antigen expression and pathological changes in hamster nasal turbinate at 120 days post infection

Tissues of SARS-CoV-2-infected animals are routinely harvested at 2–7 dpi18–21,31,32. We recently demonstrated that SARS-CoV-2 induced chronic lung inflammation and persistent alveolar-bronchiolization in the infected hamsters33. To investigate the potential long-term impact of SARS-CoV-2 on the nasal turbinate of the infected animals, we explored the resolution progress of the nasal turbinate tissues of infected hamsters post wild-type (WT) SARS-CoV-2 infection. To this end, we intranasally challenged 6-8 weeks old hamsters with 1 × 103 PFU of WT SARS-CoV-2 and sacrificed the experimentally infected hamsters at 4, 7, 42, 84, 120 dpi to collect serum, lung, and nasal turbinate tissues for virological and pathological assessments (Fig. 1a). Mock-infected hamsters were included as controls. The expression of RNA-dependent-RNA-polymerase gene (RdRp) and the subgenomic envelope (sgE) gene in the nasal turbinate peaked at 4 dpi. Interestingly, we observed prolonged viral RNA detection in the nasal turbinate tissues of infected hamsters. In a fraction of these animals, both the RdRp and sgE genes remained detectable at 120 dpi (29% or 2/7 positive for RdRp and 57% or 4/7 positive for sgE (Fig. 1b). In keeping with the virus gene copy results, the expression of SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (N) protein in the nasal turbinate of infected hamsters was most robust at 4 dpi but was persistently detected in a fraction of the infected hamsters at 42 dpi (14% or 1/7), 84 dpi (20% or 2/10), and 120 dpi (18% or 2/11) (Fig. 1c). In contrast, we did not detect infectious titer in the nasal turbinate of infected hamster starting at 42 dpi, suggesting a rapid diminish of infectious virus particles despite persistent retention of viral component in the infected tissue at extended time points post infection (Fig. 1d). In addition, we quantified the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in the infected hamster nasal turbinate tissues. Unexpectedly, our result demonstrated a progressive induction of pro-inflammatory cytokine response, which gradually peaked at 84 dpi. At 120 dpi, the expression of most of the evaluated pro-inflammatory markers, including IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-6, IP-10, CCL-3, and CCL-5, remained substantially higher than that of the age-matched mock-infected hamsters (Fig. 1e). H&E staining showed that extensive submucosal immune cell infiltration and epithelial cell death, resulting in loss of the surface layer of epithelial cells while the nasal cavity contains exudates mixed with red blood cells, immune cells and apoptotic cells at 4 and 7 dpi (Fig. 2). Importantly, we found that excessive neutrophil infiltration and respiratory epithelium damage were readily detected in infected hamsters at 42 dpi (57% or 4/7 of hamsters), and this pattern of excessive cellular infiltration and epithelium cell death remained evident at 84 dpi (33% or 1/3 of hamsters) and 120 dpi (29% or 2/7 of hamsters) when compared with the mock-infected hamsters (Fig. 2). Together, these findings indicate that viral RNA, viral N protein, and chronic pathological changes in the nasal turbinate of SARS-CoV-2-infected hamsters persist at least until 120 dpi in the infected hamsters.

Dysregulated cellular apoptosis in hamster nasal turbinate during the chronic phase of SARS-CoV-2 infection

Coronavirus infection triggers apoptosis in the infected cells, which contributes to coronavirus replication and tissue damage34–36. We next performed terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining of the nasal turbinate and found that persistent apoptotic signatures (DNA fragmentation) remained readily detected in the nasal epithelium of infected hamsters at 120 dpi but not in the age-matched control hamsters, indicating prolonged apoptosis was triggered in the nasal epithelium upon SARS-CoV-2 infection (Fig. 3a). Furthermore, analysis of apoptosis-related gene expression revealed sustained upregulation of pro-apoptotic markers, including FASL, FADD, and caspase-3, in the infected hamster nasal turbinate tissues through at least 120 dpi, while the expression of the anti-apoptotic gene BCL-2 was not significantly different when compared to the age-matched mock-infected controls (Fig. 3b). Interestingly, we observed distinct temporal patterns of apoptosis-related gene expression between lung and nasal turbinate tissues. In lung tissues, the expression of pro-apoptotic markers (BAX, FASL, FADD, and caspase-3) peaked at 42 dpi, followed by a gradual return to baseline levels by 120 dpi when compared to age-matched controls. Interestingly, the anti-apoptotic gene BCL-2 exhibited sustained downregulation in lungs. At 120 dpi, BCL-2 expression in lung remained significantly lower when compared with the age-matched controls (Supplementary Fig. 1). Taken together, these findings demonstrate a persistently elevated apoptotic response in the nasal turbinate of infected hamsters following primary SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Prolonged changes in the expression of viral entry factors in hamster nasal turbinate at 120 days post infection

To explore the potential of altered susceptibility to secondary viral infections following primary SARS-CoV-2 infection, we assessed the expression levels of viral receptors and entry factors, including angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2), transmembrane protease serine 2 (TMPRSS2), dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP4), aminopeptidase N (APN), glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78), CX3C motif chemokine receptor 1 (CX3CR1), nucleolin (NCL), nectin cell adhesion molecule-4 (Nectin-4), that facilitate the entry of various respiratory viruses, including SARS-CoV-1, MERS-CoV, HCoV-HKU1, HCoV-229E, HCoV-NL63, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and measles virus (MV)37–48. Interestingly, by comparing the gene expression level between day 0 and day 120 of the mock-infected hamsters, we observed a significant age-dependent increase in gene expression for ACE2, TMPRSS2, and Nectin-4. For these genes, the expression in the Day 120 age-matched mock controls was significantly higher when compared to their Day 0 controls (Fig. 4). In contrast, age-related expression was observed for TMPRSS2 and APN in hamster lungs (Supplementary Fig. 2). Importantly, we found that the expression of CX3CR1 was significantly upregulated following SARS-CoV-2 infection and remained elevated until at least 120 dpi in the nasal turbinate tissues, showing a 7.7-fold increase compared with the day 120 mock control. A similar trend was also observed for DPP4 and GRP78, which were upregulated by 2.8-fold and 8.6-fold, respectively, relative to the day 120 mock control, although these differences did not reach statistical significance. (Fig. 4). Taken together, these findings suggest that prior SARS-CoV-2 infection may enhance susceptibility in the hamster respiratory tracts to certain secondary viral infections.

Persistent upregulation of the Notch pathway in hamster nasal turbinate during the chronic phase of SARS-CoV-2 infection

Our previous study demonstrated that the persistent activation of the Notch pathway in hamster lungs during the chronic phase of SARS-CoV-2 infection might disrupt tissue regeneration33. The Notch signaling pathway is a highly conserved system regulating cell proliferation, stemness maintenance, differentiation, fate specification, and cell death49. To investigate whether this pathway is similarly activated in the nasal turbinate, we analyzed the expression of Notch pathway related genes. Notably, the expression of Notch1, Notch3, PSEN1, RBPJ, HES1, and HEY1 were significantly upregulated following primary SARS-CoV-2 infection, peaking at 84 dpi. Interestingly, Notch3 and HEY1 expression remained significantly elevated compared to the age-matched mock-infected controls at 120 dpi (Supplementary Fig. 3), indicating sustained Notch pathway activation in the nasal turbinate during chronic infection. Together with the dysregulated apoptotic response in the nasal turbinate, these data suggest that primary SARS-CoV-2 infection impairs tissue repair and remodeling in the upper respiratory tract of hamsters.

The impact of reinfection on the prolonged nasal turbinate pathology at 84 dpi

It is well-documented that prior infection reduces the virus replication of secondary infection in SARS-CoV-2 reinfection events. However, it is less clear how reinfection will modulate the residual nasal turbinate pathology resulted from the primary infection. To this end, we performed rechallenge at 84 dpi using SARS-CoV-2 Beta (B.1.351) or WT strains. The 84 dpi time point was selected for rechallenge since the peak cytokine response was detected in the nasal turbinate tissues at this time point (Fig. 1e). To this end, we intranasally rechallenged previously infected hamsters at 84 dpi with SARS-CoV-2 Beta (B.1.351) or WT. We included hamsters with primary Beta and WT infection as controls and sacrificed the infected hamsters at 4 dpi to collect serum, lung, and nasal turbinate tissues for virological and pathological assessments (Supplementary Fig. 4a). We performed microneutralization test (MNT) to determine neutralizing antibodies titer against each variant in the serum of convalescent hamsters at day 84 after primary WT infection (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Serum neutralization titer against homologous virus (Geometric Mean of WT = 51.9) was significantly higher than the titer against Beta (Geometric Mean of Beta = 13.0). Consistent with reinfection performed at earlier time points reported in previous studies27,28,50, viral RNA level in both lung and NT were significantly lower in the reinfected hamsters when compared to hamsters with primary infections (Supplementary Fig. 4c), while infectious titers and expression of viral N protein largely diminished in the reinfected hamsters (Supplementary Fig. 4d, e). These results suggested that prior infection effectively limited virus replication in the hamster NT tissues when reinfected at 84 dpi.

As the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokine, cell infiltration, and respiratory epithelium apoptosis remain elevated in infected hamsters at 84 dpi (Figs. 1–3), we evaluated the potential impact of reinfection at 84 dpi to the residual histological changes in hamster nasal turbinate tissues. We detected acute pathology in the nasal turbinate tissues all reinfected hamsters at 4 dpi resembling primary infection, including extensive submucosal immune cell infiltration, hemorrhage, and widespread epithelial cell death and desquamation (Fig. 5a), despite viral replication was significantly restricted in nasal tissues (Supplementary Fig. 4c–e). Interestingly, reinfection did not upregulate pro-inflammatory cytokine/chemokine genes (IFN-α, IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-6, IP-10, CCL-3, CCL-5) in nasal turbinate at 4 days post reinfection compared to the 84 dpi baselines (Fig. 5b). In summary, these results suggest that both homologous and heterologous reinfection at 84 dpi induce severe nasal turbinate pathology despite the absence of pro-inflammatory marker upregulation. The dissociation between inflammation and tissue damage implicates non-inflammatory effector mechanisms, including cytotoxic T cell activity or metabolic stress, in mediating injury during rechallenge.

Discussion

As SARS-CoV-2 Omicron transitions to the endemic phase, it generates subvariants with distinct immune escape capacities and virological characteristics, which drive recurrent waves of infection in human populations26,51–57. A substantial proportion of acute COVID-19 survivors experience long-term respiratory symptoms, including dyspnea, erratic breathing patterns, tachypnea, and persistent cough2,3. However, the underlying pathological mechanisms driving these respiratory sequelae remain poorly understood. In this study, we investigated the resolution of infection in the nasal tissues following primary infection with WT SARS-CoV-2 in a hamster model. Our findings revealed persistent SARS-CoV-2 N protein expression, sustained viral RNA detection and chronic pathological changes in nasal tissues of SARS-CoV-2 infected hamsters, accompanied by sustained dysregulation of pro-inflammatory, pro-apoptotic, and Notch signaling pathways, along with altered viral entry factor expression in respiratory tissues through 120 dpi. These results suggest that SARS-CoV-2 infection may induce long-lasting changes in nasal tissues, potentially contributing to the persistence of post-acute symptoms.

Persistent upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines/chemokines (IFN-α, IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-6, IP-10, CCL-3, CCL-5), apoptosis-related genes (BAX, FASL, FADD, caspase3), Notch pathway-related genes (Notch3, HEY1), and upregulation of host factors (CX3CR1) involved in virus entry, accompanied by abnormal immune cell infiltration and respiratory epithelium apoptosis, was observed in nasal turbinates, which suggests that the virus can trigger long-term pathological remodeling. Supporting our results, a clinical study reported that SARS-CoV-2 N protein was identified in the nasal mucociliary mucosa of all eight patients who had chronic inflammatory rhinopathy disease more than 80 days after the initial diagnosis of mild COVID-1910. Inflammation in nasal tissues is considered as one of the factors contributing to olfactory impairment caused by SARS-CoV-210,58,59, but the precise mechanism or pathogenesis remains unclear. Interestingly, inflammatory markers in the infected hamsters peak at 84 dpi, when viral antigen positivity is relatively low. This finding indicates that persistent viral antigens may drive innate immune activation via pattern recognition receptors, establishing a pro-inflammatory and interferon-rich microenvironment. Over time, the inflammation may be self-sustaining or immune-mediated, independent of viral remnants. The upregulation of viral entry factors post-infection, such as CX3CR1, suggests SARS-CoV-2 infection may establish an increased state of susceptibility to other respiratory viruses that utilize these factors for entry. Furthermore, the correlation between older age and heightened expression of certain viral entry factors (ACE2, TMPRSS2, and Nectin-4) in the nasal turbinate implies that older individuals may face a greater risk of secondary infections following SARS-CoV-2 infection.

The Syrian golden hamster serves as a primary rodent model for studying SARS-CoV-2. Unlike mice, which typically require genetic modification, hamsters naturally express a human-like ACE2 receptor, making them highly susceptible to infection and better able to mimic post-infection disease progression in humans60. Furthermore, ethical and practical limitations in obtaining sequential human tissue samples make the hamster model invaluable for studying the dynamic pathology of long-term disease development. Importantly, golden hamsters have an average lifespan of 2–3 years61. A period of 120 hamster days after maturity translates to approximately 15-years in humans62. While the physiological conditions of hamsters may not completely compares to those of human beings, the findings of SARS-CoV-2-induced pathological changes in the upper tract of infected hamsters at 120 dpi will warrant more extensive longitudinal studies in convalescent human patients.

Overall, our study demonstrates that SARS-CoV-2 infection can cause persistence of viral components, long-term inflammatory and apoptotic responses, activation of the Notch pathway, and changes in gene expression of viral entry factors in upper respiratory tissues. These persistent alterations at least in part account for the observed respiratory long COVID sequelae. Our results highlight the importance of using this hamster model in addressing long-term virus-induced respiratory pathology in mammals. The identified alterations reveal potential targets for future therapeutic strategies to mitigate chronic tissue damage and improve patient outcomes.

Methods

Viruses, cell lines, and biosafety

The SARS-CoV-2 Beta (B.1.351) strain (GenBank accession number EPI_ISL_2423556) and wild type (HK-13) strain (GenBank accession number MT835140) were isolated from lab-confirmed COVID-19 patients in Hong Kong63,64. All the SARS-CoV-2 stocks were prepared and titrated for plaque-forming units (PFUs) in VeroE6-TMPRSS2 cells. All presented experiments involving live SARS-CoV-2 were performed in a Biosafety Level 3 facility at the University of Hong Kong (HKU) following approved standard operating procedures.

Animals

Male Syrian golden hamsters, aged 6–8 weeks, were obtained from the Chinese University of Hong Kong Laboratory Animal Service Centre through the HKU Centre for Comparative Medicine Research. All animal experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Committee on the Use of Live Animals in Teaching and Research (CULATR) of The University of Hong Kong (Protocol number: 5783-21).

Intranasal inoculation of hamsters with SARS-CoV-2

To characterize the dynamic pathological changes in respiratory tracts following SARS-CoV-2 infection, hamsters were anesthetized with ketamine (200 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) and inoculated intranasally with 50 μl PBS containing wild-type SARS-CoV-2 (1 × 10³ PFU) at day 0. Age-matched control animals received an equal volume of PBS via identical administration. Cohorts were humanely euthanized by intraperitoneal pentobarbital sodium injection (200 mg/kg) at 4, 7-, 42-, 84-, and 120 dpi. To evaluate infection-induced immune protection, convalescent hamsters were similarly anesthetized and challenged intranasally with SARS-CoV-2 variants (1 × 10³ PFU in 50 μl PBS) at 84 days post-primary infection. Control groups received PBS alone. All animals were euthanized at 4-dpi. At each endpoint, comprehensive samples including nasal turbinate, lungs, and serum were collected for virological and/or histopathological studies. Our study was conducted using a time-series analytical framework based on a longitudinal cohort design. A single cohort of animals was inoculated and followed.

Cytokines/Chemokines, viral receptors, apoptosis, and Notch signaling genes profiling

Total RNA from lung and nasal turbinate tissues was extracted with MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (9767 A, Takara) and then reverse transcribed into cDNA with PrimeScriptTM RT reagent kit (RR036A, Takara). RT-qPCR was carried out with gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table 1) using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II Kit (RR820A, Takara). Values of each gene were normalized with hamster house-keeping gene β-actin and presented as 2−ΔCas we previously described65.

Microneutralization antibody assay

Neutralizing antibody titers against SARS-CoV-2 in serum taken at different time point after vaccination and virus challenge were determined by microneutralization assay in VeroE6-TMPRSS2 cells. Serum samples were 2-fold diluted serially starting at 1:5, mixed with 100 TCID50 of SARS-CoV-2 (Beta and WT), and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Then the mixture of serum/virus was added to VeroE6-TMPRSS2 cells in 96-well plates for one-hour virus adsorption at 37 °C. Finally, the cells were washed with PBS and cultured at 37 °C in 5%CO2 Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (11965092, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 units penicillin, 100 μg/ml streptomycin, and 2% G418 (ant-gn-5, InvivoGen, China). Cytopathic effects (CPE) were evaluated at 72 h after cell culture. The microneutralization titre (MNT) was defined as the highest dilution of serum that completely inhibited cytopathic effect in 50% of the wells of VeroE6-TMPRSS2 cells.

Determination of viral load by real-time reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and fifty percent tissue culture infectious doses (TCID50) assay

Lung and nasal turbinate tissues were homogenized, and total RNA was extracted with MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (9767A, Takara). SARS-CoV-2 RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) and subgenomic E (SgE) gene copies were determined by one-step RT-qPCR using QuantiNova Probe RT-PCR Kit (208354, Qiagen) with the Light Cycler 480 Real-Time PCR System (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The primer and probe sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 1. Infectious virus titer in lung and nasal turbinate samples was determined by TCID50 assay in VeroE6-TMPRSS2 cells as we previously described36. In brief, tissue samples were homogenized in culture medium, then the supernatant was collected after centrifugation. The samples were 10-fold diluted serially with culture medium and inoculated into confluent monolayer of VeroE6-TMPRSS2 cells. After 1-h virus absorption time at 37 °C, the inoculum was removed, cells were washed with PBS and cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Cytopathic effects (CPE) were observed after 72 h cells culture, and 50% tissue culture infectious doses were calculated.

Histopathology, immunofluorescence staining, and TUNEL staining

Formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded lung and nasal turbinate tissues were cut into 4-μm tissue sections and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological examination as we previously described66. Immunofluorescence (IF) staining was performed using a standard protocol. Briefly, after blocking with 1% BSA, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies to viral antigens (1:4000) (in-house produced rabbit anti-SARS-CoV-2 N antibody) overnight at 4 °C60. FITC-conjugated (Invitrogen, 65-6111) secondary antibodies were used for immunofluorescent staining, while the nuclei were counterstained with VECTASHIELD Antifade Mounting Solution/DAPI (Vector Laboratories). TUNEL staining was performed for in situ apoptosis detection by using Click-iT Plus TUNEL Assay kit (Invitrogen, C10617). Immunofluorescent and bright field images were captured using Olympus BX53F Light/Fluorescence microscope (Olympus Life Science) and Tiles was applied to capture the monolayer image of three lobes of hamster lung by using Cytation 7 imaging multimode reader (BioTek, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. An unpaired two-tailed t-test was used to determine statistical significance between two groups. One-way ANOVA was used to determine statistical significance between three or more groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The figures and graphs in the manuscript were prepared with GraphPad Prism 8, Adobe Illustrator, and BioRender.com.
