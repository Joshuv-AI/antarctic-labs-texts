Puberty-Associated Emergence of Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Gil Mashiah, Nili Schamroth, Gili Yaeger-Yarom, Livia Kapusta, Alexander Lowenthal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a genetically heterogeneous disease with variable and incomplete penetrance, with phenotypic expression occurring across developmental stages, potentially influenced by hormonal and other biological modifiers. Genotype-positive individuals may remain phenotype-negative during childhood, with phenotypic expression emerging during adolescence or early adulthood. Factors influencing the timing of phenotypic conversion remain incompletely understood.Case SummaryA 13-year-old asymptomatic boy with a strong family history of hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy underwent routine cardiology follow-up. History of Presentation

A 13-year-old boy underwent routine follow-up in our pediatric cardiology clinic because of a positive family history of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM). Growth and pubertal development had been normal to date, and he remained asymptomatic (phenotype-negative) from a cardiovascular standpoint. During a scheduled preparticipation evaluation, a cardiac systolic murmur was detected.

Past Medical History

The patient had no prior cardiovascular events or interventions. At age 9, he underwent electrocardiography (ECG), which showed sinus rhythm, a normal axis, no ST-T changes, and a normal QTc interval. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated normal biventricular size and function, no evidence of left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction, an interventricular septal thickness of 51 mm, and a posterior wall thickness of 55 mm. Holter monitoring performed at that time revealed no significant conduction abnormalities.

His family history was significant for hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM) on the paternal side: his father, paternal grandfather, and paternal aunt were affected, and the paternal aunt's daughter also carries an MYH7 variant. The paternal aunt experienced sudden cardiac arrest at age 12 and subsequently underwent heart transplantation. The paternal grandfather also underwent heart transplantation due to heart failure attributed to his HOCM. The father is asymptomatic but has a prophylactic implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD). Genetic testing of the patient confirmed a pathogenic MYH7 variant (c.2471C>T, exon 22).

Investigations

Now at age 13, an ECG showed sinus rhythm with left axis deviation, incomplete right bundle branch block, normal PR and QTc intervals, and mild ST-T elevation in V2-V3. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved biventricular function, significant asymmetric septal hypertrophy (20 mm septum, 8 mm posterior wall), normal mitral valve anatomy, and systolic anterior motion with LVOT obstruction (see Figure 1).

Echocardiographic Temporal Findings

Parasternal short-axis view at age 9 years showing no left ventricular hypertrophy (A), with development of left ventricular hypertrophy at age 13 years (B), and the corresponding parasternal long-axis view at age 13 years, showing a maximal septal thickness of 20 mm (C).

Holter monitoring revealed occasional premature ventricular complexes and isolated episodes of second-degree atrioventricular block (Mobitz type I).

Cardiac magnetic resonance confirmed asymmetric mid-basal septal hypertrophy (maximal thickness: 1.7 cm), systolic anterior motion, LVOT obstruction, mild apical narrowing with preserved contractility, and minimal late gadolinium enhancement at the superior and inferior septal insertions. Native T1 and T2 mapping values were within normal limits (see Figure 2).

Management

The patient was asymptomatic from a cardiovascular standpoint. Based on clinical findings, his estimated 5-year risk of sudden cardiac death was 1.9% according to the European Society of Cardiology risk calculator.1

Accordingly, the patient was advised to engage in low- to moderate-intensity physical activity and to avoid high-intensity or exhaustive exertion, defined as exercise during which conversation cannot be maintained. Physical activity was recommended only in environments with immediate access to an automated external defibrillator and supervision by a trainer certified in basic life support.

At this stage, pharmacologic therapy with beta-blockers was not initiated given the absence of symptoms, preserved functional capacity, and lack of high-risk features, and there was no indication for ICD implantation.

Follow-Up and Outcomes

Planned follow-up included exercise stress echocardiography and repeat ambulatory ECG (Holter) monitoring at 6 months. Ongoing close clinical surveillance was recommended.

Notably, the patient's 11-year-old sister, who had also been under routine follow-up because of the strong family history, had previously demonstrated normal findings on cardiac evaluation. At age 11, recent transthoracic echocardiography revealed new septal hypertrophy with a maximal thickness of 11 mm. ECG, which had previously been unremarkable, now demonstrated sinus rhythm with left axis deviation and T-wave inversions in leads I and aVL. She was referred for further evaluation, including ambulatory ECG monitoring, cardiac magnetic resonance, and exercise stress echocardiography (Figure 3).

Discussion

HCM is a genetically heterogeneous disorder characterized by variable and incomplete penetrance with phenotypic expression occurring at different stages of somatic growth and hormonal development.2,3 Pathogenic variants in MYH7, one of the most frequently implicated sarcomeric genes, follow autosomal dominant inheritance and are characterized by incomplete penetrance and marked intrafamilial variability.4 Accordingly, genotype-positive individuals may have normal cardiac structure and function during childhood, with phenotypic conversion occurring later in life.5

Consistent with this paradigm, pediatric cohort studies and meta-analyses demonstrate that phenotypic expression in genotype-positive, phenotype-negative children is uncommon during childhood, with reported penetrance rates of approximately 6% to 7%, and additional conversion occurring during adolescence or early adulthood. Long-term follow-up studies show that approximately 6% to 7% of mutation-positive children develop HCM by early adulthood, supporting the need for continued surveillance beyond childhood.3,6 A recent meta-analysis further reported a pooled phenotypic conversion rate of 15% over a mean follow-up of 8 years, with higher conversion rates observed in MYH7 carriers compared with other sarcomeric genes, highlighting gene-specific and variable penetrance across developmental stages.7

In line with these observations, the 2024 American College of Cardiology guideline recommends ongoing cardiac surveillance for genotype-positive, phenotype-negative individuals at all ages, noting that 3% to 5% of children convert before age 18 and that phenotypic expression may also emerge later in young adulthood.8

The temporal association between puberty and the emergence of HOCM in this patient raises the question of whether pubertal growth and hormonal changes may act as modifiers or triggers of phenotypic expression in genetically predisposed individuals. Although experimental and clinical data directly linking puberty to disease expression in HCM are limited, rapid somatic growth, increased myocardial workload, and hormonal influences during adolescence have been proposed as potential contributors to myocardial hypertrophy.9,10 However, robust longitudinal data addressing this specific transition period are lacking, and causality cannot be established. Our case highlights the importance of serial imaging in patients with a family history of HCM before and after puberty.

This case, together with the recent phenotypic changes observed in the patient's younger sister, underscores the importance of continued surveillance in patients with a strong family history and not to stop surveillance in phenotype-negative children who may become phenotype-positive at a later age, particularly during periods of accelerated growth. Current practice guidelines recommend periodic cardiac evaluation in at-risk pediatric populations; however, the optimal timing and frequency of reassessment around puberty and growth spurts remain uncertain. These observations support consideration of intensified screening during adolescence, even in patients with previously normal investigations.

Sports participation in genotype-positive or early phenotype-positive adolescents remains a challenging and evolving area. Contemporary guidelines emphasize shared decision-making and individualized risk assessment rather than blanket restrictions.11, 12, 13 In this patient, pharmacologic therapy with beta-blockers was not initiated given the absence of symptoms, preserved functional capacity, and lack of high-risk features. Current clinical guidelines, including those of the European Society of Cardiology,14 generally recommend beta-blocker therapy primarily for symptomatic patients or those with significant LVOT obstruction. ICD implantation was also not indicated due to the low estimated 5-year risk of sudden cardiac death and the absence of additional major risk factors. These considerations supported a permissive approach to low- to moderate-intensity physical activity, with appropriate safety measures in place. Nevertheless, the dynamic nature of disease expression during adolescence underscores the need for ongoing reassessment of exercise recommendations over time.

Overall, this case illustrates the dynamic and age-dependent penetrance of MYH7-related HCM and highlights puberty as a potential window for phenotypic transition. It emphasizes the need for longitudinal follow-up of genotype-positive individuals, careful counseling regarding physical activity, and heightened clinical vigilance during adolescence.
