Progress and prospects of gut microbiota-targeted therapy for primary biliary cholangitis
Authors: Xuan Tang, Xin You
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary biliary cholangitis (PBC) is a chronic cholestatic autoimmune disease. Current therapeutic options are limited, with some patients responding poorly to first-line treatment with ursodeoxycholic acid. However, second-line drugs are difficult to develop. There are no drugs available to treat liver dysfunction. Currently, the etiology of PBC is unknown, and the intestinal flora affects the liver through the gut‒liver axis. The hypothesis of intestinal dysbiosis has gradually been accepted and involves mechanisms such as leaky gut, abnormal bile acids metabolism, and dysregulated immune tolerance. We found that gut microbiota-targeted therapy, including antibiotics, dietary regulation, probiotic supplementation, and fecal microbiota transplantation, can effectively improve liver function, remodel the intestinal microbiota, and alleviate symptom. However, this therapy has limitations, such as large individual differences and unknown long-term efficacy and safety. Large-scale and long-term clinical studies are expected to promote the broad application of gut microbiota-targeted therapy in the clinic.Keywords: Primary biliary cholangitis, Intestinal microecology, Dietary regulation, Probiotic, Fecal microbiota transplantation

Research background and significance

Increasing incidence of PBC

PBC (primary biliary cholangitis) is an autoimmune liver disease characterized by cholestasis, destruction of intrahepatic small bile ducts, and hepatic dysfunction [1], with an unknown etiology. Recent epidemiological analyses across multiple countries and regions globally have revealed increasing trends in both the incidence and prevalence of PBC [2]. PBC can occur at any age but predominantly affects women aged 40–70 years [1]. Over the past two decades, the female-to-male incidence ratio has gradually decreased from 9:1 to 4:1 [3]. Compared with females, male patients are often diagnosed at later disease stages, exhibit higher rates of hepatocellular carcinoma, and have poorer prognoses [3]. The laboratory hallmarks of PBC include elevated serum biomarkers of cholestasis, such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP), γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT), and bilirubin [4]. Additionally, PBC is associated with disease-specific autoantibodies, notably anti-mitochondrial antibodies (AMA) targeting pyruvate dehydrogenase complex-E2 (PDC-E2) on the inner mitochondrial membrane, which are present in more than 90% of patients [4] and lead to biliary damage [5].

Limited therapeutic options for PBC

Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) remains the only first-line therapy approved for PBC. UDCA improves biochemical parameters, delays disease progression, and prolongs transplant-free survival [6]. However, approximately 40% of patients exhibit suboptimal responses to UDCA despite adherence, with ongoing disease progression [2].

The development of second-line therapies is a critical focus in PBC, yet few options have demonstrated confirmed efficacy and safety. Obeticholic acid (OCA), a farnesoid X receptor (FXR) agonist [7], indirectly suppresses bile acids (BAs) synthesis by inhibiting cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase (CYP7A1) expression. Pruritus (77%) and fatigue (33%) are common adverse effects [8], with pruritus exhibiting dose dependency [9] (incidence was 56% in the 5-10 mg group, 68% in the 10 mg group and 58% in the placebo group [10]). OCA also reduces high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, potentially increasing cardiovascular risk [9, 10].

Fibrates (e.g., fenofibrate [11] and benzofibrate [12]) are also used to treat PBC. Fibrates can alleviate the degree of pruritus and reduce ALP levels compared with those in the placebo group. Importantly, the combination of UDCA and fibrates has been associated with skeletal muscle pain and increased serum creatinine levels compared with UDCA monotherapy [13]. Moreover, fibrates have not been approved for use in PBC and are still used beyond the drug’s specifications.

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPAR) are key factors in the regulation of hepatic lipid metabolism, and their abnormalities may be associated with hepatic steatosis, hepatic fibrosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma [14]. Elafibranor, a dual agonist of both PPARα and PPARδ, significantly reduced the levels of biochemical markers in patients compared with those in the placebo group (51% vs. 4%) [15]. In the study by Hirschfield et al. [16], Seladelpar, a selective PPARδ agonist, also provided support for the use of PPAR agonists, with results of 62% vs. 20% (compared with the placebo group). Extensive studies on PPAR agonists are still underway.

Although PBC is an autoimmune disease, no significant benefit has been found for the use of immunosuppressants and biologics [6]. Liver transplantation remains the definitive intervention for decompensated cirrhosis or refractory pruritus. Thus, novel therapeutic strategies are urgently needed.

The gut microbiota influences PBC through the gut‒liver axis

The gut microbiota, comprising bacteria, archaea, fungi, and viruses, influences host resistance to pathogenic infections and the maturation of organismal immunity [17]. The gut microbiota is closely associated with a variety of autoimmune diseases, such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), rheumatoid arthritis, and Sjögren syndrome. Increased intestinal mucosal permeability and aberrant immunomodulation by the gut microbiota and derivatives of their metabolism affect the development and progression of these diseases [18–20]. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), a microbiota-targeted therapy, can effectively treat SLE [21] and has become a potential direction in this field.

A series of studies have shown that intestinal dysbiosis is common in patients with PBC (Table 1). Compared with healthy controls, PBC patients exhibit a reduced abundance of beneficial bacteria (e.g., Bacteroidetes) and increased opportunistic pathogens (e.g., Firmicutes and Proteobacteria) [22–25]. In addition, Actinobacteriota, Desulfobacterota, and Verrucomicrobiota influence the therapeutic response to UDCA in PBC patients [23, 26].

The gut‒liver axis involves two-way communication between the gut, the gut microbiota, and the liver. The liver directly interacts with the translocated gut microbiota and its various metabolites through the portal vein [27, 28]. In the bile duct epithelium, antigen-presenting cells (including dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells, T cells and natural killer cells) are constantly exposed to a variety of pathogen-associated molecular patterns of intestinal origin [29]. These cells activate both the intrinsic and adaptive immune systems and secrete proinflammatory cytokines. This ongoing immune response can cause damage to the bile ducts and ultimately lead to the development of hepatic fibrosis [30]. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) and other immunosuppressive cells can reduce proinflammatory processes [31].

Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota is considered an important driver of PBC progression [32]. It is characterized primarily by an imbalance between beneficial and protective substances produced by microorganisms, as well as aberrant immune system responses to these substances [33]. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are the main metabolites of dietary fiber fermented by the gut microbiota. In addition to being a vital nutrient source for colonic epithelial cells, SCFAs also play a role in regulating host immune and inflammatory responses [34]. Butyrate inhibits the expression of nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) and reduces the production of proinflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [35]. Butyrate and propionate also inhibit lipopolysaccharide-induced cytokine production in human monocyte-derived dendritic cells [36]. Butyrate enhances and repairs barrier function by increasing the expression of the tight junction protein claudin-1 and promoting the redistribution of the tight junction proteins occludin and zonula occludens-1 [37, 38].

Jiang et al. [39] demonstrated that transplanting the fecal microbiota from PBC patients into germ-free mice can increase the serum ALP and total BAs levels and cause PBC-like liver lesions in mice. Further transplantation of the samples into PBC model mice revealed a more pronounced degree of liver injury than before transplantation.

The gut microbiota plays an important role in the occurrence and progression of PBC. Moreover, recent studies based on animal experiments have shown that butyric acid has a positive effect on alleviating PBC by modulating myeloid-derived suppressor cells function [40]. These findings suggest that the modulation of the gut microbiota and its metabolites may lead to the development of effective methods for the treatment of PBC.

Leaky gut and hepatic cholestasis

The gut microbiota maintains intestinal barrier integrity via colonization resistance [41], pathogen recognition receptor binding [42], immune stimulation [43], and anti-inflammatory SCFAs production [17]. Under normal conditions, hepatic immune tolerance prevents disease despite microbial antigen exposure via the portal circulation [44].

Increased intestinal permeability is common in PBC patients [45]. Enterococcus gallinarum and Klebsiella pneumoniae promote intestinal barrier dysfunction, enhance Th17 cell migration to the liver, and exacerbate cholangitis [46]. Lipoteichoic acid, a bacterial cell wall component, has been detected in damaged bile ducts of PBC patients, implicating bacterial translocation in pathogenesis [47]. Bacterial products such as lipopolysaccharides enter systemic circulation, trigger inflammation, impair hepatobiliary function, and worsen cholestasis [48]. In PBC murine models (dnTGFβRII mice), gut barrier disruption promotes hepatic bacterial translocation and T-cell-mediated cholangitis [49].

BAs metabolic dysregulation

BAs serve as potential biomarkers in PBC [50]. At physiological concentrations, they regulate metabolism and immunity. However, elevated BAs activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, driving cellular senescence and death [28, 51].

The gut microbiota modulates BAs synthesis and biotransformation [52], including CYP7A1 expression [53, 54]. Imbalanced bacterial BAs metabolism contributes to metabolic, inflammatory, and neoplastic disorders [55]. BAs reciprocally shape microbial diversity. Tauro-β-muricholic acid and taurocholic acid critically influence the composition of the adult gut microbiota [56]. High concentrations of deoxycholic acid and taurine-conjugated BAs disrupt microbial nucleotide and carbohydrate metabolism in mice [57].

Microbiota-mediated FXR dysfunction drives cholestasis and intestinal barrier failure. Normally, BAs activate FXR to suppress CYP7A1 and maintain BAs homeostasis [58]. Intestinal dysbiosis disrupts this feedback, exacerbating cholestasis.

Disturbances in BAs metabolism affect the degree of response to UDCA in PBC patients. Martinez et al. [26] reported that the relative abundance of glycine-conjugated BAs and taurine-conjugated UDCA was greater in the feces of UDCA responders, suggesting better hepatic recombination and excretion, reflecting improved cholestasis and liver function. Additionally, the study revealed that poor responders to UDCA had a lower abundance of the bile salt hydrolase (BSH) gene-carrying bacterial phylum than UDCA responders did.

Cross-reactivity targeting cholangiocytes

AMA is a significant biomarker for PBC [2]. The typical immune profile of PBC is the loss of immune tolerance toward the autoantigen PDC-E2 [1]. This autoantigen is present in nearly all nucleated cells, but the autoimmune response is highly specific for the biliary epithelium [59]. In PBC, antigen-presenting cells enhance CD8+ T-cell activation via phagocytosis of AMA-PDC-E2 immune complexes, selectively destroying cholangiocytes [60]. This may be due to the presence of the intact reduced form of PDC-E2 in the apoptotic bile duct epithelium, leading to bile duct-targeted injury [61].

Human PDC-E2 has structural homology with proteins expressed by Mycoplasma, E. coli, and Sphingobacterium aromaticum [62]. AMA can cross-react with bacterial proteins, triggering the autoimmune system to undergo mislocalization, misjudging normal bile duct epithelial cells as foreign pathogens and other deleterious components and thus initiating an autoimmune response against the bile ducts [63, 64], leading to bile duct destruction, progressive cholestasis and liver fibrosis [65]. Animal experiments have demonstrated that E. coli-infected B6 (Idd10/Idd18) mice develop AMA as well as immune-mediated biliary tract damage [66]. Furthermore, patients with recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs) have a higher risk of developing PBC than healthy controls do [67, 68], and E. coli is the most common pathogen isolated from patients with UTIs [68], which highlights the role of bacterial antigens in the pathogenesis of PBC.

Similar mechanisms occur in primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), another autoimmune liver disease (ALD), where perinuclear antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (p-ANCA) cross-reacts with the autoantigen β-tubulin and bacterial filamenting temperature-sensitive mutant Z (FtsZ) proteins [69–71]. Cross-recognition of self-antigens and bacterial homologues by p-ANCA may trigger the immune system to attack its own tissues, which in turn leads to autoimmune diseases.

Antibiotics regulate the gut microbiota

Given the potential role of intestinal microecological imbalance in biliary tract injury, some studies have proposed that antibiotics may be applied in the treatment of ALD (Table 2). Recently, clinical trials have reported that vancomycin, metronidazole, and rifaximin have potential therapeutic value in treating PSC [72–74]. In particular, the combination of metronidazole and UDCA significantly reduces the level of ALP, and improves the Mayo Risk Score [75]. The specific mechanism is unclear, and its clinical efficacy varies. Antibiotic therapy has not been widely adopted and remains in the exploratory stage.

Antibiotic therapy for PBC remains at the stage of animal experiments. Schrumpf et al. [76] showed that germ-free mice had less severe biliary injury than conventionally raised mice did (with reduced common bile duct dilation and periportal inflammation), indicating that the gut microbiota plays an important role in the progression of PBC. Additionally, oral antibiotics improved transaminase levels and biliary injury in conventionally raised mice. Similarly, another animal study showed that the modulation of intestinal flora with quadruple antibiotics reduced hepatic inflammation and associated splenomegaly [77].

Currently, antibiotic trials remain small-scale and limited in number, yet the results are generally favorable.

Dietary regulation reshapes the gut microbiota

Diet is a primary determinant of the gut microbial composition. Exogenous dietary components can specifically remodel gut microbial communities [78], thereby modulating the immune system and offering therapeutic potential for liver diseases such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), cirrhosis, and PBC.

Multiple randomized controlled trials have shown that Mediterranean diet (predominantly plant-based foods, with the intake of high-quality fats and proteins) improves the diversity of the gut microbiota, mainly by increasing the abundance of bifidobacteria and lactobacilli [79, 80]. It also increased the SCFAs content in the gut and decreased aminotransferase levels and liver cirrhosis in NAFLD patients [81].

High-fiber diets modulate the intestinal pH, favor beneficial bacteria (e.g., Lachnospiraceae) [82, 83], and enhance gut motility to limit pathogenic colonization [84, 85]. Lachnospiraceae are the main species that produce SCFAs [86], and dietary fiber fermentation by the gut microbiota generates SCFAs (especially butyric acid), which exert anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects by suppressing Th17 differentiation and promoting Tregs production [87]. Moreover, butyrate can attenuate immune-mediated bile duct injury in PBC by modulating myeloid-derived suppressor cell function [40]. Orliacq et al. [88] reported a negative correlation between dietary fiber intake and liver fat content, whereas a positive correlation was observed between the intake of refined grain starch and liver fat content.

The ketogenic diet has also been a hot research topic in this field recently. An 8-week clinical trial revealed that a ketogenic diet altered the human gut microbial composition and reduced intestinal proinflammatory Th17 cell levels [89]. Consistent with the findings of Smith et al. [87], the reduction in Th17 cell differentiation promoted the production of Tregs, which attenuated the autoimmune response.

Gluten proteins are difficult to digest by gastro-pancreatic enzymes and may form immunogenic polypeptide chains that activate the autoimmune system [90]. Additionally, gluten proteins are epithelial cytotoxic, disrupting epithelial integrity and causing more exogenous fragments to enter the circulation. Gluten-free diet (GFD) demonstrates potential therapeutic benefits in cholestatic liver diseases (CLD) (Table 3). In a 12-week prospective study [91], GFD was found to improve clinical and biochemical outcomes in PBC patients. Specifically, PBC patients'malaise and depressed mood were elevated from baseline. Moreover, dietary regulation was most effective at improving the levels of the inflammatory markers high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) [baseline level of 1.11 (IQR: 0.68−1.75), which decreased to 0.88 (IQR: 0.56−1.54) after the intervention, p=0.015] and GGT [baseline level of 1.36 (IQR: 0.64−2.19), which decreased to 0.87 (IQR: 0.66−1.5) after the intervention, p=0.01].

Liwinski et al. [92] also conducted a preliminary exploration of GFD for the treatment of PSC. Although liver stiffness and clinical symptoms were not significantly altered, the expression of several cytokines (e.g., IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, and TNF-α) in the intestinal mucosa was significantly decreased (p < 0.001). Further transcriptome analysis of the colonic mucosa revealed a significant increase in pathways associated with the expression of tight junction proteins, suggesting that dietary regulation may improve the integrity of the intestinal barrier. Two key substances associated with hepatic fibrosis and matrix remodeling (platelet reactive proteins 2 and 4) were also significantly reduced (p < 0.01).

Probiotic improves host health

Probiotic is active beneficial microorganisms that can improve BAs metabolism and repair intestinal barrier dysfunction by promoting the balance of the gut microbiota [93].

Probiotic therapy is widely used in CLD patients. A 21-day animal experiment demonstrated that supplementation with VSL#3 probiotic significantly altered the intestinal microbiota [94]. Specifically, there was a high abundance of Firmicutes, increasing from 37% pre-treatment to 51.47% post-treatment. In contrast, the abundances of Bacteroidetes (from 60.1% to 44.22%) and Proteobacteria (from 2.36% to 1%) decreased. Additionally, the VSL#3 probiotic was found to promote the dissociation of BAs in the ileum and the excretion of fecal BAs while also reducing hepatic cholestasis. The probiotic Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG) also has hepatoprotective effects [95]. Compared with control mice, experimental group presented significant improvements in hepatic inflammation, injury, and fibrosis. Further studies revealed that LGG decreased the concentration of taurine‐β‐muricholic acid (an FXR antagonist) and increased the concentration of chenodeoxycholic acid (an FXR agonist) in mice. All of these factors help reduce hepatic cholestasis. Lactobacillus acidophilus (L. acidophilus) plays an important protective role in PBC [96]. In studies with model mice, supplementation with L. acidophilus led to an obvious reduction in liver injury and a decrease in total liver BAs levels, along with an increase in the excretion of fecal BAs. A clinical randomized controlled trial was also conducted to verify these findings. Additionally, L. acidophilus was found to inhibit BAs synthesis through negative feedback by reducing the expression of CYP7A1 and fibroblast growth factor 15 (FGF-15). Animal studies have demonstrated that the probiotic Pediococcus pentosaceus Li05 can significantly reduce hepatic cholestasis and improve bacterial endotoxin translocation caused by intestinal barrier dysfunction [97].

In a randomized controlled clinical trial [98], NAFLD patients did not show significant changes in transaminase levels or the amount of liver fat after probiotic supplementation (MCP BCMC strain). However, at the micro-environmental level, specifically in the intestinal mucosa, probiotic helps stabilize mucosal immune function and reduce intestinal permeability. Probiotic therapy has demonstrated beneficial protective effects in alcoholic fatty liver disease (AFLD) [99]. Studies conducted by Lunia et al. [100] and Bajaj et al. [101] demonstrated that probiotic, specifically VSL#3 and LGG, can effectively improve intestinal dysbiosis in patients with hepatic encephalopathy (HE). These probiotic helps reduce inflammatory factors and lower arterial blood ammonia levels. Additionally, the safety and tolerability of probiotic in the treatment of HE have been thoroughly verified.

These findings underscore the potential of probiotic (Table 4), although strain selection, dosage, and treatment duration require personalized optimization.

FMT: applications and efficacy

FMT involves transferring the gut microbiota from a healthy donor into the patients’ intestine. This can be accomplished through oral administration, a nasoenteric tube, colonoscopy, or an enema. FMT can reshape the intestinal micro-ecological balance and effectively improve intestinal dysbiosis [102]. FMT was initially used for the treatment of recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection, and it is a reliable alternative treatment to vancomycin [103], with a success rate approaching 90% [104, 105].

FMT has been applied to various diseases, including multiple sclerosis [106], psoriatic arthritis [107], and rheumatoid arthritis [108]. This treatment can increase the number of probiotic associated with the production of SCFAs, modify the microbial community, and reduce disease activity.

FMT is also widely used to treat liver diseases (Table 5). Allegretti et al. [109] administered FMT treatment to 10 PSC patients. Among these patients, 30% experienced a greater than 50% decrease in ALP levels from baseline and an increase in the abundance of Odoribacter, Alistipes, and Erysipelotrichaceae, which produce SCFAs. Increased flora diversity was observed after one week after FMT and persisted until week 24. Clinical and basic researches on FMT treatment for liver cirrhosis have been carried out for many years, animal experiments have shown that FMT can improve the diversity, richness, and evenness of the gut microbiota [110], as manifested by an increase in the abundance of Lactobacillus and Bacteroidetes, with the abundance of Clostridium decreases. Moreover, the degree of liver fibrosis in the rats improved after FMT treatment. A clinical study conducted by Bajaj et al. [111] also showed that for patients with advanced liver cirrhosis, a single FMT treatment can restore gut microbiota diversity and functional pathways related to the microbiota. Mehta et al. [112] performed a single FMT in HE patients and reported an evident reduction in arterial ammonia levels. In addition, both the Child–Turcotte–Pugh scores and the end-stage liver disease modeling scores were lower than those before treatment (p < 0.01). Philips et al. [113] treated AFLD patients with one week of FMT. After one year of follow-up, they observed significant improvements in markers related to the severity of liver disease. Specifically, the mean bilirubin level decreased from 20.5 ± 7.6 mg/dl to 2.86 ± 0.69 mg/dl (p=0.01), and the Child-Turcotte-Pugh score improved from 14.5 ± 0.8 to 7.7 ± 1.2 (p < 0.01). These findings suggest that FMT treatment may improve disease severity and increase disease survival.

FMT has shown significant promise in the treatment of liver diseases. However, there are currently no published studies on FMT for PBC. The field is still in the stage of continuous exploration and improvement. In the future, larger and longer-term clinical studies are necessary to better assess its effectiveness and safety, providing a more robust foundation for the broader application of FMT in PBC.

Conclusion

The etiology of PBC remains unclear. Patients mainly have non-specific symptoms like fatigue and pruritus, along with specific autoantibodies and elevated transaminases. Most PBC petients previously first visited gastroenterology. Over the past two decades, studies on its pathogenesis have linked PBC closely to autoimmune disorders, leading to its classification as a rheumatic immune disease. About 40% of patients respond poorly to first-line therapy UDCA [2], and require second-line drugs. Advances in high-throughput sequencing and multi-omics have clarified the role of gut microbiota and their metabolites in various diseases.

PBC patients commonly have gut microbiota dysbiosis [22–24], which affects disease progression via the gut-liver axis, involving intestinal leakage, microbial translocation, bile acid metabolism, and abnormal immune responses. Gut microbiota-targeted therapy for PBC is still exploratory, with key limitations including individual differences. Variations in disease stage, underlying chronic conditions, and intestinal microecology among patients affect host-microbiota interactions. Another limitation is that most current studies have short observation periods, leaving long-term efficacy unclear. While short-term data and clinical practice show benefits like improved biochemistry and increased microbial diversity, long-term follow-up is lacking.It remains uncertain if such improvements persist, halt PBC progression, or reduce liver-related complication risks.

Dietary interventions, probiotic, and prebiotic have been used in PBC. Currently, there are no reports on the use of FMT for PBC. However, as mentioned earlier, FMT has shown initial efficacy in treating various acute and chronic liver diseases, and demonstrated great therapeutic potential in autoimmune diseases. Additionally, there are registered clinical trials of FMT for PBC treatment (ChiCTR2400081306).

Gut microbiota-targeted therapy, particularly FMT, has safety concerns that may limit its use in PBC treatment. The most common adverse reactions of FMT are mild to moderate gastrointestinal discomfort [114–116], like short-term abdominal distension, diarrhea, constipantion, and abdominal pain, most of which can resolve on their own. FMT also carries the risk of infection [117]. If the donor screening process is not thorough or there are flaws in the operation process, potential harmful microorganisms may be introduced into the patients’ body, may cause dangerous complications such as bloodstream infections. Though no cases have been reported, this procedure is not entirely safe. Therefore, prior to donor selection, infection screening is required [118], such as blood tests (cytomegalovirus, Epstein-Barr virus, hepatitis A virus, hepatitis B virus, hepatitis C virus, blood routine, etc.) and stool tests (Clostridium difficile, Salmonella, norovirus, etc.). Strict standards must also be followed during transportation and operation.

Changes in the intestinal microbiota can affect the immune system [117]. The gut microbiota and immune system maintain complex interactions. When FMT introduces foreign flora, it disrupts the original intestinal microbial balance, possibly leading to an overreaction, and such disruption may exacerbate inflammatory responses or trigger other adverse immune-related reactions.

To effectively understand the patients’ individual differences, it is essential to identify the most suitable dietary interventions, probiotic species, and donor sources for FMT. Additionally, determining the appropriate medication dosage and treatment duration is important. This requires integrating various tests and evaluations with long-term follow-up.
