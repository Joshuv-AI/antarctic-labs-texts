Identifying evidence-based treatments for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) among adolescents in sub-Saharan Africa: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Cebokazi Ngcakani Mtati, Katherine Sorsdahl, Claire van der Westhuizen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionChildren and adolescents in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) experience exceptionally high levels of trauma, including experiencing multiple forms of abuse and witnessing violence. Exposure to such traumatic events may lead to the development of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), or other health-related or behaviour-related harms. Untreated PTSD in adolescents results in significant impairments in their adaptive, social, emotional and academic functioning. To date, various intervention strategies have been developed internationally to reduce the severity of PTSD symptoms among children and adolescents. However, despite the documented mental health burden, there is limited evidence of culturally and contextually appropriate PTSD interventions in SSA. The objective of this scoping review is to investigate the evidence-based treatments available for adolescents diagnosed with PTSD in SAA. This investigation includes evaluating whether identified treatments were considered culturally and contextually appropriate.Methods and analysisThis scoping review will follow the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley. This framework outlines six essential stages for conducting scoping reviews: (1) Defining the research question, (2) Locating pertinent studies, (3) Study selection, (4) Charting the data, (5) Collating, summarising and reporting the results and (6) Consulting with stakeholders. Findings will be presented using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines. Major databases, including PubMed (Medline), Scopus Academic Search Premier, Africa-Wide Information, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, Education Resources Information Centre, Healthsource: Nursing/Academic, PsycINFO, PsycARTICLES and SocINDEX will be searched.Ethics and disseminationThis review will not involve the collection of primary data from human participants. While formal ethics approval is not required, permission to conduct the study has been obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Health Sciences at the University of Cape Town. The results of the scoping review will be submitted to a relevant academic journal, including presentations at relevant conferences, to contribute to academic literature and inform future research.Keywords: Adolescents, MENTAL HEALTH, Psychosocial Intervention, Child & adolescent psychiatry, Psychological Stress, Africa South of the Sahara

Background

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a significant public mental health concern among adolescents in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). According to the 5th edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5-TR), PTSD is a mental health condition that may develop after exposure to a traumatic or threatening event, whether it be by experience, witnessing the event or hearing about the event happening to others.1 The condition results in significant impairments in day-to-day functioning. Many adolescents who have experienced a traumatic event go on to develop symptoms of PTSD. PTSD may develop following a single traumatic event or from extended exposure to trauma. Symptoms of PTSD include ongoing intrusive memories, avoidance of trauma-related stimuli, negative changes in thoughts and mood, and heightened arousal.1

The prevalence of trauma exposure and mental health conditions among adolescents in SSA is substantial. Considering that 50% of adolescents aged 15–17 years across 24 African countries have experienced physical, sexual or emotional violence,2 we can estimate a high prevalence of PTSD among this population. According to meta-analyses, 15.9% of adolescents who have experienced trauma go on to develop PTSD.3 Although there are limited studies on the prevalence of PTSD in SSA, a systematic review estimated that the point prevalence for PTSD was 21.5% among adolescents in the region.4 Further, a systematic review by Cortina5 assessing the prevalence of child mental health problems in community settings in SSA found that 10% of children and adolescents met the diagnostic criteria for a mental health condition. Depressive, anxiety, conduct, disruptive and PTSDs were the most frequently reported.5 Considering that 23% of the population in SSA is made up of adolescents aged 10–19 years, these prevalence statistics underscore the significance of the issue.4

PTSD in adolescence is a debilitating condition that can leave lasting consequences on academic achievement, relationships and overall well-being. First, if left untreated, PTSD can persist for years,6 increasing the adolescents’ chances of participating in risky behaviours such as substance use, delinquent behaviours and risky sexual behaviours which can lead to unplanned teenage pregnancies and sexually transmitted infection/HIV.7,12 Second, adolescents with PTSD symptoms are significantly more likely to experience academic difficulties, due to impairments such as diminished executive functioning, problems with verbal fluency, disinhibition, reduced cognitive flexibility (adaptability and the ability to shift thinking crucial to academic learning) and reduced working memory.13 Thirdly, adolescents with PTSD are at a higher risk of developing other mental health conditions which may harm their psychosocial functioning in future life.6 9 Literature suggests that PTSD in adolescents is highly comorbid with other mental health conditions, such as depression,10 anxiety disorders,14 15 conduct disorders, adjustment disorders, attention disorders and substance use disorders.10 14 16 Fourthly, individuals experiencing PTSD symptoms often struggle with emotional regulation,17 engaging in unhealthy emotional coping strategies and negative thinking patterns.18

Globally, there is substantial evidence that psychological treatment can be effective for adolescents with PTSD, including those whose symptoms have persisted for many years.19,22 To date, various intervention strategies have been developed to reduce the severity of PTSD symptoms among children and adolescents. These include psychological therapies such as trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy (TF-CBT), eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing, narrative exposure therapy (KidNET) and prolonged exposure therapy.1923,31

A few studies have been conducted in SSA to evaluate the effectiveness and contextual relevance of adolescent PTSD interventions.32,36 These studies found that TF-CBT and prolonged exposure therapy (PE) could be feasible and effective treatment options for adolescents with PTSD in low-resource settings. Furthermore, a systematic review of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) conducted in SSA evaluated the efficacy of school-based psychological interventions in addressing common mental health conditions, including PTSD, among adolescents and young adults aged 10 to 24 years.33 Findings suggested that CBT was most frequently used and found to be more effective in reducing mental health symptoms among adolescents in SSA, as compared to other therapeutic approaches.33

Despite the knowledge of what works for the treatment of PTSD, access to mental health services for adolescents in SSA is often limited.37 Although there are several contributors to limited access to care,38 39 one major contributor is the availability of specialist mental healthcare providers.40 For example, across the 47 countries in the WHO African region, the average number of psychiatrists is 0.1 per 100 000 people, which is significantly lower than the global average of 0.3 per 100 000 for the population.41 Given the limited access to specialist mental healthcare service providers, strategies such as task sharing have been advocated for as potential solutions. In mental healthcare, task-sharing refers to equipping non-specialist healthcare workers without formal mental health education or prior experience in mental health with the necessary skills to provide psychological support, including short-term, low-intensity therapeutic interventions.42

There is substantial evidence supporting task-sharing mental health services in SSA3343,46 with several RCTs investigating the effectiveness of task-sharing mental health intervention in the treatment of common mental health conditions.3545 47,49 While task-sharing has been widely implemented to address common mental conditions such as depression and anxiety, PTSD-specific interventions using the task-sharing approach are limited. Only a few studies have been conducted in SSA focusing specifically on the treatment of PTSD in adolescents using a task-sharing approach.34 35 For example, a study was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of an individually delivered TF-CBT intervention using 18 local lay counsellors, with no previous professional training in mental healthcare to deliver the intervention in Zambia.34 The intervention was delivered to 58 children and adolescents (5–18 years), and the counsellors were provided with training and ongoing supervision. The study found significant reductions in PTSD symptoms among the children and adolescents, which provided support to the feasibility of using lay counsellors.34 In South Africa, an RCT study further highlighted the effectiveness of using a task-sharing approach using psychosocial-treatment-naive nurses to deliver a prolonged exposure therapy (n=31) as well as supportive counselling (n=32) to a total of 63 adolescents.35 The study proved that evidence-based PTSD treatments such as prolonged exposure therapy can be implemented by non-specialists using the task-sharing approach in low-income and middle-income countries.35

This scoping review seeks to provide a broader exploration of the available studies, not only mapping out RCTs but also including other study designs to capture a wider range of PTSD interventions. This will allow the researcher to identify gaps in the literature and to highlight neglected areas, such as long-term intervention effectiveness and culturally adapted approaches beyond TF-CBT and prolonged exposure therapy interventions. Furthermore, insights from diverse studies will provide guidance for developing PTSD interventions that are contextually appropriate for adolescents within SSA.

Methods

This study will use a scoping review methodology to identify evidence-based treatments for adolescents diagnosed with PTSD in SSA, as well as to map the existing literature on the effectiveness of these treatments. A scoping review was considered as it identifies key concepts, theories, evidence sources and research gaps, particularly when the research topic has not been thoroughly reviewed previously.50 This scoping review will adhere to the Arksey and O’Malley50 methodological framework, which was subsequently refined by Levac et al.51 This framework outlines six essential stages for conducting scoping reviews: (1) Defining the research question, (2) Locating pertinent studies, (3) Study selection, (4) Charting the data, (5) Collating, summarising and reporting the results and (6) Consulting with stakeholders. The findings will be presented using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) (online supplemental appendix I).52

Search strategy

The electronic literature search for this review will implement the three-step search strategy proposed by the Joanna Briggs Institute.53

The author consulted and collaborated with a UCT librarian to develop a thorough and effective search strategy (table 1). First, a preliminary search will be conducted in a common online database such as PubMed (Medline) and Scopus. Scientific databases will be selected based on their relevance and comprehensive coverage of mental health-related topics. Major databases such as Academic Search Premier, Africa-Wide Information, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, Education Resources Information Centre, Healthsource: nursing/academic, PsycINFO, PsycARTICLES and SocINDEX will be accessed via EBSCOhost. The search strategy will be piloted to check the appropriateness of keywords, and databases will be revised as necessary. Boolean search will allow for the combination of keywords. The search terms will include Medical Subject Headings and keywords associated with mental health conditions and treatment, specifically PTSD interventions among adolescents, such as ‘PTSD’, ‘Trauma-focused’, ‘Treatment’, ‘Intervention’ and ‘Adolescents’. The keywords used in the titles and abstracts of the identified articles will be analysed. Then, the relevant keywords found in the titles and abstracts of the identified papers will be extracted to compile a list that will direct a comprehensive literature search across all databases. As a second step, major databases will be accessed. As the third and final step of this stage, the reference lists of the included studies will be reviewed to identify relevant studies. This search will be specifically limited to the reference lists of the included articles, ensuring a focused and efficient identification of further relevant literature. All eligible articles will be uploaded into EndNote V.21 and Rayyan, a web and mobile application for systematic reviews,54 and duplicates will be identified and removed.

Stage 3: study selection

The first level of screening will be done independently by the author and a second reviewer using Rayyan to assist the review team in selecting suitable studies at the abstract and title phase for inclusion.54 After identifying and selecting relevant abstracts, the author will obtain the full text versions of these articles. Full-text articles of selected abstracts will be retrieved, and both reviewers will conduct full-text screening of the selected studies for eligibility. Any disagreements will be resolved by consensus with the study supervisor. The study selection process for inclusion in the review is anticipated to be completed within 6 weeks.

To assess and highlight the methodological quality of the included studies, the appropriate JBI critical appraisal tools will be used descriptively to provide an overview of study rigour but will not be used as a basis for study exclusion (https://jbi.global/critical-appraisal-tools).

Stage 4: charting the data

Data extraction will be conducted independently by the author and the reviewer to ensure accuracy and minimise bias. An MS Excel spreadsheet will be used to systematically record and extract relevant data from each included study (online supplemental appendix II). Categories in the spreadsheet will include: (1) Publication year, (2) Author, (3) Study location (country), (4) Study setting (ie, clinic, community, home), (5) Target population, (6) Age, (7) Intervention type, (8) Outcome measures, (9) Outcomes of the study, (10) Intervention duration, (11) Number of participants, (12) Method of delivery, (13) Professional background of treatment provider and (14) Methodology for study. Other categories may be added during this process. The reviewers will use the tool independently to extract the data from each included study, with regular discussions held to ensure coding accuracy. Any disagreements between the researcher and the reviewer regarding the intervention evaluation will be discussed with a third reviewer for a conclusion to be reached. The completed data extraction spreadsheet will be attached as an appendix in the final review.

Stage 5: collating, summarising and reporting the results

The findings from the included studies will be synthesised using a narrative approach to provide a comprehensive understanding of PTSD interventions for adolescents in SSA. The synthesis will involve identifying common themes, intervention strategies and outcome patterns. The results will be reported following the PRISMA-ScR guidelines.52 Themes and gaps emerging from the synthesis will inform recommendations for future research and the development of tailored PTSD interventions for adolescents in this context

Stage 6: consultation

This phase will include engaging with stakeholders at the centre for public mental health throughout the scoping review process to ensure that the review is comprehensive and relevant.

This review will not involve the collection of primary data from human participants. The results of the scoping review will be submitted to a relevant academic journal to contribute to academic literature and inform future research.
