A Life‐Threatening Case of Fournier's Gangrene
Authors: Rova Malala Fandresena Randrianarisoa, Scholastique Ngo Souck M, Thileli Amrane, Meroua Bouakrif, Liliane Iribaziga, Cherif Omeiri, Catherine Letrillard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report severe Fournier's gangrene in a 69‐year‐old man presenting with septic shock and extensive perineal gas on computed tomography. Enterococcus faecium was identified. Fournier's gangrene may occur without classical risk factors. Early recognition, broad‐spectrum antibiotics, and urgent surgical debridement are essential to improve survival and reduce mortality.

Case Image Presentation

Fournier's gangrene (FG) is a rare, rapidly progressive, and life‐threatening necrotising soft tissue infection involving the perineum and external genitalia. Despite advances in critical care, antimicrobial therapy, and surgical techniques, FG remains associated with high morbidity and mortality, largely due to delays in diagnosis and treatment [1]. Early recognition based on clinical suspicion, supported by imaging when required, is crucial to improve outcomes. We report a severe case of FG illustrating characteristic clinical and imaging features, highlighting an uncommon microbiological etiology and emphasizing the need to consider this diagnosis even in the absence of classical risk factors.

A 69‐year‐old immunocompetent man was admitted with scrotal pain evolving over 3 days. His medical history included a previous myocardial infarction, stroke, and long‐term indwelling urinary catheterisation. On admission, he presented with severe sepsis associated with hypotension and mottled skin. Physical examination revealed inflammatory oedema and necrotic plaques involving the scrotum, penis, and anterior perineum, highly suggestive of FG (Figure 1). Laboratory investigations showed a C‐reactive protein level of 367 mg/L and a serum lactate of 4.2 mmol/L. Blood and urine cultures were positive for Enterococcus faecium . Abdominopelvic computed tomography demonstrated perineal soft tissue infiltration with subcutaneous emphysema and gas tracking along the fascial planes (Figure 2). Broad‐spectrum antimicrobial therapy with piperacillin–tazobactam and linezolid was initiated, followed by extensive surgical debridement and temporary penile burial in the suprapubic region, resulting in progressive clinical and local improvement.

FG is a rare but severe necrotising soft tissue infection, with an estimated annual incidence of approximately 1.6 cases per 100,000 men [1]. It predominantly affects elderly patients, most frequently between 50 and 79 years of age, and continues to carry a high risk of mortality despite improvements in management. The infection is usually polymicrobial, reflecting contamination from the genitourinary or gastrointestinal tract. The most frequently isolated pathogens include Escherichia coli , Klebsiella pneumoniae , Bacteroides fragilis , Streptococcus species, Pseudomonas species, and Staphylococcus species. Fungal etiologies are uncommon [2, 3]. These microorganisms act synergistically to induce rapid tissue destruction through toxin production, microvascular thrombosis, and impaired host immune responses. In contrast, the isolation of E. faecium in our case represents an uncommon etiology, underscoring the microbiological diversity of FG and supporting the need for broad‐spectrum empirical antimicrobial therapy. Classical risk factors for FG include diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, and chronic alcoholism [2]. In our patient, the absence of these traditional factors highlights advanced age and prolonged urinary catheterisation as significant predisposing conditions. Diagnosis is primarily clinical, with early symptoms including fever, pain, and erythema of the genital or perineal region, progressing to necrosis at later stages. Computed tomography is valuable for confirming the diagnosis and assessing disease extent, with perineal infiltration and gas formation as characteristic findings [2]. Given its fulminant course and high mortality, FG requires immediate and aggressive management combining prompt surgical debridement, broad‐spectrum antibiotics, and intensive supportive care [1, 2].
