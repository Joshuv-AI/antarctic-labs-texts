Comparison of the standard McCoy laryngoscope and video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope for laryngoscopy and intubation in patients with simulated difficult airway
Authors: Teena Bansal, Mamta Jain, Chetan Kumar, Anish Kumar Singh, Jatin Lal, Suresh Singhal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Aims:Video laryngoscopes provide better visualisation of glottis and easier tracheal intubation as compared to the McCoy laryngoscope. However, cost constraints limit their use. The video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope provides the advantage of both, the McCoy laryngoscope and standard video laryngoscope and is cost-effective. The present study was conducted to compare the standard McCoy laryngoscope and video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope in patients with simulated difficult airway.Methods:This randomised single-blind crossover study included 60 patients of either gender aged 18 to 65 years. In sequence I (n = 30), 1st laryngoscopy was done with the standard McCoy laryngoscope and the 2nd with the video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope. In sequence II (n = 30), 1st laryngoscopy was done using the video-assisted McCoy and the 2nd with the standard McCoy. Intubation was done after 2nd laryngoscopy in both sequences. The primary objective was grade of glottic visualisation using percentage of glottic opening (POGO) score and modified Cormack Lehane (CL) grade. The secondary objectives were time for glottic visualisation, time for tube insertion, total intubation time, first attempt intubation success, optimisation manoeuvre, and complications. Quantitative variables were compared using t-test, and qualitative variables were compared using Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact tests.Results:Significantly better mean POGO scores [65.66 (27.03)% vs 44.50 (31.59), P < 0.001] and modified CL grades [I/2a/2b/3 in 20/22/16/2 vs 10/19/26/5 patients, P = 0.03] were observed with the video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope. Less time for glottic visualisation, tube insertion, and total time of intubation were observed with the video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope. First attempt intubation success, optimisation manoeuvre, incidence of trauma, and postoperative cough were comparable.Conclusion:The video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope provided significantly better glottic visualisation in patients with simulated difficult airway.

INTRODUCTION

The McCoy laryngoscope is a modification of the Macintosh blade with a hinged tip, which upon flexing lifts the epiglottis and improves glottic visualisation. The McCoy laryngoscope has been reported as improving the Cormack Lehane (CL) grade by 1 in 45.1% patients wearing a rigid cervical collar.[12]

Video laryngoscopes improve the visualisation of the larynx to make intubation easier. They lead to better visualisation of the glottis and easier tracheal intubation as compared to the McCoy laryngoscope.[345] However, despite promising results of video laryngoscopes, cost constraints limit their use. Hence, there is a need for a video laryngoscope which is affordable. The essence of video laryngoscopes lies in the presence of the video camera facility which helps in visualising the airway structures.

A video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope can be prepared using a standard McCoy laryngoscope and endoscopic camera. This video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope is similar to a standard McCoy laryngoscope and differs by the presence of a small camera fitted on the laryngoscope blade. Muhamed et al.[6] used an endoscopic camera-mounted McCoy laryngoscope in a study and concluded that this video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope provides the advantage of both, the McCoy laryngoscope and the standard video laryngoscope.

The present study was conducted to compare the standard McCoy laryngoscope and video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope in patients with a simulated difficult airway. We hypothesised that a video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope will provide a better glottic view during laryngoscopy as compared to the standard McCoy laryngoscope. The primary objective was the grade of glottic visualisation using percentage of glottic opening (POGO) score and modified CL grade. The secondary objectives were time for glottic visualisation, time for tube insertion after glottic visualisation, total intubation time, first attempt intubation success, optimisation manoeuvre (rotation of tube), and complications.

METHODS

The present randomised crossover single-blind study was conducted prospectively from July 2024 to June 2025 (1 year duration) in a tertiary healthcare institute after obtaining approval from the institutional ethical committee (BREC/24/944 dated 24/04/24). The study was registered in the Clinical Trials Registry-India (CTRI/2024/05/068047 dated 29 May 2024). Sixty patients of either gender, aged 18 to 65 years, belonging to American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status I or II with normal airway and scheduled for elective surgery under general anaesthesia requiring orotracheal intubation were included in the study. Cervical collar was applied to simulate a difficult airway. Patients with anticipated difficult airway, body mass index >30 kg/m2, pregnancy or lactation, full stomach, and refusal to participate in the study were excluded. Written informed consent was obtained from the patients for participation in the study and use of the patient data for research and educational purposes. The study was carried out using the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, 2013 and good clinical practice guidelines.

All the patients were evaluated during the preoperative visit, a day prior to surgery. Routine investigations including haemoglobin, bleeding time, clotting time, and urine examination were carried out in all the patients. Other investigations were carried out as per requirement. Patients were kept nil per oral for 6 hours. They were premedicated with tablet alprazolam 0.25 mg and tablet pantoprazole 40 mg on the night before and in the morning 2 hours before the surgery. Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitors including heart rate, non-invasive blood pressure, electrocardiogram, and pulse oximetry were attached.

Patients were randomly allocated to one of the two sequences using a computer-generated sequence of random numbers. Random allocation into two sequences was done with an allocation ratio of 1:1. Allocation concealment was done using the sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelope method by the operation theatre technician before the induction of anaesthesia.

In sequence I (n = 30), the 1st laryngoscopy was done using a standard McCoy laryngoscope and the 2nd laryngoscopy was done using a video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope, and in sequence II (n = 30), the 1st laryngoscopy was done using a video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope and the 2nd laryngoscopy was done using the standard McCoy laryngoscope.

The standardised anaesthesia protocol was followed. The universal serial bus (USB) camera app was downloaded on android phone using Google Play Store. The video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope was prepared by the anaesthesiologist deputed for tracheal intubation using the standard McCoy laryngoscope and a waterproof endoscope camera to be used with an android phone [Figure 1]. The tip of the camera was kept in the same position as that of the bulb of the McCoy laryngoscope. The camera was fixed using medical grade tape, and the fixing was done in such a way that the tape did not come in direct contact with the oral cavity. All the intubations were performed by the same anaesthesiologist.

A semi-rigid cervical collar was applied to the patients. After pre-oxygenation with 100% oxygen for 3 minutes, anaesthesia was induced with glycopyrrolate 0.2 mg, fentanyl 2 µg/kg, and propofol 2 mg/kg. Increments of propofol were given till loss of consciousness and loss of response to verbal command were achieved. The ability to mask ventilate the patient was judged before giving the neuromuscular blocking agent. Muscle relaxation was achieved with atracurium 0.5 mg/kg. The patients were ventilated for 3 minutes via a face mask and anaesthesia breathing system using 2% sevoflurane in 100% oxygen.

In both the sequences, a neutral position was used for laryngoscopy and intubation and two laryngoscopies were done by the crossover method and intubation was done after the second laryngoscopy. Depending on the randomisation, either the standard McCoy laryngoscope or video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope was used first for laryngoscopy. In sequence I, 1st laryngoscopy was done using the standard McCoy laryngoscope, optimal external laryngeal manipulation was applied, and after getting the best glottic view, the POGO score was noted. The laryngoscope was removed and ventilation with 100% oxygen was resumed for 1 min. This was followed by the 2nd laryngoscopy which was done using the video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope; optimal external laryngeal manipulation was applied, and after getting the best glottic view, the POGO score was noted and endotracheal intubation was done using a styletted endotracheal tube. In sequence II, the 1st laryngoscopy was done using a video-assisted laryngoscope and then the 2nd laryngoscopy was done using a standard McCoy laryngoscope. An endotracheal tube of size 7 mm internal diameter (ID) and 8 mm ID was used for females and males, respectively. In case of difficulty in introducing the tube, rotation of the tube was done. The cuff of the endotracheal tube was inflated and confirmation of the tube was done by chest auscultation and capnography. The anaesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane (minimum alveolar concentration 1) and 67% nitrous oxide (N2O) in oxygen. Non-visualisation of glottis on 2nd laryngoscopy and a duration of more than 60 seconds for insertion of an endotracheal tube or desaturation [peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) <94%] were considered as failure of intubation. In case of failure of intubation, the cervical collar was removed, mask ventilation was done, and an alternative method for airway management was chosen. At the end of surgery, after achieving adequate recovery, patients were extubated and transferred to the post-anaesthesia care unit.

The grade of glottic visualisation was noted using POGO score and modified CL grade. Time was measured using a stopwatch. Time for glottic visualisation (TG) was taken as time from insertion of the laryngoscope into the mouth to visualisation of best glottic view (TG standard and TG video-assisted). Time for tube insertion (TI) was taken as time from visualisation of best glottic view to insertion of the tracheal tube into the trachea and confirmation via capnography. Total intubation time (TT) was defined as the sum of second TG + TI. First attempt intubation success was taken if the endotracheal tube was inserted within 60 seconds without desaturation (SpO2 >94%). Rotation of tube was considered as a manoeuvre. The ability to intubate the trachea without a manoeuvre in the first attempt was considered easy. Presence of blood on the laryngoscope and in the oral cavity was considered as trauma. All the patients were reviewed for cough and hoarseness at the end of surgery, after 1 hour in the post-anaesthesia care unit.

The sample size was calculated using Epi Info Software [statistical software manufactured by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Atlanta, Georgia (United States of America)]. As per the pilot study of 10 cases (five cases in each sequence), the average improvement in POGO was 32% using the video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope and the standard deviation was 42%. We calculated the sample size with a 95% confidence interval, 80% power, and an alpha level of 0.05. Twenty-seven pairs were required in each sequence for two-sided testing in this 2 × 2 crossover study. Therefore, 30 patients were taken in each sequence to balance 10% dropouts.

At the end of the study, all data were compiled and analysed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25.0 for Windows software program (SPSS Inc., International Business Machines Corporation, Armonk, New York, United States). The data were checked for normality before statistical analysis using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and were normal. Quantitative variables were presented as mean (standard deviation) and were compared using the t-test. Qualitative variables were presented as frequencies/percentages, and Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used for comparison. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

No sequence effect and period effect were observed. The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) diagram is depicted in Figure 2. Both sequences were comparable in terms of demographic profile [Table 1]. Significantly better mean POGO scores [65.66 (27.03)% vs 44.50 (31.59), P = 0.0001] and modified CL grades [I/2a/2b/3 in 20/22/16/2 vs 10/19/26/5 patients, P = 0.03] were observed with the video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope as compared to the standard McCoy laryngoscope. Significantly less time for glottic visualisation, tube insertion after glottic visualisation, and total time of intubation were observed with the video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope [Tables 2 and 3]. First attempt intubation success and optimisation manoeuvre were comparable with the video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope and standard McCoy laryngoscope [Table 3]. The incidence of trauma and postoperative cough were comparable with the video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope and standard McCoy laryngoscope. No hoarseness was observed in any patient.

DISCUSSION

In the current study, a significantly better mean POGO score [65.66 (27.03)% vs 44.50 (31.59), P = 0.0001] and modified CL grade [I/2a/2b/3 in 20/22/16/2 vs 10/19/26/5 patients, P = 0.03] were observed with the video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope as compared to the standard McCoy laryngoscope [Table 2]. Direct laryngoscopy requires alignment of the oral, pharyngeal, and laryngeal axes for intubation. In contrast to direct laryngoscopy, video laryngoscopy utilises indirect laryngoscopy and a camera, thereby eliminating the need for a direct line of sight to align the three airway axes for optimal glottic visualisation.[78] In the present study, a cervical collar was used to simulate a difficult airway. The cervical collar prevents head extension and neck flexion necessary for the optimal alignment of the three airway axes, leading to poor exposure of the vocal cords. Further, cervical spine mobility gets decreased by 30–50% when a cervical collar is used.[4] The most likely reason for better Modified CL grade and POGO with the video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope in the present study could be that alignment of the three axes is not required with it.

In the present study, when laryngoscopy was done using the video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope, modified CL grades 1, 2a, 2b and 3 were observed in 33.3%, 36.66%, 26.66%, and 3.3% patients, respectively, and Modified CL grades 1, 2a, 2b, and 3 were observed in 16.7%, 31.67%, 43.33%, and 8.33% patients, respectively, with the standard McCoy laryngoscope [P = 0.03, Table 2].

Improvement in CL grade with the video laryngoscope is of utmost importance for airway management. In the present study, in sequence 1, 19 patients showed improvement in CL grade by 1, when the video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope was used. While the McCoy laryngoscope could not directly identify the laryngeal structures through the oral cavity, the laryngeal structures could be observed with a video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope because of the camera position in the pharynx.[9] Hence, we can say that any device, which improves the glottic view, is extremely valuable in a difficult airway scenario.

POGO score represents the portion of the glottis visualised. The score ranges from 0% when none of the glottis is seen to 100% when the entire glottis including the anterior commissure is seen. It is highly accurate and reliable. In the present study, in sequence 1, 29 patients (96.6%) showed improvement in mean POGO score by 22.5 ± 8.68%. Though POGO score is inter-observer reliable, POGO scoring was done by the same observer in all the patients to remove the observer bias.

Video-assisted McCoy laryngoscopy in this study was associated with a higher proportion of easy airways (70% vs 48.33%) and fewer difficult airways (30% vs 51.67%) than the standard McCoy laryngoscopy. These findings align with broader evidence that video laryngoscopy generally improves glottic visualisation and intubation conditions compared with direct laryngoscopy.

In sequence 1, difficult airway (2b/3) was observed in 14 patients (46.7%) when laryngoscopy was done using the standard McCoy laryngoscope, which was made easy with the video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope in 8 patients (26.7%), Further, it is pertinent to mention here that in sequence 1, the view improved from 3 to 2b in 3 patients (10%) using the video-assisted laryngoscope, which converted blind intubation into intubation under vision, which is of prime importance.

Video laryngoscopes have been found to be superior to McCoy laryngoscopes for airway management in patients with cervical spine immobilisation.[101112] Faster achievement of an adequate glottic view may translate into fewer optimisation manoeuvres and smoother intubation. The present study shows similar results as significantly less time was observed for glottic visualisation using the video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope as compared to the standard McCoy laryngoscopy (28.23 ± 4.28s vs 30.62 ± 4.97s) [Table 2]. Significantly less time was observed for tube insertion and total time of intubation while using the video-assisted laryngoscope in the present study [Table 3].

First attempt intubation success is of prime importance for airway management. Multiple intubation attempts may lead to supraglottic oedema and induce soft tissue bleeding, which eventually further hinders the subsequent intubation attempts and can result in cannot intubate, cannot ventilate situation.[13] Video camera-assisted intubation can offer significant advantages with respect to attempts at intubation even in difficult airway settings.[14] First attempt intubation success was observed in 100% patients using the video-assisted laryngoscope in the present study [Table 3].

Inserting the endotracheal tube is difficult when it hangs up on structures of the laryngeal inlet. It may damage the larynx and the vocal cord, leading to post-operative complications.[15] Several manoeuvres have been proposed to overcome this difficulty. In the present study, we used rotation of tube to overcome this difficulty. Rotation of tube was required in 7 patients (23.3%) and 6 patients (20%) while doing intubation with the standard McCoy laryngoscope and video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope, respectively [Table 3]. Trauma was observed in 5 patients (16.7%) and in 1 patient (3.3%) while doing intubation with the standard McCoy laryngoscope and video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope, respectively [Table 3]. There was no significant difference in the incidence of post-operative cough between the two sequences [Table 3]. Also, no post-operative hoarseness was observed in the present study.

We compared the laryngeal view obtained with the two devices in the same patient, which helped us in the immediate assessment of improvement in each patient. There may have been ergonomic differences such as looking at a handheld android phone screen for glottic visualisation, while looking at the built-in videolaryngoscope monitor in a conventional video laryngoscope. Nevertheless, we could manage this. After using the video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope for intubation, the camera was removed from the laryngoscope. Its tip was flushed with running water and washed with soap. After drying, it was wiped with spirit. High-level disinfection was done with cidex (2% glutaraldehyde).

The present study was a single-blind study in which only the patient was blind. The laryngoscopist and outcome assessor could not be blinded, which is a limitation of the study. Simulated (cervical collar) versus true difficult airway limits the generalisability. The collar reduces mobility by 30–50%, but real scenarios (e.g., trauma, obesity, pathology) differ, which is another limitation of the study.

A further limitation of the study is its single-centre design with a relatively small sample size. Limited literature is available on the use of the video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope. Therefore, further trials enroling more patients are needed in this direction in the future.

CONCLUSION

The video-assisted McCoy laryngoscope provides significantly better glottic visualisation when compared to the standard McCoy laryngoscope in patients with a simulated difficult airway.
