Influence of Ocular Biometric Parameters on IOL Power Calculation Accuracy in Short and Normal Axial Length Eyes
Authors: Yuye Zhang, Shiyu Tang, Zihan Xie, Fan Yang, Peimin Lin, Chenxi Li, Tianyu Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe aim of this study was to assess the effect of including lens thickness (LT) in modern intraocular lens (IOL) formulas and evaluate the influence of ocular biometric parameters on IOL power calculation accuracy in short and normal axial length (AL) eyes.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 174 eyes (74 short AL < 22 mm, 100 normal AL 22–24 mm). Preoperative biometry was obtained using IOLMaster 700. Predicted refractions were calculated using SRK/T, Haigis, Barrett Universal II (BUII), Kane, and Emmetropia Verifying Optical (EVO) formulas, with and without LT. Spearman correlation and multivariate regression analyses were performed to identify biometric predictors of refractive error.ResultsShort eyes had significantly shallower anterior chamber depth (ACD), smaller white-to-white (WTW), thicker LT, and higher mean keratometry (Km) and corneal astigmatism (CA) (all p < 0.01). No significant differences in mean absolute error (MAE) were detected between most formula pairs after adjustment in either subgroup. LT inclusion resulted in a less myopic mean prediction error in both subgroups (all p < 0.01), but MAE improvements were only seen in normal eyes (p < 0.05). AL and WTW were consistent predictors of absolute prediction error (APE) overall, with shorter AL and smaller WTW associated with greater APE. In short eyes, AL showed the strongest association with APE, whereas in normal eyes, WTW was most strongly correlated with APE.ConclusionsShort eyes exhibited greater biometric variability. Incorporation of LT improved the overall prediction accuracy of modern IOL formulas, particularly in normal AL eyes. AL and WTW were the most influential biometric factors affecting refractive prediction outcomes.Trial RegistrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR), ChiCTR2600116749; retrospectively registered on 14 January 2026.

Introduction

Achieving high refractive accuracy in intraocular lens (IOL) power calculation is a fundamental determinant of success in modern cataract surgery [1]. Errors in postoperative refraction not only reduce patient satisfaction but may also require additional refractive correction or secondary procedures [2]. Even with advanced biometry and modern formulas, a significant challenge persists in eyes with short axial lengths (AL), which are consistently associated with higher refractive prediction errors compared with eyes with normal AL, making them a recognized “difficult population” for IOL calculation [3, 4].

In short eyes, the increased error arises from complex interactions among ocular anatomical parameters. For instance, proportional relationships among AL, anterior chamber depth (ACD), lens thickness (LT), and white-to-white (WTW) diameter become nonlinear in these eyes, making accurate modeling of effective lens position (ELP) more difficult [5, 6]. Traditional IOL power formulas, which are primarily derived using AL and keratometry (K) as primary inputs, are particularly prone to inaccuracies in such eyes [7]. With the advent of modern optical biometers (such as the IOLMaster 700), reliable measurement of parameters such as LT is now feasible. In recent years, newer generation formulas such as Barrett Universal II (BUII), Kane, and Emmetropia Verifying Optical (EVO) formulas have attempted to mitigate these limitations by incorporating additional biometric parameters (e.g., LT) into their predictive models [8]. However, whether the inclusion of LT systematically yields clinically meaningful improvements in prediction accuracy, especially in short eyes, remains a matter of active debate [9–11]. Current literature indicates that while newer formulas may incorporate additional parameters to refine ELP estimation, quantitative studies focusing explicitly on how LT influences refractive outcomes in short AL subgroups remain limited, leaving its potential benefit in this population unquantified [12].

Consequently, it remains unclear whether the inclusion of LT systematically reduces prediction errors in short eyes, as existing evidence is fragmented and not specifically targeted. Moreover, the key biometric parameters that most significantly explain refractive deviations in short eyes are yet to be definitively identified. This underscores the need for focused research to elucidate how parameters such as LT interact with other ocular dimensions in short eyes, ultimately guiding formula optimizations for better surgical outcomes.

The primary aim of this study was therefore to compare the refractive prediction accuracy of multiple IOL power calculation formulas, with and without LT, in eyes with short and normal AL. Additionally, we sought to investigate the influence of various ocular biometric parameters on refractive prediction errors.

Methods

Study Design and Ethics

This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study conducted at the Department of Ophthalmology, Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital, Shanghai, China (ChiCTR2600116749). The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital (SHSY-IEC-6.0/25KY50/P01), and the study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent for the use of anonymized clinical data was obtained from all participants.

Participants

Patients who underwent uneventful phacoemulsification and in-the-bag implantation of a non-toric IOL between October 2024 and July 2025 were consecutively reviewed. The implanted IOL models included Johnson & Johnson Tecnis PCB00 (n = 59), AMO Tecnis ZA9003 (n = 30), Zeiss CT Asphina 409MP (n = 22), HumanOptics ASPIRA-aAY (n = 20), Aibo Nord Preloaded Aspheric IOL (n = 15), HumanOptics MC X11 ASP (n = 14), and Bausch & Lomb Akreos Adapt AO (n = 14). All surgeries were performed by a single experienced surgeon (Dr. Tianyu Zheng). For patients with bilateral surgery, only one eye was randomly selected. Inclusion criteria were: (1) uneventful cataract surgery with IOL implantation in the capsular bag; (2) complete preoperative ocular biometry obtained by swept-source optical biometry (IOLMaster 700, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Germany); (3) postoperative refraction measured at 1–3 months after surgery; and (4) AL < 24.0 mm. Exclusion criteria were: (1) history of ocular trauma, previous ocular surgery, corneal or retinal pathology, or glaucoma; (2) complicated cataract surgery (e.g., posterior capsule rupture, IOL decentration); (3) incomplete biometric or refractive data; and (4) failure of IOL power calculation due to ocular parameter or target refraction values being out of bounds in any formula.

Biometric Measurements and IOL Power Calculation

Preoperative biometric parameters, including AL, ACD, LT, central corneal thickness (CCT), K1, K2, mean keratometry (Km), corneal astigmatism (CA), and WTW were obtained from the IOL Master 700. Postoperative spherical equivalent (SE) was recorded at 1–3 months postoperatively.

Predicted postoperative refractions were obtained using multiple IOL power calculation formulas. SRK/T and Haigis formulas were computed using Python scripts based on published algorithms. BUII, Kane, and EVO formulas (each with and without LT input) were obtained via the official online calculators. IOL constants were retrieved from the IOLCon database (https://iolcon.org/lensesTable.php), with no further constant optimization. This approach was chosen to reflect routine clinical practice and to avoid potential bias introduced by formula-specific optimization.

Statistical Analysis

Eyes were categorized by AL into short (AL < 22 mm) and normal (22 mm ≤ AL < 24 mm) subgroups. The prediction error (PE) was defined as the difference between the actual postoperative SE and the predicted postoperative SE. PE was defined as positive for patients with hyperopic refractive outcomes and negative for patients with myopic refractive outcomes. The absolute prediction error (APE) was the absolute value of PE. Accuracy was evaluated using mean prediction error (MPE), the minimum and maximum values of PE, mean/median absolute error (MAE/MedAE), root mean square absolute error (RMSAE) [13], and the percentages of eyes within APE thresholds of 0.25 D, 0.50 D, and 1.00 D.

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.12.7). Normality was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) as appropriate. Between-group comparisons were conducted using independent-sample t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables.

The PE and APE were compared among different IOL power calculation formulas. Analyses were performed for all eyes combined, as well as for two AL subgroups. For each subgroup, comparisons were conducted across formulas within the same condition (with LT or without LT). Parametric or nonparametric tests were applied as appropriate, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

Paired comparisons were performed to evaluate the effect of LT inclusion on the refractive prediction accuracy of the BUII, Kane, and EVO formulas. For each eye, the differences in PE (ΔPE) and APE (ΔAPE) were calculated as with LT − without LT. The distributions of ΔPE and ΔAPE for each formula were visualized using overlaid histograms with kernel density estimation. Normally distributed data were analyzed with paired t-tests, otherwise the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

The relationships between biometric parameters (including WTW, AL, ACD, CCT, LT, Km, and CA) and both PE and APE were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. Mean PE and APE values were plotted within binned intervals (1.0 mm for AL and 0.5 mm for WTW). Smooth curves were generated using cubic spline interpolation for all eyes and for each AL subgroup. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed for each formula, with PE or APE as dependent variables. Independent variables included WTW, AL, ACD, CCT, LT, Km, CA, and age. Multicollinearity was examined using the variance inflation factor. Regression outcomes were reported as unstandardized coefficients (Coef.), standardized coefficients (β), t-values, p values, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each predictor variable. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Significant differences among formulas without LT for both the MPE and the MAE in the overall cohort (p < 0.001). The Haigis formula consistently produced the largest (most positive) MPE, reflecting a tendency toward hyperopic shift, and differed significantly from all other formulas (all p < 0.001). The BUII formula showed significantly smaller MPE, indicating a mild myopic shift compared with the Kane and EVO formulas (p < 0.001). Additionally, the SRK/T formula showed significantly smaller MPE compared with EVO (p = 0.031). Similarly, significant differences were observed among formulas incorporating LT (MPE: p < 0.001; MAE: p = 0.010). The BUII with LT yielded significantly smaller (more myopic) MPE compared with both the Kane and EVO formulas (p < 0.001). Within subgroups, similar patterns were observed. In the short AL subgroup, the Haigis formula again exhibited the largest (most hyperopic) MPE, significantly higher than all other formulas (all p < 0.001). The BUII formula produced the smallest (most myopic) MPE. In the normal AL subgroup, the Haigis formula consistently produced the largest MPE, significantly differing from all other formulas (all p < 0.001). The SRK/T formula produced the smallest (most myopic) MPE, significantly differing from all other formulas (all p < 0.001). Across all comparisons, MAE differences were limited after adjustment, suggesting that no formula achieved clear superiority in absolute prediction accuracy.

Between-group comparisons according to AL showed that, for MPE, the Haigis formula produced significantly higher errors in eyes with AL < 22 mm (p < 0.001), indicating a greater tendency toward hyperopic shift in this short AL group. In contrast, SRK/T yielded significantly lower MPE in the normal AL group (p = 0.025), suggesting a relative myopic shift in this group. No significant differences between AL groups were observed for the other formulas (all p > 0.05). For MAE, the BUII formula with LT (p = 0.020), EVO formula with LT (p = 0.015), and Haigis formula (p = 0.020) exhibited significantly higher errors in eyes with short AL compared with normal AL, whereas the other formulas did not reach statistical significance between the two groups (all p > 0.05).

Effect of Including LT on Prediction Accuracy

We compared the PE and APE of the BUII, Kane, and EVO formulas with and without incorporating LT (Fig. 1). For all three formulas, incorporating LT significantly reduced the systematic myopic bias observed when LT was omitted. In the full cohort, inclusion of LT led to less negative MPE and modest but significant reductions in MAE across all three formulas, indicating improved overall accuracy. Subgroup analyses showed consistent MPE changes in both short and normal eyes. Spearman correlation between biometric parameters and refractive PE and APE are shown in Fig. 2. WTW and AL was the most consistent biometric factor associated with refractive outcomes across formulas overall (Fig. 2A). WTW correlated positively with PE in SRK/T (ρ = 0.23, p = 0.003) and negatively with APE across all formulas (ρ range: −0.27 to −0.23, all p < 0.01). AL correlated negatively with APE in all formulas (ρ range: −0.26 to −0.19, all p < 0.02), indicating greater prediction deviation in shorter eyes. In the short AL group (Fig. 2B), AL remained the most influential factor associated with refractive PE and APE. AL correlated positively with PE in BUII without LT (ρ = 0.31, p = 0.007) and BUII with LT (ρ = 0.33, p = 0.004), and negatively with APE across multiple formulas. WTW showed a positive correlation with PE in SRK/T (ρ = 0.24, p = 0.038) but was not significantly correlated with APE in any formula. In the normal AL group (Fig. 2C), WTW emerged as the most influential biometric factor associated with refractive PE and APE. WTW showed a weak but significant positive correlation with the PE of SRK/T (ρ = 0.28, p = 0.006), while it was negatively correlated with the APE across all formulas (ρ range: −0.26 to −0.33, all p < 0.02), suggesting that larger WTW was associated with smaller APE. No meaningful associations were found for AL (all p > 0.05).

To further visualize these relationships and explore the trends of refractive prediction errors across continuous biometric variations, smoothed plots illustrating the associations between refractive PE and APE with ocular biometric parameters are shown in Fig. 3. In eyes with shorter AL, PE tended to be more negative, indicating a greater myopic shift. For the BUII, Kane, and EVO formulas, PE values were more negative when LT was not incorporated compared with those calculated with LT. The magnitude of APE increased as AL decreased, suggesting poorer refractive predictability in shorter eyes. In the normal AL group, APEs obtained from the BUII, Kane, and EVO formulas without LT were larger than those with LT, whereas the difference between the two conditions was smaller in the short AL group. Discussion

This study comprehensively evaluated the influence of ocular biometry and LT inclusion on the refractive prediction accuracy of several modern IOL power calculation formulas across short and normal AL eyes. By analyzing postoperative refractive outcomes with and without LT and stratifying eyes by AL, we provide new evidence on how biometric geometry modulates formula performance under different anatomic conditions.

Across formulas, no significant differences in MAE were detected between most formula pairs after adjustment in either subgroup, suggesting broadly comparable absolute accuracy among contemporary formulas. This finding is consistent with recent systematic reviews and network meta-analyses showing that no single formula demonstrates consistent superiority in short eyes, although modern methods tend to narrow the gap [14, 15]. Reported rankings vary with cohort composition, IOL constants, and the biometer used [8].

Despite advances in formula design, eyes with short AL still exhibited relatively larger MAE. This vulnerability can be attributed to several factors. AL measurement errors are more influential in short eyes, as even a minor deviation in AL measurement can lead to a disproportionately large refractive error in IOL power calculation [16]. In addition, very short eyes often require high-power IOLs, in which small errors in ELP or true lens power are magnified [17]. Furthermore, short eyes typically exhibit compact ocular anatomy characterized by shallower ACD, smaller WTW, thicker LT, and steeper corneas [18, 19], resulting in nonlinear scaling among biometric parameters. These structural features reduce the reliability of ELP estimation [5, 20], explaining why MAE remains elevated in short eyes regardless of formula choice.

Incorporation of LT into modern formulas significantly reduced myopic bias (MPE) in both AL subgroups, but a statistically significant MAE improvement was observed only in normal eyes in our study. This indicates that while LT provides useful predictive information, its stabilizing effect is more pronounced in physiological AL ranges. Mechanistically, the modest refinement in ELP estimation provided by LT is insufficient to counterbalance the heightened refractive sensitivity and increased measurement variability inherent to short eyes [19, 21]. In contrast, in eyes with normal AL where biometric relationships are more proportional and measurement noise is lower, LT-derived ELP refinement translates more effectively into improved refractive precision. Taken together, the benefit of adding LT is formula-specific and AL-dependent. Clinically, our data support entering LT in all available formulas to reduce systematic error. In short eyes, LT should still be entered to reduce bias, but clinicians should anticipate limited improvement in dispersion and consider further safeguards, such as IOL constant optimization and cross-checking with multiple top-performing formulas [22].

Only a few studies have specifically examined the influence of including the optional parameter LT in new-generation IOL power formulas, and their results remain inconsistent. Taroni et al. [9] conducted a dedicated “lens thickness study” in predominantly normal-AL eyes and reported that entering LT into modern formulas did not produce any statistically or clinically meaningful improvement in MedAE, MAE, or the proportion of eyes within ± 0.50 D. Similarly, Wendelstein et al. [10] reported that including LT caused a myopic shift in PE compared with calculations without LT, whereas APE decreased slightly for BUII but remained unchanged for EVO and Kane. Their cohort also consisted mainly of normal-AL eyes (mean AL of 23.97 ± 1.08 mm). Chen et al. [11] found that in eyes with AL < 24.5 mm, inclusion of LT led to more myopic PE in the thick-lens subgroup (LT ≥ 5 mm), while no significant difference was found in the thin or medium LT groups. In contrast, previous studies comparing BUII [23] and Kane [24] formulas with and without LT focused solely on differences in calculated IOL power for emmetropic targets, without postoperative refractive outcomes. They showed that the calculated IOL power differed more in short eyes, indicating that LT may have greater theoretical influence when the ocular geometry is compact, but those studies could not determine whether this translated to actual refractive accuracy.

Although LT inclusion significantly altered MPE and improved MAE in normal eyes, neither subgroups demonstrated any significant LT–PE or LT–APE correlations across formulas. These findings represent different analytic levels: Spearman and multivariate models evaluate eye-to-eye variation, whereas entering LT into a formula modifies the underlying ELP estimation and reduces systematic bias across the cohort. LT primarily improves global formula behavior rather than explaining residual refractive variability at the individual level.

Additionally, our study revealed that shorter AL and smaller WTW were consistently associated with greater APE across formulas. Subgroup analysis highlighted differential influences: AL was the main determinant of APE in short eyes, whereas WTW showed the strongest correlation in normal eyes. The effect of WTW likely reflects the geometric contribution of the anterior segment to ELP estimation. This observation aligns with the concept of relative anterior microphthalmos (RAM), defined as eyes with normal AL (AL > 20.5 mm) but a disproportionately reduced anterior segment (WTW < 11 mm and ACD ≤ 2.2 mm) [25–27]. Previous studies reported that RAM eyes tend to show greater refractive prediction error and are particularly sensitive to anterior-segment parameters in ELP modeling [2, 7, 28]. Although our study did not categorize eyes by RAM, the finding that smaller WTW predicted larger APE in normal-AL eyes supports the notion that anterior-segment geometry rather than overall eye size drives prediction variability in such eyes. These findings underscore the importance of individualized ELP modeling based on the proportional relationships among multiple biometric parameters rather than any single predictor. Furthermore, WTW and AL reflect the horizontal and longitudinal proportions of the globe, and their combined influence may capture subtle anatomic variations that modulate IOL formula performance.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center, retrospective study with a moderate sample size, which may limit generalizability. Second, multiple IOL models from different manufacturers were included, and formula-specific IOL constant optimization was not performed. This approach reflects routine clinical practice and ensures a standardized comparison across formulas. While it may introduce lens-specific noise and a small degree of systematic prediction error, it is unlikely to substantially affect the comparative analyses of biometric predictors. Third, extremely short eyes (AL < 19 mm) were excluded owing to the input range limitations of modern IOL formulas, which restricts extrapolation of the results to these rare but clinically challenging cases [7, 29]. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes, formula-specific constant optimization, and advanced modeling approaches, such as artificial intelligence (AI)-driven algorithms and three-dimensional ocular geometry, may further improve ELP estimation and enhance predictive accuracy in short and atypical eyes.

Conclusions

Including LT in modern IOL formulas significantly reduced systematic myopic bias across all axial lengths, with meaningful improvements in refractive precision observed mainly in normal eyes. AL and WTW were key predictors of accuracy, with shorter AL and smaller WTW linked to greater error. Clinically, LT should be incorporated in routine IOL calculations to minimize bias, while surgeons should apply additional caution and constant optimization in short eyes with compact anterior segments.
