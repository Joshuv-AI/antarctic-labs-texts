The Effect of Implant Size on Fretting Currents of Acetabular Cup‐Liner Tapers During in Vitro Cyclic Loading
Authors: Peter W. Kurtz, Annsley Mace, Charley M. Goodwin, Jeremy L. Gilbert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAs dual mobility hips become more popular, there is a clinical need to investigate design features that will impact tribocorrosion of these devices. While previous studies have investigated both corrosion and wear damage, the effect of device size on fretting corrosion response has yet to be evaluated. In this study, we compare two implants of the same design (EMPHASYS Hip Solution) but with different‐sized acetabular cups (66 mm vs 44 mm) representing the range of sizes typically used. We hypothesized that the smaller implant would perform better due to the smaller taper engagement area and the smaller moments imparted during normal activities. Each device underwent a physiologically representative intermittent cyclic load between 400 N and 4000 N in 50,000 cycle increments with pauses in between, up to a total of 3,000,000 cycles and 110 h at 9 Hz in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). Under potentiostatic control (−50 mV vs Ag/AgCl), fretting corrosion currents were generated and monitored with intermittent measurement of baseline currents. Post‐testing, solution ion levels were measured using inductively coupled mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS). Results showed there was no difference between devices when comparing average current, average cumulative charge, average decay constant (s‐), total volume lost and solution ion levels. We report a total volume loss of 0.0028 ± 0.0015 mm3 and 0.0052 ± 0.0035 mm3 for the 66 mm and 44 mm implants, respectively. The devices had only minor evidence of fretting or corrosion damage. These results suggest there is no difference in fretting corrosion performance based on implant size.

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is considered a successful orthopedic surgery with 10‐year revision rates less than 5% [1]. Despite high long‐term survival rates, a small percentage of the implants fail due to aseptic loosening, instability, and infection [2]. Due to the large number of THA's performed each year as well as the projected increase in THA's in the coming decades, these failures represent an appreciable number of patients [3, 4, 5]. Previous research studies investigating potential causes for implant failure in THA have narrowed in on the relationship between Cobalt Chrome Molybdenum (CoCrMo) and soft tissue necrosis of the periprosthetic tissue [6, 7, 8]. CoCrMo has been associated with device failure due to adverse local tissue reactions (ALTR) and aseptic lymphocyte‐dominated vasculitis‐associated lesions (ALVAL) [8, 9, 10]. Other research studies have presented CoCrMo implants as a source of systemic cobalt toxicity [11, 12, 13]. While CoCrMo femoral heads have largely been replaced by ceramic heads for conventional THA designs, other THA designs with CoCrMo components continue to be used.

One such device design is the dual mobility hip, popular in younger patients due to its limiting of dislocation and increase in range of motion [14]. Modular taper interfaces in dual mobility THAs are an example of a modular junction and are of concern due to the potential negative patient outcomes associated with corrosion and metal wear debris generated at the junction [15, 16]. Modular junctions are often metal‐on‐metal or metal‐on‐hard material combinations, such as CoCrMo, Ti‐6Al‐4V, or ceramic [17, 18]. These devices are susceptible to tribocorrosion at their load‐bearing modular junctions due to micromotion between components [17, 18]. Another known issue with modular junctions is the tight tolerances between components, which result in a crevice‐like environment prone to mechanically assisted crevice corrosion (MACC) [19, 20, 21]. Separated from the bulk, the solution between the liner and acetabular shell of a dual mobility hip may undergo rapid changes to pH and temperature [21]. These conditions cause a synergistic effect that can accelerate corrosion at a far quicker rate at the modular junction compared to the rest of the device.

Thus, it is important to thoroughly investigate which design features influence fretting corrosion and MACC of such devices. For acetabular modular tapers, component size is one such design feature that has been understudied.

Fretting corrosion performance can be evaluated in vitro with a previously developed and recently modified intermittent cyclic fretting corrosion test method [22, 23]. This test relies on quantifiable fretting currents as the oxide film of the passive metal is disrupted and repassivates. In this study: (1) 66 mm and 44 mm (n = 5) dual mobility EMPHASYS Hip Solution (DePuy Synthes) acetabular cup and liner components underwent intermittent cyclic fretting corrosion testing; (2) The average volume loss mm3 was estimated using the results of the fretting corrosion testing; (3) Ion levels of solution during fretting corrosion testing were calculated using inductively coupled plasma mass spectroscopy (ICP‐MS); (4) Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive x‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) were used to evaluate visible damage to the cup and liner.

We tested low‐carbon CoCrMo liners paired with Ti‐6Al‐4V acetabular shells of two different sizes, one large and one small. These devices featured respective outer acetabular shell diameters of 66 mm and 44 mm. Thus, it is hypothesized that the 44 mm and 66 mm designs will both exhibit fretting corrosion damage. However, the 44 mm implant should perform better (i.e., less damage/lower currents) than the 66 mm design, due to the larger implant featuring greater bending moments at the modular taper surface, due to large moment arms. In addition, it is hypothesized that the amount of fretting corrosion damage will be low for both implant groups, consistent with prior studies [22, 23].

Materials and Methods

Sample Preparation

The samples were provided by Depuy Synthes (Warsaw, IN) in a condition identical to a device ready for implantation (i.e., passivated and sterilized in boxes). Ten total EMPHASYS Hip Solutions implants were used, 5 small (44 mm) and 5 large (66 mm). The implants consisted of a low‐carbon CoCrMo acetabular liner and a Ti‐6Al‐4V acetabular shell. A titanium wire was attached to the bottom of the shells using silver conductive wire glue paste (McMaster‐Carr, Elmhurst, IL, US). To provide a smooth surface to attach the titanium wire, a section of approximately 1 square centimeter on the bottom of the shells was polished smooth with sandpaper. To give the acetabular shells a stable base, the acetabular shells were potted in an acrylic (Ortho‐Jet Liquid Clear REF 1306, Powder Clear REF 1370, Lang Dental Manufacturing Company Inc., Wheeling, Illinois, US).

Pre‐Test Assembly

Before cyclic fretting corrosion testing, the unassembled acetabular shell and liner were placed in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, P3813‐10PAK, SIGMA‐ALDRICH, St. Louis, MO) for 1 h, while open‐circuit potential (OCP) was monitored. A 3 electrode‐setup with a carbon rod as the counter electrode and a Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference electrode was used. This was repeated for every component before assembly.

The implant was then hand assembled by placing the liner in the shell and pressing firmly until the liner no longer had vertical or horizontal movement. After hand assembly, the final seating was performed using the MTS Bionix Servohydraulic benchtop system (model 370.02, MTS Systems, Eden Prairie, MN, US). The seating procedure was as follows: the assembled liner‐shell construct was placed at a 0‐degree angle (loading head axis was perpendicular to the cup). Using an ultrahigh molecular weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) loading fixture (provided by Depuy Synthes), a load was ramped to 4 kN at a rate of 10 N/s, and then the load was released.

Cyclic Loading Test Procedure

The implant assembly was placed at a clinically relevant 45° angle in a mount within a fluid chamber in the MTS. The fluid chamber was filled with 0.75 L of PBS (see Figure 1). The counter electrode and reference electrode were placed in the chamber. A constant potential of −50 mV versus Ag/AgCl (based on the individual components' OCPs) was applied to the system, and current was recorded via a Bi‐Potentiostat (Model AFRDE4, Pine Research Instrumentation Inc., Durham, NC) with the 2019 edition of LabVIEW (National Instruments Corp., Austin, TX) software.

The three‐electrode system captured the electrochemical current generated during intermittent resting and the cyclic loading. The implant (A) was the working electrode. This was done by attaching a titanium wire to the titanium shell. An Ag/AgCl electrode (B) was used as the reference electrode and a carbon rod (C) was used as the counter electrode.

Once the baseline current (no fretting present) was stable, the following procedure was performed:

Step 1: An axial load was ramped over 10 s until −2 kN was reached, which was held for 46 min to measure baseline current.

Step 2: A cyclic load of −0.4 kN to −4 kN (R = 0.1) was applied for 50,000 cycles at 9 Hz.

Step 3: Applied load returned to a constant −2 kN for another 18 min to allow baseline to recover.

After 18 min, the load was cycled again for another 50,000 cycles. In total, steps 2 and 3 were repeated 60 times, until 3 million cycles were completed. During the entire process, axial load, displacement, and fretting and baseline currents were recorded (1 s of data every 1111 s, to provide one data point per baseline current and 5 data points per fretting cycle).

After 75 h, 0.25 L of PBS was added to the mount chamber to replenish PBS lost to evaporation.

Post‐Test Analysis

Plots of the baseline current, the fretting current, and the net fretting current (fretting minus baseline) over time were generated. Net fretting current was calculated with this equation INet=(IF1+IF2+IF3+IF4+IF5)5−(IB1+IB2)2

By averaging the 5 data points recorded during cyclic loading (IF1, IF2, IF3, IF4, IF5) and subtracting the average of the two baseline current values taken before and after cyclic loading was initiated for each interval (IB1, IB2), the true fretting current (INet) was determined. Over the 3,000,000 cycles, this gave 60 time points where a net fretting current was calculated. These data were used to calculate the average fretting current for both the 66 mm and 44 mm groups over different time periods. We evaluated average fretting current over the entire 110‐h test (3,000,000 cycles), the first 20 h of the test (~550,000 cycles), and the final 20 h of the test.

From the fretting current measurement, cumulative charge over time was calculated by numerical integration of the current over time for each implant. This was done by averaging the current and multiplying it by the elapsed time for each interval. We used this equation QInterval=I1+I22*(T2−T1)

Where QInterval is the charge (µC) at each interval, T1 is the time elapsed (s) at the beginning of the interval, T2 is the time elapsed (s) at the end of the interval, I1 is the net fretting current (µA) at the beginning of the interval, and I2 is the net fretting current at the end of the interval. The cumulative charge was calculated from the summation of QInterval for all 60 time points.

Plots of QInterval over time were created for each implant and a line of decay was fit. The average decay constant for both the 66 mm and 44 mm groups was calculated.

The decay constant was calculated using this equation with the solver function in excel at each time point: Q=A+Be−kt

Finally, the average volume loss was calculated with the assumption that all the material loss was from the CoCrMo liner [24, 25]. V=Q*MWρ*n*F

Where V is the volume lost, Q is the cumulative charge, MW is the molecular weight of the alloy, ρ is the density of the alloy, n is the valence, and F is Faraday's constant. Ion Analysis

An acid matrix of 5% HNO3 was used for ICP‐MS analysis. Trace metal grade HNO3 was used for all blanks, rinse solution, samples and calibration standards. Following three million cycles, 5 ml was taken from the remaining solution for ion analysis (iCAP RQ, Thermo Fischer Scientific, Bremen, Germany). The 5 ml sample solution was combined with 5 ml of 10% Nitric Acid (HNO3, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) generating a 5% HNO3 test solution. To quantify ions released during cyclic testing, calibration curves of known ion amounts were created using a standard (VeriSpec® Multi‐Element Environment Standard Cat#RV010838‐100N, Ricca Chemical Company, Arlington, TX) containing 100 ppm of 59 Co, 52 Cr, 98 Mo, 47 Ti, 27 Al and 51 V. The standard was serially diluted to 10, 5, 0.5, 0.05, and 0.005 ppm concentrations. In addition, a blank solution containing only 5% HNO3 and a blank test solution containing untested PBS at 5% HNO3 concentration were analyzed to determine possible contamination. All samples and blanks were measured for 52 Cr, 59 Co, 96 Mo, 47 Ti, 27 Al, and 51 V ions. Each blank, calibration sample, and test solution was tested in triplicate. Mean and relative standard deviation were reported.

SEM/EDS Analysis

An in‐depth analysis of potential damaged sites on the implants was performed using scanning electron microscope imaging (SEM, Hitachi S‐3700N, Tokyo, Japan). Subsequent compositional analysis and the generation of elemental maps were performed using energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, Aztec, Oxford Instruments, Abington, UK).

Statistics

Student's t‐tests were used to perform statistical analysis between implant groups. An alpha of 0.05 was used. This was done to test for significance between the 44 mm and 66 mm implant when comparing average current, average cumulative charge, average decay constant, average decay constant over the first 20 h of testing, average volume loss, and ion concentration in the test solution. Student's t‐tests were also used when comparing the average current generated in the first and last 20 h of cyclic loading. This was done for both the 44 mm and 66 mm implants.

Results

Net Fretting Current, Cumulative Charge, and Material Loss

The test method, with its intermittent pauses in loading, allows for both the currents associated with cyclic loading and the underlying baseline currents when no cyclic loading is taking place to be captured over the course of the test (See Figure 2). Once both the current and the baseline current are determined, subtracting these two yields the currents associated with fretting only (Figure 2). In the example shown, the baseline currents show an initial drop to a relatively small current, while the fretting currents also start high and fall with cycling. Examples of both the baseline current at a 1 s sampling interval (Figure 2B) and the fretting current over 1 s resulting from the 9 Hz loading (Figure 2C) can be seen. Note, the fretting currents are larger than the baseline and reflect the cyclic loading behavior.

(A) A summary plot from one test (44 mm sample), which shows the analyzed baseline currents, the fretting currents and the difference between these, which yields the net fretting currents. (B) a 1 s sampling of the baseline currents while cyclic loading was paused. (C) One second of total currents sampled while cyclically loading. Note the 9 Hz current response in sync with the applied cyclic load. The average current of two baseline points (B), one before and after five fretting points, was subtracted from the average of the five fretting points (C) to calculate the net fretting currents (total current minus the baseline current).

A representative graph of the cumulative charge, with the line of decay fit to that data, can be seen in Figure 3. It is important to note that the charge was highest during the beginning of the test.

This representative example shows the accumulated charge over the entire 110‐h cyclic loading test (3,000,000 cycles) procedure for sample B from the 44 mm group. Charge (µC) was calculated by finding the area under the curve of the net fretting current (Figure 2A) for each implant. A decay line was fit to the charge to determine how quickly the charge dropped throughout the test.

Over the entire 110‐h procedure, statistical analysis showed no difference between the groups (n = 5) when comparing average current (p = 0.22). The average current of the 66 mm group was 0.15 ± 0.074 µA and the average current of the 44 mm group was 0.26 ± 0.17 µA (Figure 4A). There was also no difference between the 66 mm and 44 mm implant groups (n = 5) for average cumulative charge (p = 0.18). The average cumulative charge was 5.2*104 ± 2.8*104 µC and 9.8*104 ± 6.4*104 µC for the 66 mm and 44 mm groups, respectively (Figure 4B). There was no difference between the 66 mm and 44 mm groups when comparing the average decay constant (p = 0.10). The average decay constant of the 66 mm group was 1.8*104 ± 1.0*104 s‐ and the average decay constant of the 44 mm group was 4.0*104 ± 2.3*104 s‐ (Figure 4C).

There was no statistically significant difference between the average current (A), average cumulative charge (B) and decay constant (C) generated over the full 110 h (3,000,000 cycles) test period.

For both the 66 mm implant and the 44 mm implant, the average current was higher during the first 20 h of the test when compared to the final 20 h of the test (p = 0.002, p = 0.045). In the first 20 h of the cyclic loading test procedure, the average current of the 66 mm group (n = 5) was 0.45 ± 0.19 µA versus 0.044 ± 0.03 µA for the last 20 h (Figure 5A). The average current of the 44 mm group (n = 5) was 0.55 ± 0.40 µA for the first 20 h (Figure 5B). For the last 20 h the average current was 0.1 ± 0.129 µA.

There was significantly more average current generated over the first 20 h compared to the last 20 h (~550,000 cycles) of testing for both the 66 mm (A) and 44 mm implant (B). In addition, there was a significant difference between the two implant groups when looking at the average decay constant during the first 20 h of the experiment (C). The 44 mm implant had a larger average decay constant.

Over the first 20 h of testing, the average decay constant of the 44 mm implant was larger than the 66 mm implant group (n = 5, p = 0.01). The average decay constant of the 66 mm group was 6.9*103 ± 2.9*103 (s‐) and the 44 mm group was 1.3*104 ± 2.5*103 (Figure 5C).

Ultimately, there was no significant difference between the implant groups when comparing average volume loss (p = 0.18) (Figure 6). It is important to note that the values for both implant sizes are low.

The average volume loss for the 66 mm implant was 0.0028 ± 0.0015 mm3 and the average volume loss for the 44 mm implant was 0.0052 ± 0.0035 mm3.

Visible Wear

The implants had minimal visible wear, corrosion, and fretting damage (Figure 8). The only easily visually identifiable difference between the implants when new and post cyclic loading was that each implant had an approximately 1 mm thick line around the circumference of the CoCrMo liner where titanium had transferred (Figure 8B).

Photos taken of the implant post‐test show minor visible damage to both the shell (A) and liner (B) of the 44 mm implant and the 66 implant (C, D). Embedded titanium can be seen in the liner of both the 44 mm (B) and 66 mm (D) implants. Light evidence of fretting can be seen closer to the rim of the liners (B, D).

This damage was confirmed with SEM and EDS analysis (Figure 9). Elemental maps show the grooved machining marks in the CoCrMo acetabular liner (Figure 9A–D) filled with titanium, aluminum and vanadium (Figures 9E–G). This was seen across all implants for both groups.

Backscatter SEM image (A) shows that there is material embedded into the machining marks on the CoCrMo liner. EDS maps show that the CoCrMo liner (B–D) is embedded with Ti‐6Al‐4V (E–G) from the acetabular cup.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the relationship between the severity of fretting corrosion damage and implant size for acetabular cup‐liner tapers. We put two implants of differing sizes, 66 mm, and 44 mm, but otherwise of identical design, through 3,000,000 cycles of intermittent cyclic fretting corrosion testing. In line with our hypothesis, we found low levels of fretting corrosion for both the 66 mm and 44 mm implants. While we hypothesized the 44 mm implant would have lower levels of fretting corrosion, our results show that implant size had no effect.

When comparing the results of the small and large implants, there was no difference between the two for average current, cumulative charge, volume loss, and solution ion concentration. While there was no difference on average fretting current when stratifying by implant size, there was significantly more current generated during the first 20 h of the cyclic loading compared to the final 20 h. This was true for both the 66 mm and 44 mm implant (p = 0.002, p = 0.045). This can be seen in Figure 2A where the peak of the net fretting current is at the start of the test and slowly drops over time. Within a clinical context, that may mean that the time immediately after implantation is when the device is most susceptible to fretting damage. We quantified this drop off by fitting an exponential line of decay to the charge over time for each implant. The smaller implants had a faster rate of decay during the first 20 h of testing than the larger implants (p = 0.01). This meant that while the large and small implants had no difference in the total amount of current generated (p = 0.22), the larger implants spent more time generating high amounts of fretting currents, which could indicate that the “settling time” or time where the device is most susceptible to fretting damage is prolonged in the larger implants. This may be due to the difference in acetabular shell wall thickness. Digital optical images at 20x magnification showed that the 66 mm implant had a wall thickness of 3.9 mm while the 44 mm implant had a wall thickness of 1.9 mm (Figure 10). The thickness of the liner was the same for both sized implants at approximately 0.5 mm. Previous studies show that when impacted, acetabular cups with a smaller wall thickness deform more, which in some cases affected the interaction between the acetabular cup and liner [26, 27]. During the seating of the implant in this experiment, the interaction between the ultrahigh molecular weight polyethylene loading fixture, the liner, and the acetabular cup may have been more favorable to the smaller implant. Further tests are needed to fully explore this idea for dual mobility designs.

Digital optical microscope images at 20x magnification of the 66 mm acetabular cup (A) and the 44 mm acetabular cup (B) show that the 66 mm implant has an increased wall thickness when compared to the 44 mm implant. The deformation of the thinner‐walled 44 mm implant during pre‐test seating may have played a role in the smaller implants having a faster rate of decay during the first 20 h of testing.

Our results from the acetabular shell‐liner interface in dual mobility hips contrast with existing literature on the effect of implant size in conventional THA. In a study by Hirakawa et al. it was found that as the size of the femoral head increased, so did wear volume in the periprosthetic tissue [28]. In addition, there are concerns regarding increased head size and damage and wear to cross‐linked polyethylene liners [29, 30, 31]. For dual mobility acetabular tapers, implant size having no effect on the fretting current and ion release may be considered a positive attribute. While primarily considered for stability and dislocation reasons, these results support dual mobility hips' potential as a reduced‐wear design [32]. We report the average material loss for these implants at 0.0028 ± 0.0015 and 0.0052 ± 0.0035 mm3 for 66 mm and 44 mm implants, respectively. Again, it should be noted that these values are very low.

Ion analysis of the test solution showed low concentrations for both implants across all tested elements. Cobalt concentrations of 0.087 ± 0.054 and 0.057 ± 0.0094 ppm for the 66 mm and 44 mm implants are far lower than what is seen in the tissue of failed orthopedic implants [33, 34].

To the naked eye, both the 66 mm implant and 44 mm implant appear to have minimal damage, with the exception of a gray line of embedded titanium in the CoCrMo liners. This is supported by the SEM/EDS analysis.

Within a clinical context, the range these results fall into indicates a well‐performing, low‐wearing implant design. We urge against using these results as a projection for performance in vivo. Mechanically assisted crevice corrosion is a complex mechanism that stems from biological conditions that are hard to replicate in vitro, combined with fretting corrosion. Even though future work is needed to replicate MACC in vitro, cyclic fretting corrosion testing is still valuable as it simulates the tribocorrosion component of this complex damage mechanism. Within this study, the use of PBS is a limitation as it does not accurately reflect the biological milieu. Future experiments should investigate the effects of accelerated testing conditions, such as adding heat and lowering the pH of the testing solution. Other limitations to this study include sample size. A final group size of n = 5 lowered the power of the study.

Conclusion

In this study, we evaluated the effect of implant size on fretting currents of acetabular cup‐liner tapers during in vitro cyclic loading. We found that size had no effect on the average current, cumulative charge, volume loss, and solution ion concentration. The resulting volume loss and test solution ion concentration were very low. High current levels during the first 20 h (~550,000 cycles) of testing indicate that the implant is at the highest risk of fretting damage immediately after implantation.
