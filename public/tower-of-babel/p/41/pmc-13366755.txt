Development and internal validation of a nomogram for predicting 30-day mortality in older patients with hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis
Authors: Jun Zhou, Jingping Liu, Hua Yin, Mingjun Xie, Hua-Guo Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop and validate a prognostic model for predicting 30-day mortality in older patients with hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH).MethodsThis retrospective cohort study enrolled 204 HLH patients aged ≥ 65 years from January 2015 to November 2023. We divided the cohort into development and validation cohorts in a 7:3 ratio. Then we used logistic regression analysis and the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression (LASSO) to develop a prognostic model. Performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsThe 30-day mortality rate was 40.7%. Multivariate analysis identified five risk factors independently associated with 30-day mortality of older patients with HLH: Age, platelet (PLT), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), UREA, and ferritin. The model has good discrimination and calibration ability (AUC: 0.828 (0.755–0.886) for the development cohort and 0.773 (0.654–0.891) for the validation cohort). The model showed excellent calibration and clinical utility. Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis showed that patients with the nomogram value > 0.3851 were positively correlated with higher 30-day mortality (P < 0.001).ConclusionThe model incorporating age and four routine clinical parameters accurately stratifies 30-day mortality risk in older HLH patients demonstrating strong discriminative ability and clinical applicability, thereby providing a basis for clinical decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07622-4.Keywords: Older patients, Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, Prediction Model, Laboratory Parameters, 30-day mortality

Introduction

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a hyperinflammatory and often life-threatening condition, typically triggered by underlying infections, malignancies, or autoimmune disorders. Its clinical presentation commonly includes persistent fever, cytopenias, and progressive multi-organ dysfunction [1–3]. While etoposide-based regimens have improved overall survival from 21% to 61%, early mortality remains substantial [4, 5]. Older patients experience a disproportionately worse prognosis, frequently attributed to immune senescence [6]. Although age is an established independent predictor of poor outcomes, prognostic studies specifically focusing on older HLH populations are scarce [7], highlighting a pressing need for accurate risk stratification tools to guide clinical management.

Previous research has enhanced predictive capabilities by developing prognostic models or nomograms [8–11]. However, existing studies have largely focused on pediatric and general adult cohorts, with a notable lack of prediction tools designed for older adults. This study aimed to develop and validate a robust model for predicting 30-day mortality in older HLH patients. By systematically training, comparing, and evaluating nomograms using common clinical and laboratory parameters, we sought to identify the most effective model for precise risk stratification, thereby supporting individualized clinical decision-making and potentially improving outcomes in this high-risk population.

Materials and methods

Data source and study design

This retrospective, single-center cohort study included patients aged 65 years or older diagnosed with HLH and admitted to the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University between January 2015 and November 2023.

Diagnostic criteria

The diagnosis of HLH adhered to the HLH-2004 criteria (12), requiring the fulfillment of at least five of the following eight diagnostic indicators: (1) fever; (2) cytopenia affecting at least two lineages (hemoglobin < 90 g/L, platelets < 100 × 10⁹/L, or absolute neutrophil count < 1.0 × 10⁹/L); (3) hypertriglyceridemia (fasting triglycerides ≥ 3.0 mmol/L) and/or hypofibrinogenemia (fibrinogen ≤ 1.5 g/L); (4) splenomegaly; (5) hemophagocytosis identified in bone marrow, spleen, or lymph node specimens without evidence of malignancy; (6) diminished or absent natural killer (NK) cell activity; (7) elevated serum ferritin (≥ 500 µg/L); (8) increased soluble CD25 (sCD25 ≥ 2.4 × 10⁶ U/L).

Study outcome and cohort

The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality within 30 days of diagnosis. The inclusion criteria involved patients aged 65 or above with confirmed HLH. Patients lacking follow-up data, those who received protein infusion or dialysis prior to admission were excluded from the analysis. The final cohort comprised 204 patients, who were randomly allocated into a model development cohort (70%, n = 142) and a validation cohort (30%, n = 62) using stratified sampling by mortality outcome (via scikit-learn v1.2.2) to ensure balanced outcome distribution between the groups (Fig. 1). The study was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital With Nanjing Medical University (Nanjing, China) (2019-SR-066). All data were anonymized, and participant confidentiality was ensured throughout the study. Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design. All the methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The clinical trial number is not applicable.

Clinical data

Demographics, clinical manifestations, laboratory findings and the treatment regimens (etoposide-based HLH-2004 therapy or supportive care) were retrieved from electronic medical records. For patients with multiple hospitalizations, only data from the initial admission were analyzed. Collected data included age at diagnosis, sex, underlying etiology, presence of fever, splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, and other relevant symptoms. Laboratory parameters pertinent to HLH, including complete blood count, coagulation profile, hepatic and renal function tests, lipid panels, and other biochemical markers, were recorded.

Feature selection and model development

To identify the most relevant predictors from demographics, clinical manifestations, laboratory findings and the treatment regimen, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was applied. Multicollinearity among the LASSO-selected variables was subsequently evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF). Predictors with a VIF > 5 were eliminated due to high collinearity. Then multivariate regression analyses were used to identify independent predictors. Predictive accuracy was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and calibration plots, bootstrapped with 1000 resamples. Clinical utility was evaluated through decision curve analysis (DCA). To ensure a robust evaluation, five standard metrics were employed: AUC, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0, univariate and multivariable analysis, multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression), R (version 4.3.3, glmnet for LASSO, rms for nomogram and calibration plots, pROC for ROC curves) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0, the Kaplan-Meier method). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables following a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed variables are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as counts (percentages) and were compared using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Survival probabilities were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared via the log-rank test. The optimal cutoff value was determined using Youden’s index (maximum sum of sensitivity and specificity). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Variables with a missing data rate below 5% were imputed using a random forest method, while those missing ≥ 5% of data were excluded from the analysis (Table S1). Sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the impact of missing data imputation. Using the same predictive variables, model performance (AUC) was comparable between complete-case analysis and random forest imputation, suggesting the robustness of our model (Table S2).

Results

Patient characteristics

The study cohort comprised 204 older patients diagnosed with HLH. Among them, 83 patients (40.7%) died within 30 days. The median age was 70 years (interquartile range (IQR): 60–75), and the majority were male (65.7%, 134/204). Based on etiology, patients were categorized into three groups: infection-associated HLH (I-HLH), malignancy-associated HLH (M-HLH), and HLH from other causes. No statistically significant differences were observed among these groups regarding sex distribution, age, or 30-day mortality rates (infection-related: 39.4%; malignancy-related: 44.3%; other: 29.6%; P = 0.368).

Demographic and clinical data are summarized in Table 1. Patients were randomly allocated into a model development cohort or a validation cohort in a 7:3 ratio, with no significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups. In the development cohort, comparative analysis between survivors and non-survivors revealed significant differences in multiple parameters, including age, neurological symptoms, and a range of laboratory values: platelet (PLT), prothrombin time (PT) and its international normalized ratio (PT-INR), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), fibrinogen (FIB), thrombin time (TT), D-dimer, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase (CK), hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase (HBDH), total bilirubin (TBIL) and its direct (DBIL) and indirect (IBIL) components, total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), total protein (TP), globulin (GLB), urea (UREA), creatinine (CREA), and ferritin (all P < 0.05).

Model development and validation

LASSO regression first identified ten key variables: age, LDL, neurological symptoms, UREA, D-dimer, APTT, CREA, ALT, ferritin, and PLT (Fig. 2). As shown in Fig. 2A, the partial likelihood deviance (binomial deviance) curve was plotted versus log (lambda). Dotted vertical lines were drawn at the optimal values using the minimum criteria, and the 1 standard error of the minimum criteria. As shown in Fig. 2B, a coefficient profile plot was produced against the log (lambda) sequence. The optimal tuning parameter (lambda) selection in the LASSO model used 10-fold cross-validation and the 1 standard error of the minimum criterion. Multicollinearity assessment revealed no significant correlation among the following predictors: Age (VIF: 1.182), neurological symptoms (VIF: 1.217), D-dimer (VIF: 1.516), UREA (VIF:1.728), APTT (VIF:1.430), ALT (VIF:1.127), LDL (VIF: 1.235), CREA (VIF: 1.587), ferritin (VIF:1.644), and PLT (VIF:1.321). Multivariate logistic regression was then used to further screen independent predictors. As shown in Table 2, five variables were proven to be independent prognostic factors: PLT: odds ratio (OR) 0.990, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.981–0.999, P = 0.023; Age: OR 1.127, 95% CI 1.040–1.221; ALT: OR 1.005, 95% CI 1.001–1.009, P = 0.024; UREA: OR 1.120, 95% CI 1.030–1.218, P = 0.008; ferritin: OR 1.0001, 95% CI 1.000042–1.000214, P = 0.004.

A prognostic model was established based on the five independent risk factors, with an AUC of 0.828 (95% CI:0.755–0.886) for development cohort and 0.773 (95% CI:0.654–0.891) for validation cohort (Fig. 3A-B). The C-index in this study was 0.794, showing a good prediction performance. Compared with the independent indicators, the model had the best predictive performance with a sensitivity of 77.6% and a specificity of 78.6% (Table 3). The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness of fit test showed a P-value of 0.455 for the development cohort and 0.250 for the validation cohort, indicating that the model was well calibrated. In addition, the model’s predictive ability was assessed in each etiologic subgroup (Table S3).

Discussion

HLH is a systemic inflammatory condition driven by excessive and dysregulated immune cell activation, leading to a cytokine storm that can precipitate widespread organ dysfunction. Older patients with HLH are particularly vulnerable to severe organ damage compared with younger patients, primarily due to diminished physiological reserves and a greater burden of comorbidities [13, 14]. Existing prognostic research on HLH has largely focused on pediatric and general adult populations, employing conventional statistical approaches to evaluate single or composite biomarkers. Such studies are frequently constrained by small sample sizes, limited variable selection, and a lack of robust validation, ultimately compromising their predictive utility.

Our retrospective analysis utilized LASSO regression to select readily available clinical and laboratory parameters. We subsequently developed and validated a model consisting of five key predictors (Age, ferritin, PLT, UREA, and ALT) to predict 30-day mortality in older HLH patients. The model demonstrated a sensitivity of 77.6% and a specificity of 78.6%, indicating strong predictive performance. Validation using a separate patient cohort confirmed the model’s robustness, with favorable ROC performance and substantial net clinical benefit on DCA. These results suggest the model possesses strong generalizability and clinical applicability for 30-day prognosis in this population, providing a novel tool for individualized risk stratification.

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a frequent and serious complication in HLH, adversely affecting remission rates and survival [15, 16]. Its pathogenesis involves the systemic inflammatory cascade, direct cytotoxic effects of inflammatory mediators, or the underlying primary disease. Biomarkers such as β2-microglobulin and various inflammatory markers have been linked to AKI and poor outcomes in adult HLH patients [17, 18]. Our team’s earlier work also established renal impairment as a critical prognostic determinant [7]. Renal dysfunction reduces renal perfusion and glomerular filtration rate, leading to systemic accumulation of UREA. Consequently, marked elevations in these markers typically signify severe kidney injury and indicate a worse prognosis.

Ferritin, an acute-phase reactant, is integral to HLH pathophysiology and serves as both a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker [12, 19]. Consistent with our prior research, which highlighted different risk hierarchies [7], this analysis identified serum ferritin as an independent risk factor for 30-day mortality in older HLH. Each 1000 µg/L increase in ferritin corresponds to an approximately 10% increase in the risk of 30-day mortality. Notably, some studies have reported that admission ferritin levels are not predictive of 6-month mortality in adult HLH [20]. This discrepancy may stem from variations in study populations, analytical methodologies, and selected observational endpoints.

The hyperinflammatory state in HLH often triggers coagulation abnormalities and a profound bleeding tendency, which can hasten disease progression toward disseminated intravascular coagulation or multi-organ failure. Coagulopathy is widely recognized as a marker of adverse prognosis across numerous conditions [21, 22]. For example, PT and D-dimer are independent prognostic indicators in pancreatic cancer [23]. Coagulation dysfunction, particularly hypofibrinogenemia, is present in approximately 68% of severe HLH cases [24, 25]. Previous study has shown that PLT at diagnosis is correlated with survival outcomes [26]. Platelet secretion defects may contribute to in coagulation dysfunction. In infection-related HLH, pathogens can directly interact with platelets, promoting platelet activation [27]. Tumor cells and platelets also interact to induce platelet activation [28]. Additionally, circulating free DNA and histones released by neutrophils or neutrophil extracellular traps can induce platelet aggregation and coagulation cascade activation [29], which are associated with the severity and mortality of DIC [30]. Furthermore, consistent with our previous research results, age is also an independent risk factor for poor prognosis in patients with HLH [7]. Immunosenescence creates conditions for cytokine storms by activating pro-inflammatory pathways and weakening immune regulatory capabilities [31]. Meanwhile, the decline in organ reserve capacity renders older patients more vulnerable to organ damage when encountering cytokine storms, leading to rapid progression of multiple organ dysfunction and a more critical condition. Older patients with HLH often have multiple chronic diseases, with problems such as insufficient nutrient intake, malabsorption or metabolic disorders, which further exacerbate the reduction in platelet production.

Although not part of the formal diagnostic criteria, hepatic involvement is common in HLH, with many patients presenting hepatomegaly. Our earlier studies identified elevated ALT as a poor prognostic sign in adult HLH patients [20]. This finding is corroborated by Shen et al., who incorporated ALT into a prognostic model for adult HLH [32]. Similarly, pediatric patients with infection-related HLH and concomitant liver injury are more prone to multi-organ dysfunction and poorer outcomes [33]. Activated hemophagocytic tissue cell infiltration or excessive production of cytokines leads to liver damage in HLH patients [34]. Older HLH patients are particularly vulnerable to liver damage mediated by cytokines due to reduced liver reserve capacity. Moreover, their dysfunctional immune systems exhibit impaired cytokine regulation, rendering them unable to suppress excessive inflammatory responses effectively. Consequently, these patients are prone to severe complications, including marked ALT elevation and liver failure, resulting in poor prognosis. This collective evidence underscores that HLH is a multi-system disorder characterized by concurrent abnormalities across several organ systems, justifying why a multi-variable predictive model outperforms reliance on any single indicator.

To assess the incremental value of our model, we compared its predictive performance with previously published HLH prognostic tools, including the model by Shen et al. [32] and our earlier model [20] (Supplementary Table S4). Our model achieved an AUC of 0.828, which was numerically higher than that of the Shen model (AUC = 0.650) and the Zhou model (AUC = 0.689). The DeLong test confirmed that the differences were statistically significant (P < 0.001 and P = 0.002, respectively). These findings suggest that our model offers superior prognostic accuracy compared with existing tools in this older HLH cohort.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to develop and benchmark a prediction model for older HLH using routine laboratory data. However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the single-center, retrospective design may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader populations. Single tertiary hospital recruitment may overrepresent severe HLH cases, which may cause potential selection bias. Second, despite employing multiple imputation for variables with low rates of missing data (< 5%) and excluding those with higher rates, the potential for residual bias from unavailable data persists. Third, our model did not incorporate certain established prognostic markers, such as sCD25, due to a high missing data rate (81.9%) and limited clinical availability of sCD25 testing in our center during the study period. Future large-scale, multi-center prospective studies incorporating these and other relevant variables could further refine and enhance the model’s clinical utility and widespread applicability.

In summary, we developed a model that identifies ferritin, PLT, UREA, and ALT as significant risk factors for predicting 30-day mortality in older HLH patients. Relying solely on routinely measured laboratory indicators, this model demonstrates excellent discriminative power, calibration, and clinical applicability. Our findings suggest that this model can assist clinicians in the early identification of high-risk patients, thereby facilitating optimized clinical management and resource allocation.
