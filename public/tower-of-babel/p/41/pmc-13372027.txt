Pulsed field energy: a superior approach for ablation of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation? Evidence from randomized clinical trials
Authors: Hussam Ali, Ernesto Cristiano, Pierpaolo Lupo, Sara Foresti, Guido De Ambroggi, Silvia Magnani, Maria Giovanna Bucci, Eduardo Celentano, Riccardo Cappato
Published: 2026

Catheter ablation has been established as a valuable therapy for atrial fibrillation (AF), with radiofrequency (RF) and cryoballoon (CBA) energy forms having shown comparable outcomes.1 In recent years, pulsed-field ablation (PFA) has emerged as a novel approach for AF treatment.2,3 Its cardiac tissue selectivity may reduce collateral damage to adjacent structures, such as the oesophagus and the phrenic nerve.

Despite the rapid expansion of PFA and numerous studies and registries, only a limited number of randomized clinical trials (RCTs) provide a head-to-head comparison between PFA and thermal AF ablation.

The aim of this Rapid Communication is to collect evidence-based data from available RCTs evaluating PFA vs. RF and/or CBA performance. We conducted a structured systematic review of PubMed and Embase databases, searching for RCTs that (i) provided comparative assessment of efficacy and safety outcomes, (ii) included ≥2 patient arms comparing ‘pure’ PFA against thermal energy, and (iii) enrolled ≥100 AF patients with follow-up ≥1 year. Study eligibility and data extraction were performed by two researchers (H.A. and E.C.), with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer (R.C.).

Four RCTs were eligible for this analysis including 1393 patients undergoing AF ablation.3–6 All but one RCT,6 were designed for PFA non-inferiority assessment, and two RCTs were funded by the producers of PFA system under investigation.3,5 All patients had paroxysmal AF, mean age was 62.4 ± 8.5 years, 35.8% were women, and CHA2DS2VASC score was 1.6 ± 1.2. Left atrial diameter and left ventricular ejection fraction, available in 1104 patients, were 38.4 ± 5.6 mm and 61.3 ± 5.9%, respectively. Follow-up was limited to 12 months in all RCTs with only one4 using implantable monitors. All trials adopted pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) as the ablation endpoint. Cavotricuspid isthmus ablation was uncommon, except for the ADVENT trial where it was performed in ∼25% of both arms.

Across all RCTs, procedural efficiency favoured PFA with a shorter procedure time (random-effects mean difference −22.18 min, 95% CI [−39.22 to −5.14], P = 0.026), albeit with substantial heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 90.3%). Conversely, fluoroscopy time did not show a significant difference between groups (random-effects mean difference 8.06 min, 95% CI [−14.31 to 30.42], P = 0.261). Notably, the heterogeneity in procedure and fluoroscopy times likely reflects multiple factors. In the ADVENT trial, the use of general anaesthesia in >90% of patients may have prolonged the procedure.3 In addition, operator familiarity with newer PFA technologies was still limited in ADVENT and InsightPFA.3,5 Conversely, the use of 3D mapping during RF ablation may play a role, and its ongoing integration with PFA systems may influence the results of future studies.

Efficacy outcomes were comparable with a pooled random-effects odds ratio of 0.90 (95% CI 0.59–1.37, P = 0.50; I2 = 7.9%), corresponding to 172/697 (24.7%) recurrence events in the PFA group vs. 185/695 (26.6%) in the non-PFA group. Of note, the only study using continuous monitoring reported higher recurrences with thermal ablation,4 highlighting the potential impact of follow-up methodology. Moreover, the recurrence rates (∼25% at 1 year) may reflect the inclusion of a paroxysmal, younger AF population with low risk factors, limiting generalizability to higher-risk AF populations.

Procedure-related major complications, such as death, stroke or transient ischaemic attack, persistent phrenic nerve injury, atrio-oesophageal fistula, severe (≥70%) pulmonary vein stenosis, and major vascular access complications, were low in all studies. The pooled random-effects odds ratio was 0.62 (95% CI 0.16–2.37, P = 0.343; I2 = 10.8%), with 12/697 (1.7%) vs. 20/696 (2.8%) events, respectively.

Sensitivity network meta-analysis showed no significant differences in recurrences (95% CI of OR PFA vs. CBA 0.53–1.14, PFA vs. RF 0.65–1.38, CBA vs. RF 0.72–2.09) or major complications across different techniques (95% CI of OR PFA vs. CBA 0.66–9.01, PFA vs. RF 0.43–1.83, CBA vs. RF 0.08–1.62). Pulsed-field ablation remained faster than both thermal modalities, while CBA was faster than RF (−15.54 min, 95% CI −22.72 to −8.36), supporting workflow-related heterogeneity.

Overall, the present data show comparable 1 year efficacy outcomes and no significant differences in major peri-procedural complications between thermal energy and PFA for the treatment of patients with paroxysmal AF.

While complication rates are likely decreasing over time and the included RCTs were powered for efficacy assessment, it would be challenging to conduct RCTs powered for safety analysis which would likely require the enrolment of several thousand AF patients. Notably, the lower absolute number of major complications in the PFA group (12 vs. 20) was mainly driven by fewer vascular access complications (2 vs. 9), which are unrelated to the type of energy adopted. Furthermore, most RCTs did not systematically assess PFA-specific adverse events, including haemolysis indicators, jaundice, renal injury, and coronary spasm. Rare delayed adverse events such as myocardial ischaemia and malignant arrhythmias have been recently reported following PFA.7 While PFA systems were not specifically designed or safety assessed for ablation beyond PVI, targeting additional substrates in real-world practice may influence outcomes compared with the results reported in the included RCTs.

Accordingly, there is currently no compelling evidence that PFA is safer or more effective than thermal ablation for paroxysmal AF. Moreover, although the present analysis revealed a shorter procedure ‘skin-to-skin’ time with PFA, the included studies did not specifically assess total laboratory occupancy ‘room time’, which may be influenced by the use of general anaesthesia or deep sedation, more commonly required with PFA.8 Furthermore, while a shorter procedure is desirable, it is not the primary goal in clinical practice nor the patient’s main concern.

Our analysis assessed PFA outcomes in paroxysmal AF at 1 year follow-up. Recently, the ADVENT-LTO study demonstrated favourable long-term PFA outcomes at 4 years,9 and the AVANT-GUARD trial showed PFA superiority as a first-line strategy over medical therapy in persistent AF.10

The promising safety profile and efficient workflow of PFA have generated widespread enthusiasm within the electrophysiology community and industry, leading to global adoption and rapid replacement of thermal ablation despite the latter’s well-established long-term safety profile. While the race to develop novel PFA devices among industries is accelerating, the scientific community should maintain vigilance, as cardiac selectivity does not imply that PFA is risk free, and uncommon or delayed complications may not be fully captured in currently available trials. Larger, multicentre, and investigator-driven RCTs are needed to assess the long-term outcomes of PFA across different AF populations.
