Gradual spatial constraint feature selection for robust biomarker discovery in high-dimensional gene expression data
Authors: Hong Wang, Xiaolong Ou, Wenshuai Zhang, Ben Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-dimensional microarray gene expression data presents unique challenges for feature selection to identify sparse, biologically relevant biomarkers conflicts with computational constraints. To overcome these limitations, this paper proposes a Gradual Spatial Constraint Feature Selection framework (GSCFS), a novel wrapper method specifically designed for genomic data that strategically balances search space compression with functional feature interaction modeling. The framework introduces three key innovations. First, a two-stage gradual spatial constraint mechanism based on dimension prioritization, which dynamically controls the decision space of candidate solutions to improve optimization efficiency. Second, a spatial convergence operator employing a chain-based convergence strategy, enabling self-adaptive individual updates based on historical states—reducing external dependencies while accelerating convergence and computational efficiency. Finally, spatial diffusion operator integrating three synergistic strategies (group-space constraints, elite-space constraints, and spatial-domain constraints) to facilitate hierarchical feature co-optimization and enhance feature interactions. Extensive experiments on real-world high-dimensional gene expression datasets demonstrate the statistically validated superiority of the proposed approach. Statistical analysis confirms that GSCFS ranks first in classification accuracy among nine state-of-the-art methods. It successfully isolates minimal feature subsets without compromising predictive performance. Furthermore, the framework achieves an order-of-magnitude reduction in computational time compared to traditional evolutionary wrappers, effectively resolving the conflict between biomarker sparsity and search efficiency.Keywords: Feature selection, Gene expression data, Classification of diseases, Gradual spatial constraints

Introduction

The rapid advancement of high-throughput technologies, particularly in genomics, has led to an explosion of high-dimensional biomedical data, such as microarray gene expression profiles, which are pivotal for cancer detection and biomarker discovery [1]. In such datasets, the dimensionality often far exceeds the number of samples (e.g., thousands of genes vs. hundreds of patients), introducing critical challenges like feature sparsity, computational complexity, and the curse of dimensionality [2]. These issues obscure latent class structures (e.g., cancer vs. normal samples), degrade model generalizability, and amplify risks of overfitting [3]. Direct classification on raw high-dimensional gene data further suffers from prolonged training times and suboptimal performance due to combinatorial explosion and low signal-to-noise ratios inherent in biological systems [4]. Effective feature selection thus becomes essential to identify discriminative genes, reduce computational burdens, and enhance the interpretability of predictive models in precision oncology [5, 6].

Feature selection approaches are generally divided into three categories [1]: filter methods, wrapper methods and embedded methods. The filter based feature selection employs various metrics (distance/information/correlation-based) to evaluate feature-class relevance and inter-feature independence [9–8]. Though computationally efficient, they ignore feature interactions and may overlook critical features in high-dimensional spaces [9]. Unlike filter methods, wrapper based feature selection optimizes feature subsets via classifier performance [10]. While capturing feature interactions and avoiding local optima, wrapper methods essentially pose an n-dimensional combinatorial search problem and their computational cost grows exponentially with dimensionality [11]. Embedded methods integrate feature selection into model training (e.g., sparsity-inducing regularization or tree/ensemble models), providing a compromise between computational cost and predictive performance [12]. However, embedded methods are model-dependent and may inherit model-specific bias or instability.

As a result, most researchers resort to heuristic algorithms, such as Evolutionary Multitasking (EMT) [13], Differential Evolution (DE) [14] and Bacterial Algorithm (BA) [15] and so on. While these methods significantly reduce search time and often yield satisfactory results, these methods face serious scalability issues in ultra-high-dimensional spaces. As the feature space grows exponentially, heuristic algorithms tend to converge prematurely or become trapped in regions dominated by redundant features, leading to high computational costs and degraded performance.

To address these limitations in high-dimensional genomic data analysis, we propose the Gradual Spatial Constraint Feature Selection (GSCFS) framework, a novel wrapper method specifically designed for genomic data that strategically balances search space compression with functional feature interaction modeling. Unlike conventional approaches that treat dimensionality reduction as an afterthought, GSCFS embeds dimensional constraints throughout the optimization process while preserving critical gene-gene relationships – a paradigm shift demonstrated to improve both computational efficiency and biological interpretability in cancer biomarker discovery. Related papers in the past five years

The first perspective considers how algorithms incorporate dimensionality considerations into their frameworks. Three dominant strategies have emerged in contemporary research. The Dimension-as-Goal (DaG) approaches explicitly optimize feature count reduction, often through multi-objective frameworks that balance gene subset size with classification accuracy (e.g., Pareto-optimal solutions for tumor subtype prediction). Alternative methods adopt a Dimension-as-Constraint-Component (DaCC) paradigm, where dimensionality limits are embedded as either hard constraints or penalty terms within the objective function. More sophisticated techniques employ Dimension-as-Constraint-Guidance (DaCG), which dynamically steer search processes using dimensionality-aware mechanisms (e.g., evolutionary algorithms adaptively pruning gene subsets based on pathway coherence).

The second analytical dimension concerns the fundamental evaluation philosophy underlying these methods. Filter-based approaches, focusing on Feature Quality, they prioritize individual gene relevance using statistical measures (e.g., mutual information to identify differentially expressed genes in adenocarcinoma). In contrast, wrapper and embedded methods prioritize Solution Quality by evaluating gene subsets via classifier performance though computationally intensive for large-scale genomics. Bridging these paradigms, hybrid methods demonstrate Synthesis Quality by combining statistical (e.g., removing low-variance housekeeping genes) with iterative wrapper refinement, achieving state-of-the-art results in pan-cancer biomarker panels.

This dual-axis classification framework provides a structured basis for examining the methodological evolution in high-dimensional feature selection. The following discussion will elaborate on these categories, analyzing their respective advantages and implementation challenges while identifying promising directions for future development.

Feature-quality-scored approaches

All the aforementioned methods regard the number of features in the feature subset, that is, the solution dimension, as the objective. Some studies have considered the quality of the feature subset and the solution dimension separately from a multi-objective perspective to optimize and solve for the best [7,39,40,15,27,34]. Other studies have combined multiple objectives, such as solution dimension and feature subset accuracy, into a single objective through weighting, to select a relatively better solution by considering the combined impact [18,1,41,19,23,25,28]. These methods require substantial computational costs when dealing with high-dimensional and ultra-high-dimensional data. This paper proposes constructing an algorithm framework based on the dimension-priority concept. Differences in dominant ideologies

Solution-quality-driven approaches

In recent years, feature selection methods dominated by the idea of solution quality have been widely applied to and studied in high-dimensional and ultra-high-dimensional data [42], with the ultimate goal of selecting an optimal feature subset that is as small as possible [3]. Faced with the vast search space of high-dimensional and ultra-high-dimensional data, most researchers divide the huge search space into smaller ones using various methods and develop effective search strategies within these smaller spaces using wrapper methods [31]. Multi-task [14], multi-population [43], hierarchical learning [7], and ensemble learning [44] are currently common ways to divide large spaces into smaller ones. For instance, X. Wang [18] proposed a multi-task evolutionary learning method based on particle swarm optimization, which uses multi-task learning to optimize related tasks and transfer knowledge; Y. Wang [32] introduced a multi-task feature selection method based on a clonal selection algorithm, identifying optimal features by transferring useful knowledge from two derived related tasks; J. Zhao [7] proposed a multi-objective firefly algorithm based on hierarchical learning, which enhances the performance of feature selection in high-dimensional data by evaluating the clustering initialization population from multiple perspectives. Although these methods have somewhat mitigated the impact of the curse of dimensionality and combinatorial explosion, their demand for computational resources remains high, and splitting the data can easily lead the algorithm into new local optima.

Synthesis-quality-driven hybrids

Another commonly used approach to dealing with high-dimensional and ultra-high-dimensional datasets is the synthesis quality method. Some researchers first quickly reduce the dataset dimensionality using filter based feature selection and then further screen effective features using wrapper based feature selection [21, 38, 45]. Others have incorporated evaluation indicators from filter methods into wrapper methods, such as information theory [45, 46], correlation [1, 24] and multi-filtering [20, 47] to accelerate the convergence of the algorithm on high-quality features and reduce the focus on redundant or useless features in high-dimensional data. Although these methods have to some extent accelerated the feature selection process for high-dimensional data, their running speed is still limited, and their fragmented consideration of the synergy between features makes them prone to falling into local optima.

Overall, to tackle the challenges of high-dimensional data, researchers have employed methods such as multi-task learning [14], multi-population optimization [43], hierarchical learning [17], and ensemble learning [44] and have adopted strategies like hybrid feature selection and incorporating filter-based evaluation systems into wrapper methods to improve search efficiency and reduce computational costs. Despite demonstrating certain performance capabilities when dealing with high-dimensional data, these methods still have limitations, such as high computational costs and a tendency to fall into local optima. Moreover, the instability of algorithm performance is also a significant issue; the “curse of dimensionality” in high-dimensional data remains unresolved, and the efficiency and stability of algorithms when handling ultra-high-dimensional data still need to be improved.

Dimension priority emphasizes transforming the dimension metric from the objective domain to the constraint domain, prioritizing the compression of the search space for high-dimensional and ultra-high-dimensional data and then considering the combined synergistic effects between features. Dimension priority does not deny the effectiveness of considering solution dimension evaluation in the objective function. Instead, it focuses more on the strategies and rules when the solution dimension is used as a constraint during the search phase of the algorithm. By optimizing the solution dimension as a constraint, the algorithm identifies the advantageous range of dimensions and then further considers the combined effects between high-quality features.

The effectiveness of wrapper feature selection methods in high-dimensional and ultra-high-dimensional data has been elaborated upon in the preceding text. Common wrapper search paradigms include swarm intelligence, evolutionary computation, and rule-based heuristics. Owing to their superior global search capabilities, swarm intelligence and evolutionary computation-based wrapper feature selection frameworks are widely and effectively employed in feature selection to efficiently search for high-quality feature solutions [48]. However, when dealing with high-dimensional or ultra-high-dimensional datasets, these frameworks encounter the curse of dimensionality and combinatorial explosion, resulting in overly lengthy feature solutions and a significant increase in computational costs [45]. Moreover, the problem search space grows exponentially with the increase in data dimensions, generating numerous local optima. Additionally, the increase in dimensions further exacerbates combinatorial explosion, making the interactions and associations between features more difficult to discover and exploit. Under such circumstances, swarm intelligence and evolutionary computation-based wrapper feature selection frameworks are prone to getting trapped in local optima, leading to premature convergence.

In contrast, rule-based heuristic algorithms, which leverage the specific structure of the problem or empirical rules to guide the search process, can provide an effective solution for feature selection in high-dimensional data. Therefore, designing appropriate rule-based heuristic algorithms for high-dimensional and ultra-high-dimensional feature selection problems can, to some extent, avoid the aforementioned issues. Rule strategies constructed around the dimension-priority concept can effectively explore large-scale feature spaces and evade local optima. Although they do not guarantee finding the optimal solution, they can identify a near-optimal feature subset within acceptable time and computational resource constraints, better addressing the complexity of high-dimensional and ultra-high-dimensional cancer genomic data.

The proposed approach

Feature selection represents a combinatorial optimization problem that systematically evaluates feature subsets to identify optimal combinations [45]. Traditional heuristic approaches face significant scalability challenges when applied to high-dimensional and ultra-high-dimensional datasets, primarily due to (1) excessive memory requirements for maintaining multiple search agents and (2) computational overhead from repeated solution evaluations. While existing optimization strategies attempt to mitigate these issues through either agent reduction or computation simplification, both approaches introduce critical limitations: reduced population diversity undermines collective learning, while oversimplified evaluations risk premature convergence to suboptimal solutions.

To address these dual challenges, this paper introduces a novel rule-based heuristic framework grounded in dimension-priority principles. The proposed Gradual Spatial Constraint Feature Selection (GSCFS) framework achieves computational efficiency through a two-phase optimization process: (1) rapid solution space compression via dynamic dimensionality control, followed by (2) intensive optimization within pruned solution sets. This approach maintains the exploratory benefits of multi-agent systems while dramatically reducing computational overhead.

The framework’s core innovation lies in its adaptive spatial operations (Fig. 2): Spatial Convergence Operation and Spatial Diffusion Operation. The Spatial Convergence Operation implements a chain-based convergence mechanism that progressively focuses the search space. Spatial Diffusion Operation incorporates three complementary strategies: group-based, elite-based, and best-solution-guided constraints. During execution, each agent autonomously selects operations based on its current position in the problem space and relative fitness within the population, with strict single-operator enforcement per iteration to maintain search discipline. The subsequent sections provide formal specifications of this framework and its constituent operators.

Gradual spatial constraint framework

As established in prior analyses, conventional feature selection methods suffer from prohibitive computational costs when processing high-dimensional or ultra-high-dimensional data. To address this limitation, we propose a dimension-constraint-led gradual spatial constraint framework designed to accelerate solution refinement while enhancing computational efficiency. The framework operates by transforming dimension metrics from the objective domain to the constraint domain, prioritizing search space compression for high-dimensional data before optimizing feature synergy.

The framework employs a dimension adjustment function to iteratively constrain the dimension space, with dimensionality evolution governed by two complementary processes: spatial convergence and spatial diffusion. These dynamics are regulated by a space convergence factor (SCF) and a spatial diffusion factor (SDF), generated through an inverse-S-shaped dimension adjustment function (analogous to a sigmoid function), as formalized in Eq. Beyond their mathematical definitions, these two factors carry specific biological connotations tailored for genomic data analysis:

SCF: Biologically, SCF corresponds to the “Genomic Redundancy Elimination” phase. In high-dimensional microarrays, the vast majority of genes are noise or non-differentially expressed. SCF enforces a strict, high-pressure selection environment in the early stage to rapidly filter out these biologically irrelevant features, akin to purifying selection in evolutionary biology.

SDF: Biologically, SDF corresponds to the “Functional Module Exploration” phase. Since disease biomarkers often function synergistically via pathways or regulatory networks, strictly reducing dimensions might break these links. SDF relaxes the constraints in the later stage, allowing the algorithm to explore subtle gene-gene interactions and form cohesive functional modules within the refined feature space.

This mechanism enables indirect control over feature solution dimensionality during selection, facilitating rapid algorithm convergence while mitigating local optima entrapment.

The SCF controls the rate of convergence, while the space chain convergence strategy dictates the method of convergence. This strategy adheres to a chain-like principle: within a convergence cycle, each individual’s spatial domain evolves independently of the broader population, the global best individual, or unselected features. It is worth noting that while Eq. (9) adopts the standard classification error, the proposed framework still mitigates the class-imbalance bias that is prevalent in genomic datasets. In high-dimensional spaces with limited samples, a wrapper evaluator can easily appear good by exploiting accidental correlations, because majority-class samples are more frequent, redundant genes are more likely to correlate with the majority class purely by chance, yielding deceptively low global error and thus forming majority-guessing local optima. The GSCFS framework counters this tendency through complementary search dynamics. First, the Spatial Convergence Operation aggressively compresses the solution space and removes redundancy, which reduces the degrees of freedom available for such majority shortcuts and acts as an implicit regularizer. Second, the Spatial Domain Constraint provides a controlled restart mechanism: when an agent stagnates in a safe but non-informative subspace, it is forced to jump out and regenerate under spatial guidance, restoring population diversity and enabling the discovery of sparse subsets with genuine discriminative signals for minority classes.Consequently, together with the feature-size penalty in Eq. (9), the algorithm is driven toward robust class separation rather than mere accuracy.

Comparative algorithms

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of our proposed GSCFS method, we conducted extensive experiments on 12 real-world microarray gene expression datasets, comparing against nine state-of-the-art feature selection algorithms: SFE, SPFSR, INF, TMGWO, ISSA, and mgFPA. These algorithms were carefully selected to represent: (1) Three cutting-edge approaches specializing in high-dimensional data (SFE, SPFSR, INF), each exemplifying distinct optimization philosophies (solution quality, synthesis quality, and feature quality respectively); (2) Six high-performance wrapper-based methods (TMGWO, ISSA, mgFPA, IGPSO, LAPSO-DR, RLPSO-ASM) representing recent advances in high-dimensional feature selection. The comparative algorithms are described in details as follows:

SFE (Simple, Fast, and Efficient) [31]: A lightweight wrapper-based algorithm designed specifically for high-dimensional datasets. SFE utilizes a unique single-agent search mechanism with two complementary operators (non-selection and selection) to efficiently explore the feature space. The default parameters are UR = 0.3, UR_Max = 0.3, UR_Min = 0.001.

Experimental setup

To ensure a rigorous and fair comparison, all methods were evaluated under the same experimental protocol. Each algorithm was executed for 20 independent runs with different random seeds, while the train/test split of each dataset was kept identical across methods within each run. For population-based approaches, we used a population size N = 20 and a maximum iteration number T = 500, which corresponds to 10,000 fitness evaluations per run. For methods without an explicit population, we matched the evaluation budget by setting the maximum number of fitness evaluations per run to 10,000. We employed an SVM classifier with RBF kernel as the wrapper evaluator, using C = 1.0 and the library default setting for the remaining kernel hyperparameters, identically for all methods and datasets. For each baseline algorithm, we used the parameter values recommended in the original publication (or the authors’ released code when available) and kept them fixed across all datasets; we did not re-tune baseline parameters on our datasets to avoid introducing dataset-specific advantages.

Evaluation metrics

The comparative evaluation incorporated five complementary performance metrics to provide comprehensive assessment across different dimensions. Classification accuracy (ACC) served as our primary measure of predictive performance, calculating the proportion of correctly classified instances relative to the total sample size. While informative for balanced datasets, we complemented ACC with the F1-score - the harmonic mean of precision and recall - to better capture performance on potentially imbalanced distributions. The area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) provided additional discrimination capability assessment, measuring the model’s ability to distinguish between classes across all possible classification thresholds. We computed AUC values ranging from 0.5 (random performance) to 1.0 (perfect discrimination), with higher values indicating superior classification performance. To evaluate feature selection efficiency, we recorded both the cardinality of selected feature subsets (quantifying dimensionality reduction effectiveness) and algorithm running times (measuring computational efficiency). This multi-metric approach enabled holistic comparison of algorithmic performance across accuracy, robustness, and efficiency dimensions.

The effectiveness of GSCFS stems largely from its spatial diffusion mechanism, which integrates three constraint strategies—group space, elite space, and spatial domain constraints—to collaboratively optimize feature combinations. These strategies enhance inter-feature synergy at different levels: group space constraints leverage collective advantages to refine top individuals; elite space constraints guide general individuals using high-performing exemplars; and spatial domain constraints align exploratory moves with promising solution regions. Together, they help the algorithm escape local optima in later stages and identify more discriminative feature subsets, thereby improving feature selection quality.

A critical challenge in datasets like T9 (where the minority class constitutes only 3.33% of samples) is the tendency of algorithms to ignore the minority class to maximize overall accuracy. As shown in Table 4; Fig. 11, traditional evolutionary wrappers often struggle with this imbalance, yielding lower F1-scores and AUC values (e.g., SPFSR and mgFPA achieve AUCs near 0.5–0.6 on T9, suggesting poor discrimination of the minority class). In contrast, GSCFS achieves the highest F1-score (0.65) and AUC (0.73) on T9. This superiority is attributed to the Spatial Domain Constraint Strategy. When a search agent is trapped in a local optimum (e.g., a solution that simply predicts the majority class), the “jump-out” mechanism (Fig. 8) regenerates the individual. This prevents the population from stagnating in safe but non-informative subspaces. By enforcing strict dimensionality constraints, GSCFS strips away the high-dimensional noise that typically overshadows the subtle genomic signals of rare cancer subtypes, ensuring that the selected biomarkers are genuinely relevant to the disease pathology rather than artifacts of class distribution.

MYH9 is frequently overexpressed in colorectal cancer and has been reported to correlate with lymph node metastasis and poor survival, with functional evidence that it promotes CRC growth and metastasis [58]. COL11A2, an extracellular-matrix collagen component, shows detectable protein expression in colorectal cancer specimens in the Human Protein Atlas, consistent with ECM remodeling in CRC [59]. DES is a stromal/perivascular marker; increased desmin-positive stromal/pericyte signals have been reported in colorectal cancer and correlate with more advanced stage and angiogenic microvessels [60]. Finally, PRPS1, a key enzyme in nucleotide biosynthesis, shows elevated activity/phosphorylation in colorectal cancer tissues and promotes tumorigenesis, linking the selected features to proliferative metabolic programs [61].

Taken together, these representative genes span epithelial homeostasis/differentiation, cytoskeleton-driven invasion/metastasis, ECM remodeling, tumor angiogenesis/stroma, and nucleotide metabolism, providing concrete biological support for the relevance of the selected features.

Ablation study

To quantify the contribution of each key component in the proposed GSCFS framework, we performed an ablation study on all 12 datasets under an evaluation protocol identical to that used in the main experiments. Starting from the complete model (GSCFS), we constructed five ablated variants by removing one strategy at a time: GSCFS_no_dp (without the Dimension Priority mechanism), GSCFS_no_chain (without Space Chain Convergence), GSCFS_no_group (without Group Space Constraint), GSCFS_no_elite (without Elite Space Constraint), and GSCFS_no_domain (without Spatial Domain Constraint).

As can be seen from Table 7, the Dimension Priority (DP) mechanism is the structural backbone of GSCFS. Without DP (GSCFS_no_dp), the search effectively returns to the ultra-high-dimensional space, inflating subset size and evaluation cost and thus hurting generalization; ACC and F1 drop by roughly 30% and 41%. This confirms that progressive space compression is essential rather than a mere efficiency tweak.

Among the diffusion-stage strategies, Elite and Domain mainly improve reliability. Removing Elite (GSCFS_no_elite) weakens anchoring to good regions, yielding about 3–4% lower ACC and 5% lower F1 with higher run-to-run fluctuation and a worse worst case. Removing Domain (GSCFS_no_domain) has smaller mean losses but a clearer robustness penalty, consistent with increased local trapping on noisier settings.

Chain and Group have subtler, partially overlapping roles. Without Chain (GSCFS_no_chain), the method over-compresses and slightly loses discrimination, supporting its function of using historical states to moderate convergence. Without Group (GSCFS_no_group), coordination among top individuals is reduced, making information sharing less orderly with only marginal impact on average performance.

Overall, DP provides the dominant gain, Elite/Domain enhance stability and robustness, and Chain/Group refine exploration-exploitation, giving GSCFS the most reliable performance across datasets.

Conclusions

High-dimensional microarray gene expression data pose significant challenges for cancer biomarker discovery due to their inherent complexity, including excessive dimensionality (e.g., > 2000 genes vs. <200 patient samples), sparse informative features (few disease-relevant genes), and computationally intensive search spaces. Our proposed framework addresses these challenges through a novel dimension-priority approach that simultaneously compresses the search space and enhances biologically meaningful feature interactions, enabling efficient identification of discriminative gene signatures. Experimental results on 12 cancer microarray datasets (spanning colon, leukemia, brain, and prostate tumors) demonstrate that our approach outperforms nine state-of-the-art methods, achieving 3–20% higher classification accuracy with 20–50% smaller gene subsets while maintaining computational efficiency – a critical advantage for clinical translation where interpretability and speed are paramount. The selected features consistently aligned with known cancer pathways, validating the method’s biological relevance.

Future research will focus on extending the proposed framework to address two key challenges: (1) handling ultra-high-dimensional omics data (e.g., single-cell RNA-seq) with large patient cohorts, leveraging distributed computing for population-scale biomarker discovery, and (2) addressing real-world data quality issues like sample imbalance (e.g., rare cancer subtypes) and sparsity (e.g., dropout effects in low-quality biopsies), ensuring reliability in clinical settings. These advancements will strengthen the framework’s utility for precision oncology applications, from early cancer detection to therapeutic target identification.
