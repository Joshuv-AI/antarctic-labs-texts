Single nucleus DNA sequencing of flow sorted archived frozen and formalin fixed paraffin embedded solid tumors
Authors: Yumei Zhou, Xianfeng Chen, Jocelyn S. Chapman, Michael T. Barrett
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArchived samples, including frozen and formalin fixed paraffin embedded (FFPE) tissues, are a vast resource of clinically annotated materials for the application of high-definition genomics to improve patient management and provide a molecular basis for the delivery of personalized cancer therapeutics. Notably, FFPE tissues are stable, provide repeat sampling of tissues of interest, and can be stored indefinitely at ambient temperature. The development of single cell DNA sequencing (scDNA-seq) technologies provides an unparalleled opportunity for the study of tumor heterogeneity and the identification of often rare subclonal cell populations that drive tumor evolution and progression to advanced therapy resistant disease. However, major limitations to the use of archived tissues for scDNA-seq include the low yields of intact cells in the presence of high levels of subcellular debris in biopsies, and the highly variable quantity and quality of the DNA extracted from samples of interest. The latter is of high significance for the use of FFPE tissues due to the presence of DNA–protein crosslinks. In addition, many samples, notably tumors arising in solid tissues, contain admixtures of reactive stroma, inflammatory cells, and necrosis in immediate contact with tumor cells.ResultsTo expand their use for translational studies, we optimized flow sorting and sequencing of single nuclei from archived fresh frozen (FF) and FFPE tumor tissues. Our methods, which include isolation of intact nuclei suitable for library preparations, quality control (QC) metrics for each step, and a single cell sequencing bioinformatic processing and analysis pipeline, were validated with flow sorted nuclei from matching FF and FFPE ovarian cancer surgical samples and a sequencing panel of 553 amplicons targeting single nucleotide and copy number variants in genes of interest.ConclusionsOur flow sorting based protocol provides intact nuclei suitable for snDNA-seq from archival FF and FFPE tissues. Furthermore, we have developed QC steps that optimize the preparation and selection of samples for deep single cell clonal profiling. Our data processing pipeline captures rare subclones in tumors with highly variable genomes based on variants in genes of interest. Background

Archived samples, including both frozen and formalin fixed paraffin embedded (FFPE) tissues are a vast resource of clinically annotated samples with patient follow-up data including pathological stage, therapeutic response, and survival. Notably, FFPE tissues are stable, provide repeat sampling of tissues of interest, and can be stored indefinitely at ambient temperature. As such, these samples represent highly desirable and informative materials for the application of high definition genomics to improve patient management and provide a molecular basis for the selection of personalized therapeutics. The development of scDNA-seq technologies provides an unparalleled opportunity for the study of tumor heterogeneity and the identification of often rare subclonal cell populations that drive tumor progression and the evolution of resistance to targeted therapies [1, 2]. However, major limitations to the use of archived tissues for scDNA-seq include the low yields of intact cells in the presence of high levels of subcellular debris, and the highly variable quantity and quality of the DNA extracted from samples of interest [3–6]. The latter is of high significance for the use of FFPE tissues due to the presence of DNA–protein crosslinks.

Recent studies have described methods to exploit FFPE samples for mutation and copy number analyses with a variety of technologies. These typically select samples exceeding a threshold for tumor cell content based on histological methods such as evaluation of H&E stained slides and macrodissection prior to analysis [7]. Once selected samples are processed in bulk using various protocols consisting of dewaxing, removal of protein crosslinks, followed by DNA extraction and purification [8, 9]. Histology-based processes including laser capture microdissection (LCM) are often used to enrich tissue samples for molecular analyses. However, LCM can be time consuming and labor intensive with low yields of intact analytes when purifying tumor cells and non-tumor cells from admixed solid tissue biopsies. Consequently, current scDNA-seq analyses have focused on liquid tumors, optimally collected fresh tissue, or model systems [10–15]. The inability to exploit archived solid tissue samples has limited the potential of scDNA-seq for advancing translational genomics for patients with cancer and a wide variety of conditions and diseases.

Methods

Tumor samples

All patients gave written informed consent for collection and use of the samples. All tissue was obtained for this study from the University of California San Francisco (UCSF) Gynecologic Oncology Tissue Bank (J. S. Chapman MD, director, UCSF IRB approval #10–04727). Samples were leftover specimens from a clinical diagnostic or therapeutic procedure. Specimens were collected for research purposes only (including extra samples taken during a clinical procedure). All tumor samples were histopathologically evaluated by a board-certified GU pathologist prior to genomic analysis. All research conformed to the Helsinki Declaration (http://www.wma.net/en/30publications/10policies/b3/).

Flow cytometry

Excess paraffin was removed from each FFPE sample with a scalpel from either side of scrolls then processed according to our published methods [16]. In each sorting experiment we used one to three 50 µm scroll(s) from each FFPE tissue block and ~100 mg of FF tissue to obtain sufficient numbers of intact nuclei for sorting and molecular assays. FF tissue samples were minced and suspended in the presence of Nonidet P40 with salts and tris (NST) buffer with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Typically, a range of 5 × 105—2.5 × 106 per ml of nuclei was obtained from FFPE and a range of 2.5—12.5 × 106 per ml of nuclei was obtained from FF tissue samples. Nuclei from each sample, FFPE or frozen tissue, were disaggregated then filtered through a 40 µm mesh prior to flow sorting with an Aria III cytometer (Becton–Dickinson, San Jose, CA) with ultraviolet excitation and DAPI emission collected at > 450 nm. The optimal sorting parameters for FF and FFPE samples with the Aria III sorter were as follows: Sheath fluid pressure was typically 20 psi with a 100 µm nozzle. Drop formation was achieved with piezzo amplitude of 10–25 V and a drop frequency of 30 khertz with a drop delay of 26–27. The sort mode was set to 4-way purity yield with flow rate of 1.44 ml/hour (flow rate setting = 1.0).

Library preparation and QC

The target DNA library was generated using the Tapestri platform V2 according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Mission Bio, San Francisco, CA, USA). Briefly, sorted intact nuclei were concentrated and resuspended in 50–60 µl of cell buffer. Aliquots of ~ 10 µl were used for DNA extraction to assess nuclei DNA quality. Following filtering through a 10 µm mesh the single nuclei suspension was then used as input for cell encapsulation, lysis, barcoding and target amplicon amplification. Additional polymerase chain reaction (PCR) cycles were done as needed to enrich the final library for next generation sequencing (NGS). After clean-up with AMPure XP beads to remove primer dimers, the libraries were quantified and validated for the 460-480 bp targeted PCR amplicon on a TapeStation gel and histogram tracings (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). The DNA library sequencing was then performed on the Illumina NovaSeq S1 NGS platform with 150 bases paired end index reads. Our custom target gene panel includes 553 PCR amplicons each spanning 190-260 bp targeted to coding regions for TP53 (11 amplicons), BRCA1 (37 amplicons) and BRCA2 (53 amplicons), and regularly spaced loci throughout the genome for copy number variants (CNVs) (452 amplicons).

aCGH

All array-based comparative genomic hybridization (aCGH) was done according to our published protocols [16–19]. Briefly, DNAs from tissue samples were treated with DNAse 1 for 30 min prior to Klenow-based labeling. In each case 1 µl of 10 × DNase 1 reaction buffer and 2 μl of DNase 1 dilution buffer were added to 7 μl of DNA sample and incubated at room temperature then transferred to 70 °C for 30 min to deactivate DNase 1. Sample and reference templates were then labeled with Cy-5 dUTP and Cy-3 dUTP respectively using a BioPrime labeling kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) according to our published protocols [17]. All labeling reactions were assessed using a Nanodrop assay (Nanodrop, Wilmington, DE) prior to mixing and hybridization to 400 k CGH arrays (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) for 40 h in a rotating 65 °C oven. After washing microarrays were scanned using an Agilent 2565C DNA scanner and the images were analyzed with Agilent Feature Extraction version 11.0 using default settings. The aCGH data was assessed with a series of QC metrics then analyzed using an aberration detection algorithm (ADM2) [20].

Secondary data processing

We followed Tapestri v2, the secondary data processing pipeline from MissionBio as the basis for developing our FFPE snDNA-seq data analysis pipeline [21]. Specifically, we applied the following steps: 1) fastq reads QC, 2) adapter trimming using Cutadapt [22] and barcode extraction, 3) alignment with BWA to human genome HG19, 4) PCR amplicon QC: we removed 4 amplicons with total reads less than 10,000 over all 16 samples. We then set a read count ≥ 8 as cut-off for defining a sequenced PCR amplicon and plot the density of sequenced amplicons per cell to assess the % of sequenced amplicons over cells for the threshold of PCR amplicon performance in cell calling. 5) Cell QC and detection: For deriving qualified cells (refer to Step 5: Cell calling – Mission Bio Support Center) we 5a) selected the barcodes that pass a total reads cutoff, which was defined by the number of amplicons in the panel times 8 reads, 5b) calculated the panel performance as 0.2 * the mean of all amplicon reads for all qualified barcodes, defined good-performing amplicons as those that pass the panel performance threshold, and 5c) identified the barcodes with at least 55 good-performing amplicons (≥ 10% amplicons per barcodes, based on the density plot of amplicon performance over FFPE samples). These barcodes were then called cells. 6) For single nucleotide variant (SNV) calling, we performed Genotyping with GATK and annotated with ClinVar, and generated a VCF file combining per cell mutations. 7) To remove FFPE artifacts for each qualified cell, we performed somatic calling with GATK Mutect2 and collected read orientation information. We then built a read orientation model with LearnReadOrientationModel followed by filtering with FilterMutectCalls to generate an artifacts list with the information of (Cell.ID | Chr | Pos | Ref | Alt). Artifacts were then removed from the VCF file. 8) For copy number variant (CNV) calling, we used matching aCGH data to build a model for PCR amplicon adjustment and then used the adjusted PCR amplicon depth as CNV measurement at a given region.

PCR amplicon evaluation

We used Gini scoring of the amplicon read count distribution to evaluate the PCR amplicon representation (present/absent) in each library. A high Gini score denotes a high level of absence. The Gini scores were determined with the following steps: 1) Merge 8 frozen samples together, merge 3 DNA index number (DIN) > 4 FFPE samples together, merge 5 DIN < 4 FFPE samples together. 2) From each group, randomly take N cells (N = 1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 500) 100 times. For each time, sum the reads from the N cells per amplicon and calculate the Gini score from the 553 amplicons. Thus, we obtained 100 Gini scores for each set of “N cells”. 3) Draw boxplot for each set of “N cells”, generating sets of “N cells” x “3 groups” = 6 × 3 = 18 boxplots, each boxplot includes 100 Gini scores.

SNV co-occurrence validation

To evaluate SNV co-occurrences which can be used for subclone analysis, for any co-occurrent two SNVs (one from TP53 and another from either BRCA1 or BRCA2) we calculated the count of cells within each sample. We then calculated the Pearson correlation with the cell count of the co-occurrent two SNVs between matching samples.

CNV calling evaluation at flow sorted tumor cell population level

Our panel includes 452 PCR amplicons with spatial resolution of 1-10 Mb for CNV detection. Lower sequencing quality from FFPE samples and the limited spatial resolution of our panel CNV design did not provide reliable read depth for CNV mapping in a single nucleus. Therefore, we performed the CNV comparison between aCGH and snDNA at the population level for each sorted sample. For CNV detection with panel sequencing data, read depth normalization must be performed for 1) comparing read depth of a particular amplicon between samples, and 2) comparing read depth of an amplicon to another amplicon within a sample. To deal with step 1 we normalized each amplicon depth in a snDNA sample with divided by total number of raw read count of the sample (DBNRRC) at the population level, and then convert to log scale (LDBNRRC). To deal with step 2, one obstacle is the efficiency of PCR amplification. Primers designed for each region have different sequences with distinct target binding efficiencies for loci of interest. Therefore, we may observe read depth fluctuation across the amplicons in a normal tissue sample. We have aCGH whole genome CNV data for each flow sorted frozen sample which consists of 411,056 biological probes, 5,126 QC and 1,000 5X replicate probes. The read depths of the probes are normalized (to log10 scaled values) by aCGH processor (We used green background as sample and red background as control). Those values are comparable between probes within a sample as well as between samples. For each PCR amplicon in a snDNA sample, we identified corresponding aCGH probes within 500 bp on the 3’ and 5’ sides of each targeted sequence. A total of 122 PCR amplicons had corresponding aCGH probes. To estimate the correlations, we generated a linear model (using R function ‘lm’) for each PCR amplicon with their bulk level depths from the 8 samples (we did frozen and FFPE separately) and their corresponding probes from aCGH samples. Based on the model, we got efficiency adjusted depth (corresponding to aCGH probe values) given a LDBNRRC of a PCR amplicon of a cell (with R function ‘predict’), since the normalized aCGH probe values are comparable between probes within a sample as well as between samples, and so do the adjusted snDNA amplicon depths.

To generate a linear model, for each amplicon i, we have Yi = ai + biXi where Xi is the LDBNRRC of amplicon i, Yi is the corresponding aCGH probe depth. The model is generated from 8 paired values (yij, xij), where yij represents the aCGH probe depth of amplicon i from samples j, and xij represents the snDNA LDBNRRC of amplicon i from samples j. The 8 paired values are shown in black circles and the formed linear model is shown as a black line. With the model, given a xij, we are able to predict a corresponding aCGH probe value xpij by xpij = ai + bixij. The 8 new paired values (yij, xpij) are shown in red circles. We then generated another linear model based on the new paired values shown in the red diagonal line which means the predicted amplicon depths from snDNA are scaled similar with those from aCGH probes, therefore the same as the depths of aCGH probes. The predicted amplicon depths from snDNA become comparable between and within samples. These new predicted values as adjusted read depths.

After read depth normalization, we evaluated the overall CNV performance. For each sample, we 1) take their final CNV calls from aCGH data, 2) find the overlapped PCR amplicons from snDNA data, and 3) plot them where each dot denotes an overlapped PCR amplicon from snDNA data, with the x-axis the corresponding CNV values (log2 scaled) from aCGH, and the y-axis the raw read depth or adjusted read depth with log10 scaled.

CNV calling evaluation at flow sorted tumor snDNA level

For snDNA depth normalization we used xpijk = ai + bixijk where xpijk represents the predicted corresponding aCGH probe value of amplicon i from samples j of cell k. and xijk represents the snDNA LDBMRRD of PCR amplicon i from samples j of cell k.

Results

To address challenges for the use of archived solid tissue tumors as sources for scDNA- seq, we used resected pancreas and ovarian cancer samples to optimize flow sorting parameters for recovery of intact nuclei from FFPE tissues suitable for single nucleus DNA sequencing (snDNA-seq) (Fig. 1). DNA content based flow assays discriminate cell/nuclei populations based on ploidy including diploid, aneuploid, and elevated 4N(G2/M tetraploid) fractions from biopsies of interest [23]. These assays can be combined with tissue and tumor specific markers to sort subpopulations of diploid and aneuploid populations from routinely collected samples [24–26]. Our previous studies with archived solid tissue tumor samples have shown that sorted populations provide optimal templates for high resolution detection of somatic aberrations in each cancer genome [17, 19, 27]. Optimized flow cytometry parameters included nozzle size, flow rate, and DAPI gating threshold for efficient recovery of intact nuclei from archived samples with a single parameter DAPI based flow cytometry assay. Given the frequent high content of cellular debris present in FFPE tissues, we changed the flow threshold from size-based forward scatter to a florescence-based DAPI threshold to efficiently remove the debris in samples. In each assay, a low threshold (e.g. 1000) was used to initially distinguish population peaks and debris. A high threshold ranging from 10,000 – 20,000 was then applied for sorting to account for cellular debris and intact nuclei in each sample. The use of adjustable thresholds for individual samples highly improved the efficiency and accuracy of nuclei flow sorting for FFPE tissue samples (Fig. 1A-B). In each assay we collected up to four streams based on the appearance of distinct peaks in the DNA content histograms. This allows the simultaneous identification and collection of any or all 2N(G0/G1), 4N(tetraploid/G2/M) and aneuploid populations in each sample of interest [16, 17]. Visual inspection of sorted DAPI stained nuclei confirmed the removal of debris and the presence of intact nuclei in concentrations suitable for the preparation of high complexity snDNA-seq libraries (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 1). We used a bespoke sequencing panel targeting prevalent mutations in serous ovarian carcinoma (SOC) and providing broad genome coverage for use with the Tapestri® single cell sequencing platform [28].

We flow sorted 8 pairs of matching archived frozen and FFPE ovarian samples; 6 high grade SOCs, 1 low grade SOC and one endometrioid ovarian carcinoma, to enrich tumor nuclei suitable as inputs for library preparations. Tissue sample ages ranged from 9 to 19 years (Supplementary Table 1). QC analyses on each step of our workflow confirmed the size, purity, and content of the libraries from each flow sorted sample (Fig. 2A, B, Supplementary Table 2). Each frozen tissue derived library had > 70% on-target versus primer dimer sequences. However, the content of the FFPE derived libraries varied from 28 to 80% target sequences. The DNA integrity numbers (DINs) for flow sorted frozen samples were ≥ 7.9 while FFPE samples were 3.0 to 5.2, reflecting their lower DNA quality and DNA fragment sizes relative to frozen tissues (Supplementary Fig. 2). Notably, FFPE samples with DIN > 4 resulted in the highest complexity on target libraries with reduced levels of primer dimers and provided the highest coverage for each targeted locus across single cells (Fig. 2C). Together, 5/8 FFPE samples, including two with DINs < 4 (FFPE 3 and 7) had > 50% on target sequences (Fig. 2A). To adapt the Tapestri® pipeline for FFPE samples we processed and rigorously evaluated data generated with our flow sorted archived samples and our 553 amplicon NGS panel (Fig. 3) [21]. Briefly, after sample and library preparation and QC we included a series of filtering steps followed by target identification and correlative data analysis. These include 1) adapter trimming and extraction of cell ID barcodes [22], 2) alignment of reads to hg19 with BWA and delivery of separate BAMs by barcode (i.e., each cell has a BAM file), 3) density plots of sequenced amplicons per barcode and detection of qualified cells, 4) genotyping per cell with GATK HaplotypeCaller [29] and combining SNVs from cells for generating a genome VCF file (gVCF), 5) application of GATK modules for FFPE artifact treatment that include generating a read orientation model with LearnReadOrientationModel followed by filtering with FilterMutectCalls [30], 6) generation of loom files, and 7) normalization of amplicon read counts and CNV calling. Matching aCGH data bench-mark model was used for PCR amplicon depth normalization and CNV measurement at given region. Density plots of sequenced amplicons (raw read counts ≥ 8) per cell were used to assess the threshold for amplicon performance in cell calling (Supplementary Fig. 3). This step was essential to address the variable quantity and quality of DNA in archived tissues. We used a threshold of 55 sequenced amplicons (10%) per cell for FFPE samples. Consequently, we identified 2.3—10 k cells from FF and 1.1—9 k from FFPE samples (Supplementary Table 3). We used a series of metrics to assess the comparison of frozen and FFPE scDNA-seq results. For SNV analysis, we used the ClinVar database to identify all SNVs of interest, pathogenic and others, counted both heterozygous and homozygous SNVs as the same mutated cells, and used a threshold of 0.02 (mutated cells/genotyped cells) for identification of single cells with clonal mutations. For each frozen sample, we performed Pearson (red) and Spearman (green) correlations with mutated cell counts from the 8 FFPE samples forming a box-plot. The paired sample correlations are marked as a star. Pearson correlation of paired samples is a range of ≥ 0.9. Spearman correlation of paired samples is a range of 0.55–0.85. Spearman correlations, using a 0.02 cut-off for detection of mutant single cells, were 0.55–0.85 with increased values associated with DIN > 4 samples (Fig. 2D-E). FFPE artifact treatment significantly improved the association between FF and FFPE (Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 4). To evaluate the PCR amplicon representation in each nucleus we used Gini index scores, a measure of the degree of inequality in distributions (Fig. 2F) [32]. Frozen vs FFPE Gini index scores, although high for single nuclei, decreased significantly for > 10 cell comparisons notably for DIN > 4 samples.

We identified subclones based on pathogenic TP53, BRCA1, and BRCA2 variants in the matching frozen/FFPE samples (Fig. 4, Supplementary Figs. 5–7, Supplementary Table 5A-B). For example, we sorted a near tetraploid aneuploid population in matching samples from the grade 2 endometrioid tumor and detected single cells with a TP53 indel and heterozygous pathogenic variants BRCA1C1225Afs*10 and BRCA2N1287Ifs*6 that co-occurred in < 1.0% of the 1,609 frozen and 196 FFPE cells sequenced in the absence of any TP53mut clones (Fig. 4A-C). In contrast, a grade 3 high grade SOC had a series of homozygous TP53E310* mutant clones with combinations of heterozygous BRCA15074+1G>A splice donor and BRCA2N1287Ifs*6 indel pathogenic variants in the 2,878 frozen and 557 FFPE cells sequenced (Fig. 4F-H). Although the detection and prevalence of clones was affected by the quality of the tissue, each of the subclonal populations in the frozen sample was detected in the sorted FFPE. To assess the performance of our panel to measure CNVs, we profiled each flow sorted frozen tissue with 400k aCGH (Agilent Technologies) (Fig. 4D, I). Aberrant CNV intervals were defined by aCGH log2ratios < -0.25 (loss) and > 0.25 (gain) with our established methods and used as references for CNV detection in matching frozen and FFPE samples [16, 17, 20]. After adjustment based on the aCGH data, the correlation between CNV calls from matching frozen and FFPE samples with aCGH was 0.79 and 0.83 respectively (Supplementary Figs. 8, 9). These included single copy gains and losses as well as focal amplicons including an 8q24 amplicon that included the MYC locus, with log2ratio of > 2.0 identified in both frozen and FFPE single cell data for a high-grade SOC (Fig. 4E, J, Supplementary Fig. 10). The Gini scores for FFPE samples with DIN > 4 and using data from > 5 nuclei are equivalent to the Gini scores for single cells from frozen samples (Fig. 2F). This supports the use of > 5 PCR amplicons for CNV detection of loci of interest with flow sorted FFPE samples that have a DIN > 4 in future panel designs.

Discussion

The high-resolution genomic analyses of biomaterials from human specimens are highly dependent on the cellular composition of the specimens [33, 34]. For example, a high degree of surrounding normal cells in a tumor biopsy can make it difficult to isolate a sufficient number of neoplastic cells for analysis of cancer genomes with a high degree of sensitivity [34–36]. Archived samples, notably FFPE tissues present a number of challenges for single cell sequencing. These include isolation of sufficient numbers of single cells as inputs for library preparation, the variable quality of tissues and DNA, and the presence of admixtures of tumor and non-neoplastic cells. The latter can reduce the resolution of detection of rare premalignant and tumor populations in samples of interest. DNA content flow cytometry sorting enables isolation of nuclei from dominant aneuploid and tetraploid populations in large numbers of solid tissue tumors. Our study highlights the development and application of quantitative QC metrics. Visual inspection provides a rapid and efficient confirmation that sorted nuclei are intact and that subcellular debris has been removed during flow sorting (Fig. 1C). The DNA quality for intact nuclei can vary extensively likely due to the presence and removal of DNA–protein crosslinks. Notably, the DIN value of DNA from flow sorted samples provides a robust QC metric that can inform selection of samples as inputs for snDNA-seq. In the present study, samples with DIN > 4 provided the higher complexity libraires and extended coverage of genes in the NGS panel (Fig. 2C-F). However, samples with relatively low DIN values (< 4) can be explored at the single cell level by increasing the number of nuclei used for library preparation (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Previous studies with optimal samples applied relatively high thresholds for the number of sequenced PCR amplicons in each single cell or nucleus [14, 31]. To account for the variable quality of FFPE samples including those with low QC metrics we used a threshold of 55 sequenced amplicons (10%) per nucleus for FFPE samples (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 3). Consequently, we identified 2.3—10 k cells in FF and 1.1—9 k in FFPE samples (Supplementary Table 3). This provided the identification of co-existing mutations and clonal populations in our set of archived high grade SOC samples that had been collected and stored from 9 to 19 years previously.

.Our panel CNV design did not provide reliable read depth for CNV mapping in a single nucleus (Supplementary Figs. 9–10). Although limited by the sparse genomic spacing of CNV amplicons in our panel, once adjusted to data from established aCGH methods, our results displayed relatively high correlations for the detection of gains and losses in each sample of interest. However, to increase the resolution of CNV detection in single nuclei, future studies will include panel designs that include multiple PCR amplicons for loci of interest. Notably, the use of flow sorted FFPE samples with DIN > 4 will provide higher resolution of snDNA-seq CNV and SNV detection in samples of interest. Nevertheless, suboptimal DNA samples can still be exploited for snDNA-seq with our workflow and data pipeline (Supplementary Fig. 11).

We have flow sorted nuclei from a variety of solid tissue tumors. These include primary and metastatic tissues of pancreatic adenosquamous and adenocarcinoma cancers, as well as cancers of the prostate, colon, and breast, and melanoma among others [16–18, 37, 38]. Samples have included archived FFPE and optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT) tissues, needle biopsies, surgical resections and preclinical models. The present study leveraged our access to matching FFPE and FF tissue from ovarian tumors to develop and validate our workflow for snDNA-seq. Consequently, based on our extensive history of sorting tumor samples and this current study, our snDNA-seq protocols are compatible with fresh and archived solid tissue tumors and premalignant tissues. Panel design in future studies can be customized for different tissues of interest and incorporate cancer specific driver mutations, recurring CNVs, and break points associated with translocations.

A limitation of our single parameter DNA content based approach is the diploid nature of subsets of tumors that cannot be readily distinguished from non-neoplastic populations. Focussing on 4N(G2/M) fractions, which are enriched with cells actively transiting through the cell cycle, can improve the purity of sorted diploid tumors [18, 19, 27, 39]. In addition, we and others have shown that combined DNA content with tissue specific antibodies such as those targeting proliferation (Ki67) and differentiation (cytokeratins, vimentin) increases the resolution of genomic analyses for a variety of tumors regardless of DNA content [17–19, 40]. Future snDNA-seq studies will include combinations of additional flow cytometry markers with DNA content to profile distinct subsets of tumor populations. Of significant interest for ovarian cancer will be nuclear oncoproteins including mutant TP53 that can be exploited for studies of early lesions including serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma (STIC).

Conclusions

Our flow sorting based protocol provides intact nuclei suitable for snDNA-seq from archival frozen and FFPE tissues. Our approach integrates our expertise in flow sorting of solid tissue tumors including FFPE samples, next generation sequencing, and clinical studies and management of patients with SOC. This includes QC steps that optimize the preparation and selection of samples for deep single cell clonal profiling and a snDNA-seq data processing pipeline that captures rare subclones in tumors with highly variable genomes based on variants in genes of interest. These methods will enable novel high resolution snDNA-seq studies of valuable archived samples including FFPE tissues from patients enrolled in clinical trials and serial biopsies collected over extended periods of time from patients at risk of cancer and who progressed to advanced stages of disease.
