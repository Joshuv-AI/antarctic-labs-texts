Introduction

Synovial chondromatosis (SC), also known as synovial chondroidosis, affects young adults aged 20 to 50, with a male-to-female ratio of 4:1. This condition involves the transformation of the synovial membrane into cartilaginous or osteocartilaginous bodies within the joint. SC is relatively rare in the ankle, ranking fifth in prevalence after the hip, shoulder, elbow, and knee. This report compiles essential diagnostic, etiopathogenic, and therapeutic characteristics of this uncommon ailment and underscores its key attributes through a literature review. The present case study adheres to the SCARE criteria (25).

Case presentation

A 26-year-old patient sought medical attention due to left ankle pain and recurrent trauma persisting for 6 months. The patient had no previous medical history or allergies.The patient had no family history of osteochondroma. Physical examination revealed ankle joint swelling, with preserved range of motion in all planes. Ankle radiography displayed a calcified oval foreign body (Fig. 1), while arthrography revealed multiple well-defined calcified loose bodies (Fig. 2). Arthroscopy revealed several loose bodies (Fig. 4) and a hypertrophic synovium. Arthroscopic resection of the loose body (Fig. 5) and partial synovectomy were conducted without complications. Pathological analysis confirmed synovial chondromatosis diagnosis. Post-operatively, the patient's ankle mobility improved, and no complications were reported during a 01 year follow-up period (Fig. 3, Fig. 6).

Discussion

A benign metaplasia of the synovial membrane can result in synovial chondromatosis of the ankle [2]. It is a very rare chronic condition that has only been reported four times in all history. Ambroise Paré reported first seeing this condition at the knee in 1558. SC affects the monoarthleitc joints— or the same bone in only one ligament—such as the knee, hip, shoulder and elbow [1,2]. Up to 50 % or more of people with SC have their predilection site at the knee, and up to 65 % have it at the hip. Jacob Jaffé postulated in 1951 that granulomatous degeneration of the synovial lining caused the affliction. Laennec established the synovial nature of the disease in 1893. There is no definitive cause for synovial chondromatosis. Hypotheses regarding the condition's etiology range from reactive changes in the synovial membrane due to residual mesenchymal cells or vasoconstrictive and inflammatory effects caused by microtraumas. An additional theory posits that synovial chondromatosis results from secondary trauma. This suggestion seems more plausible because trauma was present in several reported cases of synovial chondromatosis and because of our diagnosis [3].

The disease can be classified into 3 phases based on maturation [4]. Milgram described this classification system in 1977: early, transitional, and late. Early phase is described as active synovial disease without the presence of loose bodies. Transitional phase is described as the presence of synovial dis- ease and loose bodies. Late phases is described as loose bodies without synovial involvement [5].

Diagnosis may be difficult,although a history of ankle sprains is often described, patient may not experience any trauma, inflammatory or other joint disease [[6], [7], [8]],

Clinical symptoms typically include pain, swelling, stiffness and restriction of ROM [2,9]. Aching is described as chronic, subtle, a “catching sensation”, or dull; in some cases, patients refer to “walking on pebbles” [10,11]. Discomfort is aggravated by physical activity, running, shoe-wearing, weight bearing and climbing stairs, which could last all-day long [6,7,10,12]. Patients may also report tingling and burning along the plantar aspect of the foot [6,7]. But the physical exam can be normal.

Plain radiographs are mandatory for the diagnosis of SC.

Features of SC include multiple intra articular chondral bodies of uniform size distributed within the joint, with “ring-and-arc” chondroid mineralization [2,13]; calcified bodies are usually smooth, round and finely stippled [14].

Calcifications or mineralization are common, osseous erosion is also typical but not correlated with symptoms.

The diagnosis is confirmed by histo pathological examination, which reveals a «cobblestone appearance» of lobulated hyaline cartilage [2,8,15,16].

Synovial chondromatosis of ankle is an exceedingly rare entity with very few published reports in literature. Isolated cases of ankle synovial chondromatosis have been reported by Pathak [17], Ozyurek [11] and Ozmeric [17]. These cases were treated with either open or arthroscopic excision. Galat reported the largest case series of patients with foot and ankle synovial chondromatosis in 2008 [18].

In the of Galat study 8 patients underwent synovectomy and excision of the loose bodies.

Over a follow up of 9 years, 4 patients remained asymptomatic without any recurrence. One patient had midfoot arthrodesis following degenerative changes. 3 patients underwent below knee amputation, one for multiple recurrences and two for malignant transformation to low grade chondrosarcoma. This report suggests that ankle synovial chondromatosis has a potential of leading to degenerative changes in the joint and also malignant transformation.

Hence, the condition should be promptly diagnosed and treated with synovectomy and loose bodies removal [19].

Traditionally, synovial chondromatosis of ankle has been treated with open surgery [1,18]. Arthroscopic excision and synovectomy is now getting acceptance [20,21]. Arthroscopic treatment has potential advantages like better access to the joint, lesser morbidity, early rehabilitation and recovery [22].

However, there is possibility of limited synovectomy or residual loose bodies with arthroscopic surgery.

Loose bodies removal and synovectomy is becoming the standard arthroscopic treatment for management of ankle synovial chondromatosis [19].

Conclusion

Synovial chondromatosis of the ankle is a chronic and rare condition. Arthroscopic treatment offers effective and reliable outcomes, boasting advantages over open surgery. However, arthroscopy may carry the risk of incomplete synovectomy or residual loose bodies. Effective management involves complete synovectomy with wide margins and removal of all loose bodies. While rare, the potential for malignant transformation underscores the importance of diligent care. This case report is presented due to the rarity and clinical significance of the condition.
