Study of kinetic model for fungal spore germination under dynamic conditions: Case study on germination of Penicillium expansum spores
Authors: Panuluk Boonruang, Nuttapol Lerkkasemsan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The germination of fungal spores under non-isothermal conditions has been studied for several years, however, no mathematical model has been able to describe them suitably. Therefore, in this paper, we developed a new model to clarify the understanding of fungal spore germination under non-isothermal conditions by considering the effects of magnitude of condition change and difference in phase of spore during condition change on kinetic parameters, i.e., the mean rate of germination (μ) and the time that half of spores had germinated (th). To combine both effects into our new model, it was necessary to develop other two new models. The first model was used for estimating the swelling time of spores. The second model was the relationship between thvs μ. After we had completed both models and successfully combined both effects into the new model for fungal spore germination under non-isothermal conditions, the new model was validated against experimental data on germination of Penicillium expansum spores in the literature. The prediction results showed that the new model well described the germination of this fungus under non-isothermal conditions.

Introduction

The fungal life cycle has three phases: spore production, spore germination, and mycelium growth. Fungal contamination on food is usually the spore phase [1]. The spores are not dangerous and cannot directly damage the food. However, they may germinate and become mycelium, if they are in conditions suitable for germination [1], causing food spoilage and toxicity for surrounding organisms, especially animal and human health [2,3]. Therefore, understanding the mechanism of spore germination to discover methods for inhibiting the germination may extend the life of food. The mechanism of spores germination, under various conditions, has been studied by Gervais et al. [4], who showed spore germination had two main steps (see Fig. 1). The first step was swelling of spores, resulting increase in their diameter and mass. This step was controlled by various factors, including intracellular metabolism, nutrient penetration barriers, self-inhibitors, humidity and defective genes [2,4,5]. Defective genes affected protein synthesis in spore cells, which controlled the rate of increase in diameter and mass of spores in the swelling step [5]. After swelling, the spores will generate a germ tube, until the length of germ tube is equal to the width of the greatest dimension of the swollen spore: then, the germ tube will become the mycelium [6]. The germination time is defined as the time from the first step until the germ tube was formed [[7], [8], [9]].Fig. 1Germination of fungal spores.Fig. 1

Germination of fungal spores.

Behaviors of spore germination at various conditions have been shown to be different [1,[10], [11], [12]]. Thus, development of a mathematical model for spore germination under various conditions is important for understanding its mechanism. The prediction of the fraction of germinated spores is more suitable than the prediction of individual spore, as the characteristics of each spore are different. Research indicated that the germination time of each spore incubated under stress conditions was highly variable [1,13]. Therefore, all developed models estimated the fraction of germinated spores as a function of time to overall describe the germination mechanism of spores in the system. The logistic and Gompertz models have been commonly used for predicting the spore germination [14]. Both models were originally used for predicting bacterial growth [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]] and were usually used to overall predict macro-scale organism growth to describe the growth mechanism of micro-scale organism [20]. Moreover, in 2011, Dantigny et al. [21] proposed a new model for predicting spore germination. Their model could well predict the germination of Penicillium chrysogenum spores at different conditions.

These models are based on different hypotheses. The logistic model assumed that the growth rate of organism followed a normal distribution (Symmetry growth) [21]. While the Gompertz model and Dantigny et al.’s model assumed that there are more faster growing organisms than slower growing ones (Asymmetric growth) [21,22]. The germination of P. chrysogenum showed that the behavior of the germination followed the logistic model better than the Gompertz model [6]. Other researchers similarly reported that the logistic model was able to predict spore germination of other fungi species better [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. However, these models are only reasonable for the germination under constant conditions. In practical, environmental conditions, i.e., temperature and moisture, are dynamic (may change at any time), thus the traditional form of the models might be incorrect [1,[27], [28], [29]].

The model for predicting the germination of fungal spores under dynamic conditions was discussed by Gougouli and Koutsoumanis [1], but they were less successful in predicting spore germination under non-isothermal conditions, as their model did not include the effects of magnitude of condition change and difference in phase of spores during condition change on kinetic parameters. Both effects on kinetic parameters have been confirmed by Kalai et al., 2014 [30], however, there is still no mathematical model to describe them. Therefore, we attempted to develop a new model that combines both effects to clarify the understanding of fungal spore germination under dynamic conditions. We selected temperature as an environmental factor for modeling fungal spore germination as it was practical to be controlled. In addition, the germination data of fungal spores under dynamic temperature conditions was sufficient to be formed as a mathematical model [1,30]. So, the “dynamic conditions” term, in this work, referred to the “non-isothermal conditions” term. We hoped that a more complete understanding of spore germination under dynamic conditions would be beneficial for microbiologists to find the conditions for spore growth control.

The considered kinetic parameters for spore germination prediction consist of the mean rate of germination and the germination time (time required for 50 % of viable spores to germinate) [21,29,31]. Both parameters depend on the environmental factors, for example, temperature, composition of the atmosphere, and humidity [29,[32], [33], [34]]. The mean rate of germination at various conditions can be explained by the Cardinal Model with Inflection (CMI) [35]. However, there is still no mathematical model for precisely predicting the relationship between environmental factors and the germination time. Gougouli and Koutsoumanis [1] explained this relationship using the modified CMI model. They showed that the germination time at the boundary, could not be calculated precisely. Other research [1,24,36] found that the germination time and the mean rate of spores germination are related, but a mathematical model of this relationship was not completely developed. Parameters in this relationship might be used as a physiological index of fungi spores for the germination [1], i.e., constant of fungal spore which is independent from environmental factors. Therefore, our contribution was formally modeling this relationship.

Modification of μ under non-isothermal conditions

The modification of μ depends on the time when the temperature is changed and the magnitude between initial temperature (Ti) and final temperature (TF). If the temperature is changed in swelling phase (ts < t2), the value of μ will be adapted instantaneously to the new temperature (μ(TF)) as it is not disturbed from the effects of germinated spores. However, in case of the temperature change in the exponential phase (ts > t2), the adaptation of μ is required to be considered from two effects. The first effect is the stress of spores due to the magnitude of temperature change. Another effect is the fraction of spores showing germ tube (difference in phase of spore) during the change in temperature.

The stress of spores due to the magnitude of temperature change can be explained by using Fig. 4 (a, b), which shows that, if the final temperature is between Ti,min to Ti,max, there is no effect of the spore stress. Validation of the mathematical models for fungal spore germination under isothermal conditions

The experimental data from Gougouli and Koutsoumanis [1], which we collected by using “Web Plot Digitizer” [39], was fitted to the logistic (equation (2)), Dantigny et al. (equation (22)) and Gompertz (equation (23)) models in order to compare the prediction precision. Gougouli and Koutsoumanis [1] studied the germination of P. expansum spores under pH 4.2, a water activity (aw) of 0.997, and temperatures of 0.1, 5.2, 10.5, 15, 19.5, 25.5, 27.5, and 30 °C. Unfortunately, we could not collect the germination data of P. expansum spores under 15, 19.5, and 27.5 °C because the figure in their article is unclear [1].

Validation of the mathematical models for fungal spore germination under non-isothermal conditions

The experimental data used in this section was collected from the study of Gougouli and Koutsoumanis [1]. They studied the germination of P. expansum spores by varying the incubated temperature while P. expansum spores were germinating (P ≠ Pmax). Then, they developed new models (equation (30) – (31)) for predicting the germination of P. expansum spores under non-isothermal conditions. However, their models (equation (31) – (32)) were less successful to predict the germination under non-isothermal conditions [1]. In this work, we formed new models for the germination under non-isothermal conditions, expressed in equations (10)–(21). The new models (equation (10) – (21)) would be fitted to the germination data of P. expansum spores compared with the models of Gougouli and Koutsoumanis (equation (31) – (32)).

Results and discussion

Fungal spore germination under isothermal conditions

The logistic, Gompertz and Dantigny et al. models are compared in Fig. 6. The kinetic parameters and statistics of the germination prediction under isothermal conditions are shown in Table 1. Fig. 6 and the metrics in Table 1 show that experimental data were well fitted with all models. The values of R2, SE and RMSE of these models were insignificantly different. However, the swelling time of fungal spores estimated by the logistic model (t2) was better than the Gompertz model (λ). Thus, the germination of fungal spores at the swelling stage followed the logistic model more than the Gompertz model, as shown in Fig. 6. Precise estimation of the spore swelling time at any condition could guide the precise prediction of fungal spore growth at isothermal and non-isothermal conditions, leading the microbiologist to find the condition to accelerate or inhibit the fungal spore growth. For this reason, the logistic model was more suitable than the Gompertz model. Comparing the logistic model and Dantigny et al.’s model, showed that the curves of both models were almost similar. Moreover, the values of th from both models, in Table 1, were insignificantly different. However, the design parameter (d) in Dantigny et al.’s model was not a function of environmental factors and not a constant of fungal species [21]. Since P0 was defined as the fraction of germinated spores at t = 0, it should be absolute zero as there was no germinated spore at that time. However, the fraction of germinated spores, in other words, could be referred the average growth fraction of spores in the system. If the growth fraction of spores was involved with the proportion of mass or size (or other possible parameters) of spores at time t to time t→∞, it meant that the initial fraction could not be absolute zero because determining P0 = 0, might imply that there was no spore in the system at the starting point. The first criteria for predicting germination under non-isothermal conditions was determining the values of Tb,min and Tb,max calculated from equation (15), which led to Tb,min = 1.95 °C and Tb,max = 33.5 °C. If the temperature was lower than Tb,min or higher than Tb,max, while the germination time was between t2 and th, the fraction of spores showing germ tubes would affect values of th and μ. On the other hand, if the temperature lay between Tb,min and Tb,max, the kinetic parameters would be affected by spore stress only. Germination, involving the effect of spore stress only, is shown in Fig. 11, Fig. 12, Fig. 13, Fig. 14, Fig. 15, Fig. 16 (f = 1), while another effect is illustrated in Fig. 17. Fig. 11, Fig. 12, Fig. 13, Fig. 14, Fig. 15, Fig. 16, Fig. 17 also include the integrations of μ and 1/th with respect to temperature. The integrations were used to determine k and ta,s with temperature change. To simplify the calculations in this work, Fig. 10 (a, b) shows tn (see equation (21)) for the cases in Fig. 11, Fig. 12, Fig. 13, Fig. 14, Fig. 15, Fig. 16, Fig. 17 tn was an important parameter for determining ta,s.

Fig. 11 shows spore growth at 25 °C (Ti) for 9.7 h, then the temperature was dropped to 10 °C (TF) – as shown by the dashed line linked to the axis on the right. The integral of μ demonstrated that TF was lower than Ti,min, causing spore stress on μ to appear. For this case, k = 0.92, so μ' = 0.92μ(10 °C) = 11.59 h−1. The integral of 1/th showed that the intercept of the tangent on the temperature axis from Ti to TF was ∼1.22. This value was the order of n in equations (19), (21), which was used for calculating tn and ta,s. Hence, tn(10 °C) = 115.63 h (see Fig. 10 (b)) and ta,s = 2.19 h. Since the change in temperature for this case occurred after the spore swelling step (ts > t2), tr = 0 h. Since Tb,min < TF < Tb,max, ta,p = 0, so t'h = ts + ta,s = 11.89 h. The μ' and t'h were used in equation (10) to predict spore germination, after the temperature change. The prediction, shown in Fig. 11 and Table 5, demonstrated that spore germination followed our model more than Gougouli and Koutsoumanis's model. However, the metrics shown in Table 5, shows that the prediction by Gougouli and Koutsoumanis's model was still fitted as the effect of spore stress due to change in temperature for this case was not explicit (k was almost 1 and ta,s was insignificant compared to t'h).

Fig. 12 shows that the new hypothesis explained spore germination under the temperature change in the swelling step: it presents spore germination at 0 °C (Ti) for 24 h and 48 h, followed by a temperature increase to 25 °C (TF) – as shown by the dashed line linked to the axis on the right. In both cases, Ti,max was higher than the maximum germination temperature of this fungus, which implies that there was no effect of spore stress on μ, k = 1, when the temperature increased to 25 °C. Further support, that there was no effect of spore stress on μ, was no germ tubes appearing on all spores during the temperature change. As previously noted in Section 2.1, the value of μ was defined while the spore germ tubes were generated. For these reasons, in both cases, μ' = μ(25 °C) = 33.13 h−1. Moreover, similar phenomena also occurred in the integral of 1/th so the intercept of the tangent on the temperature axis from Ti to TF could not be found directly. To determine n in these cases, we use:(33)n=TF–TiTmax–Ti

From equation (33), n = 0.76. Thus, tn(25 °C) = 8.53 h (see Fig. 10 (b)) and ta,s = 0.86 h for both cases. As a result of the temperature change for both cases that occurred in the spore swelling step (ts < t2), tr must be calculated from equation (17). Leading to tr = 8.40 h for spore germination at 0 °C for 24 h and tr = 7.13 h for spore germination at 0 °C for 48 h. Corresponding values of t'h = 33.26 h (after 24 h of germination at 0 °C) and 55.99 h (after 48 h of germination at 0 °C). Predictions from equation (10), shown in Fig. 12 and Table 5, demonstrated that spore germination for both cases followed our model as well as Gougouli and Koutsoumanis's model because the effect of spore stress due to change in temperature for this case was not explicit.

To confirm that there was no effect of spore stress on μ, when the temperature was between Ti,min and Ti,max, cases where temperature changed to 25 °C (TF) after spore germination under 0 °C (Ti) for 163 h and 185.3 h were selected for prediction and are shown in Fig. 13. The patterns of the temperature change in Fig. 12, Fig. 13 were similar, leading to similar values of μ' and ta,s in both figures. However, in Fig. 13, the temperature was changed after the swelling step, in contrast in Fig. 12, the temperature was changed during swelling. Therefore, tr in Fig. 13 could be negligible. Hence, t'h = 164.90 h for germination for 163 h and t'h = 186.01 h for germination for 185.3 h. Predictions from equation (10), shown in Fig. 13 and Table 5, demonstrated that our model could predict the germination for both cases better than Gougouli and Koutsoumanis's model. Moreover, we concluded that the effect of spore stress on μ would not appear when Ti,min < TF < Ti,max. In both cases, the predictions by Gougouli and Koutsoumanis's model, when the temperature was 0 °C, were obviously lower than the experimental data due to the overestimation of λ calculated by the modified CMI model (see equation (24)).

In Fig. 11, Fig. 12, Fig. 13, the temperature was changed only once. However, this pattern was impossible to generate in the practical situations, the germination temperature could be affected by external factors affecting the ambient temperature. Therefore, to show that our model could predict these practical situations, spore germination under multiple temperature changes was predicted by our model. Fig. 14 shows the effect of fluctuating temperature conditions.

In Fig. 14, we illustrate a scenario in which the temperature was set at 25 °C (Ti) for 9 h, and then dropped to 10 °C (TF), for 3.5 h and then raised to 25 °C again – as shown by the dashed line linked to the axis on the right. When the temperature was changed to 10 °C, this was similar to the change in Fig. 11, so μ' = 11.59 h−1 and ta,s = 2.19 h. Since the temperature was changed at 9 h, which was higher than the spore swelling time (ts > t2), tr = 0 and then t'h = 11.19 h. The integral of μ, right panel of Fig. 14, showed that the increase in temperature from 10 °C to 25 °C did not stress the spores on μ. Therefore, μ' = μ(25 °C) = 33.12 h−1. From the integral of 1/th, we found that the intercept of the tangent on the temperature axis from TF to Ti was n2 ∼ 0.65. Hence, tn(25 °C) = 8.67 h (see Fig. 10 (b)), ta,s = 0.64 h and t'h = 13.14 h for the return to the original temperature. The μ' and t'h values for each period were used in equation (10) to predict spore germination. Fig. 14 demonstrates that spore germination was well described under multiple temperature changes, and thus, it was reasonable to conclude that our model could apply in real situations. Moreover, the metrics shown in Table 5, shows that the germination followed our model more than Gougouli and Koutsoumanis's model. However, Gougouli and Koutsoumanis's model was still acceptable as the effect of spore stress on μ for this case was not explicit.

The change in temperature, shown in Fig. 11, Fig. 12, Fig. 13, Fig. 14, were between Tb,min and Tb,max. However, temperatures outside the germination limits are usually used in food storage systems for extending the life of food. Therefore, it was necessary to verify, that our model could describe the behavior of spore germination, for temperatures outside of the germination limits, in several steps, shown in Fig. 15, Fig. 16, Fig. 17.

In Fig. 15, we show spores germinated at 25 °C (Ti) for 9.7 h, followed by a decrease to 0 °C (TF) – see the dashed line. Though the temperature, TF < Tb,min, the temperature was changed after th, at which, it was assumed that germ tubes appeared on all spores. Therefore, the fraction of spores showing germ tubes, f = 1 and ta,p = 0 h, see equations (14), (20). The integral of μ demonstrated that TF < Ti,min, then, k = 0.3301 and μ' = 0.3301μ(0 °C) = 0.58 h−1. Furthermore, the intercept of the tangent of the integral of 1/th on the temperature axis from Ti to TF (n) was ∼3.51. Thus, tn(0 °C) = 115.63 h (see Fig. 10 (a)), ta,s = 54.61 h and t'h = 64.31 h. The values of μ' and t'h were used in equation (10) for the prediction, shown in Fig. 15. Fig. 15 shows that the germination followed our model while Gougouli and Koutsoumanis's model was overprediction after the temperature changed to 0 °C. The overprediction was a result of regardless of the spore stress due to the change in temperature in their model.

Fig. 16 shows an immediate change in temperature from 25 °C (Ti) to 1 °C (TF), while all spores were swelling. Therefore, there was no effect of the fraction of spores showing germ tubes after the temperature change to TF (f = 1 and ta,p = 0 h). The integral of μ showed that TF was lower than Ti,min. However, the effect of spore stress on μ could be ignored (k = 1) because no germ tubes appeared on spores when temperature change. So, μ' = μ(1 °C) = 2.55 h−1. In addition, the intercept from the integral of 1/th showed on the temperature axis from Ti to TF (n) was ∼3.11. Thus, tn(1 °C) = 87.87 h (see Fig. 10 (a)), ta,s = 34.72 h. Since ts < t2, tr = 97.14 h, then t'h = 128.86 h. The values of μ' and t'h were used in equation (10) for the prediction. The prediction, shown in Fig. 16 and Table 5, demonstrated that spore germination was fitted to our model more than Gougouli and Koutsoumanis's model. The prediction curve by Gougouli and Koutsoumanis's model deviated earlier than the experimental data for 35 h, approximately, as a result of regardless of the spore stress due to the change in temperature in their model. Furthermore, the predictions, shown in Fig. 12, Fig. 16, confirmed that, if the temperature was changed during swelling, the effect of spore stress on μ could be ignored but the effect of spore stress on th (ta,s) remained.

Fig. 17 shows spore germination with multiple temperature changes. This was similar to Fig. 14, but the temperature was dropped to lower than Tb,min. Initially, the spores germinated at 25 °C (Ti) for 9 h, then temperature was decreased to 0 °C (TF) for 16.3 h and then increased to the initial temperature again. When temperature was changed to 0 °C, the time was between t2 and th. At that time, the fraction of germinated spores was ∼29.16 %. Since t2 < ts < th and TF < Tb,min, the effect of the fraction of spores showing germ tubes on the kinetics parameters was necessary must be considered. From equations (14), (20), f = 0.58 and ta,p = 38.03 h for the first change in temperature at 9 h. The integral of μ in Fig. 17 demonstrated that TF < Ti,min, causing spore stress on μ to appear. Then, k = 0.3301 and μ' = 0.71 × 0.3301 × μ(0 °C) = 0.34 h− 1. The integral of 1/th showed that n ≈ 3.51 for the first change, Ti→TF, thus tn(0 °C) = 115.63 h (see Fig. 10 (a)), ta,s = 54.61 h and t'h = 101.64 h. On the other hand, the effect of spore stress on μ would be negligible, when the temperature increased from 0 °C to 25 °C. Therefore, k = 1 and μ' = μ(25 °C) = 33.13 h−1. For the second change, TF→Ti, we had n ≈ 0.76. Hence, tn(25 °C) = 8.53 h (see Fig. 10 (b)), ta,s = 0.86 h and t'h = 26.16 h. The μ' and t'h for each time of the temperature change were used in equation (10) to predict spore germination. Fig. 17 shows that the prediction by Gougouli and Koutsoumanis's model was unable to precisely predict when the temperature was dropped to 0 °C as there were effects of spore stress and the fraction of spores showing germ tubes on the kinetic parameters, however, their model did not include these effects. On the other hand, their model could predict the germination as well as our model when the temperature returned to 25 °C again, as both effects disappeared.

Fig. 11, Fig. 12, Fig. 13, Fig. 14, Fig. 15, Fig. 16, Fig. 17 clearly show that our new hypothesis (equation (10) – (21)) could predict the germination of P. expansum spores under non-isothermal conditions well. We expected that our new hypothesis could be applied to describe the germination of other fungal species. In addition, we also conjectured that other environmental factors, for example, water activity and pH, could be adapted into equations (10)–(21) by replacing the temperature parameter with those environmental factors. This conjecture was necessary to be proved in future work. If it was true, it might conclude that the hypothesis, we developed, was a complete one in describing the germination of fungal spores under dynamic conditions. This would be beneficial for microbiologists in the study of fungal spore growth.

Conclusions

In this work, we developed new mathematical models for describing the germination of P. expansum spores under isothermal and non-isothermal conditions. The germination data was collected from literature. For under isothermal conditions, it showed that the germination of P. expansum spores followed the logistic model more than the Gompertz and Dantigny et al. models. Furthermore, based on the logistic model, we developed a new model for describing the relationship between the time that half the spores had germinated and the mean rate of spore germination. Constant in this model could be used as a representative for this fungus, as it was independent from environmental factors, for example, temperature, humidity, and ambient gases. To handle the germination under non-isothermal conditions, we developed new models, considering the effects of spore stress due to the magnitude of temperature change and the fraction of spores showing germs tubes during temperature change on the kinetic parameters. Overall, the predictions, confirmed that our new models could predict spore germination under non-isothermal conditions well.
