Ixekizumab-Associated Generalized Vitiligo and Pruritus in a Psoriatic Patient
Authors: Dayeon Jung, Dong Y. Oh, Eun J. Park, Kwang J. Kim, Kwang H. Kim
Published: 2024

Sir,

IL-17 is a key driver of psoriasis pathogenesis, and immune-targeting has transformed the treatment modality of severe plaque psoriasis. Injection site reactions and upper respiratory tract infection are the main adverse events of ixekizumab, an IL-17A humanized monoclonal antibody; however, vitiligo and pruritus have not been reported in trials.

A 70-year-old Korean woman with psoriasis vulgaris for 20 years presented with erythematous scaly plaques on the scalp, trunk and extremities (PASI 11.6, BSA 15). The patient had no comorbidities or family history of autoinflammatory disorders. She was on cyclosporine for total of five years and methotrexate for 18 months, which were discontinued due to loss of efficacy. Thus, treatment with guselkumab was initiated.

After 16 months of guselkumab, psoriasis improved (PASI 3, BSA 4), but clinical lesions remained on visible areas, decreasing quality of life. Therefore, guselkumab was replaced with ixekizumab. After three months, clinical lesions greatly improved (PASI 1.2, BSA 1) but she experienced generalized pruritus. After seven months, depigmented macules appeared on the upper extremities and additional lesions emerged on the forehead, abdomen, flank and lower extremities [Figure 1a, c, e] with positive wood lamp enhancement [Figure 1b, d, f], leading to the diagnosis of vitiligo. Laboratory test results for underlying causes were normal. After nine months, ixekizumab was halted due to patient refusal. Pruritus showed good response to antihistamines and mometasone. The patient switched to ustekinumab (PASI 1.2, BSA 1), and vitiligo was treated with phototherapy and topical tacrolimus 0.1% ointment. Vitiligo did not progress, and phototherapy was halted due to patient refusal.

(a, c, e) Clinical presentation of generalized vitiligo on the (a) lower extremity, (c) abdomen and (e) flank in a patient with psoriasis vulgaris seven months into ixekizumab treatment; (b, d, f) Positive wood lamp enhancement on corresponding lesions

Cytokine imbalance and alternative pathway activation such as type 1 interferon are the proposed hypotheses for paradoxical skin reaction of vitiligo. IFN-α, a type 1 interferon, plays a crucial role in vitiligo by inducing adaptive T cell responses through recruiting and activating CXCR3+ T helper (Th) 1 and cytotoxic T (Tc) 1 cells.[1] CXCR3, CXCL9 and CXCL10 expression are increased in perilesional skin of progressive vitiligo.[1]

Our patient experienced generalized pruritus after ixekizumab treatment. Although the mechanism remains unclear, pruritus may be caused by 1) skin barrier dysfunction and 2) reciprocally upregulated Th2-type immune responses upon inhibition of IL-17.[2] Despite its role in psoriasis, IL-17 is a key cytokine that enhances mucosal barrier function and defends against infection through antimicrobial peptide production and neutrophil recruitment.2 Studies have shown upregulated Th2 cell-associated proteins, such as IgE and eotaxin, after administration of IL-17A inhibitor.[2]

Previous cases of localized pruritus on the face,[3] localized vitiligo on the face,[4] and a case of generalized vitiligo after ixekizumab[3] have been reported; however to our knowledge, this is the first case of generalized vitiligo occurring with generalized pruritus within a single patient. We hypothesized there may be a shared mechanism, with upregulation of CXCL10/CXCR3 signalling holding the key. Murine model studies reported CXCL10/CXCR3 signalling upregulation may contribute to pruritus, while CXCR3 blockade alleviates chronic pruritus.[5] Thus, CXCL10/CXCR3 signalling upregulation may be the connection between the two phenomena. Although further studies are required, this nevertheless highlights the possibility of combined development of pruritus and vitiligo during ixekizumab treatment.
