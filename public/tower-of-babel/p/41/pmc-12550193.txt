Effect of flaxseed protein on pasting behavior, gelatinization properties and water distribution of mung bean starch
Authors: Cong Min, Haodong Yang, Xiaoli Zou, Furong Hu, Jingzhang Geng, Yinku Liang, Wen Su, Jianwei Dong, Ya Dang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe effects of flaxseed protein (FP) on the pasting behavior, gelatinization properties, and water distribution of mung bean starch (MBS) were investigated. Results showed that increasing FP content elevated the viscosity, breakdown value, and pasting temperature of MBS/FP mixtures, while the gelatinization enthalpy exhibited a decreasing trend, indicating that proteins influenced the gelatinization process of starch granules. Additionally, the addition of FP reduced gel strength by 45–74 % and significantly altered water distribution, notably decreasing the proportion of free water. Critical molecular forces governing the MBS/FP mixtures included disulfide bonds, electrostatic interactions, and hydrogen bonds. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis demonstrated that the homogeneous porous network structure of MBS gels was progressively disrupted and the microstructure loosened as the FP concentration increased. Therefore, FP serves as a significant modifier for MBS, effectively regulating its pasting, gelatinization, and gelation properties, which could be leveraged to tailor MBS-based food textures.Keywords: Binary mixture system, Macronutrients, Gel, Microstructure, Molecular forces

Introduction

Starches and proteins are fundamental biomolecules extensively utilized in the food industry and serve as cornerstone components of various food systems. These molecules engage in diverse interactions, both covalent and non-covalent, to form intricate binary and multicomponent structures, thereby improving the texture and sensory attributes of food products (Du, Lu, Zhang, Mata, & Fang, 2021; Hadde & Chen, 2021).

Starch is a typical type of polysaccharide which can serve as the gelling agent and texture modifier, influencing the structural and sensory attributes of food products (Jones & Mcclements, 2011). The gelatinization of starch is a fundamental physicochemical process governing food texture, critically influencing the water-holding capacity, mechanical strength, and freeze-thaw stability of products like flour-based goods, minced meat, and plant-based analogs (Zheng, Zhang, Ai, Cheng, & Yang, 2024). Consequently, its mechanism remains a central research focus in food colloid science. Recently, driven by the clean label trend, MBS has gained prominence as an ideal plant-based alternative to traditional cereal starches due to its hypoallergenicity, high amylose content, and slow digestibility (Ge et al., 2025). MBS possesses distinctive gel, gelatinization, thermal, and digestion properties due to its unique composition, offering enhanced thermal and freeze-thaw stability, particularly advantageous for frozen food processing (Choi & Oh, 2013). Moreover, MBS is valued in food formulation for its functional gelling properties. It acts as an effective thickener or gelling agent, enhancing the overall texture and sensory qualities of food systems through its interactions with proteins, lipids, and other macromolecules (Li, Ward, & Gao, 2011). These attributes make MBS a promising ingredient for low-glycemic index (GI) and vegan food products (Yao et al., 2019). However, its application in high-quality foods is limited by a critical weakness: during heat-induced gelatinization, MBS is highly vulnerable to water migration, molecular chain rearrangement, and phase separation. These processes often result in a weak, loose gel network, undermining its functional performance.

To address this limitation, the synergistic effects of other macronutrient components on starch have been extensively explored to improve the functionality of starch gel. Among them, proteins can not only serve as nutritional fortification components, but their unique structural flexibility and interfacial activity may more efficiently regulate the formation of the gel network through competitive hydration, interaction, and other pathways (He et al., 2024). FP stands out as a high-quality plant protein, which constitutes approximately 20–25 % of flaxseed—a proportion that provides a favorable basis for its potential large-scale applications in the food industry (Kajla, Sharma, & Sood, 2014). FP is prized for its exceptional amino acid composition, which contributes to its high nutritional value and a range of demonstrated health benefits. These include antioxidant, hypoglycemic, cholesterol-lowering, antihypertensive, antibacterial, and immunosuppressive properties (Hwang, Chen, Luo, & Chiang, 2016). FP has received much attention in recent years due to the breakthroughs in the improvement of its functional properties (Yang et al., 2026; Ye, Gan, Wang, Yang, & Dong, 2025). Unlike soybean or pea protein, FP possesses a wider distribution of hydrophobic regions and a high content of sulfur-containing amino acids (e.g., cysteine). These features enable it to cross-link through hydrophobic interactions and disulfide bonds during heat treatment, thereby synergistically stabilizing the starch-protein composite system (Sharma & Saini, 2022). We hypothesize that these characteristics enable FP to not only compete for water binding but also to form extensive hydrophobic associations and, crucially, heat-induced disulfide bonds. This combination of interactions is anticipated to effectively reinforce the fragile MBS gel network, offering a novel strategy for texture modification. Despite its potential, the multi-scale mechanism by which FP modulates the MBS gelation process remains unclear. Key questions include how FP dynamically remodels water distribution and how it balances hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and disulfide bonding to optimize the gel's microstructure.

The present study aims to investigate the effects of FP on the pasting behavior, gelatinization properties and water distribution of MBS. It was hypothesized that FP modifies the MBS gel matrix through a combination of competitive hydration and synergistic molecular interactions, which collectively remodel water distribution and gel microstructure. The influence of FP was investigated at specific addition ratios—a range selected to probe the concentration-dependent effects on the composite system. By utilizing methods such as rapid viscosity analyzer (RVA) testing, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR), and SEM, we conducted an in-depth analysis of changes in gel color, thermal properties, intermolecular forces, and microstructure to reveal the regulatory pathways of FP on the evolution of water phases, the contribution of molecular forces, and the reconstruction of mesoscopic structures during the gelation of MBS. These findings are of significant importance for the development of novel gel-based foods with varying textures and gel strengths, catering to the diverse nutritional and sensory preferences of consumers.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemical reagents

MBS was purchased from Hengshui Fuqiao Starch Co., Ltd. (Jingxian, Hebei, China). The flour had a moisture content of 14 % and an amylose content of 59 %. FP (containing at least 81 % protein, 3.62 % water, and 5.24 % lipids) was obtained from Shaanxi Kangduopu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xi'an, Shaanxi, China). All other chemicals used in the study were of analytical grade.

Preparation of the samples

The mixture samples were prepared according to the method used by (Min, Ma, Kuang, Huang, & Xiong, 2022). Briefly, MBS and FP powder were sieved through a 100 mesh sieve. For the starch gel, 8.0 g of MBS was dispersed in 100 mL of deionized water in a 250 mL beaker, stirred with a magnetic stirrer at room temperature (22 ± 1 °C) and a speed of 300 rpm for 1 min, and then heated at 85 °C for 10 min. FP was hydrated in deionized water, stirred with a magnetic stirrer for 2 h at room temperature to form a 30 % (w/v) FP suspension. To create the protein-starch mixtures, an appropriate amount of the FP stock solution was mixed into the predetermined MBS solution. The final concentrations in a 250 mL beaker were 8 % (w/v) for MBS and 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 % (w/v) for FP. The mixture was stirred with a magnetic stirrer at room temperature for 20 min and then heated at 95 °C for 30 min. The gels were formed by cooling the heated paste overnight.

Color

The color of the gel samples was measured using a colorimeter (Konica Minolta CM-5, Konica Minolta Inc., Tokyo, Japan). The L* (brightness), a* (red/green), and b* (yellow/blue) values were directly obtained for each sample with different FP concentrations. The chroma (C*, representing color saturation) and hue angle (h°, representing color tone) were calculated based on a* and b* values.

Pasting profile

The pasting properties of the MBS/FP mixtures were evaluated using a rapid viscosity analyzer (RVA, Perten Instruments, Warriewood, Australia). Stir the suspension of MBS and FP magnetically at room temperature for 20 min, and then perform RVA analysis. The RVA testing protocol was set as follows: pre-shearing at 50 °C for 1 min, followed by heating at a rate of 12.16 °C/min until reaching 95 °C, maintaining at 95 °C for 2.5 min, cooling to 50 °C at a rate of 11.84 °C/min, and holding at 50 °C for 2 min. The shear rate was 160 rpm.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The gel samples were frozen at −80 °C overnight and subsequently freeze-dried for 48 h. The freeze-dried samples were ground into powder, and 3.0 mg of the powder sample was sealed in an aluminum crucible, and analyzed using a differential scanning calorimeter (Q2000, TA Instrument, New Castle, DE). The instrument parameters were set with a heating rate of 10.0 °C/min and a temperature range of 30–200 °C. The DSC analysis included determining the onset temperature (To), peak temperature (Tp), endset temperature (Te), and enthalpy change (ΔH).

Determination of gel strength

The gel strength was evaluated by using a texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems Ltd., Godalming, UK). Briefly, the cylindrical gel sample (20 mm in diameter and 15 mm in height) was equilibrated for 30 min, then was tested by the texture analyzer via a probe (P/0.5). The parameters were set as follows: pre-test/post-test speed 5.0 mm/s, in-test speed 1.0 mm/s, sample deformation 8.0 mm. The gel strength was defined as the initial pressure when the probe penetrates through the gel.

Low field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR)

The water distributions (T2 relaxation time) in the MBS/FP mixtures were determined using the LF-NMR according to the method reported earlier (Min et al., 2024). Aliquots of 1.0 g of different gel samples were placed in the LF-NMR detection tube, and analyzed by the Carr Purcell Meiboom Gill (CPMG) pulse sequence mode to measure the T2 relaxation time distribution of each sample. The parameters were set as follows: spectral width (SW) 200 kHz, echo count (EC) 15,000, number of scanning cycles (NS) 4, sampling repetition time (TW) 6000 ms, magnetic field intensity 0.5 T.

Molecular forces

The molecular forces within the gels were assessed by analyzing the solubility via using different reagents according to the method described by (Tiong et al., 2024). Briefly, five disrupting solvents were prepared. Solvent R1 was distilled water; solvent R2 was R1 + 0.09 mol/L glycine+0.086 mol/L Tris+0.4 mmol/L Na2EDTA (pH = 8); solvent R3 was R2 + 2 % sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS); solvent R4 was R3 + 8 mmol/L urea; solvent R5 was R4 + 1 % 2-mercaptoethanol. After lyophilization, 2 g of gel samples were dispersed in 30 mL of the above solvents using a turbine mixer XH-D (Jiangsu Tianling Instrument Co. Ltd., Yancheng, China) for 1 min. The solutions were centrifuged at 7100 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration of the supernatant was measured using the biuret method. Protein solubility was expressed as the percentage of the protein content in supernatant relative to the total protein content.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The microstructural characteristics of the samples were investigated using a FEI Q45 scanning electron microscope (SEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Oregon, USA) at an accelerating voltage of 2 kV. Freeze-dried gel samples were manually fractured to expose cross-sectional surfaces, adhered to the specimen holder with conductive adhesive tape, and sputter-coated with gold prior to observation. High-resolution secondary electron imaging was performed under high vacuum conditions (≤5 × 10−3 Pa), with representative micrographs captured at magnification 500× to document structural features across multiple length scales.

Data analysis

The experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the results were presented as mean values with standard deviations. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan's test through SPSS 22.0 software (IBM software, NY, USA). A significance level of P < 0.05 was used to determine statistical differences.

Results and discussion

Color

This study investigates the impact of varying concentrations of FP on the appearance characteristics of MBS. Upon incorporation of FP, the heat-induced MBS gels exhibited opaque and smooth visual characteristics similar to those of MBS-only gels. However, a distinct chromatic transition from bright white to yellowish tones was observed, with the coloration progressively intensifying with increasing FP concentrations, as indicated in Fig. S1. Color parameters, including brightness (L*), redness/greenness (a*), and yellowness/blueness (b*), are crucial sensory indicators for gel-based food products. As summarized in Table 1, the brightness (L*) of the gels showed no significant difference at lower FP concentrations compared to the control (8 % MBS alone). However, a significant increase in the difference was observed at the 4 % protein concentration level. This effect can be partly attributed to the impact of high protein concentration on the gel network structure, which modifies its light scattering and reflection properties (Peng et al., 2019). The redness value (a*) shifted significantly from negative values (indicating greenness) at ≤3 % FP to a positive value (0.35 ± 0.08) at 4 % FP, indicating a transition toward red (Hou et al., 2022). Similarly, the yellowness value (b*) changed from negative (blueness) at ≤2 % FP to strongly positive values at higher concentrations, confirming a pronounced yellow color development. To further interpret these shifts, chroma (C*, saturation) and hue angle (h°) were calculated. Chroma increased markedly with FP addition, indicating enhanced color intensity. Correspondingly, the hue angle decreased systematically from ∼190° (bluish-green) to ∼88° (yellow), confirming a definitive shift in hue toward yellow with significant differences between all FP concentrations. Overall, increasing FP content resulted in a gradual color shift toward red and yellow hues, driven by the intrinsic pigmentation of FP. Similar color alterations have been reported in other mixed biopolymer systems. For instance, Chen, Fang, Federici, Campanella, and Jones (2020) observed that whey protein incorporation reduced the transparency of potato starch gels and induced browning, largely due to protein thermal aggregation during gelation. The changes observed in this study align with such phenomena, underscoring the role of protein-induced structural and chromatic modifications in composite gels.

Pasting properties

The pasting properties of the MBS/FP mixtures, detailed in Fig. 1 and Table 2, demonstrate that the inclusion of FP changed key parameters compared to the 8 % MBS control. These changes were observed in the pasting temperature, peak, trough, and final viscosity. Specifically, no significant difference in pasting temperature was observed when 1 % FP was added to the mixture. However, a significant difference was noted when the FP content exceeded 2 %. The peak and trough viscosities of the 4 % FP mixture increased approximately 1.8-fold and 1.5-fold, respectively, compared to MBS alone. Moreover, the final viscosity of the mixtures exhibited a significant increase with higher FP content, with a 2.5-fold increase observed at 4 % FP content. This phenomenon may be attributed to FP/MBS interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonding or hydrophobic associations) that sterically hinder water accessibility to starch molecules, thereby delaying the onset of granular disruption (Chen, Zhang, Li, Xie, & Chen, 2018). Protein molecules can influence the swelling of starch particles and the release of amylose, leading to an increase in viscosity, thus affecting the stability of the mixtures under high temperature and shear (Schober, Bean, & Boyle, 2007). Furthermore, the pasting process of starch was also affected by the reduced water availability resulting from competitive water absorption by protein molecules, as reported by Yu, Ren, Zhao, Cui, and Liu (2020). These findings indicate that incorporating FP alters the pasting behavior of MBS, thereby changing the properties of the starch-based gel mixtures.

The increase in breakdown value (from 433 cP to 1047 cP; Table 2) with higher FP concentration indicates that the gel's structural complexity made it less stable under continuous shear during heating. A potential mechanism is that the heterogeneous network formed by FP and MBS generated stress concentration points under shear stress, accelerating localized fracture (Chen et al., 2020). A 3.53-fold increase in the setback value (from 420 cP to 1903 cP) highlights FP's role in promoting gel network reconstruction during cooling. This is likely due to FP accelerating the rearrangement of starch molecules and facilitating the formation of linear retrograded starch (Zhang et al., 2021).

The RVA data support a “two-stage interaction model” for the FP-MBS gels. In the heating stage, FP formed a heterogeneous network with starch via hydrophobic and hydrogen bonds, which concurrently inhibited granular swelling (evidenced by increased trough viscosity) and enhanced shear sensitivity (leading to a high breakdown value). In the subsequent cooling stage, the nucleation effect of FP synergized with starch retrogradation to facilitate a dense dual-network structure, resulting in a significant rise in the final viscosity and setback value. During this process, the β-sheet structures of FP may act as templates guiding the ordered arrangement of amylose chains. Taken together, FP significantly regulates the pasting and retrogradation behavior of MBS.

Thermal characteristics

The thermal stability play crucial roles in determining the processing quality of gel-based food products subjected to heat treatment (Jiang, Xiong, & Chen, 2010). The energy alteration associated with starch gelatinization is manifested as an endothermic peak on the DSC analysis graph. The magnitude of the endothermic peak on the DSC curve, known as thermal transition enthalpy, serves as a metric for assessing the extent of starch gelatinization (Chen et al., 2020). To further investigate the impact of protein content on the thermal characteristics of protein-starch mixture systems, DSC analysis was conducted on MBS/FP mixtures with varying FP content. The DSC curves of different mixtures exhibited significant distinctions, as depicted in Fig. 2. Notably, when the FP content exceeded 3 %, the To, Tp, and Te of the mixtures exhibited varying degrees of increase compared to the alone MBS (Table 3). In particular, at 4 % FP content, the characteristic values of the mixtures notably escalated, with the To value increasing by 12.86 °C, the Tp value by 15.96 °C, and the Te value by 11.5 °C in comparison to the control. Notably, the gelatinization temperature of the mixture determined by DSC was higher than that measured by RVA, indicating that when starch granules begin to swell in the mixture, the crystalline regions of MBS have not yet started to melt. Additionally, the enthalpy (ΔH) of the mixtures decreased with varying protein content. For FP content exceeding 4 %, the ΔH value decreased by 8.47 J/g (Table 3). Wu et al. (2023) observed similar phenomena in the study on rice starch and rice protein mixtures. The alterations in the thermal properties of the starch-protein mixed system are associated with structural modifications of starch granules induced by protein molecules under thermal treatment. As shown in the DSC curves (Fig. 2), the endothermic peak shifted toward higher temperatures with a reduced peak area, indicating that FP may destabilize the native crystalline regions of starch to form amorphous complexes while inducing partial pregelatinization of starch granules. This structural reorganization reduces the energy requirement for subsequent heating processes, thereby influencing the crystalline architecture of starch (Chen et al., 2018). On the other hand, the differential thermal properties observed among the mixtures can be further attributed to how protein-starch intermolecular interactions during gelation impact water availability (Ren & Wang, 2019). Protein molecules, through their hydrophilic amino acids, absorb water and consequently reduce the amount available for the swelling and gelatinization of starch granules (Chen et al., 2020). The observed increase in gelatinization temperature alongside a decrease in enthalpy indicates that FP delays gelatinization onset via competitive hydration and a barrier effect, while simultaneously compromising the crystalline integrity of starch, thereby lowering the energy required to disrupt the remaining double-helical structures (Luo et al., 2025).

Gel strength

Gel strength serves as a key indicator of the structural stability of food gels. The gel puncture test was commonly employed to assess variations in gel strength among MBS and MBS/FP mixture samples. In the present study, the strength value of the MBS/FP mixture gel differed significantly from that of the control gel (8 % MBS), as illustrated in Fig. 3A. Specifically, the strength value of the pure MBS gel was approximately 2.08 N. In contrast, the strength value of the MBS/FP mixture gels exhibited a notable decrease (P < 0.05) compared to the pure MBS gel, with a gradual decline observed with increasing FP concentration. Notably, when the FP content reached 4 %, the gel strength decreased to 0.54 N, representing approximately 26.0 % of the control gel strength. This finding is consistent with reports that the addition of rice protein decreased the hardness of rice starch gels (Wu et al., 2023). The possible explanation for this phenomenon might be due to the alterations in the network structure of the mixture gels (Bai et al., 2024). The protein molecules, enriched with hydrophilic groups, may preferentially adsorb free water in the system, resulting in insufficient water availability for starch granules during gelatinization (Niu, Zhang, Xia, Liu, & Kong, 2018). This hinders the full swelling and ordered cross-linking of starch chains, thereby compromising the strength of the gel network, as discussed earlier. Furthermore, high protein concentrations may exert steric hindrance by occupying binding sites between starch molecules, thereby disrupting amylose chain reorientation and amylopectin crystallization, which compromises the gel's structural compactness (Min et al., 2022). These findings further support the formation of a mixture gel system between FP and MBS. However, compared to pure MBS gels, the MBS/FP mixture gels exhibited significantly reduced network structural stability and gel strength. This diminished mechanical performance may facilitate their potential application in soft gel-based food products, where lower rigidity and enhanced deformability are desirable functional attributes (Nishinari, Ishihara, Hori, & Fang, 2020).

NMR relaxation analysis

The low field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) was utilized to examine the T2 relaxation time distribution of MBS and MBS/FP mixtures, further to identify the effect of FP on the internal water distribution within the MBS. The results revealed that the addition of FP significantly changed the water distribution state of the MBS gel system, as shown in Fig. 3B. Four distinct peaks with varying intensities were observed across all tested samples, denoted as T2b (0.02–0.8 ms), T21 (3–24 ms), T22 (36–270 ms), and T23 (330–2171 ms). The T2 relaxation time values (T2b–T23) corresponding to these peaks in samples along with their proportions (M2b–M23) are detailed in Table 4. Notably, T2b reflects the bound water fraction tightly associated with starch, protein, and other macromolecular constituents within the mixture gel network (Yu et al., 2020). In the present study, although the relaxation time of T2b shortened from 0.34 ms to 0.19 ms, the M2b decreased from 97.7 to 72.8, indicating that the binding strength of bound water increased with the increase in FP concentration while its proportion significantly decreases. Furthermore, the water fluidity linked to T21 and T22 in Fig. 3B were relatively limited, collectively termed as the immobilized water (Wang, Wang, Liu, & Liu, 2021). T21 represents a small portion of water weakly bound to polymer molecules in the gel system (Min et al., 2024). The relaxation time of T21 increased from 5.17 ms to 14.7 ms, while the M21 rose from 26.2 to 42.2, indicating that the proportion of weakly bound water increased but its binding strength decreased. This may reflect that the local hydration layers formed by FP and MBS have lower stability, leading to water being more easily detached from the gel network. On the other hand, high concentrations of FP (4 %) significantly increased the relaxation time of T22 from 62.9 ms to 117 ms, but the M22 decreased from 525 to 417, suggesting that the heterogeneity of the gel network increased and water distribution became looser. This change resulted in a weakened water-holding capacity (Wu et al., 2022). T23 corresponds to freely mobile water within the gel network (Mitchell, Gladden, Chandrasekera, & Fordham, 2014). The substantial reduction in free water, evidenced by a sharp decrease in T₂₃ relaxation time (from 1534 to 580 ms) and M₂₃ value (from 134 to 34.7) as the FP concentration increased to 4 % was partially caused by competitive water absorption between starch and protein. This competition reduced the system's overall water retention capacity (Niu et al., 2018). During gel formation, the interaction between FP and water hindered the full water absorption of MBS. This impediment restricted the swelling of starch granules and the release of amylose, ultimately reducing the number of hydrogen bonds in the mixture. Consequently, the gel network's capacity to retain free water was diminished. Additionally, the competitive binding between FP and starch may disrupt the regular arrangement of starch chains, form unstable complexes, and thereby reduce the network's water retention capacity (Wang, Li, Guo, Zhu, & Wu, 2024).

Molecular forces

The protein solubility was measured to clarify the potential molecular forces involved in the MBS/FP mixtures. The mixtures were dissolved in five different solvents: water only as control (R1), Tris-glycine buffer (R2), and Tris-glycine buffer with SDS (R3), urea (R4), or 2-mercaptoethanol (R5), which disrupt the electrostatic interactions, hydrophobic amino acids, hydrogen bond, and disulfide bonds, respectively (Chu, Yang, Li, Lin, & Zheng, 2019). The difference of protein solubility between solvents R2 and R1 represents the electrostatic interactions; the difference between R3 and R2 represents the hydrophobic interaction; the difference between R4 and R3 represents the hydrogen bond interaction; and the difference between R5 and R4 represents the disulfide bonds interaction (Tang, Wu, Chen, & Yang, 2006). As shown in Fig. 4, the intensity of the molecular interactions in the mixtures with a low concentration of FP (1 %) followed the order of disulfide bonds > electrostatic interactions > hydrogen bond > hydrophobic interaction. The disulfide bonds, electrostatic interactions, and hydrogen bond significantly decreased with the increasing FP content, while there was no significant change observed in the hydrophobic interaction. These results indicated that disulfide bonds, electrostatic interactions, and hydrogen bond were important molecular forces in the MBS/FP mixtures, especially at low concentration of FP. Although disulfide bonds are not a direct force between starch and protein, they obviously contributed to FP aggregation within the mixture gels. This was consistent with reports on soy isolate protein-wheat gluten gels, in which the molecular interactions were mainly electrostatic interactions and disulfide bonds (Qin et al., 2017).

For MBS-based gels, the amylose released from MBS after heating and cooling becomes entangled and rapidly forms a three-dimensional network under the action of molecular forces such as hydrogen bonds, i.e., the short-term retrogradation of amyloses. For the mixture gels, the pH values of gels with FP concentrations of 1–4 % FP were 4.1, 4.4, 4.7 and 4.9, respectively. The isoelectric point pI of FP is approximately 5.0 (Karaca, Low, & Nickerson, 2011). When the pH is lower than the isoelectric point, protein molecules acquire a net positive charge, facilitating electrostatic interactions. As the pH of the gels rose, the electrostatic interactions were gradually weakened. When the FP concentration is low, the mixture gel formed is mainly MBS gel. There are abundant hydrogen bonds formed between starch molecules and dispersed protein granules (Fig. 4). With the increase of FP concentrations, more protein granules were dispersed in the starch network in the form of aggregates, as discussed in our previous report (Min et al., 2024). The aggregation of protein granules could limit the exposure of protein side chains. This, in turn, disrupts the hydrogen bonding between starch hydroxyl groups and the amino or carboxyl groups on the protein residues, ultimately reducing hydrogen bond interactions (Jones and Mcclements, 2011). Previous reports have suggested that protein denaturation resulting from preheat treatment can induce the exposure of sulfhydryl groups and facilitate the formation of disulfide bonds (Ren et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2022). In mixture gels with low concentrations of FP, the proteins were dispersed in the network as small particles with large surface areas. The exposed sulfhydryl groups are more prone to forming disulfide bonds (He et al., 2024). Increasing the FP content promotes protein aggregation into larger particles. This reduces the available surface area, which hinders sulfhydryl group interaction and disulfide bonds formation. Additionally, this phenomenon can be attributed to the incomplete denaturation of FP that occurs prior to gel formation following heat treatment below the denaturation temperature (Oomah, Der, & Godfrey, 2006). The hydrophobic interactions remained a minor and stable component of the molecular force profile in the MBS/FP mixture gel network. This phenomenon may be attributed to the partial exposure of hydrophobic groups from incomplete denaturation, as well as a change in hydrophilicity induced by hydrogen bonding between starch hydroxyl groups and protein carboxyl or amino groups (Chu et al., 2019; Ji, Xiong, & Jiang, 2024). Notably, the alterations in the molecular interactions seem to be consistent with the reduced gel hardness and the weakened water retaining capacity of the mixtures, as shown in Fig. 3A and B, contributing to the specific gelling properties in the MBS/FP mixtures.

Microstructure

The microstructure of the MBS and MBS/FP mixture samples are shown in Fig. 5. SEM analysis revealed that the addition of FP significantly altered the microscopic morphology of the MBS gels. The pure MBS gel exhibited a uniform porous network structure with a relatively regular pore size distribution, indicating that the starch formed a stable three-dimensional skeleton through hydrogen bonding and amylose recrystallization (Wu et al., 2023). The addition of 1 % FP resulted in a localized protein layer on the gel surface and some pore filling, yet the structural network remained continuous. However, at 2 % FP, the system formed composite protein-starch aggregates, leading to a decrease in pore size and a more heterogeneous distribution. The initial structural changes were primarily attributed to protein binding with starch chains, which inhibited starch molecular rearrangement and disrupted the native network order (Niu, Wu, & Xiao, 2017). As the FP concentration increased further, a significant deterioration of the gel network was observed. At 3 % FP, a discontinuous interface formed between the protein aggregates and the starch matrix. This progressed to a broken lamellar structure with nearly vanished pores at the 4 % FP concentration. This pronounced breakdown is likely due to excessive proteins introducing steric hindrance that completely disrupted starch chain cross-linking, thereby loosening the microstructure (Scott & Awika, 2023). These microstructural alterations were highly consistent with the macroscopic data. At lower FP concentrations (1–2 %), the protein acted as a partial pore-filling agent, forming complexes that may have enhanced local cross-linking via hydrogen bonds. However, the overall effect of this network densification was a decrease in gel strength and a weakened water-holding capacity, as evidenced by Fig. 3A and B. At a high FP concentration (≥3 %), the phase separation and structural fracture directly accounted for the sharp deterioration of the gel strength and water-holding capacity.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the incorporation of FP into MBS modulates their physicochemical, structural, and functional properties in a concentration-dependent manner. At low FP concentrations (1–2 %), molecular interactions (primarily disulfide bonds, electrostatic interactions, and hydrogen bonds) facilitated a heterogeneous network, enhancing viscosity and retrogradation stability. At higher concentrations (≥3 %), competitive water absorption, steric hindrance effects, and protein aggregation became dominant, leading to a concurrent attenuation of major molecular forces and ultimately causing structural degradation, reduced gel strength, and phase separation. These findings underscore the dual role of FP: it improves retrogradation resistance and viscosity at low concentrations but impairs structural integrity at elevated concentrations. To optimize the food application performance of MBS/FP gels, it is essential to control protein incorporation levels and explore protein modification strategies to enhance interfacial compatibility. A limitation of the present study is that the interactions were investigated only in a model system, without the complexity of real food matrices. In summary, FP provides a natural and effective means to tailor the functional properties of MBS, thereby paving the way for the development of novel foods in the realms of plant-based alternatives, reduced-fat formulations, and specialized nutrition.
