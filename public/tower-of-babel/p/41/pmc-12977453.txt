Prediction for occurrence of subdural hematoma following spontaneous intracranial hypotension: a large cohort of 575 adults in a single center from 2023 to 2024
Authors: Kun Wang, Keng Chen, Zundao Shan, Yinxin Zhu, Bo Li, Jingyang Hong, Xinwei Li, Feifang He, Yirong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH) is a disabling neurological disorder. Currently, no validated prediction model is available, limiting effective risk stratification and early intervention. The purpose of our study is to analyze the risk factors for subdural hematoma (SDH) in patients with SIH and to construct a clinical prediction model for early identification and clinical decision-making.MethodsA total of 575 SIH patients hospitalized at Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University, from January 1, 2023 to December 30, 2024, were included. Patients were divided into SDH and non-SDH (NSDH) groups based on the presence of subdural hematoma. Demographic, clinical, and imaging data were collected and analyzed. 80% of the cohort was randomly selected as the training set for model development, and the remaining 20% was used as the test set. In the training set, univariate logistic regression was performed to identify potential factors associated with SDH occurrence, followed by Lasso regression for variable selection. Selected variables were incorporated into a multivariate logistic regression model using stepwise regression. Model validation was conducted using 500 bootstrap resampling iterations. Model performance was assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for discrimination, calibration curves for calibration, and decision curve analysis (DCA) for clinical utility.ResultsAmong 575 patients, 101 (17.6%) developed SDH (14 patients with unilateral SDH and 87 patients with bilateral SDH). Multivariate analysis identified history of prior treatment for intracranial hypotension, pre-admission subdural effusion and presence of dural enhancement as predictors of SDH occurrence, and these variables were also independent risk factors (P < 0.05). The model demonstrated strong discrimination, with AUCs of 0.839 (training set), 0.837 (test set), and 0.790 (overall). Sensitivity and specificity were 0.438/0.965 (training), 0.421/0.965 (test), and 0.434/0.963 (overall). Calibration curves showed minimal deviation (mean absolute error < 0.05), and DCA indicated favorable clinical utility when the high-risk threshold was below 0.73.ConclusionHistory of prior treatment for intracranial hypotension, pre-admission subdural effusion and presence of dural enhancement are independent risk factors for SDH in SIH patients. The prediction model shows strong discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility, supporting early risk assessment and personalized clinical management.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH) is a disabling neurological disorder characterized by orthostatic headaches and a broad range of associated symptoms [1]. Global epidemiological data remain limited, but reported incidence rates range from 3.7 to 3.8 per 100,000 adults per year [2–3]. Because of its nonspecific clinical presentation, SIH is frequently underdiagnosed, suggesting that its true prevalence may be higher. SIH most often affects adults aged 35–55 years, with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 2:1 [4–5].

The understanding of SIH pathophysiology has expanded beyond traditional cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pressure criteria (< 60 mmH2O) [6–7]. It is now recognized that SIH may occur even with normal or increased CSF pressure [5, 8], and CSF pressure may normalize over time [9]. Thus, SIH is better understood as a condition caused by reduced CSF volume rather than low pressure alone [4, 10]. The underlying mechanism involves CSF volume depletion due to spinal CSF leaks, including dural tears, meningeal diverticula, or CSF-venous fistulas [11]. CSF loss leads to brain sagging, traction on pain-sensitive structures, venous congestion, and a spectrum of neurological symptoms [12]. Meta-analyses indicate that 97% of SIH patients experience headache (92% orthostatic), with common associated symptoms such as nausea, neck stiffness, and vestibulocochlear disturbances [4, 13]. Symptoms typically worsen when standing and improve when lying down, severely impacting daily function. In severe cases, life-threatening complications may develop, including subdural hematoma (SDH), cerebral venous thrombosis, and altered consciousness [14]. These complications may occur days or weeks after headache onset, may overshadow the headache, or may even appear after the headache has resolved [4].

SDH is a major complication of SIH, occurring in approximately 16.5–32.5% of patients [15–16]. The primary mechanism is thought to involve rupture of bridging veins caused by excessive traction from brain descent. Small SDHs may resolve after epidural blood patch (EBP) [1], whereas large or symptomatic hematomas often require surgery [15–16]. Surgery without adequate treatment of the CSF leak may result in recurrence or clinical deterioration [15–16], emphasizing the need for early detection and comprehensive management.

Several risk factors for SDH in SIH patients have been proposed, including age, male sex, prolonged symptoms, dural enhancement, venous sinus distension, and antithrombotic use [17]. However, existing findings are inconsistent, likely due to differences in inclusion criteria, sample sizes, and statistical approaches. No validated prediction model is currently available, limiting effective risk stratification and early intervention.

Therefore, this study aims to develop a clinical prediction model for SDH in SIH patients, using the largest single-center cohort to date. By systematically assessing demographic, clinical, and imaging data, we seek to identify independent risk factors for SDH occurrence and establish a prediction model to support early risk assessment and personalized management.

Methods

Participants

This study enrolled a total of 575 patients diagnosed with SIH who were admitted to the Departments of Neurosurgery and Pain Management at Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, from January 1, 2023 to December 30, 2024. Demographic, clinical, and radiological data were collected.

Diagnostic criteria

The 2021 SIH diagnostic criteria modified by Schievink [4], based on the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3), were used: (A) Any headache fulfilling criterion C, caused by low CSF pressure or CSF leakage. (B) At least one of the following: low CSF pressure (< 60 mmH2O); imaging evidence of CSF leakage. (C) The headache has a temporal relationship with low CSF pressure or CSF leakage, or leads to its discovery. (D) The headache is not better explained by another ICHD-3 diagnosis. (Note: The modified criteria also apply to patients without headache whose symptoms are better explained by SIH. Patients with known procedures or trauma capable of causing CSF leakage within one month cannot be diagnosed with SIH.)

The clinical diagnosis and management of all patients were conducted in accordance with the Chinese Expert Consensus on Diagnosis and Treatment of Spontaneous Intracranial Hypotension (2024) (Table 1) [18].

Inclusion criteria

① Patients with complete baseline data, comprehensive medical history, and complete auxiliary examinations. Patients and their family members fully understood the condition and treatment plan, provided written informed consent, and agreed to data use for research.

② SIH diagnosis established according to the 2021 Schievink modified criteria and the Chinese Expert Consensus on Diagnosis and Treatment of Spontaneous Intracranial Hypotension (2024) [18].

Exclusion criteria

① History of trauma capable of causing CSF leakage within one month before symptom onset.

② Patients with congenital or acquired coagulation disorders were excluded.

③ Patients with recent spinal surgery, or other iatrogenic causes of intracranial hypotension (e.g., procedures performed in the days immediately preceding symptom onset) were excluded.

④ Incomplete baseline data or missing medical history or auxiliary examinations.

Clinical data collection and definitions

(1) Data on patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and imaging findings were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical record system and radiology information system. Demographic data included sex, age, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI). Clinical data included clinical manifestation (orthostatic headache, neck pain or stiffness, disturbance of consciousness, tinnitus, hearing loss, dizziness), comorbidities, recurrent SIH, prior spinal surgery, history of spinal trauma, Traditional Chinese medicine physiotherapy, history of previous treatments for intracranial hypotension, use of anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy, lumbar puncture opening pressure, time from symptom onset to admission and duration of hospitalization. Imaging data included pre-admission subdural effusion, presence of subdural hematoma (SDH), SDH thickness and location, brain sagging, pituitary height, dural enhancement, venous sinus distension, presence and location of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, and spine imaging characteristics.

(2) Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²). Comorbidities included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, connective tissue disease, viral hepatitis and migraine. Traditional Chinese medicine physiotherapy included spinal manipulation, massage, acupuncture, or small needle-knife therapy performed more than one month prior to hospitalization. Previous treatments for initial and recurrent intracranial hypotension were defined as conservative management (bed rest, intravenous hydration, other non-invasive measures), CSF leak repair surgery and EBP received prior to the current admission. Subdural hematoma (SDH) was identified based on the synthesis of findings from both head computed tomography (CT) and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which were performed for all patients. Brain sagging was assessed by measuring the position of the cerebellar tonsils relative to the foramen magnum on sagittal T1-weighted contrast-enhanced brain MRI. A negative value indicated that the tonsils were above the foramen magnum plane, while a positive value indicated descent below it. Pituitary height was measured on sagittal T1-weighted contrast-enhanced brain MRI. Dural enhancement was defined as diffuse, smooth, and continuous enhancement of the dura mater on contrast-enhanced T1-weighted brain MRI. Venous sinus distension was identified by an outward convexity of the inferior border of the dominant transverse sinus on contrast-enhanced brain MRI. CSF leakage was determined by identifying the leakage site on whole-spine sagittal T2-weighted MRI, spinal MR myelography (n = 88, 15.30%) and CT Myelography (CTM, n = 97, 16.87%). Spine imaging characteristics included the presence of cervical/thoracic/lumbar disc herniation identified on whole-spine sagittal T2-weighted MRI.

Statistical analysis and model construction

Statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using R software (version 4.3.2). Categorical variables were summarized as frequency (percentage) and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. All variables were compared between the SDH and NSDH groups within the entire cohort. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

From the total population, 80% of the samples (n = 461) were randomly selected using stratified sampling by group (SDH vs. NSDH) to form the training set for model development, while the remaining 20% (n = 114) served as the test set for internal validation. For variables with less than 20% missing data, mean imputation was applied.

In the training set, univariable logistic regression was performed to assess the association between each variable and SDH development. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariable analysis were included in the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression for variable selection. Features selected by LASSO were then used to construct the final multivariable logistic regression model via a stepwise approach.

The model underwent internal validation using bootstrap resampling with 500 iterations. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for discrimination, calibration curves for model calibration, and decision curve analysis (DCA) for clinical utility (Fig. 1).

Results

Demographic characteristics of SIH patients

This study included 575 patients diagnosed with SIH (SIH) who were admitted to the Departments of Neurosurgery and Pain Medicine at Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, from January 1, 2023 to December 30, 2024. Demographic, clinical, and imaging data were collected for all SIH patients. The mean age of the cohort was 37 years (IQR: 13 years), including 172 males (29.9%) and 403 females (70.1%). Among them, subdural hematoma (SDH) was identified in 101 patients (SDH group), while the remaining 474 patients comprised the NSDH group. The median duration of hospitalization was 10 days (IQR: 3 days). Regarding clinical characteristics, the majority of patients (n = 567, 98.61%) presented with orthostatic headache, while a much smaller subset (n = 8, 1.39%) reported non-orthostatic headache. Concomitant neck pain or stiffness was a typical feature, present in 152 (26.43%) patients. Disturbance of consciousness was documented in 5 (0.87%) patients. Vestibulo-auditory symptoms were commonly observed: 274 (47.65%) patients reported tinnitus, 6 (1.04%) reported hearing loss, and 169 (29.39%) reported dizziness. Comorbidities were present in 85 patients, including hypertension in 49 (8.52%), diabetes mellitus in 15 (2.61%), viral hepatitis in 15 (2.61%), connective tissue disease in 5 (0.87%) and migraine was present in only 1 (0.17%) patient. A history of intervertebral disc herniation was common, with involvement at the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar levels reported in 487 (84.70%), 104 (18.09%), and 289 (50.26%) patients, respectively. Prior spinal surgery was documented in 9 (1.57%) patients, and a history of significant spinal trauma was reported in 6 (1.04%). In terms of previous interventions, 25 (4.35%) patients had undergone traditional Chinese medicine physiotherapy. 508 (88.35%) patients had received prior treatments for intracranial hypotension before the current admission. 45 (7.83%) patients presented with a recurrent episode of SIH transferred from other hospitals. Regarding the initial treatment received for SIH, 25 (4.35%) patients underwent EBP, 11 (1.91%) underwent targeted CSF leak repair surgery, and 9 (1.57%) were managed conservatively. The use of medications affecting coagulation was assessed. 9 (1.57%) patients were using antiplatelet or anticoagulant agents prior to or at the time of admission. Of the total cohort, 117 (20.35%) patients demonstrated a CSF opening pressure of less than 60 mmH2O, 53 (9.22%) patients had an opening pressure greater than 60 mmH2O, and 405 (70.43%) patients did not undergo CSF pressure measurement. On pre-admission imaging, the presence of subdural effusion was identified in 62 (10.78%) patients. Dural enhancement was observed in 440 (76.5%) patients. Venous sinus distension was identified in 269 (46.8%). Subdural hematoma (SDH) occurred in 101 patients, yielding an incidence of 17.6%. Among patients with SDH, 14 (13.9%) had unilateral and 87 (86.1%) had bilateral involvement.

Based on the occurrence of SDH, patients were categorized into the SDH group (n = 101) and the NSDH group (n = 474). Comparative analysis showed no statistically significant differences between the two groups in the following variables: body mass index (BMI, P = 0.106), connective tissue disease (NSDH: n = 2, 0.4%; SDH: n = 3, 3.0%; P = 0.056), viral hepatitis (NSDH: n = 11, 2.3%; SDH: n = 4, 4.0%; P = 0.552), migraine (NSDH: n = 1, 0.2%; SDH: n = 0, 0.0%; P = 1.000), recurrent SIH (NSDH: n = 37, 7.8%; SDH: n = 8, 8.0%; P = 1.000), cervical disc herniation (NSDH: n = 404, 85.2%; SDH: n = 83, 82.2%; P = 0.534), Thoracic disc herniation (NSDH: n = 88, 18.6%; SDH: n = 16, 15.8%; P = 0.615), lumbar disc herniation (NSDH: n = 233, 49.2%; SDH: n = 56, 55.4%; P = 0.299), Prior spinal surgery(NSDH: n = 6, 1.3%; SDH: n = 3, 3.0%; P = 0.417), History of spinal trauma(NSDH: n = 4, 0.8%; SDH: n = 2, 2.0%; P = 0.630), Traditional Chinese medicine physiotherapy (NSDH: n = 20, 4.2%; SDH: n = 5, 5.0%; P = 0.953), use of anticoagulant or antiplatelet agents (NSDH: n = 9, 1.9%; SDH: n = 0, 0.0%; P = 0.340), presence of CSF leakage (P = 0.471), cervical CSF leakage (NSDH: n = 376, 79.3%; SDH: n = 78, 77.2%; P = 0.738), thoracic CSF leakage (NSDH: n = 389, 82.1%; SDH: n = 87, 86.1%; P = 0.402), lumbar CSF leakage (NSDH: n = 201, 42.4%; SDH: n = 43, 42.6%; P = 1.000), brain sagging (P = 0.841), pituitary height (P = 0.270), venous sinus distension (P = 0.475), lumbar puncture opening pressure (P = 0.720), and time from symptom onset to admission (NSDH group: 21 (16); SDH group: 30 (24); P = 0.472).

Validation and clinical utility assessment of the clinical prediction model

Internal validation of the model’s predictive performance was conducted using the bootstrap method with 500 resamples. As shown in Fig. 4A, the LASSO regression model demonstrated good discriminatory ability. In the training set, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was 0.806 (95% CI: 0.764–0.847). In the test set, the AUC was 0.740 (95% CI: 0.626–0.853). In the entire cohort, the AUC was 0.793 (95% CI: 0.754–0.833). The P-value for the difference in AUC values across the datasets was 0.995, indicating consistent performance without evidence of overfitting.

As illustrated in Fig. 4B, the logistic regression model demonstrated strong discriminatory performance across all datasets. In the training set, the model achieved an AUC of 0.839 (95% CI: 0.793–0.885). At the optimal cutoff value of 0.563, the specificity was 0.965 and the sensitivity was 0.438. In the test set, the AUC was 0.837 (95% CI: 0.744–0.930). At the optimal cutoff value of 0.276, the specificity and sensitivity were 0.965 and 0.421, respectively. In the entire cohort, the AUC was 0.790 (95% CI: 0.804–0.885), with a specificity of 0.963 and a sensitivity of 0.434 at the optimal cutoff value of 0.276. The P-value for the difference in AUC values across datasets was 0.641, confirming stable and consistent performance without evidence of overfitting.

The AUC values of the LASSO regression model and the logistic regression model were compared. The logistic regression model demonstrated superior discriminatory ability while providing a more parsimonious structure. Therefore, the logistic regression model was selected as the final model. As shown in Fig. 4C, the model exhibited good calibration across all datasets. The mean absolute error (MAE) was 0.043 (< 0.05) in the training set, 0.049 (< 0.05) in the test set, and 0.048 (< 0.05) in the entire cohort. MAE values below 0.05 across all datasets indicated high consistency between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes.

In the decision curve analysis (DCA), the vertical axis represents net benefit and the horizontal axis represents the risk threshold. The “None” line indicates the strategy in which all patients are considered negative and no interventions are applied, while the “All” line represents the strategy in which all patients are considered positive and receive intervention. The model curve represents the net benefit of applying the prediction model at different risk thresholds. A model is clinically beneficial when its decision curve lies above the “None” line.

As shown in Fig. 4D, the model demonstrated favorable clinical utility. In the training set, the model provided a net benefit when the risk threshold was below 0.74. In the test set, a net benefit was observed for risk thresholds below 0.69. In the entire cohort, the model showed a net benefit for risk thresholds below 0.73.

Discussion

SIH, first characterized in 1938 [19], is now recognized as a disorder of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage leading to significant morbidity [1]. Its estimated annual incidence is 3.7–3.8/100,000, though true rates are likely higher due to underdiagnosis [2–3, 20–21]. While often considered a self-limiting condition with generally favorable outcomes [9, 22–23], a major complication is subdural hematoma (SDH), occurring in 16.5–32.5% of patients [15–16]. Unlike typical chronic SDH, SIH-related SDH affects younger individuals, has a distinct pathophysiology, and portends a more challenging clinical course with potential for severe neurological sequelae [14, 24]. Despite improved diagnosis, a critical unmet need remains for reliable tools to stratify SDH risk. The development of a sensitive and accurate predictive model is therefore essential for early identification of high-risk patients and timely intervention.

A major strength of this study is its large cohort of SIH patients. We conducted a systematic retrospective analysis of 575 individuals diagnosed with SIH and admitted to our center from January 1, 2023 to December 30, 2024. Within this cohort, approximately 17.6% of SIH patients developed SDH, consistent with previously reported incidence rates of 16.5%–32.5% [15–16]. We applied both LASSO regression and logistic regression to identify factors associated with SDH development in SIH patients. Comparison of the models’ discriminatory performance, based on the Area Under the Curve (AUC), demonstrated that the logistic regression model outperformed the LASSO model. Given its superior discrimination and greater simplicity and practicality, the logistic regression model was selected as the final predictive model. Analysis using this final model identified history of prior treatment for intracranial hypotension, pre-admission subdural effusion and presence of dural enhancement as independent risk factors for SDH in SIH patients. The model demonstrated excellent discrimination without evidence of overfitting, along with good calibration. Decision Curve Analysis further confirmed its favorable clinical utility across a wide range of risk thresholds.

Our comparative analysis between the NSDH and SDH groups showed that females accounted for approximately 70.1% of the SIH cohort, whereas males accounted for 29.9%, yielding a female-to-male ratio of about 2.3:1. The probability of SDH development was 29.1% in males compared with 12.7% in females, indicating a significantly lower risk of SDH in females. This directional association, though not independent in our final model, may reflect underlying biological or anatomical differences, or it may be mediated or confounded by other covariates included in the analysis. Consistent with our findings, previous studies reported a higher prevalence of SIH in females [4–5], while males demonstrated a greater tendency to develop SDH among SIH patients [17, 25]. Male sex has been identified as a risk factor for SDH in SIH, independent of age and time to presentation [25]. Although the mechanisms of SDH formation in SIH differ from those of primary cSDH, the two conditions may share overlapping pathophysiological pathways. Many studies report a higher incidence of cSDH in males than in females [26–27], which is commonly attributed to higher exposure to head trauma in males. Furthermore, substantial evidence suggests that estrogen exerts vasculoprotective effects [28]. Through estrogen receptor-mediated gene regulation, estrogen promotes vascular endothelial cell growth and improves regenerative capacity after endothelial injury [29–30], potentially conferring a lower risk of hemorrhage in females. Our analysis identified age as an independent risk factor for SDH in SIH patients. The median age was 36 years (IQR: 12 years) in the NSDH group compared with 40 years (IQR: 16 years) in the SDH group, suggesting that older patients are more susceptible to SDH. Tearing of the bridging veins between the dura mater and arachnoid mater is a major mechanism underlying SDH. Age-related brain atrophy increases the subdural space, allowing greater relative movement between the skull and the brain. Additionally, bridging veins become stretched and more vulnerable to rupture, promoting hematoma formation [31–32]. Aging also leads to a decline in overall physiological function, including vascular health. At the microscopic level, senescence of vascular endothelial cells impairs their function, increasing susceptibility to vascular injury [33–34]. In our study, both height and weight were identified as potential risk factors for SDH, warranting further investigation with larger sample sizes to clarify their precise roles. Height may influence intracranial negative pressure through its effect on the hydrostatic indifference point (HIP) of the CSF. According to hydrostatic principles, taller individuals have a greater hydrostatic pressure gradient from the head to the sacrum, potentially resulting in a lower HIP. This may lead to more pronounced negative intracranial pressure in the upright position [35–36]. However, the underlying biomechanical and physiological mechanisms remain speculative. We propose this as a conceptual framework to guide future research.

The presence of comorbidities may influence the risk of SDH in SIH patients. Univariable analysis identified associations between hypertension, diabetes mellitus, connective tissue disease, and SDH risk, although these factors were not retained in the final multivariable model. Mechanistically, chronic hypertension can cause endothelial damage and vascular sclerosis, increasing vessel fragility and predisposing to rupture during minor stress or blood pressure fluctuations. Diabetes mellitus can lead to microangiopathy, similarly increasing vascular fragility [37–38], which may elevate hemorrhagic risk in the setting of SIH-related mechanical stress. Connective tissue disorders are characterized by structural abnormalities in collagen and elastin fibers, which are crucial for maintaining the integrity and function of various tissues, including the meninges. The mechanisms linking connective tissue disease to SDH risk in SIH remain unclear, but prior studies report increased susceptibility to intracranial vascular abnormalities in these patients [39–41]. The failure of hypertension, diabetes, and connective tissue disease to emerge as independent predictors in our multivariable model may reflect limited sample size or collinearity with other variables, indicating that larger studies are needed to clarify their roles. Antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapies typically increase the risk of intracranial hemorrhage [42]. These agents inhibit platelet aggregation and reduce the activity or synthesis of coagulation factors, thereby impairing vascular endothelial repair and increasing the susceptibility of microvessels to rupture under hemodynamic stress [43]. Several studies have shown a higher prevalence of antiplatelet or anticoagulant use in patients with non-traumatic intracerebral hemorrhage compared with those with traumatic hemorrhage [43–45]. However, this trend was not observed in our study, likely due to the relatively young patient population, the low prevalence of antithrombotic medication use, and the resulting limited sample size.

Among the SIH patients with history of spine-related diseases, 49 had undergone traditional Chinese medicine physiotherapy such as manipulations, massages, acupuncture, or small needle-knife treatments for discomfort in the cervical, shoulder, or lumbar regions, which they had mistakenly attributed to fatigue. The evidence synthesized herein clearly delineates that interventions involving deep tissue manipulation and the use of invasive instruments, carry a significantly elevated risk of precipitating SIH. One study reported that SDH may present as an initial symptom following spinal manipulation in SIH patients [46]. Recent clinical observations have highlighted that certain therapeutic interventions, such as acupuncture, or minimally invasive surgical techniques, may inadvertently trigger SIH in susceptible patients, particularly those with pre-existing vulnerabilities in their dura mater or connective tissue structures [47]. Furthermore, other studies indicated that significant mechanical forces applied to the spine may induce CSF leakage [48–49]. Therefore, the connection between therapeutic practices and the onset of SIH underscores the necessity for clinicians to be cognizant of the potential risks associated with such interventions, especially in patients presenting with unexplained headaches or those with known risk factors for CSF leaks [50].

Prior to admission, 88.35% of the patients had received management for intracranial hypotension. Among those patients, 45 (7.83%) patients presented with a recurrent episode of SIH transferred from other hospitals. Regarding the initial treatment received for SIH, 25 patients underwent EBP, 11 received targeted CSF leak repair surgery, and 9 were managed conservatively. The potential impact of prior treatment on SDH risk is multifactorial. First, the need for any treatment itself is a marker of disease severity and duration. Specifically, failed or incomplete treatment may prolong the state of intracranial hypotension. Second, different treatment modalities may confer distinct risks [51]. While successful EBP or surgical repair aims to rapidly normalize CSF dynamics and theoretically reduce SDH risk [52], conservative management alone might allow the underlying pathophysiological process to progress. Notably, the presence of a recurrent episode suggests either an elusive leak source, inadequate initial repair, or inherent tissue fragility, all of which could perpetuate the risk for SDH development.

Pre-admission subdural effusion, dural enhancement, venous sinus distension, pituitary hyperemia or enlargement, and brain sagging are characteristic imaging features of SIH. In the final predictive model, both pre-admission subdural effusion and dural enhancement emerged as the strongest independent predictors for subsequent SDH development. Spontaneous CSF leaks lead to a persistent reduction in CSF volume, which in turn causes a decrease in intracranial pressure (ICP). This hypovolemic state results in the downward displacement or sagging of brain tissue within the cranial vault. Brain sagging exerts mechanical traction on bridging veins, increasing venous wall tension and permeability, which facilitates plasma extravasation into the subdural space, initiating fluid collection formation [53]. In SIH, decreased CSF volume results in compensatory increases in intracranial blood volume and venous distension, which manifest radiologically as dural enhancement, venous sinus distension, and pituitary engorgement. Brain sagging reflects reduced CSF buoyancy supporting the brain. Dural enhancement - one of the most characteristic imaging findings in SIH - was absent in only 1 of the 101 SDH patients in our study. This may be attributed to several factors, including imaging performed very early in the disease course or technical variations in MRI acquisition. Its prevalence was significantly higher in the SDH group than in the NSDH group. Previous studies also reported a higher frequency of dural enhancement in SIH patients who developed SDH compared with those who did not [17, 54]. This finding may reflect greater disease severity. Pronounced dural enhancement may indicate more substantial CSF leakage and increased intracranial venous engorgement. Some studies suggest that highly distended and engorged dural vessels may be more fragile and could serve as a source of bleeding, contributing to SDH formation [54–55]. No significant differences were observed between the NSDH and SDH groups in venous sinus distension, pituitary enlargement, or brain sagging. In contrast, one study reported a significant difference in venous sinus distension between the two groups [17]. These inconsistencies may stem from differences in sample size or cohort heterogeneity, warranting further investigation with larger datasets. Pituitary height is known to decline with age [56], which may partially explain the absence of a significant difference. In our study, obvious brain sagging (cerebellar tonsils below the foramen magnum plane) was observed in 123 patients. Assessment of brain sagging relies on MRI, which is performed in the supine position in SIH patients. This positioning may influence imaging findings, as lying flat often alleviates SIH symptoms. We speculate that brain sagging may be partially reduced in the supine position during MRI [17].

Logistic regression analyses were then performed by stratifying the training set into subgroups of the patients with bilateral and unilateral hematoma, with the results summarized in Tables 5 and 6. In the subgroup of the patients with bilateral subdural hematoma, the multivariable logistic regression analysis identified history of prior treatment for intracranial hypotension and presence of dural enhancement as independent risk factors. Presence of dural enhancement was shown independently associated with SDH occurrence in the subgroup of the patients with unilateral subdural hematoma. The biomechanical interplay between the anatomical fragility of bridging veins and their variations thus forms a fundamental basis for understanding the pathogenesis of unilateral subdural hematomas secondary to SIH. This concept is supported by anatomical studies demonstrating the delicate nature of bridging veins and their variable configurations, which influence their mechanical resilience and propensity for rupture under pathological brain shifts [57–59]. Radiological and anatomical studies have documented these sinus abnormalities and their dynamic changes in response to intracranial pressure variations, underscoring their role in the pathophysiology of SIH-associated subdural hematomas [60–61]. Regions with sparse bridging veins or acute angulation at the junction are more prone to focal stress concentration and subsequent vascular injury. This biomechanical vulnerability is supported by anatomical and surgical studies demonstrating that the cortical vein-sinus junction is a frequent site of venous rupture and subdural hemorrhage in conditions involving brain sagging [57–58].

The median symptom duration prior to hospitalization was longer in the SDH group (30 days) than in the NSDH group (21 days), although this difference was also not statistically significant (P > 0.05). This lack of significance may reflect imprecise recording of symptom onset in medical records. Among the 575 SIH patients included, the shortest interval from symptom onset to SDH diagnosis was 14 days, suggesting that SDH secondary to SIH requires a certain latency period to develop. The duration of hospitalization was significantly longer in the SDH group than in the NSDH group. Several studies reported that the risk of SDH in SIH patients increases with longer hospitalization [17, 54, 62], consistent with our findings. This may be explained by the reduced CSF volume in SIH, which leads to caudal traction on the brain and stretching of the bridging veins between the dura mater and arachnoid mater [63]. Over time, cumulative mechanical stress on these stretched veins increases the likelihood of tearing. Additionally, a longer hospital stay may reflect more severe initial symptoms or a greater burden of complications, indirectly indicating a higher inherent risk of SDH.

Conclusion

SIH is a highly disabling syndrome secondary to spinal cerebrospinal fluid leak, typically characterized by orthostatic headache and a variety of other neurological symptoms. In this study, we conducted a systematic retrospective analysis of a large cohort of individuals diagnosed with SIH in our center. A clinical prediction model with high sensitivity and accuracy was constructed, which identified history of prior treatment for intracranial hypotension, pre-admission subdural effusion and presence of dural enhancement as independent risk factors for SDH in SIH patients. The prediction model shows strong discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility, supporting the early identification of high-risk patients, optimization of intervention timing, and improvement of clinical outcomes. However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study. Its single-center, retrospective design may introduce selection bias and limits generalizability, as the model was developed and validated only internally. Although internal validation suggests robustness, the high apparent performance may reflect overfitting to local patient characteristics, imaging protocols, and treatment practices. Furthermore, the statistical power for certain subgroup analyses was limited due to relatively small sample sizes in some categories. External validation in independent, multi-center prospective cohorts is therefore essential to confirm the model’s calibration and discriminatory performance before it can be considered for broader clinical use.
