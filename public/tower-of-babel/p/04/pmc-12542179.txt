Mechanisms of heat shock-induced germination in Suaeda glauca (Bunge) bunge: insights from endogenous hormonal regulation
Authors: Shaoyang Li, Hongyuan Ma, Wenwen Qi, Dandan Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFire cues, such as heat and smoke, play crucial roles in seed germination, particularly in fire-prone ecosystems. A key challenge in understanding how fire cues affect the germination process is characterizing the complex and varied relationships between fire cues and seed dormancy types. The mechanisms by which fire cues affect seed germination have long been a major focus of research. This study investigates the effects of heat shock, aerosol smoke, and their combination on the germination of Suaeda glauca, focusing on endogenous hormonal regulation. ResultsWe found that heat shock significantly reduced ABA levels and the ABA/GAs ratio, promoting the release of physiological dormancy. However, no significant synergistic effect between heat and smoke on seed germination was observed. Our findings highlight that cue intensity plays a more critical role than cue interaction in the dormancy release of S. glauca. Conclusions This work advances the current understanding of how fire cues release seed dormancy and introduces a new mechanism whereby heat shock releases physiological dormancy in S. glauca seeds by regulating the levels of endogenous hormones. Experiments targeting more species, especially those in which heat shock can release physiological dormancy, are essential to conclusively determine whether the mechanism we identified is broadly applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07470-1.

Introduction

Wildfire is a critical component of terrestrial ecosystems, playing a vital role in shaping the global distribution of biomes and driving vegetation dynamics [1–3]. The burning of vegetation creates gaps with reduced competition and high resource availability, providing a favorable environment for the seed germination of many species [4, 5]. A growing body of evidence suggests that smoke and heat from vegetation burning can act as germination cues for breaking seed dormancy in fire-prone ecosystems [6, 7]. Combined data from various fire-prone environments show that smoke or heat promotes seed germination in 42% of 586 species [8]. However, to date, most research on the role of fire in breaking seed dormancy has focused on the individual effects of smoke or heat, ignoring the effect of their interaction [9]. Therefore, whether the combined effect of heat and smoke is superior to a single fire cue remains an open question.

A key challenge in understanding how fire cues affect the germination process is characterizing the complex and varied relationships between fire cues and seed dormancy types. Seeds of different dormancy types can respond differently to postfire germination cues [10]. The basic dormancy type associated with heat release dormancy syndrome is physical dormancy, whereas that associated with smoke release dormancy syndrome is physiological dormancy [8, 11]. Although there is no strict one-to-one correspondence between dormancy types and fire cues, heat shock can also promote the germination of seeds with physiological dormancy [12]. Therefore, to better understand the effects of fire cues on seed germination, future studies should include a broader range of species from diverse environments.

Plant endogenous hormones are well known to affect seed dormancy and germination [13, 14]. Plant-derived smoke contains water-soluble germination stimulants such as karrikins, glyceronitrile, and syringaldehyde, which are washed into the soil by rainfall, promoting seed germination [15–17]. Some empirical studies have shown that these smoke-related cues stimulate seed germination by regulating endogenous hormone levels and enzyme activity [18–20]. Given that heat shock-induced germination typically occurs in species with physical dormancy, related research has mostly focused on the changes in the physical structure of seeds. Past studies have shown that heat shock releases physical dormancy in multiple species by disrupting the integrity of the seed coat structure [11, 21, 22]. In a few cases, heat shock has also been shown to promote the germination of seeds with physiological dormancy [12, 23, 24]. However, as an uncommon phenomenon, the underlying mechanism through which heat shock releases physiological dormancy remains poorly understood.

A recent study suggests that both smoke and heat shock can break the physiological dormancy of S. glauca, a species from a non-fire-prone environment [25], making it an ideal model for investigating the mechanisms through which fire cues affect seed germination. S. glauca is an annual herbaceous plant in the Amaranthaceae family, a typical halophyte and an important component of the saline-alkaline grasslands [26]. Investigating the effects of fire cues on the seed germination of S. glauca is essential to understanding the population dynamics of this species following accidental fire events. Therefore, we conducted germination experiments on S. glauca using multiple gradients of fire cues to address the following specific questions: (1) Does the combination of heat and smoke confer a significant advantage in promoting germination compared to a single fire cue? (2) What is the mechanism through which heat shock induces the release of physiological dormancy? Does it share a common pathway with smoke?

Materials and methods

Study species and seed collection

Mature seeds of Suaeda glauca (Bunge) Bunge were collected in October 2021 from the Da’an Saline-Alkali Land Ecological Experiment Station, China (123°50′27″−123°51′31″E, 45°35′58″−45°36′28″N), which is an important agro-pastoral transitional zone. Seed collection of S. glauca was authorized by the local agricultural administration and experiment station. The voucher specimens were preserved in the Northeast Institute of Geography and Agroecology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, and identified by Dr. Hongyuan Ma. Given the dimorphic nature of S. glauca seeds, we selected the soft brown type as the experimental material to ensure the reliability of the results [25]. Collected seeds were stored in a laboratory environment at approximately 25 °C and 40%−50% relative humidity until the experiment began in December 2021.

Germination experimental design

For the heat shock treatments, mature seeds were placed in envelopes and exposed to high temperatures in an oven (BoXun, BGZ-456). Heat shock was applied at six combinations of temperature and duration: 60 °C, 80 °C, or 100 °C for 5 or 10 min. These temperature intensities have been applied multiple times in previous studies [22, 27]. Seeds stored at room temperature (~ 25 °C) without heat shock treatment were used as controls.

In the experiments assessing germination responses to aerosol smoke, seeds were placed inside a smoke box (78 × 56 × 45.5 cm; length×width×height; Figure S1). The dry biomass of various grasses (e.g., Leymus chinensis, Setaria viridis, Phragmites australis) was ignited in the smoke generator, and the resulting smoke entered the box through the pipe. Seeds were directly exposed to smoke for 0, 2.5, 5, 10, or 15 min, depending on the treatment duration.

In experiments combining fire cues, seeds were subjected to heat shock treatments at three temperature levels: room temperature (~ 25 °C), 80 °C for 10 min, and 100 °C for 10 min. After heat shock, the seeds were placed in the smoke box where the dry biomass was ignited in the smoke generator, and the resulting smoke entered the box through the pipe. The smoking durations were set to 0, 5, and 10 min, respectively. In the above three germination experiments, 25 seeds were placed in each Petri dish containing 0.7% agar medium, and each treatment was repeated four times [19]. The Petri dishes were then placed in an artificial climate chamber (SaiFu, PGX-450 C) set to a 16/28°C temperature regime with a 12 h light/12 h dark photoperiod, under a combination of fluorescent and incandescent white light at an intensity of 54 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹. Daily observations were conducted to record the number of germinated seeds, defined by radicle emergence of at least 2 mm.

Biochemical determination

We placed the seeds subjected to heat shock (80 °C for 5 min), aerosol smoke (10 min), combined heat shock + aerosol smoke (80 °C for 5 min followed by 10 min of smoke), and the control treatment (~ 25 °C) into separate tea bags, with three replicates for each treatment. The tea bags were soaked in 50 mL of water for 14 h under a 16/28°C temperature regime (7 h dark/7 h light). After soaking, seeds in an imbibed but non-germinated state were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently ground using a high-throughput tissue grinder (Xinzhi, SCIENTZ-48). The treated seeds were divided into two parts: one for α-amylase activity measurement and the other for endogenous plant hormone analysis.

The activity of α-amylase was determined using a colorimetric assay kit (Suzhou Keming Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and absorbance was measured at 540 nm.

Fifty milligrams of the ground sample were weighed and extracted with 1 mL of methanol/water/formic acid (15:4:1, v/v/v) solvent. The mixture was thoroughly vortexed for 10 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube for concentration. After evaporation, the residue was reconstituted in 100 µL of 80% methanol/water solution, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and transferred to a sample vial for the determination of GA and ABA contents.

Hormone quantification was performed using an ultra-performance liquid chromatography system (UPLC, ExionLC™ AD, SCIEX) coupled with a tandem mass spectrometer (MS/MS, QTRAP® 6500+, SCIEX). Chromatographic separation was carried out on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column (1.8 μm, 100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d.), using a mobile phase consisting of ultrapure water with 0.04% acetic acid (solvent A) and acetonitrile with 0.04% acetic acid (solvent B). The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min, the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the injection volume was 2 µL. Mass spectrometry data were processed using Analyst 1.6.3 and MultiQuant 3.0.3 software. Hormone measurement was supported by Wuhan Maiwei Metabolism Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Statistical analyses

All data were analyzed using R (version 4.3.0). We used generalized linear models (GLMs) with binomial error structure and the logit link function to evaluate the effects of fire cue treatments on germination. For inclusion in the model, when the germination percentage was 0, we converted it to 1% [28]. However, all figures present the original, untransformed data. Tukey’s HSD post hoc test was conducted for pairwise multiple comparisons between different treatments. The linear models (LM) were employed to analyze differences in endogenous hormone levels and amylase activity among seeds subjected to fire cues.

Results

Effects of heat shock and smoke on seed germination

Heat shock (df = 6, χ2 = 183.72, P < 0.001) and smoke (df = 4, χ2 = 129.2, P < 0.001) significantly affected the germination percentage of S. glauca (Fig. 1). The germination percentage in control (T1) was 22.3%. From T1 to T4, germination showed an increasing trend, with significantly higher germination observed under T3 and T4 treatments compared to the control (Fig. 1a). High-intensity heat shock (T5, T6 and T7) significantly inhibited seed germination, and no germination was observed at T7. Exposure to smoke for 2.5, 10, and 15 min significantly increased seed germination, with the highest germination percentage of 80% observed at 10 and 15 min (P < 0.05; Fig. 1b). Exposure to smoke for 5 min moderately increased the germination percentage, reaching a marginally significant level (P = 0.086; Fig. 1b). There was a significant interaction between heat and smoke affecting the seed germination of S. glauca (df = 4, χ2 = 60.73, P < 0.001). Effects of heat shock and smoke on endogenous hormones and enzyme activity

Only five phytohormones—ABA, GA4, GA7, GA9, and GA20—were detected at quantifiable levels in the tested seeds. GA4 and GA7, both biologically active forms of gibberellins, were jointly considered and denoted as GAs. Heat shock significantly reduced the endogenous levels of ABA (−25.2%; F1,8 = 11.42, P < 0.01; Fig. 3a). Although heat shock did not significantly affect the levels of GAs (F1,8 = 2.96, P = 0.12; Fig. 3b), it significantly increased the content of GA7 (+ 46.2%; F1,8 = 6.48, P = 0.03; Fig. S2b). We also found heat shock significantly reduced the ratio of ABA/GAs (−12%; F1,8 = 6.48, P < 0.01; Fig. 3c). However, smoke significantly reduced the content of GAs (F1,8 = 5.83, P = 0.04; Fig. 3). No significant main or interaction effects on GA4, GA9 and GA20 were observed (P > 0.05; Fig. S2). Discussion

Our study tested how fire-related cues affect the germination performance of S. glauca from a non-fire-prone grassland. We found that smoke and heat shock could significantly promote the germination of S. glauca under suitable gradients. However, contrary to our expectation, no significant synergistic effect between heat shock and smoke was observed. Our results further showed that heat shock released physiological dormancy by affecting the levels of endogenous hormones, thereby enhancing our understanding of the mechanism of fire-released seed dormancy.

Although our experiment demonstrates that fire cues can significantly promote the germination of S. glauca from non-fire-prone grasslands, we must acknowledge that fire-released dormancy is most strongly expressed in habitats where fire plays a vital ecological role in maintaining ecosystem vitality and renewal [8, 29]. This is particularly evident in Mediterranean-type ecosystems [30] and savannas [31]. Godoy et al. [32] found that plants reliant on fire for germination in fire-prone environments lose this dependence when introduced to non-fire-prone environments. For plants with smoke-released dormancy found in both fire-prone and non-fire-prone environments, Flematti et al. [33] argued that seed germination response to smoke is a fundamental capacity of plants. It is worth noting that S. glauca seeds were stored under warm conditions (~ 25 °C) for two months prior to the experiment. Although relatively short, such storage may have induced partial after-ripening, which is known to reduce the depth of physiological dormancy in some species [34, 35].Therefore, experiments should ideally be conducted on freshly dried seeds to minimize the potential impact of after-ripening on dormancy status; otherwise, the promotive effect of fire cues on germination may be underestimated.

Theoretically, both heat shock and smoke are necessary to release seeds from combination dormancy (physical dormancy + physiological dormancy) [36, 37]. Only under these conditions can heat shock and smoke release dormancy through distinct mechanisms (heat shock increases permeability to smoke and water, while germination stimulants in smoke promote the synthesis of related enzymes), thereby achieving a synergistic effect [38]. S. glauca exhibits physiological dormancy [39], potentially explaining the lack of a significant synergistic effect between heat shock and smoke on its seed germination. We also found that high-intensity heat shock significantly inhibited germination. This is consistent with previous observations that the germination of Bituminaria bituminosa was completely inhibited by heat shock treatments of 110–150 °C for 10 min [40]. Therefore, conducting timely viability tests following fire-related germination experiments is essential for accurately inferring the fate of ungerminated seeds–specifically, whether they remain in a dormant state or have lost viability.

We found that heat shock promoted the release of physiological dormancy in S. glauca. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have also reported that heat shock can release physiological dormancy [12, 23]. Auld and Ooi [23] suggested that the impacts of heat on germination may extend beyond merely serving as a form of scarification. Indeed, we also found heat shock significantly reduced the levels of ABA and the ABA/GAs ratio. Among the various factors that influence seed germination, the phytohormones ABA and GA are recognized as the most essential endogenous signals, with their balance being a key determinant of whether a seed remains dormant or initiates germination [14, 41]. Similar findings have been reported in studies on the mechanisms by which smoke cues promote seed germination. For example, Gupta et al. [19] found that plant-derived smoke promotes the germination of Lactuca sativa seeds by reducing ABA levels and increasing enzyme activity. While this study focused solely on the mechanism in S. glauca, the extent to which heat shock-induced hormonal regulation releases physiological dormancy in other physiologically dormant species is unknown.

In our study, smoke cues increased the germination percentage of S. glauca to 80%, showing a stronger promotive effect than heat shock. This further confirms that the dormancy syndrome associated with smoke release is physiological dormancy [8]. Previous studies on L. sativa [19] and Lupinus angustifolius [42] have demonstrated that smoke cues can promote seed germination by reducing ABA levels. Similar results have also been observed in Arabidopsis thaliana, where Nelson et al. [43] found that smoke promoted seed germination without altering ABA levels. Interestingly, a seemingly paradoxical phenomenon was observed in our experiment: although smoke significantly promoted germination, it also reduced the levels of GAs. In fact, the regulation of seed dormancy by endogenous hormones typically depends on the dynamic balance between ABA and GA [44]. Our results showed that smoke did not significantly alter the ABA/GAs ratio, suggesting that its promotive effect on germination may rely on alternative pathways. These findings indicate that the mechanisms underlying seed germination responses to fire cues may vary depending on the type of cue and the plant species involved.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides new insights into the mechanisms by which heat shock releases physiological dormancy, highlighting the role of hormonal regulation. Our findings underscore the importance of cue intensity over combination effects for S. glauca and expand our understanding of fire-related germination cues in non-fire-prone ecosystems. This study is beneficial for promoting the establishment and regeneration of S. glauca populations in natural habitats, improving their survival rate. It can also serve as a reference for exploring the mechanisms by which fire cues promote seed germination. Given the seed dimorphism in S. glauca, and the fact that our study focused exclusively on the soft brown seed morph, future research should aim to elucidate how different seed morphs respond to fire-related cues and the underlying mechanisms involved.
