Seasonal influenza vaccination: A global review of national policies in 194 WHO member states in 2022
Authors: Shoshanna Goldin, Donald Brooks, Pernille Jorgensen, Pushpa Wijesinghe, Heeyoun Cho, Rania Attia, Reena Doshi, Francisco Nogareda, Belinda Herring, Laure Dumolard, Randie Gibson, Christopher Chadwick, Shalini Desai, Alba Vilajeliu, Ann Lindstrand, Stefano Tempia, Joshua Mott, Sarah Hess
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction: Seasonal influenza vaccination prevents severe influenza disease and death. The World Health Organization (WHO) encourages all countries to consider annual seasonal influenza vaccination for health workers, people with chronic conditions, older adults, pregnant women and other high-risk populations as relevant for their national context. This paper provides a global update on the status of countries' influenza vaccination policies and programmes as of December 2022.Methods: We analysed the WHO-UNICEF (United Nations Children's Fund) Joint Reporting Form on Immunization's influenza vaccine-related data. We used STATA 17 to conduct descriptive analyses of reported seasonal influenza vaccine availability and seasonal influenza vaccination policies globally.Results: Seasonal influenza vaccine doses were available in 74 % of WHO Member States (143/194) in 2022. Fewer countries, 66 % of WHO Member States (128/194), had a seasonal influenza vaccination policy, of which 68 countries reported having a policy for the public sector, 53 for the public and private sectors, two for the private sector only, and five did not report the sector. More than half of WHO Member States (100 countries) recommend annual seasonal influenza vaccination for all four of the WHO recommended priority groups. Influenza vaccination coverage data were reported by 64 countries; globally the median coverage rates varied by group: 37 % for pregnant women, 55 % for older adults and 62 % for health workers.Discussion: The number of countries using seasonal influenza vaccines has grown over time, but there is still opportunity for continued development and strengthening of national programmes, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). To support countries, WHO is providing technical guidance and resources to enable better reporting of influenza vaccination data. More complete and higher quality data will help countries and global health stakeholders to support national decision-making and programme strengthening. Where available, WHO encourages countries to co-administer influenza and COVID-19 vaccination to increase programmatic efficiency and coverage of both vaccines among recommended groups.

Introduction

In 2019, WHO launched the Global Influenza Strategy 2019–2030 [1]. The Strategy encourages expansion of seasonal influenza prevention and control policies and programmes, with a focus on seasonal influenza vaccination. Seasonal influenza vaccination prevents significant morbidity and mortality, particularly among high-risk groups such as older adults, people with chronic conditions and pregnant women [1,2]. The strategy advocates for increased access to and use of seasonal influenza vaccines globally, particularly among the groups recommended by the WHO Strategic Advisory Group of Experts on Immunization (SAGE). Influenza vaccination has been documented to be cost-effective, and even cost-saving, among the WHO recommended priority groups [2]. The WHO recommended priority groups include the above-mentioned high-risk groups and health workers, as seasonal influenza vaccination of health workers can reduce nosocomial infections and absenteeism among the health workforce [3].

Currently, influenza vaccines are used in all six WHO regions. In 2022, considering the latest evidence and SAGE recommendations, WHO published an updated position paper on seasonal influenza vaccination, recommending that all countries should consider implementing seasonal influenza vaccination programmes for health workers, individuals with comorbidities and underlying conditions, older adults and pregnant women [4]. In addition, WHO advises countries to consider additional risk groups, such as children, people at high risk of severe influenza living in congregate-living settings, and indigenous and disadvantaged populations, if relevant to their country context, priorities and capacities. As part of the recommendations, WHO considers annual seasonal influenza vaccination important not only for protecting vulnerable populations against seasonal influenza epidemics but also for building and strengthening pandemic preparedness and response capacities.

The Immunization Agenda 2030 (IA2030) set a global vision and strategy for 2021–2030 in which all people benefit from recommended vaccinations, including seasonal influenza vaccines. IA2030 monitors the national introduction of influenza vaccination, with a special focus in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). It also promotes strengthening vaccination policies and service delivery throughout the life course and the effective integration of vaccination with other essential health services [5].

This paper provides an update to previous publications that examined the global influenza vaccination policy landscape in 2014 and 2018 [6,7]. The paper examines the availability of seasonal influenza vaccine doses, the number of countries with formal national policies and/or recommendations for seasonal influenza vaccination, and coverage rates.

Methods

Primary data sources

We characterized national influenza vaccine availability, policies and vaccination overage using data from the WHO/UNICEF Joint Reporting Form on Immunization (JRF) which was reviewed by WHO regional offices. Since 2012, a range of data related to national influenza vaccination have been collected through the JRF. The data are used to monitor national, regional and global vaccination trends and identify gaps; track the implementation of global strategies such as IA2030 [8]; and inform [9] implementation of the Global Influenza Strategy 2019–2030 [1]. Countries self-report their vaccination data to the JRF annually.

Beginning in 2021, WHO/UNICEF introduced the eJRF – an electronic platform that provides a better user/data collection experience with faster and more secure data transmission. It added real-time validation checks, automatic calculations and a monitored review process to augment the standard checks already in place to improve data quality and consistency [10]. The JRF questionnaire reflects a consensus-based decision-making process involving staff from UNICEF headquarters, WHO headquarters, the regional offices of both agencies, and some selected WHO and UNICEF country offices, as well as representatives from the immunization department of the ministries of health of the same countries.

Seasonal influenza vaccination data were reported by countries between 1 March 2023 and 23 October 2023. The data reported reflect the 2021–2022 northern hemisphere/2022 southern hemisphere influenza seasons, depending on the national context (from this point forward noted as 2022). For countries that did not respond to the question on influenza vaccination policy, we used historic JRF data to supplement. The JRF data were reviewed by WHO regional offices and country offices, where applicable, to fill in data missing on the presence of an influenza vaccination policy, priority groups and coverage. The 2014 and 2018 data were abstracted from the last two landscape analyses of seasonal influenza vaccines conducted by Ortiz et al. and Morales et al., respectively [6,7].

Data processing and analysis

Survey data from all sources were combined and analysed using STATA 17. Descriptive analysis provided the number and proportion of WHO Member States that reported policies (including recommendations for priority populations), vaccine use, administrative coverage and administrative data reporting patterns related to influenza. Results were compiled at the global and WHO regional levels [11], by eligibility for financial support from Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance (Gavi) [12] and by World Bank income classification [[13], [14], [15]].

The eJRF asks countries to describe their influenza vaccination policies in terms of the populations to which the policies apply and directly queries eight distinct priority groups for vaccination. We present results for each of these groups and focus on a subset of four groups recommended for influenza vaccination: health workers, older adults, pregnant women, and children and adults with underlying health conditions [4]. As countries with universal recommendations offer influenza vaccination to their full population (aged 6 months and older), countries with a universal recommendation were assumed to have a recommendation for all of the WHO recommended priority groups.

Results

Response rate

In 2023, 153 (79 %) of the 194 WHO Member States submitted an eJRF with a response to the question on the presence of a national influenza vaccination policy in 2022. Response rates ranged from 55 % (South-East Asia Region) to 89 % (European Region) with varying levels of reporting across WHO regions (Eastern Mediterranean Region: 67 %; African Region: 72 %; Western Pacific Region: 81 %; Region of the Americas: 86 %). Response to this question was highest among upper-middle-income (91 %) and high-income (85 %) countries compared with low-income (50 %), lower-middle-income, Gavi-eligible (75 %) and lower-middle-income, not-Gavi-eligible (77 %) countries. For the 41 countries that did not respond to the 2022 eJRF, the corresponding WHO regional office provided information collected through regional surveys, country missions, and/or email communications with relevant national officials.

Availability of seasonal influenza vaccines

Seasonal influenza vaccines were available in the public and/or private sector in 74 % of WHO Member States (143/194) in 2022. Influenza vaccines were available in all countries in the European region and nearly all (91 %) countries in the Region of the Americas, while in the African Region only 30 % of countries reported having seasonal influenza vaccines available for use. Influenza vaccine availability increased with the World Bank income status of countries, from 19 % for low-income countries to 95 % for high-income countries. Globally, 99 of the 143 countries with vaccine available (69 %) reported data on influenza vaccine doses distributed. Together, these countries reported distributing approximately 450 million doses of seasonal influenza vaccines in 2022 which is nearly double the number of doses that countries reported distributing in 2014. In the JRF, countries reported distributing approximately 230 million doses of seasonal influenza vaccines in 2014 and 276 million doses in 2018.

Coverage

Sixty-four (33 %) of the 194 Member States reported coverage data for at least one WHO recommended priority group. Influenza vaccination coverage data were most frequently reported for older adults, health workers, and pregnant women. Many countries that recommend seasonal influenza vaccination for WHO recommended priority groups are not reporting coverage data. For example, 109 countries recommend influenza vaccination for older adults, but only 54 countries provided coverage data for this priority group. Similarly, 101 countries recommend seasonal influenza vaccination for health workers and 93 countries recommend seasonal influenza vaccination for pregnant women. However, only 42 countries provide coverage data for health workers and 40 countries report coverage data for pregnant women.

Reported coverage varied widely, with some countries reporting coverage above 100 % or below 1 %. Coverage among older adults ranged from 0 % to 103 % (median: 55 %). Thirteen countries achieved the World Health Assembly goal of 75 % coverage of older adults. In comparison, only two countries achieved this coverage goal in 2014 [6]. Of the thirteen countries that achieved the coverage goal in 2022, four were high income, six were upper-middle income, two were lower-middle income, and one was a low-income country. Influenza coverage among health care workers ranged from 1 % to 123 % (median: 62 %; three countries reported values >100 % or < 1 %). Reported coverage among pregnant women ranged from 0 % to 109 % (median: 37 %).

Vaccine products

Of the 143 countries that reported having seasonal influenza vaccines available in 2022, 116 reported using inactivated influenza vaccines (81 %). Of these, 28 countries reported using only trivalent influenza vaccines (TIVs), 68 countries reported using only quadrivalent influenza vaccines (QIVs), and 20 countries reported using both TIVs and QIVs. As B/Yamagata has not been circulating for years, WHO encourages countries to procure and administer trivalent influenza vaccines. Twenty-nine countries (20 %) reported using adjuvanted vaccine and 22 countries (15 %) reported using high-dose vaccines. Twenty-five countries (17 %) reported using live attenuated influenza vaccines. Northern and southern hemisphere formulations were used by 76 and 25 countries, respectively; 11 countries used both formulations, and 31 countries that reported having seasonal influenza vaccines available did not characterize the formulation used. National seasonal influenza vaccination policies and priority groups for vaccination

While 143 countries reported having seasonal influenza vaccines available, 15 of these countries did not yet have a national seasonal influenza vaccination policy in place. Globally, 66 % of WHO Member States (128/194) had a seasonal influenza vaccination policy in 2022. In comparison, 74 countries (38 %) reported having a seasonal influenza vaccination policy in 2006 and 115 countries (59 %) reported having a policy in 2014 [6]. National seasonal influenza vaccination policies were present in all WHO regions and across all World Bank income groups. Sixty-eight countries reported having a policy for the public sector, 53 for the public and private sectors and two for the private sector; five countries did not indicate to which sector(s) their policy applies. Of the 194 WHO Member States, 57 % recommended vaccination for health workers, 57 % for people with chronic conditions, 56 % for older adults and 53 % for pregnant women in 2022. This represents an increase from 2014 when 47 % of WHO Member States recommended vaccination for health care workers, and 46 % for chronic conditions. In 2022, more than 40 % of countries recommended seasonal influenza vaccination for children above the age of 6 months compared to 28 % of countries in 2014. In 2022, 16 % countries reported having a universal recommendation for seasonal influenza vaccination compared to 11 % in 2014. Countries also reported that their national influenza policies included recommendations for residents of long-term care facilities (84 countries) and/or travellers (54 countries). Forty-three countries provided recommendations for vaccination of other groups. Recommendations that countries frequently noted in open text included: first responders (e.g. police and firefighters) and/or essential personnel (e.g. frontline workers and essential services); workers who have contact with poultry, swine, or other farm animals, including veterinary workers; people living with or caring for someone who is immunocompromised or at risk of complications from influenza; members of the military; and flight crews and public transit or port of entry workers. Twelve countries reported a national policy without identifying any specific recommendations for subpopulations.

Strength of immunization system

National immunization technical advisory groups (NITAG) play a critical role in reviewing evidence to make vaccine recommendations. As such, the presence of a functional NITAG was used to assess the strength of the immunization system in both the 2014 and 2018 seasonal influenza vaccination analyses [16,17]. Discussion

Seasonal influenza vaccination is an important and growing component of the global immunization landscape [18]. As the second-largest vaccine market by volume annually, seasonal influenza vaccines are available in nearly three quarters of the world. However, 15 countries, primarily those classified as high income, use more than 75 % of the procured influenza vaccine doses [19]. While high-income countries are the largest users of seasonal influenza vaccines, there is growing interest in this vaccine among LMICS. For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic period (from 2020 to 2022), six LMICs reported introducing seasonal influenza vaccination as a way of protecting their vulnerable populations and reducing the burden on their health systems.

This analysis compared seasonal influenza vaccination data across three time stamps (2014, 2018, and 2022). In looking across this eight year period, a clear positive trend of countries reporting increasing interest in and use of seasonal influenza vaccination is evident. Within this eight year period, 13 countries introduced a national seasonal influenza vaccination policy, the number of doses distributed globally has nearly doubled, and coverage rates have increased over time. In particular, the COVID-19 pandemic brought a global focus on the importance of adult vaccination programmes, particularly for health workers. As the world considers how to maintain the capacities gained during the pandemic years, many countries have expressed interest in respiratory vaccinations (influenza, COVID-19, and RSV) for at-risk adult populations. Vaccination of adults against respiratory pathogens can provide a platform for preparedness for future epidemics and pandemics.

Although seasonal influenza vaccination has been found to be cost-effective and cost-saving in LMICs [2], procuring the vaccine and implementing the vaccination programme annually can be resource-intensive [20]. Some countries may have influenza vaccination policies but may not procure vaccines if there is not dedicated funding through national budget allocations or procure the vaccines in a very limited way. Efforts are underway globally to develop improved influenza vaccines that could have a longer duration of effectiveness, which could reduce seasonal influenza vaccination programmatic costs. The COVID-19 pandemic spurred considerable attention to and progress in the pipeline of next generation and improved influenza vaccines with several vaccines now in Phase 3 clinical trials [20]. In addition, combination vaccines which may include influenza, COVID-19 and/or RSV antigen are currently undergoing clinical trials [21]. If reasonably priced, these vaccines may provide LMICS with new opportunities to protect vulnerable populations against respiratory pathogens.

While the world waits for improved and next-generation influenza vaccines, WHO encourages all countries to implement annual vaccination with the currently available vaccines. Where available, WHO encourages countries to co-administer influenza and COVID-19 vaccination to increase programmatic efficiency and coverage of both vaccines among recommended groups. Following the issuance of new recommendations for seasonal influenza vaccination in 2022, WHO developed a policy brief that provides an overview of the key elements of a national policy and highlights technical resources that may be useful to national decision-makers, members of national immunization technical advisory groups and other relevant technical focal points [4,22]. These materials and other resources can be accessed through the WHO seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccination toolkits [23,24]. The toolkits are intended to help interested countries develop and strengthen their national seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccination policies and programmes. New resources are anticipated to be added to support countries in collecting, analysing and reporting coverage data.

Within the JRF, countries are asked to provide the number of doses distributed. Yet the amounts provided by countries through the JRF accounted for approximately half of the doses documented in the 2023 analysis of Palache et al. (nearly 800 million influenza vaccine doses distributed globally in 2021) [18]. The doses reported in the JRF are a significant underestimation of total global influenza vaccine use as 44 countries that reported having influenza vaccines available to the JRF in 2022 did not provide any data on the number of doses distributed. In addition, with more complete data, we could stratify the doses distributed by the public or private sector. Particularly in LMICs, the availability of vaccines in the public sector is crucial for vaccine equity and access among the most vulnerable populations. Similarly, limited influenza vaccination coverage data are reported by countries through the JRF. Less than half of the countries that reported using seasonal influenza vaccines in 2022 provided coverage data that year. If collected and analysed, countries and WHO could use seasonal influenza vaccination coverage data to determine the burden of seasonal influenza-related hospitalizations and deaths averted by vaccination annually. Data collection and analysis can also help countries to understand uptake of the vaccine in recommended priority populations and measure programme improvements over time. WHO is developing technical guidance on monitoring and reporting influenza vaccination in response to countries' challenges in reporting data.

This manuscript encourages countries and global health stakeholders to focus on strengthening monitoring and reporting of seasonal influenza vaccination programmes. Monitoring, reporting, and tracking influenza vaccination coverage over time has been a consistent challenge. In particular, countries have expressed difficulties in accurately estimating the size of target population groups and tracking the number of people who have received a vaccine (particularly through the private sector) [25]. Difficulties with both the numerators (number of people vaccinated) and denominators (number of people in the priority group) are visible in the wide range of coverage rates reported which frequently range from less than 1 % to over 100 %. Given similarities in the priority groups for seasonal influenza vaccination and for COVID-19, there may be lessons that can be applied from the COVID-19 vaccination monitoring experience. In countries where both vaccination programmes exist, the denominator estimates used for COVID-19 may be able to be used directly by influenza vaccination programmes. Other countries have recently included seasonal influenza vaccination data in their electronic medical information systems [26]. While country-level work aims to provide robust, bottom-up numerator and denominator estimates, global COVID-19 methodologies or the development of a maturity matrix for seasonal influenza vaccination may offer a short- to medium-term solution to understanding influenza programme performance over time.

Conclusion

WHO encourages all countries to consider annual seasonal influenza vaccination for health workers, older adults, people with chronic conditions and pregnant women. As countries transition from childhood vaccination towards life course immunization programmes, influenza is a vaccine that can serve as a pathfinder for reaching and delivering vaccines to adult populations which strengthens national pandemic preparedness. More complete and higher quality influenza vaccination programme data, as well as use of WHO's technical tools and guidance, will enable countries to identify and respond to programmatic challenges and to monitor progress over time.
