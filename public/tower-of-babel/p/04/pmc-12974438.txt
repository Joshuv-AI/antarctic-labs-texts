Competing like cats and dogs: Cougars lose their hard-won prey to wolves
Authors: John D. C. Linnell
Published: 2026

The very core of ecology is the study of interactions between organisms. Most attention traditionally focused on the classical interactions like predation and exploitative competition as drivers of population dynamics and community structure. However, the last decades have seen a growing focus on more subtle and complex interactions, including those within taxonomic groups and functional guilds, such as the mammalian carnivores (1). Lethal intraguild interactions received a great deal of attention following documentation of their widespread nature (2) and their population-level impact on species like cheetahs Acinonyx jubatus (3). The potential impact of nonlethal interactions, through mechanisms such as loss of kills, or kleptoparasitism, also got early attention for species like African wild dogs Lycaon pictus (4). These studies drew attention to the potential importance of intraguild interactions as drivers of species distributions, densities, sociality, demography, and potential vulnerability to perturbations and extinction (1, 5, 6) in cases where there is both a strong asymmetry in body size (e.g., large carnivore kills mesocarnivore) and a similarity in body size (kleptoparasitism or killing of young among large carnivores). Other studies have underlined further interaction complexity within the carnivores, through mechanisms such as carrion provision and scavenging (6).

However, despite the constant increase in publications on the topic, good empirical quantifications of interactions between species remain sparse and are heavily skewed toward African ecosystems. Theory and concepts abound, but many potential patterns remain undocumented, or unquantified, which is hardly surprising given the low density and low visibility of apex predators in most ecosystems. In many ways, it is like studying the subtle interactions between ghosts.

The recently published study on wolf Canis lupus and cougar Puma concolor by Binder et al. (7) makes a significant addition to this literature. Their study from the northern Yellowstone ecosystem involved a massive amount of fieldwork under near-wilderness conditions. Nine years (2016-2024) of original data collection, the live-capture, collaring, and Global Positioning System (GPS) tracking of wolves and cougars, visiting almost 4,000 clusters in the field, and the subsequent investigation of >1,300 feeding events represents a truly impressive field effort to collect essential empirical data. This was combined with earlier data (1998-2005) from the same ecosystem. The resulting data permitted robust analysis of the interactions between wolves and cougars at kills across a range of habitats.

Their findings documented that the relationship between wolves and cougars is strongly asymmetric. First, wolves kill cougars, although cougars were never documented to kill wolves. Second, they demonstrate that wolves are actively attracted to areas where cougars have made a kill, whereas cougars avoid areas where wolves have made a kill. Third, they demonstrated that almost all direct interactions (measured indirectly via GPS-collar locations from both species at the same location at the same time) involved wolves at cougar kills and almost never cougars at wolf kills. In sum, these results showed clear evidence of kleptoparasitism, with wolves actively seeking out and displacing cougars from their kills.

The results also showed that the chances of wolves displacing a cougar from its kill were very dependent on the prey size. Cougars were more often displaced when feeding on kills of the larger elk Cervus canadensis than on kills of the smaller deer Odocoileus sp. Because cougars tend to spend a longer time when consuming larger kills (in many cases several days), this means that there is more time for wolves to discover the larger kills. Cougar selection for the smaller prey provides a potential coexistence mechanism that reduces both exploitation and interference competition with wolves. A final coexistence mechanism that may reduce intraguild mortality was an increased selection for rugged terrain by cougars when wolves were close. It is not without reason that cougars are often named mountain lions, showing a clear affinity for rugged terrain as a refuge to escape from both humans and wolves. This paper shows the adaptive value of such terrain and underlines the importance that habitat heterogeneity can have on predator–prey and intraguild interactions (8).

During much of the 20th century, cougars were among the most widespread of the large carnivores in North America, but now as wolf recovery continues, it is clear that they will face competition in both exploitative and interference forms. They will also have to face similar kleptoparasitism from black Ursus americanus and grizzly bears Ursus arctos (9). The combined impact of these interactions may potentially start to impact cougar behavior and demographics as well as the overall impact of predators on the prey communities (10, 11). These interactions between a large solitary felid predator and other more dominant, group living, or opportunistic predators may well be widespread, with similar examples emerging among leopard and Eurasian lynx populations (12, 13) in Africa and Europe, respectively. These studies have also shown the importance of behavioral mechanisms adopted by solitary felids to cache their prey in ways that minimize kleptoparasitism and prey loss to scavengers (12, 14, 15).

A final implication emerges from this study with respect to a current trend in both ecology and conservation to move beyond species and focus instead on guilds or functional groups (16) such as “large carnivores” or “large herbivores.”

These decades of work have led to various, nonexclusive, narratives about the nature of interactions within mammalian carnivores. Prugh and Sivy (6) drew attention to the “enemies with benefits” narrative where scavenging opportunities may have outweighed the risks. In this study, Binder et al. (7) draw attention to its corollary, the “enemies without benefits” narrative.

The one additional layer of interaction that this study only hints at is the role of humans as superpredators which can effect both wolves and cougars through mortality and disturbance. The way in which wolves and cougars, as well as elk and deer, differentially respond to humans is likely to have a major role in shaping their interactions in landscapes with less wilderness attributes than Yellowstone. Even in this remote setting, dominated by protected areas, human mortality dominated among causes of death for both of their study species. Based on what is known about these species’ relationships to humans, it seems safe to predict that the cougar-deer dyad is likely to thrive more than the wolf-elk dyad close to humans.

A final implication emerges from this study with respect to a current trend in both ecology and conservation to move beyond species and focus instead on guilds or functional groups (16) such as “large carnivores” or “large herbivores.” While the logic of this trend is make conservation more efficient, it may have the undesired effect of masking complex relationships between species within, and between, these groups (11). This will hamper both conservation planning and wildlife management of these species groups, which are often dependent on active management because of their interest to, and impact on, diverse human stakeholder groups. As we learn more about the complexity, and possible contexuality, of ecological interactions an interesting question emerges. Does accumulated knowledge lead us closer to making ecology into a good predictive science, or does it actually underline how challenging this task may be?
