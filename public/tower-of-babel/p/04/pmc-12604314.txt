Association between cardiac radiation dose and coronary artery calcification progression in breast cancer patients after radiotherapy: the modifying role of concomitant systemic inflammation (BACCARAT study)
Authors: Médéa Locquet, Georges Tarlet, David Broggio, Gaëlle Jimenez, Jérémy Camilleri, Matthieu Lapeyre, Jean Ferrières, Fabien Milliat, Sophie Jacob
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Radiation-induced cardiotoxicity remains a significant concern in breast cancer (BC) patients treated with radiotherapy (RT), with both cardiac radiation exposure and systemic inflammation emerging as key contributors to early cardiovascular complications. This study investigated whether cardiac radiation dose triggers independently or synergistically with systemic inflammation, characterized by C-reactive protein (CRP) elevation to predict coronary artery calcification (CAC) progression in BC survivors. We analyzed 101 chemotherapy-naïve BC women who underwent cardiac CT before and 24 months after RT, with dosimetric analysis of left ventricle (LV) radiation exposure. Sustained CRP elevation was defined as increased CRP at both end of RT and 6 months post-RT. CAC progression was observed in 28 patients (27.7%). Both sustained CRP elevation (OR = 3.42; 95% CI: 1.12–10.5) and mean LV dose (OR = 1.20 per Gy; 95% CI: 1.03–1.40) were independently associated with CAC progression. Although no statistically significant interaction was observed, stratified analyses showed a stronger association between mean LV dose and CAC progression among patients with sustained inflammation (OR = 1.39, 95%CI: 1.07–1.81), whereas this association was weaker and non-significant in patients without systemic inflammation (OR = 1.12, non-significant). The combined model incorporating both predictors showed improved discriminative performance (AUC = 0.733). These findings suggest that systemic inflammation may amplify the effect of cardiac irradiation and that combining inflammatory and dosimetric data improves early prediction of CAC risk. This may help identify high-risk subgroups who could benefit from enhanced cardiovascular monitoring or preventive strategies after BC RT.

Background

Radiotherapy (RT) for breast cancer (BC) can lead to long-term cardiac complications [1]and early forms of radiation-induced cardiotoxicity are now an emerging concern [2]. Recent studies suggest that both cardiac radiation exposure and systemic inflammation contribute to early coronary artery disease progression, ultimately increasing the long-term risk of atherosclerosis and further life-threatening coronary heart diseases [3]. We previously showed that mean left ventricle (LV) dose was a strong predictor of coronary artery calcification (CAC) progression within the 2 years post-RT, with a 15% increased risk per additional Gy [4]. Separately, we demonstrated that systemic inflammation, measured by sustained CRP elevation at the end of RT and at 6 months post-RT, was associated with a 3.5-fold higher risk of CAC progression compared with patients with decreasing CRP levels [5]. However, the combined role of these two risk factors remains unclear. Understanding whether systemic inflammation and cardiac radiation independently contribute to or interact in promoting CAC progression could help refine cardiovascular risk stratification in breast cancer survivors. This study aims to determine whether sustained CRP elevation and mean LV dose are independent predictors of CAC progression, or whether inflammation modifies or amplifies the effect of cardiac radiation. Such findings may support more personalized strategies for cardiovascular monitoring and prevention in post-RT breast cancer care.

Methods

The BACCARAT cohort (Breast Cancer and Cardiotoxicity Induced by RadioTherapy, NCT02605512) included 101 BC chemo-naïve women treated with RT followed in primary prevention. CAC was evaluated by cardiac CT prior to RT and 2 years after RT, with Agatston scores quantified by a single radiologist blinded to time of scanning and laterality of RT. “CAC progression” was defined as any increase in CAC at 2 years post RT compared to baseline [4]. CRP was measured by multiplex assay (custom-built kits from R&D systems, Bio-Techne®) at baseline (before RT), at the end of RT, at 6- and 24-months post-RT. “Sustained CRP elevation” was defined as increased CRP both at the end of RT and at 6 months post-RT [5]. The whole heart and cardiac substructures radiation dose evaluation was previously detailed [6], and we focused here on LV dose that was previously identified as strong predictor of CAC progression.

The relationship between LV radiation dose, sustained CRP elevation status, and the risk of CAC progression was assessed using multivariable logistic regression, yielding odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Confounders were included in the models, as previously identified as risk factors of CAC progression, including age, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular treatment use, statin therapy, and smoking status [4, 5]. Models were run in the overall BC cohort and further stratified by sustained CRP elevation status. Interaction between sustained CRP elevation and LV dose was tested (Wald test). Logistic regressions were also used to estimate the predicted probability of CAC progression for each individual. Predicted probabilities were obtained from the fitted model and used to evaluate the model’s discriminative performance using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Area under the curves (AUC) with corresponding 95% CI were calculated for each model. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS® v.9.4 and R v.4.5.0. A 2-sided p-value < 0.05 was set as the statistically significant threshold.

Results

The study population comprised 101 BC patients with a mean age of 58.4 (± 8.1) years.

In BC patients with CAC progression, the median CRP levels were 1.58 mg/L [percentile 25 (P25): 0.62 - percentile 75 (P75): 3.31] before RT, 1.77 mg/L [0.55–5.47] at the end of RT, and 1.56 mg/L [0.68–5.91] 6 months post-RT. In BC patients without CAC progression, the values were 1.11 mg/L [0.38–2.21], 1.05 mg/L [0.45–2.82], and 1.13 mg/L [0.42–3.45], respectively. There were 28 (27.7%) BC patients with a CAC score progression 2 years after RT. Sustained CRP elevation and mean LV dose were significantly associated with CAC progression when considered in separate models (Table 1). We observed no correlation between LV dose and sustained CRP elevation status (p-value = 0.99; data not shown). In the combined model, both inflammation and LV dose remained significantly associated with CAC progression with stronger risks (OR = 3.42 (1.12–10.5) and OR = 1.20 (1.03–1.40), respectively). The combined model AUC of 0.733 (0.616–0.850) exceeded that of separate models, demonstrating superior prognostic accuracy when considering both inflammation and cardiac dose. No statistically significant interaction between inflammation and LV dose was observed (p-value = 0.19). Nevertheless, stratified analyses showed that among BC patients with sustained CRP elevation, mean LV dose was significantly associated with CAC progression with a strengthened OR of 1.39 (1.07–1.81). In contrast, for BC patients without such systemic inflammation, this association was weaker and non-significant (OR = 1.12, p-value = 0.37). Discussion

This is the first study to demonstrate the enhanced predictive value of combining information on systemic inflammatory status and cardiac radiation exposure. Our results show that when both sustained elevated CRP levels and radiation exposure to the left ventricle were considered together, the combined model provided the best overall predictive ability to differentiate between patients with or without CAC progression, as characterized by the highest area under the ROC curve (AUC = 0.733 [0.616–0.850]). Such models could be helpful in stratifying individuals into groups of risk for future occurrence of coronary atherosclerosis, but they remain to be evaluated.

Interestingly, we observed that some patients exhibited sustained systemic inflammation at the end of RT until 6 months post-RT, even in the absence of high cardiac radiation exposure. We observed that post-RT inflammation was not determined by radiation dose to the heart (absence of correlation between CRP and LV dose), but it may reflect individual susceptibility. Several factors could contribute to this inflammatory response, including baseline cardiovascular risk factors such as obesity, diabetes, smoking, or metabolic syndrome, all of which are known to be associated with chronic low-grade inflammation. Additionally, concurrent treatments such as endocrine therapy may further amplify systemic inflammation. Genetic or epigenetic predispositions influencing inflammatory pathways may also play a role. These findings highlight the importance of considering systemic inflammation as both an independent risk factor and a potential modifier in studies assessing radiation-induced cardiovascular effects.

Moreover, we observed that sustained elevated CRP levels could enhance the radiation dose effect leading to coronary calcification, as suggested elsewhere [7]. Indeed, our analysis showed that the association between LV dose and CAC progression was significant in BC patients with sustained elevated CRP levels but not in patients without sustained elevated CRP levels. Early inflammation could play a key role in developing radiotherapy-induced calcification and may act as a catalyst for calcification processes. Although the formal test for interaction between systemic inflammation and mean LV dose was not statistically significant (p = 0.19), stratified analyses suggested a potential effect modification. Specifically, the association between LV dose and CAC progression was stronger and statistically significant in patients with elevated CRP, but weaker and non-significant in those without. This apparent discrepancy may be due to limited statistical power to detect interaction effects, particularly given the sample size and number of events. Therefore, while we cannot confirm a statistically significant interaction, the stratified findings raise the possibility that systemic inflammation may amplify the cardiotoxic impact of radiation exposure. This warrants further investigation in larger cohorts with sufficient power to robustly assess interaction effects.

Further research is needed regarding the underlying mechanisms, such as hypothesizing that inflammation makes tissues more sensitive to radiation effects as suggested elsewhere [8], increasing the risk of vascular issues like coronary calcification, while patients without increased inflammation may have less damage and calcification progression [9]. We found no association between sustained elevated CRP levels and cardiac dosimetry, which is consistent with previous studies [10, 11].

This study has several limitations that must be considered. Our study’s relatively small sample limits generalizability. Nevertheless, it remains among the largest on early CAC progression in BC patients post-RT. The defined endpoint, any CAC score increase at 2 years, was highly sensitive, possibly overestimating real clinical progression, but susceptible to underestimating the magnitude of the yielded effect sizes. Nevertheless, any increase in CAC score accurately reflects coronary plaque and increased heart disease risk, particularly when measured using dedicated CAC scans and Agatston scoring as standard. Additionally, the potential for residual confounding cannot be ruled out. For example, the impact of ethnicity on our results could not be evaluated as such information was not collected. Nevertheless, adjustments for key cardiovascular risk factors and the stability of the associations observed in the BACCARAT cohort suggest that this limitation is unlikely to alter the interpretation of the main findings. Finally, the absence of a cancer-free control group did not allow a direct comparison of CAC progression with the general population. While the longitudinal paired design strengthens internal validity by allowing each BC patient to represent her own control, the lack of an external reference group could reduce the generalizability of our findings.

In conclusion, these findings support the relevance of integrating inflammatory status with cardiac dosimetry to improve early risk prediction for radiation-induced coronary damage in breast cancer patients. Moreover, this study suggests that systemic inflammation may amplify the cardiac radiation dose effects. Patients with both high LV dose and sustained inflammation may represent a subgroup at particularly elevated cardiovascular risk who could benefit from closer follow-up with cardiac imaging, early lifestyle and possible pharmacological interventions, such as statins or anti-inflammatory therapies.
