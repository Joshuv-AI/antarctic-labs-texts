Exploratory analysis of infrapatellar fat pad MRI-based radiomics for detection of knee structure abnormalities in collegiate basketball players and swimmers
Authors: Jack Consolini, Kevin M Koch, Alissa J Burge, Erwin Xia, Sharmila Majumdar, Garry E Gold, Hollis G Potter, Matthew F Koff
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMagnetic resonance imaging (MRI) based radiomic evaluation of the infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP) has been shown to predict knee osteoarthritis. As IPFP abnormalities can arise from sport-related injuries, this study evaluated whether qualitatively identified knee structure abnormalities in young athletes can be detected by IPFP MRI-based radiomics.DesignBilateral knee MRIs using Dixon fat-water decomposition techniques were obtained from 46 NCAA Division 1 collegiate 46 basketball players (26 male, 20 female) and 21 swimmers (10 male, 11 female). Board-certified musculoskeletal radiologists evaluated anatomic features and patellar height using the modified Noyes score and Caton-Deschamps index. IPFP volumes were segmented, and fat fraction was computed. Radiomic features were calculated within 2D overlapping patches extracted from the IPFP in the fat and water images separately. Cross-validated logistic regression models were developed using IPFP radiomic features as predictors of an athlete’s sport and the occurrence of cartilage lesions, tendinopathy, or bone abnormalities as observed on MRI. Mann-Whitney U tests evaluated differences in fat fraction between sports and knee structure abnormalities.ResultsThe area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC; maximum 0.79) indicated that IPFP radiomics from fat-only images can differentiate between basketball and swimming athletes. Tendinopathy was identified (AUC = 0.68 ± 0.05) at larger patch sizes. Qualitative radiological assessments of cartilage lesions and bone abnormalities were not distinguished (AUC < 0.57). Fat fraction did not differ across sports or knee structure abnormality (p > 0.49, mean difference < 0.5%).ConclusionsIn the absence of inflammatory arthropathy, IPFP radiomics discriminated between sports but not cartilage lesions, tendinopathy, or bone abnormalities, suggesting structural adaptation to sport-specific loading. Abnormal IPFP signal intensity may not present in young athletes without traumatic injury or knee arthroscopy.Keywords: Infrapatellar fat pad, MRI, Radiomics, Basketball, Knee structure abnormalities

Introduction

Athletes participating in high impact sports, such as basketball, soccer, football, ice hockey, and wrestling, are more likely to develop osteoarthritis (OA) [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]] than athletes in low impact sports, such as swimming [2]. Basketball athletes are at an increased risk of developing patellar-femoral injuries [2,[7], [8], [9]] and early stage knee OA [6,7,[9], [10], [11], [12]] due to the repeated cutting and jumping maneuvers that produce ground reaction forces significantly higher than in other sports [13,14]. Patellar articular cartilage lesions, infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP) edema, patellar tendinopathy, and quadriceps tendinopathy are among the most common injuries for these athletes and may exacerbate the onset of anterior knee OA [2,7,10,11,15]. Therefore, interest has increased in developing methods to characterize early contributors to knee OA, with particular interest in the health of the IPFP.

IPFP dysfunction has been implicated as an early indicator of knee OA development and anterior knee damage [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is routinely used to assess IPFP inflammatory response to injury and an individual’s risk of developing knee OA [7,[9], [10], [11]]. Quantitative evaluations of the IPFP including fat fraction and T2* [20,21], signal intensity [22,23], and radiomic outcome variables [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]] have been shown to improve diagnosis of lesions and fibrosis. Radiomic analyses are of growing interest, as radiomic features, or image texture features [30], provide a quantitative assessment of signal intensity spatial characteristics beyond what mean signal intensity, average fat fraction, or T2* measurements can provide. By evaluating the spatial signal patterns, radiomic outcome variables provide descriptive metrics of underlying anatomical architecture; i.e., uniformity, directionality, patterning, and shape throughout a region of interest [30,31]. Though IPFP radiomic classification models have reliably identified fat pad fibrosis [21], post-anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction induced OA [29], and the presence of OA [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28]] in aged individuals, no study to date has evaluated the IPFP of athletes. Given the potential for basketball players to develop OA, we examined whether IPFP MRI-based radiomic features differ between NCAA Division 1 collegiate basketball (high impact) and swimming (low impact) athletes, and whether the IPFP radiomic differences are concomitant with radiological identification of cartilage lesions, tendinopathy, and bone abnormalities. Swimming athletes were selected as a comparison group due to their high level of physical conditioning and structured training, coupled with substantially lower joint loading. This contrast provides a stronger context for isolating the specific musculoskeletal adaptations associated with basketball. We hypothesized that IPFP radiomics would be capable of classifying knees by sport, and that alterations in IPFP signal would be associated with knee structure abnormality.

Methods

Enrollment: This was a multi-institutional cohort study (Level of Evidence III). All human subjects gave informed consent to participate in accordance with local institutional review board (IRB) approval at the Hospital for Special Surgery (New York, NY) and Stanford University (Stanford, CA). The procedures followed in the current work were in accordance with the ethical standards of the committee responsible for human experimentation (institutional and national) and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2000.

Subject enrollment included 67 NCAA Division 1 collegiate athletes: 26 male basketball players (age, 18.8 ± 0.6 years [mean ± SD], body mass index [BMI]: 23.3 ± 1.7 kg/m2) and 20 female basketball players (age,: 18.7 ± 1.0 years, BMI: 23.4 ± 2.8 kg/m2), 10 male swimmers (age,18.6 ± 0.7 years, BMI: 25.1 ± 2.3 kg/m2) and 11 female swimmers (age, 18.5 ± 0.5 years, BMI: 22.3 ± 1.6 kg/m2) (Table 1). Bilateral knee MRI examinations were performed between September 2017 and October 2019.

MRI Acquisition: Bilateral MRIs examinations were performed on clinical 3T scanners (GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI) using an 8-channel phased array knee coil (Invivo, Gainesville, FL). Morphologic imaging sequences were acquired, including a sagittal two-dimensional (2D) fast-spin-echo (FSE) acquisition with frequency-selective fat saturation, and a sagittal three-dimensional (3D) CUBE PD Isotropic Flex (2-point Dixon) acquisition [32,33] which generated in-phase and out-of-phase images from which separate fat and water images were derived (Fig. 1, a).

Image acquisition and processing pipeline. a) 3D CUBE Flex acquisition with fat and water images created from the in-phase and out-of-phase images. Manual segmentation of 49 IPFPs and training of semantic segmentation model for b) automatic segmentation of the IPFPs of the remaining knees, with manual editing as needed. c) Computation of fat fraction via percent difference of fat image signal and the combined fat-water signal. d) Radiomic feature extraction from IPFPs using PyRadiomics and development of classification models for sport and knee structure abnormalities.

The acquisition parameters for the 2D FSE images were: echo time (TE): 40ms, repetition time (TR): 4000ms, field of view (FOV): 16cm, acquisition matrix: 512 (frequency) x 384 (phase), receiver bandwidth (RBW): ± 41.7kHz, with a resulting voxel size (VS) of: 0.3125×0.4167×3mm3. Parameters for the 3D CUBE Flex sequence were: TE: 18ms, TR: 1200ms, FOV: 16cm, acquisition matrix: 224×224, RBW: ± 62.5kHz, with a resulting in VS: 0.7×0.7×0.7mm3. Seven left knees were excluded from the analysis due to poor image quality, leaving 66 right and 59 left knees for subsequent analysis (Table 1).

Clinical Assessment of MRIs: A board-certified musculoskeletal radiologist with over 30 years’ experience (HGP) evaluated patellar, femoral, and tibial cartilage lesions using a modified Noyes scoring system which was validated via direct arthroscopic evaluation of articular surfaces [34]. This system has high reproducibility with a reported kappa value of 0.93 [32]. The modified Noyes score evaluated cartilage signal abnormality (mild or extensive hyperintensity), surface chondral damage (<50% or >50%), and status of the subchondral bone (intact or eroded). Lesions measuring ≥15mm were assigned twice the points of lesions measuring 10–14mm due to the greater area of surface damage; lesions <10mm were assigned no points as they have been found not to be clinically significant [34]. Cartilage was assessed in the medial and lateral compartments of the femur and tibia as well as the femoral trochlea and patella. Cartilage scoring was dichotomized into “normal” and “abnormal” groups, with any score above 0 qualifying as abnormal for subsequent statistical analysis.

The images were reviewed for bone marrow edema at the femoral condyles and tibial plateaus, and quantified as absent, mild (<10 mm2), or severe (≥10 mm2). Posterior cruciate and collateral ligaments were assessed as intact, partially torn, or completely torn. The popliteus, patellar, and quadriceps tendons were assessed for acute or chronic tendinopathy and partial or complete tearing. The presence of medial and/or lateral meniscal tears was evaluated using the morphologic images and scored (modified WORMS [35]) as intact (0) or torn (1 = horizontal cleavage or peripheral longitudinal tear; or 2 = bucket handle, radial, or displaced flap). The presence of osteophytes and their severity was assessed (1 = small [<2 mm]; 2 = large [>2 mm]). The presence of subchondral sclerosis, tumor, infection, and fracture was recorded. Joint effusion was evaluated as the percentage of maximum joint distention and scored as none, <33%, 33%–66%, or 67%–100%. Fat pad synovitis was not evaluated because no knees had inflammatory arthropathy. Similar to cartilage scoring, the occurrence of tendinopathy and bone abnormalities (osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis, and bone marrow edema) was grouped and dichotomized into “normal” and “abnormal” categories (any score >0 = abnormal) for subsequent statistical analysis. An additional board-certified musculoskeletal radiologist with over 16 years of experience (AJB) evaluated the presence of patellar alta and baja by determining patellar height with the Caton-Deschamps Index [36].

Radiomic Analysis:

IPFP Segmentation: The entire IPFP volume was segmented from the 3D CUBE Flex fat and water images of all knees (Table 1). To facilitate image segmentation, a DeepLabv3+ convolutional neural network was developed (Mathworks, Natick, MA) and trained on 49 manually segmented IPFPs from randomly selected subjects (across sex, sport, and laterality), to automatically segment the remaining IPFPs. Manual segmentations were performed by a single observer (JC) and reviewed by a board-certified musculoskeletal radiologist with 8 years of experience (EX) prior to model training. Automatic segmentations were manually edited as needed [37] (Fig. 1, b).

Image Patch Extraction: Fat and water image slices were split into a uniform grid of element sizes: [16 × 16, 32 × 32, 48 × 48, 64 × 64, 80 × 80, 96 × 96] pixels. Two-dimensional image patches of the six sizes, with square patch areas of 25, 100, 225, 400, 625, 900 mm2, were extracted from each slice, permitted only when 50% of the patch area included IPFP tissue. The original patch grid was perturbed by half the patch side length in the horizontal, vertical, and both directions. Multi-directional 50% overlap ensured dense coverage of the IPFP and captured boundary information (Fig. 2, a-b). Patch distributions for each class and patch size are reported in Table 2.

Radiomic analysis and classification modeling pipeline. a) Uniform overlapping patches of varying sizes were extracted from the segmented IPFP. b) Valid patches were permitted for radiomic analysis if 50% of the patch area contained fat pad tissue. c) The PyRadiomics toolbox was utilized to compute 96 2D radiomic features for each patch. Radiomic classification of athlete sport, cartilage lesions, tendinopathy, and bone abnormalities was evaluated by class-balanced cross-validated logistic regression. For each patch size, image type, and classification task, d) principal component analysis was employed to reduce dimensionality in the radiomic feature set while retaining 95% of the described variance. e) Stratified five-fold cross validation was applied to the transformed data to compute the optimal regularization parameter. Optimal model performance was evaluated via an additional stratified five-fold training procedure, from which f) the area under the receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) was extracted for each fold. Final model performance was summarized by mean ± SD AUC values.

Computation of Radiomic Features: Radiomic outcome variables were extracted from the 2D fat and water image patches using the open-source PyRadiomics (v3.0) Python package [31] (Fig. 2, c). Prior to radiomic feature extraction, z-score normalization was used to standardize image intensity within each patch. For each two-dimensional patch, the gray-level co-occurrence, size zone, run length, dependence, and neighboring tone difference matrices were computed and used to derive 69 higher-order radiomic features.

Twenty-four features were extracted from the gray-level co-occurrence matrix, which captures how often pairs of pixel intensities occur adjacent to each other in specific directions (horizontal, vertical, and diagonal). These features characterize local texture and spatial relationships. Sixteen features from the gray-level size zone matrix quantified the size distribution of connected regions (zones) sharing the same gray level, independently of direction or shape. Thirteen features from the gray-level run-length matrix measured how often and how long consecutive pixels of the same intensity appeared in specific directions. Redundant run length features, such as gray level nonuniformity, were excluded because they overlapped with the gray-level size zone matrix.

The gray-level dependence matrix contributed 12 features that quantified how strongly neighboring pixel intensities depended on the central pixel, reflecting local similarity. Two dependence-matrix features, gray-level non-uniformity and its normalized form, were excluded to prevent redundancy with first order and size zone matrix features. Four features were calculated from the neighborhood gray tone difference matrix, which compares each pixel’s intensity with the average intensity of its surrounding pixels to assess local variation. The contrast feature from this matrix was excluded because it was similar to the contrast measure derived from the gray-level co-occurrence matrix

In addition to the 69 2D texture features, 18 first-order statistical metrics, such as mean, range, root mean squared, and kurtosis, and nine 2D shape features, such as elongation, flatness, sphericity, roundness, mean, were computed and included within classification modeling. A total of 96 radiomic outcome variables were extracted from each patch and utilized within classification modeling.

The relationships between each radiomic feature and sport and cartilage lesions, tendinopathy, and bone abnormalities were explored using rank-biserial correlation effect size [38]. For each image type, radiomic features were extracted from all patches and grouped by 1) sport (SW = 0, BB = 1), 2) cartilage lesions (normal = 0, abnormal = 1), 3) tendinopathy (normal = 0, abnormal = 1), and 4) bone abnormalities (normal = 0, abnormal = 1). Separate Mann-Whitney U tests were used to determine whether feature value distributions differed significantly between each classification group. The resulting U-statistic was used to calculate the rank-biserial correlation coefficient (Rrb), quantifying the strength and direction of group differences. A 95% confidence interval for Rrb was estimated using nonparametric bootstrapping with 1,000 resamples. Class distinctions were preserved during resampling to maintain the data structure. Cohen’s conventional thresholds [39] were applied to categorize effects. Rank-biserial correlation coefficients above 0.50 indicated radiomic features had a large effect on the class, while values between 0.50 and 0.30 had a medium effect and values below 0.30 had a small to negligible effect. Feature effect sizes, along with a 95% confidence interval, were evaluated along the U-statistic and p-value.

Sport and Knee Structure Abnormality Classification: Binary classification models were created to evaluate whether IPFP MRI-based radiomic features derived from fat and water images can distinguish between 1) sport (SW = 0, BB = 1), 2) cartilage lesions (normal = 0, abnormal = 1), 3) tendinopathy (normal = 0, abnormal = 1), and 4) bone abnormalities (normal = 0, abnormal = 1). The four classification tasks were evaluated independently of each other, each patch size, and image contrast. This approach was utilized as an insufficient number of knees were available for a numerically stable classification model for simultaneous predictions of sport, cartilage lesions, tendinopathy, and bone abnormalities. While radiomics of the fat and water images were evaluated separately, model performance was compared across contrasts.

Cross-validated logistic regression models were implemented using the scikit-learn machine learning Python library [40,41]. Prior to model fitting, radiomic outcome variables were standardized by removing the mean and scaling to unit variance. Dimensionality reduction was achieved through principal component analysis (PCA) to condense the feature space while retaining 95% of the full set’s explained variance (Fig. 2, d). Logistic regression with L2 regularization served as the primary classification algorithm, implemented via LogisticRegressionCV in scikit-learn. The optimal hyperparameters, including regularization strength, were tuned via stratified five-fold cross-validation (Fig. 2, e). Class-balanced five-fold cross-validation evaluated the optimal model’s performance while maintaining strict independence between training and test sets (Fig. 2, e). AUC was obtained for each fold, of which the mean and standard deviation AUC value was used to summarize model performance (Fig. 2, f). AUC values range from 0.0 to 1.0, with values above 0.7 providing acceptable discrimination, above 0.8 good, and above 0.9 excellent. AUC of around 0.5 provides no discrimination between classes, while values below 0.5 indicate the classifier is consistently misclassifying knees, worse than random guessing.

Fat Fraction Analysis: The fat fraction was calculated on a voxel-by-voxel basis by dividing the fat signal by the sum of the signal intensity of the fat and water images (Fig. 1, c). An average fat fraction was calculated from all voxels within a region of interest. Mann-Whitney U tests were used to detect statistical differences in fat fraction between sports and knee structure abnormalities. Results outside of a 95% confidence interval (p<0.05) were considered significant.

Radiomic Feature Correlation to Classifiers: Rank-biserial correlation effect sizes based on the Mann Whitney U-statistic were calculated for each radiomic feature. Across all patches extracted from fat and water images, only three features derived from 900 mm2 fat image patches demonstrated significantly large correlations with sport classification. Size zone nonuniformity (p<0.001, Rrb=-0.53 [-0.57, -0.48]), small area emphasis (p<0.001, Rrb=-0.52 [-0.56, -0.47]), and small area low gray level emphasis (p<0.001, Rrb = -0.52 [-0.57, -0.48]) were significantly larger in basketball player knees. No radiomic features exhibited large effect sizes, regardless of statistical significance, when classified by the presence or absence of cartilage lesions, tendinopathy, or bone abnormalities.

Sport and Knee Structure Abnormality Classification Performance: IPFP MRI-based radiomic classification performance of athlete sport and the presence of cartilage lesions, tendinopathy, and bone abnormalities was evaluated using 2D patches extracted from DIXON fat and water images. As expected, the number of valid patches decreased from 618248 at an initial patch area of 25 mm2 to 5632 at a final area of 900 mm2 across classes (Table 2). Across patch sizes, class distributions were consistent: 30 ± 1.6% of patches showed abnormal cartilage, 78 ± 0.2% showed abnormal tendon, and 26 ± 0.9% showed abnormal bone grades. As patch size increased, patches from swimmer IPFPs accounted for less of the total patch count, decreasing from 16% to 10% at the largest patch size. Total patch counts were indicative of underlying imbalances in classification groups; across all classification tasks, the class with fewer knees never accounted for more than 34% of the total patches. Class imbalances emphasize the necessity of applying class-balancing methods within model training.

Across all classes, image contrasts, and patch sizes, PCA reduced the number of radiomic-based principal components from 96 to 13 – 17 features (Table 4). In the fat images, increasing patch size led to a steady reduction of components required to retain over 95% of the full set’s explained variance, while the water images required 16 – 17 principal components across all sizes. Substantial reduction of dimensionality implies a high degree of redundancy in the radiomic feature sets.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the utility of IPFP MRI-based radiomic features for detection of qualitative assessments of knee structure abnormalities among NCAA Division I collegiate basketball players and swimmers. While radiomics successfully classified athletes by sport across all patch sizes and image contrasts, it failed to distinguish between normal and abnormal knees.

The observed radiomic differences between sports, in the absence of cartilage, tendon, and bone structure-related differences, suggest that IPFP architectural changes occur under high-impact loading conditions in young athletes. High-impact sports have been associated with increased articular cartilage and fat pad abnormalities as competition level increases [3], confirmed with MRI examination of former collegiate [10] and professional [9,11] athletes. However, the current cohort showed no abnormal IPFP signal, as measured through synovial grading, accompanying the cartilage changes. Despite the high prevalence of patellar tendinopathy (61.6% of knees) in basketball players, accounting for nearly all knee tendon abnormalities (Table 3), IPFP MRI-based radiomics could not classify abnormal knees, suggesting that radiomics detected nonpathological sports-related adaptations.

Loading-related changes in the fat pad of young collegiate athletes (age ∼19 years) have not been reported in the literature. Prior radiomic classification models have discriminated fat pad fibrosis/signal alternations in elderly individuals (age > 60 years) with and without knee OA [[24], [25], [26]], with the exception of two studies. The first study reported that IPFP radiomic changes indicative of tissue structure nonuniformity are associated with post-traumatic OA 10 years after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction [29]. The second study evaluated changes in IPFP MRI-based radiomic features between patients with and without patellar-femoral pain [42] and found that the texture features could not distinguish between the two groups. Considering the current results in light of previous findings, IPFP MRI-based radiomics likely do not differ in younger individuals in the absence of traumatic injury or knee arthroscopy. This aligns with the surgical history of the athletes: one patient had undergone ligament reconstruction, and none had meniscal repair or reconstructive surgery, supporting that differences across sports reflect play-related adaptations.

Qualitative evaluation of randomly selected fat pads with the fat images revealed subtle differences: basketball players’ IPFPs displayed more variable signal near the distal femoral condyle (Fig. 3, Left), while swimmers had more frequent dots of fat signal dropout (Fig. 3, Right). Fat absences are indicative of fibrotic lesions and scarring often found in patients with knee OA [21,43]; however, as all athlete fat fractions were well above those with IPFP fibrosis and knee OA [44] it is unlikely that the observed striations are indicative of early OA progression. Given the low occurrence cartilage abnormalities (Table 3) and fat fraction consistency across sports and knee structure abnormality classes (Table 5), radiomics may be detecting non-pathological play-related changes in the spatial organization of adipose tissue. These findings are further supported by the rank-biserial analysis, in which three gray level size zone matrix features significantly differed between basketball players and swimmers. These features are sensitive to the size and distribution of homogeneous signal zones, and are likely capable of detecting subtle differences in IPFP MRI signal organization between sports that are not apparent through fat fraction measurements or qualitative review.

Difference in fat distribution throughout the fat pad of a male basketball player (left) and male swimmer (right). The exact medial sagittal slice was extracted from the fat images. Observed differences appear consistent across knees, with basketball players appearing to have more variable signal near the distal end of the femoral condyle.

This study had several limitations. First, we acknowledge that the observed differences may have been influenced by cohort imbalance (46 basketball, 21 swimming) and patch distribution across sport classes. Class imbalances were controlled by training and evaluating model performance with stratified training and test sets; however, the larger IPFP of basketball players yielded more patches than that of swimmers. This imbalance became more pronounced with increasing patch size, as swimmer’s representation dropped from 16% to 10% of the total patch count (Table 2). Previous musculoskeletal radiomic studies have shown that larger patches better capture the spatial extent of the radiomic features [41], but come with the trade-off of decreasing availability. This likely contributed to the sudden improvement in tendinopathy classification at the largest patch size in the fat images, as basketball players accounted for 83% (64/77) of tendon abnormalities, which resulted in training the model to identify primarily basketball IPFPs from mostly swimmers. Similarly, improved sports classification performance might be attributed to the drop off in patches from swimmer IPFPs, leading the model to succeed in identifying specific subjects, rather than differentiating between patterns in fat pad signal. However, sport classification was nearly always above 0.7 AUC (except for the 25 mm2 patch) and with stable classification performance for cartilage lesions, tendinopathy, and bone abnormalities (excluding tendinopathy classification for the 900 mm2 fat-contrast patches), we believe patch size had minimal impact on model performance. While class-balancing techniques were employed, future studies should consider balanced cohorts to validate sport-related differences. Second, the current study only evaluated the effects of sport and knee structure abnormalities. Evaluating and comparing additional cohorts, such as non-athletic individuals, may show whether or not signal patterns of young athletes can be distinguished from aged knees and knees with OA. Finally, although traditional power analyses are not appropriate for high-dimensional radiomic modeling [41], future work should evaluate radiomic feature correlations and repeatability using ICC3 scores to identify reliable, independent features for modeling.

Conclusion

This study explored the utility of infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP) MRI-based radiomics for classifying collegiate athletes by sport and detecting the presence of knee structure abnormalities. Radiomic features from 2D patches across DIXON fat and water images successfully classified sport (AUC 0.67–0.79) but failed to detect cartilage lesions, tendinopathy, and bone abnormalities (AUC < 0.57), except for tendinopathy in large patches within the fat images—likely driven by the higher prevalence of patellar tendinopathy among basketball players. These findings suggest that in young athletes without traumatic injury or surgery, abnormal signals may be minimal or absent. While fat fraction did not differ between sports, radiomic features did capture differences in fat distribution, likely reflecting long-term play-related mechanical loading patterns. Future research should investigate radiomic changes in young athletes with ligament or meniscal reconstruction to assess early cartilage abnormalities. Overall, this study helps refine the target population for radiomic analyses, suggesting limited utility for early osteoarthritis detection in healthy collegiate athletes without chronic injury.
