A structured institutional framework for establishing a living allogenic valve transplant program
Authors: Muhammad Faateh, Muhammad Aanish Raees, Yacmet Colón Berríos, Amra Zekic, Hosam F Ahmed, Tara Karamlou, James F Cnota, Benjamin S Mantell, David LS Morales, Awais Ashfaq
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiving allogenic valve transplantation (LAVT) refers to transplantation of viable human heart valves in an orthotopic or heterotopic fashion and has recently garnered significant interest for children in need of a living, growing, regenerating valve replacement option. However, at present, there is no standardized approach for establishing and implementing such a program. We provide a practical, step-by-step blueprint of the operational, administrative and regulatory requirements needed to establish an LAVT program based on our center's experience.Keywords: Valve transplantation, Pediatric Heart Transplantation, Valvular heart disease, Congenital heart disease

Introduction

Living allogenic valve transplantation (LAVT) refers to transplantation of viable human heart valves in an orthotopic or heterotopic fashion.1, 2 Also called partial heart transplantation, LAVT is a groundbreaking advancement for children in need of growing and durable valve replacements.3, 4, 5 Aortic and pulmonary valves can be transplanted with potential for extension to atrioventricular valves.6 The first US pediatric LAVT was performed in 2022 on a 17-day old boy with truncus arteriosus; since then, >10 pediatric cases have been reported.1, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12

Barriers to the widespread adoption of LAVT include scarcity of donor hearts and logistical issues.3, 13, 14 Efforts are currently underway to address these.15, 16, 17 However, centers intending to start an LAVT program face several additional challenges as there are regulatory and administrative ambiguities unique to LAVT.18

We recently established an LAVT program at our center. We navigated through a variety of undefined administrative, operational, and regulatory requirements since there were no guidelines to start such a program. While each center intending to start an LAVT program will ultimately develop processes according to their local environment and regulations, our aim is to provide a comprehensive overview of these considerations. We outline a framework for the institutional processes to establish an LAVT program leveraged from best practices in congenital heart surgery, societal guidelines, tissue storage regulations, and clinical protocol development.19, 20, 21

Feasibility and institutional capability

The first consideration is evaluating institutional feasibility. In the future, LAVT may require the same level of resources and expertise as a conventional heart valve replacement. However, presently, LAVT requires levels of coordination, logistics and adherence to ischemic times that are almost similar to an orthotopic heart transplant (OHT).1 A good benchmark to assess institutional readiness to start an LAVT program may be inferred from a recent consensus statement endorsed by all major congenital cardiac societies which recommend that pediatric heart transplantation should be restricted to “comprehensive heart centers”.19 This statement outlines criteria including case volumes, infrastructure, and surgeon credentialing. There are important lessons to be learned from the rapid rise and subsequent poor outcomes with the Ross operation in the 1990s, when many centers performed the procedure without sufficient expertise or volume. Eventually improvement in outcomes came after regionalization and concentration of the Ross procedure in specialized, high-volume center with multidisciplinary expertise. Therefore, it may be prudent to consider whether LAVT should be limited to a “comprehensive heart centers” to foster high case volumes and maximize patient outcomes.

Administrative considerations

The next step is engaging the heart center leadership and hospital administration. LAVT is likely to be associated with increased costs due to additional monitoring, immunosuppression, and increased resource utilization for the procedure. While these costs could be mitigated by the potential benefits of LAVT, including fewer reinterventions and readmissions, and improved quality-of-life, the cost-effectiveness of LAVT relative to conventional valve replacements remains to be demonstrated. Case volume projections based on current valve replacement numbers and potential recipients can support discussions in developing reimbursement structures. Institutional endorsement of LAVT requires commitment from key stakeholders including cardiologists, cardiothoracic surgeons, nursing, pharmacy, intensive-care-unit, transplant teams and coordinators who represent a quintessential component for successful implementation of such a program.

Regulatory considerations for LAVT

Presently, LAVT falls outside the purview of United Network for Organ Sharing-Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (UNOS-OPTN) which oversees all solid organ transplant programs in the US.22 Food and Drug Administration’s (FDA) Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research recently stated that fresh allograft heart valves meet criteria for regulation under the FDA 21 CFR 1271.10(a) as human cells, tissues, and cellular and tissue-based products.20 The FDA requires donor selection and eligibility to be made by a “qualified” person and outlines infectious disease screening and documentation of the above. Another consideration is whether local institutional review board (IRB) rules deem LAVT as an experimental procedure, in which case, an IRB approved clinical trial protocol and oversight from a data safety monitoring board would be required.

Potential sources of donor hearts and allocation

Donor heart availability remains limited requiring allocation by a priority-based system managed by UNOS-OPTN. More than a third of pediatric donor hearts in the US (n=900) are not transplanted due to various reasons and could serve as a potential source for LAVT.23, 24 This may be achieved by collaborating directly with organ procurement organizations (OPO) similar to existing partnerships that valve companies maintain for cryopreserved homografts. We have experienced success in receiving offers by individually contacting and reminding all OPO’s across the US on a routine basis. This is indeed cumbersome and requires consistent time and effort highlighting the need for a “central” process for valve offers akin to organ transplant offers. This process could be mediated through UNOS-OPTN, similar to organ transplants, if UNOS added living valve transplants under their purview. The use of Organ Care System (Transmedics Inc) may be economically justifiable if donors are geographically distant and if centers would prefer to decrease the ischemic time before harvesting the valves. This approach may be more justifiable if the same heart could provide two semilunar valves for two different recipients. especially as one heart could be used as a source of two semilunar valves for two different recipients. Donation after cardiac death (DCD) donors can also be considered. The Domino approach, i.e. transplanting valves from a discarded heart of recipient of an OHT has been reported with success and offers several logistical advantages.7, 25 Valves from patients receiving a heart re-transplant or a total artificial heart could also be considered as another source.24

At our institution, allocation is primarily guided by anatomical match and clinical urgency by a multidisciplinary team. As case volumes and the size of valve transplant waitlist increases, local institutional policies should ensure equitable allocation among the center’s recipients based on severity of valve dysfunction, likelihood or mortality without intervention, time on the waitlist and lack of alternative treatment option. Allocation decisions should be made by a multidisciplinary team, at least until a centralized national allocation system for LAVT is established.

Storage and identification for LAVT

To ensure compliance with federal regulations for proper documentation and identification a unique tissue ID should be assigned to each valve upon harvest. A storage document detailing the characteristics of the donor tissue should be completed by the procurement team and accompany the tissue (e.g. Figure 1). Time of storage/removal, and names of personnel involved should be recorded. If donor tissue is deemed unsuitable for transplantation, the reason for discarding is recorded along with the surgeons’ or transplant physicians’ signature. The information document should also include a summary of donor’s demographics, donor ID, blood-type, ischemic times, infection status, valve/vessel measurements and a gross description of the allograft. Ideally, a monitored and locked cold storage/fridge should be allocated for LAVT donor tissue. (Figure 2).

At present, LAVT’s have adhered to similar ischemic time limitations and transport/storage conditions as an OHT. However, there is growing evidence that valve allografts can maintain viability for up to several days with cold storage and even up to weeks with certain preservation additives.12, 17, 26 This introduces the possibility of valve biobanks. If this becomes a practical reality, a log with details of all stored valve autografts should be maintained. At our center, we adhere to a maximum cold storage in a preservative solution of seven days, following which the grafts are discarded if not transplanted. All information should be maintained in a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act compliant secure electronic database. We utilize REDCap™ for this purpose.27 Every new entry generates a unique, unalterable ID that is printed on the forms mentioned above (Figure 3) and linked to the patient’s electronic medical record. Valve measurements, ischemic times, and donor infection status are also entered in the patient’s chart. This allows the valve records to be a permanent part of the patient’s medical history and can streamline an institutional LAVT registry.

Recipient selection and pre-LAVT evaluation

Recipient selection should follow a standardized pre-operative evaluation. An institutional clinical protocol should be established clearly outlining recipient and donor inclusion/exclusion criteria, acceptable ischemic times, immunosuppression regimens, and long-term monitoring. Patients should be initially evaluated by their primary cardiologist, cardiothoracic surgeon, or a cardiac intensivist, and eligibility for LAVT reviewed in multidisciplinary case management conference. LAVT can be considered for children and adolescents with severe valvar disease (stenosis and/or insufficiency) for whom standard surgical options are unavailable or are deemed to provide suboptimal outcomes. These patients can include those that have truncus arteriosus with a poorly functioning truncal valve, tetralogy of Fallot with pulmonary atresia, neonatal aortic stenosis/regurgitation, biventricular outflow tract obstruction, and those requiring a right-ventricle-to-pulmonary-artery conduit. Poor candidates for the Ross operation or those with neo-aortic valve disease following a Ross can also be considered.

A thorough psychosocial evaluation should be undertaken. This evaluation should ideally follow the same principles as for an orthotopic heart transplant. Key elements include an assessment of whether families can provide consistent and sustainable post-transplant care such as access to transportation for follow‑up visits and adherence to immunosuppression regimen. These requirements may evolve as evidence emerges regarding the long-term immunologic management of LAVT recipients.

A major consideration in patient selection for LAVT is the risk: benefit assessment of LAVT compared to conventional valve replacement options. Mechanical valves are durable but at the cost of lifelong anticoagulation with its associated complications. Bioprosthetic valves avoid anticoagulation but are prone to structural deterioration. Importantly, none of these conventional options have the ability to grow with the child and therefore require frequent re-operations. On the other hand, LAVT offers the potential for a growing, durable valve but introduces the adverse risks associated with immunosuppression. Decisions regarding the best approach should occur after careful discussion with the family reviewing potential short and long-term risks and benefits of each option. Comparative studies may be able to answer some of these questions once longer-term data with regards to LAVT becomes available.

Patients with end-organ dysfunction, those that require mechanical circulatory support and in whom immunosuppression is contraindicated could be considered on a case-by-case basis. Serial assessments to determine the continued need for LAVT and/or inability to wait for suitable donors requiring use of traditional valve interventions should be conducted. Surgical teams should determine the appropriate size of required valves based on pre-operative imaging and other recipient factors.

Peri-operative planning

Surgical and medical designees, akin to surgical and medical transplant directors, are essential in championing and development of an LAVT program. At our institution, a member of the operative room nursing team was designated for the development of operational processes and was vital for establishment of the program. This team organized informational/training meetings and “dry-run sessions” which were helpful to streamline procurement-to-implantation workflows, ensure compliance with regulatory requirements and clinical protocol. Consultations with programs that successfully performed LAVT’s were insightful in the lessons that they learned along the way.

Recipient and donor operative procedures

Donor heart procurement is conducted in a standard manner. Potential LAVT recipients should be informed well in advance. Recipient cardiac procedures including removal of aortic or pulmonary root will depend on patient specific anatomy. Once the donor heart is in the recipient operating room, aortic and pulmonary roots can be harvested from the donor organ similar to a root operation such as a Ross procedure or an en-bloc rotation procedure on the back-table. Following valve implantation, a postoperative transesophageal echocardiogram should be obtained to document baseline valve function.

Management of valve transplant recipients

Post-operative management is similar to a routine valve replacement procedure without the need for anticoagulation. The optimal immunosuppression regimen post-LAVT remains unknown at this time, although most protocols reviewed to date (personal communication) include a steroid taper and maintenance monotherapy with an mTOR or calcineurin inhibitor with monitoring of target levels. Immunosuppression may include pre-transplant calcineurin inhibitors and corticosteroids. At present, there is insufficient data to suggest that long-term immunosuppression can be foregone in valve transplants. However, this may change as data from preclinical studies become available. In cases of graft failure, a multidisciplinary case discussion should ensue to evaluate for alternative strategies such as mechanical valves and/or OHT. A long-term follow-up plan should be developed including defined intervals for imaging to assess valve growth and test for donor specific antibodies (Table 1).

Program evaluation and future directions

Outcomes of LAVT should be continuously analyzed and protocols and internal processes adjusted accordingly. The development of a multicenter registry is currently underway as LAVT numbers are expected to grow.28 Centers should continuously evaluate LAVT program sustainability from a clinical, financial, and institutional commitment perspective. Future directions in valve transplantation include valve biobanks, expanding the procedure to atrioventricular valves and viable xenograft valve transplants.6, 17

Conclusion

Establishment of an LAVT program requires a structured approach to navigate administrative, regulatory and operational processes. This can only be achieved by participation from a dedicated multidisciplinary team. This framework can serve as a guide for institutions looking to establish an LAVT program. The ultimate aim is to provide safe and high-quality care to congenital heart disease patients who stand to benefit most from this promising procedure.
