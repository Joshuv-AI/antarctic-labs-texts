Identifying chicken alloantigens to understand their influence on disease and production traits
Authors: Robert L. Taylor Jr., Janet E. Fulton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Self-recognition system components including red blood cell antigens and the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) are alloantigens found on multiple cell types. Specific polyclonal antisera reactivity identified fourteen chicken erythrocyte alloantigens (A, B, C, D, E, H, I, J, K, L, M, N, P, R). Multiple associations between alloantigen variation and disease or production traits have been reported. Blood group B, the MHC, was the first system whose genes and function were classified. Recent investigations sought to identify genes and chromosomal locations for other alloantigen systems. Phenotype-pooled DNA from pedigree and non-pedigree chickens having known alloantigen types underwent genome-wide association (GWAS) analyses using data from either 600K or 54K SNP panels. DNA from independent samples was used to confirm candidate gene associations. Genome sequences from elite production and experimental lines with identified alloantigen alleles were examined. Candidate genes within strong GWAS peaks were bioinformatically screened for cell surface expression and co-segregation of non-synonymous SNP with serologically defined haplotypes. Specific genes have now been identified for four alloantigen systems. Linked genes found on chromosome 26 are A = complement component 4 binding protein, membrane (C4BPM) and E = Fc fragment of IgA and IgM receptor (FCAMR). The D system gene (CD99) is on chromosome 1. Alloantigen I, equivalent to the human Rh blood group locus (RHCE), lies on chromosome 23 including deletions covering > 6,000 bp predicted to affect the last seven RHCE gene exons. Candidate regions were found for alloantigens L, H, and M on chromosomes 4, 24, and 26, respectively. Small sample size hindered definitive gene identification. In summary, four alloantigen genes with allele associated variants have been identified. Genes C4BPM, CD99, FCAMR, and RHCE play roles in processes such as complement regulation and immune cell migration. Chromosomal locations have been defined for three other alloantigen systems. Identification of specific genes responsible for the blood system alleles provides tools to study these chicken alloantigens for relationships with disease and production traits.

Introduction

Karl Landsteiner (1901) investigated the variable success of human blood transfusions, whose outcomes ranged from lifesaving to life-ending. Using blood samples from his laboratory staff, he mixed red blood cells from one person with normal serum from a different person. Each single serum sample agglutinated RBC of some but not all people tested. This result allowed individuals to be grouped by their agglutination reactions (blood groups). These isoagglutination reactions detected cellular differences among members of the same species, caused by alloantigens. Three separate blood groups were identified as A, B, and C. Blood group C was renamed O, and a fourth blood type, AB, was described later. Landsteiner’s work received the 1930 Nobel Prize in physiology and medicine.

Inconsistent results arose when blood from multiple species was tested for antibodies reacting against other members of the same species (isoagglutination). Based on the variability among species, Landsteiner and Miller (1924) injected rabbits with blood from individual chickens of various breeds to produce antibodies. Ten different rabbit anti-chicken antisera produced eight unique agglutination patterns indicating different blood types among the ten samples. Their research laid groundwork for alloantigen studies in chickens.

Todd (1930a) attempted to produce antisera against fowl plague (avian influenza) by injecting blood from infected chickens into uninfected birds. The individual antisera produced were also reactive against RBC with varied reaction patterns. Antisera pooled from multiple birds retained some broad reactivity after adsorption with individual RBC. Continued work showed that the RBC differences were inherited in Mendelian fashion as dominant traits. Utilizing three families, polyvalent antiserum was adsorbed with a single family member, one by one, followed by testing against all other family members. No two tests within a family gave identical results but some were quite similar (Todd, 1930b). Three generations of individuals identical or near-identical in their antiserum reactivity were produced by inbreeding using brother-sister matings of individuals with similar antisera reactivity (Todd, 1935).

Elwood Briles began to study chicken blood groups, alloantigens, in 1941 as a University of Wisconsin graduate student. His research sought to identify the blood groups in the interest of science and to test these alloantigens for associations with fitness traits. Despite an extended interruption by military service in World War II, two independent alloantigen systems were identified (Briles et al., 1950a). The two systems were originally designated B and D but were changed to A and B. Shortly thereafter, two more alloantigen systems, C and D, were described (Briles et al. 1950b; Briles 1951). The E alloantigen, linked closely to the A system, was the next sequential discovery (Briles, 1958).

Doug Gilmour at the University of Cambridge in England was investigating chicken blood groups at the same time as Elwood Briles. The two investigators exchanged test reagents to clarify the blood groups they had found. Gilmour found the A, B, and C alloantigens, independent of Briles (Gilmour, 1959; Briles, 1984). In addition, Gilmour found two new systems, L and N, in his chickens. Tests on different stocks revealed N system reagents reacted with the C system. This anomaly was explained by either cross reaction between the N and C systems or linkage of the two systems that occurred in one population (Briles, 1964, Briles, 1984). Additional testing was not done.

Testing blood samples within a particular laboratory using antisera from different sources was facilitated through interlaboratory reagent exchange. This allowed a unidirectional assessment of distinctiveness or similarity among systems identified at distinct locations. Exchanging red blood cells among laboratories was a challenge, even if the locations were close to each other. Systems having different names from discrete laboratories that were found to be the same were deferred to the name assigned first. New alloantigens systems F, H, I, J, K, and P were found by Briles, 1962, Briles, 1964 during his work at DeKalb AgResearch. All these systems were unique, apart from F, which was the same as alloantigen L (Briles, 1962). Matsumoto and Okada (1961) described alloantigen G which was the B system. Two other alloantigens, Q and V, were revealed to be different alleles of the C system (Gilmour, 1959).

The alloantigen designation, M, has been used twice (Table 1). Gilmour (1959) presented the first description of that system which was later shown to be identical to alloantigen E. Then, the name was reused in 2004 when Elwood Briles (unpublished data) found a new system in commercial breeding stock after testing with different antisera. Evaluation of that system was intermittent, but efforts are now in progress to characterize the M system.

The blood systems were typed in laboratories around the world. The difficulties of comparison testing different populations through reagent exchange complicated the identification. Local nomenclature of a blood system also clouded its definition. Table 1 shows the nineteen alloantigen systems discovered including two separate instances of M. Systems F, G, the first iteration of M, Q, and V, were found to be identical with systems identified previously. Alloantigen N, resides in an identity limbo, because its relationship to alloantigen C status was not clarified as a system either 1) unique, but linked to C; or 2) identical with C.

Alloantisera typing

Detection of alloantigens, described above, was usually accomplished with antisera specific for one allele of one alloantigen. Antibodies were produced when cells of one individual were injected into another individual. Multiple injections of cells were often required. The recipient individual would make antibodies against the alloantigen alleles on the cells of the donor that were absent in the recipient. Antibody specificity was achieved by adsorbing the antisera with cells that contained reactive alleles from the target system, another system or both. For example, if the donor possessed alloantigen types A1, B2, C3, and D3 whereas the recipient had A2, B7, C2, and D3 antibodies would be produced against A1, B2, and C3 because those alleles were absent from the recipient. If the objective was to make an antisera reacting against A1, the antisera would need to be adsorbed with cells having B2 and C3 but not A1. After removal of all B2 and C3 reactivity, the remaining antisera would be specific for A1 (Briles, 1984). Each adsorption diluted the antiserum, meaning that too many adsorptions could make the final antisera too weak for subsequent use.

Producing the needed typing antisera was tedious. The multiple injections followed by isolating the antisera were time-consuming. Furthermore, antibody production was variable because injecting the same cell preparation into multiple birds, even if all were of the same type, would stimulate antibody in a nuanced manner. Making the antisera, isolating it for specificity and reading the cellular agglutination reactions were equal portions of science and art. Antibody production using donors and recipients that were matched for as many alloantigens systems as possible improved the process and reduced the number of adsorptions needed. Donors and recipients would differ by one allele of a single alloantigen (i. e. donor = A1A2, recipient = A1A4) [Briles, 1984]. If the two birds were matched for three other alloantigens, the principal specificity for the antibody would be A2. Matching the donor and recipient for systems other than the target, negated the need for antibody adsorption. In addition, devoting attention to cross reactions within a particular alloantigen system extended the range of types identified.

Antisera produced to react with one allele in a particular population showed cross-reactions with multiple alleles of the same alloantigen when tested in a different population (Fanguy et al., 1961). Antisera can also cross-react with multiple alleles due to shared antigenic epitopes (Fulton et al., 1995; 1996). Briles and Briles (1982) used fifteen well characterized antisera to type 29 distinct B haplotypes. One monoclonal antibody was available that was reactive against A3, A4, and A8 but not against A2 and A5 (Fulton et al., 1990). The multiple allele reactivity of the monoclonal antibody highlights similarities among alleles which are not normally seen with polyclonal antisera. This multiple reactivity scenario will be seen again in another typing system later in this document.

Identifying genes producing alloantigens

Detection and further investigation of alloantigen was intended to find one or more systems associated with livability or production traits. Some such relationships were found to include an important tie between a blood group and disease resistance. Yet, with a single exception MHC-B, none of the genes responsible for the alloantigens were identified. This knowledge gap likely had multiple origins such as insufficient technical expertise needed to accomplish the goal or diminished interest in specific gene identification if the alloantigen effects were measurable.

Prior to 2022, blood system B, now known to be MHC-B, was the only alloantigen whose gene was identified. Alloantigen systems present a spectrum of polymorphism. The H, J, L, N, and R systems each had two alleles, the E system had 11 variants, and the B system was highly polymorphic with more than fifty serological types. Scheirman and Nordskog (1961) tested four blood groups for their association with skin allograft acceptance, i.e. histocompatibility. Systems A, D, and L as well as sex did not affect skin graft acceptance whereas system B mismatches between donor and recipient caused graft rejection. Thus, the B blood group was identified as the major histocompatibility complex (MHC-B). Briles and associates (1982) standardized the nomenclature and reference lines for twenty-seven diverse MHC-B haplotypes. The system’s structure and effects on disease resistance have been reviewed by Miller and Taylor (2016).

Multiple studies tested alloantigen genotype effects on production and health traits using diverse genetic stocks in different environments (Taylor et al., 2016). Effects were variable. The autosomal recessive chicken muscular dystrophy trait arose spontaneously in New Hampshire chicken Line N3, (Table 2) (Asmundson and Julian, 1956). The trait was subsequently determined to be inherited as a single incomplete dominant trait as the heterozygotes also showed abnormality of the muscles (Kikuchi et al., 1981). The MD line established at the University of Connecticut was outcrossed to White Leghorns followed by continual mating of MD x MD parents. Chickens of the MD line were segregating for five alloantigen systems whereas five other systems were fixed. Selection for egg production or egg size shifted alloantigen haplotype frequencies suggesting some association between these characters. Heterozygosity for alloantigen L persisted after eighteen generations of full-sib mating which implied a possible survival advantage for the heterozygous state in that alloantigen system (Gilmour, 1960; Taylor et al., 2016). Selection for diverse characters in different populations shifted the alloantigen haplotype frequencies for multiple systems. The selected characters were antibody to sheep red blood cells (SRBC), bursa of Fabricius size, and resistance to cecal coccidiosis (Taylor et al., 2016). Segregating combinations of specific alloantigen haplotype were tested for effect on Rous sarcoma virus tumor outcome. Some alloantigen haplotypes impacted tumor growth even with the key effect of the MHC-B (LePage et al., 2000; Medarova et al., 2002; Taylor et al., 2016).

Descriptions of direct or ancillary alloantigen haplotype effects on traits having economic importance renewed interest in identifying individual alloantigen genes. Availability of genetic resources, many of which have known alloantigen genotypes combined with genome sequences (International Chicken Genome Sequencing Consortium, 2004), a SNP map (International Chicken Polymorphism Map Consortium, 2004), lower cost sequencing procedures, and development of new techniques for alloantigen typing energized our joint effort to locate and identify the genes responsible for chicken multiple chicken alloantigen systems.

Genetic resources

Multiple DNA samples from birds having known serological alloantigen genotypes were available. All serology had been performed using specific antisera in the Elwood Briles’ laboratory at Northern Illinois University (NIU), DeKalb, IL. The available samples fit one of four categories: 1) NIU non-pedigree birds with information for one or more blood systems; or 2) NIU pedigree progeny segregation for one or two alloantigen systems; 3) multiple generations of birds from Hy-Line International with identified blood system information; and 4) inbred lines with known blood types reported in the literature (Table 3). The final source of genetic information was genomic sequence data for multiple inbred lines with known blood system serological typing. Their genomic sequence was examined to determine if the genome information of the candidate genes matched with their reported serology, even when no DNA was available for SNP typing (Table 3). One anomaly found in the literature was that Line UCD 001, the original chicken reference genome, was described as having multiple blood system genotypes (A, B, E, I, P, R) that differed from UCD 003. Unfortunately, the actual allelic composition for UCD 001 was not reported (Crittenden et al., 1993).

Additional samples with known serology could not be developed as the serological reagents for identifying specific alleles are no longer available. Furthermore, some specific lines of birds with known serological status no longer exist. There was one exception, which was for the blood system A, as a monoclonal antibody was still available (Fulton et al, 1990).

GWAS analysis

Methods used to identify SNP genotypes have been described in detail (Fulton et al., 2016, 2022, 2023, 2024). There were also subtle distinctions in the methods used to type each system. Briefly, DNA pools were prepared from samples having known genotypes for a particular alloantigen. Multiple pools for the same genotype (i.e. A1A1, A1A2, and A2A2) consisted of many individual samples. The Affymetrix Axiom 600 K chicken SNP array (Kranis et al., 2013) was used to obtain SNP genotypes for the three different pools of alloantigen genotypes. Regression analysis allowed calculation of a coefficient of determination (r2). These values were evaluated to choose SNP segregating with the expected genotypes from the DNA pool.

Once a candidate gene was identified, selected SNP within the candidate were further assessed for: 1) presence in an exon; 2) changing an amino acid; and 3) gene product presence on the cell membrane. This third point was important since the blood systems under discussion were detected by hemagglutination, their antigenic molecules must be expressed on the surface of red blood cells. Thus, examination of potential candidate genes was focused on those whose product was reported to be expressed on the membrane (GO: membrane). Haplotypes defined by specific SNP combinations were assigned an identification number which included the gene name. The haplotype number assigned was the same as alleles defined by serology, where possible (Fulton et al., 2022). Haplotypes developed from the SNP panel were checked against existing samples and inbred line sequences having known alloantigen genotypes (Table 3).

That paper summarized information available about the alloantigens effects on numerous traits. The current work seeks to relate gene identification to known effects as well as to add new data about relationships to immune responses rather than to reiterate all prior information. A system

Blood system A was determined to be encoded by C4BPM on chromosome 26 (Fulton et al., 2022) (Table 4). This gene is located within the regulators of complement activation region (RCA) which is syntenic to the human RCA region. Complement proteins form components of the complement cascade , a crucial part of both the innate and humoral immune system that clears pathogens from the host. Activation of the complement pathway is tightly regulated by multiple factors including C4BPM to ensure that the immune response is both targeted and controlled.

In humans, two genes of the RCA were initially identified as encoding blood system antigens; the Cromer blood system due to CD55 gene (Storry et al., 2010), and the Knops blood system due to CR1 gene (Petty et al., 1997; Moulds, 2010). Both blood systems are reported to influence disease outcomes with CD55 being a receptor for bacteria and viruses, and CR1 being associated with differential responses to diseases including malaria, leishmaniasis, and tuberculosis (Cooling, 2015) which are caused by parasitic or bacterial pathogens.

Chicken alloantigen A haplotype frequencies changed within multiple populations after selection for specific traits. Divergent selection in two separate populations produced two pairs of lines selected for high or low antibody responses against sheep red blood cells. These lines designated as HAS or LAS and HA or LA were selected at Virginia Tech (Siegel and Gross, 1980) and Wageningen University, the Netherlands (van der Zjipp and Nieuwland, 1986), respectively (Table 2). Testing of alloantigen types occurred in HAS, LAS at generation 48 and HA, LA at generation 32 and showed that A haplotypes frequencies differed between the high vs low antibody response lines within both pairs of lines (Taylor et al., 2023). Haplotype C4BPM-H01 (A1, by serology) had 53% frequency in the HAS line whereas C4BPM-H04 (A4) had 94% frequency in the LAS line. The most common haplotype in Line HA and LA was C4BPM-H05 (A5) at 70% and C4BPM-H12 (unknown) at 61%, respectively.

Beginning with a common founder population, the Lester J. Dreesen (LJD) New Hampshire Line, selection for bursa of Fabricius size (Table 2) (Glick and Dreesen, 1967) shifted alloantigen A frequencies. Haplotype A2 was absent in the Large Bursa Line (LBL) but had 70% frequency in the Small Bursa Line (SBL) and 46% in the LJD control (Scott et al., 1988). Coupled with elevated serum immunoglobulin (Yamamoto and Glick, 1982) and lower primary and secondary anti-SRBC antibody response in the LBL chickens, different alloantigen A composition between the lines suggested effects on these responses.

Johnson and Edgar (1984) selected for divergent response against Eimeria tenella (Table 2). Line R, selected for resistance to coccidiosis had a higher frequency of A7 types whereas the selected susceptible Line S, had a higher frequency of A9. The different A system haplotype frequencies tie to the role of C4BPM in complement activation. More recently, Niraula et al. (2025) examined a mixed Eimeria species infection in pedigreed progeny segregating for five different alloantigen systems. Only A2 and A8 haplotypes were present and neither affected responses against Eimeria. Finally, C4BPM was recently reported for impact on avian influenza virus replication via inhibition of type 1 interferons (Li et al, 2025).

Two traits of economic importance, body weight and egg production, had associations with alloantigen A. Allele frequencies for serological types A6 and A8 increased during selection for meat production and egg production. There was an additional negative correlation between A1 and egg production (Dietert et al., 1992). The A alloantigen is not found on white blood cells, an unusual attribute considering the complement regulatory gene(s) participate in reactions involving lymphocytes (Fulton et al., 1990).

D system

The chicken D blood system is found on chromosome 1 and is due to variation within the CD99 gene (Fulton et al., 2023) (Table 4). Its original assigned chromosome 1 location, based on test crosses, (Bitgood et al., 1991) was confirmed. Other systems A, E, H, and I had been assigned to chromosome 1 but were found to be located elsewhere (Fulton et al., 2022; 2024). The human Xg blood system is the result of two co-regulated antigens encoded by the genes Xg and CD99 (Johnson, 2011; Yeh et al., 2018). The latter of these two genes, CD99, is known to impact multiple biological processes, including cell adhesion, migration and differentiation as well as inflammation and immune responses (Pasello et al., 2018). Cellular maturation for T cells, B cells and erythroid cells were affected by CD99 (Manara et al., 2025).

Selection for antibody response to SRBC shifted the alloantigen D haplotype frequency (Taylor et al., 2023). Alloantigen D has five alleles identified by serology with eleven SNP-based CD99 haplotypes. Analysis of either DNA samples or sequences found one SNP-based haplotype each for D1 and D2 plus three SNP-based D3 haplotypes (Fulton et al., 2023). In both selection experiments for antibody response, the D3 haplotype frequency increased significantly in the HA and HAS high antibody lines. The higher D3 haplotype frequency in the HAS line was consistent with tests conducted in selection generations ten and thirteen for the HAS line (Dunnington et al., 1984; Martin et al., 1990). The SNP haplotypes, CD99-H07 and CD99-H09, had higher frequencies in the low antibody lines, LA and LAS, respectively, but these haplotypes were absent in the corresponding high antibody lines HA or HAS (Taylor et al., 2023). These two SNP haplotypes are among six total alleles whose corresponding serological allele was not identified. These novel SNP haplotypes may align with the two remaining D serology alleles not identified in DNA samples or sequences or they may be new alleles previously undefined by serology.

The D1 serological allele was associated with larger bursa size because that allele’s frequency increased in the LBL compared with the SBL (Scott et al., 1988). Impacts of the D system types on antibody response and bursa size related to a possible influence on B cell maturation. Macrophage IL-6 production was not affected by D types tested in two different MHC-B backgrounds. On the other hand, macrophage nitrite production was affected by the D system in one of two MHC-B backgrounds (Taylor et al., 2016)

Testing disease responses revealed no D blood system effect on Rous sarcoma tumor outcome (LePage et al., 2000). Responses against Eimeria spp. were influenced by the D alleles after a multi-species challenge. The CD99-H01 haplotype (D1) had a positive association with body weight gain but a negative association with feed conversion ratio (Niraula et al., 2025).

E system

Classical linkage experiments determined the close linkage (0.5-1.0 cM) between alloantigen E and A. The two systems were thought to be on chromosome 1 (Briles, 1968; Kopti et al., 1997) but GWAS and SNP analyses found them on chromosome 26. Tight genetic linkage aided the identification of the E blood system gene as FCAMR, (Fc fragment of IgA and IgM receptor) approximately 89,000 bp downstream of C4BPM (Fulton et al., 2022) (Table 4). The Fc receptors play important roles linking humoral and cellular immunity because they bind to antibodies on pathogen surfaces, coordinating communication between immune cells to facilitate pathogen destruction (Takai, 2002). While not within the defined region of complement activation, alloantigen E’s genomic proximity is consistent with strong complement pathway interaction for pathogen destruction.

The impact of E system alleles on immune related traits has been identified in lines divergently selected for antibody production against SRBC or resistance to Eimeria tenella. Haplotypes of alloantigen E shifted during selection for antibody response, however, the same E haplotypes had opposite frequency shifts in HAS vs LAS and HA versus LA. Haplotype FCAMR-H02, which corresponded to serological types E2, E5 and E7 had 59% frequency in line HA but 58% frequency in line LAS, in the other selection. The FCAMR-H03 haplotype corresponded to serological types E3 and E7 had 49% frequency in line LA but 79% frequency in line HAS (Taylor et al., 2023). The opposite effects were attributable to the diverse origins of the lines, Cornell randombred for HAS and LAS versus ISA Warren stocks for HA and LA. Earlier tests for E system effects alone were problematic due to the close linkage of the A and E systems (Taylor et al. 2016). The available DNA based tests for E system alleles now eliminates that problem.

Selection for susceptibility or resistance to cecal coccidiosis resulted in near unique E haplotypes in each line (Johnson and Edgar, 1984). High frequencies of E5 and E3 were found in Line R and Line S, respectively. The combination with A haplotypes heightened the exclusivity of E types depending on resistance or susceptibility. Interactions between the two systems were important to their contribution to the response to coccidiosis. The immune related functions for alloantigen A, complement regulation, and alloantigen E, Fc receptor for IgA and IgM, help explain the role of these genes in the response. The experiments comparing responses to Eimeria challenge in birds segregating for multiple alloantigen systems found that duodenal gross lesion scores were lower in E genotype FCAMR-H02/FCAMR-H02 compared with FCAMR-H07/FCAMR-H07 (Niraula et al., 2025).

I system

Chicken chromosome 23 is the location of RHCE (Table 4), the gene responsible for the chicken I blood system (Fulton et al., 2024). Two genes, RHCE and its paralog RHD, encode the proteins of the human Rh blood system. Variation in these two genes is responsible for the well known maternal/fetal incompatibility that results in hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN) causing fetal hemolytic anemia and potential fetal death (Myle and Al-Khattabi, 2021).

The human and chicken genome regions containing the RHCE gene are syntenic (Fulton et al., 2024). The RHD gene is found only in the primate lineage, likely due to an ancestral duplication of RHCE. The Rh system is one of the major blood systems that impact blood transfusions and organ transplants in humans. Most variation is due to multiple alleles within the RHCE gene. Lack of the RHD gene causes the Rh-negative phenotype, meaning all chickens would be Rh negative. The specific functionof the human Rh antigen is unknown, though Rh may play a role in transmembrane CO2 transportation (Kustru and Inwood, 2006). It would be interesting to determine if variation within the chicken RHCE has a role in blood oxygen levels.

Early HAS and LAS selection generations did not have altered frequencies of alloantigen I (Dunnington et al., 1984; Martin et al., 1990). The I2 allele had higher frequency in HAS compared with LAS by generation 48. On the other hand, I allele frequency did not differ between the HA or LA selected lines (Taylor et al., 2023). The I2 allele had distinct results after selection for bursa size (Scott et al., 1988). Allele frequency was higher in the SBL but that group also produced less immunoglobulin as well as more antibody to SRBC compared with LBL (Yamamoto and Glick, 1982).

Multiple tests showed alloantigen I effects on Eimeria infection. The HAS line birds had lower E. tenella lesion scores in progeny from parents homozygous for I4 compared with parents segregating for I2 and I4. No I system impact was found in LAS birds (Martin et al., 1986). Data from Niraula et al., (2025) showed RHCE-H01 reduced jejunal and cecal lesion scores compared to haplotypes RHCE-H02 and RHCE-H03 in Eimeria infected birds. In fact, RHCE-H03 had increased cecal lesions.

Other disease or immune function tests had variable results. Rous sarcoma tumor outcome was not affected by I genotype (LePage et al., 2000). Depending on the MHC-B environment, macrophage nitrite production, IL-6 levels, and phagocytosis were influenced by the I system in the presence of some MHC-B genotypes but not with others (Qureshi et al., 2000). This variability emphasizes potential interactions between the MHC-B, whose effects may be large, and other alloantigen systems, whose effects may be smaller.

Data is sparse for alloantigen effects on production characters. One notable exception is extreme I alloantigen divergence found following selection for egg weight (Abplanalp et al., 1981). The large egg weight line had I4 but not I2, while the low egg weight lines were the opposite, having I2 and not I4. Although the evidence is indirect, the data suggest further study of relationships between production traits and alloantigen systems should be done.

Systems with identified chromosomal locations but unknown genes (H, L, M)

H system

Alloantigen H has been identified as being encoded on chromosome 24 but the specific gene remains unknown (Table 4). Birds of the HAS line were fixed for the H2 allele whereas the LAS line had both H1 and H2 present (Martin et al., 1990). Neither Rous sarcoma tumor outcome (LePage et al., 2000) nor measures of macrophage function including phagocytosis, IL-6 or nitrite production (M. A. Qureshi, unpublished data) were impacted by alloantigen H.

L system

Early experiments found L alloantigen expressed on RBC and leukocytes (Kopti et al. 1999). Examining DNA samples from pedigreed progeny whose parents were both L1L2 using a 54 k SNP chip found a strong signal between 32,000,000 to 34,500,000 bp on chromosome 4 (C. M. Ashwell, unpublished data). Further analysis of DNA non-pedigreed samples, and inbred lines having known alloantigen types and SNP information from a 600 k SNP chip as well as genomic sequence analysis from inbreds lines typed for alloantigens confirmed the chromosome 4 location in a slightly different area 29,000,000 to 32,000,000 bp (Table 4). Relatively small differences in gene location are likely due to the version of the reference genome utilized in the studies.

Antibody responses, in vitro assays of macrophage function and disease challenges were influenced by alloantigen L. However, the L effects varied depending upon the MHC-B types present. Alloantigen L affected the antibody response to Brucella abortus (BA), a T-cell independent antigen and SRBC, a T-cell dependent antigen. Among birds of the B2B5 genotype, L1L1 chickens had higher secondary antibody titers against BA versus genotype B2B5 L2L2 (Medarova et al., 2003). Genotype B5B5 birds that were L system homozygotes, L1L1 or L2L2, had higher primary antibody titers against SRBC than did B5B5 L1L2 siblings. Genotype B2B5 secondary antibody to SRBC response was also affected by the L system. Higher total and mercaptoethanol (ME)-resistant antibodies were found in L1L2 and L2L2 birds compared with L1L1 (Medarova et al., 2003). Macrophage nitrite and IL-6 production was not affected by the L system in birds having B2B2 or B2B5 genotypes. On the other hand, in B19B21 birds, the MHC-B and L systems interacted as IL-6 production by L1L1 birds was lower in that MHC-B genotype but higher in B19B19 (Qureshi et al., 2000).

Other experiments showed L system effects depending on the MHC-B complex genotype. Rous sarcoma outcome was modified by alloantigen L studied in two different experiments. L system homozygous birds, L1L1, had lower Rous sarcoma tumor growth that differed significantly from L1L2 in B2B5 birds (LePage et al., 2000). Another study found that the L system heterozygotes, L1L2, had lower tumor scores in the tumor progressor genotype B5B5 (Medarova et al., 2002). Eimeria tenella lesion scores were higher in B2B2 L1L1 birds compared with the B2B2 bird sharing L1L1 or L2L2. The other B genotype in the experiment, B2B5, showed no L system effect on E. tenella lesion scores (Taylor and Briles, 2000).

DeSilva (1965) found higher fertility in eggs from matings having one parent heterozygous for the L alloantigen. Gilmour (1960) suggested broader effects when he found that lines inbred for eighteen generations still segregated for multiple alloantigen systems including L.

Two different selection experiments presented varied impacts of alloantigen L. The HAS and LAS lines were not affected by the L system as both lines were fixed for the same allele (Dunnington et al., 1984, Martin et al., 1990). The frequency of L2 was higher in SBL birds suggesting a link between L2 and small bursa size (Scott et al., 1988).

M system

The “newest” system M, has been located to a region of interest on chromosome 26 (Table 4). The M region was not correlated with alloantigen A, appearing to be on one side of the A system with the E system on the opposite side (Fulton, unpublished data). No other information is known about the M system. Limited DNA samples having known M type and lack of M antisera will hinder further investigations in this system.

Systems with unidentified genetic basis (C, J, K, N, P, R)

C system

Genomic analysis of pedigreed and non-pedigreed DNA samples using the 600 K SNP chip failed to find genomic regions having SNP types associated with alloantigen C types (Table 4). The system was characterized as a minor histocompatibility antigen based on its presence on lymphocytes combined with skin graft rejection when donor and recipient had different alleles of this system (Schierman and Nordskog, 1965; Briles and Gilmour, 1979). Alloantigen C effects on macrophage nitrite production varied in the context of different MHC-B backgrounds (Qureshi et al., 2000). Macrophage IL-6 was unaffected by alloantigen C alleles without regard to MHC-B background.

The C5 alloantigen frequency was 100% in LAS selected birds, twice the frequency in HAS suggesting that C5 allele may have contributed to the low antibody response (Dunnington et al., 1984; Martin et al., 1990). Frequency of C6 allele in LBL selected birds was 1.5x higher than that found in SBL but no other association was characterized (Scott et al., 1988). In an experimental group fixed for B17B17, Rous sarcoma growth plateaued in the C2C5 genotype but progressed in C2C2 birds (Taylor et al., 2016). In a second study with B2B5 and B5B5 MHC-B genotypes segregating, alloantigen C did not affect Rous sarcoma tumor outcome (LePage et al., 2000).

J, K, N, and R systems

Little is known of these four systems. No function has been described for alloantigen J (Briles, 1962). The K system was reported as related to vaccinia virus agglutination of RBC that was connected to the Vh locus (Gilmour, 1962; Brown et al., 1973). The N system, discovered as an independent alloantigen (Gilmour, 1959), was later thought to be identical to or cross reactive with the C system. Two different R system antisera (Crittenden et al., 1970; Bacon et al., 1996) defined susceptibility to ALV virus subgroup B (Table 4) and may define the tvb receptor, TNFRSF10B (Smith et al., 1998; Reinisová et al., 2008).

P system

Attempts to identify the gene responsible for alloantigen P failed due to lack of GWAS consistency between sample sets from different sources (Fulton, personal communication) (Table 4). The system has ten serological alleles, a high degree of polymorphism (Briles and Gilmour, 1979). Divergent selection for bursa size lowered the frequency of P4 and raised the frequency of P2 in both LBL and SBL compared to the founder population (Scott et al., 1988). These results indicated no impact of alloantigen P on bursa size. Rous sarcoma induced tumor growth in B2B2 or B2B5 chickens was not affected by system P (LePage et al., 2000). In assessing macrophage function, different P system effects occurred depending on the B system environment. The P1P4 genotype had higher macrophage nitrite production than did P1P1 or P4P4 types in B19B19 and B19B21 chickens. Macrophage IL-6 production was not affected by the P system in two test groups each having two B genotypes: B2B2 and B2B5 or B19B19 and B19B21. Monocyte phagocytosis measure in B19B19 and B19B21 chickens was higher in P4P4 birds compared with P1P1 birds (Qureshi et al., 2000). The value for the P1P4 heterozygote was intermediate compared with P1P1 and P4P4.

Non-RBC alloantigens

This review focused on red blood cell alloantigen, first characterized using specific antisera. Alloantigens are found on multiple cell types with the criteria being that the molecule on a cell stimulates antibody production when injected into a recipient that does not share that allele. Lymphocytes are another cell type for which numerous alloantigens are known. The molecules may be developmental markers, receptors or other categories. Thymus-derived lymphocytes, T cells, have alloantigens including Ly-4, Th-1 (Fredericksen and Gilmour, 1981), CD3 (Chen et al., 1986), CD4 and CD8 (Chan et al., 1988). Alloantigens on B cells include Bu-1 (cHB6) [Gilmour et al., 1976; Tregaskes et al., 1996], BAFF (Reddy et al., 2008), CD-15 (Masteller et al., 1995), and CXCR4 (Liang et al., 2001). The above list contains selected examples of non-RBC alloantigens.

Future investigations

One original purpose for studying alloantigens was to find relationships, if any, between alloantigen alleles and traits of economic importance. These alleles were typed in agglutination reactions using specific antisera. The relationship between alloantigen type and one or more characteristics was assessed through experiments of different types. Alloantigen alleles were typed in defined chicken lines having a differential response such as egg production or antibody response. The presence of different alloantigen alleles in different lines suggested a link with the response. A second strategy tested alloantigen allele frequencies in lines divergently selected for a particular response. An alloantigen frequency shift in one or both selected lines implied a relationship between the alleles and the selected response. These shifts were observed in lines selected for antibody response against SRBC (Martin, et al., 1990; Taylor et al., 2023), E. tenella resistance (Johnson and Edgar, 1984) and bursa of Fabricius size (Scott et al., 1988). The third test design involved pedigree matings to investigate the combined effects of multiple alloantigen systems on complex traits or disease responses. Previous studies utilized this strategy to examine Rous sarcoma outcome (LePage et al., 2000), macrophage phagocytic function (Qureshi et al., 2000) and multiple species Eimeria infection (Niraula et al., 2025). The approach is well-suited to further investigate multiple alloantigen impacts.

Identification of four alloantigen genes will facilitate further studies of these systems. Alloantigen typing using SNP assays is less complex and more universally applicable than agglutination tests. The alloantigen genes identified are A = C4BPM, D = CD99, E = FCAMR and I = RHCE. Adding the MHC-B system, discovered in 1961, means that five alloantigens identified all relate to the immune system, directly or indirectly. This connection alone merits investigation. Exploring the role of these alloantigens on non-erythroid cells such as lymphocytes in greater depth, may reveal novel immune regulatory pathways via cells, receptors, or cytokines.

Additional alloantigen genes should be studied as they are identified. Work is near completion to distinguish other systems for which a chromosomal location is known. The genes for these systems may impact the immune system as well or they may affect other processes. Validating the function of alloantigen genes through gene-editing technologies should be considered (e.g., CRISPR/Cas9). A centralized database for chicken alloantigen haplotypes and their associated phenotypes would facilitate future research and breeding applications.

Assessing the alloantigens for influence on response against pathogens as well as production traits is important. Multiple studies have reported a role in disease resistance for these chicken blood systems which relate to the immune system and confirms the value of further study to improve bird health. Disease challenge studies involving alloantigens should avoid extreme responder MHC-B alleles. Their major effect on multiple diseases is likely to mask smaller effects by other genes such as alloantigens. Greater emphasis on enhancing the chickens’ immune response will help meet consumer preferences of reducing medication use.
