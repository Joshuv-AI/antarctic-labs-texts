Characterization of the learning environment for teaching pharmaceutical care: a scoping review
Authors: Mayara de Almeida Lima Ribeiro, Dennis Wesley Andrade Hohenfeld, Thaís Maria Araújo Tavares, Fernando de Castro Araújo-Neto, Alessandra Rezende Mesquita, Giselle de Carvalho Brito, Divaldo Pereira de Lyra Jr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveLearning environments encompass aspects related to personal, social, organizational, physical, and virtual components and can affect not only academic performance but also the physical and emotional well-being of students, which in turn impacts the quality of services offered to society. Therefore, this study aimed to characterize the learning environment for pharmaceutical care teaching in the literature.FindingsA scoping review was conducted the databases: Pubmed (Medline), Embase, Web of Science, Scopus and ERIC. The search was conducted in July 2025, without restrictions of year or language. The methodological quality of studies was verified using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT). 11 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in this scoping review. They were published between 2010 and 2025, in the United States (n = 4), Malaysia (n = 2; 18.18%), Netherlands, Australia, United Kingdom, Kuwait, and Canada (n = 1; 9.09%). The target population was Pharmacy students. Physical and virtual environments were described, highlighting innovative approaches centered on the student, as follows: experience-based learning (EBL), problem-based learning (PBL), simulation, team-based learning, blended learning and online learning (e-learning). Personal, social, and organizational components were also identified.SummaryInnovative strategies have proven effective in the learning process of pharmaceutical care. However, it is important that these findings be reproducible in settings both inside and outside the university. Future research should deepen the understanding of learning environments for teaching Pharmaceutical Care to strengthen scientific production and guide the training of future pharmacists better prepared for patient-centered practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08876-7.

Introduction

In the past 60 years, due to the increased use of medications and related morbidity and mortality, medications have been among the leading causes of preventable adverse events, hospitalizations, and deaths, with social effects and a strong economic impact [1, 2]. In response to these demands, Hepler and Strand (1990) defined Pharmaceutical Care as “the responsible provision of pharmacotherapy with the goal of achieving measurable outcomes that improve the patient’s quality of life.” [3].

Thus, institutions, professors, and pharmacists have understood the importance of transforming secular pharmaceutical education. Pharmaceutical Care has served as a benchmark for the advancement of pharmaceutical curricula, shifting from a product-oriented approach to a person-centered approach [3–7].

In the training process for the development of clinical skills, Higher Education Institutions seek to adjust their teaching and learning strategies, as well as analyze the objective and subjective aspects of the environments necessary for patient care practices [4, 8, 9]. The quality of learning environments can affect not only academic performance but also the physical and emotional well-being of students, which reverberates in the quality of services offered to society [9–12].

Some studies suggest that inserting students into practice scenarios from the beginning of graduation can make them active and participatory subjects in the teaching and learning process [13–15]. Such insertion amplifies the subjective experiences of the labor market, may strengthen the socioemotional aspects of students, and consolidate their competences to act as a pharmacist [16]. Nevertheless, there is a lack of studies that discuss the learning environment for teaching [17–19].

Before that, literature may identify the points of improvement of formative processes to deepen and support the transformation process of pharmaceutical education. Therefore, it is needed to consider that learning environments can be a facilitator to teaching patient care, in a systematized and reproducible way, as it promotes real or almost real interactions adapted to culture and physical and virtual scenarios of practice [20, 21]. Thus, the present study aimed to characterize in literature the learning environment for teaching Pharmaceutical Care.

Methods

This scooping review was conducted based on the methodological processes proposed by the Joanna Briggs Institute Reviewer’s Manual and using the Rayyan QRCI (www.rayyan.qrci.org/reviews) as a supporting tool to answer the following question: “How learning environments are structured for teaching Pharmaceutical Care?”. In addition, the secondary question were: “What are the organizational attributes of learning environments for teaching Pharmaceutical Care?”. Therefore, the databases EMBASE, ERIC, PUBMED/Medline, Scopus and Web of Science were used with the descriptors: “learning environment”; “pharmaceutical care”; “pharmacy education”; “pharmacy students”; “teaching” and their combinations and synonyms [22].

Study selection

After the search in July 2025, two researchers conducted the process of selection (M.A.L.R. e D.W.A.H.) and, when necessary, a third researcher facilitated the consensus (T.M.A.T.). Selection followed the stages: excluding duplicates, screening titles and abstracts, and evaluating full text. There were no restrictions on language or year of publication to evaluate full text. The following were excluded: other literature reviews, abstracts published in conference proceedings, expended abstracts, letters to editor, conference articles, studies in which the learning environment was not characterized clearly, and studies that characterized the learning environment for teaching healthcare to professions of other fields or in a multiprofessional perspective, once results were reported all together and not separately by profession.

Studies were included that focus on the learning environment for Pharmaceutical Care as a central theme, describing this environment considering aspects such as: physical structure, equipment, standardization of procedures, teaching methodology used, information system and service delivery. Data extraction

Data were extracted from studies included in this review by two independent reviewers: author, year, country, design, characteristics of participants (number, level – e.g., BPharM., MPharm., PharmD. –, semester/school year, university), characteristics of learning environment for Pharmaceutical Care (scenario, teaching method, content), as well as methods of collecting and evaluating results, influence of environment in the academic educational process, study limitations, and main conclusions.

Methodological quality evaluation

The methodological quality evaluation of included studies was conducted independently by two researchers and discrepancies were resolved by consensus. The Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) was used as it approaches different study designs. This tool consists of five sections, one for each type of study, with five items in each section. Each item was marked as “Yes” if the study met the item criteria; “no” if it does not meet the criteria; “I don’t know” if it missed enough information to judge [23].

Out of the 1,511 records identified in the search, 887 were excluded for being duplicates. Therefore, 899 titles and abstracts were screened. 31 studies were selected for full-text reading and 11 were included in this review. Personal, social, and organizational components of learning environment

Personal components were identified through factors such as learning goals, prior experience, dedication, and ability to manage expectations [32]. Besides, subjective elements such as empowerment, motivation, strengthening of professional identity, satisfaction, and autonomy, reinforce engagement and student accountability for their own learning which can be observed in other studies [25, 28, 29, 33, 34]. Confidence and development of professional competence were highlighted in alignment with previous studies [26, 30].

Regarding the social component, confidence relationships, feedback, and supervision built a safe and exciting environment [32, 33]. Team-based learning, peer review, and reflexive discussion promoted knowledge construction [24, 28, 29, 31]. Moreover, they improved communication with patients, colleagues, and other health professionals [25, 30]. Organizational component, identified in all studies, included curriculum, pedagogical strategies, accreditation rules, practices, policies, regulatory setting, guidance, and learning support [24–34].

Characteristics physical and virtual of learning environments described in studies

Physical environment

Five studies included in this review highlighted the importance of the physical environment in the learning experiences of Pharmacy students, underscoring variations among real contexts (hospitals, outpatient clinics, and community pharmacies), ability labs [25, 29–32]. Skills labs were scenario for dispensing activities [29, 31]. Experiences in outpatient clinic environments were based on measuring blood pressure of elderly patients [30]. Hybrid model revealed the relevance of face-to-face activities, with evaluation of cardiovascular risk in community pharmacies, complementing the virtual learning environment [25]. The learning experiences described in the latest study occurred in hospital pharmacies and community pharmacies, highlighting the need for a safe environment with essential conditions for the learning success [32]. Virtual environment

The virtual component found in five studies seeks to improve learning experience of Pharmacy students, although they have different approaches and technologies [24–28]. One study developed an online cognitive support system to Problem Based Learning (PBL) structured in five steps of problem solving, recording and sharing the reasoning of students among colleagues, instructors, and evaluators [24].

Problem-Based Learning (PBL) was used in the study, which focused on cardiovascular risk assessment, as the basis for a blended learning environment, combining face-to-face simulation with asynchronous online activities and including digital resources such as interactive e-handouts, questionnaires with feedback, links to guidelines and practical tutorials, among others [25]. MyDispense simulated a community pharmacy offering representations of patients, prescriptions, products on shelves, and a high-fidelity interface to prescription processing [26].

Another study developed an interactive case scenario based on video to teach pain management, with different software integration and turned into a lively and interactive package [27]. The environment shows multiple choice questions integrated into the clinical case and provides immediate feedback, valuing interactivity, engagement, and instant gratification. In the last study, an i-Learn system was used, which is a virtual environment that enables sharing materials, online questionnaire applications, and interactive forum participation (i-Discuss), which stimulates collaboration, exchange of ideas and critical reflection with other students and tutors [28].

Effectiveness of learning environments

Learning environments such as internships, labs, community clinics, online simulations, and blended learning scenarios showed positive effect in developing competences in students [24–34]. Experience in virtual environments favor clinical reasoning, self-regulation, and peer collaboration [24–28].

Physical environments, on the other hand, engaged critical reasoning, professionalism, and communication abilities [25, 29–32]. Tools such as MyDispense, PBL cognitive support systems, and video interactive scenarios improved performance, confidence, and learning, complementing online activities with formative feedback [24–27]. Finally, teaching strategies including PBL, TBL, students as partners, and Experience-based learning promoted engagement, reflection, and decrease of failure rates [25, 30–33].

Evaluation of methodological quality of studies

All studies were evaluated through MMAT tool (2008) and had clear resource questions (Appendix A). Two qualitative studies included in this review met al.l evaluation criteria [32, 33], whereas the quantitative studies (n = 4) partially met these criteria [28–31]. Out of the five mixed method studies, one met al.l criteria [34], and four partially met them [24–27].

Discussion

The need for transition from a teaching model product-centered to a patient care approach has been discussed [35]. However, the observed predominance of studies conducted in the United States can be justified by its pioneering work in Pharmaceutical Care, in creating PharmD, and in increasing workload of disciplines centered on patient care [3, 4]. It is important to highlight that accreditation standards of PharmD programs in the USA that articulate the expectations of academia and practice were updated, serving as reference to other countries [36].

Personal, social, and organizational component of learning environments

This study verified the presence of diverse personal and social components that influence learning and its application to qualified provision of Pharmaceutical Care. Such finds reinforce the necessity of a holistic approach that integrate the subjective elements as part of the teaching process [37] Furthermore, motivation, autonomy, and self-confidence influence learning since students report greater engagement, academic performance, and satisfaction with the acquired competences [34, 38].

Another important aspect stands out that organization, sense of usefulness, and belonging to the pharmaceutical profession were associated with acquiring patient-centered personal and social competences [39]. Another study showed that participation in peer-assisted practices (students, teachers, and supervisors) not only strengthened the development and consolidation of personal competences, but also organizational and social ones [40, 41]. Thus, building a curriculum must be a collaborative process that involves teachers, professionals, and students to create learning environments that train pharmacists to be more autonomous, confident, and engaged with the well-being of patients.

Different learning environments and their effectiveness

Data obtained from this review shows diverse virtual learning environments, and the selection of them must be guided by specific learning objectives, so the results are effective [42]. Some studies show that virtual reality is motivating, engaging, and interacting, besides it promotes cognitive, physical, customized, collaborative, and socioemotional development [43, 44]. Therefore, teaching based on virtual models is effective, adaptable, and sustainable [45]. Moreover, it allows immediate feedback and multiple user profiles, developing competences in a convenient, ethical, and safe context since it does not require specific physical environment or real patients which enable it to be used any time of undergraduate or continuing education.

In this review, it was found different learning environments inside (ability labs) and outside the university (hospitals, outpatient clinics, and community pharmacies), that contribute to the development of professional skills when promoting early contact with patients [27, 32]. These environments showed positive results in academic performance and decreasing failure rates, reinforcing their formative potential [46]. Thus, combing environments increases social engagement and facilitates learning from medication management until patient-centered care, meeting social and work labor demands [11, 47, 48].

Learning theories

A scoping review suggested that the absence of consensus about the definition of “learning environment” in the healthcare field limits the possibility of conducting multicenter research to evaluate the impact of different curriculum contexts as well as their improvement and adaptation to national realities [8, 10, 49]. Although concept differences, it is important to highlight that the studies analyzed were based on different learning theories which strengthen the person-centered educational process [26, 27, 30, 34, 35].

No theory isolated is sufficient to explain the complexity of learning, therefore it is necessary to consider them in a complementary way [50, 51]. Thus, using these theories in areas such as Medicine and Nursing has been the basis for structuring these environments and applying them in real world, allowing mediation through language and culture (Vygotsky) [52, 53], social interaction (Constructivism) [54–56], shared decision-making among students and teachers (Students as Partners) [35, 57–62], and combining real experience, simulation, and case discussions (Kolb) [63]. In summary, the agreed definition of learning environment and the use of complementary theories can facilitate curricular changes and training of pharmacists that are caregivers.

Strategies for teaching pharmaceutical care

The teaching and learning strategies reported in studies emphasize more active participation of students. Using active methods such as PBL can stimulate the teaching of Pharmaceutical Care through problem-solving, self-directed learning, critical thinking, and communication [46]. A metanalysis pointed out that Pharmacy students enrolled in PBL presented higher performance in middle and final exams compared to traditional methods [64]. In different contexts, such as in Japan and Norway, PBL applied to pharmaceutical technology and clinical activities showed improvements in confidence, thinking, and practical applications [65, 66].

A curriculum planning that adopts different methods of complementary education, goes beyond the traditional theoretical model by increasing exposure in clinical, technological, and collaborative scenarios [28, 30, 35, 36]. These experiences contribute to valuation of different learning styles and deepen the educational process, aligning educational results with real world demands [27, 33, 34]. Therefore, the curricular update must understand not only the content changes but also the characteristics of student generations and their interrelationships with professional practice [37, 67, 68].

Limitation

The present study shows strengths and limitations. As strengths, it is highlighted how different components of learning environments complement each other to develop the required competencies for patient-centered acting. As for limitations, the research revealed a lack of studies about developing learning environments for teaching Pharmaceutical Care. In addition, the absence of concept consensus on these environments could have limited the identification and inclusion of relevant studies in this review.

Conclusion

Although the relevance of the theme, publication about learning environments for teaching Pharmaceutical Care is yet scarce and heterogeneous, without a clearly defined concept and with diverse variables related to physical, virtual or hybrid components that influence personal, social, and organizational aspects during educational process. While the findings suggest the effectiveness of innovative strategies, it is important that these environments explore the use of active methods, be reproducible in scenarios both inside and outside the university and have the capability to be adapted to different learning styles, organizational models, and social and cultural contexts. Future research should deepen understanding of learning environments for teaching Pharmaceutical Care to strengthen scientific production and guide training of future pharmacists who are better prepared for patient-centered practices.
