Effects of language sampling context on complex syntax production of deaf and hard of hearing children
Authors: Jena McDaniel, Camryn Lowe, Angie Walker, C. Melanie Schuele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study is designed to analyse the degree to which young deaf and hard of hearing (DHH) children produce complex syntax in spoken English across different language sampling contexts. Forty-six DHH children aged 3 to 8 years completed narrative, conversation, and play language samples with an examiner. We coded the transcribed language samples for two precursors to complex syntax and 12 types of complex syntax. These 14 types formed six categories. For overall complex syntax production, participants produced complex syntax more densely at the utterance level in the conversation context than the play context. The complex syntax category analyses revealed differences in the proportion of multiple complex syntax categories in the conversation context relative to the narrative and play contexts. Complex syntax production varied systematically across sampling contexts. Therefore, sampling context should be considered when eliciting and interpreting complex syntax production in DHH children.

Children need to use complex syntax accurately for a variety of social and academic tasks beginning in the preschool years. For example, subordinate clauses link events temporally (e.g. before, when, and while clauses) or show cause and effect (e.g. because and unless clauses) within a narrative. As another example, subject relative clauses concisely specify particular individuals or items (e.g. The girl who is…). These examples illustrate just a few of the many uses of complex syntax. Complex syntax is expected to appear soon after the emergence of word combinations (Barako Arndt & Schuele, 2013; Bloom & Capatides, 1987; Bloom et al., 1984; Diessel, 2004; Limber, 1973). By age four, children with typical language skills can use most types of complex syntax accurately for spoken English (Werfel et al., 2021). Given this relatively early emergence, language evaluations of preschool and school-age children should routinely evaluate complex syntax production.

Information on the production of complex syntax is particularly important for deaf and hard of hearing (DHH) children who as a group show areas of need in complex syntax for spoken language (Klieve at al., 2023; Werfel et al., 2021). This area of need may be due to a variety of factors including reduced audibility or degraded auditory signal, smaller lexicons, reduced linguistic knowledge, and weaknesses in incidental learning for language (Coyne et al., 2007; Lederberg & Spencer, 2009; Lederberg et al., 2000; Lund & Douglas, 2016; Pittman, 2008; Stelmachowicz et al., 2004). The degree to which DHH children have access to language, for spoken and/or signed language, varies widely and is influenced by multiple complex factors, especially during early childhood. Reduced access to a high-quality language input is expected to hamper linguistic development. Therefore, DHH children need careful monitoring for their language skills, including complex syntax, to maximise language outcomes. Language sampling offers an important method for evaluating the production of complex syntax, especially for DHH children. We aim to provide additional evidence of how to use language sampling effectively to evaluate the production of complex syntax in young DHH children. We evaluate the influence of language sampling context on complex syntax production for this purpose.

Complex syntax production of DHH children

A recent scoping review by Klieve et al. (2023) summarised studies of complex language comprehension and production in DHH children. Complex language was defined more broadly than complex syntax, and few included studies exclusively addressed complex syntax production. Therefore, notable gaps persist in the extant literature on how often DHH children produce complex syntax and the diversity of types produced. Werfel et al. (2021) focused specifically on complex syntax production and found that 4-year-old DHH children produced utterances with a lower density of complex syntax than same-aged peers with typical hearing. The DHH children also produced fewer correct coordinate clauses, subordinate clauses, and simple infinitives and were less accurate producing simple infinitives, full propositional complement clauses, and subject relative clauses than same-aged peers with typical hearing.

Many of the other studies evaluated complex syntax production along with other broader language structures or features. For example, multiple studies reported lower performance on the Clinical Evaluation of Language Fundamentals (CELF) Formulated Sentences subtest than same-aged peers with typical hearing (Boons et al., 2013; Kronenberger & Pisoni, 2019; Spencer et al., 2003). This subtest asks children to create sentences of increasing complexity (i.e. simple, compound, and complex sentences) using specific target words or phrases when shown a related image. Difficulty producing complex syntax could contribute to these observed differences creating sentences on the subtest. In contrast, Socher et al. (2020) did not identify a difference between DHH children and same-aged peers with typical hearing in performance on the same subtest.

Direct evidence of weaknesses in complex syntax in DHH children is relatively limited. The replicated findings of lower mean length of utterance in morphemes (MLU) and syntax skills for DHH children than for same-aged peers with typical hearing bolster the argument for suspecting areas of need in complex syntax production for DHH children. Numerous studies have found that DHH children exhibit a lower MLU than same-aged peers with typical hearing (e.g. Hammer, 2010; Koehlinger et al., 2013; Werfel, 2018). Despite relatively few studies on complex syntax specifically, weaknesses in broader syntax skills relative to age expectations as well as other language domains (e.g. vocabulary) have also been identified across multiple studies (e.g. Antia et al., 2020; Duchesne et al., 2009; Geers et al., 2009; Moeller et al., 2010). Therefore, when evaluating the language skills of individual DHH children within clinical and educational settings, it is imperative to evaluate whether the child presents with difficulties producing utterances with complex syntax.

Evaluating complex syntax in DHH children

The production of complex syntax can be evaluated through a variety of methods. Klieve et al. (2023) found that language samples were the most common method (15 of 42 included studies), followed by a combination of methods (12 of 42). The combination of methods was most often a language sample along with standardised assessments. Language samples present an opportunity to capture information that may be overlooked on norm-referenced language assessments, especially for DHH children. DHH preschool children who scored within the average range on norm-referenced language assessments (i.e. within 1.5 SD of the mean) performed worse than same-aged peers with typical hearing on several language sample metrics – MLU, number of different words, and use of Brown’s 14 grammatical morphemes (Werfel & Douglas, 2017). Thus, using language samples identified areas of need overlooked when relying exclusively on norm-referenced language assessments.

The psychometric weaknesses, risk for over-identifying and under-identifying language impairment, and restricted purposes of norm-referenced assessments also support exploring other assessment options, especially for identifying specific areas of need for intervention (Abel et al., 2017; Hendricks & Adlof, 2017; Hoffman et al., 2011; McCauley & Swisher, 1984; Peña et al., 2006; Spaulding et al., 2006). Norm-referenced language assessments are designed to determine how an individual child performs relative to same-age peers and generally focus broadly on language skills. Therefore, their utility for detailing proficiency for particular language skills, including production of complex syntax, is limited. Further, no known published norm-referenced assessments directly or exclusively assess complex syntax production. Currently available norm-referenced assessments evaluate language production more broadly with limited opportunities to specifically evaluate complex syntax skills. As a result, complex syntax abilities may be concealed. Language samples offer the opportunity to evaluate the degree to which DHH children produce complex syntax overall as well as specific types of complex syntax within an ecologically valid setting. Language samples can be collected in a manner that more directly aligns with daily language tasks across home, school, and community environments than norm-referenced assessment tasks, which are generally highly structured. They also permit more flexible evaluation of specific skills depending on the child’s language level and hypothesised areas of strength and need (e.g. tense marking, complex syntax, narrative structure).

Influence of language sampling context on language production

There are important considerations when using language sampling to evaluate language skills including the sampling context, sample length (e.g. minutes or utterances) and variables derived. For this article we focus on the effects of sampling context on complex syntax production. If children tend to produce more complex syntax tokens (i.e. individual instances of complex syntax production) or more complex syntax types (i.e. unique dependent clause structures) in one context than another, those patterns need to be considered when interpreting results. Failing to understand the influence of sampling context risks misinterpretation of children’s performance, for example, failing to address areas of need or mistakenly interpreting age-appropriate performance as below-age expectations.

Language samples may be collected from a variety of sampling contexts including narratives (e.g. wordless picture book, personal narrative), conversation, and play. Prior investigations have evaluated the influence of language sampling contexts on expressive language skills for children with typical development and children with disabilities (e.g. autism, Down syndrome [DS], and Fragile X syndrome [FXS]). These investigations have focused primarily on the number and length of utterances. Children with typical development (TD) aged 5 to 9 years exhibited greater talkativeness in a conversation context than in a narrative context (i.e. wordless picture book; Abbeduto et al., 1995). In contrast, they used longer utterances in the narrative context than in the conversation context (Abbeduto et al., 1995). Children with disabilities have shown some similar patterns, but differences also have been identified. Like children with TD, school-age children (aged 9 to 20 years) with DS and FXS exhibited greater talkativeness in the conversation context than in the narrative context (Abbeduto et al., 1995). In addition, children and adolescents with DS and FXS produced longer utterances in the narrative context than in the conversation context (Abbeduto et al., 1995; Kover et al., 2012). Individuals aged 12 to 21 years with DS exhibited a greater MLU for narratives elicited from a wordless picture book than those elicited from an interview (Miles et al., 2006), but children with TD (2 to 5 years old) did not exhibit that difference. Eight- and nine-year-old children with specific language impairment (SLI) produced more utterances in the conversation context compared with a play context; however, their utterances had a greater MLU in play compared with conversation (Evans & Craig, 1992). Additionally, preschool children with SLI produced longer utterances in a narrative context than in conversation (Wagner et al., 2000). However, they produced more complex verb forms (e.g. verb phrase with auxiliary or modal verb) in the conversation context.

Few studies have evaluated the effects of the sampling context on complex syntax production. The one known study on this topic for DHH children (Cowan et al., 2025) compared use of complex syntax across conversation (i.e. sharing personal narratives; Hadley, 1998), expository (i.e. describing rules of a game or how to care for pets), and narrative retell (i.e. describing a favourite movie, show, or book) contexts for 4-year-old DHH children who use spoken English as well as age-matched and language-matched comparison groups. All groups produced a higher proportion of utterances with complex syntax in the expository context than the conversation context.

Losh and Capps (2003) found that children with autism exhibited lower complex syntax skills than children with TD for personal narratives but not for wordless picture book narratives. Evans and Craig (1992) found that children with SLI (aged 8 to 9 years) produced most of their advanced syntactic features (including complex syntax features such as conjunction and relative clauses) in conversation (71%) rather than in play (29%). Southwood and Russell (2004) also identified use of more complex language (i.e. complex syntax and other types of elaboration such as noun phrases) in conversation relative to a play context for five-year-old Afrikaans-speaking boys with typical language skills. More complex language tasks are hypothesised to elicit more instances of complex syntax because use of complex syntax completes the task more efficiently by combining multiple ideas within a single utterance (Cowan et al., 2025). Some contexts, such as play, do not necessarily require or directly encourage a child to talk about relations between characters or events that would benefit from complex syntax in the way that a narrative and/or expository task might. As a result, the child may not display their most advanced complex syntax abilities. These limited findings support further evaluation of complex syntax more specifically as well as whether previously identified patterns in other populations and the one known study in DHH children will replicate for DHH children. Given variations in language strengths and needs across different populations, the effects of the sampling context may not be consistent across populations based on the degree to which sampling contexts tap particular language skills.

Purpose and research questions

The purpose of this report is to analyse the degree to which young DHH children produce complex syntax across different language sampling contexts (i.e. narrative, conversation, and play) in spoken English. Understanding how sampling context influences complex syntax production will aid interpretation of this skill for appropriate intervention planning at the child and population levels.

We address two related research questions (RQs) for a sample of DHH children aged 3 to 8 years from a large longitudinal, population-based study of young DHH children. Does the degree to which DHH children produce complex syntax in spoken English – overall (RQ1) and for each complex syntax category (RQ2) – vary across language sampling contexts? The amount of complex syntax is quantified by the proportion of utterances with complex syntax and complex syntax density at the utterance and sample levels. Based on available data from other populations of children, we predicted that participants would provide a higher proportion of utterances with complex syntax and greater complex syntax density in the conversation context relative to the play context and in the conversation context relative to the narrative context. We also predicted differences in use of complex syntax categories, including greater proportions of relatively early developing categories (i.e. precursors, infinitive clauses) in the play context, complement clauses in the narrative context, and conjunction clauses in the conversation context relative to the other contexts.

Methods

Caregivers provided written informed consent for participants. The University of Kansas (#145175) and Vanderbilt University Medical Center (#221188) institutional review boards approved all study procedures.

Source of data

The language samples were drawn from a sample of DHH children assessed through a state programme in accordance with a state legislative mandate ‘to assess, monitor and track the language developmental milestones of children who are deaf or hard of hearing from birth through the age of eight’ (Language Assessment Program for Children who are Deaf/Hard of Hearing [LAP-DHH], 2018, para. 1). Via this programme children at least annually receive a multi-component language evaluation in spoken English and/or American Sign Language (ASL). The language assessments, which include a language sample, address receptive, expressive and social communication skills. The current participants are a subset of children enrolled in the state programme who completed spoken English language samples in at least the narrative and conversation contexts between September 2020 and October 2023. If a participant completed language samples multiple times during this period, samples from the most recent date with the most contexts were used. All participants were at least 3 years old at the time of sampling because the narrative and conversation language sampling contexts are administered once children in the state programme turn 3.

Each participant engaged in semi-structured language samples with a LAP-DHH examiner, all of whom are certified speech-language pathologists, teachers of the deaf, or early childhood special educators who work regularly with DHH children and are familiar with this population. The sampling protocol is available upon request from the first author. The examiner followed principles for behaviour and talk to elicit communication including following the child’s lead, joining in the child’s level of play, and responding promptly to the child. The examiner also avoided giving directives, offering too many questions or comments, and speaking too quickly. Language samples were elicited in up to three contexts per child, depending on the child’s age: (a) narrative (3 years and older; approximately 8 to 13 minutes; wordless picture book), (b) conversation (3 years and older; 12 minutes), and (c) play (3 and 4 years old; 12 minutes; Abbeduto et al., 2020; Brady et al., 2014; Hadley, 1998). To illustrate, at 3;5 a child would have narrative, conversation, and play samples, but at 5;5 a child would have narrative and conversation samples only.

Procedures for the narrative and conversation context were adapted from Abbeduto et al. (2020). For the narrative context, participants narrated one of two wordless picture books (i.e. Frog Goes to Dinner or Frog on His Own). Each child’s book was selected randomly based on a coin toss. First, the examiner showed the participant each page of the book for 10 seconds to familiarise the participant with the book. Second, the participant was asked to tell the story as the examiner shows each page (i.e. second pass through book). For the conversation context, the examiner engaged the participant in a semi-structured conversation around child appropriate topics (e.g. school, family, and pets). Specific topics were discussed in a specified order for consistency across participants, including an introductory topic specific to the child’s interests (e.g. favourite toy). The examiner asked the child open-ended questions and queries for expansion (e.g. ‘Tell me more about X’.) and minimised their own contributions. For the play context, the standard toy set included a doll house with plastic and wooden figurines and furniture as well as a city set with rescue vehicles and buildings. Children were free to choose which toys to play with.

Procedural fidelity

Prior to collecting language samples with participants, examiners had to achieve the procedural fidelity criterion for two samples for each context they administered. Procedural fidelity items include but are not limited to describing the directions, allowing sufficient time for the child to describe the story on each page, and refraining from over prompting for the narrative context, introducing the topics in order, asking appropriate open-ended questions, and refraining from talking over the child for the conversation context, and asking open-ended questions, offering topic-continuing comments, and following the child’s lead for the play context. The procedural fidelity scoresheet is available upon request from the first author. Once examiners met the training criterion, one out of every five language samples was randomly selected to check for procedural fidelity. Examiners were blind to designation. On a rolling basis, examiners received feedback from fidelity checks of their samples to facilitate continued accuracy following the procedures. This feedback included the examiner’s percent accuracy, comments related to required steps followed well, and observed deviations from the protocol.

Procedural fidelity for language sample protocol compliance was evaluated for 26% of the included samples for the narrative context, 30% for conversation and 25% for play. Only two narrative samples and one conversation sample fell below the criterion. The means and SDs for the procedural fidelity results for the narrative, conversation, and play samples were 88% (SD = 9%), 94% (SD = 5%), and 96% (SD = 4%), respectively. The overall mean percent accuracy for procedural fidelity on the study samples was 92% (SD = 7%). Indications for retraining (i.e. two consecutive samples for the same context below the procedural fidelity criterion) were not met.

Participants

Participants included 46 DHH children aged 3;6 to 8;11 (mean = 5;3; SD = 18.7 months; female = 26, male = 20). Twenty participants were reported to use ASL to some degree. Of these 20 participants, one had ASL as their primary language and nine had English. For those with English as their primary language, they were commonly reported to use speech with occasional sign support expressively. Ten participants used ASL and English without a primary language designated. No participants were native ASL users. Two participants used Spanish in addition to spoken English. Spanish was the primary language for one and English for the other. Seven participants were diagnosed with another disability (e.g. autism, visual impairment). Caregivers identified 35 participants as White, three as Black or African American, two as Hispanic or Latino, two as two or more races, and one as Asian. Information was not reported for three participants. Socioeconomic information was not collected systematically.

Hearing level and current use of hearing technology is requested from caregivers annually within the LAP-DHH. The 43 participants with available hearing level data were heterogenous as to hearing level, type of hearing loss, and use of hearing technology, as shown in Table 1. Of the three participants without hearing level data available, one uses bilateral cochlear implants suggesting higher (louder) hearing levels. Hearing technology use information was not provided for one participant. Of the 28 participants with documentation of the onset of their hearing loss, hearing loss was present at birth for 27 participants and acquired after birth for one.

At the time of the included assessment, 27 participants were 3 to 4 years old, as shown in Table 1. Therefore, they contributed language samples from all three contexts, as described under Procedures, except for one participant (aged 4;11) who was missing the play context. The 19 participants aged 5 to 8 each contributed language samples from the narrative and conversation contexts. When data were extracted for this study, there were 87 children in the larger study who were at least 3 years old. All children who had an English language sample for at least the narrative and conversation contexts were included as a participant in this study. Reasons a child in the larger study would not have had an English sample include using only ASL, not yet having participated in the age 3 language samples, and being enrolled without data collection having commenced.

Procedures

Language sample transcription and initial coding

Trained research assistants (RAs) and the first author transcribed the language samples, divided utterances at the C-unit level, and completed grammatical morpheme coding using a detailed manual (available from the first author upon request) in preparation for analysis using the Systematic Analysis of Language Transcripts software (Miller & Chapman, 2020).

Transcriptionists used high-quality headphones (i.e. Sony MDR-7506), a foot pedal, and Express Scribe to transcribe the samples. Following procedures described by Abbeduto et al. (2020), a transcriptionist pair was assigned to each sample. One (lead) transcriptionist transcribed the video-recorded sample and coded for grammatical morphemes. The other (secondary) transcriptionist reviewed the written transcript while watching the language sample video recording noting disagreements. Then the transcriptionist pair reviewed the disagreements and decided what to accept and reject to create the final language transcript and grammatical morpheme coding.

Complex syntax coding

Trained RAs coded the final language transcripts for complex syntax according to the complex syntax manual (Barako Arndt & Schuele, 2013; Schuele, 2009; Schuele & Dykes, 2005; Werfel et al., 2021). For training, RAs read the coding manual, engaged in a didactic presentation with the first author, and discussed any questions. They coded language samples of nonparticipants until they achieved at least 80% small/large agreement (i.e. for the two values from the independent coders, the smaller value is divided by the larger value and multiplied by 100) for the analysed variables for three expertly coded language samples before coding independently.

Schuele (2009, 2013) has differentiated complex syntax from complex sentences. Complex syntax includes utterances with at least one dependent clause; child utterances coded for complex syntax thus include utterances with only a dependent clauses(s) and utterances with an independent clause plus one or more dependent clauses (i.e. complex sentences). The analysis of complex syntax provides a more complete picture of a child’s production of dependent clauses than only analysing dependent clauses in complex sentences. Complex sentences always include complex syntax, but complex syntax is not always produced within a complex sentence. If only complex sentences are analysed, utterances with only a dependent clause would be excluded from the analysis (e.g. ‘because they are so cute’, in response to ‘Why are they your favorite?’).

Samples were coded for two precursors to complex syntax and 12 types of complex syntax. Each complex syntax token was assigned a code. Each utterance with one of the 12 types was also appended with a [cs] code. The 12 types are grouped into six complex syntax categories. See Table 2 for details. All at least partially intelligible utterances were coded, not just complete and intelligible utterances. On average across contexts and participants, 90% (SD = 16%) of participants’ utterances were at least partially intelligible. More than 80% of utterances were at least partially intelligible for 36 participants. For two participants fewer than 50% of utterances were at least partially intelligible.

Three complex syntax variables were derived for each sample to quantify complex syntax production overall (i.e. across all complex syntax types) for RQ1. (a) The proportion of utterances with complex syntax was calculated by dividing the number of utterances with at least one complex syntax token (indicated by a [cs] code) by the total number of utterances (Werfel et al., 2021). The total number of utterances includes all verbal utterances, including partially unintelligible utterances, and excludes nonverbal utterances (e.g. ‘uh’ and ‘hmm’). (b) Complex syntax density at the utterance level was calculated by dividing the number of complex syntax tokens by the number of utterances with a [cs] code (Schuele & Dykes, 2005). (c) Complex syntax density at the sample level is calculated by the number of complex syntax tokens in the sample divided by the total number of utterances in the sample (Werfel et al., 2021). Utterances that included only complex syntax precursors of reduced infinitives or let’s clauses did not receive the [cs] code. Therefore, these utterances are not included in the proportion of utterances with complex syntax or either complex syntax density variable.

To calculate the complex syntax category variables for RQ2, the number of tokens for all types included in the category was summed and that sum was divided by the total number of complex syntax tokens, as described above, for the context of interest. For example, for the infinitive clauses category, the number of simple infinitive and unmarked infinitive clauses the participant produced in the play context were summed and divided by the total number of complex syntax tokens the participant produced in the play context.

Reliability for transcribing and coding language samples

For transcription and grammatical morpheme coding reliability, 20% of the language samples were transcribed and coded independently by a second pair of transcriptionists. As with the pair of primary transcriptionists, one (lead) transcriptionist first transcribed the sample, then the other (secondary) transcriptionist reviewed the transcript, and then the pair resolved disagreements. Primary transcriptionists were blinded to which samples would be coded for reliability. The analysed variables were compared across transcriptionist pairs via intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) to yield reliability values. The ICCs for all variables well exceeded the benchmark of .70 for ‘very good’ (Mitchell, 1979; number of complete and intelligible utterances: ICC = .96; MLU in words: ICC = .97; MLU in morphemes: ICC = .97; total number of words: ICC = .97; number of different words: ICC = .96).

Similarly, a second coder independently coded 20% of samples for complex syntax coding reliability. The RAs who initially coded complex syntax were blinded to which samples would be used for reliability. Discrepancy discussions were conducted regularly to prevent coder drift. The ICC for total instances of complex syntax (i.e. [cs] codes assigned to utterances) was strong at .98. All analysed complex syntax categories also exceeded the .70 benchmark (conjunction clauses: ICC = .98; infinitive clauses: ICC = .99; complement clauses: ICC = .92; relative clauses: ICC = .73). We excluded the ‘other’ category from this analysis due to relatively few instances, the diversity of utterances included within this category, and relatively low reliability (ICC = .51).

Analysis plan

We conducted a series of t-tests to compare the proportion of utterances with complex syntax, complex syntax density (utterance level, sample level), and the proportion of complex syntax for each category across pairs of contexts. These t-tests enable pairwise comparisons across the different contexts, which were of primary interest given our research questions. We applied a Bonferroni correction to address the family-wise error rate for multiple comparisons.

Results

RQ1: Does the degree to which DHH children produce complex syntax overall vary across language sampling contexts?

Participants produced a higher density of complex syntax at the utterance level in the conversation context than the play context with a moderate effect size (t = 2.91, p = 0.009, d = 0.64). No differences were observed between the other contexts. See Table 3 for details including descriptive statistics, which display the relatively large standard deviation observed for each variable. Note that fewer participants are included when comparing complex syntax density at the utterance level than the other two complex syntax variables because mathematically participants must produce at least one utterance coded as [cs] to avoid a denominator of zero. For the proportion of utterances with complex syntax and complex syntax density at the sample level, no significant differences were identified across contexts. The effect sizes comparing these variables were small to small-to-medium (d = 0.19 to 0.41).

RQ2: Does the degree to which DHH children produce each category of complex syntax vary across language sampling contexts?

For participants who produced complex syntax (including precursors), we analysed the degree to which they used each category. Table 4 displays the mean proportions and standard deviations of utterances included in each category. Descriptively, infinitive clauses were most common for the narrative (mean proportion = .42, SD = .28) and play (mean proportion = .42, SD = .22) contexts. Conjunction clauses were most common for the conversation context (mean proportion = .44, SD = .27).

We compared the proportions for each complex syntax category across the three contexts. See Table 5 for Cohen’s d effect sizes and significance for the pairwise comparisons and Table 6 for a summary of the results. Of their complex syntax tokens (including precursors), participants on average produced a greater proportion of precursors of complex syntax (i.e. reduced infinitives and let’s clauses) in the play context (mean = .34, SD = .24) than in the conversation (mean = .08, SD = .19) context. Approximately a third of the tokens were precursors for the play context relative to less than a tenth for the narrative and conversation contexts. The participants’ utterances were more likely to include reduced infinitives or let’s clauses within the play context than in the conversation context (n = 22, d = 0.78, p = 0.001) with a large effect size.

For infinitive clauses, participants produced a greater proportion in the play context than the conversation context and the effect size was large (n = 22, d = 0.84, p < 0.001). Other infinitive clause comparisons were non-significant. For complement clauses, participants produced a greater proportion in the narrative context than in the conversation context with a medium-to-large effect size (n = 37, d = 0.60, p < 0.001). Other complement clause comparisons were non-significant. Participants produced a greater proportion of conjunction clauses in the conversation context compared with the play context (n = 22, d = 1.40, p < 0.001) and the narrative context (n = 37, d = 0.84, p < 0.001) with large effect sizes. No context differences were observed for relative clauses.

Discussion

The purpose of this report was to evaluate whether the degree to which young DHH children produce complex syntax varies by language sampling context. Because we analysed language samples from children who completed all contexts on the same day, we can isolate the effects of the contexts from the effects of participants and time.

Overall complex syntax production across contexts

For overall complex syntax, participants produced a higher density of complex syntax at the utterance level in the conversation context relative to the play context. Participants showed a greater ability to include multiple complex syntax tokens within a single utterance during conversation than play. These findings are consistent with Evans and Craig (1992) and Southwood and Russell (2004) who identified greater use of more complex syntactic structures in a conversation context relative to a play context for children with SLI and typical language, respectively. Although Cowan et al. (2025) reported a higher proportion of utterances with complex syntax attempts in an expository context than a conversational context for DHH 4-year-old children, differences in tasks across studies may explain the difference. The conversation context we used shares features with expository context Cowan and colleagues used. For example, children may be asked during the conversation context to describe how they care for their pets or play their favourite game, which were expository tasks for Cowan et al. (2025). Thus, in both cases, the contexts that included requests for explaining tasks elicited more complex syntax than those that did not.

Production of precursors and complex syntax categories across contexts

Several patterns emerged in the production of complex syntax categories by DHH children across the language sample contexts. The participants tended to use precursors and simpler forms of complex syntax more frequently in the play context. The more informal nature of play may encourage children to use more informal and simpler language relative to the more structured conversation task. The children may engage in play activities that do not necessitate complex syntax to convey their intended meaning. It is also possible that as children play, they devote more cognitive resources to their play activities and focus less on conveying more complex messages that require increased language complexity. As a result, they may tend to produce linguistic forms for which they have a higher level of mastery than those that are developing.

The participants produced a higher proportion of complement clauses within the narrative context relative to the conversation context. Narratives may encourage children to produce more complement clauses than conversation samples due to the explicit inclusion of characters within the narrative task. Children can use complement clauses to describe characters’ thoughts. Examples include ‘The frog didn’t even know he was going there’ ([fpc]), ‘but then he was trying to tell the other boy what he did do’ ([wfc]), and ‘and then he didn’t know what to do because he didn’t see the frog’ ([wnfc]).

Participants tended to produce proportionally more conjunction clauses in the conversation context than in the other contexts. Unlike the other contexts, the conversation context does not provide visual supports to attend to (e.g. pictures for the narrative context and toys and play actions for the play context). Therefore, the child may provide more information and connect events more explicitly in the conversation context than in other contexts due to the lack of a shared situation and shared background information. Additionally, the follow-up questions and prompts from the examiner in the conversation context may encourage children to use conjunction clauses. For example, using a subordinate clause with ‘because’ is often appropriate when responding to ‘why’ questions. Answering questions about temporal events may also encourage the child to use subordinate clauses, such as ‘when’, ‘before’ and ‘after’ clauses.

Interpreting complex syntax production considering identified context effects

Given these systematic differences in complex syntax production across language sampling contexts for DHH children, it is important to consider context when interpreting results for research and clinical purposes. When evaluating results relative to comparison data, one should consider the sampling context of that comparison data and the direction of effect. For example, children are expected to produce complex syntax more densely in conversation than in a play context. One should be cautious in concluding that a child has low complex syntax density based on a play sample if their density is mildly reduced relative to comparison data from conversation samples. However, if the child scores above expectations on a play sample relative to comparison data from conversation samples, one could be more assured that the child is exhibiting expected complex syntax production skills. As another example, a child may appear to produce more complement clauses than expected within a narrative context if comparison data are from conversation and play contexts. Because children on average produce a larger proportion of complement clauses within narrative contexts than the other two, if context is not considered, the child’s skills may be overestimated.

If a researcher or clinician is specifically interested in a child’s production of a particular complex syntax category, they can use context(s) most likely to elicit those complex syntax types. For example, consider using a conversation context to elicit a relatively high proportion of conjunction clauses (i.e. coordinate and subjective clauses). If complement clauses are of particular interest, consider using a narrative context.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations and how we plan to address them in the future should be acknowledged. First, because the participants produced relatively few relative clauses during the language samples, the results for this category may be less stable than for other categories. Analysis of relative clauses may benefit from use of a direct elicitation task rather than a semi-structured language sample (Schuele & Nicholls, 2000). The sample size is reasonable for young DHH children and commensurate with published samples in the literature. Nonetheless, the presented analyses should be replicated with a larger sample of DHH children to evaluate for sample-specific results and evaluated with other populations to determine the degree to which the findings may or may not be specific to this population. We aim to have a larger population-based sample with time with which more sophisticated statistical analyses can be applied to evaluate whether the current results replicate, to conduct separate analyses for participants with and without additional diagnoses (e.g. autism, intellectual disability), and to consider the effects of the participant characteristics (e.g. age, language level, language access, bilingualism).

Other future directions include longitudinal analyses across the complex syntax production measures to provide stronger evidence about expected growth over time than the presented cross-sectional analyses. Such information will help researchers and clinicians evaluate the degree to which children are making expected progress. We also plan to evaluate how long a sample needs to be to obtain stable estimates of complex syntax production to help use resources wisely.

Conclusion

The findings show systematic variation in complex syntax production across narrative, conversation, and play contexts for DHH children. In the conversation context participants produced complex syntax more densely and produced higher proportions of conjunction clauses than in the other contexts. In the narrative context participants produced a higher proportion of complement clauses than in the conversation context. Participants tended to produce more precursors to complex syntax and simple infinitives in the play context than in the conversation context. This systematic variation should be considered when planning how to collect language samples and when analysing the results.
