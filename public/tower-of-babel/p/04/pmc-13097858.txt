What role do self-liking and self-competence play in the relationships between exercise identity and mental well-being in individuals who exercise regularly?
Authors: Meva Demir Kaya, Feridun Kaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWell-being research frequently examines self-esteem, given its substantial impact on individuals’ self-image and perception of self-worth. High self-esteem is associated with positive emotions, self-respect, and a sense of value, making it a fundamental indicator of well-being. Additionally, identity formation is closely linked to self-esteem, with positive identity development supporting higher self-esteem. Conversely, elevated self-esteem helps individuals navigate identity-related challenges. As the relationship between identity and self-esteem is bidirectional, understanding the cause-effect dynamics between these variables is essential for advancing well-being research. The aim of this study is to examine the role of self-liking and self-competence in the relationship between exercise identity and mental well-being.Subjects and methodsThe participants of the study consisted of 140 men (33.7%) and 276 women (66.3%), aged between 18 and 50, residing in Türkiye, attempting weight loss and regularly exercised in 2023. Correlation analysis was used to determine the relationship between the study variables. Regression-based parallel mediation analysis was conducted to examine the role of the self-liking and self-competence dimensions of self-esteem, in the relationship between exercise identity and mental well-being.ResultsThe significance of the mediation analysis was tested using the bootstrap method. As a result of the parallel mediation analysis based on regression, both self-liking (ab1 = 0.095, SE = 0.028, 95% CI [0.040, 0.152]) and self-competence (ab2 = 0.034, SE = 0.018, 95% CI [0.003, 0.072]) mediated the relationship between exercise identity and mental well-being.ConclusionThe findings of this study reveal that exercise identity is a significant predictor of mental well-being. Additionally, the finding that self-esteem plays a mediating role in the relationship between exercise identity and mental well-being is also considered a significant and novel contribution. These findings shed light on the factors influencing the development of mental well-being and provide valuable guidance for psychologists working in this field and conducting intervention studies.

Introduction

Factors that motivate individuals to exercise regularly include the inactivity brought on by modern living [1], increased attention to health [2], appearance concerns [3], the increasing prevalence of gyms and their cultural adoption [4], and the desire to protect both physical and mental well-being [5]. Activities undertaken by individuals who dedicate a certain amount of time to exercise contribute to a sense of belonging and socialization [6]. This, in turn, can pave the way for individuals to develop an exercise-related identity. An exercise identity, constructed in individuals who position regular and consistent exercise as a part of their lives and identity [7], contributes to increased well-being by reducing the impact of psychological problems [8]. Exercise identity provides individuals with purpose and routine in their activities, thus serving as a meaningful engagement [9]. Because engaging the mind in a functional pursuit acts as a buffer against mental illness [10], exercise identity can be said to have a direct impact on mental well-being.

Individuals with an exercise identity increase their self-confidence and feel valued as they demonstrate consistency and continuity in their activities [11]. The actions taken by individuals who engage in exercise in a controlled manner, based on their own decisions, strengthen their sense of control and awareness, thereby increasing their self-esteem [12]. Self-esteem, in turn, contributes to improved mental well-being by acting as a shield against feelings such as self-blame, feelings of worthlessness, loneliness, depression, anxiety, and stress [13]. Therefore, it is thought that exercise identity will also affect mental well-being through self-esteem. While no studies have been found examining the relationship between self-esteem, considered as a mediator variable in the current study, and exercise identity, self-esteem is known to be both associated with exercise [12] and a predictor of mental mental well-being [14]. However, no study has examined the relationship between exercise identity and mental well-being by taking self-esteem into account. In this context, this study was planned to determine the relationship between exercise identity, self-esteem and mental well-being and to examine the mediating role of self-esteem.

Mental well-being

Since ancient times, thinkers have questioned what constitutes a good life for individuals, leading to the prominence of the concept of happiness. Over the years, happiness has been studied and expressed in various forms, including well-being, subjective well-being, and psychological well-being. Well-being is considered a broad term that includes the subjective dimensions of health, social factors, and material conditions, describing how well individuals are doing in life [15]. In the literature, subjective well-being and psychological well-being are frequently used as general indicators of well-being. More broadly, these two concepts are seen as representing positive affect (hedonia) and positive functioning (eudaimonia), or pleasure and personal satisfaction [16]. While positive affect and positive functioning have traditionally been modeled as distinct concepts, recent studies have shown that positive emotions and positive functioning can be sufficiently explained by a single general factor [17]. Additionally, the World Health Organization [18] stated that positive mental well-being is a cornerstone for the well-being and effective functioning of individuals and societies. Well-being, which encompasses cognitive and emotional characteristics that help us make sense of the world and make healthy decisions, is sometimes seen as an indicator of well-being [19] and in other cases is used synonymously with mental well-being [20].

Relationships between exercise identity and mental well-being

The literature shows that factors such as physical activity [21], physical health [22], appearance [3], body image [23], body self-confidence [24] are associated with well-being. Among these factors, many studies highlight the relationship between physical appearance, health, and well-being within the context of physical exercise [25–27]. Health problems, diseases, and risks experienced in society are considered factors that strengthen the relationship between well-being and physical exercise within the context of healthy living [28]. Recent studies show that health-related quality of life, physical exercise, and well-being were also interconnected [29]. In addition to all of this, it is thought that individuals place a significant importance on regular and consistent exercise in their lives to reduce body dissatisfaction [30] and to have a positive body image [31]. At this point, the concept of exercise identity draws attention, as engaging in regular exercise reflects individuals’ self-congruence and consistency [32]. Once individuals form an image of how they see themselves over time, it is expected that they will maintain consistency [33]. Therefore, the development of exercise adherence and exercise identity is quite important for individuals who regularly, consistently, and continuously incorporate exercise into their lives [34]. Exercise identity is defined as the “salience of an individual’s identification with exercise as an integral part of the concept of self” [7]. Exercise identity is shaped by attributing value and meaning to past experiences related to exercise. Moreover, it also influences future exercise behaviors, which is an indicator of continuity in exercise [35]. Generally speaking, mechanisms such as self-awareness, establishing personal continuity, and showing consistency-whether consciously or unconsciously-are involved in identity development [33].

Identity, which is the answer given by individuals to the question ‘Who am I?’, expresses the characteristics of being the same, continuity and uniqueness. Erikson [36], who is considered as the theorist who has influenced identity studies the most, especially addresses identity as the psychosocial development phase of adolescence, but he also suggests that identity develops and changes throughout life. Individuals continue to perceive themselves with changes in each life period. Individuals are expected to exhibit continuity, consistency, and harmony with each other in the dimensions of their identity in this process over time [36]. Considering that identity has both personal and social aspects [37], it can be said that this consistency and harmony should be both with the people themselves and with society. For example, individuals demonstrate consistency when they seek continuity in their self-image and habits [33] and this, in turn, can effect identity. Habits of health behaviors are also associated with identity development [38]. More specifically, exercise habits are also associated with exercise identity and behaviors [39]. In addition, the duration of an individual’s exercise habit is known to affect mental well-being [40]. Furthermore, when behaving in ways that contrary to their self-perception within their society, individuals are acting inconsistently with their self-view [32] and this situation may negatively affect well-being by creating a maladaptive image of the self.

Individuals’ evaluation of their lives as happy or unhappy depends on the answer they give regarding who they are. Therefore, a coherent identity formation is an important key to well-being. There are many studies in the relevant literature that examine identity formation and well-being both directly [41] and indirectly [42–44]. More specifically, studies addressing exercise identity and well-being are also increasing in the literature [45–47]. Erikson [36] considers identity as a multidimensional structure in the context of social roles (such as athlete, mother, teacher, worker) and individuals may show more commitment to some of them. Individuals who place exercise or sports, one of these roles, at the center of their lives make commitments athletic identity and this increases well-being [48]. Therefore, exercise identity may affect mental well-being in individuals who exercise regularly. Accordingly, the first hypothesis of the study is presented below.

H1: Exercise identity is a significant predictor of mental well-being.

Self-liking and self-competence as mediators

Self-esteem, which reflects a person’s self-image and how they evaluate their worth [49], is considered one of the key indicators of well-being and is recognized as a significant factor in experiencing positive and negative emotions [50]. Therefore, individuals with high self-esteem not only feel valuable but also respect themselves [51]. On the other hand, there is a known relationship between types of identity that individuals possess and self-esteem [52], and successful identity formation positively impacts self-esteem [53]. Moreover, high self-esteem serves as a resource to overcome identity-related problems [54]. Since the relationships between identity and self-esteem are examined bidirectionally, it is important to explore the cause-effect relationship between these variables.

Based on the information provided above, it appears that there is a relationship between identity, self-esteem, and well-being. In the literature, well-being and self-esteem are frequently examined in the context of identity formation, particularly in relation to identity styles and a dual-cycle identity model [52, 55–56]. Although it is well known in the literature that exercise identity and self-esteem are critical variables for mental well-being, no studies have been found that address the relationship between individuals’ exercise identity, self-esteem, and their mental well-being, which is assessed a key variable in achieving a ‘good life.’

In our study, the specified variables have been tested within the framework of the cognitive appraisal theory [57]. Exercise identity is the perception of oneself as a regular exerciser, and this perception is cognitively construed through positive self-evaluations (e.g., self-liking) and beliefs about one’s ability to succeed (self-competence). These cognitive evaluations, in turn, can strengthen one’s self-perception, thereby increasing life satisfaction and psychological well-being. When the cognitive appraisal model is adapted to the current study, the presence of an exercise identity can be seen as a stimulus. The primary appraisal process in the model is the perception of an individual’s exercise identity as congruent with personal values, meaningful, and positive. The second appraisal, which follows this process, can be seen as the formation of self-efficacy beliefs regarding one’s ability to maintain this lifestyle. This allows the individual to like themselves and feel competent. The psychological empowerment resulting from these feelings can be seen as an emotional outcome. Consequently, increased self-competence and self-liking can contribute to increased mental well-being. In other words, the contribution of exercise identity to well-being may be mediated by how an individual interprets this identity. Self-liking and self-competence are outcomes of the cognitive appraisal process. Positive cognitive appraisals can increase positive affect, thereby supporting mental well-being. Furthermore, it is known that integrating exercise regularly, consistently, and continuously into one’s life enhances well-being [46] and high self-esteem contributes to well-being [13], which allows us to state that a strong exercise identity and self-esteem play a significant role in increasing mental well-being. According to the relevant literature, individuals with schemas related to exercising score higher on role identities, and for schematic individuals, role identity is a significant predictor of emotional attitudes. These emotional attitudes appear to consist of the individual’s evaluation of situations as enjoyable–unenjoyable, and pleasant–unpleasant [58]. Moreover, it is known that physical exercise enhances subjective well-being and that exercise identity plays a mediating role in this relationship, while health consciousness positively influences this mediation process [59]. Namely, when individuals develop a strong exercise identity with an awareness of the positive outcomes of physical activity, their self-confidence increases, thereby enhancing their overall well-being. Considering that individuals’ giving importance to exercise in their lives [45, 48] and evaluating themselves positively [60] increase well-being, current study is expected to contribute to the literature on exercise identity, self-esteem, and well-being and allow a different perspective on intervention programs to increase mental well-being. In addition, individuals who exercise regularly have greater awareness, autonomy, purpose in life, and athletic competence [61], which may enable them to develop an exercise identity. So much so that exercise identity reflects the degree to which individuals evaluate themselves as individuals who regularly participate in physical activity and, as a result, are aware of their competence and health consciousness [59]. Therefore, because it is assumed that individuals who exercise regularly have a stronger tendency to possess an exercise identity, attention was paid to including individuals who exercise regularly in the current study. In this context, the second hypothesis of the study is as follows.

H2: Self-esteem plays a mediating role in the relationship between exercise identity and mental well-being.

Method

Participants

Of the 416 participants, 276 (66.3%) were female and 140 (33.7%) were male. Among them, 355 (85.3%) had completed high school, and 61 (14.7%) had graduated from university. When examining the economic status of the participants, 129 (31.0%) were in the low-income group, 181 (43.5%) were in the middle-income group, and 106 (25.5%) were in the high-income group. The average age of the participants was 21.43 (SD = 4.63, min.-max. = 18–50). The weight of female participants ranged from a minimum of 75 kg to a maximum of 97 kg (Mean = 86.61, SD = 4.25), while the weight of male participants ranged from a minimum of 85 kg to a maximum of 123 kg (Mean = 101.21, SD = 9.01). In general, the weight of participants ranged from 75 kg to 123 kg (Mean = 91.53, SD = 9.32). The height of female participants ranged from a minimum of 150 cm to a maximum of 180 cm (Mean = 163.39, SD = 7.44), and the height of male participants ranged from a minimum of 160 cm to a maximum of 194 cm (Mean = 175.34, SD = 8.86). Overall, participants’ height ranged from 150 cm to 194 cm (Mean = 167.41, SD = 9.74). The body mass index (BMI) of female participants ranged from a minimum of 26.50 to a maximum of 39.58 (Mean = 32.65, SD = 3.22). The BMI of male participants ranged from a minimum of 26.04 to a maximum of 42.97 (Mean = 33.07, SD = 3.65). In general, the BMI of participants ranged from 26.04 to 42.97 (Mean = 32.79, SD = 3.37). The sample size of the study was calculated using power analysis. For this purpose, G*Power 3.1 software’s a priori analysis was used [62]. For regression analysis, with 3 predictor variables, an alpha level of 0.05, a power rate of 0.95 (1-β), and a medium effect size, it was determined that the sample size should be 90 [63]. It can be stated that the sample size of 416 participants in this study is sufficient. The reason for choosing a medium effect size in this study is that studies, particularly in the field of exercise psychology, report moderate relationships between self-constructs (e.g., exercise identity) and psychological well-being outcomes [64–65]. Although the a priori analysis identified 90 participants, one reason for collecting data from over 400 participants was to increase statistical precision in mediation analyses. Larger samples are generally required for indirect effect estimates to be stable. Another reason was to increase the generalizability of the findings by covering a wider range of participants in terms of age, gender, and exercise habits.

Data collection tools

Personal information form

Developed by the researchers, this form includes questions regarding participants’ gender, age, and educational status.

Two-Dimensional Self-Esteem Scale (TDSES)

The Two-Dimensional Self-Esteem Scale (Self-Liking/Self-Competence) was developed by Tafarodi and Swan [66]. The 5-point Likert-type scale consists of 16 items (i.e. for Self-Liking, ‘I tend to see myself as worthless’ and i.e. for Self-Competence, ‘I am quite competent in what I do’). The original scale’s internal consistency coefficient ranges from 0.82 to 0.90. The validity and reliability analyses of the scale in the Turkish culture were conducted by Doğan [67]. In this study, the internal consistency coefficient for the self-liking dimension was found to be α = 0.86, and for the self-competence dimension, it was α = 0.75.

Exercise identity scale (EIS)

This scale, developed by Anderson and Cychosz [7], is designed to measure individuals’ exercise identity strength and identification with exercise. The scale consists of 9 items (i.e., ‘Exercise is something I think about often’) and is scored on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 7 = Strongly Agree). The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the original scale is α = 0.94. The validity and reliability analyses of the scale in the Turkish culture were conducted by Aygün [68]. The conformity factor analysis (CFA) conducted demonstrated that the scale was valid, and its internal consistency values indicated reliability. The reliability coefficient obtained in the Turkish adaptation study was α = 0.92 [68].

Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale (WEMWBS)

This scale was developed by Tennant et al. [20] to assess individuals’ mental well-being levels. The scale consists of 14 items (i.e., ‘I can make my own decisions’), evaluating subjective well-being and psychological well-being. The Short Form of the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale consists of 7 items in a 5-point Likert scale (1 = None of the time, 5 = All of the time), with positive statements. The items in the short form are more related to functioning than feelings. Validity analyses have shown that the scale consists of a single factor and has high construct validity [69]. Additionally, the Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient was calculated as α = 0.84 for the Norwegian sample and α = 0.86 for the Swedish sample. The Turkish adaptation study of the scale was conducted by Demirtaş and Baytemir [70]. The CFA results indicated that the model provided good fit to the data from two different samples and provided strong evidence for construct validity. The internal consistency reliability coefficient was found to be α = 0.86 for the first sample and α = 0.84 for the second sample, confirming the scale’s reliability [70].

Data analysis

In the study, the data were initially examined through preliminary analyses. Outliers, linearity, and normality were checked [71]. Outliers were identified by converting the measurements to standardized scores, and all values were found to be between − 3 and + 3, indicating no outliers in the dataset [72]. The normal distribution characteristics of the variables were tested using skewness and kurtosis values, which were found to be between − 2 and + 2. This was considered evidence of normal distribution [73]. Subsequently, regression-based mediation analysis was conducted to test the study’s hypotheses. Before conducting the mediation analysis, the assumptions of regression analysis were examined. The independence of errors assumption was checked using the Durbin-Watson coefficient, which was found to be 1.087 in this study. According to Durbin and Watson [74], this value should fall between 1 and 3, and the value obtained in this study meets this criterion. The assumption of multicollinearity was examined using the variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values. According to this assumption, the relationships between predictor variables should not exceed 0.90 [71], the VIF value should be less than 5, and the tolerance value should be greater than 0.20 [75–76]. The results of the correlation analysis indicated that the highest relationship between predictor variables was 0.691, which is below the recommended criterion of 0.90. Additionally, the VIF values ranged between 1.053 and 1.932, which were below 5, and the tolerance values ranged between 0.518 and 0.950, exceeding the threshold of 0.20. All these results indicate that the multicollinearity assumption was met. Furthermore, Cook’s D value was used to check for the presence of multivariate outliers, as Cook’s D values greater than 1 are considered outliers [77–78]. According to the Cook’s D values calculated for the model, it was observed that no value exceeded 1. As a result, after confirming that there was no issue with multicollinearity, the analyses were performed. The study also examined multiple normality using Mahalanobis values [79–80]. If the data have a multivariate normal distribution, comparing the Mahalanobis distances with the chi-square percentiles produces an approximately linear line. According to the resulting graph, the data formed a linear line and were found to meet multiple normality. In the current study, SPSS 21.0 package program was conducted, and within the scope of mediation analysis, SPSS Process Macro Model 4 was used.

Data collection process

Ethical approval was first obtained from the Social Sciences Ethics Committee at Ataturk University for the study. Ethics committee approval for the research was obtained with document number E.88656144-000-2300378380 from the meeting held on 27.01.2023. Then, permission to use the measurement tools was obtained from the researchers. After obtaining the necessary permissions, the measurement tools were compiled into a form. The form began with an informed consent form, which provided detailed information about the research. Following that, a personal information form, the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale, the Exercise Identity Scale, and the Two-Dimensional Self-Esteem Scale were included. These forms were distributed to individuals in various centers within the city (Erzurum, one of the metropolitan cities located in Türkiye) where the research was conducted, including dietitian clinics and various sports centers and data was collected there. Information about the study was shared with the participants, and voluntary participants were asked to complete the forms. The forms were administered to participants face-to-face. Participants were specifically asked whether they had any psychological illnesses, and then their exercise habits were specifically assessed. Participants then answered the questions on the form. In this context, in the current study, inclusion criteria included being over 18 years of age, willing to participate voluntarily, and exercising at least one day a week. Exclusion criteria included having a mental illness and being illiterate. The data collection took place between 05.04.2023 and 05.11.2023. Participants completed the data collection form in approximately 10 min.

Transparency and openness

This study was not pre-registered. All measurements used in the study are reported in the manuscript, and no additional measurements were collected. Data collection tools, references, and permission details are described in the methods section. The obtained and analyzed data set is not shared publicly in a data repository due to participant confidentiality and ethical considerations. However, aggregated data, additional information regarding the data collection tools, and application processes are available upon reasonable request from the corresponding author. The study was conducted in accordance with the limitations imposed by ethical approval, encouraging transparency and openness as much as possible.

Results

Before testing the mediation hypotheses of the study, descriptive statistics were examined. When demographic characteristics were examined, it was observed that the majority of participants were individuals attempting to lose weight and exercising regularly. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to determine the relationships between the variables, and the results are presented in detail in Table 1. The findings revealed positive and statistically significant relationships between mental well-being and exercise identity (r = 0.260, p < 0.001), the self-liking dimension of self-esteem (r = 0.720, p < 0.001), and the self-competence dimension of self-esteem (r = 0.647, p < 0.001). Additionally, positive and statistically significant relationships were found between exercise identity and the self-liking dimension of self-esteem (r = 0.203, p < 0.001) and the self-competence dimension of self-esteem (r = 0.209, p < 0.001). Lastly, a positive and statistically significant relationship was observed between the self-liking and self-competence dimensions of self-esteem (r = 0.691, p < 0.001).

The analyses showed that the total effect of exercise identity on mental well-being was significant (β = 0.260, t = 5.478, p < 0.001). Additionally, exercise identity was found to positively and significantly predict the self-liking dimension of self-esteem (β = 0.203, t = 4.426, p < 0.001) and the self-competence dimension of self-esteem (β = 0.209, t = 4.338, p < 0.001). Moreover, the self-liking dimension (β = 0.512, t = 11.429, p < 0.001) and the self-competence dimension (β = 0.272, t = 6.072, p < 0.001) of self-esteem were found to positively and significantly predict mental well-being. Finally, the absence of zero in the 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals indicated that the indirect effects of exercise identity on mental well-being through the self-liking (ab1 = 0.104, SE = 0.026, 95% CI [0.052, 0.157]) and self-competence (ab2 = 0.056, SE = 0.018, 95% CI [0.025, 0.098]) dimensions of self-esteem were significant. As a result, it was determined that self-liking and self-competence play mediating roles in the relationship between exercise identity and mental well-being.

The analyses showed that the total effect of exercise identity on mental well-being was significant (β = 0.253, t = 4.879, p < 0.001). Additionally, exercise identity was found to positively and significantly predict the self-liking dimension of self-esteem (β = 0.189, t = 3.586, p < 0.001) and the self-competence dimension of self-esteem (β = 0.118, t = 2.288, p = 0.022). Moreover, the self-liking dimension (β = 0.504, t = 11.178, p < 0.001) and the self-competence dimension (β = 0.288, t = 6.220, p < 0.001) of self-esteem were found to positively and significantly predict mental well-being. Finally, the absence of zero in the 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals indicated that the indirect effects of exercise identity on mental well-being through the self-liking (ab1 = 0.095, SE = 0.028, 95% CI [0.040, 0.152]) and self-competence (ab2 = 0.034, SE = 0.018, 95% CI [0.003, 0.072]) dimensions of self-esteem were significant. As a result, it was determined that self-liking and self-competence play mediating roles in the relationship between exercise identity and mental well-being.

After controlling for gender, BMI, socioeconomic status, and education level, both dimensions of self-esteem—self-liking and self-efficacy—were found to have significant indirect effects on the relationship between exercise identity and mental well-being. Comparing the model without control variables to the model with control variables, the direct effect of exercise identity on self-efficacy was reduced in the model with control variables, while the effect of the self-efficacy dimension of self-esteem on mental well-being was increased. Furthermore, the indirect effects of both self-liking and self-efficacy were also reduced. Discussion

In the current study, the relationships between exercise identity and mental well-being were examined in individuals who exercise regularly and attempt weight loss, and it was found that self-liking and self-competence played mediating roles in this relationship. The findings confirm hypothesis 1, revealing a positive relationship between exercise identity and mental well-being. This finding suggests that exercise identity may be a significant predictor of mental well-being, implying that identity can be considered an important factor in enhancing mental well-being. The more people achieve identity integrity, the better their mental health [81]. Furthermore, the importance of identity and personal responsibility in maintaining mental health is also recognized [82]. In the relevant literature, it has been found that individuals who engage in regular and consistent exercise have better mental health [83–84]. It’s also known that individuals who exercise themselves experience less poor mental health than those who don’t [85]. In this context, exercise behaviour is a strong contributor to mental well-being. Mikkelsen et al. [5] found in their study that regular physical exercise significantly improves mental health by reducing symptoms of anxiety, stress, and depression. At this point, exercise continuity can be considered an important agent in sustaining mental well-being. Given that identity formation, characterized by order, consistency and predictability, has a positive relationship with mental health [86], it can be argued that the contributions of exercise identity, adopted as a type of identity, to mental well-being stem from both exercise itself and the success of establishing an identity. Therefore, individuals who consistently incorporate exercise into their lives are said to develop an exercise identity [46]. According to Pluta et al. [32], individuals create an exercise identity to define their past exercise behavior, and they use this identity to maintain and continue exercise in the future. This is considered a factor that enhances mental well-being [87]. In other words, it can be said that exercises that were regularly and consistently performed in the past and are sustained in the future contribute to the development of an exercise identity, which, in turn, positively impacts mental well-being. This finding supports the current study’s results. The fact that both sub-dimensions of self-esteem mediate the relationship between exercise identity and mental well-being may also contribute to explaining how identity influences mental well-being both directly and indirectly through self-esteem. According to Stryker and Burke [88], behaviors consistent with one’s identity contribute to increased self-competence. In a study by Veronese et al. [89], self-competence was found to be an important factor in protecting individuals from the effects of psychological distress and improving mental well-being. Furthermore, the study suggested that individuals’ well-being increases when their personal and relational/social resources are active. In a study by Strachan et al. [90], exercise identity was found to positively predict self-competence, while a study by Kurtović et al. [14] showed that self-competence contributes to mental well-being. Therefore, in individuals who consistently engage in exercise and perceive it as part of their identity, exercise identity interacts with self-esteem to positively predict mental well-being. This study demonstrated the relationship between identity and mental well-being through self-competence. Therefore, while exercise identity may serve as a foundation for increasing self-competence, it also contributes to the improvement of mental well-being. The relationship between exercise identity and mental well-being through self-liking is also consistent with findings in the literature. In a study by Ersner-Hershfield et al. [91],self-liking was suggested to be one of the most enduring elements of personal identity continuity. When the relevant literature is reviewed, it has been determined that individuals who exercise regularly have more positive self-body image [92], that it supports self-confidence[11], and that in this context, there is progress in improved stress coping processes [93]. In fact, a study conducted by Korn et al. [94] found that individuals who participate in physical exercise have more positive self-perceptions. Individuals who like themselves and have a positive self-perception are more likely to have higher self-confidence. White [95] also suggests that athletes often engage in positive self-talk to boost their self-confidence before a sporting event. Moreover, the mediating role of self-liking in the relationship between exercise identity and mental well-being can be explained by the value an individual places on themselves. It is known that there is a positive relationship between exercise identity and individuals’ physical self-worth [96] and that self-liking has an impact on well-being [14]. Therefore, the mediating role of self-liking in the relationship between exercise identity and mental well-being is supported by the relevant literature. The results show that exercise identity, which functions as a stimulus in terms of an individual’s self-evaluation, affects self-esteem, and that the two sub-dimensions of self-esteem, considered as a result of the cognitive evaluation process within the scope of the present study, support mental well-being. This supports cognitive evaluation theory, as it indicates a positive effect of positive cognitive evaluations on mental well-being.

In addition to all of this self-liking was observed to have a stronger mediating effect than self-competence. This may be due to attitudes related to individuals’ perceptions of their own bodies. More specifically, today’s ideal image of beauty may lead individuals to focus more on their physical appearance. In other words, the stereotypes presented by the media regarding being fit may direct individuals toward certain ways of thinking, thereby contributing to their self-liking [97–98]. This situation may have contributed to the finding that, in the relationship between exercise identity and mental well-being among individuals who exercise, self-liking plays a stronger mediating role than self-competence.

Conclusion and recommendations for future research

While presenting new and significant findings, this study also has several limitations. Firstly, the research group for this study consisted of exercise participants from Türkiye, attempting to lose weight. Future researchers examining exercise identity, self-esteem, and mental well-being could compare data obtained from different countries. This would help determine the impact of intercultural differences on these psychological variables. Furthermore, conducting studies with participant groups that include not only individuals attempting to lose weight but also those who exercise regularly for other reasons would ensure the generalizability of the findings. Secondly, individuals in later adulthood were not included in the study. Given the physical, cognitive, and socio-emotional losses experienced in later adulthood [99], individuals may sometimes turn to exercise as a coping factor in dealing with these losses [100]. Therefore, conducting research on the self-esteem and mental well-being of individuals who canconstruct an exercise identity in later adulthood could contribute to the relevant literature. Thirdly, the current study is cross-sectional. As this study was conducted using cross-sectional data, interpreting the findings in terms of causal relationships entails certain limitations. Therefore, causal interpretations should be considered more cautiously. Collecting data from individuals who exercise regularly at different times can provide information about temporal changes. Fourthly, this study developed a model addressing the mediating effect of self-esteem in the relationship between exercise identity and mental well-being. Future studies could explore the relationships between exercise identity and mental well-being by considering various psychological variables such as perfectionism, attachment styles, early maladaptive schemas, cognitive distortions, and psychological symptoms. Lastly, in-depth research on these variables could provide a more detailed examination of the impact of exercise identity on mental well-being. Additionally in the current study, because data was collected from various gyms and participants were asked whether they exercised weekly, more specific information about their exercise frequency has not beeen available. Therefore, it would be advisable for future studies to also assess participants’ exercise frequency and examine it as a control variable. This would be allowed to determine whether exercise identity uniquely predicts the mediator and outcome variables and whether this relationship stems from having a particularly strong exercise identity.

Community well-being workers who work with adult individuals can enhance the effectiveness of psychological support processes by considering levels of exercise identity and self-esteem when evaluating the mental well-being of overweight individuals seeking psychological help. In Türkiye, decreased physical activity is the leading risk factor for being overweight, with the overweight rate standing at 17% [101]. This significant rate can encourage individuals to engage in more physical activity. Some of these initiatives can be implemented by policymakers, such as opening free fitness centers or expanding limited bike paths in Türkiye. Others can be provided by well-being professionals. For example, Public Education Centers can conduct awareness-raising activities about the link between exercise and mental well-being. In addition, it can be stated that in future studies planned, it may be potentially useful to consider interventions aimed at strengthening self-esteem with the promotion of physical activity in order to improve the well-being of individuals. Experimental studies focusing particularly on strengthening self-liking and exercise identity may provide causal evidence regarding psychological well-being. The structuring of such programs contributes both to theoretical advancement and offers a valuable foundation for applied psychological research. Furthermore, seminars, panels, or similar activities on exercise identity and self-esteem could be beneficial for individuals working in community mental well-being services.
