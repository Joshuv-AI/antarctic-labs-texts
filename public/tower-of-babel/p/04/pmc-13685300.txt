Enhancement of postpartum cow health and calf performance through maternal‒offspring interactions: effects on the immunity, antioxidants, and bacterial flora induced by Guiqi Yimu Powder
Authors: Kaikai Bao, Hao Zhang, Weidong Ma, Peng Ji, Yanming Wei, Yongli Hua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the effects of dietary supplementation with Guiqi Yimu powder (GYP) on the postpartum health of beef cows and the growth performance of their calves, with an emphasis on immune function, antioxidant capacity, and the bacterial flora. Twenty-two postpartum cows were randomly assigned on the day of calving to either the control group (CON) or the GYP group. The control group was fed a basal diet, and the GYP group was fed the basal diet supplemented with 300 g/day of GYP starting on the day of calving. After 7 days of supplementation, serum samples from the cows and calves were collected, and antioxidant indices [superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione (GSH)] as well as immunoglobulins (IgA, IgM, and IgG) were analyzed. Fecal samples from cows were assessed for gut microbiota diversity and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) content; milk samples were analyzed for microbial composition and immunoglobulins. GYP was administered to the cows, and calves were exposed to GYP solely through suckling. Compared with those in the CON group, calves in the GYP group presented significantly greater weaning weights and average daily gains. The calf survival rate tended to increase, whereas the incidence of diarrhea tended to decrease in the GYP group. Among cows in the GYP group, both postpartum conception rates and estrus rates tended to increase; conversely, return-to-estrus rates and semen doses per conception tended to decrease. Serum levels of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione (GSH) were elevated, whereas malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were reduced (p < 0.05) in GYP cows. Moreover, supplementation with GYP significantly increased the serum IgG levels (p < 0.05), the milk IgM, IgA, and IgG levels (p < 0.05), and the serum IgG levels in calves (p < 0.05). Analysis of the gut microbiota of these cows revealed that, compared with CON, GYP improved the gut bacterial diversity and increased the relative abundances of UCG-002, Faecalibacterium, norank_f_F082, and Oscillibacter. Furthermore, examination of the milk microbiota revealed that GYP increased the relative abundances of Acetobacter, Lactobacillus, and Prevotellaceae_UCG-003. In addition, compared with CON, GYP significantly increased the contents of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), including acetic acid, propionic acid, isobutyric acid, n-butyric acid, isovaleric acid, and n-valeric acid, in the feces of postpartum cows (p < 0.05). GYP improves postpartum cow health and calf performance via (1) direct antioxidant and immunomodulatory effects, (2) gut microbiota remodeling and SCFAs promotion, and (3) vertical transfer of immunoglobulins and probiotics through milk. Background

The periparturient period spanning late gestation to early postpartum imposes the most severe physiological stress on beef cows. Rapid fetal development and initiation of colostrogenesis substantially increase maternal energy demands. Oxidative stress triggered by parturition manifested through elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) and reduced superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione (GSH) activity may induce immunosuppression and homeostatic imbalance [1]. After calving, the maternal body experiences massive physiological depletion, necessitating a critical recovery period to restore reproductive performance. However, insufficient postpartum dry matter intake (DMI) prevents adequate physical recovery and exacerbates negative energy balance (NEB) while compromising host immunity. Consequently, this metabolic deficit leads to breeding failures, explicitly reducing the first-service conception rate, while increasing the return-to-estrus rate and the number of services per conception. These factors collectively contribute to a decline in secondary breeding efficiency. Emerging evidence has significantly advanced our understanding of gut microbiota-host interactions during this critical period. A recent prospective nested case-control study revealed that postpartum ketosis is associated with profound ruminal dysbiosis, characterized by downregulated propionate metabolism and glucogenic amino acid pathways, which compromises the supply of essential glycogenic substrates and exacerbates negative energy balance [2, 3]. During the periparturient period, systemic inflammation is primarily driven by physiological events such as tissue trauma from parturition, uterine involution, and metabolic stress [4, 5]. However, beyond energy metabolism, a disrupted gut microbiota can further exacerbate this inflammatory state. Impaired intestinal barrier function reduces the production of beneficial metabolites like short-chain fatty acids, thereby compounding systemic inflammation and further suppressing host immunity [6]. The frequent clinical use of broad-spectrum antimicrobials to treat common postpartum infectious diseases, such as metritis and mastitis, disrupts gut microbiota equilibrium, reduces SCFAs production, and may exacerbate immunosuppression [7]. Such symptomatic interventions fail to achieve comprehensive physiological regulation, highlighting the need for safer, alternative preventive strategies. Natural product extracts and herbal medicines have shown significant potential as supportive and prophylactic strategies for alleviating postpartum metabolic stress and preventing related disorders [8, 9]. Since the health status of the dam directly influences milk composition, and milk serves as the sole source of immune substances and gut microbiota establishment for newborn calves, screening for natural additives that can improve maternal health and subsequently optimize milk quality is of great significance for enhancing the productive performance of both mother and offspring.

Guiqi Yimu powder (GYP) is a modernized adaptation of the classical formula Guiqi Yimu San, which was originally documented in Essential Prescriptions for Bovine Diseases [10, 11]. This optimized phytocomposition contains three medicinal components: Astragalus membranaceus (Huangqi), Angelica sinensis (Danggui), and Leonurus japonicus (Yimucao). The evidence-based modifications to the classical formula were guided by traditional veterinary formulation principles and modern pharmacological data tailored to bovine postpartum physiology. Specifically, the proportion of L. japonicus was increased to leverage its potent uterotonic effects [12], addressing the unique need for massive lochia clearance and rapid uterine involution in postpartum cows. Concurrently, the Astragalus to Angelica ratio was adjusted from the classical human formula. This adjustment shifts the pharmacological focus from solely supplementing metabolic deficits to synergistically nourishing the host while activating blood circulation and resolving postpartum uterine stasis [13]. Through two evidence-based modifications, i.e., enhancing Leonurus proportion to potentiate its triple therapeutic effects via blood-activating properties (via leonurine alkaloids), promoting uterine repair (through TGF-β pathway modulation), and anti-inflammatory action (NF-κB inhibition) [12], we scientifically optimized the formulation through two evidence-based modifications: increasing the ratio of Astragalus to Angelica; augmenting Qi-invigorating effects (astragaloside IV-mediated mitochondrial biogenesis); and maintaining optimal blood-nourishing capacity (ferulic acid-regulated hematopoiesis) [14, 15]. Although bioactive constituents, including astragalus polysaccharides, ferulic acid and leonurine, demonstrate antioxidative and immunomodulatory activities in in vitro or murine models, the clinical efficacy and mechanisms of GYP in periparturient cows remain unverified [16–20].

We hypothesize a dual-pathway mechanism for GYP. Maternal axis modulation: GYP potentially remodeled the gut microbiota by enriching beneficial genera such as Faecalibacterium, thereby increasing SCFAs acetate and propionate output, mitigating oxidative stress and enhancing systemic immunity. Neonatal axis transmission: GYP-derived bioactive compounds enrich the mature milk with immunoglobulins (IgG, IgM, and IgA) and probiotics, including Lactobacillus. Recognizing that physiological gut closure prevents the systemic absorption of intact macromolecules after the first 24 hours of life, we hypothesize that the continuous ingestion of this enriched milk acts locally within the calf’s intestinal lumen. This local mucosal protection neutralizes enteric pathogens and promotes healthy microbial colonization, thereby reducing diarrhea incidence and improving growth performance (average daily gain, ADG). This study aimed to systematically evaluate the regulatory role of GYP in the cow-calf health chain, providing a theoretical basis for the development of alternative Chinese herbal feed additives while also expanding the understanding of the ‘gut-milk-immune’ pathway mechanisms. Materials and methods

Experimental animals

Twenty-two healthy pregnant Qinchuan cows (parity: 2 ± 0.5; age: 23 ± 11 months; body weight: 550 ± 50 kg) were selected from the Qinchuan Cattle Breeding Farm in Shaanxi Province, China. The inclusion criteria were as follows: body condition score (BCS, on a 5-point scale) of 3.0–3.5, no history of dystocia/reproductive disorders, and expected calving dates within ±3 days of each other. The cows were fed a basal diet ad libitum for seven days prior to the experiment, with free access to water. The experimental protocol was conducted according to the Chinese National Standard GB/T 35,892–2018 (Laboratory animal - Guidelines for ethical review of animal welfare) and was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Gansu Agricultural University (Approval No. GSAU-Eth-VMC-2024–048).

Main instruments

The experimental equipment used included an FK-058 full-wavelength enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) machine from Beijing Putian Xinqiao Technology Co., Ltd., a UV spectrophotometer (725 N) from Shanghai Yuanxi Instrument Co., Ltd., a gas chromatograph (model: Agilent 8890 GC, equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID)) and a chromatographic column (DB-FFAP, 30 mm × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm) from Agilent Technologies Co., Ltd. in the United States, an AL104 electronic balance from Shanghai Mettler Toledo Instrument Co., Ltd., and a HITACHI-CT15RE Hitachi ultracentrifuge from Hitachi, Ltd.

Preparation of GYP

Angelica sinensis, Astragalus membranaceus, and Leonurus japonicus are dried medicinal herbs purchased from the Yellow River medicinal market in Lanzhou, Gansu Province, and are stored in Laboratory 612 of the Cognitive Building of Gansu Agricultural University. The identification of medicinal herbs was conducted by Professor Wei Yanming from the Department of Veterinary Medicine at Gansu Agricultural University. The medicinal materials were finely ground (particle size ≤ 80 μm), and the uniformity of mixing was verified (RSD < 5%). The GYP formulation consisted of 200 g of Leonurus japonicus, 50 g of Angelica sinensis, and 50 g of Astragalus membranaceus per 300 g aliquot.

Animal feeding and management

All experimental beef cows and their respective calves were managed in a commercial-style loose-housing and group-feeding system, providing approximately 8 m2 of space per animal under well-ventilated, dry conditions with ad libitum access to clean water. The postpartum basal diet for the cows, formulated on a dry matter (DM) basis, comprised 75% forage (40% corn silage, 20% wheat straw, and 15% alfalfa hay) and 25% concentrate supplement. Based on the standard nutritional values from the Chinese Beef Cattle Feeding Standard (NY/T 815–2004), the estimated chemical composition of this basal diet was 11.2% crude protein (CP), 48.7% neutral detergent fiber (NDF), 21.7% starch, and 7.9% ash on a DM basis. While the forage was provided ad libitum, the concentrate was supplied twice daily (08:00 and 14:00) at a fixed rate of 1.2% of body weight; daily visual inspections confirmed the absence of orts in the communal troughs, thereby ensuring a consistent concentrate intake across all animals despite the logistical constraints of measuring individual consumption in a group-housed setting. Concurrently, newborn calves received colostrum within one hour postpartum and relied heavily on maternal milk as their primary nutritional source during the initial 7-day experimental period. Although a specific pelleted calf starter (crude protein ≥ 18%, metabolizable energy ≥ 12.5 MJ/kg) was introduced at 7 days of age, followed by ad libitum high-quality alfalfa hay at 2 weeks, starter intake during this early neonatal stage was minimal and therefore not quantified. The allowance of supplemental feed was progressively adjusted according to the calves’ growth requirements, and following weaning, all calves were uniformly transitioned to a standard grower diet.

Animals and experimental design

A total of 22 pregnant cows were randomly allocated into two groups immediately after calving. To ensure accurate preventative dosing and prevent cross-feeding, all cows were housed and fed individually in tie-stalls. The experimental unit was the individual cow. The two groups were: the CON group (fed a basal diet) and the GYP group (fed a basal diet individually top-dressed with 300 g/d GYP). The experimental period lasted for 7 days. During the experiment, feeding and management were carried out according to the routine procedures of the cattle farm. As part of the standard routine management for postpartum cows on this farm, the barn environment—specifically the walkways, tie-stall floors, and manure alleys—was disinfected once daily in the morning using 0.1% sodium hypochlorite solution. The GYP supplement was individually top-dressed onto the basal diet and administered at 2:00 PM each day. Each feeding was based on the bottom of the trough to ensure that there was no residue before the next feeding. GYP was continuously fed starting from the day of calving (day 0) for 7 consecutive days. Regarding calf management, a continuous dam-rearing system was employed. Following parturition, calves were kept continuously with their respective dams in the tie-stalls and were allowed unrestricted natural suckling, ensuring ad libitum access to colostrum and subsequent mature milk. Calves were never separated from their dams prior to weaning. Due to natural variations in the calving dates of the dams, all calves were managed to be weaned at a comparable age of approximately 60 days. Consequently, calves were only exposed to the maternal treatment effects through suckling and did not receive the GYP supplement directly at any point during the experimental period.

Sample collection and processing

Blood sample collection

Before morning feeding on the eighth day after delivery, blood was collected from the cows and calves via a disposable, sterile blood collector to draw blood from the jugular vein. The blood was placed in a covered 10 mL centrifuge tube. Then, the blood samples were centrifuged at 4,000 r/min for 15 minutes, the serum was separated, and the samples were stored at −80 °C.

Collection of fecal samples

On the morning of the eighth day postpartum, fecal samples were collected uniformly from all cows strictly prior to the morning feeding (at approximately 07:00 AM) to minimize diet-induced diurnal variations. Fresh feces were collected directly from the rectum using sterile, disposable long-arm obstetrical gloves to prevent environmental contamination. The collected bulk fecal matter was quickly homogenized to ensure uniformity, and a 10 g representative aliquot was immediately transferred into a sterile cryotube, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent microbiome and SCFA analyses.

Collection of milk samples

On day 8 postpartum, milk samples were collected during the first morning milking at 07:00 AM. To avoid teat canal contamination, the first few streams of the first streams of milk were discarded, and a 10 mL sample of mid-stream milk was subsequently collected and stored at −80 °C.

Determination of antioxidant, immune, microbial, and short-chain fatty acid contents

Reproductive performance of cows and growth performance of calves

To evaluate maternal reproductive performance and neonatal growth, comprehensive reproductive and zootechnical variables were recorded and explicitly defined as follows. All reproductive and zootechnical variables were calculated separately for each experimental group.

Reproductive variables: Estrus rate (%) = (number of cows exhibiting estrus ÷ total number of eligible cows) × 100; Conception rate (%) = (number of confirmed pregnant cows ÷ number of inseminated cows) × 100; Return-to-estrus rate (%) = (number of cows returning to estrus after the first artificial insemination ÷ total number of inseminated cows) × 100; Semen consumption (doses/conception) = total number of semen doses used ÷ number of confirmed pregnancies.

DNA extraction, PCR amplification，illumina sequencing and data processing

Total microbial genomic DNA was extracted from both fecal and milk samples using the Hi Pure Fecal DNA Kit (Guangzhou Meiji Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), according to manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and concentration of DNA were determined by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, United States) and kept at −80 °C prior to further use. The hypervariable region V3–V4 of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified with primer pairs 338F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806 R(5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’) [21] byT100 Thermal Cycler PCR thermocycler (BIO-RAD, USA). The PCR reaction mixture including 4 μL 5 × Fast Pfu buffer, 2 μL 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 μL each primer (5 μM), 0.4 μL Fast Pfu polymerase, 10 ng of template DNA, and ddH2O to a final volume of 20 µL. PCR amplification cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 27 cycles of denaturing at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s and extension at 72 ℃for 45 s, and single extension at 72 °C for 10 min, and end at 4 °C. The PCR product was extracted from 2% agarose gel and purified using the PCR Clean-Up Kit (YuHua, Shanghai, China) according to manufacturer’s instructions and quantified using Qubit 4.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar amounts and paired-end sequenced on an Illumina Nextseq2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego,USA) according to the standard protocols by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The raw sequencing reads were deposited into the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database (Accession Number: PRJNA1333447).

Raw FASTQ files were de-multiplexed using an in-house perl script, and then quality-filtered by fastp version 0.19.6 [22] and merged by FLASH version 1.2.7 [23] with the following criteria:

(i) the reads were truncated at any site receiving an average quality score of < 20 over a 50 bp sliding window, and the truncated reads shorter than 50 bp were discarded, reads containing ambiguous characters were also discarded; (ii) only overlapping sequences longer than 10 bp were assembled according to their overlapped sequence. The maximum mismatch ratio of overlap region is 0.2. Reads that could not be assembled were discarded; (iii) Samples were distinguished according to the barcode and primers, and the sequence direction was adjusted, exact barcode matching, 2 nucleotide mismatch in primer matching. Then the optimized sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) using UPARSE 7.1 [24] with 97% sequence similarity level. The most abundant sequence for each OTU was selected as a representative sequence. The OTU table was manually filtered, i.e., chloroplast sequences in all samples were removed. To minimize the effects of sequencing depth on alpha and beta diversity measure, the number of 16S rRNA gene sequences from each sample were rarefied to 20,000, which still yielded an average Good’s coverage of 99.09%, respectively.

The taxonomy of each OTU representative sequence was analyzed by RDP Classifier version 2.2 [25] against the 16S rRNA gene database Silva v138 using confidence threshold of 0.7.

Statistical analysis

Based on the OTUs information, alpha diversity indices including Chao and Shannon index were calculated with Mothur v1.30.1 [26] (http://www.mothur.org/wiki/Calculators), and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to analyze intergroup differences in alpha diversity of fecal and milk microbiota. The similarity among the microbial communities in fecal samples was determined by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on unweighted UniFrac dissimilarity using Vegan v2.5–3 package. The non-parametric PERMANOVA test was used to assess whether the differences in microbial community structure among sample groups were significant using Vegan v2.5–3 package; The similarity among the microbial communities in milk samples was determined by non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis based on abund_jaccard dissimilarity using Vegan v2.5–3 package; the non-parametric PERMANOVA test was used to assess whether the differences in microbial community structure among sample groups were significant using Vegan v2.5–3 package. The Analysis of Intergroup Differences [27] was performed to identify the significantly abundant bacterial taxa at the genus level in milk and fecal samples among the different groups.The co-occurrence networks were constructed to explore the internal community relationships across the samples [28]. Microbial taxa were selected for network analysis based on Spearman’s correlation over 0.6 or less than −0.6, and the p-value less than 0.05.

Analysis of SCFAs

GC conditions

The injection port temperature was 280 °C, and the chromatographic column heating program was as follows: the initial temperature was maintained at 60 °C for 2 min, then increased to 140 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min, and then increased to 170 °C at a rate of 3 °C/min. The sample was measured via the split flow method, with a split ratio of 20:1, a manual injection volume of 1 μL, a carrier gas of high-purity (purity > 99%) nitrogen gas, a flow rate of 1 mL/min, a detector of FID, and a temperature of 300 °C.

Establishment of the standard curve

First, six standard samples of acetic acid (20 μL), propionic acid (10 μL), n-butyric acid (5 μL), isobutyric acid (5 μL), n-valeric acid (2 μL), and isovaleric acid (1 μL) were accurately measured at a volume ratio of 20:10:5:5:2:1. The six standard solutions were then mixed evenly and diluted with ultrapure water to obtain six different concentrations (5000-fold, 4000-fold, 3000-fold, 2000-fold, 1000-fold, and 500-fold) of mixed standard solutions. A total of 20 μL of n-butanol was diluted with 980 μL of ultrapure water. If a 5000-fold dilution was used, one μL of the mixed standard solution was aspirated. Subsequently, 4949 μL of ultrapure water and 50 μL of internal standard diluent were added, and the concentration of the n-butanol sample was 2.1856 mmol/L. Then, n-butanol was used as the internal standard, and 1 μL of the sample was accurately aspirated and detected with an instrument. Finally, the standard curve is obtained by plotting the content ratio on the x-axis and the peak-to-height ratio on the y-axis.

Preparation of fecal sample solution

For the comprehensive analysis of gut microbiota and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), a subset of 6 cows per group was randomly selected from the original cohort of 11 for sample collection and sequencing. A 0.20 g fecal sample was accurately placed into a 2 mL EP tube. Then, four volumes of ultrapure water were added, mixed evenly, and allowed to stand at room temperature for 20 minutes. The mixture was centrifuged at 4,000 r/min at 4 °C for 15 minutes. The obtained supernatant was added to a 2 mL EP tube. The fecal sediment was then treated with four volumes of ultrapure water in the same manner. Then, the combined supernatant from the two operations was centrifuged again, and the resulting supernatant was aspirated to 990 μL. Next, 10 μL of n-butanol diluent was added, the mixture was mixed well, and the mixture was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. Finally, 1 μL was accurately transferred for GC detection.

Statistical analysis

All the data were initially organized via Excel 2021 and are presented as the means ± standard errors of the means (SEMs). The experimental data were analyzed via SPSS Statistics 27.0 software. Normally distributed (Shapiro‒Wilk test, p > 0.05) and homogeneous (Levene test, p > 0.05) data were analyzed via an independent sample t test; parametric data were tested via the Mann‒Whitney U test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. GraphPad Prism 9.5 software was used for plotting.

As shown in Table 3, the weaning weight, average daily gain, and lactation period gain of calves in the GYP group were greater than those in the CON group. Additionally, the survival rate of calves in the GYP group reached 100%, and the diarrhea rate was 9.09% lower than that in the CON group. In terms of maternal reproductive performance, the GYP group presented a 10% increase in the conception rate during the oestrus period, a 10% decrease in the return to oestrus rate, and a 10% reduction in the semen consumption rate compared with those of the CON group (Table 4).

As shown in Fig. 1 and Table S7, compared with the CON group, the addition of GYP significantly increased the serum antioxidant capacity of the cows: GSH level increased by 12.26% (p = 0.041, Fig. 1A), MDA content decreased by 21.06% (p = 0.018, Fig. 1B), and SOD activity increased by 11.88% (p = 0.002, Fig. 1C).

Effects of GYP on the serum immune level of beef cows

As shown in Fig. 1D–F, the addition of GYP increased the serum immunoglobulin concentration of the cows: IgM activity increased by 4.16% (p = 0.338, Fig. 1D), IgG levels increased by 10.99% (p = 0.03, Fig. 1E), and IgA content increased by 1.62% (p = 0.553, Fig. 1F). These results indicate that GYP can improve the immune protein levels of pregnant cows and enhance their immunity.

Effects of GYP on the immune level of cow milk

As shown in Fig. 2 and Table S7, compared with the CON group, the addition of GYP significantly increased the immunoglobulin concentrations in cow milk: IgA content increased by 16.68% (p = 0.034, Fig. 2A), IgG levels increased by 5.68% (p = 0.039, Fig. 2B), and IgM activity increased by 4.51% (p = 0.043, Fig. 2C). Discussion

Oxidative stress is a significant underlying factor in the dysfunction of host immune and inflammatory responses, which increases the susceptibility of cows to various diseases, especially during the postpartum period [29, 30]. Supplementing diets with Chinese herbal medicines that possess antioxidant capacity and prebiotics can, to some extent, improve postpartum production performance in cows and calf health [31]. Recent studies have reported that GYP can enhance the growth performance, production performance, and immune function of livestock and poultry through mechanisms such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects [11]. This study also confirmed that adding GYP can significantly improve the antioxidant levels and immune function of postpartum cows. The reason may be that the active ingredients in Astragalus membranaceus and Angelica sinensis have antioxidant effects, which positively impact the cows’ antioxidant levels [32, 33]. Increasing the activity of antioxidant enzymes and reducing the content of oxidation products within cows helps maintain their health status. Studies have shown that Angelica sinensis polysaccharides have effects against cellular oxidative damage and improve immune function [34], and Astragalus membranaceus polysaccharides significantly impact cow antioxidant levels and immune function. Leonurus japonicus extract may also have significant antioxidant effects and immune-enhancing properties [35]. This GYP-mediated increase in antioxidant capacity and immunity was ultimately reflected in the improvement in the cows’ reproductive performance: the conception rate at first service in the GYP group increased by 10%, the return-to-estrus rate decreased by 10%, and the semen dose per conception also tended to decrease. These findings suggest that GYP provides an internal safeguard for improving reproductive efficiency by ameliorating maternal redox status and immune function.

The diversity and stability of the gut microbiota are crucial for organisms. Generally, the greater the diversity of the gut microbiota is, the greater its ability to enhance the stability of the gut bacterial community [36]. A decrease in diversity may reduce beneficial microorganisms and the expansion of pathogenic microbes [37, 38]. The metabolites of the gut microbiota are interconnected with the immune system, regulating immune responses [39] through direct and indirect interactions with host immune cells [40]. Some bacteria, including Faecalibacterium and Oscillibacter, generate SCFAs through carbohydrate fermentation. SCFAs can regulate host immune cells and provide a carbon source for colonocytes [41, 42].

Through the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, this study identified UGC-002, Faecalibacterium, norank_o_WCHB1-41, norank_f_ F082, and Oscillibacter as the main affected genera. Notably, these key genera are closely related to the production of SCFAs. Our study specifically identified UCG-002, a genus within the Ruminococcaceae family, as a key genus in cows’ feces. Ruminococcaceae are an important bacterial group for ruminants due to their cellulolytic activity and ability to convert complex polysaccharides into a variety of nutrients for the host. Thus, the presence of UCG-002 in the lower gut may be crucial for efficient post-ruminal fiber utilisation [43]. Faecalibacterium can synthesize SCFAs (especially butyrate), which not only have protective effects on digestive system health but also enhance the body’s immune system, promote metabolism, and maintain intestinal barrier integrity [44]. Moreover, it has been reported that its abundance is significantly positively correlated with the expression levels of antioxidant-related genes [45], which is consistent with the increased antioxidant levels found in the GYP group in this study. F082 belongs to the Bacteroidota phylum and is involved primarily in noncellulose degradation, with its main metabolic products being propionate and butyrate [46]. Oscillibacter is considered a potential probiotic because it plays a key role in sugar fermentation [47] and starch degradation [48]. The products of sugar fermentation and starch degradation are SCFAs. The uncultured WCHB1-41 order (norank_o_WCHB1-41) is capable of degrading mucin and converting dietary fiber into SCFAs, thereby providing nutrients for other bacteria and host cells [49]. The primary function of SCFAs is to serve as the primary energy source and substrates for glucose and fat synthesis in ruminants, accounting for 70–80% of their total energy requirements [50]. They regulate gene expression by binding to G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) and inhibiting the activity of histone deacetylases (HDACs). These mechanisms are crucial for reducing local inflammation, resisting pathogen invasion, and maintaining intestinal barrier integrity [51]. This study revealed that the concentrations of total SCFAs, acetate, propionate, and butyrate in the GYP group were greater than those in the CON group. The reason may be closely related to the increased abundance of the key SCFAs-producing genera mentioned above: UGC-002, Faecalibacterium, norank_f_F082, Oscillibacter, and norank_o_WCHB1–41.

The study revealed that GYP significantly increased IgG levels in cow serum, which was directly reflected in the elevated concentrations of IgA, IgG, and IgM in the milk. However, these results must be interpreted with physiological caution. Because passive transfer and intestinal absorption of intact immunoglobulins largely cease approximately 24 hours after birth (gut closure) [52], and GYP supplementation commenced at calving, the continuous ingestion of immunoglobulin-rich milk during days 1–7 primarily exerted a local protective effect in the gastrointestinal tract rather than directly contributing to systemic passive transfer [53, 54]. Within the gut lumen, these unabsorbed immunoglobulins act as crucial defense molecules that prevent pathogen adhesion, directly explaining the significant 9.09% reduction in the calf diarrhea rate. Crucially, this localized mucosal protection translated to profound clinical survival benefits; while two calves in the control group died due to severe diarrhea, no mortality occurred in the GYP-treated group. Interestingly, calves in the GYP group still exhibited a significantly higher serum IgG level at day 8 (by 12.72%, p < 0.05). Since this cannot be confidently attributed to delayed passive transfer, we hypothesize that this robust local intestinal protection minimized the systemic consumption of circulating IgG typically associated with enteric infections [55], or that other bioactive metabolites transferred through the milk promoted the early activation of the calf’s own active immune system.

The human milk microbiota has been confirmed to promote the maturation of the intestinal epithelial barrier, immune system, and endocrine system in early life, regulating the intestinal microenvironment [56]. This study revealed that GYP altered the microbial composition of cow milk, increasing the abundance of key beneficial genera. Acetobacter is a primary producer of acetate in the rumen [57]. Lactobacillus is a classic probiotic that exerts potent antibacterial activity through the production of lactic acid, organic acids, H2O2, or bacteriocins, thereby antagonizing pathogenic bacteria and maintaining healthy gut ecology [58, 59]. It can also improve intestinal inflammation and support the ability of beneficial microbiota to resist infection [60]. Prevotellaceae_UCG-003 belongs to the Prevotellaceae family, whose members are involved in plant cell wall polysaccharide degradation [61], protein catabolism [62, 63], etc., and are important acetate and propionate producers. Studies suggest that it may reduce inflammation and protect the intestinal barrier by lowering the concentrations of inflammatory markers (such as LPS-binding protein and C-reactive protein) [64, 65]. Through suckling, the high-abundance beneficial bacteria in milk, particularly Lactobacillus and Prevotellaceae_UCG-003, are directly transferred to newborn calves. High-abundance incoming lactobacillius can rapidly colonize calf intestines, utilizing their inherent antibacterial properties to antagonize pathogens and reduce the risk of diarrhea directly. Lactobacillus enhances the activity of intestinal digestive enzymes (such as lactase and lipase), improving the absorption efficiency of nutrients such as colostrum by calves and assisting in establishing and strengthening intestinal immune barrier function. As a vertically transmitted key bacterium, Prevotellaceae_UCG-003 produces SCFAs, provides energy substrates, and is associated with the shaping of a healthy calf intestinal microenvironment and promoting barrier integrity through its potential anti-inflammatory properties and metabolic functions. This study revealed that the abundance of Prevotellaceae_UGC-003 in milk was significantly greater than that in milk, which echoes the high abundance of this bacterial family observed in the colon of piglets fed with cow milk by Rosa et al. [66].

The significant correlations observed in this study underscore a sophisticated, multi-site microbial regulatory network that maintains host metabolic homeostasis and immune defense in cows. The positive associations between core gut genera, such as Oscillibacter, UCG-002, and Akkermansia, and fecal SCFAs (Fig. 7A) highlight their essential role as metabolic drivers. In production, these SCFA-producers are vital for reinforcing the maternal intestinal barrier and optimizing energy extraction from forage-based diets, which is critical for maintaining maternal body condition and reproductive longevity [67]. Furthermore, the consistent “gut microbiota-metabolite-host immunity” axis (Fig. 7B) suggests that these taxa systemically alleviate oxidative stress and bolster maternal humoral immunity, potentially via signaling pathways involving HDAC inhibition or GPR41/43 activation [68, 69].

Beyond the maternal environment, the specific linkage between the milk microecology and immunoglobulins (Fig. 7C) reveals a defense mechanism for the newborn calf. The inverse relationship between maternal IgG/IgM and opportunistic pathogens (e.g., Delftia and Pseudomonas) suggests an “immune-filtering” effect, whereby high-quality milk limits the pathogen load transmitted to the offspring. Given that calves rely heavily on maternal passive immunity to navigate early-life environmental stressors, this vertical transmission of protective antibodies and a “low-pathogen” microbial signature is a key determinant in programming neonatal gastrointestinal health and immune development [70]. Consequently, the maternal microbial status directly influences the passive immune protection and developmental trajectory of the suckling calf, providing a theoretical foundation for targeted nutritional interventions to optimize both cow productivity and the pre-weaning growth resilience of the offspring.

PICRUSt2-based functional predictions revealed that GYP intervention induces highly parallel functional remodeling in both maternal gut and milk microbiomes, offering a novel perspective on the systemic immunomodulatory effects of the drug mediated via the “entero-mammary axis” [71]. The attenuation of SCFA metabolism alongside the robust enrichment of Th17/IL-17 inflammatory signaling pathways in both niches of the GYP group suggests that the intervention induces an active state of “immune priming” across the systemic mucosal immune network, rather than merely acting locally within the intestinal mucosa [72]. Importantly, as milk serves as a critical biological bridge between mother and offspring, these functional shifts bear profound physiological implications for the early development of calves. The heightened quorum sensing and biofilm formation pathways in the GYP milk microbiota likely reflect an adaptive evolutionary response of commensal bacteria under GYP-induced selection pressure; this adaptation may confer a competitive spatial and colonization advantage upon their entry into the neonatal calf’s gastrointestinal tract [73, 74]. Simultaneously, the augmented immune signals secreted into the milk (e.g., IL-17-related pathways) may be vertically transmitted as natural immunomodulators, potentially accelerating the establishment and maturation of the early intestinal immune barrier in the calf [75]. This synchronized microbial and immune response across the entero-mammary axis underscores the immense potential of maternal interventions in optimizing the early-life health trajectory of postpartum cows.

Notably, the diarrhea rate recorded in this study was observed during the first week after birth (days 0–7 postpartum). This period represents a critical window for the establishment of passive immunity in newborn calves and is also a stage of high susceptibility to intestinal infections. Numerous studies have shown that the incidence of diarrhea in newborn calves during the first week of life can reach 20–30% or even higher [76], which is consistent with the 22% diarrhea rate observed in the control group, aligning with the physiologically susceptible period of newborn calves during the first week postpartum.This study revealed that GYP improved calf growth performance. However, its mechanism of action did not involve directly enhancing the antioxidant capacity of calves (serum SOD, GSH, and MDA levels did not significantly change). The data suggest that the core effect of GYP may involve dual vertical transmission via the maternal‒milk pathway: on the one hand, the high levels of immunoglobulins, particularly IgG, in milk provide calves with immediate passive immune protection, significantly increasing their serum IgG levels [77]; on the other hand, specific beneficial bacterial communities in milk, such as Acetobacter, Lactobacillus, and Prevotellaceae_UGC-003, are transferred to calves, colonizing their intestines early on and exerting antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, digestive absorption-promoting, intestinal barrier-enhancing, and energy substrate-providing effects, thereby optimizing the intestinal microbiota structure and function. Immunoglobulins and beneficial microbial communities synergistically interact within calves to jointly establish calf health. This is directly reflected in the reduced diarrhea rate in the GYP group calves, improved growth performance, and ultimately, a calf survival rate of 100%, which is higher than the 81.82% reported in the CON group.

The regulatory effects of GYP on the antioxidant and immune functions of postpartum cows and their calves involve a complex process jointly participated by the direct action of active components and the mediating role of the gut microbiota, with distinct primary and secondary contributions of these two pathways in regulating cows and calves. For cows, absorbable active components in GYP, including Astragalus polysaccharides, ferulic acid, and Angelica polysaccharides [78–80], can directly enter the bloodstream after gastrointestinal absorption to scavenge free radicals, and also activate the host’s own antioxidant defense system by regulating antioxidant signaling pathways such as Nrf2, representing the primary pathway for enhancing antioxidant capacity in cows. Concurrently, GYP reshapes the gut microbiota of cows, increasing the abundance of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing genera such as Faecalibacterium and Oscillibacter [81], whose metabolites butyrate and propionate regulate host redox balance and intestinal barrier function through the microbe-gut axis [82] , serving as an important indirect pathway mediating improvements in antioxidant and immune functions in cows. In contrast, calves do not directly ingest GYP, and improvements in their physiological status rely entirely on maternal transmission via milk. By modulating the gut microbiota structure of cows, GYP further alters the composition of antioxidants, immune factors, and milk-derived microbiota in milk. Calves acquire milk-derived immunoglobulins and beneficial bacteria through suckling [83], thereby achieving rapid enhancement of passive immunity and establishing a healthy microecological environment through early intestinal colonization of beneficial bacteria. These synergistic effects contribute to improved disease resistance and growth performance in calves, also revealing the potential role of the gut microbiota and its metabolites in the vertical transmission of physiological phenotypes from mother to offspring [82, 84].

Despite the robust biological coherence observed, this study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, we could not quantitatively isolate the direct physiological effects of the active components of GYP from the secondary modulating effects mediated by the gut microbiota. Second, the experiment was exclusively conducted on Qinchuan cattle, the predominant beef breed in northern China. While our extensive foundational data on this breed ensures high practical value for local postpartum health management, the single-breed design limits the broader generalizability of our conclusions. Variations in genetics, physiology, and baseline microbiota among different breeds necessitate caution when extrapolating these results. Third, as previously noted, the commercial loose-housing and group-feeding system precluded the precise measurement of individual feed intake for the cows. Similarly, individual maternal milk and early starter intake for the neonatal calves could not be quantified under the natural suckling conditions. Finally, the relatively small sample size constrained the statistical power of our microbiome analysis. This limitation hindered the successful application of advanced compositional analysis models (such as ANCOM-BC), necessitating the use of classical non-parametric approaches to identify candidate taxa. Future research involving larger, individually housed cohorts, cross-breed validations, and metagenomic sequencing is required to overcome these constraints. Nevertheless, the multi-site regulatory network of the maternal-offspring axis revealed in this study still holds universal biological significance for advancing postpartum health management in beef cattle.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that GYP supplementation may exert multifaceted benefits through distinct pathways in both dams and offspring. In postpartum beef cows, GYP significantly (1) enhances systemic antioxidant capacity (increased SOD, GSH; decreased MDA) and immune function (elevated serum and milk immunoglobulins), (2) modulates the gut microbiota composition (enriched Faecalibacterium and Oscillibacter), and (3) increases SCFAs production. These effects collectively promote faster postpartum recovery through a proposed “herb-gut microbiota-immunity” axis. For offspring, GYP-mediated improvements in calf performance (increased weaning weight, average daily gain) and health (reduced diarrhea incidence) appear to be mediated through milk-based pathways, as evidenced by (i) elevated immunoglobulins (IgG) in calf serum and (ii) probiotic enrichment (Lactobacillus, Acetobacter) in maternal milk (Fig. 9).
