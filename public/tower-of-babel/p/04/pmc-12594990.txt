Surrogate model for statics of fractional thin bar element and its equivalence with mass-spring metamaterial
Authors: Krzysztof Szajek, Wojciech Sumelka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe paper presents a two-level metametrial homogenization procedure, in which the mechanical behavior of a bar with non-uniform stiffness profile is considered. The connection between the microstructure (spring-mass model) and the surrogate model (simple spring model) is done by an intermediate space-fractional bar element (FBE) modeled utilizing fractional calculus (FC). The impact of the FC parameters, namely a length scale and a material order, on the global stiffness of the bar was analyzed based on 6000 numerical analyses, including various stiffness profiles - distributions of Young’s modulus and the cross-sectional area along the element axis. The study reveals that changes in FC parameters significantly influence the stiffness of the resulting bar (up to 20%), and the impact of the change on the stiffness is very difficult to predict, highlighting the need for a detailed analysis. The presented approach with the model that mimics the microstructure efficiently opens a way to analyze large lattice or chain structures expressing scale effect and then to look for their optimal design.Keywords: Mass-spring metamaterial, Non-local models, Fractional calculus, Bar stiffness matrix, SubstructureSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In material sciences, it is common to think about the design of intrinsic material structures to obtain certain properties. These special properties can be considered from different points of view, e.g., electromagnetic (to control electromagnetic waves through unique structures such as split-ring resonators)1,2, acoustic (resonant structures that create bandgaps for sound attenuation)3,4 or mechanical (vibration damping and shock absorption)5,6. Such material we often call metamaterials7,8 i.e. artificially engineered structures designed to exhibit properties not found in natural materials.

It is evident that the practical design of metamaterials requires suitable mathematical techniques. One could mention here papers by9,10 where negative effective mass was analysed, paper by11 were piezoelectric nano-metamaterials were considered11, work by12 where chain metamaterials were analysed or paper by11 where meta nano-beams were described. These achievements show that non-local models are highly effective for describing metamaterials, as they can inherently (in a homogenized way) mimic the behavior of complex internal structures. Among the others, strain gradient theories13, micropolar theory14, surface elasticity theory15,16, couple stress theory13, dipolar gradient elasticity theory17 or peridynamic models18 can be mention. However, from the perspective of the presented paper, where mechanical properties/behavior are fundamental, modeling based on fractional calculus (FC) (so-called space-fractional models) is essential - cf. pioneering work19–21.

In the following sections, the metamaterials will be considered in the form of 1D spring mass systems, for which space-fractional model can be viewed as a continualisation model (Fig. 1), and simultaneously a 1st-level model reduction (1MR) - cf. similar concepts for other non-local formalisms22–28. Herein, fundamental results have been presented in a paper by Szajek et al.29, where an equivalence between the complex discrete mass-spring structure and space-fractional model was presented for the first time in the literature. Moreover, in29 it was proved that for a single set of material parameters in space-fractional model, both static and dynamic answers of the mass-spring metamaterial and nonlocal space-fractional model converge, which was not achieved previously for other nonlocal formulations mentioned above. However, the numerical approaches available for space-fractional model are still unsuitable for 2D or 3D complex chains (due to the high computational power needed), so in the presented paper, the 2nd-level model reduction (2MR) is proposed and even extended for microstructures with non-uniform stiffness.

The concept of 1st and 2nd levels of metamaterial model reduction. From bottom to top: spring-mass metamaterial, fractional bar element and surrogate model.

The proposed methodology for 2MR has been addressed for a comprehensive study of nearly 6000 computational cases. A 2MR was uniquely obtained for every configuration, allowing the surrogate model to be obtained in the form of a single spring. This single spring manifests the same kinematics as the underlying spring-mass metamaterial and space-fractional model, giving the possibility in future work to seek optimal 2D or 3D complex non-local chains/lattices.

The paper is structured as follows. The section “Governing equations and numerical approximation” defines mass-spring metamaterial, fractional bar element, and surrogate model. The section “Procedure” describes the numerical procedure for the concept of 2MR. The section “Results” presents a comprehensive numerical study for nearly 6000 computational cases. The section “Conclusions” concludes the paper.

Procedure

The objective is to establish a connection between the microstructure, specifically the mass-spring structure (MSLS), and the surrogate model expressed as a simple spring, through the utilization of an intermediate space-fractional bar element (FBE). As mentioned, Szajek et al.31 demonstrate that within FBE, the microstructure can be identified in the form of an MSLS. Thus, the surrogate model derived from FBE can be effectively regarded as a direct substitute for the spring-mass microstructure. The overarching concept of the two-tiered reduction is illustrated in Fig. 4. Initially, the repeatable module of the MSLS, defined by the springs stiffness and the masses , is substituted with FBE, which is characterized by a combination of the length scale and the order of material . Subsequently, which constitutes the primary focus of this paper, the bar element is further simplified to a single spring with parameter k. To investigate the impact of the length scale and the material order on the global stiffness of the bar, the conversion process is applied to various stiffness profiles, including distributions of Young’s modulus and the cross-sectional area along the element axis. To elucidate the complete path of this dual-step model reduction, from microstructure to the final spring, we particularly analyze the selected microstructure identified in31, focusing on the cases of and . It is imperative to highlight the significant novelty of this study, which, in contrast to prior work, allows the microstructure spring stiffness to vary along the element axis to reflect the assumed stiffness profile.

Initially, the displacements along the specimen, which was subjected to a prescribed boundary condition, were calculated. Subsequently, to quantitatively verify the mechanical response, the elongation of both the spring-mass microstructure and the fractional bar element was determined. The results can be analyzed from two perspectives. Firstly, the interrelation between microstructure (MSLS) and the surrogate model (represented as a spring) is examined through the application of FBE. Secondly, a noteworthy aspect is the variation in global stiffness of the fractional model across different configurations of fractional parameters and . This latter discussion pertains to the equivalence between the reconfiguration of microstructure–specifically, the number of repeatable modules and the stiffness of the springs–and the overall stiffness of the entire chain (cf. Fig. 4).

The comparison of displacements for the basic uniform stiffness profile, as depicted in Fig. 8, demonstrates identical outcomes for both FBE and its surrogate model, with a substantial agreement with the MSLS. In case of MSLS, the variability in spring stiffness within the microstructure causes fluctuations in displacements around the results of the other models. Notably, these fluctuations correspond to the spring-mass modules of length , resulting in ten repeatable sections of fluctuation along the element. With changes in profile, the outcomes for the FBE and its surrogate counterpart begin to diverge. Despite the absence of span load, the FBE result in non-linear displacements along the element. It is evident that these differences are related to the profile and intensity of the changes EA, which are the most pronounced for the and the least for the profiles. Despite discrepancies between the FBE and surrogate models, the microstructure displacements consistently follow the trend demonstrated by the FBE. The quantitative validation of the preceding observation is illustrated in Table 1. The elongation of FBE and MSLS under identical tensile loads is very similar. The calculated error ranges from 0.11% to 9.28%. Nevertheless, the mean error is 2.22%, which reduces to 1.58% when the result for ’zig-zag 5’ is excluded. The biggest error exceeds 9% for the most complex stiffness profile with five local drops to 10%. Considering the application of a numerical approach to solve the differential equation, the limited number of discretization points, and the substantial occurrence of floating-point arithmetic rounding errors, the results indicate that the FBE accurately replicates the mechanical behavior of the microstructure, not only for the uniform stiffness profile but also when variations are introduced along the element. Consequently, this facilitates the analysis of the microstructure using both the fractional and surrogate models. As a result, large structures composed of an MSLS with varied stiffness can be analyzed using space-fractional elements or their simplified surrogate model.

The second issue pertains to the variation in surrogate model stiffness as a function of the space-fractional bar parameters, consequently affecting its microstructure. By analyzing the graphs presented in Fig. 11 and 12, a modification in surrogate model stiffness is observed when there is a change in either the material order or the length scale . Since the fractional model transitions to the classical model when conditions or are met, the relative stiffness in these scenarios consistently remains at one, regardless of the stiffness profile. In all instances, a reduction in or an increase in results in a divergence of stiffness from the classical model. For simplification of further analysis, the results were divided into two groups: the first group compiles results for profiles Linear, Sinus, and Local, while the second corresponds to the stiffness profile. Within the first group, the maximum variation in stiffness reaches 20% for the profile under extreme analyzed values of and . This represents the maximum across all analyzed cases. The amplification in profile change (cf. the left and right columns of Fig. 11) augments the sensitivity of stiffness to the fractional parameters across all profiles in this group. For the Linear profile, the maximal change in stiffness escalates from approximately 1% for to 10% for . Similarly, for the other profiles in the group, the maximal change in relative stiffness rises from 6% to 20% for Sinus and from 2.5% to slightly above 8% for the Local profile. For the Linear and Sinus profiles, changes between fractional parameters and stiffness are proportional. In both cases, the stiffness diminishes when decreases or increases. Conversely, in the case of the Local profile, up to ca. , the trend is similar; however, exceeding this value, the tendency is reversed. This illustrates that the interrelation between fractional parameters and the global stiffness of the element may be challenging to predict without thorough analysis. Notably, for the Local profile, a significant shift in stiffness is observed upon exceeding . This indicates that the global element stiffness is not susceptible to local reductions in stiffness and microstructure with a small length scale. Let us proceed to the analysis of the second group results concerning the stiffness profile. The previously observed trend that and significantly affect the stiffness of the surrogate model is also valid within this group. The maximum alteration in stiffness is recorded for the profile when and are involved, exceeding 15%. Concurrently, as the number of local decreases in stiffness rises, the maximum reduction diminishes, amounting to just 3% for . This indicates that a fewer number of local declines enhance the sensitivity of stiffness to local effects more than multiple declines. It is also apparent that a greater number of local declines increases the complexity of the stiffness variability. For , there are five values of length scale that form a “local” maximum for stiffness change. Let us examine the underlying mechanism responsible for the formation of local maxima in greater detail. By analyzing the stiffness profile ranging from 1 to 5, several observations can be made. First, the number of local maxima is observed to be one fewer than the number of local minima in the stiffness profile. Second, the initial maximum occurs when the length scale is approximately equal to the distance required to reduce the stiffness from its initial value to 10% of that value. This distance shall be referred to as the reduction distance. Subsequently, each successive maximum is situated at a reduced distance from the preceding one. This phenomenon directly results from the principles of fractional calculus. Here, the length scale determines the range over which information is gathered to compute stiffness at a point. When the length scale matches the reduction distance, the reduction in stiffness is most strongly incorporated into the stiffness of neighboring points, leading to a decrease that manifests as a local maximum of .

In summary, the displacement results presented exhibit a high degree of concordance between the space-fractional bar, its surrogate models, and the microstructure across all stiffness profiles. This validates the potential of the space-fractional bar as a robust intermediary between the microstructure and the simplified spring model. Within the realm of stiffness analysis, the study highlights that alterations in order of material and length scale considerably influence stiffness, with certain stiffness profiles diverging by up to 20% from traditional models. The relationship between fractional parameters and global stiffness is complex and difficult to predict, thus underscoring the need for comprehensive analysis. Overall, the approach presented, which utilizes the surrogate model to replicate the microstructure efficiently, paves the way for the analysis of large planar and spatial structures with scale effects.

Conclusions

In the paper, the two-level metametrial homogenization procedure is presented, where statics mechanical behavior is considered. The connection between the microstructure (spring-mass model) and the surrogate model (simple spring model) was done using the space-fractional bar element modeled utilizing fractional calculus (FC). The procedure was also extended for deteriorated microstructures.

The results presented led to three important conclusions.

The space-fractional bar is an efficient link between the microstructure and the simply spring model, as it accurately replicates the mechanical behavior of the microstructure, not only for the uniform stiffness profile but also when variations are introduced along the element.
