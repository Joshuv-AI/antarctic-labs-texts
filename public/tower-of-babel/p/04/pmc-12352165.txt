Fabrication of 3D Printed, Core‐and‐Shell Implants as Controlled Release Systems for Local siRNA Delivery
Authors: Dina B. Mahmoud, Christian Wölk, Michaela Schulz‐Siegmund
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development and clinical translation of small interfering RNA (siRNA) therapies remains challenging owing to their poor pharmacokinetics. 3D printing technology presents a great opportunity to fabricate personalized implants for local and sustained delivery of siRNA. Hydrogels can mimic the mechanical properties of tissues, avoiding the problems associated with rigid implants. Herein, a thermoresponsive composite hydrogel suitable for extrusion 3D‐printing is formulated to fabricate controlled‐release implants loaded with siRNA‐Lipofectamine RNAiMAX complexes. A hydrogel matrix mainly composed of uncharged agarose to protect siRNA from decomplexation is selected. Additionally, pluronic F127 and gelatin are added to improve the printability, degradation, and cell adhesion to the implants. To avoid exposing siRNA to thermal stress during the printing process, a core‐and‐shell design is set up for the implants in which a core of siRNA‐complexes loaded‐pluronic F127 is printed without heat and enclosed with a shell comprising the thermoresponsive composite hydrogel. The release profile of siRNA‐complexes is envisioned to be controlled by varying the printing patterns. The results reveal that the implants sustain siRNA release for one month. The intactness of the released siRNA‐complexes is proven until the eighth day. Furthermore, by changing the printing patterns, the release profiles can be tailored.

Introduction

The synthetic double stranded siRNA is a potential candidate in developing gene silencing therapies.[ 1 , 2 ] Nevertheless, the development and translation of siRNA therapies for clinical use remains challenging due to the poor pharmacokinetic properties of the siRNA. siRNA molecules are negatively charged, relatively large, and prone to degradation which makes their oral bioavailability very low. Further, the systemic administration of siRNA leads to rapid renal clearance[ 3 ] and susceptibility to nuclease degradation, particularly, if administered without optimum chemical modification.[ 4 ] Moreover, siRNA molecules do not promptly cross the lipid bilayers as in the case of the outer cell membranes and the intracellular endo‐lysosomal vesicles. In the latter case, the siRNA can be degraded in the lysosomes or suffer exocytosis.[ 5 ] Hence, various approaches are implemented to address these challenges such as chemical modification and the use of lipid or polymeric carriers.[ 3 ] Transfection utilizing lipids or liposomes is the most commonly used approach with a bundle of commercially available reagents which enhance the fusion with and internalization across the cell membranes[ 6 ] such as Lipofectamine RNAiMAX.[ 7 ] Recently, novel siRNA products, including some long‐acting products, have been developed and marketed, among them products for intravenous (ONPATTRO) and subcutaneous (e.g., Leqvio) application to target siRNA to liver cells via adsorption of apolipoprotein E on lipid nanoparticles or via the triantennary N‐acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) ligand directly attached to the chemically stabilized siRNA.[ 8 ] Besides, siRNA for intrathecal delivery (ALN‐APP) to target the cells of the central nervous system, for local treatment in the vitreous body of the eye or the lung are investigated in clinical trials.[ 9 ] Nevertheless, the use of these products may be limited for certain diseases, for instance, those involving genes expressed in liver.[ 10 ] Hence, there is a great demand for potent tissue‐targeted delivery approaches beyond the liver and through other administration routes and for other diseases. Generally, the delivery of complexed siRNA remains challenging because of the potentially induced toxicity and immunogenicity of the siRNA and the utilized carrier system.[ 11 ] Additionally, siRNA can induce off‐target responses due to the partial complementarity to sequences in unplanned genes.[ 12 ] Hence, local delivery of the siRNA‐carrier system appears favorable compared to systemic application in order to decrease off‐site effects, achieve sufficient siRNA doses at the target regions, and minimize the rapid siRNA clearance.[ 13 ] Meanwhile, controlling the release kinetics of siRNA is crucial especially when the reapplication of siRNA dose is not easy or requires medical assistance.[ 14 ] The individual characteristics of a disease and its localization, for example in the case of cancer, emphasize the need for personalized medicine. This can be easily achieved utilizing 3D printing technology[ 15 , 16 ] which involves precise deposition of materials in a layer‐by‐layer mode utilizing computer‐aided designs (CADs); this can produce various complex 3D geometrical structures with desirable spatial arrangements.[ 17 ] Recently, 3D printed implants have gained great interest for local drug delivery[ 18 , 19 ] allowing the perfect fitting to the anatomy of each patient besides the possibility of adjusting dose, size, shape, and release profile of the implants by controlling the printing process.[ 20 , 21 ] However, uniform drug loading with minimum waste or decomposition of the drug during the 3D printing process remains challenging. Drug loading in the 3D printed structures can be achieved either by incorporation of the drug in the polymer matrix before or during the printing,[ 22 ] or by application of post‐printing procedures, for example, immersing the drug‐free implant in a highly concentrated drug solution.[ 23 ] In the latter case, thermo‐sensitive drugs can be protected from decomposition when high temperatures are used during the printing process. The optimal solvent should dissolve adequate drug amounts to facilitate its diffusion without affecting the polymers and the printed structures. Hence, loading efficiency is limited by the specific properties of the drug, the polymers, and the solvent.[ 11 ] Furthermore, this approach requires additional production steps, which is time consuming and the use of high drug concentrations is costly, particularly, for expensive drugs. Besides, achieving high loading requires highly porous structures to facilitate drug diffusion throughout the implants.[ 24 , 25 ] In contrast, direct incorporation of the drug in the polymer matrix permits homogeneous distribution of the drug in the 3D printed structures, minimizes the complexity of production procedures, and provides more efficient diffusion barriers and sustained release. Meanwhile, a suitable printing strategy should be selected to avoid heat induced degradation of the thermo‐labile materials, such as RNA therapeutics,[ 26 ] during the printing process. Though 3D printing has the potential to fabricate siRNA delivery systems with tailored release kinetics, there are some challenges that need to be overcome to design a successful system. For instance, the choice of materials should be carefully performed to ensure their suitability for 3D printing and for effective siRNA delivery simultaneously. The material needs to be printable under acceptable conditions, cytocompatible, allow for cell adhesion, and preserve the functionality of the complexed siRNA. Agarose is a natural, neutral polysaccharide with the ability to form thermoresponsive hydrogels with high water‐absorbing capacity thus mimicking physical properties of soft tissues.[ 27 , 28 ] It has been utilized in sustained drug and gene delivery[ 29 , 30 ] along with its use in extrusion 3D printing as it is biocompatible and exhibits good mechanical stability. Nevertheless, agarose exhibits limited cell adhesion, low degradation rate, and a relatively high upper critical solution temperature,[ 23 ] which requires high processing temperatures interfering with the intactness of siRNA complexes, thus suitable modification is crucial. Herein, our aim was to develop a composite agarose‐based hydrogel with printability, degradability, and improved cell adhesion properties for the fabrication of 3D printed controlled release implants for personalized siRNA therapy. The neutral charge of agarose was envisioned to avoid the possible electrostatic interference with siRNA complexes in the matrix. For instance, the decomplexation of siRNA nanocomplexes is known to occur with charged matrices during storage and release from the matrix,[ 31 ] in addition to rapid release and aggregation problems that may occur with negatively charged matrices.[ 32 ] Furthermore, the water absorption capacity of the hydrogels is advantageous for soft tissue applications reducing the risks associated with implantation of rigid implants as in case of the commercial Gliadel wafers which were reported to induce micro‐tears post‐implantation in the brain for cancer eradication.[ 33 ] In order to combine the advantages of agarose and keep the complexed siRNA intact, we set up a simultaneous multi‐material printing process for the implants. To this end, a core–shell implant was designed with a core containing Lipofectamine RNAiMAX complexed siRNA which was embedded in an agarose composite hydrogel shell. This shell was intended to provide mechanical stability and diffusion barrier in order to control siRNA release. In contract to the agarose composite hydrogel forming the shell, the siRNA core was printed without heat. With this material concept, we envisioned to control the release of functional siRNA complexes only by varying the printing patterns of the shell hydrogel. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to control the release profile of siRNA complexes only by changing the 3D printing patterns of hydrogel implants without changing the composition of the matrix. These implants are envisioned to act as a platform for local delivery of siRNA therapeutics for various purposes achieving high concentration of siRNA at their site of action and consequently minimize the off‐target effects of siRNA. For instance, the implants may be loaded with anticancer siRNA for eradication of the residual cancerous cells after surgical removal of tumors when implanted in the resected cavities. In this case, 3D printing offers designing implants with specific shapes to fit in the resected cavity where the dose and release pattern of siRNA can be tailored to each patient's need.

Results and Discussion

Preparation of Composite Hydrogel Ink for the Shell

Composite hydrogels were formulated utilizing agarose as the main component to fabricate 3D printed soft implants for siRNA local delivery and controlled release. Owing to its uncharged properties, it is an attractive material for the delivery of siRNA‐complexes. Nevertheless, the modification of agarose matrix by mixing with other components was necessary to improve the printability, degradation rate, and the limited cell adhesion of agarose. Hence, PF127 and gelatin were added to the agarose matrix to formulate six composite hydrogels with the compositions illustrated in Table 1 . Before selecting an optimum composition for 3D printing of the implants, the prepared composite hydrogels were characterized in terms of rheological and thermal behavior, physicochemical interaction, and cell response.

Rheological Evaluation

Rheograms of the composite hydrogels of varied composition are shown in Figure 1 . Temperature sweep test revealed that all the composite hydrogels exhibit thermoresponsive properties changing from sol to gel upon cooling. This property is important for extrusion 3D printing. Further, to set the printing conditions, the sol–gel transition temperature was recorded for each composite hydrogel as the crossover point between storage and loss moduli curves (arrow Figure 1A), ranging from 37.3 to 41.2 °C. The storage moduli of all composite hydrogels at room temperature were significantly lower than the storage modulus of pure 5% agarose gel without additives (≈335 kPa). Meanwhile, all hydrogels showed high storage moduli (>30 kPa) at room temperature with statistically insignificant differences. It was also important to ensure that the hydrogel would not liquefy and convert to sol state after implantation in the body at 37 °C. Figure 1B depicts that there is no crossover between the storage moduli and loss moduli upon increasing the temperature of the formed gels from 20 to 60 °C. This signifies the thermal stability of the formed hydrogels when implanted at body temperature.

TGA Analysis

TGA is a useful tool to investigate temperature induced changes in the compositions of polysaccharides and their thermal stability.[ 34 ] TGA thermograms of agarose, PF127, gelatin, and the composite hydrogels, dried at ambient temperature, are depicted in Figure S1, Supporting Information. It is noticeable that TGA thermogram of agarose powder depicts three weight loss events, the first occurs at 80 °C due to water loss followed by a major weight loss at 316 °C which corresponds to the degradation of polysaccharide chains,[ 35 ] and a final event at 597 °C. Similarly, the thermogram of gelatin powder shows three events, first at 96 °C due to water evaporation followed by major weight loss at 324 °C corresponding to gelatin decomposition[ 36 ] and final weight loss that occurs at 688 °C. Additionally, PF127 shows only one drastic weight loss (98%) at 315 °C, in accordance with a previous report.[ 37 ] Dried agarose gels (3% and 5%) showed an initial weight loss (≈8%) due to water evaporation at 130 and 139 °C, respectively, followed by a major step at 309 °C, corresponding to the decomposition of polysaccharide (59% and 56%, respectively). The effects of the additives in the composite agarose gels are summarized in Table 2 . Overall, we observed some differences between the thermograms of the pure agarose hydrogel and the composite hydrogels. The 3% agarose containing composite hydrogels depicted lower weight loss (≈4%) due to water desorption at lower temperatures (95–100 °C) when compared with the pure 3% agarose gel (8% at 130 °C). Similarly, the 5% agarose containing composite hydrogels showed a weight loss of ≈5.5% due to water evaporation at statistically significant lower temperatures than the pure 5% agarose gel (107–118 °C). Both, the lower water content of the air dried composite hydrogels and the lower temperature of water loss suggest a weakening of water immobilization possibly due to polar interactions in the composite hydrogel by the addition of PF127 compared to the pure agarose gels. Additionally, the composite hydrogels with 5% agarose showed higher weight loss due to water evaporation at higher temperatures compared to their corresponding 3% agarose composite hydrogels. This indicates stronger water binding in the composite hydrogels of 5% agarose compared to the 3% agarose composite hydrogels. Comparing the additive containing hydrogels Agarose/PF127 and Agarose/PF127/gelatin with pure agarose, a statistically significant increase in % weight loss in the chain decomposition region was observed for the composite hydrogels, for both, the 3% and 5% systems (Table 2). This revealed that the introduction of PF127 or PF127/gelatin decreased the thermal stability of agarose hydrogels and weakened the network of agarose. This may be beneficial to fasten the low degradation rate of agarose and subsequently modify the release profile of the incorporated therapeutic moieties. Further, results determined the temperature range of thermal stability for the composite hydrogels to ensure that no thermal decomposition occurs at the printing temperature.

DSC Analysis

Thermograms of agarose, PF127, gelatin, and the composite hydrogels are depicted in Figure 2 . PF127 shows an endothermic sharp peak at ≈57 °C corresponding to its melting temperature.[ 38 , 39 , 40 ] The melting peak also appears in all thermograms of the composite hydrogels containing 3% agarose indicating that PF127 is still in its crystalline form. We observed a shift to lower temperatures as well as a pronounced decrease in the enthalpy of melting in the composite hydrogels (Table S1, Supporting Information). This observation may be attributed to interaction between agarose and PPO blocks of PF127, which in turn might suppress the space available for the crystallization of PEO blocks of PF127. The PF127 melting peak was absent for composite hydrogels that contain 5% of agarose. This reveals that PF127 has been converted to the amorphous form, imparting higher flexibility to the system.[ 41 ] The observed agarose concentration‐dependent reduction of PF127 crystallinity may be attributed to plasticization of PF127 by agarose suggesting possible intermolecular interactions between both polymers. Additionally, all composite hydrogels showed a second broad endothermic peak referring to dehydration in the polymer matrix at around 103–115 °C. The enthalpy of water dehydration is regarded as the amount of energy required to remove the immobilized water molecules from a hydrated material.[ 42 ] The hydrogels containing 5% agarose showed higher enthalpy of dehydration at higher temperatures and consequently higher water uptake and tighter linking of water with the hydrogels compared to 3% agarose containing hydrogels (Table S1, Supporting Information). This immobilized water is suggested to maintain PF127 in the amorphous form in the hydrogel matrices. Results suggested that composite hydrogels containing 5% agarose are more suitable than 3% agarose hydrogels for the printing of our systems.

FTIR

FTIR is a widely used technique to investigate the conformational changes regarding the gelation or micellization of pluronic containing systems.[ 43 , 44 ] Thus it was utilized in the current study to examine possible interactions of PF127 with agarose and gelatin in the composite hydrogels. The molecular structures of agarose, gelatin and PF127 are shown in Figure S2, Supporting Information.[ 45 , 46 , 47 ] The whole spectra of agarose, gelatin, PF127, and the dried composite hydrogels are displayed in Figure S3, Supporting Information. For PF127 powder the two bands at 1342 and 1360 cm−1 correspond to wagging (ω) vibration of ─CH2 group and bands at 1241 and 1279 cm−1 to the twisting vibrations (Figure 3A). Additionally, the stretching (ν) vibration of C–O–C can be seen as three main bands at 1060, 1099, and 1146 cm−1 (Figure 3B). The observed peak at 961 (Figure 3C) corresponds to the rocking (ρ) of ─CH2 group. These results agree with previous reports in literature.[ 40 , 41 ] Presence of water usually introduces dramatic changes in the FTIR bands making them weaker, broader, or sometimes shifted in frequency and merged to broader peaks. For instance, the stretching bands of C–O–C tended to merge to a broad band and shifted to a lower wavenumber from 1099 to 1071 cm−1 in the composite hydrogels containing 5% agarose (Figure 3B). This band corresponds to the combination of the stretching vibrations of C–O–C in both PEO and PPO blocks and this shift may be due to hydrogen bond formation between water and the oxygen atoms in PF127 ether backbone in 5% agarose/PF127 or agarose/PF127/gelatin composite hydrogels. Furthermore, in the composite hydrogels containing 5% agarose the ω (CH2) vibration band at 1360, the other ω (CH2) band at 1342, the ─CH2 rocking (ρ) band at 961 cm−1 and the twisting (τ) vibrations of CH2 at 1241 and 1279 cm−1 have been disappeared. This may indicate the presence of PF127 in hydrated amorphous form. This is also confirmed by the disappearance of the bands at 2881 (Figure 3D) and 1466 cm−1 (Figure 3E) which correspond to asymmetric stretching (ν as) of CH2 and CH2 scissoring (δ) vibration, respectively, upon increasing the concentration of agarose (from 3% to 5%) or gelatin (from 0.25 to 0.5%). These observations are in agreement with DSC results which revealed the disruption of the crystalline structure of PF127 in 5% agarose composite hydrogels. The previous observations can also be seen with the spectra of the hydrogel composed of 3% agarose/3%PF127/0.5% gelatin but not with 3% agarose/3%PF127 and 3% agarose/3%PF127/0.25% gelatin hydrogels. Taking in consideration the DSC results above, it is suggested that PF127 partially lost its crystalline structure in 3% agarose/3%PF127/0.5% gelatin.

The spectrum of agarose depicts a broad peak at 3361 cm−1 referring to the stretching vibration of O─H group[ 48 ] (Figure S3, Supporting Information). Besides, the bands of 3,6‐anhydrogalactose bending at 931 and 771 cm−1 along with the C─H bending vibration of β‐galactose units at 881 cm−1 and the stretching vibration band of C─O at 1037 cm−1 are observable in Figure 3F.[ 49 , 50 , 51 ] It is noticeable that the bands at 931, 771, and 1037 cm−1 disappeared in the hydrogels of 3% agarose. This can be justified by the involvement of 3,6 galactose ring in the interaction with PF127. However, these bands are still visible with 5% agarose concentration, which also reveals that the 3% concentration of agarose was not enough to interact completely with the current amount of PF127 in the gels.

Gelatin spectrum reveals a broad peak at 3275 cm−1 corresponding to N─H group along with two bands related to amide C═O stretching and N─H bending at 1628 and 1519 cm−1, respectively[ 52 ] (Figure S3, Supporting Information). The decrease in N─H band intensity in all hydrogel composites at 1519 cm−1 (Figure 3G) suggests the interaction of the N─H group of gelatin in the composite hydrogels. Generally, FTIR results are in accordance with DSC results confirming the interaction between PF127, gelatin, and agarose in the hydrogel matrix. This interaction was envisioned to reduce the storage modulus of the composite hydrogel as well as aid in the diffusion of siRNA complexes from the composite hydrogel matrix compared to the pure agarose hydrogel which is known to hinder the movement of the complexed siRNA in gel electrophoresis studies. Based on these results (TGA, DSC, and FTIR), we determined that composite hydrogels containing 5% agarose are more suitable than 3% agarose hydrogels for further characterization for printing our systems.

Cell Adhesion

Cell adhesion on the material surface is beneficial in siRNA delivery through "substrate mediated uptake" in which cells, which are in direct contact with nucleic acid delivery system, internalize the nucleic acids. This mechanism can augment the transfection efficiency (by 10–100 fold) through concentrating the therapeutic agents at the material–cell interface.[ 53 , 54 ] The use of charged polymers in hydrogel formulations is known to directly affect the intactness of released siRNA complexes.[ 26 ] Herein we investigated agarose and PF127 as the main components of the hydrogels as they will not compete with the charged siRNA or the cationic carrier (Lipofectamine RNAiMAX). Nevertheless, agarose exhibits poor cell adhesion retaining round cellular morphology.[ 55 ] Approaches to solve this problem include covalent modification of agarose with adhesive proteins or peptides[ 56 , 57 , 58 ] and physical blending of agarose with other materials such as collagen,[ 59 , 60 ] lysine, poly(lysine), fish derived gelatin,[ 61 ] and graphene oxide.[ 62 ] In this study, we incorporated small quantities of gelatin in the hydrogel formulations (0.25% and 0.5% w/w) to improve the cell adhesion to our agarose/PF127 composite hydrogels. Microscopic images of L929 fibroblasts grown on the hydrogels are depicted in Figure 4 revealing that L929 cells grow denser and elongated on the hydrogel formulated with 0.5% gelatin while there are less cells growing on the hydrogels without or with only 0.25% gelatin. Additionally, fluorescence images are depicted in Figure S4, Supporting Information, in which live L929 cells (stained green with Calcein AM) are discriminated from the dead cells (stained red with ethidium homodimer‐1). It is noticeable that cells did not adhere and proliferate much on agarose/PF127 hydrogels while incorporation of gelatin by 0.5% in the hydrogels improved the adhesion of the cells on the surface of the hydrogels.

Microscopic images exhibits the growth of L929 cells after 6 days on: A) 5% agarose, 3% PF127 hydrogel, B) 5% agarose, 3%PF127, 0.25% gelatin hydrogel, C) 5% agarose, 3% PF127, 0.5% gelatin hydrogel, and D) control cells cultured on polystyrene well plate. The scale bar represents 100 µm.

Based on the aforementioned results, the composite hydrogel that was composed of 5% agarose/3% PF127/0.5% gelatin w/w was selected for further investigation as a potential ink for extrusion 3D printing. As the introduction of PF127 and gelatin (0.5%) was able to interrupt and weaken the matrix of agarose, which was envisioned to have an impact on improving the low degradation of agarose with subsequent release of the entrapped therapeutics. Additionally, using agarose in the concentration of 5% was able to produce interaction with PF127 converting it to the amorphous form in the hydrogel matrix. Further, this hydrogel depicted good cell adhesion owing to its gelatin content.

Printability of the Composite Hydrogel Ink

Thus the extrusion printability and the printing conditions of this hydrogel were examined. Adjusting the printing conditions is a critical step in 3D printing in which obtaining continuous and uniform filaments is desirable. Hence, optimization should be done to determine the optimum speed of the printhead along with the extruded amount of the hydrogel ink. It is noticeable from Figure 5A,B that at low feed rate, thick non uniform filaments were obtained. Further upon increasing the feed rate value from 6 to 24 mm s−1, the mean filament diameter decreased from 1.62 ± 0.32 to 0.69 ± 0.3 mm, respectively. However, the feed rate of 24 produced also discontinuous filaments as the printhead moved faster than the speed required for the optimum hydrogel extrusion. Finally, the filaments obtained using feed rate 18 was continuous and uniform. Hence the feed rate was set to be 18 mm s−1 in printing the implants.

Printability of PF127 Core Ink

In order to print siRNA loaded implants, an ink that is printable at room temperature is favorable. PF127 is an ideal ink for printing regarding the ease of extrusion and the printing precision. However, it lacks the mechanical stability if used alone (i.e., the printed structure collapses when an attempt is made to remove it from the printing stage[ 63 ]). Thus PF127 was selected as a suitable ink for loading siRNA complexes into the implant utilizing a second printing head for the PF127 hydrogel containing the complexed siRNA. This step eliminated the necessity of post‐printing loading of the siRNA. PF127 was 3D printed into a disc shape core between the shell layers printed with 5%agarose/3%PF127/0.5% gelatin ink. Figure 5C depicts extruded filaments of 30% w/w PF127. Further, fluorescent labeled AF 488 siRNA‐lipofectamine RNAiMAX complexes could be loaded homogenously in PF127 core ink as shown in Figure 5D.

Printing of the Assembled Implants

For the implant printing, four designs were set up by changing the number of layers and line spacing with the aim to control the release of siRNA complexes. The layer thickness was adjusted based on the line space in each layer. Design 1 and Design 2 were composed of ten layers but differed with reference to the line spacing as follows: Design 1 was fabricated with line space of 0.4 mm in each layer with interchangeable vertical and horizontal filling pattern. Design 2 was composed of five layers with line space of 0.4 mm, three layers with line space of 0.8 mm, and two layers with line space 0.5 mm in the order shown in Figure 5E. Designs 3 and 4 were fabricated with only six layers but they differ in line spacing where it is 0.4 mm in four layers and 0.7 mm in two layers in design 3 and 0.4 mm in the six layers in design 4 as illustrated in Figure 5E.

Swelling Index

The ability of the implants to swell in water is an important property to be studied as it has an impact on the diffusion of the therapeutic molecules from the implant.[ 64 ] Figure 6 shows that all the implants have high swelling properties. This can be justified by the water absorption capability of the hydrogels as well as the presence of interconnected macropores in the 3D structures. It is reported that agarose is able to absorb water up of 4.6 fold its dry mass.[ 34 ] Swelling of the implants was tested in deionized water and H‐N buffer pH 7.4. Results revealed that the swelling of the implants was generally higher in buffer than in water, however that differences were not significant except for implant design 1 at early time points (30 min and 1 h) and implant 4 at 30 min. This observation can be explained by the presence of gelatin because the swelling behavior of gelatin is highly affected by the concentration of salts in solution.[ 65 ] Results also revealed that the highest swelling index was obtained after 30 min in buffer in case of implant design 3 with a value of 4.02 ± 0.01, which is statistically higher than that of implant designs 1 and 2 (swelling indices are 2.56 ± 0.02 and 3.45 ± 0.20). After 1 h, the swelling index of implant design 3 remained significantly higher than that of design 1, but the difference was insignificant when compared with implant design 2. This can be attributed to the wider printing pattern and lower layer number of design 3 which facilitated rapid water diffusion inside the implant and consequently rapid swelling. This also can explain the peak swelling index of design 3 at 30 min which started to decrease thereafter to become the least of all designs at 24 h, as the rapid swelling may trigger a faster degradation of the core and/or the hydrogel components. It is also noticeable that design 2 that was fabricated with wider line spacing than design 1 showed a statistically significant higher swelling index than design 1 at 30 min and 1 h. Further, implant design 4 depicts statistically significant higher swelling index than design 1 at all the time points. This can be justified by the lower number of layers of design 4 than design 1, meaning that intrusion of water in the ten layer implants is restricted for a longer period. It is also noticeable that starting from 2 h time point, the swelling indices of the implants in water was comparable to that in the buffer with insignificant differences.

Determination of % Mass Remained of the Implants after Extraction

The % mass remained of implant designs 1, 2, 3, and 4 after extraction of the implants with H‐N buffer are depicted in Figure 6D. It is noteworthy that all the implants were fabricated using the same design of PF127 core which means that they all have the same theoretical weight of the core (28.9 ± 1.2 mg) but differ in the shell weight. The average weights of implant design 1, 2, 3, and 4 (directly recorded after printing) were 755.7 ± 45.1, 554.4 ± 11.6, and 388.7 ± 16.5, 472.6 ± 20.1 mg; respectively. Thus the core constitutes 3.8%, 5.2%, 7.4%, and 6.1% of average implant total weight. It can be noticed from Figure 6D that implant design 3 depicts the highest % mass loss among all the investigated designs followed by design 4, 2, and 1, respectively. The mass loss of design 3 implant is statistically significant higher than implant design 1 at all the time points (2, 5, 10, 20, and 30 days). In case of design 3, on day 2 the % remaining dry mass was 61.3 ± 2.1% from the initial dry weight of the implant and decreased to 52.2 ± 1.6% after 30 days of incubation in buffer pH 7.4. This can be attributed to the dissolution of PF127 from the core and hydrogel matrix as the total amount of PF127 in both the core and the shell in design 3 contributes to 52.9% of the initial dry mass of the implant. The difference in % mass remained between design 1 and 2 was not statistically significant at any time point, while, there were statistically significant differences between design 2 and 3 at all‐time points starting on day 10. Comparing the mass loss profiles of designs 1 and 4, which contain the same line spacing but different layer numbers, revealed that there is a significant difference between the % remaining mass on day 2 and 5. Further, comparing designs 3 and 4 which contain the same layer number but differ in line spacing revealed statistically significant differences starting from day 5 to day 30. Generally, increasing line spacing or decreasing the layer numbers leads to faster mass loss as this may allow water to penetrate faster and dissolve the polymer matrix and the PF127 core. Additionally, there is a uniform pattern of mass loss in all the four tested designs as there is a distinct initial mass loss till day 5 followed by a steady state. This may be attributed to the rapid release of PF127 and gelatin from the composite hydrogels leaving agarose, with its low degradation rate, in the implants.

Stabilizing effect of PF127 Ink on siRNA Complexes

The use of pluronics for fabricating nanoparticle delivery systems has gained great interest as an alternative stabilization strategy to PEGylation.[ 66 ] Besides, they have been utilized in siRNA delivery systems in some reports to stabilize siRNA complexes.[ 67 , 68 , 69 ] Since PF127 has been used as hydrogel ink to fabricate the core part of the implants, we tested for the stabilizing effect on siRNA complexes via a functionality test. Cell death inducing siRNA (CD siRNA) was used as a model siRNA and the % viability of SAOS‐2 cells was used as an indicator of the functionality of siRNA. Since one of the potential applications for these siRNA loaded implants is thought to be eradication of the residual cancerous cells after surgical removal of the tumor, when implanted in the resected cavity, we employed osteosarcoma cells (SAOS‐2 cells) for this experiment. As shown in Figure S5A, Supporting Information, the CD siRNA complexes loaded in PF127 ink have comparable activity to the freshly prepared complexes without PF127 resulting in a cell viability of ≈11% after transfection, while the non‐coding siRNA resulted in >80% cell survival. We then investigated the effect of PF127 on the stability of the complexed siRNA and found its activity retained until day 15with no statistically significant difference between the % cell viability compared to the freshly prepared complexes. In contrast, the PF127‐free complexes started losing their functionality gradually after 24 h till complete loss of the activity when stored in medium until 15 days (comparing lipo CD with PF lipo CD in Figure S5A, Supporting Information. Though, the activity of PF127 CD‐siRNA decreased after 30 days incubation with cell culture medium, it remained superior to the complexes without PF127 with % cell viability of 46 ± 5% and 91 ± 3%, respectively. It is also noticeable that PF127 did not cause cytotoxicity at all the tested doses as depicted in Figure S5B, Supporting Information.

In Vitro Release Study of siRNA from the 3D Printed Implant

The release profiles of siRNA complexes are depicted in Figure 7A. Results revealed that the 3D printed implants could successfully sustain the release of siRNA complexes over a period of more than 30 days. The cumulative % siRNA released in 30 days was 35.1 ± 5.3%, 38.5 ± 7.6%, 70.4 ± 5.4%, and 89.4 ± 1.2% in case of designs 1, 2, 4, and 3, respectively. We found that the release of the complexes from design 3 was the fastest followed by that of designs 4, 2, and 1, respectively. Generally, there was an initial burst release within the first 5 days followed by a stage of more controlled release till the end of the experiment at 30 days. For instance, the cumulative % siRNA complexes released from design 3 was 80.4 ± 1.1% in 5 days while it is 66.9 ± 5.7%, 23.9 ± 6.4%, and 17.4 ± 2.2% in case of designs 4, 2, and 1, respectively. These results are in accordance with the implant mass loss results which revealed that implants have an initial rapid mass loss in the first 5 days followed by steady degradation profile till day 30. As illustrated, Designs 3 and 4 consisted of less layers (6) than Designs 1 and 2 which were composed of ten layers but differed in line spacing. It is also noticeable that the release profile from design 2 was faster than that of design 1. Similarly, the release of design 3 was faster than that of design 4 and that of both design 1 and 2; and design 3 and 4 contained the same number of layers but different line spacing pattern. Generally, wider line spacing pattern achieved faster release profiles. The differences in the release profile between design 3 and 4 was statistically significant starting from the second day. Further, the difference in line spacing between design 1 and 2 caused significantly higher release from design 2 during the first 4 days. However, this difference was not relevant for longer release periods. Comparing design 1 with design 4, as the latter has the same line spacing but fewer layer numbers, we observed that there is a significant increase in the cumulative % drug release at all‐time points. This indicates that changing layer numbers is more effective in tuning the release profile than changing line spacing. Overall, inducing pronounced changes in the 3D printing pattern through changing both layer number and the line spacing had strong effects on the siRNA release (e.g., comparing design 1 with design 3). Fitting the release data to various kinetics models (Figure S6, Supporting Information; Table 3 ) revealed that the faster releasing implants (designs 3 and 4) did not fit well any of the tested models due to the biphasic release pattern with a burst release of the complexes in the first 5 days. Hence, fitting the release data only for the first 5 days[ 70 ] were done for implants 3 and 4. Higuchi model was not applicable for design 3 and 4 since data points fulfilling the fitting requirement for this design were missing. The release of designs 3 and 4 was best fitting first order kinetics indicating that the release of the complexes from the implants was concentration dependent.[ 71 ] On the other hand, designs 1 and 2 fit well Higuchi model with diffusion‐governed release mechanism through the ten layers of the shell which act a constrained area. This is in agreement with our observation in a preliminary study where we visualized the diffusion of fluorescently labeled siRNA complexes loaded in a core compartment embedded in the composite hydrogel shell. We detected the time dependent homogeneous distribution of fluorescent labeled‐siRNA (AF488) complexes along the hydrogel shell, as shown in Figure S7, Supporting Information. The diffusion exponent (n) values were <0.5 in all designs suggesting the release mechanism is governed by Fickian diffusion.[ 72 , 77 ]

Agarose Gel Electrophoresis

The implants were designed to sustain the release of siRNA complexes, thus the daily released amounts were not be sufficient to run the gel electrophoresis. Hence, we pooled the samples released from days 1 to 8 in order to have detectable concentrations of siRNA for the study. Figure 7B revealed that the bands of the released siRNA samples did not migrate in the agarose gels compared to the free siRNA samples which migrated in the gels. The result suggested that the released siRNA samples remained in the complexed form with no detectable free siRNA. This can be justified by the stabilizing effect the PF127 in the core ink which probably contributed to maintaining the functionality of the complexes. Additionally, the neutral nature of agarose being the main component of the composite hydrogel of the shell, may also justify this result confirming the study concept to avoid decomplexation via ionic interactions between hydrogel and complexes.

Functionality of siRNA Complexes Released from the 3D Printed Implant

In order to investigate if the released siRNA remained functional, we tested the effect of CD siRNA again on SAOS‐2 cells as a model cancer cells since osteosarcoma is one of the potential applications of the implants. We developed a special cell culture set up in which the implant was moved from well to well every second day, while the implant was not in direct cell contact. This was attained by a supporting agarose ring allowing to place the implants above the cells, thus the cells can be treated with the released siRNA complexes as explained in Figure 8 . The experiments were performed with implants of designs 2 and 4. The % viability of SAOS‐2 cells after treatment is depicted in Figure 9 . In case of implant design 4 (Figure 9A), the % viability of the cells treated with CD siRNA loaded implants were lower than the cells treated with NC siRNA loaded implants at all‐time points. The decrease in % cell viability obtained with implants loaded with CD siRNA (CD implants) was statistically significant compared to both implants loaded with NC siRNA (NC implants) or the plain implants in the first, third, and fourth time points. In case of implant design 2 (Figure 9B), the % viability of the cells treated with CD siRNA loaded implants appeared to be lower than that of the cells treated with NC siRNA loaded implants at all‐time points, however the difference between both treatments was only significant at days 4–6 where the % cell viability was 29.4 ± 13.5% and 110.1 ± 22.0% in case of the CD implants and NC implants, respectively. Generally, it is obvious that the % cell viability was low in the first two days. This can be attributed to the degradation products of the implants in addition to the effect of the released complexes. Though the implants showed minimal cytotoxicity on L929 cells, we observed some cytotoxic effects on SAOS‐2 cells in this transfection experiment in the first time point. This can be justified by the differences between the used two protocols. For instance, the implants were incubated directly with the cells in the transfection experiments for 2 days in each well plate and the medium volume was 700 µL, this means that concentration of the degradation products in the media in this protocol was higher than the concentration in the other protocol (cytotoxicity study on L929) where the cells were incubated for 24 h with the implants` releasate (1 mL). We were limited to this media volume and the longer exposure time in the transfection protocol to ensure that the released amount of siRNA is concentrated enough to show significant effects. Moreover, for the transfection experiments we reduced the serum concentrations as suggested by the manufacturer. This may have engraved any toxic effects of extracted implant additives. Hence, for the transfection experiment we tested the cytotoxicity of the implants on this cell line (SAOS‐2 cells) using the same protocol and conditions that is, we tested CD siRNA while NC siRNA containing implants as well as plain implants serve as a control for this experiment to test the effects of the degradation products of the implants in the same circumstances.

Conclusion

To conclude, we aimed to fabricate a 3D printed "core and shell" implant for local delivery of siRNA. The "core and shell" strategy for implant fabrication has been recently reported by Padmakumar et al.[ 73 ] who developed an innovative nasal depot comprising a PCl shell with a PF127 core to deliver synthetic single‐stranded oligonucleotide based molecule. A conventional dip coating method has been used to fabricate the implant, in which, a glass rod has been used as a coating substrate for dipping in the polymer solution. This method utilized numbers of repetitive dipping and drying steps to obtain a shell with optimum thickness. The filling of PF127 core inside PCL shell has been done manually followed by sealing the open end of the shell by heat from one side. The implant released ≈5% FITC dextran over 1 month, this might be attributed to the rigidness of PCL shell which may not be favorable for certain applications.

Herein, we present the 3D printing technique as a simple automated fabrication strategy to deliver implantable siRNA based therapeutics. Interestingly, tailoring the release profile can be achieved simply through controlling the computer aided designs. Thanks to the 3D printing, diverse shapes, and dimensions can be tailored according to each patient's need. Moreover, we propose a composite hydrogel as a shell material since hydrogel‐based implants are more advantageous than rigid polymer‐based implants for soft tissue applications. The composite hydrogel was fabricated using agarose, PF127, and gelatin in a composition of 5%, 3%, 0.5%, respectively. Interestingly, PF127 converted into the amorphous form inside the hydrogel interacting with agarose matrix. The hydrogel depicts thermoresponsive properties with good cell affinity owing to its gelatin content; thus it was utilized for 3D printing to fabricate controlled release implants for a local personalized siRNA therapy (e.g., within a resected tumor cavity). A direct one‐step fabrication method was utilized to minimize the exposure of siRNA complexes to the heat necessary for printing agarose. This involved loading siRNA complexed with the commercially available transfection reagent lipofectamine RNAiMAX into a 3D pluronic F127 core inside the implant. This core acts as a reservoir for siRNA complexes with a stabilization function. Further, the fabricated composite agarose, PF127, and gelatin hydrogel was utilized to 3D print a shell enclosing the siRNA loaded pluronic core to impart the implant mechanical strength and to control the release of the siRNA complexes from the core. Through changing the printing patterns of the implant design, the release profiles of siRNA complexes have been successfully governed, thus, faster or sustained release profiles could be attained. Our 3D printed implant presents a promising delivery system for personalized siRNA therapy. Thanks to the water absorption capacity of the hydrogels along with their resemblance to the texture of the tissue, this implant has the potential to be used in treating diseases affecting the soft tissues when implantation of rigid implants can be harmful to adjacent tissues. A future application for this implant can be the post‐surgical implantation for the delivery of cancer cell‐killing siRNA therapeutics to brain or breast cancers. For instance, the hydrogel based implants can be a solution for the complications associated with the rigidness of the commercially available brain wafers. Further, the low degradation rate of agarose in the implant may be advantageous to fill the resected tumor cavity in the breast with a soft siRNA releasing implant. Recently, cell‐free scaffolds are gaining great interest for tissue engineering for instance to repair bone defects owing to their ease of application, low cost, and minimal complications in their clinical translation.[ 74 ] However, few studies have reported the use of 3D printed, cell‐free scaffolds that are loaded with RNA, regardless the type of RNA used.[ 75 ] Overall, our proposed 3D printed implants represent a delivery system for RNA that can be used as therapies for the treatment of various diseases or as a cell free RNA loaded system for tissue engineering purposes.

Experimental Section

Materials

Agarose, RNAase free, Gelatin from porcine skin, gel strength ≈300 g, Bloom Type A, BioReagent was purchased from Sigma Aldrich, Germany. Pluronic F127. AllStars Negative siRNA AF 488, AllStars Hs Cell Death siRNA, and AllStars Negative Control siRNA were purchased from Qiagen, USA. HEPES (4‐(2‐hydroxyethyl)−1‐piperazineethanesulfonic acid) and sodium chloride were purchased from PanReac AppliChem ITW reagents (Darmstadt, Germany). Lipofectamine RNAiMAX was purchased from Applied Biosystems by Thermo Fisher Scientific (Foster City, CA, USA). McCoy´s 5A Medium, Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium‐Low glucose, penicillin/streptomycin and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich. Calcein‐AM and ethidium homodimer were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA.

Preparation of Composite Hydrogel Ink for the Shell

Hydrogel blends had been prepared using agarose, PF127, and gelatin. PF127 was dissolved in deionized water at 4 °C followed by the addition of weighed amounts of agarose and gelatin and then the mixtures were heated in a water bath that was kept at 80 °C to dissolve the polymers with stirring at 200 rpm. The composition of the prepared hydrogels is depicted in Table 1.

Rheological Evaluation

In order to determine the temperature responsiveness, the printing conditions, and the mechanical strength of the prepared hydrogels, rheological study had been conducted using Physica MCR 301 oscillatory rheometer, Anton Paar GmbH, Graz, Austria. Temperature sweep test had been done by decreasing the temperature of the heated hydrogels from 80 to 20 °C (5 °C min−1), at a frequency of 1 Hz, and amplitude gamma = 1%. Storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were recorded. Sol–gel temperature was generated for each hydrogel as a crossover point of G′ and G″ curves. Additional temperature sweep test had been done to ensure that the hydrogels will not liquefy at body temperature, thus the test was done by increasing the temperature of the gelled samples from 20 to 60 °C (5 °C min−1) at a frequency of 1 Hz and Amplitude gamma = 1%, G′ and G″ were recorded.

Thermogravimetric Analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed by means of by thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA/DSC1 system with STARe Software 14.0, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). The hydrogel samples were dried at room temperature for 48 h and then weighed in 70 µL alumina pans. The measurements were done for Agarose, PF127, and gelatin powder besides the dry hydrogel samples under the temperature range of 25 to 800 °C with the heating rate of 10 K min−1 and nitrogen flow rate of 50 mL min−1. The method involved an initial isothermal phase at 25 °C for 5 min followed by dynamic phase where the samples were heated from 25 to 800 °C and finally kept at another isothermal phase for at 800 °C for 5 min.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)

DSC study was performed using hydrogel samples that were dried at room temperature for 48 h. Samples (5 mg) of Agarose, PF127, and gelatin powder besides the dry hydrogel samples were weighed in 40 µL Al‐crucibles and the measurements were done in a temperature range 25–250 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 under nitrogen atmosphere.

Fourier‐Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR analysis was performed utilizing Bruker Alpha II FTIR spectrometer equipped with a Platinum ATR and operated with OPUS software. Analysis was conducted for agarose, PF127, and gelatin powder besides the dry hydrogel samples in the range of 4000−400 cm−1.

Cell Adhesion

Adhesion of L929 cells on the composite hydrogels was investigated using Live/dead staining protocol with Calcein AM and Ethidium Homodimer‐1. Aliquots of 350 µL of each hydrogel composite were casted in 24 well plate and hydrated with 500 µL DMEM low glucose medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% Penicillin/streptomycin in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C; 60×103 cells were seeded on the hydrogel in each well. Medium were changed every 2 days, at the sixth day the culture medium was removed, the discs were washed with PBS three times and the staining solution was added with incubation of the cells for 45 min. Finally, the samples were washed with PBS before imaging with fluorescence microscope (Nikon DS‐Ri2, Japan) at λ ex/λ em of 488/520 nm for calcein and at λ ex/λ em of 528/617 nm for ethidium homodimer‐1 in live and dead cells, respectively.

Printability of the Composite Hydrogel Ink

Hydrogel ink with a composition of 5% agarose, 3% PF127, and 0.5% gelatin had been tested for the printability. RegenHU 3D bioprinter (3D Discovery; RegenHU, Switzerland) had been used for the extrusion printing. The external pressure was set to 3 bar and the internal pressure for the printhead to 0.1 bar. The printhead temperature was adjusted to 64 ± 1 °C. Needle 23 with the internal diameter of 0.3 mm was used. The screw feed was set to 15 with no synchronization. Different feed rate values (6, 12, 18, and 24) were tested through printing simple design, the filaments diameters were recorded using image J software and accordingly the optimum rate was determined. The layer thickness and the line space were adjusted and varied for each design during implant printing.

Preparation and Printability of siRNA Complexes Loaded Core Ink

Allstar siRNA‐AF488 was complexed with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent according to the ratio suggested by the manufacturer protocol (1 pmole siRNA: 0.3 µL lipofectamine) for 5 min at room temperature. Core ink was prepared by dissolving PF127 powder in DEPC water overnight at 4 °C, the complexes were prepared and mixed with the cold PF127 solution to obtain a final polymer concentration of 30% w/w and siRNA loading of (10 µg g−1). The ink was filled in a syringe and kept at room temperature to form a gel for printing. The printing process was performed in RNAase free environment under these conditions: external pressure of 3 bar, internal printhead pressure of 1.75 bar, the feed rate of 4, layer thickness of 0.3 mm, line space of 0.3, and needle diameter of 0.33 mm.

Design of 3D Printed Core and Shell Implant

The assembled structure was designed to be round in shape and of 16 mm diameter. In order to assess the viability of 3D printing parameters to tailor siRNA release, four designs were fabricated by varying the number of the layers or the line space in each layer. All implants had a core composed of two circular layers of PF127 (diameter of 6 mm) embedded in the center of the implants. The whole structures were printed and the weight of the implants was recorded.

Swelling Index

The printed implants were dried in an oven at 60 °C for 48 h and the weights of the dried mass (W d) were recorded. The implants were rehydrated in water or H‐N buffer, pH 7.4 (10 mm HEPES, 150 mm NaCl), maintained at 37 °C with shaking at 60 rpm, the weights of the swollen implants (W s) at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 7, and 24 h were recorded and the swelling index was calculated using this equation:[ 76 ] (1) SI=Ws−WdWd where W s is the weight of the swollen implant and W d is the initial dry weight of the implant.

Determination of % Mass Remained of the Implants after Extraction

The dried implants were incubated at 37 °C with H‐N buffer with shaking at 60 rpm, the samples were collected at predetermined time intervals of 2, 5, 10, 20, and 30 days, dried in oven at 60 °C for 48 h and the remained solid mass was recorded.

% mass remained was calculated from this equation: (2) %massremained=WtWd×100 where W d is the initial dry weight of the implant and W t is the dried weight at time t.

The Viability of PF127 Ink to Stabilize siRNA Complexes in Cell Culture Medium

Pf127 had been used as an ink to print siRNA complexes avoiding the exposure of siRNA to heat. Since PF127 is a widely used stabilizer for the formulation of nanocarriers, it was important to check the possible stabilization effect of the printing ink on the RNAiMAX complexes. siRNA loaded PF127 ink was prepared as mentioned above using cell death (CD) siRNA and non‐coding (NC) siRNA, kept for 1 h at 5 °C, weighed amounts of ink (equivalent to 200 ng siRNA) were added to 1.5 mL McCoy's 5A culture medium supplemented with 2%FBS[ 77 ] (reduced serum medium was used to eliminate any possible interaction of serum with siRNA complexes) and 1% l‐glutamine. RNAiMAX complexes were also prepared without PF127 and then all the samples were incubated for predetermined periods (0, 1, 2, 3, 15, and 30 days). At each time point, the samples were taken and used for transfecting SAOS‐2 cells. Transfection was done in 48 well plates, where 30 000 cells were seeded in each well 24 h prior to sample addition. The concentration of siRNA in each well was 10 nm and the incubation time of the cells with the sample was 24 h. After that, the medium was changed and replaced with fresh McCoy's 5A medium containing 15% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 1% glutamine‐s for further 48 h, then the cell viability was measured photometrically using Rottitest protocol according to the manufacturer protocol. The results were normalized to cells treated with the culture medium only. Additionally, to exclude any possible toxicities due to PF127 itself, the cells were incubated with complexes free ink at 25%, 50%, 100%, and 200% of the same concentration used in the transfection study for 24 h and then cell viability was estimated.

In Vitro Release Study of siRNA from the 3D Printed Implant

Three implants from each design (Designs 1, 2, and 3), loaded with AF 488‐siRNA complexes, were individually placed in a 12 well plate and incubated with 1 mL of H‐N buffer kept at 37 °C with shaking at 60 rpm, the samples of the release medium were collected every day, replaced with 1 mL fresh medium, and kept at −20 °C. The frozen samples were freeze dried, reconstituted with fresh 150 µL H‐N buffer and then the fluorescence intensity was measured using well plate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek, Bad Friedrichshall, Germany) at λ ex/λ em of 490/525 nm, respectively. The amount of the released AF 488‐siRNA was quantified against calibration curve of AF 488‐siRNA‐complexes. Release profiles were generated by plotting the cumulative % AF 488‐siRNA complexes released against time.[ 78 ] The release kinetics for siRNA was studied as a function of time through application of various release kinetic models including zero order, first order, and Higuchi model. Further, Korsmeyer–Peppas model was applied and the diffusion exponent (n) was generated to understand the release mechanism.[ 79 ] The equations used for fitting are[ 80 ] (3) Zeroorder:Mt=Mo+Kot (4) Firstorder:LogMt=LogMo−Kt/2.303 (5) Higuchi:Q=KH×t1/2 (6) Korsemeyer-Peppas:lnMtMo=nlnt+lnK where, M t is the amount of drug released at time t, K H, K, and K o are constants.

Agarose Gel Electrophoresis

In order to investigate the integrity of the released siRNA complexes the releasate of implants designs 2 and 4 from day 1 to 8 were pooled (to increase the amount of siRNA) and lyophilized. The samples were reconstituted in 100 µL H‐N buffer then 28 µL samples were added to 4 µL of the loading dye solution. The dye composed of 0.1% xylencyanol in 50% glycerol in water. After that the samples were loaded into the wells prepared in the casted 4% agarose gel (150 mL) along with (10 µL) ethidium bromide. The gel electrophoresis was conducted for 50 min runtime at 100 V (PowerPac Basic, Bio‐Rad, USA), then the gel page was visualized at 312 nm transillumination and the image of the fluorescent bands was captured with 500 ms exposure time. Bands of the releasate samples were compared to the free siRNA standards bands.

Cytotoxicity of the 3D Printed Implants

The cytotoxicity of the 3D printed implants were assessed using L929 cells. Implants were incubated with 1 mL DMEM low glucose medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Cells were seeded in 12 well plate (1.2 × 105 cells per well) for 24 h. The medium was changed and the cells were treated with the medium that was previously incubated with the implants, for further 24 h. After that, the cell culture media were removed and replaced with 500 µL fresh media in addition to 50 µL Rottitest Vital reagent in each well. The cells were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2 and then the absorbance of the samples was measured at 450 nm using plate reader (Synergy H1, Biotek, Bad Friedrichshall, Germany). The cell viability [%] was calculated according to the following equation, where the control group represents the L929 cells treated only with the medium without any implants: (7) %Cellviability=OD450nmofthesampleOD450nmofthecontrol×100

Functionality of the Released siRNA Complexes from the 3D Printed Implant

The viability of SAOS‐2 cells after incubation with cell killing siRNA loaded implants (Designs 2 and 4) was used as an indicative measure of the functionality of the released complexes. Cells (3 × 104) were seeded in 12 well plates in which 200 µL of 6% agarose solution were casted on the walls of the wells to form rings to prevent the physical contact between the cells and the implants as explained in Figure 8. Implants of design 2, either loaded with CD siRNA complexes or NC siRNA complexes, were printed and incubated directly with the cells that were seeded in the first well plate 24 h prior to the incubation. The implants in each well were treated with 700 µL of McCoy's 5A medium supplemented with 2% FBS and 1% l‐Glutamine; and then the well plate were incubated for 2 days. After that, the implants were consecutively transferred to a second well plate and the procedures were repeated to 8 days of cumulative incubation (4 well plates, 2 days incubation with the implants in each well plate). After removal of the implants from each well plate, the medium was changed and replaced with 700 µL of McCoy's 5A medium supplemented with 15% FBS, 1% Pencillin/Streptomycin, 1% l‐Glutamine, plates were incubated for further 2 days and the % cell viability were determined using Rotti test reagent. Samples were normalized to the control untreated cells. The same procedures were done for testing implant design 4, except that 6 × 104 cells were seeded in each well.

Statistics
