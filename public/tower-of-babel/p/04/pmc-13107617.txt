Limosilactobacillus reuteri enhances production performance and ovarian function via modulation of the gut-ovary axis in laying hens
Authors: Yanchao Geng, Hui Wang, Fenglong Cai, Liang Hong, Lei Pu, Shunyi Qin, Hua Yang, Jianbin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDeclines in nutrient absorption and reproductive function following peak laying significantly compromise egg production and quality in hens. Bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed is a promising intervention to improve gut health and production metrics. However, its mechanism of action remains unclear.ResultsBacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed supplementation in laying hens significantly enhanced egg production, egg weight, and feed intake while reducing the feed conversion ratio. Egg quality improved markedly, denoted by an increase in albumen height, the Haugh unit, and eggshell strength and thickness. The apparent digestibility of calcium in laying hens increased. Although the tibial growth indices remained unchanged, bone microarchitecture improved, evidenced by increased trabecular mineralisation and a reduction in the number of osteoclasts. Intestinal integrity was strengthened based on an elevated villus/crypt ratio, upregulation of barrier genes (ZO-1, Claudin-1, Occludin), increased IL-10 expression, and decreased IL-6 and TNF-α expression. Bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed reshaped the caecal microbiota, enriching Limosilactobacillus. This genus was also enriched in the oviduct and ovary. SourceTracker analysis revealed a caecum–oviduct–ovary microbial transmission route. In the ovary, bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed enhanced the expression of FSHR, AMH, and antioxidant genes (Nrf2, SOD1, CAT, GPX3, and HO-1); upregulated Bcl-2; and downregulated Caspase-3 and Caspase-8, supporting improved follicular health and antioxidant defences.ConclusionsThis study is the first to clarify the regulatory mechanism of bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed on laying performance of laying hens from a microbial perspective. The co-fermented feed improved the laying performance, egg quality and tibial microarchitecture of laying hens by optimizing the microbial composition of the cecum, oviduct and ovary, thus delaying ovarian aging and enhancing ovarian function. Furthermore, we found the ovarian microbial composition of laying hens was affected by the oviduct microbiota, which was in turn influenced by the intestinal and cloacal microbiota, providing experimental evidence for the gut-oviduct-ovary axis in laying hens and a new perspective for exploring the correlation between feed nutrition and reproductive performance of laying hens. Background

Layer farming is a critical component of the livestock industry; it plays a vital role in ensuring egg supply and meeting market demand [1, 2]. Each laying hen undergoes a peak laying period during which the reproductive system functions at high efficiency, while the metabolic system operates under considerable stress [3]. Following this phase, age-related changes in the digestive and reproductive systems severely constrain production performance and egg quality [3, 4]. The ovary, as the central reproductive organ of laying hens, is closely associated with laying capacity [5]. Ovarian function gradually declines with advancing age [6], leading to impaired follicular development [7], increased follicular atresia [8], and reduced ovulation rates [9], all of which markedly restrict the productive potential of laying hens. Therefore, maintaining ovarian health, delaying the decline in laying performance, and enhancing production potential have become critical objectives in poultry nutrition regulation and feed intervention research.

At the same time, the layer industry increasingly demands green, efficient, and environmentally sustainable feed additives [10]. Bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed, a novel functional feed additive produced through the co-fermentation of microorganisms and enzymes, has attracted growing attention for its potential to improve feed utilisation, animal growth performance, and gut health [11–14]. During fermentation, microbial strains and exogenous enzymes act synergistically to degrade anti-nutritional factors and to increase the bioavailability of nutrients, while simultaneously enriching beneficial metabolites. This strategy enhances feed efficiency and improves host health and overall productivity [15–18]. Moreover, multiple studies have reported that bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed significantly modulates the gut microbiota, strengthens intestinal barrier function, and improves laying performance and egg quality in hens [19–21]. However, its underlying regulatory mechanisms remain largely unknown.

Hens possess a unique reproductive anatomy, where the oviduct is relatively long and converges with the terminal gut at the cloaca [22]. This anatomical feature provides the possibility for microbial migration from the gut to the reproductive tract, so the gut microbiota may contribute to the regulation of ovarian function. Indeed, previous studies have revealed substantial overlap between the gut and oviductal microbiomes [23]. Thus, modulating the gut microbiota may represent a promising strategy to indirectly improve ovarian function.

Based on this rationale, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed on laying performance, egg quality, gut and reproductive microbiota composition, and ovarian function in laying hens, thereby exploring its potential role in mediating gut microbiota–ovary interactions. These findings will contribute to elucidating the intrinsic link between gut microbial communities and reproductive function in laying hens, and provide a theoretical basis for precision nutritional interventions as well as practical support for the development and application of functional fermented feed.

Materials and methods

Preparation of bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed

Bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed was prepared by supplementing the basal diet with 6.0% Limosilactobacillus reuteri fermentation broth, 0.03% compound enzyme preparation, 2.0% sucrose, and an equal weight of water. The mixture was homogenised, sealed in fermentation bags with air expelled, and fermented at 33.0 °C for 42 h. The L. reuteri strain used in this study was obtained from the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC No. 34177).

Animals, diets, and experimental design

A total of 224 laying hens (56 weeks of age) were randomly assigned to 4 groups, with 4 replicates per group and 14 hens per replicate. The trial included a 1-week adaptation period followed by an 8-week feeding period. The groups were as follows:Control (C) group fed the basal diet;Low-dose (L) group fed 80% basal diet + 20% bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed;Medium-dose (M) group fed 70% basal diet + 30% bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed;High-dose (H) group fed 60% basal diet + 40% bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed.

Feeding and management

The experiment was conducted at the experimental farm of Tianjin Agricultural University. Hens were housed in a four-tier battery cage system (2 hens/cage) with a uniform distribution across replicates. They were fed once daily at 08:00, with 110 g feed per hen per day, and had ad libitum access to water. A 16-h light schedule (natural light supplemented with artificial lighting) was maintained. The health status, environmental temperature, and humidity were monitored daily, and housing hygiene was maintained.

Sample collection

Fresh eggs were randomly collected from each replicate at weeks 2, 4, 6, and 8 for egg quality assessment. Fresh faeces were collected daily for 7 consecutive days during week 7. Faeces samples were dried at 65 °C to a constant weight, ground, and stored for later analysis.

At the end of the trial, hens were fasted for 12 h, and one bird per replicate (close to the average body weight) was euthanised by cervical dislocation. The jejunum and ovary were collected; one portion was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological analysis, and two portions were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for gene expression analysis. The contents of the caecum were collected, and sterile swabs (MEIDIKE GENE, Shenzhen, China) were employed to collect samples from the cloaca, vagina, uterine, magnum, and ovary. Subsequently, the swabs were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at –80 °C for 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene sequencing.

Both tibiae were collected. For the left tibia, the proximal third was cut off, and the attached muscles and connective tissue were removed. It was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for future tibial tissue morphological analysis. For the right tibia, the attached muscles and connective tissue were removed. Then, it was used to assess tibial growth parameters and tibial calcification-related indicators.

Measurements and analytical methods

Production performance

Daily feed intake, refusals, and the number of eggs were recorded per replicate. Weekly feed consumption, the laying rate, average egg weight, average daily feed intake, and the feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated.

Egg quality

Fresh eggs were collected daily to analyse egg quality. The weight of a single egg was measured with an electronic balance with precision of 0.01 g. Yolk colour was evaluated using the Roche yolk colour fan (FHK Co., Ltd., Japan). The length (L) and width (W) of the egg were measured with an egg shape index tester (FHK Co., Ltd., Japan), and the egg shape index was calculated as L/W. Eggshell strength was measured using an eggshell strength tester (FHK Co., Ltd., Japan) by applying vertical pressure to the blunt end of the egg, and the maximum force of rupture was recorded (N). Eggshell thickness was measured at the blunt end, the equatorial region, and the sharp end by using an eggshell thickness gauge (FHK Co., Ltd., Japan) after removal of the shell membrane (precision of 0.01 mm). Then, the average value was calculated. Albumen height was measured with an albumen height tester at three equidistant points in the thick albumen around the yolk, and the average value was calculated (mm). Apparent digestibility was determined based on the acid-insoluble ash method according to the China National Standard (GB/T 23742–2009) [24]. Tibial quality assessment

Tibial quality was evaluated based on tibial growth indices and tibial mineralisation indices. Tibial growth indices were assessed based on morphological parameters and fat-free dry weight.

Tibial morphological parameters

The tibial diaphysis length was measured with vernier callipers along the long axis of the tibia, from one epiphyseal end to the other. The tibial circumference was measured at the midpoint by using a soft measuring tape. The tibial weight was determined by weighing the fresh tibia on an electronic analytical balance. Finally, after removing surface moisture with filter paper, the relative fresh weight was calculated as (fresh tibial weight/body weight) × 100%.

Fat-free tibial weight

After removing the surrounding soft tissue, tibiae were soaked in anhydrous ether to extract fat, then dried to a constant weight at 105℃ in an oven. The fat-free weight (W, g) was defined as the tibial weight after fat removal.

Tibial mineralisation was assessed based on the ash, calcium, and phosphorus contents, and bone density. The bone ash content was determined according to China National Standard GB/T 6438–2007 [25]. The calcium content was determined according to China National Standard GB/T 6436–2018 [26]. The total phosphorus content was determined according to China National Standard GB/T 6437–2018 [27]. The calcium and phosphorus contents are expressed as the percentage of fat-free tibial weight. The tibial volume (V, cm3) was determined by using the water displacement method. Then, bone density (g/cm3) was calculated as the fat-free tibial weight (W, g) divided by bone volume (V, cm3), according to Kaantee [28].

Tibial microarchitecture

Proximal tibiae were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified in 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (4–5 μm thick) were prepared. After deparaffinisation in xylene and rehydration through a graded ethanol series, sections were subjected to tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) and toluidine blue staining.

Toluidine blue–stained sections were analysed after grayscale conversion to obtain the total area (T.Ar), the mineralised bone area (B.Ar), and the bone perimeter (B.Pm). Jejunum and ovary histology

Jejunum and ovary samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and subsequently embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded tissues using a microtome (Leica RM2016, Shanghai, China) were sectioned at 4–5 μm and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (HE) and then used to examine the histological morphology. For jejunal samples, at least eight intact villi were randomly selected, and villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were measured. The villus height-to-crypt depth (VH/CD) ratio was subsequently calculated.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Intestinal, ovarian, and uterine tissues stored at − 80 °C were homogenised, and total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA concentration (ng/μL) and purity (the ratio optical density at 260 nm to the optical density at 280 nm) were determined. Then, all RNA samples were diluted to the same concentration and reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) by using the Evo M-MLV RT Kit (Accurate Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hunan, China).

RT-PCR was performed using the FQD-96A Fluorescent Quantitative PCR Instrument (Hangzhou BoRi Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) and a commercial real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR kit (Shanghai Sanang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The amplification program was set according to the kit protocol. The primers were designed using the NCBI online database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/); the sequences are listed in Table S1. Relative messenger RNA (mRNA) expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. β-actin was used as the internal control for all target genes.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

DNA was isolated from the colon content samples by using the OMEGA Soil DNA Kit (M5635-02; Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified with the primers 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′), along with a barcode. The presence of unique amplicons was confirmed by detecting a 480-base pair (bp) band by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. DNA libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The library quality was assessed with an Agilent High Sensitivity DNA Kit (Agilent Technologies Inc, Santa Clara, California, USA). Sample libraries were pooled at an equal molar concentration and sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina), generating 250-bp paired-end reads. Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology (QIIME-2, version 2020.6) software was used for data analysis. Sequencing reads were trimmed and denoised using DADA2. Using SILVA as the reference database, taxonomic annotation of feature sequences was performed by combining a naive Bayes classifier with alignment methods. Beta diversity analysis, using UniFrac distance metrics, was performed to investigate changes in the microbial community structure between samples, and the results were visualized through principal coordinates analysis and non-metric multidimensional scaling. The significance of differences in the microbial community structure between groups was evaluated using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (adonis function). The linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) method was used to detect differentially abundant taxa between groups.

Statistical analysis

SPSS Statistics 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis, and graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analysed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s multiple range test for post hoc comparisons. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

Bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed improved laying performance in hens

During the early phase of the trial (57–58 weeks), supplementation with bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed did not significantly affect the laying rate, egg weight, or the FCR compared with the C group (P > 0.05, Table 2). However, in the later phase (61–64 weeks), hens receiving the bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed exhibited a significantly higher laying rate, egg weight, and average daily feed intake, alongside a significantly reduced FCR (P < 0.05, Table 2). These findings indicate that bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed can enhance laying performance, although a certain adaptation period is required before its beneficial effects become evident. Bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed improved egg quality

Bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed had no significant effect on the egg shape index or yolk colour (P > 0.05, Table 3). However, it significantly enhanced eggshell strength and thickness at 60, 62, and 64 weeks of age. At 62 and 64 weeks, hens fed the bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed also exhibited significantly higher albumen height and HU compared with the C group (P < 0.05, Table 3). Notably, the M and H groups demonstrated markedly greater eggshell strength at 60, 62, and 64 weeks compared with the C and L groups. Similarly, at 62 and 64 weeks, eggs from the M and H groups showed a significantly greater shell thickness, albumen height, and HU than those from the C and L groups (P < 0.05, Table 3). Regarding reproductive function–related genes (Fig. 4B–E), hens fed bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed had significantly higher ovarian AMH expression compared with the C group, with the M group showing the highest expression among all the groups (P < 0.05). Ovarian FSHR expression was significantly higher in the M group compared with the C group (P < 0.05). LHR and ESR1 expression did not differ among the groups (P > 0.05).

For the antioxidant capacity–related genes (Fig. 4F–J), CAT, GPX3, and HO-1 expression was significantly higher in the bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed groups compared with the C group (P < 0.05). Specifically, the M group exhibited significantly higher CAT and Nrf2 expression compared with the other groups (P < 0.05). SOD1 expression was significantly higher in the M and H groups compared with the C and L groups (P < 0.05), while HO-1 expression did not differ among the L, M, and H groups (P > 0.05).

For apoptosis-related genes (Fig. 4K–M), expression of the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2 was significantly upregulated in the bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed groups compared with the C group (P < 0.05). In contrast, expression of the pro-apoptotic genes Caspase-3 and Caspase-8 was significantly lower in the bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed groups (P < 0.05), with no significant difference in Caspase-8 expression between the M and H groups (P > 0.05).

For genes related to immune function (Fig. 4N–Q), expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was significantly upregulated in the bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed groups, with the M group showing the highest level (P < 0.05). Conversely, expression of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α was significantly higher in the C group compared with the bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed groups (P < 0.05). There were no significant differences in TNF-α expression among the bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed groups or in IFN-γ expression across all groups (P > 0.05).

Discussion

After the peak laying period, laying hens experience a decline in physiological functions, and their ability to absorb and utilise nutrients gradually decreases [29]. This change directly leads to a sharp decrease in feed utilisation efficiency in laying hens, with many nutrients failing to be fully digested and absorbed, resulting in resource waste. Insufficient nutrient absorption and utilisation further lead to a decline in production performance, manifested by a reduction in the egg production rate and egg quality. Because production performance is one of the core indicators of economic efficiency in layer farming, it is particularly critical to explore effective strategies to improve the performance of laying hens after the peak laying period. Bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feeds is a novel type of bio-feed that has been widely applied in laying hen farming in recent years [30]. Multiple studies have shown that fermented feed can improve the production performance of laying hens [31–33]. In the early stages of this experiment, fermented feed had little effect on production performance; however, in the later stages, it significantly improved the egg production rate, egg weight, and average daily feed intake, while reducing the feed-to-egg ratio. These results suggest that fermented feed requires a certain period of time to regulate the physiological state of laying hens before exerting its beneficial effects. Taken together, throughout the entire experimental cycle, fermented feed significantly enhanced the production performance of laying hens, a finding that is consistent with previous studies.

Egg quality is also one of the core indicators for evaluating production efficiency in laying hens. The significant decline in egg quality after the peak laying period has become an important bottleneck restricting the sustainable development of the poultry industry. The HU is an important index used to evaluate egg freshness and albumen quality [34]. A higher HU indicates thicker albumen and fresher eggs. The albumen of eggs is produced in the magnum and secreted by glandular cells and goblet cells. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) produced by fermented feed regulate the proliferation and differentiation of oviduct epithelial cells, enhance the secretory function of the oviduct, and promote the synthesis and secretion of albumen proteins [84]. After fermentation, the feed can degrade indigestible macromolecular proteins in the feed into easily digestible and absorbable small molecules such as small peptides and free amino acids, significantly improving the absorption and utilization rate of nutrients by laying hens, providing sufficient and easily available raw materials for albumen protein synthesis, and thereby directly promoting the increase of Haugh unit (HU). Furthermore, in our study, the bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed improved the microbial flora in the magnum of laying hens. The laying hens fed with bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed had a higher relative abundance of Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, Limosilactobacillus, and Lactobacillus in the magnum. Oxidative stress in laying hens is one of the causes of oxidative denaturation of albumen proteins and a decrease in HU. Beneficial microorganisms can reduce oxidative stress in the magnum, avoid oxidative denaturation of albumen proteins, maintain the viscosity and structural stability of albumen, and thus increase HU. Reports have shown that dietary supplementation with fermented feed can increase albumen height and the HU in eggs [35]. Yang et al. [36] also reported similar results: feeding laying hens fermented corn by-products improved yolk colour, albumen height, and the HU. In the present study, both the M and H groups showed an increase in albumen height and the HU, consistent with previous findings. Additionally, fermented feed improved eggshell thickness and strength, and increased calcium digestibility in laying hens, suggesting that enhanced calcium absorption and utilisation contribute to improved eggshell quality. The digestibility of crude protein and crude fibre was also significantly improved in laying hens. This may be due to the reduced crude fibre content in feed after fermentation, as fibre is broken down, making feed easier to digest.

In this study, calcium digestibility was improved in laying hens fed with fermented feed. To further investigate the effects of enhanced calcium absorption and utilisation on tibial quality, we assessed the growth indicators, mineralisation, and microstructure of the tibia. During eggshell formation, large numbers of active osteoclasts are rapidly recruited to mobilise and absorb calcium from medullary bone to meet the calcium requirement for shell formation [37]. However, with the extension of the laying cycle, bone formation cannot keep pace with the demand, and osteoblast function becomes insufficient, leading to inadequate new bone generation. Meanwhile, excessive osteoclast activity results in excessive bone resorption, continuously reducing bone mass [38, 39]. Over time, bone quality in laying hens gradually declines, potentially leading to osteoporosis and other skeletal problems, which not only affect movement and health, but also indirectly impair egg production and egg quality. In this study, fermented feed had no significant effects on tibial mineralisation or growth indicators, but it increased tibial density. At the microstructural level, fermented feed increased B.Ar, Tb.Ar, and Tb.N, while reducing the number of osteoclasts, thereby improving tibial microstructure. This suggests that fermented feed may enhance tibial microstructure by improving calcium utilisation in laying hens; however, the specific mechanisms require further investigation.

The health of the intestinal tract provides the physiological basis for efficient nutrient utilisation, and its functional state is positively correlated with digestive efficiency, immune defence, and growth performance [40, 41]. The intestinal microbiota, as the core component of the host’s micro-ecosystem, builds a biological barrier against pathogen invasion through competitive exclusion and interacts with the host immune system to form a dynamically balanced mucosal immune barrier [42]. The intestinal epithelium constitutes the largest mucosal surface in the body, consisting of a single layer of cells, including crypts and villi [43]. It plays a vital role in protecting the host from pathogenic microorganisms, viruses, and harmful substances [44]. Villi and crypts are structural foundations for nutrient absorption and are closely related to intestinal mucosal barrier function [45]. The VH/CD ratio is positively correlated with nutrient absorption capacity in animals [46]. The small intestine is the major digestive organ in animals. Numerous studies have shown that fermented feed can increase villus height in chickens [47–50]. In the present study, jejunal villus height and the VH/CD ratio were significantly higher in the fermented feed groups compared with the C group, consistent with previous reports. Furthermore, fermentation produces short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which promote villus elongation and improve intestinal morphology [51, 52]. These results suggest that fermented feed promotes intestinal development by improving jejunal absorptive function.

The basis of the intestinal barrier structure is the integrity of intestinal epithelial cells and the tight junctions between them. Tight junctions are located near the apical side of the lateral cell membrane. Occludin and the claudin family are among the most important components regulating intestinal epithelial barrier function, as they link adjacent intestinal epithelial cells through extracellular loops [53]. Studies have shown that feeding broilers fermented sorghum increased intestinal Occludin expression [54]. In addition, adding fermented feed to broiler diets increased intestinal IL-10 mRNA abundance, while reducing IL-1β, TLR4, and IL-6 mRNA abundance [55]. IL-10 is generally recognized as an anti-inflammatory cytokine, and IL-10 production, together with regulatory T cell activity, participates in immune tolerance to the intestinal microbiota [56, 57]. In the intestine, fermented feed increased the mRNA abundance of ZO-1, Claudin-1, Occludin, and IL-10, and reduced the abundance of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α. These findings indicate that fermented feed enhances intestinal barrier function, improves intestinal immunity, and promotes gut health.

The intestinal microbiota plays a crucial role in maintaining host health and has become a research hotspot in recent years [58]. As a symbiotic microbial community, the intestinal microbiota contributes to food digestion, immune homeostasis, and pathogen resistance [59]. Its composition is regulated by multiple factors, including host age, diet, and environment, with diet being the primary factor shaping gut microbial composition and metabolism [60]. Previous studies have shown that fermented feed optimises the gut microbiota structure, suppresses excessive pathogen-induced inflammation, and maintains intestinal micro-ecological balance [61]. In this study, Bacteroidota and Firmicutes were the dominant phyla in the caecal microbiota of laying hens. These phyla are also the main gut phyla in birds and mammals [62], indicating that fermented feed did not alter the dominant phyla in the caecum. At the genus level, Bacteroides, Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, and Phascolarctobacterium were dominant in the caecum. However, Limosilactobacillus relative abundance was increased in the caecal microbiota of the M and H groups, possibly because the fermented feed contained Limosilactobacillus, which colonised the gut after ingestion. In contrast, Megamonas was more abundant in the C group. In August 2022, Lactobacillus reuteri was reclassified as Limosilactobacillus reuteri. Previous studies have demonstrated that L. reuteri plays an important role in regulating gut metabolism and maintaining homeostasis [63]. Phascolarctobacterium is classified as a beneficial genus that produces SCFAs such as acetate and propionate [64]. In contrast, Megamonas belongs to Firmicutes, and its increased relative abundance has been associated with metabolic disorders and inflammatory states [65, 66]. In this study, the fermentation strains included Limosilactobacillus, which colonised the gut after daily ingestion of fermented feed and exerted beneficial effects. Overall, our findings suggest that fermented feed improves gut microecology by suppressing pathogenic microbes and promoting beneficial microbial growth.

The ovary is a key organ that influences the performance of laying hens. Follicular atresia, apoptosis, and hormone secretion are the main factors affecting ovarian function [67]. Follicular development in poultry is regulated by multiple factors, including follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and its receptor (FSHR) [68]. FSHR is a G protein-coupled receptor that plays a critical role in reproductive endocrinology [69]. Studies have shown that increasing FSHR expression enhances follicular responsiveness to FSH and luteinising hormone (LH), thereby promoting follicular maturation [70]. In this study, fermented feed upregulated FSHR mRNA abundance, indicating that it promotes follicle maturation. Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) is produced by granulosa cells of growing follicles and serves as a marker of ovarian reserve [71]. Ovarian reserve, determined by the quality and quantity of primordial follicles, declines with age [72]. We found that fermented feed increased ovarian AMH mRNA abundance, suggesting improved follicle quality. Combined with the ovarian morphological findings, fermented feed improves the quality and quantity of dominant follicles and promotes follicle maturation in laying hens.

Follicular atresia and apoptosis are closely related to granulosa cell apoptosis [73]. Pro-apoptotic genes such as Caspase-3 and Caspase-8 primarily mediate cell death signals [74]. In this study, the mRNA abundance of the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2 was significantly increased, suggesting that fermented feed suppresses apoptosis and increases the number of normally developing follicles. This is consistent with the ovarian histological observations.

During growth and after peak laying, reactive oxygen species accumulate in the ovary, elevating oxidative stress, disrupting homeostasis, damaging cells and tissues, and ultimately leading to ovarian ageing and reproductive decline. Animals rely on antioxidant enzyme defence systems to resist oxidative stress [75]. In addition to antioxidant enzymes, stress response proteins such as HO-1 play key roles in the oxidative stress response, with HO-1 serving as a rate-limiting enzyme in haem degradation. Nrf2 is part of an important signalling pathway that downregulates oxidative stress by activating downstream HO-1 expression to exert antioxidant effects. In this study, fermented feed increased ovarian Nrf2, SOD1, CAT, GPX3, and HO-1 mRNA abundance, suggesting that it enhances ovarian antioxidant capacity by activating the Nrf2–HO-1 signalling pathway, alleviates oxidative stress, and delays ovarian functional decline.

Ovarian mRNA abundance of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was elevated, while the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α were reduced, indicating that fermented feed enhances ovarian immune capacity and reduces inflammation-induced ovarian damage. In summary, fermented feed improves ovarian function by enhancing the antioxidant and immune capacity, delaying ovarian ageing, suppressing apoptosis, reducing follicular atresia, and upregulating FSHR and AMH expression, thereby improving dominant follicle quality and quantity and promoting follicular maturation, which ultimately enhances laying performance.

Eggshells are synthesised in the uterus of laying hens. In this study, fermented feed increased eggshell thickness and strength. Furthermore, mRNA abundance of the calcium-related genes Ca2+-ATPase and CaBP-d28k was significantly increased in the M and H groups. This suggests that fermented feed enhances calcium utilisation by upregulating Ca2+-ATPase and CaBP-d28k expression. Combined with results showing significantly improved apparent digestibility of calcium and phosphorus, we infer that fermented feed has a remarkable impact on calcium and phosphorus metabolism, though the precise mechanisms require further investigation.

The M group exhibited superior intestinal and ovarian function compared with the other groups. To investigate whether the caecal microbiota could influence ovarian microbiota via the reproductive tract, thereby regulating ovarian function, we conducted microbial analysis of the intestine, oviduct, and ovary in the C and M groups. Fermented feed did not alter the cloacal microbiota composition but did modify the caecal, vaginal, uterine, magnum, and ovarian microbiota. In the reproductive tract of laying hens in Group C, the relative abundances of Corynebacterium and Brevibacillus were relatively high. Corynebacterium belongs to Actinobacteria, and Brevibacillus belongs to Firmicutes. The dominant genera in the reproductive tract of laying hens in Group M, Lactobacillus and Limosilactobacillus, belong to Firmicutes. Previous research indicated that Actinobacteria are more abundant in the uterus of aged hens, but lower in younger hens [22]. In the present study, Actinobacteria were reduced in the oviductal microbiota of the M group, suggesting that fermented feed delays oviduct ageing. Actinobacteria produce metabolites with anti-inflammatory, immunoregulatory, and antimicrobial activities [76], playing key roles in maintaining host microenvironment homeostasis [77]. Brevibacillus, which belongs to Firmicutes, is a rich source of antimicrobial peptides. The higher relative abundance of Actinomyces and Brevibacillus in the oviduct of the C group suggests an adaptive response to ageing environments, maintaining microenvironmental homeostasis. Corynebacterium, a genus of Actinobacteria, is often associated with inflammatory diseases [78, 79]. Romboutsia, an anaerobic genus widely present in poultry gut, plays important roles in maintaining gut homeostasis [80, 81]. Together, these findings suggest that fermented feed delays oviduct ageing, improves oviductal microbial composition, increases beneficial bacteria, and decreases harmful bacteria such as Actinomyces and Corynebacterium.

Studies have reported a large overlap between the gut and oviductal microbiota in poultry [23]. Given that the reproductive and digestive tracts terminate in the cloaca, this anatomical connection provides a basis for the gut microbiota to influence the reproductive microbiota, with the cloaca serving as the bridge. Our SourceTracker analysis confirmed that the oviductal microbiota is strongly influenced by gut and cloacal microbiota, while the ovarian microbiota is largely influenced by oviductal microbiota. The high relative abundance of Lactobacillus and Limosilactobacillus in the reproductive tract was due to the fermentation strains included in the diet. Daily ingestion of fermented feed enabled Limosilactobacillus to colonise the intestine, subsequently influencing the oviduct and ovary, thereby increasing its relative abundance in the reproductive tract. In other words, the gut microbiota can reach the oviduct via the cloaca.

Limosilactobacillus reuteri as a commensal gut microbe, L. reuteri exhibits strong adhesion to intestinal mucosa. During metabolism, it produces reuterin, which suppresses pathogenic bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Salmonella, maintaining microbial balance [82]. In addition, it secretes organic acids (primarily lactic acid) and functional enzymes such as lipase and bile salt hydrolase, which improve feed utilisation [83]. In this study, Limosilactobacillus relative abundance increased in the caecum, oviduct, and ovary of hens fed fermented feed, suggesting that fermented feed regulates gut, oviduct, and ovarian microbiota via Limosilactobacillus, thereby improving their function. Furthermore, cecal-derived Limosilactobacillus was detected in both the oviduct and ovary of laying hens, which provides potential experimental evidence for the migration of gut microbiota from the intestine to the ovary via the oviduct. Based on this, we speculate that the gut-oviduct-ovary axis may serve as an important pathway for microbial transmission in laying hens. In general, although this study initially explored the potential mechanisms by which bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed improved laying performance through regulation of the microbial composition of the intestine, oviduct, and ovary and enhancement of reproductive system function, it did not investigate endocrine regulatory mechanisms at different stages of follicular development (e.g., small white follicles and small yellow follicles), nor the signaling pathways involved in the interaction between follicular development and the microbial community. In future studies, integration with the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis may further clarify the mechanisms by which fermented feed acts on different follicular stages and regulates ovarian function through endocrine–microbiota crosstalk.

Conclusion

Our study was the first to elucidate, from a microbial perspective, the regulatory pathway by which bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed improved egg production performance in laying hens. First, the results showed that bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed improved production performance, enhanced egg quality, and simultaneously improved tibial microarchitecture in laying hens. Specifically, bacterial and enzymatic co-fermented feed optimized the microbial composition of the cecum, oviduct, and ovary, delayed ovarian aging, and enhanced ovarian function, thereby further improving production performance. Second, we found that ovarian microbial composition was influenced by oviduct microbial composition, while oviduct microbial composition was affected by intestinal and cloacal microbial composition. These findings provided evidence supporting the gut–oviduct–ovary axis in laying hens and offered a new perspective for exploring the relationship between feed nutrition and reproductive performance.
