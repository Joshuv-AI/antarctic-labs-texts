Myosin-5a facilitates stress granule formation by interacting with G3BP1
Authors: Rui Zhou, Jiabin Pan, Wen-Bo Zhang, Xiang-dong Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractStress granules (SGs) are non-membranous organelles composed of mRNA and proteins that assemble in the cytosol when the cell is under stress. Although the composition of mammalian SGs is both cell-type and stress-dependent, they consistently contain core components, such as Ras GTPase activating protein SH3 domain binding protein 1 (G3BP1). Upon stress, living cells rapidly assemble micrometric SGs, sometimes within a few minutes, suggesting that SG components may be actively transported by the microtubule and/or actin cytoskeleton. Indeed, SG assembly has been shown to depend on the microtubule cytoskeleton and the associated motor proteins. However, the role of the actin cytoskeleton and associated myosin motor proteins remains controversial. Here, we identified G3BP1 as a novel binding protein of unconventional myosin-5a (Myo5a). G3BP1 uses its C-terminal RNA-binding domain to interact with the middle portion of Myo5a tail domain (Myo5a-MTD). Suppressing Myo5a function in mammalian cells, either by overexpressing Myo5a-MTD, eliminating Myo5a gene expression, or treatment with myosin-5 inhibitor, inhibits the arsenite-induced formation of both small and large SGs. This is different from the effect of microtubule disruption, which abolishes the formation of large SGs but enhances the formation of small SGs under stress conditions. Introduction

Stress granules (SGs) are biomolecular condensates composed of mRNA and proteins in the cytosol when the cell is under stress [1–3]. Although the composition of SGs varies in response to different stress signals, they consistently contain several core components, including Ras GTPase-activating protein SH3 domain binding protein 1 (G3BP1) and T-cell intracellular antigen 1 (TIA1) [4–6]. As a central node of SG assembly, G3BP1 plays a critical role in triggering RNA-dependent liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) to assemble SGs [7–9]. G3BP1 and its homologous protein G3BP2 share some overlapping functions in SG assembly and appear to be functionally redundant [7, 10–12]. Both G3BP1 and G3BP2 consist of an N-terminal nuclear transport factor 2-like domain, two segments intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), and a C-terminal RNA binding domain (RBD), which consists of a typical RNA recognition motif (RRM) and an arginine-glycine-rich IDR [13].

SGs are relatively large biomolecular condensates that grow in size through both the addition of SG materials and the fusion of small SGs [1, 10, 14–16]. The timely formation of SGs in response to stress is critical for cell survival, sometimes lasting as little as 10–20 min in living cells [16]. However, diffusion and distribution of macromolecules in the cytoplasm are highly restricted, and the rapid assembly of SGs breaks the rules of diffusion in the viscous cytoplasm, suggesting that SG assembly might be facilitated by cytoskeleton-dependent active transport. Indeed, disruption of microtubule structure prevents the formation of large SGs (~ 2 μm in diameter) and generates numerous small SGs (~ 1 μm in diameter) [17], indicating that the assembly of large SGs depends on transportation along microtubules. In addition, it has been reported that the two microtubule-dependent molecular motors, dynein and kinesin, exhibit antagonistic activities in SG dynamics, with dynein mediating SG assembly and kinesin directing SG disassembly [17]. On the other hand, the role of actin cytoskeleton and the associated myosin motor proteins in SG assembly has been controversial [17–19].

Class V myosin (Myosin-5) is an actin-based motor protein responsible for the transport and localization of a number of organelles [20, 21, 21, 22]. Myosin-5 is widely distributed throughout eukaryotes, ranging from lower eukaryotes (such as fungi) to higher eukaryotes (such as mammals). Vertebrates have three myosin-5 genes that encode Myo5a, Myo5b, and Myo5c. Myo5a is one of the best characterized myosin motor proteins. Myo5a can be structurally divided into three distinct regions: the N-terminal motor domain (the head), the neck region, and the tail domain. The tail domain of Myo5a consists of a series of coiled-coils and the C-terminal globular tail domain (GTD). A portion of the coiled-coil region is encoded by alternatively spliced exons, giving rise to melanocyte-specific and brain-specific isoforms of Myo5a [23–25]. Through its tail domain, Myo5a engages with a diverse array of adaptor proteins, thereby contributing to a broad spectrum of biological functions [26].

In this work, we identified G3BP1 as a novel Myo5a-binding protein. We demonstrated a specific interaction between G3BP1-RBD and the middle portion of the Myo5a tail domain (Myo5a-MTD), and this interaction is regulated by the dephosphorylation of the N-terminal portion of G3BP1. We have shown that suppressing Myo5a function, either by overexpression of Myo5a-MTD, deletion of Myo5a gene expression, or treatment with a Myo5 inhibitor, inhibits the arsenite-induced formation of both small and large SGs in mammalian cells. This is different from the effect of microtubule disruption, which abolishes the formation of large SGs but enhances the formation of small SGs under stress conditions. Based on these findings, we propose that, under stress conditions, Myo5a facilitates the formation of SGs at an earlier stage than the microtubule-dependent process.

Results

G3BP1 is a Myo5a-binding protein

To explore the functions of Myo5a, we used a yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) system to screen for Myo5a-binding proteins from a human kidney cDNA library, using the tail domain of mouse brain-type Myo5a (BrM5a-Tail, residues 1106–1828) as the bait (Fig. 1A). The Y2H analysis revealed a strong interaction between BrM5a-Tail and the C-terminal region of human G3BP1 (residues 347–466) (Fig. S1). To validate the interaction between G3BP1 and Myo5a, we cloned the cDNAs of G3BP1 from fetal mouse brain, expressed the GST-tagged G3BP1 and its truncated fragments using the insect Sf9 cells/baculovirus system, and obtained purified proteins by affinity chromatography. GST pulldown assays showed that both GST-G3BP1 and GST-G3BP1-RBD, but not GST-G3BP1ΔRBD, interact with Flag-BrM5a-Tail (Fig. 1B). Interestingly, BrM5a-Tail bound to G3BP1-RBD more strongly than to full-length G3BP1, suggesting an inhibitory role of the N-terminal region of G3BP1.

We also used recombinant G3BP1 and its truncated fragments expressed in E. coli for GST pulldown assays, and the results were similar to those obtained with recombinant G3BP1 expressed in insect Sf9 cells (Fig. S2A and S2B). G3BP1-RBD consists of RRM and IDR3. GST pulldown assays showed that Flag-BrM5a-Tail primarily interacts with IDR3 region (Fig. S2C). G3BP1-RBD exhibited a more pronounced interaction with Flag-BrM5a-Tail than G3BP1-IDR3, suggesting that the RRM may play a supportive role. Since the recombinant G3BP1 proteins expressed in either E.coli or insect Sf9 cells displayed similar properties in interacting with BrM5a-Tail, all the subsequent experiments were performed using E. coli-expressed G3BP1 proteins.

The interaction between G3BP1-RBD and BrM5a-Tail was highly sensitive to high ionic strength (Fig. S3, left panel), suggesting that ionic interactions play a key role. In contrast, the interaction between G3BP1 and BrM5a-Tail appeared to be insensitive to changes in ionic strength (Fig. S3, right panel). We reasoned that the overall effect of high ionic strength on the interaction between G3BP1 and BrM5a-Tail represents two opposing effects: weakening the interaction between G3BP1-RBD and BrM5a-Tail and enhancing the interaction by weakening the inhibition of the RBD by the N-terminal region of G3BP1.

The conformation of G3BP1 is regulated by phosphorylation [6, 12, 27]. Phosphorylated G3BP1 adopts a compact conformation in which the N-terminal region binds to the RBD and prevents it from binding to RNA; dephosphorylation of G3BP1 weakens the interaction between the N-terminal region and the RBD, allowing the RBD to bind RNA and participate in SG formation [7, 8, 12]. To investigate whether phosphorylation influences the interaction between Myo5a and G3BP1, we created two G3BP1 variants, i.e., a phosphomimetic form (S84E/S166E) and a non-phosphorylatable form (S84A/S166A). GST pulldown assays reveal that, while the S84A/S166A mutations slightly enhanced the interaction with BrM5a-Tail, the S84E/S166E mutations markedly decreased the interaction (Fig. 1C). Because arsenite treatment causes G3BP1 dephosphorylation in cells [6], we expected that arsenite would enhance the interaction between Myo5a and G3BP1. To test this possibility, we overexpressed Flag-BrM5a-Tail in human osteosarcoma cells (U2OS cells), followed by arsenite treatment and immunoprecipitation. As expected, Western blotting showed that arsenite treatment substantially enhanced the amount of G3BP1 co-immunoprecipitated with Flag-BrM5a-Tail (Fig. 1D). G3BP1 plays a key role in driving the formation of stress granules (SGs) under stress conditions. To determine whether the arsenite-enhanced interaction between BrM5a-Tail and G3BP1 is upstream or downstream of SG formation, we treated U2OS cells with cycloheximide, which blocks SG formation[28], before performing arsenite treatment and immunoprecipitation. Cycloheximide treatment blocked the arsenite-induced SG formation, but did not affect the amount of G3BP1 co-immunoprecipitated with Myo5a-tail (Fig. S4), indicating that the arsenite-enhanced interaction between G3BP1 and BrM5a-Tail occurs upstream of SG formation.

G3BP1 binds to the middle portion of Myo5a tail

Myo5a tail comprises three sections, i.e., the proximal tail domain, the intermediate tail domain, and the C-terminal globular tail domain (GTD) (Fig. 1A). To narrow down the G3BP1-binding site in Myo5a, we produced a series of truncated constructs of BrM5a-Tail, including BrM5a-1106–1418 and Myo5a-GTD, and examined their interactions with GST-G3BP1-RBD. GST pulldown showed that G3BP1-RBD interacts with BrM5a-1106–1418, but not with Myo5a-GTD (Fig. S5A). When GST-G3BP1-RBD was incubated with a mixture of above three truncated proteins of BrM5a, both BrM5a-Tail and BrM5a-1106–1418, but not Myo5a-GTD, were pulled down with GST-G3BP1-RBD (Fig. S5B), indicating that the G3BP1-binding site is located in the intermediate tail of BrM5a.

The C-terminal portion of the intermediate tail domain of Myo5a is encoded by exon-A to -G, including three alternative spliced exon-B, -D, and -F (Fig. 1A). While exon-B is present in BrM5a (brain-type), exon-D and -F are in McM5a (melanocyte-type). GST pulldown showed that G3BP1-RBD binds to the intermediate tail domains of both BrM5a and McM5a (Fig. S5C). These results suggest that the G3BP1-binding site is not located in the regions encoded by the alternatively spliced exons. Further truncation analysis of the intermediate tail of McM5a revealed that G3BP1-RBD interacts strongly with McM5a-1106–1467 and McM5a-MTD (residues 1193–1467), but weakly with McM5a-MTD∆G (residues 1193–1436), highlighting the essential role of the exon-G-encoded region (residues 1436–1467) in interacting with G3BP1 (Fig. 2A). We measured the dissociation constant (Kd) of McM5a-MTD to G3BP1-RBD by MST and obtained a Kd of 1.43 μM (Fig. 2B). Deletion of exon-G from McM5a-MTD substantially weakens the interaction, preventing us from obtaining the Kd. The interactions between G3BP1-RBD and Myo5a are summarized in Fig. 1A.

Because G3BP1-RBD interacts with both Myo5a and RNA, it is possible that these two interactions interfere with each other. To clarify this issue, we performed the GST pulldown assay using GST-G3BP1-RBD with Flag-McM5a-MTD in the presence of RNase A or RNA. We found neither RNase A treatment nor the addition of RNA greatly affected the amount of Flag-McM5a-MTD pulled down with GST-G3BP1 (Fig. S6A). Furthermore, we assessed the effect of RNase A treatment of the lysate of U2OS cells that had been transfected with Flag-McM5a-MTD on the co-immunoprecipitation of G3BP1 with Flag-McM5a-MTD. As shown in Fig. S6B, additional of RNase A did not change the amount of G3BP1 co-immunoprecipitated with Flag-McM5a-MTD. The above results indicate that the interaction between G3BP1-RBD and McM5a-MTD is not affected by RNA. This is consistent with our finding that M5a-Tail predominantly binds to the IDR3 (Fig. S2C), which is located at the C-terminus of the major RNA-binding site RRM.

Given the similar structure and function between G3BP1 and G3BP2, we also examined the interaction between G3BP2 and Myo5a. GST pulldown assays showed that, similar to G3BP1, G3BP2 also interacts with BrM5a-Tail via the RBD (Fig. 3A). Besides Myo5a, two other class V myosins (Myo5b and Myo5c) are also expressed in mammalian cells. These three Myo5 isoforms share a similar structure, including the intermediate tail. GST pulldown showed that, in contrast to the intermediate tail of Myo5a, the intermediate tail of neither Myo5b nor Myo5c strongly interacts with G3BP1-RBD or G3BP2-RBD (Fig. 3B), indicating an isoform-specific interaction between G3BP1/2 and Myo5a.

Overexpressing Myo5a-MTD inhibits arsenite-induced SG formation

G3BP1 is essential for the formation of SGs in mammalian cells. To investigate whether the interaction between Myo5a and G3BP1 is essential for SG formation, we examined the effect of overexpressing Myo5a-MTD in U2OS cells on the arsenite-induced SG formation. RT-PCR and DNA sequencing showed that U2OS cells express melanocyte-type Myo5a, but not brain-type (Fig. S7). Therefore, we transiently overexpressed the DsRed-tagged McM5a-MTD in U2OS cells and examined the localization of two SG markers, G3BP1 and TIA1 before and after induction with arsenite. In uninduced U2OS cells, G3BP1 is localized in the cytoplasm, TIA1 is enriched in the nucleus, and the overexpressed DsRed-McM5a-MTD is distributed in the cytoplasm similarly to G3BP1. After induction with arsenite, untransfected U2OS cells form large SGs as indicated by G3BP1 (Green) and TIA1 (Magenta), whereas the DsRed-McM5a-MTD-expressed cells did not (Fig. 4A). In contrast, the U2OS cells expressing DsRed-McM5a-MTDΔG, which interacts weakly with DsRed-RBD, displayed normal arsenite-induced SGs (Fig. 4A). Plotting the cumulative area of SGs versus the size of SG clearly shows a decrease in the number and size of the arsenite-induced SGs in the DsRed-McM5a-MTD transfected U2OS cells (Fig. 4B). A similar effect of DsRed-McM5a-MTD was observed in A549 cells (Fig. S8). The above results indicate that McM5a-MTD plays a dominant negative role in the arsenite-induced SG formation. We speculated that Myo5a facilitates SG formation via interaction with G3BP1 and this interaction is disrupted by the over-expressed McM5a-MTD.

Inhibiting Myo5a motor function prevents SG formation in U2OS cells

To investigate the role of Myo5a motor activity in SG formation, we treated U2OS cells with the Myo5 inhibitor pentabromopseudilin (PBP) prior to inducing cell stress with sodium arsenite. Considering that the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of PBP on Myo5a is 1.2 μM [29], we treated U2OS cells with 1 μM or 10 μM PBP. Treatment with 1 μM or 10 μM PBP greatly reduced the arsenite-induced SG formation in U2OS cells and decreased the number of cells containing SGs (Fig. 5). In contrast, treatment with blebbistatin (Bleb), a specific inhibitor of the motor functions of class II myosins, had no discernible impact on SG formation in U2OS cells. These results suggest that the motor function of Myo5a is essential for SG assembly in U2OS cells.

It has been shown that the assembly of large SGs from small SGs depends on microtubules and the associated motor proteins [17–19, 30]. Consistent with previous reports on the essential role of microtubules in SG formation, disruption of microtubules in U2OS cells by nocodazole inhibited the arsenite-induced formation of large SGs and produced numerous small SGs (Fig. S9). Compared with nocodazole treatment alone, treatment of U2OS cells with PBP together with nocodazole caused a further decrease in the number of small SG. In other words, PBP inhibited the formation of small SGs induced by arsenite in the presence of nocodazole. We therefore speculated that Myo5a is involved in the earlier stages of SG assembly than microtubules.

Eliminating Myo5a expression inhibits SG formation in U2OS cells

To further investigate the role of Myo5a on SG formation, we generated two stable Myo5a-knockout (Myo5a−/−) U2OS cell lines using the CRISPR/Cas9-mediated editing system. We designed two guide RNAs targeting exon 12 and exon 19 of the Myo5a gene, respectively. DNA sequencing revealed the reading frame shifts at the exon 12 in Myo5a−/− #1 cells and exon 19 in Myo5a−/− #2 cells (Fig. S10). Western blotting of the cell lysate using an antibody against Myo5a confirmed the absence of Myo5a signals in the two Myo5a−/− cell lines (Fig. 6C). Under the non-induced conditions, the G3BP1 distribution of both Myo5a−/− cell lines was similar to that of wild-type cells (Fig. 6A). It appears that Myo5a−/− cells had a relatively reduced intensity of G3BP1 signals (Fig. 6A). However, Western blotting showed a similar level of G3BP1 protein in the wild-type and in the Myo5a−/− cells (Fig. 6C), indicating that Myo5a knockout did not alter the G3BP1 at protein level. Upon arsenite induction, both Myo5a−/− cell lines formed fewer and smaller SGs than the wild-type cells (Fig. 6A and 6B). These findings suggest that Myo5a facilitates SG formation.

Discussion

In this study, we identified the SG marker protein G3BP1 as a Myo5a-binding protein and demonstrated that Myo5a facilitates the formation of SGs via the interaction with G3BP1 based on the following three lines of evidence. First, overexpression of the Myo5a tail in U2OS cells and A549 cells caused a reduction in the formation of SGs, indicating a dominant inhibitory effect on endogenous Myo5a in SG assembly. Second, treatment of U2OS cells with PBP, a specific inhibitor of Myo5a motor activity, produced significantly fewer SGs than the untreated controls. Third, elimination of Myo5a expression by gene knockout in U2OS cells inhibited SG formation.

SGs are relatively large biomolecular condensates, ranging from approximately 50 nm to 3 μm, that form rapidly in the cytoplasm in response to stressors [1, 14, 15]. The assembly of SGs in the cytoplasm is likely the result of the collision of a large number of messenger ribonucleoproteins and the fusion of small SGs. The timely formation of SGs in response to stress may be critical for cell survival. It usually takes only 10–20 min for living cells to form SGs after induction by arsenite. However, such a rapid assembly of SGs breaks the rules of diffusion in the viscous cytoplasm, where the diffusion and partitioning of macromolecules are highly restricted by a network of cytoskeleton, including actin filaments. The distance between actin filaments in cells has been estimated to be 100 nm [31, 32].

At the initial stage of SG formation, due to the high concentration and small size of SG molecules, rapid assembly of mini-SGs smaller than 100 nm could be achieved solely by passive diffusion. However, the diffusion of large molecular aggregates is highly restricted in the cytosol, possibly due to the network of actin filaments [33, 34]. It has been proposed that the assembly of large SGs is facilitated by cytoskeleton-dependent active transport [18]. Indeed, disruption of microtubule structure prevents the formation of large SGs (size > 4 μm2) and generates numerous small SGs in the micron range [17–19]. It is now generally accepted that small SGs can be actively transported along microtubules to form large SGs [14]. However, even small SGs cannot be rapidly assembled by diffusion alone because particles larger than 100 nm in the cytosol are immobile due to actin network. Based on the current finding that disruption of Myo5a function inhibits the arsenite-induced SG formation, we propose that Myo5a mediates the early stage of SG condensation by transporting submicrometric SGs along actin filament via the interaction with G3BP1, thereby promoting the rapid formation of small micrometric SGs, and then small SGs are transported along microtubules to form large SGs (Fig. 7C).

The role of actin filaments in SG formation has been analyzed in previous work. Disruption of actin filaments by cytochalasin B leads to the formation of smaller and more numerous SGs [17], an effect similar to the one upon disruption of Myo5a. Puzzlingly, disruption of actin network by Latrunculin B appears to have no effect on the formation of SGs [18]. As discussed above, we expect that the actin network in the cytosol plays two opposing roles in the formation of SGs: as a barrier that limiting the passive diffusion of the SGs larger than 100 nm in diameter and as a track for myosin molecules to transport SGs. Thus, the ineffectiveness of disruption of actin filaments on the formation of SGs may represent an overall effect of removing both the barrier and the track functions of actin filaments.

Myosin-5 is widely distributed throughout eukaryotes, ranging from lower eukaryotes (such as fungi) to higher eukaryotes (such as mammals). Many characterized myosin-5 isoforms are processive motors, making them ideal for transporting cargos (e.g. vesicles, organelles, RNA). We expect that the transport of SGs by myosin-5 will ensure that SGs are assembled more efficiently and that their spatial distribution more precise, and that myosin-5 may be involved in the formation of SGs in a wide range of cell types.

Materials and methods

Materials

Restriction enzymes and modifying enzymes were purchased from New England Biolabs (Beverly, MA). Oligonucleotides were synthesized by Invitrogen Co. (Beijing, China). The GoScript Reverse Transcription System Kit was from Promega. Ni-nitrilotriacetic acid-agarose (Ni-agarose) was purchased from Qiagen (Hilden, Germany). Glutathione (GSH)-Sepharose 4 Fast Flow was from GE Healthcare. Anti-Flag M2 affinity agarose and HPR conjugated anti-Flag M2 antibody were from Sigma Co. (St. Louis, MO). FLAG peptide (DYKDDDDK) was synthesized by Augct Co. (Beijing, China). Pentabromopseudilin (PBP) was from AdipoGen Life Sciences (Switzerland). Blebbistatin (-) was from Selleck (Houston, USA). Nocodazole was from Shanghai yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. Sodium arsenite was from BeNa Culture Collection (Beijing, China). Cycloheximide (CHX) was from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Proteins

All Myo5a constructs in this study were derived from the melanocyte type Myo5a [35] or the brain type Myo5a, which was generated from the melanocyte type Myo5a by inserting exon-B and deleting exons-D and -F as described [36]. The cDNAs of the truncated tail domains of Myo5b and Myo5c were amplified by PCR with the plasmids encoding Myo5b and Myo5c as template [37, 38] and subcloned into the bacterial expression vector pET30HFa (a modified pET30a vector encoding His-tag and Flag-tag). The recombinant tail domains of myosin-5 containing an N-terminal His-tag and Flag-tag were expressed in BL21(DE3) E. coli and purified by Ni-agarose affinity chromatography using standard procedures.

The cDNAs of G3BP1 and G3BP2 were amplified by RT-PCR with the total RNA from fetal mouse brain as the template. The cDNAs encoding full-length G3BP1 and G3BP2 and truncated proteins were subcloned into the pGEX4T2 vector (Amersham Biosciences) using BamHI site and XhoI site. Point mutations were introduced using Fast Mutagenesis System (TransGen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The recombinant proteins were expressed in BL21(DE3) E.coli as GST-tagged proteins and purified by GSH-Sepharose affinity chromatography using standard procedures. The His-GST-G3BP1 and its truncated fragments were subcloned into pFastHTc (Invitrogen) from pGEX4T2 by using BamHI and XhoI sites. Recombinant baculovirus was generated using Bac-to-Bac system. The recombinant proteins expressed in Sf9 insect cells was purified by Ni-agarose.

Primary antibody

Anti-Myo5a antibody was generated by immunizing rabbits with the residues 909 to 1234 of mouse Myo5a (Myo5a-909–1234) as follows. The cDNA of Myo5a-909–1234 was amplified by PCR using mouse Myo5a cDNA as the template, cloned into pGEX4T2 vector and pET30a vector, and expressed in BL21(DE3) E.coli as GST-tagged protein (GST-Myo5a-909–1234) and His-tagged protein (His-Myo5a-909–1234), respectively. His-Myo5a-909–1234 was purified using Ni–NTA agarose chromatography. GST-Myo5a-909–1234 was bound to a GSH-Sepharose column, followed by incubation with thrombin for the cleavage of the GST tag, and the eluted Myo5a-909–1234 was used for immunizing rabbits. Anti-Myo5a antibody was affinity-purified from antisera over a column of His-Myo5a-909–1234 coupled to cyanogen bromide-activated Sepharose 4B (GE Healthcare) using standard procedures.

Other primary antibodies for immunofluorescence and Western blot are mouse anti-G3BP1 (PTM BIO, PTM-5700,; 1:200 for immunofluorescence and 1:1000 for Western blot;), and rabbit anti-TIA1 (PTM-5518, PTM BIO; 1:200 for immunofluorescence), rabbit anti-β-tubulin (PTM-6414, PTM BIO; 1:200 for immunofluorescence); Mouse anti-β-actin (CW0096M, cwbio; 1:1000 for Western blot).

Yeast two-hybrid assay

Co-culturing a human kidney cDNA library strain (Y187) with a BrM5a-Tail bait-expressing reporter strain (AH109), the human kidney cDNA fusion library was screened using the BrM5a-Tail/pGBKT7 fusion vector in a yeast-mating approach. A mixture of 100 μl of AH109 strain containing the bait and 100 μl of the human kidney cDNA library strain (Y187) was prepared. To this mixture, 400 μl of YPDA liquid medium was added to the centrifuged pellet, and yeast mating was facilitated at 30 °C with shaking for 8 h. The mating-resultant zygoate cells from mating were spread on QDO to select for putative positive two-hybrid interactions. Yeast single colonies that grew normally after 3–7 days on the medium were selected based on their size (able to grow to > 2 mm). Subsequently, the putative positive colonies were further characterized using QDO/X-α-Gal. The interaction intensity was inferred from the color intensity (shades of blue), and distinctly positive colonies were rescued through plasmid Segregation in bacteria experiments and then identified by sequencing.

GST pulldown assay

GST pulldown assays were performed as described previously [39]. For GST pulldown of GST-G3BP1 or G3BP2 with Flag-BrM5a-Tail, GSH-Sepharose beads (10 μl) were first bound with GST-G3BP1 or G3BP2 by incubating with 95 μl of 2.5 μM GST-tagged protein in Pulldown Buffer (5 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT and 1 mM EGTA) with rotation at 4 °C for 1 h. The GSH-Sepharose beads were washed 3 times with 200 μl of Washing Buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, and 1 mM EGTA, 1% NP-40) to remove the unbound GST proteins, then mixed with 95 μl of 5 μM Flag-BrM5a-Tail in Pulldown Buffer with rotation at 4 °C for 1 h. Finally, the GSH-Sepharose beads were washed 5 times with 200 μl of Washing Buffer and then eluted by Elution Buffer (10 mM GSH, 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM DTT, and 200 mM NaCl). The inputs and the eluted proteins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and visualized by Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) staining or Western blotting using the indicated antibody.

Immunoprecipitation

To detect the interaction between Myo5a and G3BP1 under sodium arsenite treatment, immunoprecipitation was performed following established protocols [40]. Briefly, U2OS cells were cultured in 6-well plates for over 24 h. The cells were then transfected with the Flag-BrM5a-Tail/pcDNA3.1 plasmid. Two days after transfection, the cells were either treated with or without 100 µg/ml cycloheximide for 30 min, followed by exposure to 500 μM sodium arsenite for 1 h or left untreated. Following this, the cells were harvested in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP40, 100 mM PMSF, 2% protease inhibitor cocktail) and combined with 10 μl of anti-FLAG M2 affinity agarose, then rotated at 4 ℃ for 2 h. Finally, the anti-FLAG M2 affinity agarose was washed 4 times with 200 μl of Washing Buffer (5 mM Tris–HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM DTT) and eluted with 0.1 mg/ml FLAG peptide in Washing Buffer. The inputs and the eluted proteins were detected by Western blot using the indicated antibody.

Microscale thermophoresis

The equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) was measured using the Monolith NT.115 from Nanotemper Technologies. The GST-G3BP1-RBD was fluorescently labeled with ATTO-488 NHS ester (ATTO-TEC GmbH) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A range of concentrations of ligands (His-tagged McM5a-MTD and McM5a-MTDΔG, ranging from 40 μM to 1.2 nM) was incubated with 20 nM of ATTO-488-labeled GST-G3BP1-RBD in 65 mM NaHCO3, 5 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 125 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.05% Tween 20. The samples were loaded into the Nano Temper glass capillaries and microthermophoresis was carried out using 20% Excitation power and 60% MST power. The Kd values were calculated based on the mass action equation using KaleidaGraph (Synergy Software, Reading, PA). Duplicate reads of triplicate experiments were performed for each interaction.

Identification of Myo5a isoforms expressed in U2OS and A549 cells by RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from U2OS and A549 cells using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher), and reverse transcription was performed according to the protocol of the FastKing cDNA first chain synthesis kit (KR116, TIANGEN). Fragment 1 was amplified using a pair primers (F1, 5′-AAGGAAGAAGAAAGGCCACAG-3′; R1, 5′-AGGGCCTCAGCCTCATTCTCA-3′) corresponding to the coding sequence upstream of exon-A and that of exon-E of Myo5a, respectively. Fragment 2 was amplified using a pair primers (F2, 5′-AGAATCCCAGCTACAGTCAC-3′; R2, 5′-TCGATGATCTGTCCTGGAGA-3′) corresponding to the coding sequence of exon-D and that downstream of exon-G of Myo5a, respectively. Fragments 1 and 2 were subjected to DNA sequencing.

Mammalian cells culture and transfection

U2OS cells and A549 cells were provided by Junjie Hu Lab (Institute of Biophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences) and Xuming Zhou Lab (Institute of Zoology, Chinese Academy of Sciences), respectively, and were cultured as described [41, 42]. Briefly, U2OS cells and A549 cells were cultured in DMEM medium (HyClone) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and penicillin/streptomycin (1% v/v) in a humidified chamber (37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator). Transfections of U2OS cells and A549 cells were performed using Hieff Trans Liposomal Universal Transfection Reagent (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The plasmids for transfection were created by inserting the cDNA of McM5a-MTD and McM5a-MTDΔG into the pDsRed-N1 vector using XhoI and HindIII sites.

Induce of SG and immunostaining of SG proteins in mammalian cells

Cells were grown in 24-well plates on the coverslips and transfected after 24 h of culturing. Two days after transfection, the cells were treated with 500 μM sodium arsenite in conditioned medium for 1 h. Subsequently, cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.4% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min, and then blocked with 1% BSA for 1 h, with all steps performed at room temperature. U2OS cells were incubated with primary antibodies in blocking buffer overnight at 4℃, then washed with PBS for 3 times and incubated with secondary antibodies (anti-mouse IgG coupled with CoraLite 488, Proteintech; anti-rabbit IgG coupled with DyLight 549, Jackson) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counter-stained with DAPI. The images were captured by Leica STELLARIS 5 fluorescence microscope.

For cycloheximide treatment, U2OS cells were incubated with 100 μg/ml cycloheximide for 30 min at 37 ℃, prior to inducing SGs by sodium arsenite. To examine the effects of myosin inhibitors on SG formation, the cells were pretreated with the indicated myosin inhibitors (PBP or Blebbistatin) in DMEM containing 10% FBS for 1 h at 37 °C, prior to inducing SGs by sodium arsenite as described above. For nocodazole treatment, U2OS cells were incubated with 6.6 μM nocodazole for 2 h at 37 ℃, prior to inducing SGs by sodium arsenite. For nocodazole and PBP treatments, U2OS cells were first exposed to 6.6 μM nocodazole for 1 h, followed by treatment with indicated myosin inhibitors (PBP or Blebbistatin) with 6.6 μM nocodazole in DMEM containing 10% FBS for 1 h at 37 °C, prior to inducing SGs by sodium arsenite.

Generation of Myo5a.−/− U2OS cells by CRISPR-Cas9

Myo5a−/− U2OS cells were generated using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated editing system. The targeting sequences for the Myo5a sgRNAs were as follows: 5′-CACCGGAGCAAGAAGAATATATGA-3′ (targeting exon 12) and 5′- CACCGGATCCAGAAGACCATCCGA-3′(targeting exon 19). The sgRNA expression plasmids were generated by inserting annealed primers into the vector HP180-CBH-Cas9-CMV-EGFP [43]. U2OS cells were transiently transfected with the sgRNA expression plasmids and cultured for 2 days. Single GFP-positive cells were sorted into 96-well plates using a fluorescence-activated cell sorting sorter (BD LSRFortessa SORP). Genomic DNA of from the Myo5a gene of Myo5a−/− cells were amplified by PCR using the following primers (5′-TGTAAATCATGCTTCTCCCGTGAGG-3′ and 5′-GGTCTAGTGTCCCTTTGGAATGAAA-3′ for exon 12; 5′-CTTCTATTTGCTCAACTT-GCATAGG-3′ and 5′-GTTATCTTTTCAGCTATGGTGACCA-3′ for exon 19) and subjected to DNA sequencing. The expression of Myo5a in U2OS cells was examined by Western blot of cell lysate using antibody against mouse Myo5a.

Quantification of the number and size of SGs

The G3BP1 immunofluorescence images were imported into image J. The threshold was adjusted to ensure that all SGs within the field of view were visible. The area of each granule was automatically detected by image J with the granule area of 0.2–25 μm2 and circularity of 0.2–1. All relevant cells were analyzed regardless of presence or absence of SGs. The granules were arranged from smallest to largest and the cumulative areas were calculated. The data were normalized to give the cumulative area of SGs per individual cell. The SG size on the X-axis represents the SGs from all analyzed cells considered individually. The curve of cumulative area of SGs versus SGs size was plotted using GraphPad Prism 9.
