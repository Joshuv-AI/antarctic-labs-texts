Ponatinib Safety Profile: An Analysis of 10 Years of Real-World Experience
Authors: Elias Jabbour, Fausto Castagnetti, Mouna Bardey-Kanaan, Romuald Soichez, Nhan Thanh Nguyen, Deborah Kelly, Federica Rizzo, Petros Patos, Valentín García-Gutiérrez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPonatinib is a third-generation BCR::ABL1 tyrosine kinase inhibitor indicated for the treatment of chronic myeloid leukemia and Philadelphia chromosome–positive acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Following its initial commercial availability in the USA on 14 December 2012, safety concerns, notably vascular occlusive events, led to the implementation of post-approval risk-management measures (RMMs).ObjectiveThis retrospective study assessed the impact of these measures on adverse event (AE) reporting rates for ponatinib’s important identified risks (IIRs).MethodsAEs related to ponatinib’s IIRs were identified from the Incyte Global Safety Database for the period 14 December 2012 to 13 December 2022, using a Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities–based search of postmarketing reports. Reporting rates were calculated as the ratio of reported AEs to estimated postmarketing exposure.ResultsDuring the first year of commercialization, the most frequently reported AEs for ponatinib pertained to the IIRs of myelosuppression, skin reactions, infections, and arterial occlusive events, with reporting rates of 0.505, 0.362, 0.330, and 0.291, respectively. Following implementation of post-approval RMMs in December 2013, reporting rates of these IIRs decreased considerably during the first 2 years of commercialization. By the end of year 10, reporting rates of these IIRs further decreased to 0.043, 0.030, 0.045, and 0.038, respectively. Notably, reporting rates for each IIR across the 10-year period were considerably lower than the reporting rates observed during the first year of ponatinib commercialization.ConclusionThis study shows declining AE reporting rates for ponatinib’s IIRs since it first became commercially available, potentially because of the implementation of RMMs and increased clinicians’ awareness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40264-026-01695-1.

Key Points

Post-authorization safety concerns with ponatinib in Philadelphia chromosome–positive acute lymphoblastic leukemia and chronic myeloid leukemia patients led to implementation of risk management measures (RMMs).A decade of real-world safety data demonstrates that RMMs led to a decrease in ponatinib’s important identified risks.

Introduction

The introduction of BCR::ABL1 tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) has revolutionized management of chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) – caused by clonal expansion of myelopoietic cells carrying the BCR::ABL1 oncogene – transforming it from a fatal disease to a manageable chronic condition for most patients [1, 2]. Similarly, Philadelphia chromosome–positive acute lymphoblastic leukemia (Ph+ ALL), a high-risk leukemia subtype, also benefits from TKI-based therapies [3].

Ponatinib is an orally active, third-generation BCR::ABL1 TKI, with potent inhibitory activity against both native and mutant forms of BCR::ABL1, including the gatekeeper mutation T315I that confers high resistance to all first- and second-generation TKIs, including imatinib, dasatinib, nilotinib, and bosutinib [4]. Following positive results in the pivotal phase 2 Ponatinib Ph+ ALL and CML Evaluation (PACE) trial (NCT01207440) [5], which demonstrated significant efficacy in heavily pretreated patients with and without T315I mutations, ponatinib received accelerated approval in the USA on 14 December 2012, and in Europe on 1 July 2013, for the treatment of adults with CML and Ph+ ALL who are resistant or intolerant to dasatinib or nilotinib and for whom subsequent treatment with imatinib is not clinically appropriate, or who had the T315I mutation [6, 7].

On 30 October 2013, approximately 10 months after the first marketing authorization of ponatinib, a safety signal was identified regarding vascular occlusive events (VOEs) associated with the drug. These events included both arterial and venous thrombotic events, such as myocardial infarction, stroke, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, retinal occlusion (both arterial and venous), and other VOEs [8, 9]. This safety signal led to the implementation of various post-approval risk-management measures (RMMs) to improve safety associated with ponatinib (Fig. 1). These measures included revised United States Prescribing Information (USPI), European Union Summary of Product Characteristics (EU SmPC), and Patient Information leaflets to include a warning regarding the risk of developing VOEs in patients treated with ponatinib and guidance to reduce the ponatinib dose for patients achieving a major cytogenetic response. At the end of 2013, a Direct Healthcare Professional Communication (DHPC) was distributed in Europe, and in early 2014, letters to healthcare professionals (HCPs) and professional societies, including a non-promotional fact sheet, were distributed in the USA as part of the Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) [9] to inform HCPs about the risks associated with ponatinib and to provide guidance to manage these risks and minimize their occurrence. Additionally, in December 2014, a specific follow-up questionnaire on VOEs was introduced to collect structured and relevant information on these events, allowing a better assessment of the reports. In June 2015, the distribution of HCP brochures was initiated to provide detailed information on the risks of ponatinib, including recommendations for monitoring and dose adjustment. During the same period in 2015, the phase 2 Optimizing Ponatinib Treatment in Chronic phase (CP)-CML (OPTIC) trial (NCT02467270) was initiated to evaluate the efficacy and safety of ponatinib using a novel, response-based, dose-reduction strategy in patients with CP-CML resistant to at least two TKIs or who harbor the T315I mutation [10]. The study aimed to optimize the efficacy and improve the safety of ponatinib administered in patients with highly resistant CP-CML or the T315I mutation. More recently, in 2022, a retrospective analysis of the arterial occlusive events (AOEs) reported in the PACE trial was conducted by an independent adjudication committee to provide a clinically meaningful characterization of ponatinib-associated AOEs, aiding safety risk assessment and enhancing the understanding of the overall safety profile of ponatinib [11].

As ponatinib has now been in clinical use for over a decade, accumulating real-world evidence provides a valuable opportunity to evaluate the long-term safety profile of the drug. This retrospective study analyzes 10 years of real-world data, evaluating the effect of post-approval RMMs on adverse event (AE) reporting rates for ponatinib’s important identified risks (IIRs) globally.

Methods

IIRs of ponatinib included VOEs, myelosuppression, skin reactions, infections, hypertension, pancreatitis, cardiac failure/left ventricular dysfunction, edema/fluid retention, hepatotoxicity, and hemorrhage (Fig. 2). The Incyte Global Safety Database includes postmarketing reports by HCPs and patients, non-interventional study reports, and non-study literature reports. The database was searched to identify AEs pertaining to ponatinib’s IIRs reported worldwide between 14 December 2012 and 13 December 2022. A Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities search strategy for each IIR was used to extract AEs from postmarketing sources. The search strategies are described in the Online Supplementary Material (OSM; see OSM Table S1). The post-marketing exposure was estimated based on the sales data and an average daily dose of ponatinib. Between 14 December 2012 and 13 December 2016 the worldwide average daily dose was calculated based on the total drug used by US patients/total days for US patients. Since 14 December 2016 a worldwide average daily dose of 30 mg was assumed since individual patient data could not be tracked in any country. The reporting rate was then determined using the ratio between the total number of AEs reported and the estimated postmarketing exposure during the same period (Fig. 2).

Results

The estimated cumulative exposure to ponatinib worldwide was 29,682 patient-years for the period 14 December 2012 to 13 December 2022. During this time, a total of 9067 AEs were reported for the ten analyzed IIRs (reporting rate, 0.305) (Table 1). Of these, 4040 (45%) events were reported from spontaneous reporting sources, and 5027 (55%) events were reported in non-interventional studies. Overall, the reporting rate of the total IIRs was the highest in the first interval—14 December 2012 to 13 December 2013 (2.369)—and subsequently declined and stabilized over time, reaching a lower rate of 0.219 in the last interval—14 December 2021 to 13 December 2022. A similar downward trend was observed across all individual IIRs (Fig. 3), suggesting a substantial and consistent improvement in the safety of ponatinib over time.

Among the ten IIRs, myelosuppression, skin reactions, infection, and VOEs (AOEs and venous thromboembolic events (VTEs)) were the four most frequently reported AEs for ponatinib based on reporting rate.

Myelosuppression

Myelosuppression consistently represented the highest number of events and reporting rates across all 13 intervals. Consistent with the overall trend observed for total IIRs, the reporting rate for myelosuppression was the highest during the initial interval (0.505) and declined to 0.254 within the first 2 years following commercialization, representing a 50% reduction. This decrease continued gradually over time, reaching 0.043 in the final interval, which corresponded to a decrease of 91% compared with the initial rate. Over the 10-year postmarketing period, 1863 myelosuppression events were reported, corresponding to a reporting rate of 0.063.

Skin Reactions

Skin reactions were the second most frequently reported IIR during the initial interval. Similar to myelosuppression, a clear and consistent decline in reporting rates was observed over time, with a notable 70% reduction within the first 2 years post approval, from 0.362 to 0.107. The reporting rate continued to decrease to 0.030 in the final interval, reflecting a 92% reduction compared with the initial rate. Over a 10-year period, there was a cumulative total of 1304 events of skin reactions, with a reporting rate of 0.044.

Infections

Infection events are likely associated with ponatinib-induced myelosuppression, which impairs immune function and increases vulnerability to infections. The reporting rate declined progressively from 0.330 in the first interval to 0.252 after 2 years of commercialization, representing a 24% reduction. A more pronounced decrease was observed in the third year, when the reporting rate dropped to 0.119 and continued to decline steadily to 0.045 in the final interval, reflecting an overall reduction of 86% compared with the initial rate. In total, 1539 infection-related events were reported over a 10-year period, corresponding to a cumulative reporting rate of 0.052.

Vascular Occlusive Events (VOEs) (Arterial Occlusive Events (AOEs) and Venous Thromboembolic Events (VTEs))

VOEs, including AOEs and VTEs, were identified early in the post-authorization period, prompting the implementation of RMMs. In the initial reporting interval, the reporting rates of AOEs and VTEs were 0.291 and 0.055, respectively. Over the following year, these rates declined steadily, followed by a marked reduction in the third year, reaching 0.136 and 0.022, respectively. By the final interval, the reporting rates had further dropped to 0.038 (AOEs) and 0.003 (VTEs), representing reductions of 87 and 95%, respectively. Over the 10-year period, a total of 1461 AOEs and 149 VTEs were reported, corresponding to cumulative reporting rates of 0.049 and 0.005, respectively.

Other Important Identified Risks (IIRs)

The remaining IIRs included hypertension, pancreatitis, cardiac failure/left ventricular dysfunction, edema/fluid retention, hepatoxicity, and hemorrhage. The reporting rates of these IIRs were 0.181, 0.168, 0.133, 0.129, 0.126, and 0.087, respectively, during the first 12 months of commercialization. In line with the overall trend of total IIRs and the four most frequently reported IIRs, the reporting rates of the remaining IIRs decreased considerably during the first 2 years of commercialization, with rates reduced to 0.091, 0.072, 0.067, 0.043, 0.054, and 0.043 for the period 14 June to 13 December 2014. By the end of the 10-year period after approval, the reporting rates had decreased to 0.009, 0.013, 0.007, 0.008, 0.015, and 0.008, respectively, corresponding to reductions of approximately 90% for all IIRs.

The implementation of various post-approval RMMs (Fig. 1) to inform, prevent, and manage the risks of ponatinib was associated with substantial decreases in reporting rates of the ten individual IIRs of ponatinib during the 10-year postmarketing period. Moreover, these reporting rates remained considerably lower over the 10-year period than those observed during the first months of commercialization before implementation of the RMMs and were comparable to the rates over the most recent 12-month reporting period, highlighting the sustained impact of the RMMs on the safety profile of ponatinib over time (Table 1).

Discussion

This retrospective study aimed to evaluate the impact of RMMs on AE reporting rates for ponatinib’s IIRs by analyzing real-world safety data collected globally over the 10-year period post approval. This analysis revealed a significant and sustained reduction in the reporting rates of all ten individual IIRs over time (Fig. 4). Initially, the reporting rates for these IIRs were the highest in the first year following commercialization, with the most frequently reported IIRs being myelosuppression, skin reactions, infections, and particularly VOEs. Following the introduction of various RMMs, including the product information update, the dissemination of DHPCs, and the distribution of educational materials to HCPs, the reporting rates of all IIRs declined markedly after 2 years of commercialization. The downward trend continued over the subsequent years to reach a reduction of approximately 90% in all IIRs after 10 years. The consistently stable and low reporting rates in recent years highlight the potential impact of the RMMs in improving clinicians’ awareness of AEs, patient monitoring, and patient care.

During the period covered by this analysis, the effectiveness of the implemented RMMs was confirmed and some were stopped. Following the analysis of the survey results to assess the effectiveness of the DHPC and the HCP brochure in Europe, it was concluded that an appropriate level of awareness among HCPs had been achieved, and the information regarding treatment standards and risk of VOEs was well understood. The distribution of the HCP brochure and VOE follow-up questionnaire was discontinued in Europe on 5 September 2019 (Fig. 4). In the USA, the assessment of the REMS demonstrated that the REMS communication plan had met its goals to ensure that the benefits of ponatinib in the treatment of adults with CML or Ph+ ALL outweighed its risks. Therefore, on 29 May 2018, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) determined that a REMS was no longer required for ponatinib (Fig. 4) [12].

Notably, eight of the ten ponatinib-associated IIRs analyzed have been adequately characterized with appropriate management recommendations provided to the European Medicines Agency, and these IIRs have been removed from the ponatinib EU Risk Management Plan (Table 1; Fig. 4) [13]. Following the positive opinion of the Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use in March 2022, ponatinib is no longer subject to additional monitoring, and the black triangle has been removed from the SmPC and package leaflet (Fig. 4) [7].

The two IIRs remaining on the ponatinib EU Risk Management Plan are serious infections and VOEs [13]. The VOEs associated with ponatinib are primarily driven by AOEs [14]. These IIRs have been well characterized, and relevant guidance has been provided within the EU SmPC and USPI since the emergence of the VOEs signal. Ponatinib should not be used in patients with a history of myocardial infarction, prior revascularization, or stroke unless the potential benefit of treatment outweighs the potential risk. Before starting treatment with ponatinib, the cardiovascular status of the patient should be assessed and then monitored, along with evidence of arterial occlusion [7]. Guidance to reduce the ponatinib dose for patients achieving a major cytogenetic response was also provided in the two labels.

Given that the occurrence of AOEs in patients treated with ponatinib had been found to be dose dependent [15] and that preliminary analyses of the PACE trial demonstrated that responses were maintained despite dose reductions as a result of AEs or after prospective dose reduction implemented in October 2013 to mitigate safety risks [16], the prospective OPTIC trial sought to evaluate a response-based ponatinib dosing strategy to optimize efficacy and improve safety of ponatinib in adults with CP-CML who were resistant to at least two TKIs or who harbored the T315I mutation [10]. Participants in the OPTIC trial were randomized to ponatinib starting doses of 45 mg, 30 mg, and 15 mg once daily. Upon achievement of ≤ 1% BCR::ABL1IS, doses were reduced to 15 mg in the 45 mg and 30 mg cohorts. The primary end point, achievement of ≤ 1% BCR::ABL1IS at 12 months, was met in 44.1% of patients in the 45 mg cohort, 29.0% in the 30 mg cohort, and 23.1% in the 15 mg cohort. The rates of grade ≥ 3 treatment-emergent AOEs were similar between the three cohorts, and the study demonstrated a manageable safety profile overall for ponatinib in patients with CP-CML. The study suggested a novel ponatinib treatment regimen (a 45 mg starting dose and reduction to 15 mg upon reaching ≤ 1% BCR::ABL1IS), which maximizes clinical response while minimizing toxicity. Consistently, the final 5-year analysis of the PACE trial in 2018 demonstrated that the prospective dose reductions of ponatinib implemented after October 2013 did not impact the durability of clinical responses [17]. The OPTIC dosing regimen was updated in the USPI in 2020 [6].

Recently, long-term results from the OPTIC trial further confirmed the clinical benefits of ponatinib in patients with CP-CML resistant to at least two TKIs or harboring the T315I mutation [18]. By 60 months, response rates of ≤ 1% BCR::ABL1IS were achieved by 60, 41, and 40% of patients in the 45 mg, 30 mg, and 15 mg cohorts, respectively. Importantly, exposure-adjusted AOE rates were similar across the three cohorts. These results were consistent with primary OPTIC analyses and reinforced the assertion that a ponatinib dosing regimen starting with a dose of 45 mg once daily and reducing to 15 mg once daily upon attainment of ≤ 1% BCR::ABL1IS offers an optimal benefit-risk ratio. In October 2025, the 5-year data from the OPTIC study were incorporated in the ponatinib USPI and SmPC [6, 7]. The current SmPC states that reducing the dose of ponatinib to 15 mg should be considered for CP-CML patients who have achieved molecular response (MR2, i.e., ≤ 1% BCR::ABL1IS), taking the following factors into account in the individual patient assessment: cardiovascular risk, side effects of ponatinib therapy, time to response, and BCR::ABL transcript levels. If dose reduction is undertaken, close monitoring of response is recommended. In patients with loss of response, the dose of ponatinib can be re-escalated to a previously tolerated dosage of 30 mg or 45 mg orally once daily. Ponatinib should be continued until loss of response at the re-escalated dose or unacceptable toxicity [7].

A retrospective analysis of 5-year AOE data from the PACE trial was conducted by an independent cardiovascular adjudication committee using a charter-defined process and standardized event definitions to better understand the clinical relevance of the ponatinib-associated AOEs [11]. Results showed lower rates of adjudicated AOEs (17%) compared with non-adjudicated rates (25%), suggesting a prior overestimation of ponatinib-related AOE risk. The most common symptomatic events adjudicated as not being AOEs were angina pectoris, chest pain, and non-cardiac chest pain. Notably, the rate of adjudicated AOEs was higher for patients with multiple baseline cardiovascular risk factors: 29% in patients with at least three risk factors, 13% in those with one or two risk factors, and 3% in patients with no risk factors. This adjudication study provided a more accurate understanding and estimation of the ponatinib-associated AOE risk. Together, the findings from the OPTIC trial [10, 18–20] and the retrospective PACE AOE analysis [11] contribute to a more comprehensive characterization of ponatinib’s safety profile, helping guide decisions when treating patients with ponatinib.

The present study provides an in-depth evaluation of AE reporting rates for ponatinib’s IIRs; however, certain limitations linked to the use of postmarketing data should be acknowledged. Firstly, postmarketing case reports are often submitted voluntarily, which can lead to biases, such as the under-reporting of AEs. This includes the Weber effect, a reporting bias first reported in 1984 associated with less frequent reporting of AEs in the later years following a drug’s regulatory approval [21, 22]. However, more recent studies have found varied reporting patterns that do not match the Weber effect [23–25]. Of particular note, a study of AE reporting patterns in oncology drugs approved by the FDA from 2004 to 2007 found no single consistent pattern and no evidence of the Weber effect [23]. Therefore, one cannot assume that AE reporting for ponatinib should follow the Weber effect.

Other factors that can contribute to reporting bias include notoriety bias [26] and reliance on estimated patient exposure. Despite these limitations, our findings are a valuable resource for clinicians, demonstrating how AE reporting of ponatinib’s IIRs has evolved over time and suggesting a positive impact of RMMs on the management of ponatinib safety risks.

Conclusions

Adverse event reporting rates for ponatinib’s IIRs have declined since it became commercially available. This may be attributable to the implementation of RMMs, which aid in informing, preventing, and managing the risks associated with ponatinib, as well as an increased knowledge of the drug among clinicians. Despite the potential limitations of this analysis, such as the under-reporting of AEs, the Weber effect, and the use of estimated patient exposure, our results show that the introduction of measures designed to minimize the risks related to ponatinib use was associated with reduced rates of AE reporting over time suggesting a positive impact of RMMs on the management of ponatinib safety risks.
