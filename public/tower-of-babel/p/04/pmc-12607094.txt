Should pregabalin be regulated under the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances (NDPS) Act of India?
Authors: Surender Grover, Jadeer K Muhammed, Atul Ambekar, Priyanka Saha
Published: 2025

Pregabalin is a gabapentinoid which was initially used as an adjuvant in antiepileptic treatment for partial seizures and later gained US FDA approval for its use in neuropathic pain in 2004.1 In India, Pregabalin is not included in Schedule H1 of the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, which require strict monitoring and record-keeping of prescriptions to prevent abuse. It is also not considered a narcotic or psychotropic medication under India's NDPS Act. However, due to rising abuse, states like Telangana and Punjab have implemented prescription-only restrictions. In 2018, the WHO's Expert Committee on Drug Dependence (ECDD) reviewed pregabalin in response to reports of its misuse around the world. The WHO ECDD reported about limited data on the extent of problems related to pregabalin abuse in general population and further recommended that “pregabalin should not be scheduled but should be kept under surveillance”.2

Thus, in absence of any international obligation to schedule pregabalin as a controlled narcotic or psychotropic, all countries, including India, have the option of notifying its regulation under their national laws. Countries that introduced tighter controls on pregabalin reported substantial declines in its dispensing.3 In this context, it is important to examine whether pregabalin is only a drug which may be occasionally misused or is acquiring the status of a recreational drug available in the illicit market. In recent times, it appears that availability of pregabalin has increased in black markets and is often used in combination with other psychoactive substances. Systematic review from published reports have raised concerns about the abuse potential of pregabalin. From India, reports from Punjab are based on the treatment-seeking population.4

A decision regarding level of regulation for pregabalin in India would encounter a well-known dilemma; more stringent regulations may curb the non-medical use, while simultaneously limiting availability for legitimate medical purpose (as has been the case with pharmaceutical opioids).5 Notifying pregabalin under the NDPS Act would restricts availability of such products. Notably, India regulates some pharmaceutical products more stringently than is recommended under the international obligations. For instance, tramadol and ketamine have not been recommended to be scheduled as narcotic or psychotropic by the WHO ECDD and yet they are notified as psychotropic substances in India. Therefore, achieving a balance between preventing misuse and ensuring availability for medical purpose would be critical but is a real tightrope walk. Meanwhile, healthcare providers must remain informed of addictive potential of pregabalin and maintain clinical vigilance.
