Metformin-induced encephalopathy in a peritoneal dialysis patient: the critical role of hemodialysis in reversing lentiform fork sign: a case report
Authors: Haoyuan Ma, Xiaoxuan Li, Xiaofang Cheng, Litao Gao, Jianxia Zhi, Lianyuan Feng, Shusheng Jiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetformin remains a first-line therapy for type 2 diabetes mellitus. However, its use in patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD), particularly those on dialysis, carries risks such as lactic acidosis and encephalopathy. Metformin-induced extrapyramidal syndrome accompanied by encephalopathy is rare.Case presentationWe report a 60-year-old man on peritoneal dialysis (PD), for glomerulonephritis who developed metformin-induced encephalopathy shortly after initiating the drug for new-onset diabetes. In addition to extrapyramidal symptoms, such as tremor in the right upper limb, the patient exhibited significant higher cortical dysfunction—manifested as markedly slowed responsiveness, lethargy, apparent apathy, psychomotor slowing, cognitive impairment, drooling, and failure to recognize familiar faces. These features reflect a severe neuropsychiatric phenotype that has rarely been emphasized in previous reports of metformin-associated encephalopathy. Brain magnetic resonance imaging showed symmetric basal ganglia hyperintensities with the "lentiform fork sign." Neurological symptoms persisted despite metformin discontinuation and only improved after transitioning to hemodialysis. A mild resting tremor remained at 24-month follow-up.DiscussionThis case illustrates metformin-induced extrapyramidal syndrome with encephalopathy in a patient with ESRD on dialysis. Proposed mechanisms include metformin-induced mitochondrial complex I inhibition, lactic acidosis exacerbating the accumulation of uremic toxins, and potential thiamine deficiency.ConclusionThis case highlights that metformin can precipitate severe encephalopathy even when initiated in patients with ESRD on PD. It is imperative to rigorously reevaluate metformin safety thresholds in ESRD patients and maintain a high vigilance for neurological symptoms in dialysis patients treated with metformin. Early recognition and prompt initiation of hemodialysis are essential for achieving optimal outcomes.

Introduction

Metformin, a cornerstone first-line therapy for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), is generally regarded as safe, yet its use carries significantly heightened risks in patients with renal impairment [1]. In individuals with end-stage renal disease (ESRD), particularly those dependent on dialysis, metformin accumulation can lead to life-threatening complications, including lactic acidosis and severe neurological sequelae. While metformin-associated lactic acidosis is well-documented, metformin-induced encephalopathy presenting with extrapyramidal syndrome in the context of ESRD and dialysis remains an exceptionally rare and poorly characterized clinical entity [2, 3]. This condition typically manifests with movement disorders and symmetric basal ganglia lesions, often visualized on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) as the distinctive "lentiform fork sign" [4].

Given these characteristic clinical and radiological findings, there is a pressing need to document additional cases of metformin-induced encephalopathy exhibiting the "lentiform fork sign." Such reports would significantly enhance our understanding of this rare phenomenon and help elucidate its underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. Previous reports have predominantly emphasized extrapyramidal symptoms such as tremor and chorea. However, it remains uncertain whether other clinical manifestations or neuroimaging abnormalities beyond the extrapyramidal system may also occur.

We describe a rare and instructive case of metformin-induced encephalopathy in a 60-year-old man on peritoneal dialysis (PD) recently diagnosed with diabetes. Following metformin initiation, the patient developed progressive neurological deterioration accompanied by characteristic bilateral basal ganglia lesions on MRI, demonstrating the 'lentiform fork sign.' Notably, in addition to classic extrapyramidal features, the patient exhibited clear signs of higher cortical dysfunction—such as slowed responsiveness, lethargy, apathy, and pronounced psychomotor slowing—distinguishing this case from previously published reports and broadening the phenotypic spectrum of metformin-related neurotoxicity.

Case presentation

A 60-year-old man presented with a two-week history of dizziness, unsteady gait, and dysarthria. His medical history included glomerulonephritis, renal hypertension, and right nephrectomy. He had been on PD for six years. One month prior to admission, he was diagnosed with T2DM at an external hospital, and metformin (500 mg twice daily) was initiated. His medications also included eprosartan/hydrochlorothiazide (162.5 mg once daily). He denied use of any other drugs or toxic substances.

At admission, vital signs were stable. Neurological examination revealed only mildly dysfluent speech. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) of the brain demonstrated bilateral basal ganglia hyperintensities. Laboratory findings included normal sodium, potassium, and chloride levels; fasting blood glucose 5.35 mmol/L (reference range: 3.9–6.1 mmol/L); renal function tests: urea 24.1 mmol/L (3.6–9.5 mmol/L), creatinine 1388 μmol/L (57–111 μmol/L), β₂-microglobulin 42.3 mg/L (1.3–3 mg/L), and myoglobin 432 ng/mL (0–90 ng/mL); glycated hemoglobin 7.4% (4.0–6.0%); arterial blood gas analysis: pH 7.315(7.35–7.45), PO₂ 93.6 mmHg(83–108 mmHg), PCO₂ 40 mmHg(35–45 mmHg), actual bicarbonate 19.8 mmol/L(22–26, mmol/L), actual base excess −5.4 mmol/L(−3–3,mmol/L), standard bicarbonate 19.9 mmol/L(22–26 mmol/L), standard base excess −5.3 mmol/L(−3-3 mmol/L), lactate 6.3 mmol/L (0.5–1.6 mmol/L); ionized calcium 1.07 mmol/L(1.15–1.29mmol/L). Complete blood count showed hemoglobin 92 g/L(130-175g/L), mean corpuscular volume 98.6 fL(82-100fL), hematocrit 27.9%(40–50%), and red blood cell count 2.83 × 1012/L (4.3–5.8 × 1012/L). On hospital day 3, the patient developed involuntary tremor of the right upper limb, required assistance for ambulation, and exhibited increased somnolence. Repeat laboratory tests showed urea 25.1 mmol/L, creatinine 1409 μmol/L, fasting glucose 6.84 mmol/L, and β₂-microglobulin 47.0 mg/L (1.3–3 mg/L). Nephrology consultation recommended discontinuation of metformin, avoidance of nephrotoxic agents, and intensification of dialysis. Metformin was promptly stopped; however, due to initial refusal of hemodialysis by the patient’s family, enhanced PD was continued. By day 5, the patient’s condition progressed with symptoms including drooling, flat affect, disorientation to time and place, markedly reduced verbal output, executive dysfunction, acalculia, and significant cognitive impairment—to the extent that he was unable to eat independently or recognize family members. Ambulatory electroencephalogram showed mild abnormalities with background slowing. Neurological examination confirmed severe higher cortical dysfunction, slowed responsiveness, persistent right upper limb tremor, muscle strength grade 4/5 in both lower limbs, and ataxia. Unfortunately, laboratory results from this time point are unavailable.

An urgent multidisciplinary consultation involving nephrology, endocrinology, radiology, and clinical pharmacology was held. Consensus was reached to discontinue metformin, initiate insulin therapy, and transition from PD to hemodialysis with hemoperfusion.

On hospital day 7 (the second day after treatment adjustment), the patient’s hypersomnia began to improve. Repeat laboratory tests showed urea 15.7 mmol/L, creatinine 1375 μmol/L, arterial pH 7.429, and lactate 4.4 mmol/L.

By the fifth day of the modified treatment regimen, the patient was alert and fully oriented, with coherent though mildly dysfluent speech. Executive and calculation abilities had recovered, and he regained independence in activities of daily living. However, involuntary tremor of the right upper limb and a festinating gait persisted. Given these parkinsonian features, Madopar (levodopa/benserazide) was initiated at a quarter tablet three times daily, resulting in improved gait stability, though the right arm tremor remained. Subsequent biochemistry revealed urea 10.60 mmol/L, creatinine 884 μmol/L, and elevated parathyroid hormone at 553 pg/mL. After exclusion of alternative etiologies—including antipsychotic medication exposure, structural brain lesions, hepatolenticular degeneration (Wilson’s disease), vitamin deficiencies, and other inherited metabolic disorders—metformin-induced uremic encephalopathy was identified as the most likely diagnosis. The observed clinical improvement following drug discontinuation and enhanced dialysis further strengthens the causality.

After 20 days of sustained hemodialysis, the patient was discharged with residual involuntary tremor in the right upper limb. Post-treatment arterial blood gas parameters normalized. Post-dialysis laboratory values demonstrated reductions in serum creatinine, parathyroid hormone, and β2-microglobulin. A follow-up brain MRI three months post-discharge (Fig. 1 f-j) showed no significant diffusion restriction on DWI. T1-weighted sequences revealed bilateral basal ganglia hypointensities, with corresponding hyperintensities on T2-weighted images. No notable abnormalities were observed on T2-FLAIR.

Discussion

Metformin-induced encephalopathy (MIE) represents a rare yet life-threatening complication of metformin therapy, particularly in patients with impaired renal function. This case report describes a 60-year-old male with ESRD on long-term PD, who developed extrapyramidal syndromes of chronic kidney disease and dialysis (EPS-CKDD) with concurrent advanced cortical dysfunction following short-term metformin administration for newly diagnosed diabetes. To our knowledge, this case is distinctive in its presentation of combined severe extrapyramidal manifestations and multifaceted higher cortical impairment (including disorientation, executive dysfunction, acalculia, cognitive decline, and prosopagnosia) in the context of EPS-CKDD, thereby expanding the clinical spectrum of MIE and highlighting critical considerations for diagnosis, pathogenesis, and management.

EPS-CKDD is an emerging entity in patients with ESRD or long-term dialysis, primarily characterized by extrapyramidal symptoms. Although bilateral basal ganglia lesions are a hallmark of this syndrome, they are relatively uncommon and often pose diagnostic challenges. In this case, the patient developed extrapyramidal symptoms along with ataxia/dysarthria two weeks after initiating metformin, in the absence of antipsychotic drug exposure, structural brain injury, normal blood glucose and liver function, and no signs of febrile seizures or similar episodes. EPS-CKDD was initially suspected. As symptoms progressively worsened, higher cortical dysfunction eventually emerged. Discontinuation of metformin and switching from PD to intensified hemodialysis (HD) led to symptomatic improvement, further supporting a causal relationship. This aligns with previous reports of reversible neurotoxicity associated with metformin use in the context of renal impairment.

Notably, this case differs from previous reports of EPS-CKDD, which have primarily emphasized extrapyramidal symptoms—such as tremor and chorea—while overlooking cortical dysfunction. The extensive cortical deficits observed in our patient suggest that MIE may present with a broader neurological phenotype than commonly recognized.

The neurotoxicity observed in this case arises from interconnected metabolic, toxic, and vascular mechanisms, exacerbated by the patient’s ESRD and PD-dependent clearance limitations. Metformin is primarily excreted via the kidneys; in ESRD, even PD fails to adequately clear the drug, leading to systemic accumulation [5]. Although metformin was discontinued early and intensified PD was initiated, the patient exhibited persistent neurological deficits. This incomplete recovery may be explained by the pharmacokinetic limitations of PD in clearing metformin. Despite its small molecular size, metformin's large volume of distribution and extensive tissue sequestration—particularly in muscle and liver—limit the efficacy of PD, which primarily clears solutes from the intravascular compartment. Comparative studies indicate that metformin clearance by PD is substantially lower than that achieved by hemodialysis. Consequently, tissue-bound metformin may continue to exert mitochondrial toxicity even after plasma levels decline. Critically, the patient’s initial lack of improvement with PD highlights a key limitation of this dialysis modality: PD is ineffective for clearing small molecules like metformin [6]. In contrast, intensive HD rapidly reversed symptoms, underscoring the need for prompt HD initiation in severe MIE cases.

A key driver of MIE is metformin’s inhibition of mitochondrial complex I, which disrupts the electron transport chain, reduces adenosine triphosphate production, and shifts metabolism toward anaerobic glycolysis—ultimately increasing lactate generation [7, 8]. This mechanism mirrors that of 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine, a toxin used to induce Parkinson’s disease models, which forms MPP + to inhibit complex I and cause dopaminergic neuronal damage [9]. Moreover, metformin competitively inhibits intestinal thiamine absorption [5]. Thiamine is an essential coenzyme for glucose metabolism; its deficiency—prevalent in ESRD and diabetic patients—further exacerbates lactic acidosis and mitochondrial impairment, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of metabolic stress [10].

The accumulation of lactate disrupts the blood–brain barrier, enabling lactate and metformin to penetrate the central nervous system [11]. In the brain, excess lactate competes with neurons for glucose uptake, impairing aerobic glycolysis—the primary energy source for cortical neurons. This energy crisis selectively damages cognition-relevant regions (prefrontal cortex, hippocampus), leading to adenosine triphosphate depletion, synaptic transmission dysfunction (e.g., glutamate/GABA imbalance), and the cortical deficits (inattention, memory impairment) observed in our patient [12]. Lactic acidosis also contributes to the characteristic MRI finding of the “lentiform fork sign” in bilateral basal ganglia. The lentiform nuclei exhibit heightened vulnerability to metabolic disturbances, and lactate-induced osmotic stress promotes vasogenic edema [13]. Astrocytes, which can utilize lactate as an alternative energy source for axons and oligodendrocytes, may exacerbate regional damage by diverting resources away from neurons—further explaining the selective basal ganglia involvement [14].

Severe uremia in our patient amplified neurotoxicity through multiple pathways. Cyanate-mediated injury involves urea crossing the blood–brain barrier and being metabolized into cyanate, which overactivates N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors. This leads to excessive calcium influx, mitochondrial dysfunction, overproduction of reactive oxygen species, and neuronal apoptosis—particularly affecting CA1 hippocampal neurons, which are highly vulnerable to calcium-mediated damage. This mechanism underlies the memory impairment observed in patients and may also explain the hypocalcemia noted in the patient. Excitotoxicity occurs as cyanate inhibits presynaptic glutamate reuptake, causing glutamate accumulation in the synaptic cleft. This disrupts prefrontal cortical circuits, worsening executive dysfunction. Cerebral hemodynamic dysfunction results from urea and guanidine-related toxins impairing cerebral endothelial function by inhibiting endothelium-dependent vasodilators (e.g., nitric oxide), reducing vasoreactivity and shifting the cerebral autoregulation curve rightward. Even in normotensive conditions, this shift causes cortical hypoperfusion and hypoxia—further damaging energy-dependent cortical neurons.

During the patient's hospitalization, neurological symptoms progressed in parallel with rising BUN and lactate levels. Symptomatic improvement occurred only after hemodialysis effectively reduced both parameters (Table 1). This temporal correlation suggests that dynamic metabolic fluctuations, rather than absolute threshold values, may better explain the clinical course of metformin-induced encephalopathy in the setting of ESRD.

Our patient’s markedly elevated parathyroid hormone levels also likely contributed to neurotoxicity. Metabolic acidosis stimulates parathyroid hormone hypersecretion by reducing renal hydrogen ion excretion and enhancing bicarbonate reabsorption. Additionally, metformin may directly affect parathyroid cells, further increasing parathyroid hormone levels—though this mechanism requires further validation.

Regarding management and preventive strategies, the safe use of metformin in renal impairment remains debated. Prevention hinges on renal assessment prior to metformin initiation. In ESRD or dialysis patients, guideline adherence is critical to prevent neurotoxicity and metabolic complications [15]. The glomerular filtration rate of our patient at the time of metformin initiation remains unknown. However, the absence of timely estimated glomerular filtration rate assessment prior to prescription underscores that metformin should not have been used—highlighting the critical importance of adherence to clinical guidelines to prevent inappropriate medication use in such cases. While some cases of MIE resolve simply with discontinuation of the drug, patients with ESRD require more aggressive management. In ESRD patients on metformin, serum and cerebrospinal fluid lactate levels should be monitored even in the absence of acidosis [12]. For patients with progressive symptoms, prompt initiation of hemodialysis (HD) is crucial, as PD does not achieve adequate clearance of the toxin. Thiamine (vitamin B1) supplementation should also be initiated to correct for deficiencies commonly seen in ESRD and diabetes, thereby addressing the associated metabolic cofactor deficit.

The prognosis of MIE in ESRD depends on timely intervention: early diagnosis and intensive HD are associated with favorable outcomes, whereas delayed treatment may result in residual neurological deficits [16]. Notably, MIE carries a better prognosis than diabetic uremic encephalopathy, emphasizing the need for prompt differentiation between these entities [8].

This case has several limitations. First, although metformin has been reported to inhibit intestinal thiamine absorption—a potential contributor to neurological dysfunction—our nutritional assessment during the patient's hospitalization was insufficient. We did not provide timely nutritional support, nor did we measure thiamine levels; therefore, we cannot definitively establish whether thiamine deficiency played a pathogenic role. Only routine oral supplementation with thiamine and mecobalamin was administered. In retrospect, earlier and more comprehensive nutritional intervention might have facilitated more rapid symptomatic recovery. Second, cerebrospinal fluid metformin levels were not measured to confirm central nervous system accumulation. Third, the potential role of metformin in directly regulating parathyroid hormone secretion remains speculative and warrants further investigation.

Conclusions

MIE is a serious complication in patients with renal impairment, as illustrated in this distinctive case presenting with combined extrapyramidal and cortical dysfunction. The underlying pathophysiology involves metformin accumulation, mitochondrial inhibition, lactic acidosis, and synergistic neurotoxicity from uremic metabolites—all exacerbated by inadequate dialysis clearance.

This report underscores the critical importance of adhering to metformin prescribing guidelines in patients with kidney disease, particularly avoiding its use when the estimated glomerular filtration rate falls below 30 mL/min/1.73m2. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion in at-risk populations to prevent inappropriate metformin use and must remain vigilant for both motor and cognitive symptoms in potentially affected individuals. Early intervention—including prompt drug withdrawal, intensified hemodialysis, and vitamin B₁ supplementation—is essential to limit neurological damage.
