Antibiotic self-medication behaviours and contributing factors among adults in the Southern West Bank, Palestine
Authors: Mohammad Qtait, Fuad Farajalla, Nesreen Alqaissi, Khalaf Awwad, Nadia Amro, Yousef Jaradat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntibiotic self-medication (ASM) is a recognized contributor to antimicrobial resistance, particularly in settings characterized by constrained healthcare access and weak regulatory enforcement.ObjectivesTo estimate the prevalence of ASM, examine antibiotic-related knowledge and behavioural patterns, and identify independent sociodemographic predictors of ASM among adults in the southern West Bank, Palestine.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted in May 2025 among 301 adults recruited from community pharmacies in Hebron and Bethlehem. Participants completed a validated structured questionnaire assessing antibiotic knowledge, access pathways, and self-medication practices. Associations were evaluated using χ2 tests, and independent predictors were identified via multivariable binary logistic regression.ResultsASM was reported by 76.4% of participants within the preceding three months. Among self-medicators, 91.7% obtained antibiotics directly from community pharmacies without prescription. The mean antibiotic knowledge score was 56.7% ± 28.6, with only 25.3% demonstrating high knowledge. Inappropriate antibiotic use patterns were common, including dose or antibiotic modification (58.3%) and symptom-based discontinuation (40.4%). Only 26.1% reported completing a full antibiotic course, while 37.4% reported adverse drug reactions. Multivariable analysis indicated that ASM was independently associated with male sex [adjusted odds ratio (AOR) = 2.1, 95% CI 1.2–3.7], age >30 years (AOR = 1.9, 95% CI 1.1–3.5), lack of health insurance (AOR = 2.6, 95% CI 1.4–4.8), and high antibiotic knowledge (AOR = 2.3, 95% CI 1.1–4.1).ConclusionsASM represents a highly prevalent mode of antibiotic use in the southern West Bank and appears to be driven primarily by structural and economic barriers rather than knowledge deficits alone. Strengthening pharmacy regulation, improving healthcare accessibility, and implementing context-sensitive antimicrobial stewardship strategies are urgently needed.

Introduction

Antibiotic self-medication (ASM), defined as the use of antibiotics without a valid medical prescription or professional supervision, is a major contributor to antimicrobial resistance (AMR) worldwide.1–3 This practice includes obtaining antibiotics directly from community pharmacies, using leftover medicines from previous illnesses, or relying on non-professional advice, and is associated with inappropriate drug selection, incorrect dosing, premature discontinuation of therapy, adverse drug reactions, and the emergence of resistant pathogens.4,5 In response, the World Health Organization has identified irrational antibiotic use as one of the most serious global public health threats and has called for strengthened antimicrobial stewardship, particularly in community settings.5–8

The burden of ASM is disproportionately high in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where weak regulatory enforcement, limited access to physicians, and heavy reliance on private pharmacies create an environment that facilitates non-prescription antibiotic use.2,6,8 A recent global meta-analysis estimated that between 38.8% and 92% of antibiotics in LMICs are obtained without prescription, with community pharmacies functioning as the primary access point.9–11 Qualitative and mixed-methods syntheses further demonstrate that ASM is driven not only by limited access to healthcare but also by cost barriers, long waiting times, self-perceived clinical competence, and culturally embedded practices of sharing medications within families and communities.8,12–14

In the Middle East, ASM remains widespread despite formal prescription-only regulations. Community-based surveys have reported ASM prevalence of 78.7% in Saudi Arabia,14 40.4% in Jordan,15 and approximately 30% in Iran.16 Across the region, antibiotics are routinely dispensed without prescription through private pharmacies and misconceptions that antibiotics can treat viral infections, shorten illness duration, or prevent complications continue to drive inappropriate use.15,17–19

Palestine represents a particularly vulnerable context for ASM. Political instability, movement restrictions, fragmented governance, and under-resourced public healthcare systems significantly constrain timely and affordable access to physicians.20,21 Consequently, community pharmacies frequently function as de facto primary care providers, enabling widespread over-the-counter antibiotic sales despite regulatory prohibitions.7,22 Previous Palestinian studies have reported ASM prevalence of approximately 50% among primary-care patients,19 65.7% among university students,20 and over 70% among parents self-medicating their children.16 However, these studies were conducted in specific sub-populations or limited geographical areas and do not capture adult antibiotic use behaviour in the southern West Bank.

Hebron and Bethlehem are socioeconomically distinct governorates characterized by high population density, substantial poverty, variable insurance coverage, and heavy reliance on private pharmacies. These districts are under-represented in Palestinian antimicrobial stewardship research, and there remains limited evidence on how sociodemographic, behavioural, and structural determinants interact to drive ASM in this region. Moreover, previous national studies have focused primarily on prevalence and knowledge, with little attention to the independent predictors of ASM in high-risk communities.16,19,20,22

International evidence indicates that ASM is driven not only by knowledge deficits but also by structural barriers, social norms, and health-system failures that undermine rational antibiotic use.8,10,12,23,24 Understanding how these factors operate in a politically constrained and resource-limited setting such as southern Palestine is therefore essential for developing effective antimicrobial stewardship strategies in fragile health systems.

Accordingly, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of antibiotic self-medication, assess knowledge and behavioural patterns, and identify socio-demographic predictors of ASM among adults in Hebron and Bethlehem in the southern West Bank. By focusing on an under-studied Palestinian region and integrating behavioural, knowledge-based, and socio-economic determinants, this study provides novel evidence to inform pharmacy regulation, community education, and national AMR control strategies in Palestine and comparable LMIC settings.

Method

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional community-based survey was conducted between 1 and 31 May 2025 in the southern West Bank, Palestine, specifically in the Hebron and Bethlehem governorates. These districts were selected because they are densely populated, socioeconomically diverse, and characterized by heavy reliance on private community pharmacies as the primary point of access to medicines, including antibiotics.19,22

Target population and eligibility criteria

The target population comprised adults aged 18 years or older residing in the southern West Bank who were directly responsible for decisions regarding antibiotic use for themselves or their household members. Only participants who confirmed that they personally decided when and how antibiotics were obtained or used were eligible. Individuals who were collecting medicines on behalf of others, those unable to understand the questionnaire, or those with evident cognitive or communication impairment were excluded to ensure behavioural validity.

Sampling strategy and recruitment

A pharmacy-based convenience sampling approach was employed. Probability sampling was not feasible because no registries of pharmacy clients exist and because mobility restrictions and security conditions limited systematic household sampling. To reduce selection bias, several safeguards were applied.

First, 12 community pharmacies were randomly selected from official Ministry of Health lists in Hebron and Bethlehem using a computer-generated sequence. Second, data collection was distributed across different days of the week, including weekends, and across different time blocks (morning, afternoon, and evening) to capture variability in client flow. Third, all adult customers exiting the pharmacy during recruitment periods were approached systematically.

Participants were approached only after completing their pharmacy visit to avoid influencing purchasing behaviour. They were asked whether they had obtained antibiotics during that visit or within the previous 3 months and whether they personally made decisions about antibiotic use. Only eligible decision-makers were invited to participate. This strategy enabled inclusion of both individuals actively purchasing antibiotics and those using previously obtained or leftover antibiotics.

Sample size

The adult population of Hebron and Bethlehem is approximately 420 000. Using Raosoft® for population-based sample size estimation with a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and 50% response distribution, the minimum required sample was 384 participants. Due to field access and security constraints, 320 individuals were recruited, of whom 301 completed the questionnaire (response rate 94.1%). This sample size remained sufficient to detect moderate associations in multivariable analysis.

Measurement instrument

Data were collected using a structured Arabic questionnaire adapted from validated instruments previously used in Palestinian and international ASM studies.7,19,20,22 The questionnaire comprised three domains: (i) sociodemographic characteristics, (ii) antibiotic knowledge (eight items), and (iii) ASM behaviours and practices.

Knowledge items were scored as correct (1) or incorrect/unknown (0), converted to percentage scores, and classified using Bloom’s cut-off points: poor (<60%), moderate (60%–79.9%), and high (≥80%).25 Internal consistency was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.74 for knowledge and 0.79 for practice).

The questionnaire was forward–backward translated following WHO procedures and pilot-tested in 25 adults from the same population; these data were excluded from the final analysis.

Data collection procedures

Trained research assistants collected data onsite in participating pharmacies. After providing written informed consent, participants completed the questionnaire anonymously under supervision to ensure comprehension. No identifying information was recorded.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 29. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sociodemographic characteristics, ASM prevalence, knowledge scores, and behavioural patterns.

Bivariate associations between ASM (yes/no) and independent variables were examined using chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent-sample t tests for continuous variables. Variables with P < 0.20 in bivariate analysis were entered into a multivariable binary logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of ASM. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Bias management

Selection bias was mitigated through multi-site pharmacy recruitment, time-based sampling, and inclusion of only decision-makers. However, pharmacy-based sampling may under-represent individuals who rely exclusively on public clinics or who do not seek pharmacy care. Recall bias was reduced by limiting ASM recall to the previous 3 months.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the College of Nursing, Palestine Polytechnic University (Approval No. EA/2025/35). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. Data were collected anonymously, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Of these, 261 (86.7%) were recruited in Hebron and 40 (13.3%) in Bethlehem. The mean age was 28.9 ± 9.7 years, and 55.5% were male. Prevalence of antibiotic self-medication

Discussion

This study provides robust evidence that antibiotic self-medication (ASM) is the dominant mode of antibiotic use among adults in the southern West Bank, with more than three-quarters of participants reporting non-prescribed antibiotic use within the preceding 3 months. This prevalence exceeds estimates reported in most Middle Eastern settings and is consistent with data from other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where pharmacies function as the primary gateway to antimicrobial access.2,9–11,14,15 The findings indicate that ASM in this setting is not an occasional behaviour but a structurally embedded practice driven by health-system constraints rather than individual negligence.

The experience of adverse drug reactions was classified within unsafe antibiotic practices because ADR occurrence in this context reflects clinically unsupervised drug exposure rather than patient-specific pharmacological vulnerability. In self-medication settings, ADRs often arise from inappropriate drug selection, incorrect dosing, drug interactions, contraindications, or unnecessary antibiotic use. Consequently, ADR reporting functions as an indirect safety indicator of irrational antibiotic use rather than a behavioural act per se, aligning with pharmacovigilance frameworks that treat preventable ADRs as medication-related safety events.14,15

Early discontinuation of antibiotic therapy requires careful interpretation. Traditionally, incomplete antibiotic courses have been framed as a major contributor to antimicrobial resistance (AMR). However, contemporary antimicrobial stewardship literature suggests that shorter antibiotic durations may be clinically appropriate and, in many conditions, preferable for minimizing adverse effects and resistance selection pressure.5,14,15 Importantly, evidence supporting shorter regimens is derived from diagnosis-specific protocols implemented under clinical supervision. In contrast, symptom-based cessation observed in this study reflects unsupervised decision-making without diagnostic certainty. Therefore, the concern is not solely shortened duration but rather the absence of clinical assessment guiding antibiotic initiation, selection, and duration.14,15

A considerable proportion of participants reported discontinuing antibiotics when symptoms resolved (40.4%), reflecting deviation from traditionally recommended treatment durations.14,26

The central role of community pharmacies in facilitating ASM is particularly evident, with more than 90% of self-medicating participants obtaining antibiotics directly from pharmacies without a prescription. Similar patterns have been documented across LMICs, where commercial pressures, limited regulatory enforcement, and the absence of viable primary care alternatives normalize non-prescription antibiotic dispensing.2,12,13,27 In Palestine, this dynamic is intensified by political instability, movement restrictions, and fragmented healthcare governance, which collectively undermine access to physicians and continuity of care.20–22 Consequently, pharmacies increasingly operate as de facto primary care providers, placing pharmacists at the frontline of antimicrobial decision-making without the institutional support required for effective stewardship.

An important and novel finding of this study is the paradoxical association between higher antibiotic knowledge and increased ASM. After adjustment, individuals with high knowledge were more than twice as likely to self-medicate. This aligns with qualitative and quantitative evidence demonstrating that greater familiarity with antibiotics can foster therapeutic overconfidence, leading individuals to self-diagnose and self-prescribe based on prior experience rather than professional guidance.8,10,28,29 In socially dense communities such as those in southern Palestine, this behaviour is further reinforced by peer advice and the cultural acceptability of sharing medications.16,23 Thus, knowledge in isolation does not function as a protective factor and may instead facilitate inappropriate autonomy over antibiotic use.

The identification of male sex, older age, and lack of health insurance as independent predictors of ASM highlights the strong influence of socioeconomic and structural barriers. Men and older adults are more likely to be economically active and time-constrained, increasing reliance on pharmacies for rapid symptom relief.2,15,30 The strong association with lack of insurance underscores the financial drivers of ASM, as uninsured individuals face higher out-of-pocket costs and longer delays when accessing formal healthcare services.19,23 These findings are consistent with international evidence showing that cost, convenience, and accessibility are more powerful determinants of ASM than knowledge or attitudes alone.8,12,27

The widespread prevalence of unsafe practices, including dose modification, early discontinuation, and use of multiple brands of the same antibiotic, demonstrates that ASM in this setting is not limited to acquisition but extends to irrational patterns of use that directly promote antimicrobial resistance.1,4,5,9 Such behaviours compromise treatment efficacy and increase the risk of adverse drug reactions, which were reported by more than one-third of self-medicators in this study.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. Pharmacy-based sampling may under-represent individuals who rely exclusively on public clinics or who do not seek pharmacy care, and the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. However, pharmacy-based recruitment is methodologically appropriate for studying ASM, as pharmacies are the principal source of non-prescription antibiotics in this context.2,13,19

From a policy perspective, these findings demonstrate that educational campaigns alone will not substantially reduce ASM. Effective antimicrobial stewardship in Palestine requires integrated strategies that address financial barriers, regulatory enforcement, and healthcare accessibility alongside public awareness.5,8,9,23 Strengthening pharmacy regulation, supporting ethical dispensing practices, expanding insurance coverage, and improving access to primary care are essential to reducing the structural incentives for self-medication.

Importantly, the relevance of these findings extends beyond Palestine. Many fragile and conflict-affected health systems face similar combinations of weak regulation, constrained access to physicians, and reliance on private pharmacies.8,23,26 By elucidating how ASM operates within such an environment, this study contributes to the global understanding of antimicrobial misuse in structurally constrained settings and informs the design of context-appropriate stewardship interventions.

Implications for public health and policy

The findings underscore an urgent need for multilevel interventions targeting individual, community, and system determinants of ASM. At the community level, public education campaigns are essential to dispel misconceptions about antibiotic use, particularly regarding viral infections, treatment duration, and leftover use. Education should go beyond knowledge transfer and incorporate behavioural change communication strategies based on social norms and risk perception models.

At the structural level, pharmacy regulation and antimicrobial stewardship must be strengthened. The near-universal sourcing of antibiotics from community pharmacies highlights a critical gap in enforcement of prescription laws. National health authorities should implement systematic pharmacy audits, penalties for noncompliance, and professional development programs for pharmacists emphasizing ethical dispensing and AMR awareness.

Improving healthcare accessibility is equally vital. Expanding insurance coverage, reducing clinic waiting times, and ensuring affordable primary care services could reduce economic and logistical motivations for self-medication. Additionally, integrating pharmacist-led education and counselling within pharmacy practice can bridge the gap between accessibility and safe use.

Finally, these findings hold significance for AMR policy development. Palestine, similar to other LMICs, faces an escalating AMR burden that threatens the effectiveness of first-line treatments. Evidence from this study can inform the implementation of the WHO Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance, particularly strategic objectives 1 and 2 improving awareness and understanding of AMR and strengthening knowledge through surveillance and research.

Conclusion

In summary, the study provides compelling evidence that antibiotic self-medication remains a critical and multifactorial public health issue in the southern West Bank. Despite relatively high formal education levels, knowledge gaps, cultural norms, and economic hardship drive the continued misuse of antibiotics. The strong influence of pharmacies and inadequate enforcement mechanisms further perpetuate the problem. Effective interventions must combine public education, pharmacy regulation, and health system reform to promote rational antibiotic use and mitigate AMR in Palestine.
