Accessible Double Nanohole Raman Tweezer Analysis of Single Nanoparticles
Authors: Behnam Khosravi, Reuven Gordon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRaman spectroscopy allows for material characterization of nanoparticles; however, probing individual nanoparticles requires an efficient way of isolating and enhancing the signal. Past works have used optical trapping with nanoapertures in metal films to measure the Raman spectra of individual nanoparticles; however, those works required custom laser tweezer systems that provided a transmission signal to verify trapping events as well as costly top-down nanofabrication. Here, we trapped Titania nanoparticles in a commercial Raman system using double nanoholes (DNH) and measured their spectra while trapped. The microscope camera allowed for measuring the trapping event in reflection mode, and a simultaneous Raman spectrum was recorded to allow for material characterization. The Raman signal was comparable to a past work that used particles a million times larger in volume without utilizing double nanoholes, and all other features were similar. The DNHs were created with a colloidal lithography technique and identified in the microscope, as confirmed by electron microscopy registration. Therefore, this approach allows a simple way of characterizing the Raman signal of individual nanoparticles while in solution by using existing commercial Raman systems.

Introduction

Raman spectroscopy is a valuable tool for the analysis of nanoparticles because it provides information on molecular structure and size effects.1−9 In some cases, it is desirable to have access to single nanoparticle data, for example, to explore heterogeneity and look at trace materials or molecular reactions.8,10−12 One way to isolate single nanoparticles is with trapping. Trapping of single nanoparticles has been achieved using a tightly focused (NA = 1.25) Raman microscope with 120 mW of incident power achieving 13 counts/second signal for polystyrene beads of 40 nm diameter.13

To achieve more efficient trapping (e.g., for smaller particles) and a higher Raman signal, trapping with gap plasmons has been attempted. Gap structures have been used extensively in enhancing Raman.14−25 One approach is to trap metal nanoparticles and this provides a gap enhancement when trapped.26−29 Nanopores were also used for detecting nanoparticles in solutions.30−32 For analysis of nonmetallic particles, the enhancement should come from the aperture alone, which has been achieved for rectangular and DNH apertures.33,34 Those works required top-down nanofabrication of the apertures and a custom optical tweezer system to verify trapping through transmission.34 In general, shaped nanoapertures have been used widely for trapping nanoparticles, ranging from quantum dots to single proteins.35−41

There has been extensive literature on single molecule and nanoparticle SERS measurements, including works on proteins,42 DNA,43,44 extracellular vesicles,45 viruses,46 and other particles.47

Here we use reflection mode to observe the particle trapping on a commercial Raman system with the built-in camera, where the camera is used both to identify DNHs for trapping and to observe the onset of trapping. To fabricate DNHs, a colloidal lithography method was used.48 The combination of a commercial system and a bottom-up fabrication method makes this approach highly accessible to a wide range of applications. The DNH enhances not only the trapping efficiency but also the Raman signal, resulting in 400 counts/s with 14 mW of power for 21 nm diameter Titania particles.

Methods

DNHs used in this work were made using a top-down approach using polystyrene nanospheres to create DNHs with the desired gap size.48 A solution of 600 nm polystyrene nanospheres from Alpha Nanotech was drop-coated on a glass slide. Plasma etching was used to reduce the gap formed by the touching nanospheres from 150 to 20 nm. It also reduces the diameter of the spheres from 600 to 400 nm. Similar results have been reported here.48 These results were obtained empirically, and calibration curves are required to tune the parameters precisely. By using the plasma etching machine (Harrick, PDC-002), polystyrene sizes were reduced to create different gap sizes between 20 and 60 nm.49 This resulted in a diameter of approximately 400 nm for each nanohole. Then, the glass slide was coated with 100 nm of gold, and by removing polystyrene beads, the holes would appear in the gold film.

The distribution of holes can be tuned by the polystyrene solution concentration. The optimum concentration will create single holes and a few DNHs with few clusters of polystyrene. Detecting is possible with this approach.50

The trapped particles were titania (TiO2) powder with particle sizes of 21 nm from Sigma-Aldrich (718467) diluted in water. The solution was placed between the gold film and a coverslip. A No. 1 coverslip (130–160 μm thick) was facing the 50×, 0.75 NA objective on a Renishaw InVia Raman system. Figure 1 shows the sample structure under the objective. The trapping laser was the same as the Raman excitation laser, with a wavelength of 785 nm, and the signal was measured in reflection only. The Raman setup used a long-pass filter to prevent the reflected laser from reaching the detector.

(A) The gold sample with DNH and TiO2 solution under a Raman microscope objective. (B) Scanning electron microscope (SEM) image of a DNH in the gold layer. (C) Picture of the sample under the microscope objective of the Raman system.

The fabrication process creates random holes on the gold surface. The desired DNHs are spread across the sample with different orientations. In order to find and determine their orientation, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) has been used in the past.50 It was found that the camera image on the Raman system can be used to determine which hole is a DNH, and the orientation can be estimated with high accuracy. Figure 2 shows the SEM image and camera image on the Raman system with DNHs visible and circled. This figure shows how hole mapping with the camera alone can be used to precisely investigate the DNHs for trapping to determine their shape, size, and orientation. In other words, it is possible to find the DNHs without requiring an SEM.

(A) SEM image of an area on the sample. (B) Camera image of the same area on the Raman system. Comparison of these two images shows the location of the DNHs, visible in the SEM and identifiable by the camera image in the microscope. DNHs are circled in both images. (C) SEM image of single and double nanoholes. (D) Camera image of single and double nanoholes. DNHs are indicated by arrows.

Using the camera image with white light illumination, we could differentiate between double and single nanoholes, as shown in Figure 2C,D. After the measurements, we confirmed that these holes were double nanoholes by SEM images as shown in Figure 2A. We observed that some holes were two single nanoholes close to one another, but they did not show the Raman enhancement, which provides another way of confirmation.

Results

The camera field of view is 90 μm by 58 μm, its resolution is 750 × 480 pixels, and the video has 15 frames per second. Each pixel corresponds to a square with dimensions of 120 nm by 120 nm. After converting the video format to mpeg, the resolution was 1280 by 720 and the analysis was done on this format.

Figure 4 shows the Raman spectrum for a trapped TiO2 particle in a DNH in the gold film. Four main Raman peaks are visible in the spectrum. The Stokes lines correspond to the molecular vibrations of anatase Titania, as reported here.52 They correspond to vibrations Eg(ν1), B1g(ν2), B1g(ν4), and Eg(ν6).

Subtraction of Raman spectrum for a trapped TiO2 particle showing peaks at 145, 399, 516, and 640 cm–1.53

For further analysis, the intensity of the spectrum at 145 cm–1 had been measured to determine the effects of DNH on the Raman intensity. The maximum intensities for a few different DNHs fabricated with different cusp separations (gaps) are shown in Figure 5. It can be seen that decreasing the gap distance in the DNH typically increases the Raman signal.

Intensity of the Raman signal at 145 cm–1 for TiO2 at different gap separations of DNHs. Vertical error bars are from the shot noise, and horizontal error bars are from the size measurement.

The general trend observed was that decreasing the gap size increased the Raman signal, but this is also linked to the plasmonic enhancement. Future work may explore the exact geometry that allows for optimal enhancement for each gap size.

The value at 145 cm–1 is based on past work.53 We calibrated the Raman system with silicon and found that it was shifted by 2 cm–1 compared with the expected values.

While the smaller peaks are visible at the expected locations, their signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) was not suitable for quantification of the Raman enhancement. We were unable to improve this by integration beyond the 30 s we used without saturating the detector by the low wavenumber peak.

Discussion

The presented approach provides an accessible platform for the investigation of single isolated nanoparticles in solution. The main features that make this approach accessible (as compared to past works) are that we use a colloidal nanofabrication approach which does not require any top-down lithography; we can identify DNHs and their orientation using the microscope’s built-in camera, and we can observe the trapping by changes in the camera’s image in reflection mode. This approach is, therefore, accessible to users with a commercial Raman system.

Furthermore, the approach provides enhanced Raman from the DNH gap, allowing for 400 counts/s with 14 mW of incident power, a 50× objective for 21 nm particles. This result can be compared with a past work that obtained similar signal performance for Titania particles with a million times larger volume without employing metal nanostructures,54 where all other parameters (e.g., laser wavelength, power, integration time) were comparable. Therefore, we show that the DNH platform provides enhancement that benefits both trapping and the Raman signal for assessing the Raman spectra from individual nanoparticles.

We found that it was possible to reuse the sample at least ten times before it became contaminated. The samples were cleaned in acetone overnight and dried with dry nitrogen gas.

It is possible to optimize the samples for improved Raman enhancement by tuning the sizes of the apertures and the gaps. In this work, we focused on 400 nm aperture diameters and cusp sizes approximately equal to the nanoparticle size we wanted to study; however, we also attempted 200 nm apertures and they showed slightly higher enhancement.

Cross-polarized detection of the Raman signal is also possible. It should be noted that this will be impacted by the orientation of the DNH. For the case where the DNH is at 45 deg, then cross-polarization can be used to minimize the elastic reflection of the incident laser.49

In the future, it would be of interest to use this approach to study heterogeneity of the nanoparticles, for example, biological nanoparticles55 or different crystal structures.

Conclusions

In this work, we have demonstrated the use of bottom-up DNHs for trapping and Raman analysis of single nanoparticles using a commercial Raman laser system in reflection mode. Not only do these features make the approach accessible to a wide range of end-users that do not have access to top-down nanofabrication or customizable laser tweezer systems, but we also demonstrate the ability to locate, orient, and identify trapping based on the imaging camera integrated with the microscope system. Based on these features, we believe that this will be an attractive approach to analyze heterogeneity in a variety of nanoparticle samples including analyzing differences in crystal structure or biological properties.

In the future, it will be possible to use the laser to heat the sample and potentially observe conformational changes or interactions in situ. We, and others, have recently reported on heating in DNH apertures.35,44,56−60
