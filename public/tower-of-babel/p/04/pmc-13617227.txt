Impact of Access to Pasteurised Donor Human Milk on Rates of Necrotising Enterocolitis in Neonatal Units in Australia: A Cohort Study

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimIn 2018, a pasteurised donor human milk (PDHM) service was established within Australia's national blood service, Lifeblood, and subsequently expanded to a national service. Before 2018, five milk banks operated in Australia, but most infants admitted to Australian neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) still did not have access to PDHM. This study aimed to compare infant outcomes before and after Australian NICUs had access to an assured supply of PDHM from Lifeblood.MethodsCohort study utilising Australian and New Zealand Neonatal Network data for infants born < 32 weeks' gestation admitted to 15 NICUs across Australia over a 3‐year period (2018–2020; n = 4395 infants). The primary study factor was NICU access to an assured supply of PDHM from Lifeblood. Neonatal outcomes were compared in periods with “PDHM available” versus “PDHM not available”. The primary outcome was the proportion of infants born < 28 weeks' gestation who developed NEC. Secondary outcomes included duration of parenteral nutrition, retinopathy of prematurity, infection, death, length of stay, weight at discharge, and breast milk feeding at discharge.ResultsAfter adjusting for confounding risks, NEC relative risk was 38% lower for infants born < 28 weeks' (aRR 0.62, 95% CI 0.46–0.83) when Lifeblood PDHM was available. Weight at discharge (2828.8 vs. 2939.56 g, p = 0.001) was lower when PDHM was available. Breast milk feeding at discharge, infections and death were not associated with PDHM availability.ConclusionsReliable access to PDHM was associated with reduced risk of NEC for infants born < 28 weeks' gestation in Australian NICUs.

Introduction

Mother's own milk (MOM) is the best source of nutrition for preterm infants. Many preterm infants in a neonatal intensive care units (NICU), however, are temporarily unable to be fed MOM and are given supplemental nutrition. Pasteurised donor human milk (PDHM) is recommended by the World Health Organisation [1] when sufficient MOM is not available.

Internationally, using PDHM instead of infant formula for infants born < 32 weeks' and/or < 1800 g when sufficient MOM is not available is associated with approximately 2‐fold reduction in necrotising enterocolitis (NEC) [2] and improved feed tolerance [3]. Access to PDHM has also been associated with improved health service outcomes, such as reduced length of stay [4, 5], primarily resulting from a reduction in NEC and its complications. However, reduction in NEC is not observed with PDHM use in all settings [6, 7] and its impact on late‐onset sepsis [2, 6], retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) [4], and adverse long term neurodevelopmental outcomes [8] remains uncertain.

The major risk associated with PDHM is slower early growth [2], although multi‐nutrient fortification may reduce this risk [9]. There is also a risk that access to PDHM may unintentionally adversely impact breastfeeding rates [10] if not provided in conjunction with strong support for maternal lactation.

Most infants admitted to Australian NICUs did not have access to PDHM before 2018. While five milk banks were operating [11], only 25% of high‐risk preterm infants had access to PDHM [4]. Australia's national blood service, Australian Red Cross Lifeblood (Lifeblood), began operating a centralised milk service in September 2018 to provide PDHM to preterm infants in NICUs who did not have access to another source. The service was expanded to a national service over several years. At the end of 2020, 11 NICUs had received a reliable supply of PDHM from Lifeblood, without any episode of interruption or supply shortage.

As relatively few infants had been able to receive PDHM in Australia until 2018, it is important to define the potential benefits and risks of PDHM use as it becomes widely available [12]. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of reliable PDHM supply in neonatal units caring for infants born very and extremely preterm, in terms of proportion of infants with NEC as well as other clinical outcomes and breastfeeding outcomes at hospital discharge. The primary outcome was the proportion of infants born < 28 weeks' gestation who developed NEC.

Methods

Study Design

A population‐based cohort study using the Australian and New Zealand Neonatal Network (ANZNN) database for 2018–2020. ANZNN prospectively collects data on all infants admitted to Australian NICUs born < 32 weeks and/or < 1500 g.

Setting

22 of 23 Australian NICUs gave permission for their ANZNN data to be used in for the purpose of this study. To accurately assess the impact of access to a sufficient, reliable supply of PDHM, we excluded seven NICUs that either already received a regular supply of PDHM from an existing milk bank prior to 2018 (n = 5) or did not report data for all study years (n = 2).

We included only NICUs (n = 15) that began to receive regular PDHM supply from Lifeblood during the study period (n = 11) or NICUs that did not have any access to a regular supply of PDHM up until the end of 2020 (n = 4).

The five NICUs with a regular supply of PDHM prior to 2018 were excluded as we had no capacity to objectively determine the reliability of PDHM supply from other milk banks during the study period. We were able to assess ‘reliability’ of supply from Lifeblood through routinely collected Lifeblood records: all PDHM orders placed by hospitals were delivered in full, on time over the study period. There were no unfilled or partially filled orders, which would indicate interrupted supply or supply shortages.

Across participating units, infants were eligible to receive PDHM if they were born < 32 weeks' gestation or weighing < 1500 g and did not have sufficient MOM, with access usually continued up to 34 weeks' corrected age.

Participants

Infants born < 32 weeks' gestation between 1 January 2018 and 31 December 2020 and admitted to an eligible Australian NICU (n = 15).

Data Sources

Data was acquired from the ANZNN database. The ANZNN collects standardised, audited data on outcomes for all high‐risk newborns admitted to Australian NICUs. Dates of NICU access to Lifeblood PDHM supply was determined by month and year of first PDHM delivery from Lifeblood internal records.

Variables

The primary outcome was the proportion of infants born < 28 weeks' gestation who developed NEC in a time‐period when NICUs were receiving Lifeblood PDHM, compared to when the NICUs were not receiving Lifeblood PDHM. The ANZNN defines NEC as (1) NEC diagnosis at surgery or post‐mortem, or (2) radiological diagnosis of NEC—a clinical history plus pneumatosis intestinalis, or portal vein gas, or a persistent dilated loop on serial X‐rays, or (3) clinical diagnosis of NEC—a clinical history plus abdominal wall cellulitis and palpable abdominal mass [13].

Secondary outcomes were the proportion of infants born < 32 weeks' gestation who developed NEC, infections, weight at discharge to home or at term, duration of parenteral nutrition, breast milk feeding at discharge, retinopathy of prematurity, death prior to discharge and length of NICU stay.

Other study variables included infant sex, year of admission, delivery mode, birth weight, antibiotic provided to mother, probiotics given to the infant, respiratory support at 36 weeks' postmenstrual age, receipt of donor milk, and hospital. Variables are defined in Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterise variables for extremely preterm (< 28 weeks' gestation) and all preterm infants born < 32 weeks' gestation.

For the analysis, infants were categorised into two groups based on the availability of Lifeblood PDHM in the unit at the time that they were admitted, irrespective of whether the individual infant received PDHM. Time periods were categorised as “PDHM not available in NICU” or “PDHM available in NICU”. Across the 15 units in the analysis, 270 months were included in each of the “PDHM not available in NICU” and “PDHM available in NICU” periods.

We fitted a log‐binomial model under the generalised linear model (GENMOD) framework to examine the association between PDHM access and outcomes with binary responses, including NEC, infection, weight at discharge, breast milk feeding at discharge, retinopathy of prematurity, and death prior to discharge. Relative risk (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated after adjusting for sex, year of admission, delivery type, birth weight, antibiotic provided to mother, and probiotics given to the infant. As continuous outcome variables (duration of parenteral nutrition and length of NICU stay) were skewed, nonparametric Mann–Whitney U tests were used to estimate the difference in two groups and calculate p‐values. Data analysis was performed using SAS version 9.4.

Sample Size

To estimate the required duration of the study we did a study size calculation prior to commencement. ANZNN 2015 registry data indicated that 1000 infants were born < 28 weeks in 2015 and that 8.2% developed NEC [14].

Sample size estimation was based on a staged roll out of PDHM across NICUs included in the cohort study. Assuming a medium effect size (50% reduction in NEC from 8.2% to 4.1% in extremely preterm infants), with assumption of Type‐I error of 0.05, and Type‐II error of 0.2 (power = 0.8), and an allocation ratio of 3‐1 (intervention to routine‐care) for a two‐tail z‐test, the study reaches desired power at 36 months.

Ethics Statement

Ethical approvals were granted by the South Eastern Sydney Local Health District (2019/ETH10608) and Lifeblood (Klein 12112020) Human Research Ethics Committees.

Infants Born < 28 Weeks' Gestation

Between 2018 and 2020, 1438 infants were born < 28 weeks' gestation, with 722 (50.2%) born when the NICU had access to Lifeblood PDHM. The 107 cases of NEC occurred in infants born < 28 weeks' gestation: NEC rate 7.4% overall.

NEC rates were significantly lower in infants born extremely preterm when PDHM was reliably available in the unit where they were admitted. The rate of NEC was 6.65% in the period when Lifeblood PDHM was available compared to 8.24% when the units did not receive Lifeblood PDHM. The rate of NEC was 38% lower when Lifeblood PDHM was available (aRR 0.62, 95% CI 0.46–0.83) (Table 2).

Discussion

In preterm infants, NEC is a debilitating illness with a high risk of mortality and morbidity. Interventions which reduce NEC are very important, particularly as Australian units are admitting more infants born ≤ 24 weeks' gestation [15], who are at highest risk.

During the period when NICUs had access to a sufficient supply of PDHM from Lifeblood, NEC rates were lower in infants born extremely preterm (< 28 weeks' gestation) and all infants born < 32 weeks' gestation. This is consistent with the international evidence [7, 16, 17, 18, 19], though effect size in this cohort study (aRR 0.67) was not as large as reported in a recent Cochrane review (2‐fold reduction in NEC in infants < 32 weeks receiving PDHM instead of infant formula) [2].

The smaller effect size observed in this study, compared to published randomised controlled trials, may be explained by the fact that several NICUs later supplied by Lifeblood had already been accessing small quantities of donor milk from other sources. Notably, the ANZNN definition of ‘donor milk receipt’ does not exclude peer‐shared human milk, and units may have used different definitions of donor milk when reporting to the database. In the ‘pre‐intervention’ period, between 0% and 23% of infants in these NICUs received donor milk. PDHM use increased after Lifeblood supply became available but this study may encompass a transition or adoption period where PDHM was not yet consistently offered to all eligible infants or consented to by families when sufficient MOM was unavailable. An average of 60% of infants across the 11 NICUs received donor milk, which is less than expected. A US survey reported that in more than half of participating NICUs, ≥ 80% of very low birthweight infants received PDHM in hospital [20]. Previous research has shown breast milk feeding rates differ for extremely preterm infants among Australian and New Zealand NICUs, with some units achieving high rates of MOM feeding but Indigenous and other minority groups at risk for lower rates of receiving MOM [21]. Moreover, even when donor milk is available families may encounter barriers to accepting it for their child due to lack of their own or their healthcare provider's knowledge of PDHM use, concerns about the safety or quality of PDHM, or cultural, religious or social concerns about donated milk or donor identity [22]. It is unknown if lower use in this study is due to higher availability of maternal milk and/or differences in patient population, parental consent rates, or other factors.

Preterm nutrition practises and other NEC prevention practises also vary considerably among and within Australian NICUs, which may also contribute to the results observed in this study. Surveys have shown Australian NICUs significantly differ in their protocols for start and advancement of feeds, use of human milk fortifiers and other supplements, and use and strains of probiotics [23, 24, 25]. Standardised, evidence‐based nutrition protocols have been shown to sustainably improve infant outcomes, including for NEC [26]. Therefore, while access to a sufficient source of PDHM is a critical foundational step, further work to develop and implement standardised nutrition care is also needed to further reduce NEC rates [23].

Weight at discharge was lower for infants born < 28 weeks' gestation when Lifeblood PDHM was available. Reduced or delayed growth is considered the key risk associated with the use of PDHM [5], although human milk may promote better quality growth (in terms of body composition) than infant formula [27]. PDHM typically has lower protein and energy content than preterm MOM or infant formula. A recent study of Lifeblood PDHM found mean protein content was lower than would be expected in preterm MOM or formula [28]. Slower growth may be offset by routine fortification [29], which is now standard in many Australian NICUs. Fortifier use varies considerably among units in Australia, and lower weight at discharge in extremely, but not all, preterm infants may reflect this.

Other secondary outcomes were also impacted by PDHM availability. Risk of ROP was slightly lower when Lifeblood PDHM was available. This is consistent with evidence that a human milk diet reduces ROP [30] and has been reported in some studies of PDHM, although it has not been a consistent finding across studies [4, 31]. In this study, the association between human milk and ROP may be confounded by the lack of detailed data available about the quantity of MOM received by infants who were also fed PDHM. Further research is needed to confirm this finding.

Extremely preterm infants had a 4 day longer median duration of parenteral nutrition after Lifeblood PDHM became available. This is unexpected, as PDHM is generally associated with improved feed tolerance and randomised controlled trials have found markedly shorter [3] or no difference [6] in duration of parenteral nutrition in PDHM compared to formula supplemented groups.

It is important to ensure that PDHM availability does not adversely impact maternal breastfeeding. Internationally, evidence around the impact of PDHM on MOM feeding is mixed [10, 19, 32]. In this study, rates of breast milk feeding at discharge were lower after PDHM was reliably available, but this difference was not statistically significant when the unit was treated as a random effect to account for variation in feeding practises and maternal lactation support between units. Infants only receive PDHM when insufficient MOM is available, and it is important that PDHM availability does not reduce staff support for maternal lactation. PDHM should always be provided alongside lactation support and clear messaging that PDHM is not a replacement for MOM [33]. Importantly, lactation support in NICUs was also impacted by the COVID‐19 pandemic in 2020. Further research should explore variation in Australian unit practises around PDHM use that might support or unintentionally discourage maternal breastfeeding. For example, one NICU in South Australia reported that average breast milk feeding at discharge increased from 63% to 77% after PDHM became available [34].

This is the first study that investigates the benefits and risks of access to a reliable source of PDHM for infants born < 32 weeks' gestation in Australia. This study is strengthened by using data routinely collected by the ANZNN, which ensures its completeness and validity.

Limitations

This study has limitations due to the nature of the dataset. ANZNN do not collect data on infant formula or fortifier receipt, so we cannot determine overall use of supplemental nutrition or exposure to cow's milk products. Moreover, donor milk use is recorded as a binary variable (yes/no). Information about whether donor milk was offered, but not consented to by the family, is not recorded. Therefore, we cannot determine the proportion of the infant's diet that was PDHM or formula compared to MOM. The amount of supplementation an individual infant requires varies considerably, and the proportion of MOM may be a predictor of infant outcomes [35]. Finally, outcomes in this observational study may have been affected by practise changes during the COVID‐19 pandemic in 2020.

Conclusions

Reliable access to PDHM was associated with a decreased rate of NEC and ROP for infants born < 28 weeks' gestation in Australian NICUs. Further work is needed to ensure that PDHM is always used as part of a strategy which emphasises maternal breastfeeding and lactation support.
