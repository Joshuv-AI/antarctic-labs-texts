Mechanisms of dexamethasone-induced bone toxicity in developing bone: a single-cell perspective
Authors: Kelly Warmink, Philip Lijnzaad, Stephanie A Schubert, Baojie Zhang, Tristan C W Vermaat, Lisa Jansen, Marloes C C van Gend, Aleksandra Balwierz, Natalie Proost, Marieke van de Ven, Johannes H M Merks, Thanasis Margaritis, Claudia Y Janda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlucocorticoids, such as dexamethasone, are essential for treating severe childhood conditions, including cancer, organ transplantation, and inflammatory disorders. However, their long-term use can impair bone development, posing risks to pediatric bone health, which is vital for lifelong skeletal integrity. A mechanistic insight into how glucocorticoids negatively impact bone could improve decision-making in patient care, thereby improving the quality of life for pediatric cancer patients and survivors. In this study, we aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying dexamethasone-induced bone toxicity in developing bones using single-cell transcriptomics. We treated skeletally immature C57BL/6JRj male mice with dexamethasone for 28 days, and assessed the bone architecture with micro-computed tomography, and characterized bone and bone marrow cells from the femurs using single-cell RNA sequencing. Our findings revealed a marked reduction in osteoblast and chondrocyte cell populations and impaired function of pre-osteoblasts. Additionally, dexamethasone adversely affected B cell subsets, significantly depleting early B cell progenitors while allowing some further developed immature B cells to persist. These cellular changes were accompanied by reduced longitudinal bone growth, compromised bone architecture, and increased bone fragility at the highest doses of dexamethasone. Interestingly, unlike observations in adults, dexamethasone did not enhance osteoclast activity in our model. Overall, our study suggests that the adverse effects of dexamethasone on bone development are primarily due to its impact on osteoblastic, chondroblastic, and B cell lineages. This disruption affects the critical signaling crosstalk between the cells necessary for both bone development and hematopoiesis.

Introduction

Glucocorticoids are widely used for treating various diseases, including cancers, organ transplantation, and autoimmune disorders. These drugs have potent immunosuppressive properties, making them indispensable for managing inflammation and immune responses. In pediatric oncology, glucocorticoids are essential components of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and lymphoma therapy and are used to alleviate tumor edema- and therapy-related side effects, such as nausea, allergic reactions, and other immune-related side effects. However, prolonged glucocorticoid use, especially at higher doses as often required in pediatric oncology, is associated with significant side effects, particularly bone-related complications like growth retardation, loss of bone mineral density (BMD), bone fragility, necrosis, and an increased risk of fractures.1,2 Clinical data show that reduced BMD is common among ALL patients undergoing therapy, with deficits emerging as early as the first month of chemotherapy, even in those patients with normal BMD at diagnosis.3 Additionally, low BMD often persists post-treatment, and up to every third patient experiences fractures.4,5 The association of glucocorticoids with bone-related adverse events in ALL patients has been demonstrated in some studies and has recently been summarized by Velentza et al.1 Treating fractures in these patients is challenging due to the high risk of implantation failure in osteoporotic bone, potentially resulting in chronic pain, limited mobility, and long-term disability. Furthermore, pediatric ALL and lymphoma patients often achieve high survival rates, leading to a long life expectancy. This highlights the extended duration of survivorship and the potential long-term effects of treatment.

Dexamethasone and other glucocorticoids exert their effects by binding to the intracellular glucocorticoid receptor expressed in most cell types, including bone (progenitor) cells.6 The glucocorticoid-receptor complex acts as a transcription factor, activating or repressing the transcription of glucocorticoid-responsive genes. At physiological concentrations, glucocorticoids support bone formation and the accumulation of bone mass by directing the differentiation of mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs) and stromal progenitors toward the osteoblastic lineage, while inhibiting differentiation into adipocytes.7 However, when administrated at therapeutic concentrations, glucocorticoids reduce bone formation and increase bone resorption, thereby decreasing overall bone mass and elevating fracture risk.8 Several mechanisms have been identified by which glucocorticoids directly or indirectly affect skeletal cells, which are comprehensively reviewed by Velentza et al. and Gado et al.1,8 For example, high concentrations of glucocorticoids inhibit the differentiation of MSCs and osteoblast progenitors toward the osteoblastic lineage, while promoting their differentiation into adipocytes by decreasing the expression of key transcription factors essential for osteoblast differentiation, like Sp7/Osterix (Sp7).9,10 Furthermore, glucocorticoids inhibit pre-osteoblast maturation by suppressing Wnt and BMP signaling, both of which are crucial for osteoblast differentiation.11 Additionally, glucocorticoids impair osteoblast function by reducing the expression of bone matrix proteins, including collagen type I and osteocalcin.12,13 Glucocorticoids also induce apoptosis in osteoblasts and osteocytes,14,15 and disrupt the RANKL/OPG balance in these cells, thereby enhancing osteoclast activity and promoting bone resorption.16,17 Together, these mechanisms disrupt the intricate balance between bone formation and resorption, resulting in detrimental effects on bone structure, metabolism, and bone function.

The underlying mechanisms of glucocorticoid-induced bone toxicity have predominantly been investigated using cell lines and mouse models involving skeletally mature mice. However, there may be significant differences in the mechanisms, severity, and duration of dexamethasone’s effects between children and adults, as pediatric patients have developing bones characterized by high turnover rates and ongoing remodeling.6,18 In developing bones, bone growth relies on chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation, followed by endochondral ossification in the epiphyseal plates. Dexamethasone in pediatric patients may disrupt these critical processes of bone growth and remodeling. Yet, the specific molecular mechanisms by which glucocorticoids negatively impact bone formation and resorption in developing bones have not been thoroughly investigated, despite the high need for improved treatment options with fewer side effects in these patients.

Recent advancements in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have enabled detailed analysis of the cellular composition of adult mouse bone tissue, revealing a diverse array of cell types, including immune cells, MSCs, osteoblasts, chondrocytes, fibroblasts, and various other cellular subtypes.19–21 In this study, we employed scRNA-seq to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying dexamethasone-induced bone toxicity in skeletally immature mice, aiming to characterize the effects of dexamethasone on specific cell populations and key bone biomarkers. The insights could enhance patient care and the quality of life for pediatric patients and cancer survivors.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male 6-wk-old C57BL/6JRj mice (n = 40, Janvier Labs) were randomized at baseline into four groups (n = 10 per group), receiving 0 mg/kg (vehicle), 5 mg/kg (DEX 5), 20 mg/kg (DEX 20), or 50 mg/kg (DEX 50) dexamethasone (Centrafarm B.V.) in 10 μL per gram body weight through daily intraperitoneal injections for 28 d. Body weight was measured daily, and tail and body length weekly. After 4 wk, mice were euthanized, and spleens and thymuses were harvested. The left hindlimb was cut at the distal femur and fixated in 4% buffered formaldehyde for micro-CT (μCT) imaging and histology. The remaining hindlimb bones were dissected, cleaned, viably frozen, and stored in liquid nitrogen for scRNA-seq. Furthermore, ten untreated 58-wk-old male C57BL/6JRj mice (Janvier Labs) were included for μCT and scRNA-seq.

Mice were housed at five animals per cage (individually ventilated), provided with enrichment, ad libitum water and chow, and maintained with temperature control (21 ± 0.5 °C), humidity control (45 ± 2%), and a 12:12 light-dark cycle. Humane endpoints included ≥20% body weight loss overall, 15% body weight loss within 2 d, circulation or breathing issues, or aberrant behavior/movement. The study protocol was approved by the ethical committee and animal welfare body under CCD number: AVD3010020198564 Appendix 1 Protocol ID 21.1.10472 and 21.1.10191, complying with European Community guidelines.

Excluded animals

One DEX 50 mouse died unexpectedly after 8 d, likely due to intestinal bleeding, and was excluded for insufficient treatment duration. Two DEX 50 mice were sacrificed after reaching the humane endpoint of ≥20% weight loss on days 22 and 23. While tissues were collected per protocol, these mice were excluded from time-sensitive analyses (serum, scRNA-seq, and histology), but included in μCT measurements. Following consultation with the animal welfare body, the remaining six DEX 50 mice were sacrificed to prevent further suffering and ensure proper harvesting. Consequently, the DEX 50 group includes nine mice treated for 21-26 d instead of 28. Additionally, one vehicle-group mouse was excluded from μCT and histology due to a proximal tibial outgrowth. See Table S1 for details.

Micro-computed tomography

The left hindlimbs were imaged using the Quantum FX μCT (PerkinElmer; SAP160036a) at 90 kV and 180 μA, at the proximal tibia to measure trabecular thickness and volume fraction (3 min, voxel size 9.8 μm) and total tibia to measure tibia length and muscle thickness (4.5 min, voxel size 39.1 μm). μCT images were analyzed in ImageJ (1.47v). Using the BoneJ plugin, the bone was segmented using a local threshold algorithm (Bernsen algorithm, radius 5). Regions of interest (ROI) to measure trabecular thickness and bone volume fraction (BV/TV) were drawn in transverse plane 103 slides (±1 mm), starting below the growth plate going distal on the tibia, while excluding the cortical bone. The thickness of the cortical bone was determined by measuring the mean thickness of 11 slides (±100 μm) in the transverse plane 1.5 mm below the tibia growth plate. In addition, the number of perforations in the tibia subchondral plate was counted. The tibia length was measured from the medial meniscus to the medial malleolus. The muscle thickness was defined as the perpendicular line until the edge of the gray area, at half of the tibia length. All μCT analyses were done blinded and randomized by two independent observers.

Histology

After μCT imaging, bones were decalcified in 0.5 M EDTA (pH 7.0) for 3 wk, refixed in 4% buffered formaldehyde, dehydrated in ethanol (70%-100% ethanol), cleared in xylene, and paraffin-embedded in sagittal orientation for 4 μm sections. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed at the Diagnostics Department of the Princess Máxima Center.

Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining for osteoclasts involved incubating the slides for 4 h at 37 °C, with 2.5 mg/mL Naphtol AS-MX phosphate and 5.5 mg/mL Fast Red TR salt in 0.2 M acetate buffer (pH 5.0) with 50 mM tartaric acid. Osteocalcin and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) immunohistochemistry were used to detect osteoblasts. For both immunostainings, antigen retrieval was performed in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0, 30 min, 37 °C). Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4 °C with 0.1 μg/mL osteocalcin polyclonal antibody (PA5-78870, Invitrogen), 5 μg/mL ALP polyclonal antibody (PA5-106391, Invitrogen). Slides were incubated (30 min, RT) with anti-rabbit IgG HRP (ab205718, Abcam) or Envision anti-rabbit IgG HRP (k4003, Agilent Technologies), followed by DAB+ (Agilent) for 5 min.

Images were taken using a Leica DMi8 light microscope or Aperio GT 450 DX slide scanner, and two to four sections within an image were analyzed in ImageJ (v2.9.0) or using the EBImage package (version 4.48.0) in R studio (version 2025.05.0-496). Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase-stained areas were quantified according to guidelines,22 by measuring osteoclast surface normalized to bone surface. Osteocalcin-positive osteoblasts directly neighboring bone tissue were counted. ALP was quantified by measuring the surface of osteoblasts and pre-osteoblasts normalized to bone surface. The ROI for this analysis encompassed the entire bone area within a 10× field of view. All image acquisition and analysis were done blinded and randomized.

Serum measurements

On day 28, tail vein blood was collected in serum tubes and centrifuged (1800 RCF, 10 min), and the serum was then snap-frozen and stored in liquid nitrogen until analysis. Serum cytokines and chemokines were measured using a 32-multiplex assay (Merck, MCYMAG-70K-PX32) at the MultiPlex Core Facility, University Medical Center Utrecht, The Netherlands. Analytes with >25% out-of-range values (IL-3, IL-7, IL12p70, and LIF) were excluded. IFNγ was below, and LIX above the detection limit. The remaining out-of-range values (6% of all measurements, all below detection limits) were set at one half of the lower quantification limit.

Statistics imaging and serum data

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism (v9.3.1, GraphPad) and R (v4.2.1). Differences between treatment groups were assessed with one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for μCT, histology, and serum data. Data distribution was checked using Q-Q plots of residuals. Longitudinal data (body mass and length and tail length) were analyzed with a mixed-effects model (REML), with time point and treatment group as fixed effects and individual animals as random effect. The p-values ≤.05 were considered significant. Data are presented as boxplots (median, interquartile range, min-max) with significance indicated as *(p ≤ .05), **(p ≤ .01), ***(p ≤ .001), and NS (not significant). Group allocation was blinded during post-life measurements, and all procedures were randomized.

scRNA-seq sample preparation

Single-cell analysis of femurs was performed using the 10x Genomics Chromium 3′ Gene Expression solution v3.1, see extended materials and methods section. In brief, femurs from mice were pooled per treatment group and of 20 vehicle-treated mice to ensure sufficient cells for scRNA-seq. Bone fragments were digested in two 30-min rounds at 37 °C using digestion buffer containing Opti-MEM (Gibco, 12634010), 100 U/mL DNase (Stemcell, 7469), 10 mM HEPES buffer (Gibco, 15630056), 1 mM CaCl2, 10 μM ROCK (Y-27632, Biogems, 1293823), and 0.1 mg/mL Liberase DL (first round; Sigma, 5466202001) or 0.1 mg/mL Liberase TM (second round; Sigma, 05401119001). Bone fragments were filtered (70 μm) and washed after each step, then resuspended in FACS buffer (magnesium-free PBS, 1% BSA, 1 mM EDTA, 10 μM ROCK). After red blood cell lysis (Invitrogen), samples were blocked with anti-CD16/CD32 antibody (rat anti-mouse BD, 553079). Cells were stained with CD45-FITC (Stemcell, 60161.2, Clone 2.4G2), DRAQ5 (Biolegend), and DAPI (Biolegend). Sorting was performed using a Sony SH800 Cell Sorter (100 μm nozzle chip). CD45+ and CD45− cells were combined in a 50:50 ratio, with 45 000 cells per condition. cDNA libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq6000 (Illumina).

scRNA-seq data processing

Reads were mapped to mouse mm10-2020-A transcriptome using the Cell Ranger (v6.1.1) and analyzed through Seurat (v4.1.1 and v4.2.1) in R. Low quality cells and doublets were filtered out based on number of transcripts (nCount_RNA <500 or >40 000), percentage of mitochondrial transcripts (>25%), percentage of hemoglobin transcript (>5%), and doublets annotated using scDblFinder (v1.10), see extended materials and methods section. The resulting object was normalized using the SCTransform and the top 3000 most variably expressed genes were defined. Cell cycle and correlated genes were identified, see extended materials and methods section, and removed from the list of variable genes. Hemoglobin, ribosomal, and stress-related genes were removed from the variable features, resulting in 2799 variable features. Clustering was performed using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions, with 35 principal components and a resolution of 1.8.

The resulting 46 clusters were annotated using SingleR (v1.10.0), with the MouseRNAseqData and ImmGenData reference datasets. Manually accrued marker genes (see Table S2) and the top 5 most variable genes per cluster were used to refine cell annotations. The stromal cell clusters were confirmed with cell projection,23 using the mouse bone marrow stroma data by Baryawno et al.19

scRNA-seq data analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified per cluster for each treatment condition compared to the vehicle group using FindMarkers function, with logfc.threshold = log2(1.5) and min.cells.group = 10, and filtered on a p_val_adj < 0.1. Top 25 DEGs of the cell clusters underwent GO enrichment analysis (Biological Process) with enrichGO from clusterProfiler (v4.8.2). B-cell trajectory inference was performed after re-clustering the B-cells separately with a Gaussian mixture model using mclust (v6.0.0). Slingshot (v2.8.0) was used to infer the pseudotime. Genes (anti)correlating maximally with the B cell Trajectory pseudotime were identified using Pearson correlation and subjected to gene set over-representation analysis. For each cell population, we used Pearson’s chi-squared test (via the R function prop.test) to access differences in cell type proportions across the three treatment groups, a combined dexamethasone group and the aged group, using the vehicle group as the expected reference distribution. This analysis was performed separately for stromal and immune cell populations. The p-values ≤.05 were considered significant and are indicated in the bar graphs with the ‡ symbol. Exact p-values are provided in the Table S4 and S5.

Results

To investigate the effects of glucocorticoids on developing bones, skeletally immature mice (6-wk-old, male, C57BL/6JRj) were injected daily for 28 d with 0 mg/kg (vehicle), 5 mg/kg (DEX 5), 20 mg/kg (DEX 20), or 50 mg/kg (DEX 50) dexamethasone (Figure 1A). We focused our study on male mice to avoid variability introduced by the estrous cycle in female mice, which begins at 6-8 wk of age and causes hormonal fluctuations that could significantly influence bone metabolism and drug response. The age of the mice corresponds approximately to 11.5 yr in human terms,24 which is within the age spectrum for pediatric ALL cases. The 5 mg/kg dose of dexamethasone in mice, approximately equivalent to 0.41 mg/kg in humans, is close to the dose received by pediatric ALL patients during the induction phase (about 0.2-0.3 mg/kg/day depending on the child’s weight). However, the 50 mg/kg dose is significantly higher. After treatment, bone architecture was assessed by μCT, and bone and bone marrow cells were analyzed by scRNA-seq and histology. Notably, the DEX 50 group exhibited body weight loss during treatment (Figure S1A), with two mice reaching the humane endpoint, indicating that this dose approaches the maximum tolerated dose for skeletally young C57BL/6JRj mice. While the relative tail length remained comparable, there was a trend toward reduced growth in body length among the dexamethasone-treated mice compared to the vehicle group (Figure S1B and C). Spleen weight decreased across all dexamethasone-treated groups (Figure S1D). Although not significant, a similar trend was observed for thymus weight (Figure S1E), suggesting a potential impact on immune cell development and activation. Treatment duration, dosing, and analysis are summarized in Table S1.

Dexamethasone adversely affects longitudinal growth and bone architecture

Bone growth impairment is a well-documented side effect of long-term glucocorticoid therapy in pediatric patients.1,6,18 To assess the impact of dexamethasone on skeletal development, we examined tibial growth using μCT. We observed a dose-dependent decrease in tibia length, with higher dexamethasone concentrations leading to a more pronounced reduction (Figure 1B and C), while body mass at endpoint was not significantly different between the groups, except for the DEX 50 group (Figure S1A). In contrast, muscle thickness, measured as mid-tibia μCT gray area thickness relative to tibia length, showed no significant differences (Figure 1D).

Bone volume loss is one of the main bone-related side effects in patients undergoing dexamethasone treatment.1,6,18 To further explore the effects of dexamethasone on bone volume and architecture, we conducted μCT measurements on the proximal tibia metaphysis (Figure 1B). We found that the bone volume fraction (BV/TV) was significantly reduced, and trabecular thickness showed a trend toward a decrease in DEX 50 mice compared to the vehicle-treated group (Figure 1E-G). However, these parameters remained comparable to the vehicle group in the DEX 5 and DEX 20 groups (Figure 1E-G). Furthermore, the cortical thickness measured 1.5 mm below the growth plate did not significantly vary across the groups (Figure 1H). Given that trabecular bone is more metabolically active than cortical bone, the stronger effect of dexamethasone on the trabeculae was expected. These findings suggest that in young mice, alterations in bone microstructure detectable by μCT occur only at the highest dexamethasone dose of 50 mg/kg, while impaired longitudinal growth is evident even at the lower dose of 5 mg/kg.

To contextualize the effects of dexamethasone on bone loss in skeletally young mice, we compared the bone architecture of the proximal tibia metaphysis of dexamethasone-treated mice to that of aged controls (aged; 58-wk-old, male, C57BL/6JRj mice from the same breeder), as aging is a well-established risk factor for bone loss across species, including human and rodents. Although age- and dexamethasone-induced bone loss arise from different mechanisms, comparing their effects on bone volume and architecture provides important context by illustrating how dexamethasone-induced changes differ from the more pronounced alterations associated with aging. Interestingly, compared to dexamethasone-treated and vehicle controls, aged mice exhibited significantly lower BV/TV but greater trabecular and cortical thickness (Figure 1E-H). In summary, our results indicate that dexamethasone treatment negatively impacts both longitudinal bone growth and bone architecture by reducing bone volume fraction and trabecular thickness in skeletally immature mice.

Dexamethasone-induced bone fragility is unrelated to increased osteoclast activity

Long-term glucocorticoid treatment is known to contribute to bone fragility.1,6,18 In adults, dexamethasone-induced bone loss is attributed to an imbalance between bone formation and resorption, characterized by reduced osteoblast activity and increased osteoclast activity.25 Consistently, our study found that dexamethasone treatment increased tibia subchondral plate perforations, characterized by full-depth holes in the normally dense bone layer beneath the cartilage (Figure S2A-C). Subchondral plate perforations and porosity have previously been linked to different mechanisms, including osteoclast activity.26 Of note, in our model, the observed impaired bone development was not linked to increased osteoclast activity. This is evident by the TRAP staining, which detects osteoclast activity, remaining consistently low in the tibia epiphysis across all treatment groups (Figure 2A-C). Moreover, TRAP activity in the tibia metaphysis showed a decreasing trend with dexamethasone treatment, although this was not statistically significant. These findings suggest that bone fragility and impaired bone development seen in these skeletally immature mice are not driven by enhanced osteoclast activity.

scRNA-seq revealed distinct bone and bone marrow cell populations

To deepen our understanding of the molecular mechanisms driving dexamethasone-induced bone toxicity, we performed scRNA-seq on the femurs from dexamethasone-treated and vehicle-treated mice, and a separate group of aged mice (Figure 3A). After data acquisition, we merged the datasets from the different groups and applied rigorous quality control measures to minimize bias and enhance biological accuracy. The number of transcripts per cell was used as a proxy for cell quality. Low transcript counts indicated poor cell quality, while excessively high counts suggested doublet formation. To ensure data integrity, we set broad cutoffs for transcript counts (≥500, and ≤40 000) and applied filtering on cell barcodes and genes to exclude low-quality cells, including those with >25% mitochondrial transcripts (indicative of dying cells) and >5% hemoglobin transcripts (signifying erythrocyte and erythrocyte precursor contamination). This approach yielded 42 957 cells, unevenly distributed across the three experimental conditions and two control groups (Figure S3A).

scRNA-seq analysis of femurs from dexamethasone-treated mice. (A) Schematic overview of the scRNA-seq experiment. Created with BioRender.com. (B) UMAP plot showing the final annotation of all identified cell clusters. Clusters with mixed cell signatures are indicated with a forward slash. (C) Heatmap showing the top 5 most variably expressed genes for each cell cluster.

We performed cell clustering, focusing on the top 3000 most variably expressed genes. Genes associated with cell cycle phases, hemoglobin, ribosomal proteins, and stress responses were excluded from the analysis. This analysis resulted in distinct cell clusters (Figure 3B). Immune cell annotations were refined using external bulk-sorted datasets. Stromal cell annotations were refined using Seurat by projecting the stromal cell populations onto the mouse bone marrow stroma dataset from Baryawno et al.,19 as described by Stuart et al.23 Ultimately, we identified 24 distinct cell populations, including 8 stromal cell populations and 16 immune cell populations, which were confirmed through marker gene expression analysis (Figures 3C, S3B, S4, Table S2). These populations included bone marrow-resident immune cells, such as neutrophils, B cells, monocytes, and their precursors, as well as stromal cells like Cxcl12-abundant reticular (CAR) cells, osteoblasts, fibroblasts, arterial, and sinusoidal endothelial cells (EC) (Figure 3B and C). The relative number of cells passing quality control decreased in samples exposed to higher dexamethasone doses. Despite the initial enrichment of CD45− cells to a 1:1 ratio before sequencing, all groups showed increased CD45+ cell count (Figure S3A). Additionally, some clusters displayed mixed cell signatures, like NK/NKT/T and monocyte/DC cell clusters (Figure 3B).

Dexamethasone depletes osteoblast cell counts and impairs pre-osteoblast function

To investigate the effects of dexamethasone at the cellular level, we analyzed cell populations using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for cluster analysis, focusing on differences in cell numbers and differential gene expression among treatment groups. This analysis yielded several key findings.

First, dexamethasone treatment markedly reduced the proportions of both osteoblasts and chondrocytes across all treatment groups compared to vehicle-treated mice (Figures 4A and B, S5A and B). This reduction was also observed in aged mice. To validate the scRNA-seq results, we performed immunohistochemistry staining for osteocalcin, an abundant non-collagenous bone matrix protein specifically expressed in osteoblasts, to quantify osteoblast cell counts in bone sections. Consistently, we observed a marked reduction in osteoblasts following dexamethasone treatment (Figure 4C), confirming the scRNA-seq findings.

Interestingly, while scRNA-seq analysis indicated an increase in the percentage of pre-osteoblasts, particularly in the DEX 5 and DEX 20 groups (Figure 4B), further Gene Ontology (GO) terms analysis revealed a decrease in expression of genes related to bone development and turnover, including processes like dephosphorylation, response to vitamin, and cytoplasmic translation (Figures 4D and S5C, Table S3). This suggests that although the relative number of pre-osteoblasts increased, their functional capacity was impaired. For instance, there was a significant downregulation of bone development-related genes Bglap and Bglap2, which encode osteocalcin, in the pre-osteoblast cell cluster of the DEX 5 and DEX 20 groups (Figure 4E). Although a similar decrease was observed in the DEX 50 group, the low number of analyzed pre-osteoblast cells limited statistical analysis. Additionally, markers for osteoblast development, including Sp7 and Alkaline phosphatase (Alpl), also showed a similar trend of downregulation between groups within the pre-osteoblast cluster, while genes like Collagen Type I (Col1a1) and Runx2 did not exhibit significant differences (Figure 4E). To further support our finding that dexamethasone significantly impairs the differentiation of pre-osteoblasts, we performed immunohistochemistry staining for ALP, a well-established marker for pre-osteoblasts and mature osteoblasts (Figure 4F). The trend toward reduced ALP staining observed in dexamethasone-treated mice compared to controls is in line with the loss of ALP-high mature osteoblasts and a relative accumulation of ALP-low/intermediate pre-osteoblasts. Taken together, these findings indicate that dexamethasone treatment leads to a near-complete depletion of mature osteoblasts in skeletally immature mice and significantly impairs the differentiation and function of pre-osteoblasts.

Dexamethasone decreases sinusoidal EC count and function

Second, we observed that dexamethasone treatment decreased the percentage of cells in the sinusoidal EC cluster (Figure 4B). This reduction was accompanied by a significant downregulation of genes related to cytoplasmic translation and other GO terms associated with protein production in sinusoidal ECs (Figure S5D-F). Sinusoidal ECs have recently been implicated in bone regeneration, with their function found to decrease in aged animals.27 These findings suggest that dexamethasone-induced reduction in sinusoidal EC counts could impair healthy bone development in skeletally immature mice.

Dexamethasone significantly decreases pro-B and immature B cells in the bone marrow

We further interrogated the effects of dexamethasone on immune cells within the bone marrow (Figures 5A, S6A and B). Overall, the number of immune cells, particularly of B cell lineage cells, in the DEX 50 group was low, prompting us to focus our analysis on the DEX 5 and DEX 20 groups.

Most notably, within the immune cell population, early developing B cells within the pro-B cell and B cell precursor clusters were depleted in all dexamethasone treatment groups, while some further developed immature B cells persisted (Figure 5A and B). As it was not possible to perform differential gene expression analysis on B cell clusters in the dexamethasone-treated groups to confirm cell typing of the different cell clusters, we performed a trajectory analysis on B cell clusters from the vehicle group (Figure S6C). All B cell clusters expressed the marker gene Cd19, and the pro-B cells showed expression of early B cell markers, such as Kit, Spn, and Dntt (Figure S6D). Genes involved in B cell activation, affinity maturation, and B cell receptor development, including H2.Eb1, H2.Aa, H2.Ab1, Cd74, Ighd, Bank1, and Cd83, were among the top 8 genes upregulated along the pseudotime trajectory (Figure S6E).28 Conversely, genes selectively expressed in pro-B cells, like Vpreb1 (CD179a) and Igll1 (CD179b), were downregulated along the pseudotime trajectory (Figure S6E).

We then assessed the impact of dexamethasone on gene expression in the immature B cells of the DEX 5 and DEX 20 groups. We observed the significant downregulation of genes essential for maintaining immature B cell commitment and survival, such as Ebf1, H3f3a, and H3f3b, in dexamethasone-treated mice compared to vehicle-treated controls (Figure 5C).29,30 However, genes associated with cell survival, proliferation, and apoptosis, such as Odc1, Pim1, S100a6, Atp2b1, Tent5c, Fosl2, and Ly6a, were upregulated in response to dexamethasone. As mature B cells typically leave the bone marrow upon completing their development, their presence is usually minimal compared to immature B cells, which is consistent with the absence of mature B cells in our dataset. In summary, these findings demonstrate that dexamethasone treatment in young animals has a detrimental effect on B cell subsets in the bone marrow, leading to a near depletion of early B cell progenitors, while some cells of the more developed immature B cell subset persist.

Dexamethasone increases bone marrow neutrophil activation and innate immunity

Furthermore, we observed an increase in the percentage of activated neutrophils, which is accompanied by an increase of overall neutrophils, following dexamethasone treatment, with the DEX 50 mice having more than double the number of activated neutrophils compared to the control mice (Figure 6A). Despite the changes in the percentage of the activated neutrophil cluster, the neutrophil cluster remained largely the same (Figure 6A). Analysis of DEGs revealed that both neutrophils and activated neutrophil clusters showed similar gene expression across the DEX groups compared to the control group (Figure 6B-C). Specifically, dexamethasone led to the upregulation of inflammatory-related genes, including Cxcl2, Nfkbia, Nfkbiz, transcription factor Jun, and the neutrophil-associated protease Asprv1 in both neutrophil populations (Figure 6B and C). On a broader scale, DEGs in neutrophils and activated neutrophils were linked to processes related to cytokine production, chemotaxis, and pathogen-associated molecular pattern recognition (Figure S7A and B). Additionally, the activated neutrophils upregulated genes associated with keratinocyte proliferation, which, in the context of neutrophils, are more likely involved in granule formation. These include Leucine-rich α2 glycoprotein (Lrg1), a component of neutrophil granules that get secreted upon activation (Figure S7B).31

In accordance with increased neutrophil activation and upregulated inflammation, the endpoint serum of dexamethasone-treated mice showed elevated levels of systemic Eotaxin and GM-GSF, and the inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and G-CSF showed a similar upward trend, but this was not statistically significant (Figure 6D). Altogether, these findings suggest that prolonged daily exposure to dexamethasone enhances bone marrow neutrophil activation and stimulates inflammatory cytokine responses in skeletally young mice.

Discussion

Glucocorticoids cause bone-related side effects in pediatric patients but remain essential for treating ALL and lymphoma.1,6,18 Understanding the molecular mechanisms driving glucocorticoid-induced bone toxicity during skeletal development could aid in managing growth suppression, bone loss, necrosis, and fracture risk during and after treatment, thereby improving the quality of life for pediatric cancer patients and survivors. Glucocorticoid-induced bone loss has been shown to manifest in rodents and humans with comparable cellular and molecular characteristics, making mice a suitable first model to study the effects of glucocorticoids.32 Hence, in this study, we investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying dexamethasone-induced bone toxicity using skeletally immature C57BL/6JRj mice. We treated mice with daily injections of 5, 20, or 50 mg/kg dexamethasone (approximately corresponding to 0.41, 1.62, and 4.1 mg/kg in humans) and evaluated its effects on cell populations of the hindleg long bones after 28 d. Our scRNA-seq analysis reveals consistent cellular alterations across the different doses, including reductions in osteoblasts, chondrocytes, sinusoidal ECs, and B cell progenitors, alongside increased neutrophil activation. Changes were associated with impaired longitudinal bone growth and bone architecture, and increased bone fragility. Notably, while cellular changes were evident even at 5 mg/kg dexamethasone, structural changes in trabecular bone were only detected at 50 mg/kg, indicating, as anticipated, that cellular alterations precede detectable structural changes and may serve as a more sensitive approach for evaluating toxicity. It should be noted that in pediatric oncology, dexamethasone is used at doses of up to 0.5 mg/kg/d, with 0.2-0.3 mg/kg/day commonly administered during the induction phase in pediatric ALL patients. Hence, the higher doses in our study exceed typical clinical practice. The need for higher doses in the used model might be attributed to the variable sensitives of mouse strains to glucocorticoid-induced toxicity, with C57BL/6JRj being less sensitive, and to reduced bone loss in younger mice (<12 wk) following glucocorticoid exposure.33

In adults, dexamethasone-induced bone loss is attributed to an imbalance between bone formation and resorption, characterized by reduced osteoblast activity and increased osteoclast activity.25 In our study with skeletally immature mice, dexamethasone strongly impacted bone-forming osteoblasts, while its adverse effects were unrelated to osteoclast activity. We observed that dexamethasone significantly reduced osteoblasts and downregulated key bone development genes, particularly osteocalcin (Bglap and Bglap2), in cells of the osteoblast lineage. This aligns with clinical observation where therapeutic doses of glucocorticoids rapidly decreased bone formation markers, such as osteocalcin, ALP, and procollagen type I N-propeptide, secreted by osteoblasts.34,35 Similarly, in rodents, glucocorticoid exposure decreases bone formation markers, osteoblast numbers, and bone mineralization.36–39 Intriguingly, glucocorticoids downregulate osteocalcin through the binding of the glucocorticoid-receptor complex to negative response elements in the enhancer region of the osteocalcin promotor,12 explaining their stronger impact on osteocalcin compared to other bone turnover markers. While BMD measurements are recommended for evaluating glucocorticoid effects on bone, bone turnover markers are also being explored.40 Given that glucocorticoids directly, yet differentially, affect the expression of the bone turnover markers, a thorough understanding of the underlying mechanisms is crucial for accurate data interpretation.

Unlike in mature bone, dexamethasone treatment in our skeletally immature mice did not alter osteoclast-related TRAP activity, suggesting that in developing bones, the detrimental effects of dexamethasone are primarily due to reduced bone formation rather than increased bone resorption. Due to challenges in isolating pre-osteoclasts and osteoclasts, we could not compare their RNA profiles across treatment and age groups to confirm this. Nevertheless, this finding has potential implications for pediatric patients with dexamethasone-induced bone loss, as treatments with osteoclast-inhibiting bisphosphonates primarily target basal osteoclast activity, rather than the increased osteoclast activity. Indeed, bisphosphonate efficacy in pediatric glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis is variable and generally less potent than in adults.41,42 However, a placebo-controlled study with 217 participants (4-18 yr) with chronic inflammatory rheumatic disease, showed a positive effect of bisphosphonates on bone density, comparable to adults.43,44 Nevertheless, a Cochrane Systemic Review concluded insufficient evidence to recommend bisphosphonates as standard therapy in children.45 Factors like inflammatory status, age, and bone development stage likely contribute to these variable outcomes and warrant further investigation.

Furthermore, our study revealed that dexamethasone depletes B cell progenitors (ie, pro-B cells, B cell precursors, and immature B cells), which are essential for maintaining a functional immune system. Similar effects have been reported in adult mice and humans treated with single or multiple injections of corticosteroids, where the underlying mechanism is thought to involve the induction of apoptosis across all B cell developmental subsets.46,47 This apoptotic response is exploited in treating childhood cancers like B cell ALL, where glucocorticoids support remission when combined with chemotherapy.48 In our study, dexamethasone downregulated key genes involved in B cell commitment and survival pathways (Ebf1, H3f3a, H3f3b), potentially shifting toward T cell differentiation, as suggested by Ebf1 downregulation and Ly6a upregulation. Upregulated genes in immature B cells (eg, Odc1, Tent5c) were associated with cell survival and proliferation, suggesting that these genes might help prevent glucocorticoid-induced B cell apoptosis. It is well recognized that immune cells within the bone marrow play a crucial role in regulating bone homeostasis and regeneration.49 Stromal cell populations, many of which originate from mesenchymal lineages, interact with immune cells to regulate the development and function of hematopoietic cells. Among the key cell types within the bone marrow niche that support B cell development are osteoblast progenitors and mature osteoblasts. These cells provide essential signals through direct cell-cell interactions and the secretion of critical cytokines and growth factors, including CXCL12, IL-7, CD147, and Galectin-1 (Gal-1). Of note, we did not observe a dexamethasone-induced downregulation of CXCL12 in LEPR+ cells. Additionally, RANKL-RANK signaling is crucial for both osteoclast differentiation and B cell development. In the bone marrow, osteoclasts and B cell precursors express the receptor RANK, while RANKL is produced by osteoblast precursors, mature osteoblasts and other stromal cells. Disruption of this signaling pathway has been shown to lead to reduced B cell populations.50 The decrease in osteoblast populations observed in our study may have contributed to the negative effects of dexamethasone on B cells, and vice versa. These findings underscore the importance of considering the interplay between immune and bone cells in understanding dexamethasone-induced bone toxicity and overall bone health.

A limitation of our study is the exclusive use of male mice, which does not capture potential sex-specific differences in dexamethasone’s effects on bone. As female mice undergo cyclic hormonal changes starting at 6-8 wk of age that significantly influence bone metabolism, we chose to use male mice to reduce experimental variability. However, future studies incorporating both sexes are essential to provide a more comprehensive understanding of drug responses and side effect profiles.
