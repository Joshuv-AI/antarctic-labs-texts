Burnout among postgraduate medical trainees in Lebanon: experiences and prevention strategies
Authors: Aline Yacoubian, Evangelia Demerouti, Sarah Kawtharani, Maya Romani, Albert El Hajj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBurnout is a common problem that negatively impacts the well-being of postgraduate medical trainees. The study aims to explore trainees’ experiences of burnout in different specialties and generate potential strategies for prevention. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with trainees at a tertiary medical center. The inclusion criteria included residents and fellows practicing in the medical center irrespective of gender, age, specialty, and year of training. There were 32 residents classified as surgical specialty versus non-surgical specialty. Interview domains revolved around understanding burnout experiences in residency, causes of burnout, and potential strategies for prevention. Work overload was the most cited reason for burnout. Other emergent factors were understaffing, specialty type, faculty members/peers, economic and political problems, and personal factors. The leadership program is recommended to be aware of the early warning signs of burnout. Effective workload assignments with rest intervals and attention to work-life balance, and to self-care could help prevent burnout and promote overall well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09537-5.Keywords: Burnout, Postgraduate medical graduates, Leadership, Well-being

Introduction

Burnout is a “prolonged job stress” with pragmatic concerns present in all types of occupations [1]. German-American psychologist Herbert Freudenberger introduced the keyword in the 1970s and described it as “failing, wearing out, or becoming exhausted” due to chronic fatigue and frustration brought up by devotion [2, 3]. Burnout corresponds to a syndrome of emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment [4]. It has been linked to patient satisfaction and physician-patient relationships, as well as healthcare outcomes [5]. The condition is prevalent and overwhelming and employees will no longer be able or willing to invest effort in their tasks [1].

Healthcare employees are more susceptible to burnout due to their demanding and challenging responsibilities [6]. The healthcare sector has played a noteworthy role in the history of research on this subject matter, with primary studies conducted on healthcare workers [7]. Burnout was added by the World Health Organization (WHO) to the 11th Revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) as an occupational phenomenon and not as a medical condition [8]. A key challenge in identifying burnout is its theoretical overlap with depression as it is a risk factor to depression [9].

Over four decades of research has yielded broad studies covering its measurement, causes, related factors, consequences, and outcomes. Research shows that postgraduate medical trainees are at higher risk for developing mental health disorders such as burnout. They are vulnerable since they serve both as learners and providers of care for patients during their training journey. For example, the prevalence of burnout is 64.8% in a national sample of 3,741 obstetrics and gynecology residents in the United States (US) [10]. A survey in Belgium among 236 residents revealed that 41.5% had burnout, 53.0% scored high on exhaustion, 53.0% on depersonalization, and 23.4% on reduced personal accomplishment [11].

One of the first attempts to measure burnout in Lebanon was conducted among 155 medical postgraduate medical trainees in 2010 by the Maslach Burnout Inventory that revealed 67.7% in the domain of emotional exhaustion for high burnout [12]. Two more recent studies in a tertiary medical center in Lebanon in 2022 showed high rates among 188 postgraduate medical trainees [13, 14]. The Oldenburg Burnout Inventory indicated that the percentage with high scores was 51.1% for exhaustion and 37.2% for disengagement [13]. The Copenhagen Burnout Inventory showed that the percentage with high scores was 68.6% for personal burnout, 63.3% for work-related burnout, and 35.1% for patient-related burnout [14]. Physicians in Lebanon are also at high risk for burnout and a survey among 398 physicians by the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory in 2020 reported that 90% had moderate and high level of burnout [15]. This implied that higher threat perception and exposure to stressful situations due to the country conditions and uncertainty were correlated with higher burnout [12, 15].

However, little is known about how postgraduate medical trainees experience burnout and what they perceive as risk factors and what potential ways they consider to mitigate it. Managing this problem differs from managing other mental conditions that might manifest similarly. Many trainees would have difficulty differentiating between burnout and fatigue due to other medical or cognitive reasons. This shows that occupational well-being is a necessary professional asset linked to enhanced performance, productivity, and organizational success [16]. Therefore, it is essential to prevent burnout among postgraduate medical trainees to foster their well-being and increase their productivity as well as morale.

The leadership program may consist of the program director, program coordinator, assigned faculty members, heads/chairpersons, and the Graduate Medical Office personnel. This program is responsible for ensuring and maintaining the well-being of trainees [17]. Well-being must be appropriately managed to ensure adequate and proper training for a motivating and healthy work environment to graduate solid physicians [18]. Evaluating and monitoring the workplace environment, identifying arising issues, and providing solutions to prevent burnout is critical [19] especially in a country like Lebanon with an alarming level of burnout among physicians and postgraduate medical trainees [13–15].

The study aims to explore the experiences and insights of postgraduate medical trainees regarding burnout in a developing country with several political and socio-economic challenges. It aims to address the following research questions: What are the experiences and insights of burnout among postgraduate medical trainees during their training? What approaches do postgraduate medical trainees identify as effective solutions for preventing burnout? What strategies can the leadership program implement to successfully mitigate burnout among postgraduate medical trainees? The themes may serve as guidance tools and actionable solutions for postgraduate medical training programs across the globe to prepare not only qualified physicians but also resilient and robust ones.

Method

Research design

A qualitative study was carried out, utilizing semi-structured interviews with postgraduate medical trainees at a tertiary medical center in Beirut, Lebanon. The participants were recruited through the convenience sampling method. Inclusion criteria were any resident or fellow practicing in the center irrespective of gender, age, specialty, and year of training. An email was sent to all participants on May 20, 2024 with the details of the study and the research team’s contact addresses. The email was sent to all postgraduate medical trainees in the medical center through the LimeSurvey portal exclusively supervised by the information technology specialist employed by the institution. Three reminders were sent weekly. Trainees interested in participating contacted the research team, particularly the first author, to set the agenda for a suitable date and time for the interview.

Data collection

A topic guide was prepared and used to allow for a smooth process and lengthy discussion. This interview guide was developed specifically for the study. The questions focused on their overall understanding of burnout, when they learned about it, how they deal with it, how to help burned out trainees, reasons for burnout, and how to prevent burnout. Informed consent and demographic information were obtained at the beginning, and the participants were ensured of the confidentiality and anonymity of the interview. The questions were structured and followed the same order for all participants, but probing was used when needed.

The first author, an expert in overseeing qualitative studies, conducted the interviews in her private office. The interviews were conducted face-to-face or online through the Webex application in a private setting with the participant. A relaxed setting was maintained, comprising two chairs and a table between the participant and the interviewer. The room was quiet and had minimal distractions with adequate lighting and tolerable temperature. Interviews that were audio recorded were transcribed verbatim when permitted. If not, detailed notes were taken. The authors made an effort to include an equal number of participants from each gender and to continue until data saturation was reached.

Data analysis

The first author collected all interviews in a Word document and subjected them to thematic analysis. The third author checked them independently and then these two authors met and agreed on the themes. Thematic analysis is an interpretive process that thoroughly examines data to recognize repeated patterns that rationalize the phenomenon. The analysis focuses on finding, evaluating, and deciphering meaningful outlines in the data to explore the participants’ experiences in detail and depth [20]. The thematic analysis was first conducted question by question and then through holistic analysis by the first author to reflect transparency and rigor. The two authors attempted to look at overall emerging themes as the aim of the study was exploratory rather than comparative. In case of disagreements between the authors, they tried as much as possible to use the participants’ information in an objective way to reach a consensus by an insightful discussion and re-examination of the original data.

Ethical approval

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the university affiliated with the medical center on May 15, 2024. Oral consent was obtained for the recording and transcription of the interviews instead of written consent to protect the identity of the participants so that they did not need to sign their names. By this way, their names did not appear anywhere in the study. The participants were informed that their responses would be remain confidential and would not affect their training or academic assessments.

Results

The final sample consisted of 32 participants who were all residents and no fellows. A description of the participants is presented in Table 1 labelled “Participant characteristics: Gender, age, specialty, and recording of interview”. These residents were from different specialties and years of training. The participants were from postgraduate year one (PGY1) until the sixth year (PGY6). Of these, 16 were males and 16 were females. The participants were classified as surgical versus non-surgical specialties, and the year of training was not reported for confidentiality purposes. The first 21 interviews were conducted between May 20, 2024, and July 18, 2024; the remaining interviews were conducted between September 18, 2024, and November 26, 2024. Data analysis of interviews explored participants’ understanding burnout experiences in residency, causes of burnout, and potential strategies for prevention of burnout. Understanding burnout experiences in residency

There was uniform positive feedback about the overall residency journey. Most participants described their residency training as enjoyable, rewarding, and satisfactory. However, in the mean time they labeled it as overwhelmingly exhausting due to the workload, which required a lot of time and energy and attributed to the numerous roles and responsibilities ranging from patient care to education and research.

“Residency teaches you how to go through hell with a smile. It teaches you how to handle tough situations. We get to know a lot of people that become family and friends.” (Female resident, surgical specialty).

The participants who responded positively described their environment as supportive fostered by faculty members and peers. Remarkably, eight participants described their training as bittersweet, as it induced them to develop independence due to non-constructive criticism and high expectations from faculty. This experience was often equated with a toxic environment, which the participants referred to as “interdisciplinary toxicity.”

All participants agreed that burnout affected them both mentally and physically. Most of them mentioned that they used to go home exhausted and unable to be active or productive. More than half of the participants stated they were introduced to this topic through medical school lectures or orientation sessions at the beginning of their residency training. However, they raised concerns that the topic needed to be addressed more extensively because it could influence other aspects of their lives which were not exclusive to their work.

When reporting burnout to the leadership program, the responses were striking. Only ten residents indicated they would report it, while 22 chose not to. Some refusals stemmed from senior faculty members’ reactions, who considered burnout a normal part of physicians’ training and informed residents to simply “hang in there.” Others avoided reporting due to fear of being perceived as weak. A small number regarded the topic as taboo, equating burnout with a mental disorder and associating it with stigma. Among the older generation of physicians, burnout was not a recognized term during their training, often being mistaken for incompetence or a lack of dedication.

“They do not understand your situation; if you have personal issues or if you have family problems. They do not understand that you have a lot to deal with and that you are already under a lot of stress. Approaching them will be perceived as weakness and make you feel that you are not strong enough or doing enough.” (Male resident, surgical specialty).

Causes of burnout

Work overload was the most cited reason for burnout. This included long hours, demanding assignments, continuous requirements, understaffing, and multi-tasking. Heavy clinical cases drained their energy upon taking care of patients because these trainees indulged too much in the care of the patients, saddened by the morbidity they would be dealing with, as many participants mentioned.

Faculty members were seen as having a dual influence on burnout, with participants expressing contrasting views on their role. On one hand, faculty members could help prevent burnout, but on the other hand, they could also contribute to it. Burnout was more likely when faculty members set high and unrealistic expectations on residents especially for surgical specialties. The participants added harsh communication, such as speaking aggressively in the operating room or being overly critical to them. In addition, a few participants started experiencing bullying from faculty members, wherein they had to grow independently without sufficient support or guidance.

Other participants asserted that they had faculty members who were supportive and understanding. The same was expressed for peers, where some residents reported that being surrounded by colleagues who were not supportive could exacerbate the severity of this condition. There were 28 participants who agreed that the likelihood of burnout increased due to the severe conditions in the country (Coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic, port explosion, economic collapse, political turmoil, earthquake, currency drop, war, etc.). For instance, the fuel crisis made them unable to commute to work in the morning. The electricity cut-offs did not allow residents to prepare for lectures and complete their studies and research work. An act as simple as charging a laptop or a mobile phone could not be accomplished. The financial condition of their patients may also indirectly affect them, because patients may get upset for not being able to afford treatment and project their frustration on the trainees.

The port explosion in August 2020 was a traumatic incident not just for the citizens of Beirut but, most importantly, for the doctors, trainees, and medical staff in Beirut. During the recent conflict in October 2023 in the neighboring countries, many participants expressed worries and anxiety about the uncertainty of maintaining a peaceful environment in the country. Many residents suffered from post-traumatic stress disorder, as mentioned by several participants. A systematic review analyzing 3,957 healthcare workers in 15 cross-sectional studies in Lebanon showed that 35% of these employees experienced post-traumatic stress disorder [21].

Potential strategies for prevention of burnout

Almost all participants highlighted the importance of prioritizing themselves while recognizing this problem. They expressed that although this topic has been extensively researched, proper action has yet to be taken.

“Throughout my five years of residency, I have had this conversation with a lot of residents about how to stand still and avoid dark thoughts such as suicide. You would think you are worthless and too tired to go on anymore. You are tired of waking up and living another day. It is this bad.” (Female resident, surgical specialty).

A participant described the problem of the “elephant in the room,” where the issue was recognized yet no action was taken to address it. This means that there was refusal to acknowledge the presence of this problem and passiveness towards addressing it. Another participant made an exciting note for residents who worked in a non-supportive environment and advised them to be selfish and to take care of themselves to concentrate on their growth. As for coping with stress during residency, each trainee expressed his/her way of handling stress, which included engaging in physical activity, bonding with family members and friends, and participating in outdoor activities and hobbies as well as prayer for conservative ones.

All participants were presented with two questions about how to help burned-out trainees and what to advise new trainees to help prevent it. For the first part, they mentioned that they could help burned-out trainees by offering them some rest days to recover. They added that helping these trainees should push the leadership program to look at the entourage of these trainees, like faculty members they trained with, especially if these faculty members were from the relatively senior generations. As for advice to new trainees, a key feature was to emphasize work-life balance and self-care as much as possible to ensure that life existed outside the walls of the hospital setting and that there was more for them than being just doctors.

Most participants would advise new trainees to be as realistic as possible by understanding their capacity and having a structured plan to perform their hobbies. For example, going to the gym, hanging out with loved ones, playing chess, reading, meditating, praying, etc. during their free time when feasible. As such, without even noticing, their productivity would increase, and their communication skills would improve, and hence, they would unconsciously perform better. Although all participants mentioned that they could not sustain a proper work-life balance, they agreed that it was cardinal to maintain it as much as possible. They discussed the significance of respecting boundaries between personal life and professional life.

The participants had different answers about their awareness of resources for mitigating burnout. These ranged from no knowledge to surveys and wellness activities (like yoga sessions or laughing sessions) that they considered helpful. Others mentioned that they did not wish to spend time with their colleagues and would rather spend time with people outside their workplace. Although they saw their colleagues every day and respected them, they still did not wish to spend time with them. For example, a participant reported hesitancy and discomfort in yoga classes with her colleagues. Time was a restraint since many asked for a proper schedule such as a time slot for those interested in engaging in wellness activities.

Other exciting insights were that if a resident (who was already burned out) was told to go hiking, this would be physically exerting and would not help him/her overcome this issue. The cost of such activities was high as there were other centers with comparable costs as that of their center. These wellness activities are valid options, but well-being is personalized; for some residents, it might be physical activity, while for others, it is family bonding, and for the rest, spirituality and prayer. Some participants specified that sustainability was an issue for wellness activities. This means that such activities should remain optional for those willing to participate.

As for those who had positive feedback about wellness activities, they stated that such activities would decrease vulnerability and increase tolerance. This means that these previously considered stressors would no longer be stressors as the trainees’ perspective would change because they would become more mindful and calmer. Other participants believed that everyone had a different way of coping with stress. Many participants asserted that social gatherings were essential as they would release tension and help them interact with faculty members and peers outside the work domain. However, some participants did not agree with the idea and gave the excuse that they did not want to meet with the people they worked with. Others perceived these outings as unrelated to burnout prevention and did not see a connection.

Most participants did not see a correlation between salary and burnout because these were perceived as unrelated to each other. They explained that this condition was considered a personal problem rather than a financial one. Other participants complained that their salaries were low, especially since they should be financially independent at this stage. They reported that their parents still supported them financially and paid for their international exams or electives. Many participants voiced that some departments used to pay for the exams of their residents, or at least a portion of it and they hoped that the leadership program would also do the same.

“As a resident, I am not looking for making money. I aim to learn and be financially independent, so they should be able to recognize what kind of income is enough for the residents to lead an independent life.” (Male resident, surgical specialty).

Many participants disagreed with reducing the hours to less than the allotted Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) 80-hour work limit per week. The 80-hour work is averaged over four weeks, to enhance patient safety and residents’ well-being and education [22]. They explained that the 80 h were doable and acceptable but highlighted the shortage of manpower or understaffing, which means the need for more residents. The same goes for taking more annual leaves, where many participants stated that they did not think this would help alleviate burnout because of shortage in coverage (if they considered the long-term prevention plan). A few opted to have more vacations to rest, unwind, and return to work with energy without giving suggestions for the long term.

Discussion

This is the first study to explore the perceptions of postgraduate medical trainees regarding the risk factors of burnout and potential ways of prevention in a medical center in Lebanon. The common interview topics are understanding burnout experiences in residency, reasons, and strategies for prevention. The most pointed contributor to burnout is workload and long working hours. Similarly, notions of overwork and over-burden among trainees have been identified in other studies [12, 15, 22–24]. As noted in another study, time spent on administrative tasks at the expense of patient care would lead to a diminished sense of meaning in trainees’ work [25]. This was also expressed by many participants in this study. Understaffing and under-resourcing generated a feeling of guilt for taking a vacation and was perceived as destructive to the team in this study. This was also noted in the literature [23, 24, 26].

The study indicates that trainees feel more valuable when they have a supportive environment with faculty members and peers, especially during difficult times. This will also lead to more productivity in their training. Faculty members could help by providing constructive feedback and objective evaluation, establishing empathy and rapport, offering positive communication and appreciation. Such factors can contribute to building competence and achieving practical learning goals and overcoming allostatic overload. Peers could help by providing friendly support, avoiding toxic relationships, and genuinely being there for each other.

The study underlines that work-life balance and self-care are crucial for fostering resilience, promoting well-being, and preventing burnout. Other studies also emphasize work-life balance and self-care during training [23–25]. This reflects the necessity of avoiding the prospect of achieving perfection in training, as it may aggravate burnout. The participants in this study believed that they were expected to attain more by working excessively at the expense of their well-being. Another study revealed such an “erosion of an idealized view of medicine” among residents in the US which was considered a stressor [27].

Furthermore, salary was not shown to be as important as other factors, such as working hours even amidst financial difficulties. Most participants noted that salary and the need to take more vacations may play a possible role in mitigating the symptoms of burnout. Still, the crucial part is in the primary factors, including work-life balance and proper allocation of duties and functions which seemed sufficient for well-being. The only part in which the trainees requested financial assistance was for electives or international exams. The cost was high (ticket, accommodation, and exam fees, etc.). As for wellness programs, there were mixed reviews regarding the actual effect of such wellness activities on preventing burnout and this is also shown by other studies wherein these activities seem helpful but not meaningly impactful on burnout [28].

Recommendations for the leadership program

Residency has tradionally been conceptualized as a period to “survive” rather than to flourish, grow, and thrive. As physicians mature, they would develop an appreciation for the more fulfilling and meaningful aspects of medicine [27]. The leadership program is recommended to render the training journey smooth and enjoyable through strategies that enhance the resistance to burnout and improve the overall quality of training programs. Literature also indicate that organizational factors are more imperative to burnout prevention rather than individual attempts [26].

The leadership program could first learn the early signs and symptoms of burnout to help the trainees accordingly. By this way they can warn the trainees before reaching the advanced stage, which means needing specialized intervention at that phase. Since working hours are a significant cause, the leadership program could pay attention to identifying residents who may be at higher risk when they start working longer hours. The leadership program may consider increasing the number of residents or in-house physicians to reduce working hours.

The culture of respect, support, and camaraderie could be accentuated. This entails continuously advising and reminding faculty members and senior peers to avert the toxic culture of reprimanding or shouting or blaming. The leadership program is recommended to fix the whole chain, starting from the highest level and down by teaching the relatively older generations that this “thing” or condition that they passed through is something real and backed up by research; it is not like “the resident is getting spoiled”. Faculty members could consider becoming trained coaches (or acquire coaching skills by a skilled coach) to help trainees unlock their maximum potential for professional development and personal growth. This can be achieved through a results-based and strength-based approach and constructive feedback aimed at skill enhancement, performance improvement, and adaptability to external changes.

Faculty members could continuously engage in active listening, positive communication, and powerful questioning with empathy and respect. The leadership program could pay attention to the specialty of trainees (surgical versus non-surgical), whereby the results of this study indicate that surgery residents suffered the most from burnout because of the additional heavy workload and hours spent in the operation room.

Limitations

Due to the LimeSurvey portal, there was a lack of demographic information about the number of invitations sent to eligible trainees in the medical center, which is recognized as a limitation. The authors were unable to find out the number of participants who refused participation as they had been contacted by those who agreed to participate. The authors believe that the reason for refusal may be workload demands and scheduling constraints.

The participants were informed from the beginning that participation was completely voluntary, that their responses would remain confidential and anonymous, and that no identifying information would be linked to their quotes. The authors acknowledge that, despite such careful measures, social desirability bias and self-report may still have influenced some participant responses, especially given the sensitive nature of this topic - burnout and its self-reported experiences. This can affect authenticity of their responses in case they answered in a socially acceptable way.

Due to the qualitative nature of the study, the findings cannot be generalizable because the perspectives of postgraduate medical trainees were explored only. Consequently, it cannot disclose the perceptions of other healthcare providers such as physicians, medical students, nurses, etc. The sample size was small owing to the explorative nature of the study. The study did not report on the findings of other medical centers in the country or region. Thus, it is impossible to indicate whether other trainees have similar or different experiences, but we would only assume so.

The study used a thematic analysis approach, reporting the themes analyzed for semantic and latent meanings. The participants may have felt hesitant to express some views even though a great effort was made to keep them comfortable, but burnout is a sensitive topic. The recruitment was conducted voluntarily, and this may reflect self-selection bias. Future studies could employ a more in-depth approach, such as phenomenology or grounded theory, for additional linguistic data analysis. This would generate interventions directed at the social settings wherein trainees practice and learn.

Conclusion

Burnout is a widespread problem that prevails among postgraduate medical trainees and, more significantly, in a country like Lebanon. It is an organizational issue; hence, the leadership program should address the demands confronted and the resources accessible. The study demonstrates how the current social, health, and financial issues and the unpredictability of external conditions severely affect such training programs. To prevent burnout, the leadership program, and trainees alike could be educated to recognize its signs, understand its contributing factors, and implement targeted interventions. The following actionable objectives are recommended for medical centers:

Raise Awareness and Foster Balanced Workloads: Implement structured initiatives to educate trainees and staff about the seriousness of burnout, stress management techniques, and coping strategies. Develop plans to balance professional and personal responsibilities by delegating tasks and optimizing task distribution with appropriate and minimizing unnecessary administrative burdens.
