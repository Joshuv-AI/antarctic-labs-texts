Clinical and neurocognitive correlates of obsessive-compulsive personality disorder symptoms: A transdiagnostic study in individuals with compulsive behaviors
Authors: H Nur Eken, Shabnam Hossein, Apphia Freeman, Danielle Nebril, Kevin Do-Nguyen, Elizabeth Bell, Luna Malloy, Mohemmed Aslam, Nastasia Mcdonald, Marlee Renard, Victoria Shaffer, Crystal Spotts, Rebecca B Price
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObsessive-Compulsive Personality Disorder (OCPD) is characterized by a lifelong pattern of excessive attention to detail, rigidity and perfectionism. Although OCPD was historically linked to Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders (OCRDs), this connection lacks consistent evidence. Compulsive behaviors, core to OCRDs, are repetitive actions aimed at reducing distress. To elucidate mechanistic links between OCPD and compulsive behaviors, this study examined relationships between OCPD traits, OCRD symptom domains, and cognitive functioning.MethodsIn a transdiagnostic sample of 229 adults with reported compulsive behaviors (CBs) enrolled in a larger clinical trial, we assessed maladaptive OCPD traits using the Pathological Obsessive Compulsive Personality Scale (POPS) and examined their associations with a range of OCRD self-report scales, including measures of hair pulling and skin picking. We evaluated cognitive performance using two NIH toolbox tests and a “two-step” reinforcement learning task.ResultsOCPD symptom scores correlated positively with OCD symptom severity on the OCI-R (r = 0.42, p < .001) and YBOCS (r = 0.38, p < .001). Higher OCPD scores were also associated with having a larger number of discrete OCRD symptom domains (r = 0.39, p < .001), and with higher scores across the majority of OCRD scales examined. OCPD scores, unlike OCD scores, showed no significant association with Flanker test or two-step task performance.DiscussionSeverity of pathological OCPD traits was related to overall higher severity of OCD symptoms, and linked to several individual domains of OCRDs. Neurocognitive indices showed modest relationships with OCRD symptom severity but not with OCPD. These findings contribute to our understanding of how these phenotypes may co-occur, and help guide future research.Keywords: Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder, Obsessive-compulsive and related disorders, Executive functioning, Model-based learning, Cognitive task performance

Introduction

Obsessive-Compulsive Personality Disorder (OCPD) is a personality disorder which is characterized by a lifelong pattern of excessive attention to detail, rigidity and perfectionism [1]. Cognitive and behavioral features associated with OCPD include indecisiveness, procrastination, difficulty with change, and emotional reactivity to loss of one’s sense of control [2]. OCPD is the most common personality disorder, with prevalence reported between 1.9 % and 7.8 %, and leads to significant impairment in functioning including professional challenges due to excessive perfectionism and procrastination, as well as interpersonal challenges resulting from inflexible ways of living [2].

Historically, OCPD was thought to be linked to Obsessive Compulsive Disorder (OCD). Specifically, the personality traits that define OCPD were thought to predispose an individual to develop OCD [3]. However, this was later refuted, with evidence from various studies demonstrating that individuals with OCD do not necessarily have an over-representation of obsessive personality traits [4] and that only a subset of individuals with OCD also meet criteria for OCPD and vice versa [5], [6]. Research investigating the overlap between OCPD and OCD has had conflicting findings. According to a systematic review from 2021, OCPD was present in 25 % of the individuals with OCD, generally consistent with the rates reported in DSM-IV [3]. This highlights a higher degree of comorbidity than with any other personality disorder, although it still represents a minority of the individuals diagnosed with OCD [7]. Notably, this study found that OCD symptoms were less severe in individuals with both OCPD and OCD diagnoses than in those with OCD alone. One study showed that OCPD diagnosis and severity were shown to predict worse outcomes with exposure and response prevention treatment for OCD, specifically when the perfectionism criteria within the OCPD diagnosis was present [8]. In terms of functional impairment in OCD, some studies showed that individuals with comorbid OCPD have significantly more impairment, suggesting that OCD with comorbid OCPD might present a distinct and particularly disabling clinical subtype [5], [9].

Compulsive behaviors are unwanted, repetitive behaviors aimed at reducing distress despite adverse consequences, and represent a core feature of obsessive-compulsive and related disorders (OCRD), a category that includes obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD), hoarding disorder, body dysmorphic disorder, hair pulling disorder and skin picking disorder, as well as others [1]While current diagnostic manuals describe OCD as a singular entity, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, as in many other psychiatric disorders, are heterogenous [10]. Several principal dimensions of OCD have been identified including obsessions around harm, contamination, scrupulosity (concerning sexual/moral/religious obsessions), symmetry and “just right” perceptions, and somatic obsessions, and compulsions associated with each of these obsessions [10], [11], [12]. This subtyping approach allows for more specificity in the identification of individuals with a wide variety of symptom profiles; however, the inherent heterogeneity is still linked to delays in diagnosing OCD and related disorders (OCRDs) among individuals who do not meet a prototypical symptom phenotype [13]. Furthermore, some disagreement remains around the categorization of the symptom profiles, with factor-analytic studies identifying different numbers of symptom dimensions in different studies [14], [15]. Genetic studies have also supported this dimensional understanding of OCD, highlighting that OCD has a polygenic structure [16].

In the past decade, researchers have investigated the psychological and cognitive mechanisms underlying OCD and compulsive behaviors at large. It was initially thought that OCD might be related to faulty value attribution to alternative behaviors, but more recent work has demonstrated that OCD is likely associated with overreliance on habit formation over goal-directed learning [17], [18], [19]. This view also aligns with the purported neurocircuitry involved in compulsive behaviors and OCD, namely the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuit [20]. While several neurocognitive substrates have been documented in the context of OCRDs, including alterations in cognitive flexibility [21], reward processing, and habit formation [22], few studies have investigated the cognitive or neural underpinnings of OCPD, and studies of OCPD have typically relied on small sample sizes. As a result, it remains unclear whether the neurocognitive substrates of OCPD and OCRDs are shared or distinct. Similarly, although there are some surface-level features shared between OCRDs and OCPD, such as rigidity/inflexibility [23], the degree to which specific subtypes within the highly heterogeneous array of OCRD presentations might be differentially linked to OCPD traits is unknown. These gaps in the scientific knowledge base limit both clinicians and patients in their ability to accurately conceptualize idiosyncratic symptom profiles, and therefore, the ability to both develop and efficiently administer targeted treatments.

In this current transdiagnostic study of 229 individuals exhibiting clinically significant compulsive behaviors (i.e., a range of OCRDs), we aimed to explore the phenomenology and clinical correlates of OCPD by examining the relationships between specific OCRD symptom domains and the presence and severity of OCPD traits. In addition, we investigated the relationship between OCPD symptoms and dimensions of cognitive functioning in our well-powered sample, using two standardized tests of executive functioning (from the NIH Toolbox), as well as a widely used measure of model-based learning (the “two-step task”) that has been previously reliably linked to transdiagnostic compulsivity [24], but not previously investigated in relation to OCPD symptoms.

Methods

Participants and study procedures

Baseline data from 229 individuals enrolled in a clinical trial (NCT04580043) on compulsive behaviors (CBs) were analyzed. Briefly, this is an ongoing clinical trial that uses neuromodulation to attempt to delineate a causal neurocognitive model for CBs in humans. As part of the clinical trial, baseline clinical data including a battery of psychiatric and cognitive assessments have been obtained, and were used for all present analyses.

Participants were recruited through online advertisements for a clinical research study of compulsive behaviors (parent trial registered at: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04580043). Participants were eligible if they were between 18 and 60 years old, endorsed problematic compulsive behaviors per self-report (i.e., >1 SD above the published average of healthy controls on 1 or more of our included OCRD self-report measures) and clinician administered (i.e., scored ‘moderate’ or greater on ≥2 items in the compulsions subscale of the YBOCS interview) measures, agreed to video taping of structured clinical interview, and reported that they would be residing in the Pittsburgh area for at least 5 weeks to overlap with the completion of the parent study.

At the time of analysis, there were a total of 244 individuals who had signed informed consent and provided at least partial baseline (pre-treatment) data in the parent clinical trial and were considered for inclusion in the present analyses. All available baseline data from a total of 229 individuals were included in the present interim analysis after applying the following exclusion criteria: reading level < 6th grade per participant report, presence of movement disorders or tics affecting manual responses, inability to read text from 2 feet away, acute suicidality or other psychiatric crises requiring treatment escalation, history of autism spectrum disorders, active psychosis spectrum disorders, active problematic (moderate-to-severe) substance use disorders. Of note, to maximize sample size, individuals who were excluded from the parent clinical trial due strictly to exclusion criteria pertaining to ensuring the safety of the study intervention (e.g., medical exclusions for neuromodulation) were not excluded from the present report, as these were not relevant to our analyses. Clinical measures

The assessments of interest for the present analysis included several validated self-report scales which were selected to capture a range of heterogeneous symptom domains prevalent across transdiagnostic OCRDs: the Obsessive Compulsive Inventory-Revised, which is an 18-item self-report scale assessing for obsessive and compulsive symptoms across 6 discrete subscales including washing, checking, hoarding, obsessing, ordering and neutralizing [25], Massachusetts General Hospital Hair Pulling Scale, a 7-item self report scale that assesses trichotillomania (hair pulling) behaviors (MGH-HPS [26]), the skin picking scale (SPS), a 6-item scale that evaluates skin picking behavior [27]; and the Threat-Related Reassurance Seeking Scale (TRSSS), an 8-item scale that assesses reassurance seeking behavior in response to evaluative and general threats [28] To measure severity of OCPD traits, we used the Pathological Obsessive Compulsive Personality Scale (POPS), a 49-item self-report scale assessing for OCPD traits across 5 dimensions, including difficulty with change, emotional overcontrol, rigidity, maladaptive perfectionism, and reluctance to delegate [29]YBOCS interviews were conducted by trained raters demonstrating adequate inter-rater reliability on interview measures (ICC’s > .8) under the direct supervision of a licensed clinical psychologist (RBP).

All measures are published in English and have been psychometrically validated previously, demonstrating adequate reliability and internal and external validity for use in clinical populations (see each measure’s original citation above for further details).

Neuropsychological measures

Neuropsychological measures were collected in a subset of n = 187 eligible participants who proceeded further in the protocol of baseline assessments. We used NIH Toolbox assessments to assess three dimensions of cognition. We employed scores from the Dimensional Change Card Sort (DCCS) and the Flanker Inhibitory Control and Attention Test (Flanker) to assess executive function, measuring flexible goal-directed cognition and inhibitory control, respectively [30]. The DCCS presents two target cards that vary on color and shape; participants sort a series of test cards first by one rule and then by the other, with “switch” trials imposing set-shifting demands. Performance is scored with an accuracy/reaction-time composite, which shows excellent test–retest reliability in adults [31].

The Flanker Test requires participants to focus on a particular stimulus and inhibit attention to incongruent flanking stimuli. It is scored with a combined accuracy/speed algorithm and also demonstrates excellent test–retest reliability.

Finally, the Pattern Comparison Processing Speed Test assesses processing speed of information by requiring rapid “same/different” judgments for simple visual pairs; the measure is brief, shows age-sensitive performance across the lifespan, and demonstrates good test–retest reliability [31], [32]. For all three tests, NIH Toolbox provides age-adjusted and (for adults) age-and-education–adjusted standardized scores based on national norms [33]. All NIH Toolbox assessments were conducted on an iPad (Apple, Inc) running iPadOS 13 or higher.

Two-step decision making task

Participants completed 100 trials of an adapted version of the two-step task [34] designed to assess model-free and model-based learning, as used previously in studies of compulsivity [19], [24], on a study laptop computer. In the first step, participants had to choose between two rockets which probabilistically traveled to one of two planets. Each rocket traveled to a preferred planet 70 % of the time (‘common’ transition) or to the other planet 30 % of the time (‘rare’ transition). Next, participants had to choose between two aliens, each with a unique probability of being rewarded. The probability of being rewarded drifted slowly and independently for each alien over the course of the task. In both stages, participants had up to 2500 ms to respond. In this task, model-based and model-free learning have distinctive behavioral signatures. A pure model-based learner considers estimates of how rewarding each alien is and how likely each rocket is to travel to the planet where that alien resided. Behaviorally, this is expressed by an interaction between reward and transition type, and reflects a greater degree of goal-directed decision making. By contrast, a pure model-free learner only considers previous reinforcement and is characterized by a main effect of reward.

Data analysis

Data processing

In order to assess the degree to which elevated OCPD traits were characteristic of specific participants presenting with different OCRD presentations within our transdiagnostic clinical sample, we calculated each participant’s Z-score on each self-report symptom scale, expressed as the number of standard deviations above or below a healthy control sample’s mean score on that scale, using previously published normative data for each of our self-report symptom scales: OCI total scores and subscales [35], MGH-HPS [26], SPS [27], and TRRSS [28]. We then dichotomized each scale/subscale into Z ≥ 1 and Z < 1, denoting relatively “high” and “low” scores on each OCRD category/domain. We also created a composite variable, “OCRD domain load,” that summed up the number of discrete OCRD domains (i.e., OCI subscales, MGH-HPS, SPS, TRRSS) for which a participant’s Z score was ≥ 1, providing a measure of each participant’s overall symptom ‘load’ and the heterogeneity or range of symptoms that each individual expressed across the unique OCRD domains that were assessed.

Two-step task data were analyzed using a hierarchical logistic regression model implemented by the lme4 R package. The model explains stay or switch (coded 1,0) behavior, from the type of transition on the last trial (coded common=1, rare=-1), whether that trial was rewarded (coded rewarded=1, unrewarded=0), and their interaction. Reward, transition and their interaction were included as random effects. The outcome of interest, model-based planning, is quantified by the interaction between reward and transition. Each individual’s subject-level reward*transition coefficient [“model-based index” (MBI)] was stored for use in further statistical analyses of individual differences (below).

Statistical analysis

In the full sample, we conducted correlations and partial correlations to assess for overall relationships between OCPD symptom severity (POPS scores) and OCRD symptom severity (total OCI and YBOCS scores), as well as relationships between OCPD and OCRD symptom severity and performance on cognitive tests (NIH Toolbox task T-scores; two-step task MBI).

We also conducted independent-sample T-tests to assess for significant differences in mean POPS scores across the “high” and “low” groups for each specific OCRD dimension. We additionally used nonparametric correlations to investigate relationships between total number of OCRD symptom domains (“load”) and POPS scores.

Next, we performed hierarchical regression analyses to better understand how neurocognitive test performance (DCCS, Flanker, and two-step task scores) related to a set of independent variables, including demographic factors, processing speed, POPS scores, and OCI scores. In Model 1, we included age, biological sex, and processing speed; in Model 2, we added POPS scores; and in Model 3, we added OCI scores. Finally, we conducted t-tests to compare the strength of the associations (standardized slopes and SE from the final regression models) between neurocognitive test scores and the two clinical predictors: OCPD (POPS scores) and OCRD (OCI scores) symptoms.

We performed statistical analyses using IBM SPSS statistics, version 28.0.1.1 and R (version 4.2.3).

Mean age of the sample was 30.89 (ranging from 18 to 60, SD 10.17). 201 participants (87.8 %) reported meeting criteria for OCD on the diagnostic interview. We found a significant positive correlation between total POPS scores and OCI-R total scores, (r = .42, p < .001, see Fig. 1). We also found a significant positive correlation between total POPS scores and total YBOCS scores (r = .38, p < .001), as well as the YBOCS obsessions subscale total score (r = .33, p < .001), and compulsions subscale total score (r = .33, p < .001). We additionally found that there was a moderate positive correlation between the POPS score and the OCRD domain “load” (count of Z scores ≥1; r = .390, p < .001), demonstrating that the more unique OCRD symptom domains an individual exhibited, the higher their OCPD score.

Relationship between OCPD severity and cognitive test scores

POPS scores and DCCS T-scores (an index of flexible goal-directed cognition) were not significantly associated (r = -.07, p = .343). POPS scores also did not significantly correlate with the Flanker test T-scores (an index of inhibitory control; (r = -.06, p = .416). Both relationships remained non-significant in partial correlations including the processing speed task T-score as a covariate (DCCS: r = .018, p = .812; Flanker Inhibitory Control Test: r = .037, p = .619).

Likewise, on the two-step task measuring model-based learning, we did not observe a significant relationship between MBI and POPS total score (p > 0.5).

Relationship between OCRD severity and cognitive test scores

Total OCI score and DCCS T-scores were not significantly associated (r = -.076, p = .297), however, OCI score did significantly and negatively correlate with Flanker test T-scores (r = -192, p = .008). Results remained consistent in partial correlations including the Processing Speed task T-score as a covariate (DCCS: r = -.049, p = .499; Flanker: r = -.190, p-=.009).

On the two-step task we observed a significant negative correlation between OCI-R total score and MBI (r(185) = -0.152, p = 0.036). We also saw a negative association between the total YBOCS score and MBI (r(186) = -0.148, p = 0.042), where the relationship was mainly driven by the compulsivity subset (r(186) = -0.181, p = 0.012) compared to the obsession subset (p > 0.2).

Collectively, these findings replicate prior research consistently showing that individual differences in compulsivity are related to cognitive deficits such as model-based planning and inhibition, and further suggest such deficits are relatively specific to OCRD severity, but do not extend to OCPD symptom severity.

Discussion

In this study among 229 patients with clinically significant compulsive behaviors, we demonstrated that scores on the POPS scale, which measures severity of pathological OCPD traits, were modestly associated with overall higher severity of OCD symptoms, and were linked to several individual domains of OCD and other transdiagnostic OC-related disorders (OCRDs); but conversely, OCPD traits were not universally elevated among every OCRD domain that was measured. Specifically, we found that there were statistically significant associations between OCPD scale scores and individuals with elevated symptoms of the washing, ordering, hoarding, neutralizing and checking dimensions, as well as with an increased tendency to seek reassurance in response to perceived threats. However, OCPD symptoms were not specifically elevated in those with high scores for the ‘obsessing’ domain (capturing the severity of intrusive thoughts), or among those with high scores for hair pulling or skin picking. These results suggest that, in our transdiagnostic sample of patients presenting with heterogeneous phenotypes of clinically significant OCRD symptoms, OCPD traits co-occurred in the context of many, but not all, OCRD presentations. Of note, our study is one of the first to examine these associations between OCRD and OCPD traits in a sample of this size.

It is notable that the “obsessing” dimension of the OCI was the only subscale for which the OCPD scores did not differ significantly across “high” and “low” groups. Neurocognitive evidence suggests that OCPD is characterized by deficits in cognitive flexibility and executive planning processes [36], which could be viewed as more closely tied to compulsive rituals than to intrusive thoughts. However, we also found that OCPD symptom severity correlated with both the obsessional and compulsion subscales of YBOCS. These findings may partly reflect differences in measurement, as the OCI’s obsessing subscale is more limited in scope and emphasizes intrusive thoughts unrelated to goal-directed behavior, contrasting with YBOCS obsessions which are idiographically and comprehensively defined during the interview, and therefore may encompass a wider range of intrusive thought content (e.g., preoccupations with symmetry, moral scrupulosity themes) which may be more congruent with OCPD’s cognitive rigidity.

Existing literature is mixed on the association between OCRD and OCPD traits. Very few studies to date have empirically tested the phenomenological overlap between these two classes of symptoms, which are diagnosed on separate ‘Axes’ of the DSM nosology. Early psychodynamic theorists believed that OCPD traits predated the development of OCD [37], but modern psychological work has not explored the link between the two diagnoses in depth, and the general consensus in the field is that OCRDs and OCPD are distinct entities with no clear overlap in etiology or underlying pathophysiology [38]. Our study in a large, transdiagnostic cohort of patients with clinically impairing compulsive traits shows that several specific phenomenological aspects of these disorders are modestly related. These findings may inform hypotheses for future research and potentially guide treatment approaches.

A trait that is noted to be shared between OCRDs and OCPD is rigidity, which raises the possibility of overlapping cognitive patterns, though this overlap has not been conclusively established. Rigidity in OCD has been studied extensively from a cognitive neuroscience lens. Individuals with OCD are posited to suffer from cognitive inflexibility, a concept with different aspects and demonstrated through corresponding experimental tasks, such as the Wisconsin Card Sorting Task, the Intradimensional/Extradimensional Shift Task, reversal learning, and tasks focusing on goal directed vs habitual learning, to name a few [19], [21]. Several studies have demonstrated significant deficits in performance-based measures of cognitive flexibility among individuals with OCD, as well as in compulsivity more broadly construed [24].

Rigidity in OCPD has been studied to a lesser extent, and in smaller samples. A study recruiting a community sample in the UK demonstrated that individuals who met diagnostic criteria for OCPD made significantly more errors in assessments testing for set shifting, executive planning and decision making [36]. In our study, we did not find any associations between performance in NIH toolbox assessments examining similar concepts and higher scores on the OCPD scales. Specifically, the DCCS is an executive function test that assesses cognitive flexibility and the capacity to adapt one’s thinking and behavior in response to new circumstances. While rigidity is one of the core features of OCPD, individuals who scored high on the POPS scale did not have worse scores on DCCS. One possible interpretation is that rigidity in OCPD may manifest differently than the cognitive inflexibility measured by these tasks, and thus may not indicate a generalized worsening of executive function to the same degree as in OCD. However, in the present sample, DCCS task performance was also unrelated to OCRD symptom severity, and thus may have simply failed to capture clinically meaningful variance in cognition due to measurement error, inadequate convergent validity, or other limitations.

By contrast, two other neurocognitive indices showed moderate relationships in our sample with OCRD symptom severity, as expected; yet notably, these relationships nevertheless failed to generalize to the OCPD symptom domain. Flanker Inhibitory Control and Attention Test is a measure of inhibitory control and attention. During this task, participants are required to suppress irrelevant information. In our transdiagnostic sample, individuals with higher scores on OCD traits (OCI scale) were more likely to underperform on the task than those with lower scores. In line with the existing literature on compulsivity in the context of OCRDs, these participants with higher OCI scores also showed impaired model-based performance on the two-step task, highlighting that they struggle with model-based learning and goal-directed decision making. Yet, in our study, neither of these two cognitive measures showed significant associations with OCPD scores. It is possible that OCPD symptoms might reflect distinct underlying cognitive processes, and could even promote certain benefits during cognitive task performance, which should be systematically investigated in future research. It is also possible that the neurocognitive tasks available in this study did not adequately capture the nuances in the cognitive patterns in OCPD. Future studies might include other cognitive flexibility tasks, such as Intra-Extradimensional (IED) Set-Shifting Task [39] as well as self-report questionnaires like the Cognitive Flexibility Inventory [40] to investigate particular deficits in executive functioning that might be present in individuals with OCPD.

Our results also highlight a potential distinction between compulsive behavior symptom severity and OCPD traits in their relationship to model-based planning. The observed negative correlation between compulsivity (captured on the YBOCS) and model-based index (MBI) aligns with prior work implicating reduced goal-directed control in compulsive behavior disorders. Specifically, in our study, individuals with higher compulsivity were more likely to rely on model-free (habit-driven) learning, consistent with the theory that compulsive actions in OCRDs may reflect a failure to integrate contextual information during decision-making. This is in keeping with the dominant neurocognitive model of OCD, where cortico-striatal circuitry alterations might predispose individuals to habitual rather than flexible, goal-oriented behaviors [20]. In contrast, the absence of a significant relationship between OCPD traits (POPS scores) and MBI suggests that the rigidity characteristic of OCPD might operate through cognitive mechanisms distinct from the reinforcement learning deficits observed in OCRDs. In fact, after accounting for OCRD severity (in the simplified regression model; Table 3d), a trend emerged in the opposing direction—i.e., OCPD symptoms were weakly (and non-significantly) associated with better model-based learning performance. While compulsivity in OCRDs appears tied to disrupted model-based planning, OCPD’s hallmark traits—perfectionism, emotional overcontrol, and inflexibility—might instead be better understood as forms of rule-bound thinking, and may instead reflect rigidity in rule-bound thinking rather than in procedural learning or decision-making. This distinction is supported by neuroimaging studies linking OCPD to prefrontal cortical dysfunction (e.g., excessive top-down control [6], whereas OCRDs are more strongly tied to striatal habit systems [41].

In general, research on many of the personality disorders remains sparse. Unfortunately, OCPD traits lead to significant functional impairment, and while psychotherapy is the treatment of choice for most cases, to date there is no empirically validated gold-standard treatment for OCPD, with no evidence favoring specific psychotherapeutic interventions for OCPD in particular [42]. Understanding clinical correlates and underlying etiology is therefore essential for designing effective treatments. Identifying symptom dimensions that tend to co-occur strongly with OCPD traits (such as hoarding and other compulsive behavior domains) might allow clinicians develop tailored interventions, including CBT related interventions such as ERP, as well as novel mechanistic interventions geared at neurocognitive targets, such as neuromodulation, and potential synergistic behavioral strategies like habit override training [17], [19] which may be beneficial transdiagnostically.

Our study has some limitations. First, we did not aim to recruit a clinical sample that met DSM criteria for OCPD, and opted to rely on self-reported OCPD traits. It is possible that the results for the cognitive tests might be different had the participants been recruited specifically with a clinical diagnosis of OCPD. However, we used the POPS scale which has been validated for the assessment of OCPD, and has been widely used in prior work [43], [44]. Future studies should broaden this line of inquiry to include clinical diagnostic interviews as well as additional OCPD scales (e.g., Compulsive Personality Assessment Scale [36] Five Factor Obsessive Compulsive Inventory [45] to assess the generalizability of findings. Recruitment targeted transdiagnostic individuals reporting clinically significant compulsive behaviors, a core feature of several mental health conditions including all OCRDs, which allowed us to parse heterogeneity across a broad range of OCRD presentations, with generalizability of findings to a wide range of real-world, treatment-seeking clinical populations; yet this design also limits the specificity of conclusions to any one diagnostic group or subgroup. In addition, while our sample includes individuals from a range of racial and ethnic backgrounds, the vast majority of the participants identified as white, non-Hispanic, and female. Future studies with higher representation of racial, ethnic and cultural minorities are crucial to understand their perspectives and any salient differences in clinical presentations, especially in disorders like OCD where the phenomenology of the symptoms can differ significantly based on an individual's cultural background [46]. Finally, it is important to emphasize that this was a correlational study and, therefore, cannot establish causal or mechanistic relationships. Future research should use longitudinal or experimental designs to directly examine potential causal links between OCPD traits and cognitive domains.

Conclusion

Here in a sample of 229 individuals reporting compulsive behaviors, we demonstrate that there are significant positive associations between several OCRD symptom domains and OCPD traits, indicating that these constructs may co-occur clinically among a heterogenous range of patients. Future studies should explore potential mechanistic mediators for this link. At the same time, we identified specific OCRD domains (“obsessing” domain of OCD, hair pulling, and skin picking), as well as specific cognitive performance patterns, that did not co-vary with OCPD symptom severity. Clarifying these patterns can help guide future research and support the development of targeted interventions for OCPD, with the goal of reducing the burden of this highly impairing condition.
