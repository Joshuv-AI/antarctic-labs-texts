Lactate metabolism and lactylation in cancer: from pathogenesis to therapeutic advances
Authors: Chunyao Fang, Suiqing Zhou, Kai Yu, Liren Zhang, Wenxin Lian, Xiaofeng Tie, Shu Chen, Jingjing Dai, Qing Li, Xuehao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lactate and its mediated modification, lactylation, are receiving increasing attention in the field of tumor biology. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the mechanistic involvement of lactate and lactylation in key oncogenic processes, such as tumor initiation, proliferation, invasion, metastasis, recurrence, and drug resistance. Rather than being a passive byproduct of glycolysis, lactate actively shapes the tumor microenvironment (TME), modulates immune responses, drives metabolic adaptation, and influences epigenetic and transcriptomic regulation. Lactylation, as a novel epigenetic mechanism, links cellular metabolism with gene expression by modifying histone and nonhistone proteins, thereby regulating chromatin accessibility, immune evasion, and DNA repair. Notably, this article proposes a new perspective: lactate accumulation in certain tumors may constitute an adaptive metabolic strategy rather than a passive consequence of altered metabolism. This paradigm challenges the traditional perception of lactate as a metabolic waste, instead proposing its dual function as both a signaling molecule and a strategic modulator of tumor progression. The interplay of lactate-mediated signaling pathways, such as the PI3K/AKT, Hippo, Wnt, MAPK, and JAK/STAT pathways, further underscores the role of lactate in cancer progression. This review also explores therapeutic opportunities targeting lactate metabolism and lactylation, including inhibitors of LDH, MCTs, and lactylation-modifying enzymes, as well as the synergistic potential of combining lactate-targeted approaches with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy. Overall, this review provides an integrative overview of lactate-mediated mechanisms and highlights their potential as targets for metabolism-oriented therapeutic interventions in oncology.

Introduction

Lactate was long considered a mere “metabolic waste” produced under impaired aerobic metabolism, accumulating during physical exertion or hypoxia and signaling a decline in energy metabolic efficiency. However, emerging insights into tumor metabolism have redefined this view. Lactate is now recognized as a key regulatory molecule in metabolic reprogramming, immune modulation, and transcriptional regulation in cancer. This expanded role has led to the discovery of lactylation, a novel posttranslational modification that provides new perspectives on epigenetic regulation in cancer.

Cancer cells exhibit a characteristic metabolic hallmark known as the Warburg effect, in which they preferentially utilize glycolysis and generate substantial amounts of lactate despite adequate oxygen availability.1 This shift reflects an underlying biological strategy: under conditions of rapid proliferation, frequent mutations, and immune evasion, glycolysis offers a rapid supply of carbon sources and energy. Simultaneously, lactate accumulation acts as an adaptive mechanism, remodeling the microenvironment, modulating immune cells, and reinforcing the malignant phenotype. Rather than being a passive “byproduct,” lactate represents a strategic metabolic adaptation that deliberately facilitates tumor growth and survival amid diverse internal and external stressors. As a major functional extension of lactate, lactylation has attracted increasing attention for its biological relevance. Lactate can transfer its lactyl group to lysine residues on proteins through enzymatic or nonenzymatic mechanisms, resulting in lactylation modifications that influence protein stability, subcellular localization, and protein‒protein interactions.2 In cancer cells, lactylation represents a novel mechanism of epigenetic regulation, particularly for histones, potentially linking metabolic states to transcriptional activity. For example, modifications at various sites, such as H3K18la and H4K12la, are associated with upregulated transcriptional activity and enhanced immune evasion in cancer. Notably, the function of lactylation extends beyond histones, with nonhistone lactylation reported across diverse functional proteins, including signaling pathway regulators, metabolic enzymes, and cytoskeletal proteins. History of lactate metabolism and lactylation research (Fig. 1)

The scientific study of lactic acid dates back to the 18th century. In 1780, Carl Wilhelm Scheele first isolated lactic acid from yogurt, marking its initial recognition in the scientific literature.3 In the 19th century, Jöns Jacob Berzelius identified lactic acid as a key product of muscle metabolism, suggesting its physiological function. A major advancement occurred in 1929 when Carl Cori and Gerty Cori proposed the “Cori cycle,” describing how lactate produced in muscles is transported through the blood to the liver and converted back into glucose, thereby creating a closed loop in glucose metabolism. The discovery of this cycle established lactic acid’s central role in systemic metabolic regulation. In the 1940s, Hans Krebs discovered the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA cycle), demonstrating how lactic acid, via its conversion to pyruvate, enters the mitochondrial oxidative metabolism system, further underscoring its pivotal role in the energy metabolism network.

In the early 20th century, Otto Warburg reported that tumor cells preferentially undergo anaerobic glycolysis and produce large quantities of lactic acid despite the presence of oxygen—a phenomenon now referred to as the “Warburg effect.” This observation underscores the extensive reprogramming of lactic acid metabolism in cancer and stimulated further research into its role in tumorigenesis. In the 1990s, the “lactate shuttle hypothesis” proposed that lactic acid could be transported between cells as a signaling molecule, prompting investigations into its nonmetabolic functions. Notably, lactic acid was widely regarded as a metabolic waste product for decades. However, in 2014, Colegio et al. reported that lactic acid promotes the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages into the M2 phenotype, suppressing antitumor immunity and modulating the immune microenvironment.4 Subsequently, Pucino et al. demonstrated that the accumulation of lactic acid in rheumatoid arthritis induces the transformation of synovial fibroblasts into an inflammatory phenotype, promoting cell migration, proinflammatory cytokine secretion, and synovial damage. Collectively, these studies challenge the traditional view of lactic acid as a waste product, highlighting its function as a microenvironmental signaling molecule involved in immune modulation and chronic inflammation.5

In 2019, Zhang et al. first reported histone lactylation, a novel posttranslational modification, marking a pivotal breakthrough linking lactic acid to epigenetics. Specifically, they demonstrated that lactic acid can modify histones, thus influencing gene transcription, and, for the first time, identified lactic acid as an “epigenetic regulator.” Subsequent studies revealed widespread lactylation across various cell types, involving both histone and nonhistone lactylation, implicating this modification in signal transduction, metabolic regulation, and cell fate determination. These findings have spurred investigations into the enzymatic mechanisms underlying lactylation. Furthermore, researchers have developed high-resolution mass spectrometry and chemical probe techniques to map lactylation modifications, advancing the understanding of protein posttranslational modifications in cellular processes.

Lactate metabolism

Glycolysis

Under physiological conditions, lactate is primarily generated via glycolysis—a central metabolic pathway in which glucose is converted into two molecules of pyruvate, yielding a net gain of two ATP and two NADH molecules in mammalian cells.6 This cytoplasmic process enables rapid energy production independent of mitochondrial function.7 The intermediates of glycolysis can be utilized for the synthesis of biomacromolecules, such as fatty acids, amino acids, and nucleotides.8 Moreover, glycolysis acts as a metabolic bridge between aerobic and anaerobic metabolism. Under aerobic conditions, pyruvate is transported into mitochondria and converted into acetyl-CoA, which then enters the TCA cycle, ultimately generating 36–38 molecules of ATP via oxidative phosphorylation. In contrast, under anaerobic conditions, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) converts pyruvate into lactate. This reaction is bidirectional; therefore, when lactate levels are high, LDH converts lactate back to pyruvate, which can then enter the TCA cycle or serve as a substrate for gluconeogenesis.9 When pyruvate is unable to enter the mitochondria and is instead reduced to lactate by LDH, the NADH generated during glycolysis is oxidized to NAD+, allowing glycolysis to continue uninterrupted.10

LDH is a tetrameric enzyme composed of four peptide chains with a molecular weight of 134 kDa. It is made up of H and M subunits, and depending on their composition, LDH exists as five isoenzymes, each with distinct functions and tissue distributions. LDH-1 (H4) is primarily expressed in the heart and red blood cells and efficiently catalyzes the oxidation of lactate into pyruvate. LDH-2 (H3M1) is abundant in erythrocytes,11 while LDH-3 (H2M2) is mainly present in the lungs and lymphatic tissues.12 LDH-4 (H1M3) is predominantly found in the kidneys and placenta.13,14 LDH-5 (M4) is primarily located in the liver and skeletal muscles, where it efficiently reduces pyruvate to lactate.15,16 A sixth isoenzyme, LDH-X (also known as LDH-C4), is composed of four C subunits and is typically found in the testes, sperm, and certain malignant tumors.17,18 Elevated serum LDH levels are frequently associated with malignancy and are widely used as biomarkers in oncology. However, emerging evidence has challenged the assumption that serum LDH levels directly reflect the tumor burden. A CT scan of 251 patients with solid tumors revealed a weak correlation between total serum LDH levels and tumor burden. Among patients with melanoma (n = 102), the correlation coefficient was 0.56 (p < 0.01), whereas in patients without melanoma (n = 149), the correlation coefficient was significantly lower at 0.27 (p < 0.01).18 These findings suggest that the relationship between serum LDH levels and tumor burden is complex, with tumor type being just one influencing factor.

This complexity may arise from the routine measurement of total serum LDH levels in patients with cancer rather than the assessment of specific isoenzymes. For example, elevated serum LDH-1 levels in patients with metastatic ovarian cancer or testicular germ cell tumors are associated with lower survival rates.19 Among 25 patients with myocardial infarction (MI), 10 (40%) and 14 (56%) exhibited increased LDH-1 activity on electrophoresis and immunochemistry, respectively.20 In patients with nephrotic syndrome, significantly elevated serum LDH-2 activity was correlated with decreased total serum protein (r = −0.665, p < 0.001) and serum albumin (r = −0.615, p < 0.001) levels but increased proteinuria (r = 0.694, p < 0.001) and elevated levels of total serum cholesterol (r = 0.723, p < 0.001).21 In a study of 252 patients with non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), an analysis of serum LDH isoenzyme profiles revealed elevated LDH-2 levels in all patients. Notably, among patients with recurrent NHL, those with high serum LDH-3 levels exhibited a markedly shorter median survival of only 2 months than those with normal LDH-3 levels, suggesting that LDH-3 is a potential prognostic marker for survival outcomes.21 Furthermore, in an investigation exploring LDH as a biomarker for malignancy risk in smokers and gutkha chewers, a study of 70 volunteers demonstrated a significant increase in salivary LDH-4, accompanied by reductions in LDH-1 and LDH-2.22 These findings underscore the importance of isoenzyme-specific LDH profiling, which may offer more meaningful insights than total serum LDH levels in both physiological and pathological settings.

Lactate in cancer

Tumors exhibit altered lactate production. Under normoxic conditions, glucose is preferentially converted to pyruvate, which subsequently enters mitochondria. The TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation are the primary energy production pathways in normal cells. However, tumor cells preferentially utilize glycolysis to produce lactate even in the presence of oxygen, reflecting the Warburg effect or aerobic glycolysis.23 This enhanced aerobic glycolysis has been identified in various cancer types, including breast, pancreatic, lung, and colorectal cancers. Under physiological conditions, the blood lactate concentration ranges from 0.5 to 2.2 mM. In contrast, the lactate concentration in the tumor microenvironment (TME) is significantly elevated, with local concentrations reaching 10–40 mM, which is substantially higher than those observed in normal tissues.24,25 The occurrence of aerobic glycolysis in tumor cells can be attributed to several factors: (1) the increased ATP demand in proliferative cells, such as tumor cells, is more rapidly met through glycolysis7; (2) aerobic glycolysis generates multiple intermediates that favor biosynthesis8; (3) tumor cells accumulate lactate intracellularly, which regulates transcription and metabolism to promote malignant transformation26; and (4) the lactate accumulated from aerobic glycolysis acidifies the TME, modulating interactions between tumor, immune, and stromal cells, thereby facilitating malignant maintenance and metastasis.27

Thus, hypoxia-driven lactate accumulation is an exception rather than a common phenomenon, challenging the view that low oxygen levels are prerequisites for lactate production and accumulation. Nearly all cases of abnormal lactate accumulation involve at least one promoting factor beyond hypoxia, such as mitochondrial overload28 or immune cell activation.29

In addition to glycolysis, glutamine metabolism represents a major source of lactate in cancer cells.30 Cancer cells actively take up glutamine through glutamine transporters, such as SLC1A5.31 Once inside the cell, glutamine is converted into glutamate by glutaminase (GLS).30 Deamination of glutamine to glutamate plays a crucial role in maintaining intracellular amino acid and carbon metabolism. Glutamate has two main fates: it can act as a nitrogen source for the synthesis of other amino acids, or, more importantly, it can be deaminated by glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) to generate alpha-ketoglutarate (α-KG). α-KG can enter the TCA cycle or branch pathways, providing substrates for the production of lactate, lipids, and nucleotides. In triple-negative basal-like breast cancer, high SLC1A5 expression significantly increases cellular glutamine uptake, accelerating glutamine breakdown and lactate production and promoting downstream mTORC1 signaling.32 In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), low SLC25A15 expression induces glutamine reprogramming by upregulating SLC1A5 and downregulating OGDHL, thereby promoting disease progression.33

In summary, cancer cells primarily promote the synthesis of lactate by enhancing aerobic glycolysis and glutamine metabolism. Lactate not only functions as a direct driver of increased malignancy but also confers a survival advantage to tumor cells by modulating diverse signaling pathways, thereby supporting tumor persistence and recurrence. Lactate shuttle (Fig. 2)

During high-intensity or prolonged exercise, skeletal muscle cells generate a substantial amount of lactate, which is transported through the bloodstream to the liver for gluconeogenesis to synthesize glucose and restore the body’s glucose balance. This process is known as the Cori cycle.34 The dynamic shuttling of lactate between the muscles and liver, facilitated by vascular connections, underscores its crucial role in metabolic homeostasis. Under normal circumstances, the two most abundant circulating carbon metabolites are glucose and lactate. However, in certain situations, some tissues, such as skeletal and cardiac muscle cells during intense exercise and the brain during prolonged starvation, rely more heavily on lactate.35 During sustained low-intensity work, serum glucose levels decrease, whereas lactate cycling increases to compensate for energy deficits. Studies using intravenous injection of 13C-labeled nutrients have demonstrated that circulating lactate provides approximately 1.1 times more energy than glucose under normal conditions; notably, this ratio increases to 2.5 during fasting.36 As an energy carrier, lactate possesses certain advantages over glucose. Unlike glucose, it bypasses the need for kinase-mediated activation and can directly enter the TCA cycle, rendering it a universally accessible energy substrate, whereas glucose is a more specialized energy source. Additionally, lactate is increasingly recognized as a signaling molecule, often referred to as a “lactohormone.”37 It is involved in various physiological processes, including glucose metabolism, fatty acid synthesis, redox homeostasis, and protein posttranslational modifications. Lactate also serves as a ligand for GPR81, stimulating the GPR81 signaling pathway.38 Lactate has been shown to regulate muscle contraction, wound healing, synaptic formation, and tumor development through autocrine, paracrine, and endocrine-like actions.39

Given the significant role of lactate shuttling in the body, understanding its mechanisms is essential. Lactate is shuttled in and out of cells via monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs), which function bidirectionally, allowing cells to flexibly adjust lactate flow according to environmental changes.39 MCTs are members of the solute carrier (SLC) family, with MCT1–4 primarily functioning as 1:1 cotransporters of lactate and protons. MCTs are highly expressed in tissues and organelles involved in rapid lactate exchange, and their expression levels can change swiftly to regulate the body’s adaptation to physiological changes induced by lactate. For example, in rat and human muscles induced by exercise, MCT1 levels increase rapidly.40 Cancer cells establish a lactate shuttle by exploiting the overexpression of two MCT subtypes, MCT1 and MCT4, which are widely expressed in cancer. MCT4 tends to export lactate, whereas MCT1 tends to import it.41 The expression patterns of MCT1 and MCT4 depend on the cell type and its role in tumor metabolism. For example, most tumor cells are highly dependent on glycolysis, exhibiting MCT4 upregulation, which is positively correlated with tumor malignancy and poor prognosis.42 A survival analysis indicated that patients with colorectal liver oligometastases exhibiting low MCT4 expression exhibited higher 3-year recurrence-free survival and overall survival rates than did those with elevated MCT4 expression.43 Notably, single MCT4 inhibition can reverse lactate-driven immunosuppression and increase the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy.44 MCT4 promotes HCC progression by regulating TRAPPC5, which enhances HCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.45 Elevated MCT4 expression is frequently associated with increased resistance to chemotherapy, complicating treatment efforts. Inhibiting MCT4 reduced the malignant traits of pancreatic cancer cells in vitro and exhibited a cooperative effect with gemcitabine therapy.46 In certain cases, tumor cells located in well-oxygenated regions express high levels of MCT1, enabling them to absorb lactate secreted by other cells, such as tumor cells in hypoxic regions, and utilize it as an energy source.47 Additionally, MCT1 is often highly expressed in stromal, immune, and endothelial cells to facilitate lactate uptake for oxidative metabolism. For example, in ovarian cancer, tumor-infiltrating Treg cells regulate cellular metabolism by upregulating MCT1, while miR-124 directly targets MCT1 and reduces lactate uptake, ultimately impairing the immunosuppressive capacity of Treg cells.48 MCT1 facilitates lactate uptake in endothelial cells and promotes the NF-kappaB/IL-8 pathway, which drives tumor angiogenesis. Thus, its inhibition offers a dual therapeutic strategy by combining antimetabolic and antiangiogenic effects.49,50 As tumor cells undergo continuous reprogramming, they may also exhibit enhanced glycolytic capacity, upregulate MCT4, and provide metabolic fuel to other tumor cells. The most typical example of this is cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs).51 Hypoxic tumor environments can accelerate TGF-β1 signaling and promote the transformation of normal fibroblasts into CAFs. Acting synergistically with TGF-β1, hypoxia induces autophagy, leading to increased aerobic glycolysis and MCT4 expression in CAFs, facilitating tumor migration and metastasis through the reverse Warburg effect.52

In summary, tumor cells dynamically regulate MCT1 and MCT4 expression both within themselves and neighboring cells based on metabolic needs and microenvironmental signals. MCT4 exports lactate to the TME, maintaining acid‒base balance and preventing intracellular acidification, while MCT1 facilitates lactate uptake to support oxidative metabolism and mitochondrial function. Tumors can increase the flux of lactate in the body by regulating the expression of MCTs, laying the basis for the lactate-mediated regulation of various biological processes, which are essential components of metabolic symbiosis.

Most evidence indicates that, rather than directly entering the mitochondria for oxidation, lactate is first converted to pyruvate within the mitochondrial intermembrane space. Pyruvate then enters the mitochondrial matrix and proceeds to the TCA cycle, where it is ultimately oxidized. Evidence on the direct transport of lactate from the mitochondrial intermembrane space to the matrix is scarce.53 Modeling the molecular structure and dynamics of the mitochondrial lactate oxidation complex (mLOC) remains challenging. The proposed mLOC model includes several core components: MCT is tightly bound to its chaperone protein CD47 and is connected to cytochrome c oxidase (COX). Mitochondrial LDH is located on the outer side of the inner membrane and is linked to both MCT and COX.39 While high-resolution protein structures are typically obtained via techniques such as X-ray crystallography or cryo-electron microscopy, these technologies may fail to capture subtle structural differences that may exist in the mLOC across different tissues and conditions. Isomeride

Lactate, with its chemical formula C₃H₆O₃, possesses a chimeric center, resulting in two enantiomeric forms: L-lactate and D-lactate.54 These stereoisomers differ in their biosynthetic origins and physiological functions in living organisms.

L-lactate is the most common form of lactate in biological systems, with an approximate L-lactate to D-lactate ratio of 100:1 in mammals.55 L-lactate is primarily produced by the enzyme LDH, which catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate under hypoxic or anaerobic conditions. This process occurs after glycolysis and allows the oxidation of NADH to NAD⁺, thereby maintaining the continuity of glycolysis.56 L-lactate is abundant in muscles, blood, and other tissues. In particular, during intense exercise or hypoxia, muscle cells produce large amounts of L-lactate to meet short-term energy demands. L-lactate can subsequently enter the liver through the bloodstream and be converted back into glucose via gluconeogenesis.57,58

In contrast, D-lactate is present in considerably lower concentrations in the human body and is mainly produced by certain gut bacteria, such as lactate bacteria and bifidobacteria, through fermentation pathways.59 While some mammalian tissues also contain D-lactate, its production is significantly lower than that of L-lactate.60 Recent studies suggest that D-lactate can be generated via a non-LDH pathway, specifically the methylglyoxal (MGO) pathway.61 This pathway represents a bypass of glycolysis and is found in various organisms.62 It involves the metabolic conversion of methylglyoxal, a highly reactive α-oxoaldehyde, which is formed either spontaneously or enzymatically from glycolytic intermediates, such as dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) or glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P).63 Subsequently, glyoxalase I (GLO1) and II (GLO2) process MGO, converting it into D-lactate or glutathione (GSH).64 In healthy individuals, D-lactate is usually rapidly cleared. However, in certain pathological conditions, such as short bowel syndrome or gut microbiota dysbiosis, abnormal accumulation of D-lactate may occur, leading to D-lactateosis.65,66 D-lactateosis can result in metabolic acidosis and neurological symptoms, including ataxia, confusion, and cognitive impairment.67

Research has identified three distinct isomeric forms of lactylation: L-lactylation, D-lactylation, and N-ε-(carboxyethyl)-lysine (Kce), with the first two being more prevalent.68,69 L-lactylation is primarily synthesized through an enzyme-dependent mechanism in which specific enzymes catalyze the covalent attachment of a lactyl group to substrate molecules, thereby influencing their structure and function. Two major pathways through which L-lactate provides lactyl groups for L-lactylation reactions have been identified. First, lactate can directly serve as a donor of the lactyl group, forming a covalent bond with specific target sites on the substrate under the catalysis of certain enzymes.70,71 Second, lactate can also serve as a precursor molecule, being converted through intracellular metabolic pathways into lactyl-CoA. As a high-energy activated intermediate, lactyl-CoA provides lactyl groups for substrate modification.72,73 Currently identified lactyl-CoA synthetases include acyl-CoA synthetase short-chain family member 2 (ACSS2) and GTPase-associated propionyl-CoA synthetase-like protein (GTPSCS). ACSS2, which belongs to the ACSS family, is an enzyme expressed in both the cytoplasm and nucleus.74 It primarily catalyzes the conversion of acetate into acetyl-CoA.75,76 In addition, studies have shown that ACSS2 can also catalyze the conversion of lactate into lactyl-CoA and function in synergy with KAT2A to transfer lactyl groups to substrate proteins.77 GTPSCS, a newly identified lactyl-CoA synthetase, translocates to the nucleus via its G1 subunit’s nuclear localization sequence. Moreover, the formation of the GTPSCS/p300 complex promotes histone lactylation modifications.

D-lactylation is primarily generated through a nonenzymatic process.78 Unlike L-lactylation, the intermediate of D-lactylation is S-D-lactylglutathione (D-lactyl-GSH). Specifically, MGO, the byproduct of glycolysis, is catalyzed by GLO1 to form D-lactyl-GSH. This intermediate is processed by GLO2, resulting in either D-lactate release or direct transfer of the D-lactyl moiety to protein lysine residues via S‒N transfer. Additionally, D-lactyl-GSH can transfer the lactyl group to CoA through S‒S bond transfer, forming lactyl-CoA.79

KL-la primarily depends on glycolytic flux and increases with rising glucose concentrations. In contrast, KD-la is mainly regulated by GLO2 activity. It is significantly upregulated in GLO2knockout cells, whereas its expression is minimally affected by glucose levels.78,80 GLO1 and GLO2 are key molecules involved in detoxifying MGOs, and their dysregulation is associated with various types of cancer.81,82 In nonmalignant conditions, both suppress tumors, but in metastatic stages with increased glycolytic flux, they drive tumor growth and proliferation.83 Although GLO2 inhibits immune responses,78 its low expression can increase the sensitivity of breast cancer cells to doxorubicin.84 Recent studies have revealed that D-lactylation modulates inflammatory responses by modifying inflammation-related proteins (e.g., RelA), thereby suppressing excessive immune activation. This process provides a negative feedback mechanism to maintain immune homeostasis.78

Although the role of D-lactylation in vivo may substantially differ from that of L-lactylation, experimental evidence with p300 inhibitors (A-485), HDAC inhibitors (TSA), and JQ-1 indicates that both enzymatic and nonenzymatic lactylation marks are recognized indiscriminately by reader proteins. This implies that the molecular machinery responsible for interpreting lactylation signals does not differentiate between the stereoisomeric forms of the modification.80

Writers

Acyltransferases known to catalyze lactylation include p300/CBP, GCN5, HBO1, TIP60, KAT8, ATAT1, and AARS1/2. Among these modifications, p300 is the most versatile and mediates various types of protein modifications, including acetylation, propionylation, butyrylation, 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation, and lactylation.85 Notably, p300 was the first acyltransferase identified with a writing function for lactylation. In an initial study reporting histone lactylation, the overexpression of p300 in HEK293T cells led to a moderate increase in histone Kla, whereas the loss of p300 in HCT116 and HEK293T cells decreased histone Kla.86 Subsequently, p300 was demonstrated to promote nonhistone lactylation and contribute to malignant tumor progression in various cancers.87,88 During the reperfusion phase of ischemic stroke, lactate supplementation has been demonstrated to confer neuroprotection.89 However, during ischemia, excessive lactate accumulation can exacerbate infarct size by promoting protein lactylation.90 The p300 antagonist A-485 effectively blocks lysine lactylation, significantly reducing neuronal death and ischemia-induced glial activation, thereby facilitating functional recovery from ischemic stroke.91 Moreover, andrographolide (AGP) interferes with p300 and regulates histone H3 lactylation to mitigate aortic valve calcification.92 CBP and p300 are homologous proteins that share highly similar structures, leading to considerable overlap in their functions and regulatory mechanisms. Consequently, they can often compensate for each other in executing specific biological processes.93 The EP300 gene, encoding p300, is located on human chromosome 22, whereas the CREBBP gene, encoding CBP, is located on chromosome 16.94 Notably, K249 lactylation of TPX2 is implicated in the progression of HCC, which is primarily influenced by CBP.95 A study published in Cell in January 2024 reported that CBP can catalyze K673 lactylation of the homologous recombination (HR) protein MRE11, counteracting the DNA damage due to chemical agents. Inhibiting CBP or suppressing LDH can restore cancer cell sensitivity to cisplatin and PARP inhibitors (PARPis), suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy to enhance the efficacy of these treatments.96

GCN5 and p300/CBP belong to the histone acetyltransferase (HAT) family.97 A study on MI revealed that histone lactylation promotes the activation of repair genes, such as Lrg1, Vegf-a, and IL-10, following MI. Additionally, GCN5-knockout significantly suppressed H3K18la modification and the expression levels of these target genes, confirming its role as a writer of lactylation.98 Rong et al. demonstrated that KAT2A interacts with ACSS2 as a lactoyltransferase, catalyzing histone lactylation specifically at H3K14 and H3K18.77 Notably, GCN5 also facilitates the lactylation of nonhistone proteins. In ovarian cancer, GCN5 enhances RAD51 expression by catalyzing H3K9 lactylation (H3K9lac). Additionally, it promotes K73 lactylation of RAD51, thereby augmenting HR repair mechanisms. Ultimately, this modification endows cancer cells with cisplatin resistance, contributing to therapeutic challenges.99

In 2024, a report in Nature Communications identified a Kla transferase, HBO1, which can regulate histone Kla both in vitro and in cells.100 SILAC and site-specific antibody validation revealed that HBO1 may preferentially catalyze H3K9la.100 HBO1, a member of the MYST family, is a multifunctional HAT capable of catalyzing histone acetylation, propionylation, butyrylation, crotonylation, and benzoylation both in vivo and in vitro.101 Additionally, HBO1 acts as a small-molecule TIP60 agonist. In a study exploring the mechanisms underlying chemotherapy resistance, researchers identified TIP60-mediated lactylation of NBS1 at K388 as a key determinant of drug resistance. This modification facilitates the assembly of the MRE11-RAD50-NBS1 (MRN) complex, thereby enhancing HR repair and ultimately contributing to chemoresistance.102 Autophagy plays a crucial role in cancer progression.103,104 Cheng X et al. identified lactate as a key link between glycolysis and autophagy. Lactate is produced through ULK1-mediated phosphorylation of LDHA and facilitates the lactylation of PIK3C3/VPS34 at lysine residues 356 and 781 via the acetyltransferase KAT5/TIP60. This modification strengthens the interaction of PIK3C3/VPS34 with BECN1, ATG14, and UVRAG, enhances its lipid kinase activity, and promotes both macroautophagy and endolysosomal degradation.105,106

KAT8 (also known as MOF or MYST1) also belongs to the MYST family and plays a critical role in regulating gene expression, DNA damage repair, and cellular functions.107 In 2024, KAT8 was first reported as a writer protein for pan-Kla. KAT8 mediates the lactylation of eEF1A2 at K408, leading to increased translation elongation and enhanced protein synthesis, thereby promoting colorectal cancer progression.108 Patients often develop hyperlactatemia following liver transplantation. KAT8 serves as a key mediator linking the hyperlactatemic environment to ferroptosis by catalyzing the lactylation of PCK2, thereby indirectly exacerbating hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury.109 Additionally, macrophages play a crucial role in lactate-induced collagen synthesis. Specifically, macrophages upregulate H4K12 lactylation (H4K12la) through a KAT5-KAT8-dependent mechanism, thereby promoting TGF-β1 and TGF-β3 transcription and ultimately increasing fibroblast collagen synthesis.110 Recent studies suggest that KAT8 may mediate PCK2K100 lactylation, promoting hepatic ferroptosis and ischemia-reperfusion injury in patients with hyperlactatemia during liver transplantation.109

ATAT1 (α-tubulin acetyltransferase 1, also known as NAA10) is an enzyme responsible for the acetylation of α-tubulin, a key structural component of microtubules.111 This acetylation occurs at lysine 40 on α-tubulin and plays a critical role in stabilizing microtubules and regulating microtubule dynamics.112 ATAT1 activity can influence various cellular processes, including cell division, intracellular transport, and maintenance of the cellular architecture.113 Recent studies have demonstrated that NAA10 serves as a lactylation writer for NSUN2. Specifically, under acidic conditions in the TME, NAA10 catalyzes the lactylation of NSUN2 at lysine 508, thereby increasing its enzymatic activity. This modification stabilizes GCLC-mRNA and increases glutathione synthesis, which promotes ferroptosis resistance in cancer cells.114 In addition, ATAT1 can catalyze the lactylation of key sites on NAT10, which increases its RNA acetyltransferase activity, thereby promoting ac4C modification of tRNA and ultimately accelerating the reactivation of KSHV.113

Recent studies have shown that L-lactate inhibits cyclic GMP‒AMP synthase (cGAS)-mediated immune responses. Using CRISPR screening and lactylation-omics techniques, researchers have identified AARS1/2 as L-lactate sensors in mammalian cells with lactate transferase activity.70 AARS1 and AARS2 belong to the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase (ARS) family and are responsible for catalyzing the attachment of alanine to its corresponding tRNA molecule, thereby supplying the necessary substrates for protein synthesis.115 Unlike the conventional lactylation process, AARS1/2 can transfer lactate groups to lysine side chains in an ATP- and L-lactate-dependent but lactyl-CoA-independent manner.70 Specifically, lactate is first activated by AARS1/2, which catalyzes its reaction with ATP to form a lactyl-AMP intermediate (Lac-AMP). Subsequently, AARS1/2 transfers the lactyl group from Lac-AMP to a lysine residue on the target protein, thus completing the lactylation process and releasing AMP.71 Furthermore, Li et al. demonstrated that the lactylation of cGAS is regulated primarily by AARS2, leading to the inactivation of cGAS, suppression of cGAMP synthesis, and inhibition of the innate immune response.70 This mechanism offers a potential therapeutic strategy—for example, inhibiting MCT1 can restore innate immune surveillance.70 Notably, as intracellular lactate levels are significantly higher than lactyl-CoA levels, this lactate-based regulatory mechanism, rather than lactyl-CoA, broadens the range of lactylation substrates, including key proteins, such as p53.71 In gastric cancer, upregulated AARS1 responds to intracellular lactate accumulation and translocates to the nucleus. This protein directly catalyzes the lactylation of nuclear YAP and TEAD1, thereby activating them and inducing the expression of downstream target genes.116 In addition, clinical data suggest that patients with bladder cancer and diabetes are less responsive to enfortumab vedotin (EV) treatment. Further studies revealed that high glucose enhances glycolysis and leads to excessive lactate production, subsequently reducing the expression of NECTIN4, which is the therapeutic target of EVs, through the AARS1/YTHDC1/JUND pathway.117

One molecule of glucose can generate two molecules, ATP and lactate, through glycolysis. Interestingly, lactate and ATP can serve as substrates for AARS1/2-mediated lactylation at a 1:1 ratio. This observation suggests that AARS1/2 may play a critical role in linking energy metabolism with signal transduction, and its potential significance warrants future in-depth investigations.

Erasers

The histone deacetylase (HDAC) family consists of enzymes that remove acetyl groups from histones, regulating gene expression and cellular functions.118 Based on structure and function, HDACs are classified into four categories: Class I (HDAC1, 2, 3, 8), which functions primarily in the nucleus to regulate transcription119,120; Class II (IIa: HDAC4, 5, 7, 9; IIb: HDAC6, 10), which can shuttle between the nucleus and cytoplasm121,122; Class III, known as Sirtuins (SIRT1--7), which are NAD+-dependent and involved in metabolic regulation and aging123,124; and Class IV, which includes only HDAC11 and exhibits mixed characteristics of Classes I and II.125,126 The currently reported erasers of lactylation mainly include HDAC1-3 and SIRT1-3.

HDAC1-3 have been identified in vitro as erasers of histone lactylation, showing strong activity toward both K(L-la) and K(D-la). Compared with K(L-la), HDAC1-3 are generally more effective at removing K(D-la) modifications.127 In vitro studies revealed that overexpression of HDAC1-3 significantly reduces H3K18la and H4K5la levels. However, total lactylation and H3K18la levels remain unchanged, whereas simultaneous knockdown of HDAC1-3 significantly affects H4K5la levels in cells. The discrepancies between the in vitro and in vivo results suggest that cofactors may provide HDACs with site-specific delactylation activity.127 Notably, the overexpression of HDAC1 and HDAC3, but not HDAC2, does not affect H3K18la levels. However, in pancreatic cancer, the increase in H3K18la levels mediated by p300 can be effectively antagonized by the overexpression of HDAC2.88,127 Therefore, HDAC1-3 may exhibit spatiotemporal specificity in the regulation of lactylation. In colorectal cancer, HDAC1 can increase ferroptosis and enhance chemotherapy sensitivity by inhibiting H4K12 lactylation and reducing GCLC expression.128 Moreover, the decreased lactylation level of HDAC1K412 promotes FSP1 mRNA degradation, increasing the susceptibility of colorectal cancer to ferroptosis.120 In nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, HDAC1, regulated by tRF-31R9J, reduces the lactylation and acetylation of ATF3, ATF4, and CHAC1, thereby inhibiting hepatocyte ferroptosis.129 The use of tucidinostat can inhibit HDAC2-mediated delactylation of methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3), thereby suppressing its interaction with WTAP (Wilms’-tumor-1-associated protein) and reducing the sensitivity of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells to cisplatin treatment. This study not only elucidates the biological function of lactylated METTL3 in tumor cells but also highlights its negative regulatory effect on cisplatin resistance.130 TRAP1 plays a crucial role in VSMC senescence and atherosclerosis by specifically downregulating HDAC3 expression through the promotion of lactate production.131 In addition, studies have shown that HDAC1-3 not only exert delactylation activity but also promote lysine lactylation. This dual function may depend on specific cellular contexts, cofactors, or substrates, reflecting the multifunctional roles of HDAC1-3 in metabolic regulation, epigenetic modifications, and disease progression.132

SIRT1-3 have also been identified as histone lactylation erasers in vitro. However, compared with HDAC1-3, SIRT1-3 displays only weak delactylation activity.127 SIRT1 reduces H3K18 lactylation via delactylation and inhibits the H19/glycolysis/H3K18la positive feedback loop, playing an antitumor role in gastric cancer.133 Moreover, under glucose deprivation conditions, SIRT1 promotes cell survival through the NMNAT1-mediated nuclear NAD+ salvage pathway and plays a crucial role in pancreatic adenocarcinoma.134 The efficiency of SIRT2 is only approximately one-thousandth that of HDAC3.135 However, specifically inhibiting SIRT2 enhances METTL16 lactylation at the K229 site, promotes m6A modification of FDX1 mRNA, and ultimately exacerbates cuproptosis in gastric cancer cells.136 Fan et al. reported that SIRT3 possesses greater erasure activity at the H4K16la site than other Sirtuins, indicating a degree of selectivity for SIRT3 in Kla removal.137 In HCC, SIRT3 is downregulated, and its activation by honokiol induces apoptosis and inhibits tumor growth through the regulation of CCNE2 lactylation, highlighting its tumor-suppressive role.138

Readers

In 2024, a study on the reprogramming of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) reported that the Brahma-Related Gene 1 (Brg1) protein can recognize histone lactylation, marking it as the first identified “reader” of histone lactylation.139 BRG1, also known as SMARCA4, is a critical ATP-dependent chromatin remodeler and the core subunit of the chromatin remodeling complex BAF (also referred to as SWI/SNF).140 In terms of cellular biology, BRG1 plays an essential role in influencing gene expression through alterations in chromatin structure.141 Research has confirmed that Dux overexpression-induced H3K18la modification regulates the metabolic H3K18la-MET network, enhancing iPSC reprogramming efficiency through a metabolic switch and recruiting p300 via its C-terminal domain. Immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry analyses of H3K18la revealed the specific recruitment of BRG1 during reprogramming. Both H3K18la and BRG1 are enriched at the promoters of genes associated with pluripotency and epithelial junctions, promoting the mesenchymal‒epithelial transition (MET) process; this underscores the role of BRG1 as a “reader” in lactylation modifications.139 Another identified lactylation reader is DNA-binding with one finger 2 (DPF2). The DPF2 protein belongs to the DNA-binding with one finger family, whose members typically contain one or more Cys2His2-type zinc finger domains, enabling them to bind DNA and participate in gene transcription regulation. DPF2 is a bona fide H3K14la effector protein that links histone lactylation to gene transcription and cancer development.142

Research on lactylation is still emerging, particularly on its “writers,” “erasers,” and “readers.” However, owing to the development of more advanced probes, antibodies, and screening methods, recent breakthroughs have been achieved in the study of lactylation-regulating enzymes, which may reveal the regulatory mechanisms of lactylation.

Histone lactylation

Histones are fundamental components of nucleosomes, with each nucleosome in eukaryotes comprising an octamer of histones H2A, H2B, H3, and H4, around which DNA is wrapped. As primary components of chromatin, histones undergo posttranslational chemical modifications that serve as key mechanisms of epigenetic regulation.143

Histone lactylation serves as an important epigenetic modification that influences chromatin structure and transcriptional regulation. This dynamic regulates gene accessibility and expression. Primarily, lactylation influences histone‒DNA interactions by relaxing or compacting chromatin. Typically, lactylation induces an open chromatin state, facilitating the binding of transcription factors and the transcription machinery, thereby activating gene expression.144,145 These histone lactylation modification sites typically include H3K9lac, H3K14lac, H3K18, H4K5, H4K6, H4K12, and H4K16. Notably, histone lactylation modifications can also downregulate gene expression by altering chromatin structure. CUT&Tag sequencing has shown that lactylated H4K12 can bind to the promoter region of SLFN5, specifically suppressing its expression and promoting the development of malignant tumors in TNBC.146 Furthermore, lactylation may alter histone conformation, affecting its interaction with other chromatin-modifying enzymes, transcription factors, and regulatory proteins, thereby modulating chromatin states and gene expression.139,142 In summary, histone lactylation plays a pivotal role in regulating chromatin dynamics and gene expression, influencing both the activation and repression of target genes in various cellular processes and diseases.

Nonhistone lactylation

Through a global lactylome analysis, Yang et al. identified 9275 lactylation sites in a hepatitis B virus (HBV)-related HCC cohort, with 9256 of these sites located on nonhistone proteins. These sites predominantly affect enzymes involved in the TCA cycle, as well as carbohydrate, amino acid, fatty acid, and nucleotide metabolism.147 This finding suggests that nonhistone lactylation may regulate cellular processes more broadly and precisely than histone lactylation.

At the functional level, nonhistone protein lactylation modulates protein function through steric hindrance, conformational changes, and charge neutralization induced by modifications. These effects ultimately influence protein function by altering molecular interactions, stability, subcellular localization, and enzymatic activity.96 For example, MRE11, a critical protein in HR, undergoes lactylation at K678, which enhances its binding to DNA, promotes DNA end resection and HR, and contributes to chemoresistance in tumor cells. The interaction between the α-myosin heavy chain (α-MHC) and Titin is crucial for cardiac structure and contraction. By modulating the lactate concentration through sodium lactate treatment or inhibiting key lactate transport proteins in cardiomyocytes, α-MHC K1897 lactylation and the α-MHC-Titin interaction are promoted, maintaining cardiac structure and alleviating heart failure—offering a novel therapeutic direction for heart failure treatment.148 In glioblastoma stem cells, lactate accumulation results in the accumulation of XRCC1 at K247la, increasing its binding with importin α proteins and facilitating its translocation to the nucleus. This promotes DNA repair and contributes to tumor resistance.149 Lactate-induced lactylation of DCBLD1 at K172 stabilizes its expression and prolongs its half-life. Overexpression of DCBLD1 inhibits the autophagic degradation of G6PD, activates the pentose phosphate pathway, and promotes cervical cancer progression.150 In conclusion, nonhistone lactylation, as a dynamic regulatory mechanism, plays a critical role in various physiological and pathological processes within cells. By modulating gene expression, cellular metabolism, and other key pathways, it contributes to disease initiation and progression.

While most research on lactylation has primarily focused on histones, studies on nonhistone lactylation have gradually increased in number in recent years. Role of lactate and lactylation in different stages of cancer

Cancer progression is a complex and dynamically evolving multistage process, encompassing the transformation of normal cells into malignant cells and the gradual development of an invasive and metastatic disease. The process includes tumor initiation, abnormal proliferation, local invasion, distant metastasis, recurrence, and resistance.151 At each stage, tumor cells undergo significant biological changes to adapt to microenvironmental stress, evade immune surveillance, and enhance their growth and survival capabilities.152

These processes are not only driven by genetic mutations but also profoundly influenced by metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic regulation.153,154 As mentioned previously, increasing evidence suggests that lactate and lactylation play crucial roles in cancer progression, highlighting the role of lactate a key signaling molecule actively involved in regulating cellular metabolic homeostasis, immune evasion, inflammatory responses, and angiogenesis.

Tumorigenesis

Metabolic abnormalities and tumorigenesis

Dysregulation of lactate metabolism is often associated with the onset and progression of tumors. The complex interplay between many metabolic diseases and cancer further underscores the critical role of lactate in increasing the risk of cancer.155 Diabetes is strongly associated with various types of cancer, such as pancreatic cancer, liver cancer, colorectal cancer, and breast cancer.156 Patients with type 2 diabetes often exhibit insulin resistance and hyperglycemia, which lead to decreased glucose utilization efficiency in tissues such as the liver and muscles. In obese individuals, inflammation in adipose tissue can cause local hypoxia, inducing the activation of the HIF-1α signaling pathway and increasing lactate production. Lactate can further promote the release of inflammatory factors (such as IL-6 and TNF-α) from adipocytes, creating a more favorable microenvironment for tumor development.157 Patients with metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) often experience hepatic metabolic imbalances and reduced lactate clearance capacity, leading to local lactate accumulation, which promotes abnormal hepatocyte proliferation and increases the risk of liver cancer.158,159 In conclusion, lactate may serve as a bridge between various metabolic diseases and cancer. The transformation of cells from a healthy state to a malignant one involves multiple critical processes, including the accumulation of genetic mutations (from single-gene alterations to polygenic mutations), adaptive remodeling of diseased cells, and dynamic regulation of the extracellular microenvironment. Throughout these complex transitions, lactate, in addition to its function as a metabolic byproduct, plays crucial roles in promoting genetic mutation accumulation, enhancing cancer cell survival, and shaping an immunosuppressive TME.

Lactate and gene mutation

Genes, the fundamental units of hereditary information in living organisms, govern cell growth, differentiation, and function. In humans, tens of thousands of genes work in concert to ensure that cells operate to maintain proper cellular programming. However, genes are susceptible to mutations, which can occur during cell division and are influenced by external environmental factors, such as radiation and chemical carcinogens, as well as internal replication errors.160 When these mutations accumulate and affect key genes, they can lead to uncontrolled cell proliferation, ultimately triggering cancer.161 Therefore, genes play a central role in cancer development, with genetic alterations often serving as the critical starting point of tumor formation.

Increasing evidence suggests that prolonged lactate accumulation can increase the frequency of gene mutations. The acidic microenvironment caused by lactate may directly exacerbate DNA damage.162 Under low pH conditions, the chemical stability of DNA decreases, which increases its susceptibility to spontaneous deamination and base damage. For example, the deamination of cytosine (C) into uracil (U) can lead to base mismatches, and if not effectively repaired, it may result in C → T mutations during DNA replication.162 Additionally, low pH can promote the loss of purine bases (guanine and adenine) from the DNA double helix, forming apurinic/apyrimidinic (AP) sites—a phenomenon known as the depurination of guanine (G) and adenine (A). This can cause errors in DNA replication and transcription.163,164 An A‒C mismatch, a noncanonical base pair formed between adenine (A) and cytosine (C) within the DNA double strand,165 is highly dependent on the intracellular pH, primarily through alterations in the protonation state of the N1 position of adenine.166 Reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are primarily derived from mitochondria, can induce mutations and genomic instability through mechanisms such as base oxidation, DNA backbone breakage, and the formation of abasic (AP) sites, thereby contributing to cellular damage, aging, and the development of various diseases.167 The regulatory relationship between lactate and oxidative stress is bidirectional.168,169 Under different physiological and pathological conditions, lactate may act as an antioxidant to alleviate oxidative stress or, in some scenarios, exacerbate oxidative damage. For example, Takeshi et al. confirmed that elevated lactate concentrations increase ROS generation in L6 cells.170

Approximately six to seven key genetic mutations have been confirmed as necessary for the formation of malignant tumors.160 These mutations involve primarily oncogenes, tumor suppressor genes, and DNA repair genes, which drive the transformation of normal cells into malignant cells; these critical mutations are referred to as “driver mutations”.171 In contrast, mutations that do not play a significant role in cancer progression are often termed “passenger mutations”.172 These typically result from genomic instability or random errors during DNA replication and have little to no impact on cancer cell growth or survival. In the early 20th century, the German scientist Theodor Boveri hypothesized that chromosomal abnormalities could lead to cancer, thus laying the foundation for cancer genetics research. With advances in molecular biology, Alfred Knudson introduced the “two-hit hypothesis” in 1971, which suggests that tumor development generally requires both alleles of a tumor suppressor gene to be mutated or inactivated.173 In the 1980s, scientists identified the first human oncogene, RAS, whose mutation leads to continuous activation of cellular signaling pathways, resulting in uncontrolled cell proliferation.174 The TP53 gene is widely recognized as the “guardian of the genome”. When TP53 is mutated or inactivated, cells lose their ability to repair DNA damage or undergo apoptosis, thus promoting tumorigenesis.175

Notably, lactate-mediated modifications, specifically P53K120la and K139la, have been shown to weaken the ability of p53 to bind DNA and undergo liquid‒liquid phase separation (LLPS), thereby diminishing its tumor-suppressive function both in vitro and in vivo.71 Moreover, targeting SLC4A4 can restore enzalutamide sensitivity in prostate cancer by inhibiting P53 lactylation.176 The MYC gene is an important regulator of cell growth and metabolism, and its overactivation can lead to various cancers, such as breast cancer and small cell lung cancer. In glioblastoma, dexmedetomidine can inhibit tumor progression by suppressing the lactylation of c-Myc.177 The application of single-cell sequencing technology has further revealed the high degree of intratumoral heterogeneity, with different cell populations potentially carrying distinct mutations, making cancer more difficult to treat. Furthermore, in EGFR-mutant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), NNMT upregulated by EGR1 has been reported to promote lactate synthesis and H3K18la by upregulating ALDH3A1 expression, thereby conferring greater survival ability to tumor cells during treatment.178 Therefore, high concentrations of lactate not only increase the likelihood of key gene mutations but also directly mediate the functional dysregulation of mutated genes through lactylation modifications.

Lactate and epigenetics

Epigenetics refers to the mechanism of regulating gene expression through reversible chemical modifications, such as DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, and noncoding RNA regulation, without altering the DNA sequence.179 The epigenome refers to the sum of all epigenetic modifications within a cell, which determines gene activity and plays a crucial role in development, cell differentiation, and disease progression.

Notably, histone lactylation is a novel posttranslational modification that involves various epigenetic modifications, such as histone acetylation and DNA methylation, forming a complex epigenetic regulatory network that contributes to cancer progression.180 Similarly, other histone modifications, histone lactylation primarily regulates gene transcription by altering the chromatin structure. For example, knockdown of LDHA can inhibit the transcription of P53 by reducing histone lactylation at the P53 promoter region.181 In contrast, histone lactylation can suppress PTEN transcription.182,183 Although evidence suggests that lactylation and acetylation may compete for histone binding sites, a global increase in lactate levels can simultaneously promote both histone lactylation and histone acetylation.86

Interestingly, H3K18ac and H3K18la have both been shown to play protumorigenic roles in various cancers.184,185 Furthermore, H3K27ac and H3K18la can directly enhance the transcription of PD-L1.186–188 These findings indicate that the two modifications may either compete or act synergistically, depending on the context. Breast cancer bone metastasis cells release lactate through MCT4, inducing the upregulation of pERK1/2 and pCREB in the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) of sensory neurons, leading to bone pain through the activation of GPR81.189 Glioblastoma (GBM) is a malignant tumor originating from the central nervous system and is the most common primary malignant brain tumor in adults, accounting for approximately 50–60% of all gliomas. The interaction between pCREB-mediated DNA methylation and histone acetylation further elevates the expression of GDNF in GBM.190 The above evidence highlights the multifaceted role of lactate in regulating tumor gene expression, particularly in the interactions among various epigenetic modifications. Therefore, targeting lactate metabolism and its epigenetic regulatory mechanisms could provide new strategies for cancer therapy and warrants further exploration.

Lactate and epitranscriptome

Certainly, gene expression can be regulated via RNA (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA, lncRNA, and miRNA) modifications, including N6-methyladenosine (m6A), 5-methylcytosine (m5C), and pseudouridine (Ψ). In addition to genetic and epigenetic regulation, this represents an additional layer of gene expression control known as the epitranscriptome.191 Specifically, RNA modifications influence various aspects of gene expression by regulating RNA stability and localization, thereby affecting processes such as the three-dimensional structure of chromatin, the binding affinity of transcription factors to DNA, and the efficiency of protein translation.191

Notably, lactate and lactylation play important roles in regulating the epitranscriptome. m6A is the most abundant RNA modification in mammals, and dysregulation of m6A modifications has been identified as a potential therapeutic target in various cancers.192 In HCC, histone lactylation promotes YTHDC1 expression, thereby increasing the stability of the long noncoding RNA NEAT1 via increased m6A modification. This, in turn, recruits p300 to activate the SCD gene, promoting lipid metabolic reprogramming in tumors.193 In antiviral innate immunity, viral infection increases ALKBH5 lactylation, which enhances its ability to remove m6A modifications from IFN-β mRNA, thereby promoting the production of IFN-β mRNA and strengthening antiviral immune responses.194 KCNK6, a potassium channel protein, facilitates potassium efflux and promotes the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, contributing to inflammation and tumor progression. The upregulation of KCNK6 expression is jointly driven by m6A modification and histone lactylation mechanisms.195 NSUN2 is a key RNA methyltransferase. In colorectal cancer (CRC), NSUN2 acts as a novel glucose sensor, regulating the cGAS-STING immune pathway by affecting the expression of downstream TREX2.196 NSUN2 not only modulates glucose metabolic reprogramming but also promotes lactate production through an m5C-dependent mechanism. Conversely, lactate accumulation in CRC cells upregulates NSUN2 transcription through H3K18la (lactylation of histone H3 at lysine 18) and further enhances glucose metabolism by inducing NSUN2 K356la. This metabolic reprogramming forms a positive feedback loop that accelerates CRC progression.197,198 Additionally, in gastric cancer, the catalytic efficiency of NSUN2 in mediating the m5C modification of GCLC mRNA is significantly enhanced by K508la.114 In conclusion, gene expression regulation is a highly complex and multilayered process involving various stages from DNA to protein, and it occurs through diverse mechanisms—highlighting the intricate and dynamic nature of biological activity.

Tumor proliferation

The clonal evolution theory suggests that cancer cells exhibit survival characteristics as they continuously accumulate mutations, ultimately leading to the formation of malignant tumors.199 As the malignancy of cells reaches a certain threshold, unregulated tumor cell proliferation becomes the next critical stage of cancer progression.151 During this process, lactate assumes multiple roles, such as a selector and a regulator. As a selector, lactate exerts positive selection pressure on tumor cells with greater adaptability, invasiveness, and resistance to apoptosis, making such cells dominate the host microenvironment.200 In addition, as a regulator, lactate actively shapes the TME through mechanisms such as immune suppression and angiogenesis, creating favorable conditions for the sustained proliferation of cancer cells.201

Lactate regulates tumor malignancy

The normal pH of human tissues is typically maintained between 7.35 and 7.45. At an extracellular pH below 7.2, normal mammalian cells cannot survive for extended periods. Notably, the TME has a pH range of 6.7–7.1, and in some cases, it can drop below 6.0.27,202 The acidic environment serves as a selection mechanism, favoring tumor cells with enhanced adaptability and invasiveness. It also acts as a “weapon” for tumors, enhancing their malignancy and enabling them to survive and proliferate under extreme conditions, thereby driving cancer progression. Lactate levels are closely associated with tumor malignancy, with the intracellular and extracellular lactate concentrations increasing progressively across tumor stages.203,204 Royal jelly acid can interfere with lactate production and lower H3K9la and H3K14la levels to reduce the malignancy of HCC.205 In breast cancer, glycolysis is significantly increased under high-glucose conditions. Moreover, KAT2A mediates RCC2 lactylation, promoting the recruitment of SERBP1 by RCC2, stabilizing MAD2L1 mRNA, and driving tumor progression.200 In summary, lactate plays a direct and critical role in promoting the malignant proliferation of tumor cells.

Disruption of immune homeostasis

During the onset and progression of cancer, the immune system acts as a double-edged sword.206 By identifying and eliminating “nonself” or abnormal cells, the immune system helps prevent tumor formation and spread. However, with the development of cancer, tumor cells adopt various strategies to evade immune surveillance. For example, they disguise themselves as normal cells, suppress immune responses, or alter the TME to render the immune system “blind” to their presence.207 Nevertheless, the immune system remains a central force in the fight against cancer.208 Understanding and leveraging this dynamic interplay not only sheds light on the nature of cancer but also paves the way for developing more effective therapeutic strategies.

Dendritic cells (DCs) serve as critical bridges between innate and adaptive immunity. They are responsible for capturing, processing, and presenting antigens to activate T cells and initiate immune responses.209 DEC205 (CD205) is a major endocytic receptor on the surface of DCs that facilitates antigen uptake. The 12 extracellular domains of DEC205 form a compact double ring-shaped conformation; however, this structure exists only at acidic pH values.210 Furthermore, DEC205 has been reported to bind to apoptotic and necrotic cells only under acidic conditions, suggesting that the function of DCs depends on an acidic environment.210 Moreover, an acidic environment can cause unstable antigenic peptides to dissociate from MHC-I molecules. MHC-I might then bind to other antigenic peptides, thereby promoting cross-presentation.211 To explore the effects of factors other than H⁺ on DCs, Sangsuwan et al. isolated bone marrow-derived DCs from mice and treated them with sodium lactate. They reported that lactate suppresses DC maturation, as evidenced by the downregulation of CD80 and MHC II expression, especially when DCs were costimulated with LPS, suggesting that lactate reduces the activation and antigen-presenting capacity of DCs.212 A negative feedback loop involving lactate–HIF-1α–NDUFA4L2 in DCs was found to inhibit autoimmune responses.213 Lactate produced by melanoma can activate SREBP2, which induces the differentiation of DCs into CD63⁺ mregDCs. These cells suppress antigen cross-presentation and promote the differentiation of immunosuppressive Th2 and regulatory T cells (Tregs), helping tumors evade immune attack.214 Blocking MCT1 in DCs can reverse the metabolic and functional skewing induced by tumor-derived glycans, such as the sialyl-Tn antigen and fucose.215 In a clinical trial of DC vaccines in advanced melanoma patients (35 participants; NCT01622933), researchers analyzed the transcriptome and immunometabolic profiles of DCs and reported that elevated expression of MCT1 in patient-derived DCs could lead to metabolic imbalance, enhance glycolysis, and ultimately impair the antigen-presenting ability of DCs. Therefore, inhibiting MCT1 or optimizing the metabolic state of DCs could enhance the antitumor efficacy of DC vaccines.216,217 Collectively, these data indicate that lactate exerts dual regulatory effects on DCs. One hypothesis suggests that the effect of lactate on DC differentiation depends on cell density. Specifically, under low cell density, lactate promotes an antitumor phenotype, whereas under high cell density, DCs tend to suppress immune responses.218

In the early stages of carcinogenesis, T cells primarily perform antitumor immune functions. However, as tumors progress, cancer cells may gradually evolve immune evasion mechanisms, leading to T cell dysfunction and ultimately impairing the ability of the immune system to effectively control tumor growth.219 When lactate levels are high, CD4⁺ T cells tend to rely more on glycolytic metabolism. In contrast, when lactate levels are low, CD4⁺ T cells preferentially utilize oxidative phosphorylation and exhibit anti-inflammatory properties.220 Marina et al. reported that in the TME, lactate suppresses the metabolic activity of CD4⁺ T cells, inhibiting both glycolysis and mitochondrial respiration.221 Moreover, metabolic profile of NKT cells differs significantly from that of CD4⁺ T cells, particularly in terms of glucose metabolism. Specifically, NKT cells primarily depend on the pentose phosphate pathway and mitochondrial metabolism rather than on lactate production. While a high-glucose environment is critical for the survival and proliferation of NKT cells, a high-lactate environment is detrimental.222 G. Comito and colleagues identified lactate as a key immunosuppressive factor in prostate cancer. Lactate inhibits Th1 cell differentiation by promoting SIRT1-mediated deacetylation and degradation of T-bet, a critical transcription factor for Th1 cells. Simultaneously, lactate activates FOXP3 via the NF-κB pathway, promoting the polarization of naïve T cells into regulatory T cells (Tregs).223 In a mouse model of experimental autoimmune uveitis (EAU), lactylation levels in CD4⁺ T cells significantly increased as the disease progressed, and this lactate accumulation led to the lactylation of the Ikaros family zinc finger 1 protein at Lys164. This modification, in turn, directly increased the expression of genes associated with Th17 cell differentiation.224 When exogenous L-lactate (50 mM) was added to T cell cultures, the proliferation of Th1 and Th17 cells decreased by 40% and 60%, respectively.225 Interestingly, high concentrations of lactate may also inhibit tumor growth. In a mouse xenograft model, irradiated tumor cells stimulated with lactate were used as a vaccine, which significantly enhanced the antigen uptake ability of dendritic cells (DCs). Moreover, the number of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells expressing interferon-γ (IFN-γ) in the spleen and lymph nodes of mice was markedly increased.226

CD8⁺ T cells play crucial roles in antitumor immune responses227; they are the primary effector cells in the immune system responsible for directly killing tumor cells by recognizing major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I)–tumor antigen complexes on the surface of tumor cells.228 Studies have shown that the response rate of patients with NSCLC to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICBs) is influenced by obesity.229 Specifically, in patients with cancer and obesity, tumor cells exhibit increased glycolytic activity, leading to enhanced lactate accumulation, which in turn increases histone lactylation levels and upregulates PD-1 expression in CD8⁺ T cells.229 In tumors, targeting LDHC can reduce lactate production and decrease the expression of immune checkpoint receptors, such as PD-1 and CTLA-4, on the surface of CD8⁺ T cells.230 Compared with the expression of PD-L1 on tumor cell surfaces, exosomal PD-L1 and lactate levels predict clinical outcomes more accurately in patients with HER2-negative metastatic SRCC treated with ICIs combined with XELOX therapy.231 In HCC, PRMT3 enhances the kinase activity of PDHK1 by mediating asymmetric dimethylation at R363 and R368. A subsequent increase in lactate promotes tumor PD-L1 transcription through H3K18la modification. Therefore, inhibiting PRMT3 or targeting PD-L1 can restore CD8⁺ T cell function.232 Furthermore, the upregulation of H3K18la in tumors enhances the expression of B7-H3, which suppresses CD8⁺ T cell activity.233 In the STING-KO mouse model, deletion of the STING gene induced excessive glycolysis in CD8⁺ T cells and further suppressed IFN-γ secretion, inhibiting the differentiation of CD8⁺ T cells into memory T cells.234 In colorectal cancer cells, lactate upregulates the expression of the chemokines CCL2 and CCL7 by activating the HCAR1 signaling pathway. These chemokines, in turn, attract immunosuppressive cells, particularly CCR2⁺ polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells, into the TME.235 The accumulation of these immune cells suppresses the antitumor activity of CD8⁺ T cells, thereby promoting tumor growth.235 In addition to MCT1, CD8⁺ T cells also increase lactate uptake by upregulating MCT11, which accelerates the exhaustion of effector T cells.236 In contrast, knockout of the glucose transporter GLUT10 in CD8⁺ T cells inhibits glucose uptake, glycolysis, and antitumor function, as lactate can directly bind to the intracellular domain of GLUT10, thereby reducing its glucose transport capacity.237 Moreover, the entry of lactate into CD8⁺ T cells leads to lysosomal acidification, which inhibits the diacylglycerol-protein kinase Cθ signaling pathway.238 Leveraging advancements in single-cell transcriptomics and spatial transcriptomics, Deblina et al. revealed specific patterns of H3K18la and H3K9la modifications in different CD8⁺ T cell subsets, which were closely related to the unique metabolic characteristics of each subset.239

Tregs are a specialized subset of T cells that are typically characterized by the expression of CD4, CD25, and the transcription factor FOXP3. FOXP3 is a crucial marker that determines the development and function of Tregs.240 Tregs attenuate immune responses by suppressing the activity of effector T cells (such as CD4+ and CD8+ T cells) and other immune cells (including dendritic and B cells).241 This suppression helps prevent immune attacks on tissues, thereby maintaining self-tolerance and reducing the risk of autoimmune diseases. However, in tumors, extensive Treg infiltration is often associated with poor prognosis. Increasing evidence suggests that Treg depletion can induce and enhance antitumor immune responses.242 Under normal conditions, the primary metabolic pathways of Tregs are fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and OXPHOS. However, studies have shown that human thymus-derived regulatory T cells (tTregs) undergo a glycolytic switch under costimulation with CD3 and TNFR2. This metabolic shift increases intracellular lactate levels, promotes tTreg proliferation, and simultaneously maintains their cellular characteristics and suppressive function.243 Lactate upregulates FOXP3 expression and enhances Treg function. In a solid tumor model established by the subcutaneous injection of Hepa1-6 cells into B6 mice, the administration of a lactate dehydrogenase inhibitor (LDHi) significantly reduced the lactate concentration and FOXP3 expression, thereby suppressing the immunoregulatory function of Tregs in the TME.244 Further studies revealed that lactate modulates Treg development and function through lactylation of the MOESIN K72 residue. MOESIN K72la enhances transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) signaling in Tregs via TGF-βRI, activating the downstream SMAD pathway.244 However, the level of lactylation at MOESIN Lys72 does not always correlate positively with lactate levels; when the lactate concentration reaches a certain threshold, MOESIN K72la actually decreases.244 Conventional CD4⁺ T cells can be converted into Tregs under the influence of TGF-β, and lactate enhances the effect of TGF-β, thereby promoting the accumulation of Tregs. This mechanism depends on the acidic TME established by lactate rather than being a direct effect of lactate itself.245 Moreover, lactate is a key factor contributing to the reduced efficacy of CAR-T cells in solid tumors. By directly upregulating the expression of immunosuppressive genes (CD39, CD73, and CCR8) through H3K18la, lactate induces the conversion of CAR-T cells into an immunosuppressive phenotype resembling CAR-Tregs.246

Natural killer (NK) cells originate from bone marrow hematopoietic stem cells and are critical components of the innate immune system. They are capable of recognizing and eliminating virus-infected cells and tumor cells without prior exposure to specific antigens.247 In GPR132 knockout (GPR132⁻/⁻) mice, the proportion of mature NK cells is significantly increased. Moreover, NK cells from GPR132⁻/⁻ mice exhibit enhanced cytotoxicity against melanoma cells.248 Treatment of uveal melanoma (UM) cells with interleukin-6 (IL-6) significantly enhances their resistance to NK cell-mediated killing. Further studies revealed that IL-6 induces increased glycolysis in UM cells, resulting in increased lactate secretion. Lactate then upregulates PD-L1 and HLA-E expression via the GPR81–cAMP–PKA signaling pathway, thereby promoting immune evasion.249 The activity of tumor-infiltrating NK cells is pH-dependent, and increasing the pH of the microenvironment helps to restore NK cell function.250 Patients with breast cancer who exhibit high expression levels of LDHB or LDHC often have better prognoses. NK cells in breast tumors have been demonstrated to be activated through LDHB-mediated lactate clearance.251 NAD⁺ is mainly maintained through the salvage pathway, in which nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT) is the rate-limiting enzyme. Exogenous supplementation with NAD⁺ enhances NK cell cytokine production, cytotoxicity, and survival. However, lactate accumulation in the TME is a key factor that reduces NAMPT expression.252 Notably, compared with conventional NK cells, tissue-resident NK (trNK) cells are more significantly and severely inhibited by lactate. For example, in the liver—a metabolically active organ—lactate accumulation is often prominent, particularly under hypoxic or inflammatory conditions. The heightened sensitivity of hepatic trNK cells to lactate is largely attributed to their relatively limited mitochondrial function, which increases their susceptibility to metabolic dysfunction and oxidative stress in high-lactate environments.253

B cells are central to humoral immunity and play multiple roles in cancer. In addition to their antitumor effects via antibody production, B cells can regulate immune responses through antigen presentation, cytokine secretion, and other mechanisms.254,255 Recent studies have shown that lactate can promote immunosenescence in B cells. Notably, senescent B cells exhibit increased oxidative phosphorylation and glycolytic activity. These metabolically active B cells release lactate, which enhances their antibody production and proinflammatory responses and drives the polarization of T cells toward proinflammatory CD4⁺ T cell phenotypes.256,257 However, currently, studies on the effects of lactate on B cells within tumors are limited, necessitating further research on how lactate influences B-cell differentiation, antibody production, and their immunoregulatory roles within the TME.

Inflammation

Inflammatory responses within the TME are widely recognized as key drivers of cancer progression.258 Persistent inflammatory signaling can promote tumor cell survival, proliferation, and invasion. The role of inflammation in cancer depends on its duration, type, and the status of the immune system. While acute inflammation may help eliminate cancer cells by activating the immune response, chronic inflammation is often a risk factor for various types of cancer.259 As early as the last century, scholars proposed that cancer is akin to a wound that does not heal.260 During this process, significant changes occur among various cell types within the microenvironment, collectively promoting tumor progression. For example, under inflammatory stimulation, epithelial cells undergo genetic mutations and uncontrolled proliferation and may acquire invasive and migratory capabilities through epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT).261,262 Macrophages shift from the antitumor M1 phenotype to the protumor M2 phenotype, secreting TGF-β and VEGF to promote angiogenesis and immune evasion.263,264 Neutrophils can form neutrophil extracellular traps and release proinflammatory factors, exacerbating the inflammatory response.265 Therefore, proper regulation of inflammation is crucial in cancer prevention and treatment. Currently, COX-2 inhibitors, such as aspirin, have been shown to reduce the incidence of certain cancers, such as colorectal cancer.266

Specifically, during chronic inflammation, CD4⁺ T cells upregulate the expression of SLC5A12, leading to increased lactate uptake. Consequently, CD4⁺ T cells increase IL-17 secretion and become retained in inflamed tissues, exacerbating chronic inflammatory diseases.267 Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are recruited to and activated within the TME and primarily originate from circulating monocytes. During the early stages of tumor development, TAMs typically exhibit an immune-promoting phenotype with proinflammatory and antitumor functions, similar to the M1 phenotype.268 Factors in the TME, such as lactate accumulation and hypoxia, can subsequently induce metabolic reprogramming in TAMs, resulting in an immunosuppressive phenotype resembling the M2 phenotype.268 This shift facilitates tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis, which are associated with poor prognosis. Increased lactylation of macrophage proteins promotes TAM polarization toward the M2 phenotype, thereby enhancing tumor malignancy.269,270 For example, in bladder cancer—particularly muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC)—H3K18la promotes the polarization of M2-type macrophages by regulating PRKN-mediated mitophagy.271 Additionally, lactate secreted by cervical cancer cells can upregulate H3K18la in macrophages, thereby increasing the expression of GPD2 and further promoting M2 macrophage polarization.272 Notably, although tumors produce large amounts of lactate during disease progression, macrophages can also increase endogenous lactate production by increasing glycolysis.273 In conclusion, sustained inflammatory state and lactate production are critical components that contribute to malignant tumor progression.

However, in certain nontumor diseases, the anti-inflammatory effect of lactate serves as a signaling mechanism for improving pathological conditions. In glycogen storage disease type Ib (GSD-Ib), the accumulation of lactate and H3K18la in macrophages due to G6PT deficiency leads to the upregulation of ALKBH5 expression. This, in turn, reduces the m6A modification of NLRP3 mRNA, thereby inhibiting the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome.274 Chen et al. were the first to reveal that exercise-induced lactate can exert antiatherosclerotic effects through the MeCP2 K271la–H3K36me3/RUNX1 axis and that enhancing MeCP2 K271la promotes the infiltration of pro-repair M2 macrophages.275

In summary, lactate plays distinct pleiotropic roles in different inflammatory contexts. Its role in any disease is dependent on the tissue microenvironment and metabolic adaptation, offering new perspectives for precise tumor interventions.

Extracellular matrix remodeling

During dynamic changes in the TME, the extracellular matrix (ECM) is remodeled, leading to alterations in its structure and composition that create a more favorable environment for tumor growth.276 The ECM is a crucial noncellular component of the tissue microenvironment. It is a highly dynamic three-dimensional network primarily composed of structural proteins, adhesive glycoproteins, proteoglycans, and their glycosaminoglycan side chains, as well as a variety of regulatory molecules involved in its assembly and degradation.277 Fibroblasts are mesenchymal-derived cells whose main function is to synthesize and maintain the ECM, including components such as collagen, fibronectin, and hyaluronic acid. Under normal conditions, fibroblasts play essential roles in tissue repair, wound healing, and maintenance of structural integrity.278 However, fibroblasts exhibit remarkable plasticity within the TME. Tumors can induce fibroblasts to transform into CAFs through various mechanisms, including cytokine signaling, metabolic reprogramming, direct cell‒cell contact, and microenvironmental stress.278 Notably, CAFs constitute a highly heterogeneous cell population. In addition to quiescent fibroblasts residing in the host tissue, their origins include mesenchymal stem cells, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, macrophages, pericytes, and adipocytes.279

CAFs are among the key supporters of tumor progression. The development of various types of tumors is driven by the aberrant activation of fibroblasts, particularly in malignancies characterized by significant tissue stiffening and ECM remodeling.151 HCC often arises in the context of liver fibrosis or cirrhosis, where chronic inflammation and collagen deposition reshape the TME, enhancing tumor cell proliferation and invasion.280 In patients with liver fibrosis, IGF2BP2 is frequently overexpressed and binds to m6A-modified ALDOA mRNA, increasing its stability and translation efficiency.281,282 ALDOA is a key enzyme in glycolysis regulation, and its upregulation leads lactate accumulation, driving histone lactylation in hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and facilitating their differentiation into myofibroblasts.282 Lung adenocarcinoma is closely associated with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF).283 Lactylation, particularly at H3K18la, exacerbates m6A-mediated effects by regulating YTHDF1 expression, thereby promoting the progression of pulmonary fibrosis.284 In the lung, activation of the glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) can reduce histone lactylation and the expression of profibrotic genes by inhibiting the NLRP3 inflammasome and PFKFB3-mediated glycolysis, thus exerting antifibrotic effects.285 Furthermore, GLP-1R helps preserve mitochondrial function and lowers oxidative phosphorylation levels, further reducing the activation of pulmonary fibroblasts.285 Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is known for its dense stromal components and extensive fibrosis. Activated CAFs contribute to the formation of an immunosuppressive environment by secreting cytokines and ECM components.286 In breast cancer, particularly triple-negative breast cancer, tumors exhibit greater invasiveness in highly fibrotic tissues.287

Although CAF formation is highly dependent on the active glycolytic metabolism of tumor cells,288,289 as the TME continues to evolve, CAFs themselves can undergo significant metabolic reprogramming, particularly with sustained enhancement of the glycolytic pathway.290,291 Under stimulation by tumor cell-derived growth factors, inflammatory cytokines, and lactate, the metabolic profile of CAFs changes dramatically. The glycolytic capacity of these cells is markedly increased, accompanied by enhanced glucose uptake, upregulated expression of key glycolytic enzymes, and elevated lactate production. This process allows CAFs to remain highly active in the harsh TME and supports metabolic symbiosis between the tumor and the stroma.292 In other words, lactate secreted by CAFs is taken up by tumor cells as a metabolic substrate, providing an additional energy source to support rapid tumor cell proliferation and migration; this phenomenon is known as the reverse Warburg effect.293 Notably, CAFs can further induce tumor cells to upregulate the expression of glycolysis-related enzymes and the lactate transporter MCT1, increasing their lactate uptake and utilization efficiency and forming a positive metabolic feedback loop.294

The importance of the reverse Warburg effect extends beyond the energy supply. In breast cancer, lactate released by CAFs enters tumor cells and promotes their invasion and metastasis primarily by activating the TGFβ1/p38 MAPK/MMP2/9 pathway and increasing mitochondrial activity.295 Through enhanced glycolysis and lactate secretion, CAFs can induce histone H3K18 lactylation in tumor cells, activating the transcription of perineural invasion (PNI)-related genes, such as L1CAM and SLIT1, ultimately promoting neural invasion in pancreatic cancer.291 CAF-secreted lactate can significantly reduce the pH of the TME, forming an acidic barrier that effectively suppresses the activity of immune cells, including T cells, thereby facilitating immune evasion.223 In SCLC cells, CAFs can increase glycolysis and activate the STING signaling pathway, upregulating the expression of T cell chemokines, including CCL5, CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11; however, they fail to effectively promote CD8⁺ T cell infiltration.296 Conversely, SCLC cells may convert CAFs into antigen-presenting CAFs, increasing the number of Tregs and establishing an immunosuppressive environment.296

In summary, tumor cells can induce CAF formation and upregulate aerobic glycolysis through lactate accumulation. Subsequently, CAFs promote tumor malignancy by secreting lactate, which contributes to the energy supply, metabolic adaptation, immune evasion, and microenvironmental remodeling. This positive feedback loop between tumor cells and tumor-supportive stromal cells serves as a crucial driver within the tumor metabolic network.

Angiogenesis

Rapid tumor cell proliferation requires a large amount of oxygen and nutrients such as glucose and amino acids.297 As tumor volume increases, simple diffusion becomes insufficient; thus, tumors larger than 1 mm in diameter establish a blood supply system to sustain accelerated growth and expansion.298

Abnormal endothelial cell proliferation plays a central role in tumor angiogenesis.299 Compared with quiescent endothelial cells in normal blood vessels, in tumor blood vessels, endothelial cells have a considerably increased metabolic demand for glucose, particularly during tumor-induced angiogenesis.300 Therefore, a lack of glucose in the stroma can lead to defective tumor angiogenesis and areas of reduced vascularity within the tumor. Paradoxically, stromal lactate acidosis also stimulates angiogenesis. In tumors, lactate levels are positively correlated with the levels of various angiogenesis-promoting factors.301 Lactate-induced endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in endothelial cells increases their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory capabilities.302 While glucose availability is suggested to be the initial foundation for new blood vessel formation, subsequent blood vessel maturation relies on long-term lactate stimulation. In noncancerous tissues, proliferating endothelial cells produce lactate and ATP through aerobic glycolysis during angiogenesis.300 Therefore, regardless of the use of noncancerous or cancerous cells, lactate is required for the biosynthesis of the cellular components necessary for growth and tissue expansion.

Single-cell spatial transcriptomic analysis has demonstrated that GP73-overexpressing HCC cells can communicate with vascular endothelial cells and promote angiogenesis through multiple receptor–ligand interactions.303 Histone lactylation enhances GP73 expression, which in turn promotes angiogenesis via the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway and the ER stress signaling pathway.303 Végran et al. reported that lactate entering endothelial cells via MCT1 activates the NF-κB/IL-8 signaling pathway by promoting IκBα phosphorylation, ultimately enhancing cell migration and angiogenesis.49 These findings suggest that inhibiting MCT1 may be a promising antiangiogenic strategy. Moreover, lactate can induce aberrant neovessels to form an “immune barrier,” establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment by modulating immune cell recruitment and activity.304,305 VEGF, a key factor secreted by tumor tissues to stimulate neovascularization, can also upregulate H3K9la levels after entering endothelial cells, suppressing HDAC2 expression. H3K9la and HDAC2 form a positive feedback loop that continuously amplifies VEGF-induced angiogenic signaling.306 Under hypoxic and lactate-rich conditions, endothelial cells upregulate VEGFR2 to synergize with VEGF.49,307 Furthermore, DKK2 promotes aerobic glycolysis in tumor cells, leading to lactate accumulation in the microenvironment. As the final effector in a VEGF-independent, nonclassical pathway, lactate can directly stimulate angiogenesis.308 Additionally, MMPs released by the ECM can degrade matrix components, facilitating endothelial cell migration and new blood vessel formation.309,310

Tumor invasion and metastasis

Local invasion

A positive feedback loop among angiogenesis, inflammation, and fibrosis collectively establishes a microenvironment that enables tumor cells breach the basement membrane and invade adjacent tissues, driving the tumor into a locally invasive phase. This transition is critical for malignant transformation and metastasis, characterized by rapid tumor cell proliferation, dissociation from neighboring cells, and infiltration into the surrounding stroma. At the molecular level, this process is regulated by coordinated mechanisms, such as EMT, activation of matrix-degrading enzymes, and cytoskeletal remodeling.

EMT enables tumor cells to acquire enhanced migratory and invasive capabilities, characterized by the downregulation of adhesion molecules (such as E-cadherin), loss of cell polarity, and increased motility.313 During invasion, tumor cells can progressively infiltrate neighboring adipose tissue, muscle layers, nerves, blood vessels, or organ parenchyma, resulting in structural damage and functional impairment. Lactate promotes EMT in multiple cancers, including HCC, pancreatic cancer, gastric cancer, and CRC.313–316 Supplementing lactate in a time-dependent manner can downregulate the expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin and upregulate that of the mesenchymal markers N-cadherin and vimentin in HepG2 cells.315 However, in CRC cells, E-cadherin levels may remain unchanged after lactate treatment, whereas EMT-related genes (N-CADHERIN, ZEB1, VIMENTIN, and SNAIL) are upregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels.316 This discrepancy could be attributed to differences in lactate concentration. For example, high lactate concentrations induce EMT in alveolar epithelial cells, whereas moderate concentrations do not.317 Moreover, high lactate levels can directly induce Snail1 lactylation, promoting its nuclear localization and initiating EMT.313 Compound Kushen injection (CKI), a traditional Chinese medicine commonly used as an adjuvant therapy for cancer, inhibits EMT and metastasis by suppressing key glycolytic metabolites (e.g., citrate and lactate) and enzymes (HK and PK).318 In addition to undergoing EMT, tumor cells and related components in the TME, such as CAFs and macrophages, can secrete large amounts of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). These enzymes degrade the basement membrane and ECM, promoting tumor cell dissemination.319

Distant metastasis

Distant metastasis typically indicates the onset of advanced-stage (stage IV) cancer and is the leading cause of mortality in most patients with malignant tumors. It is a complex, multistep process that involves intravasation into blood or lymphatic vessels, circulation, extravasation, and subsequent colonization.

Infiltration

During the process of tumor metastasis, newly formed blood vessels differ significantly from those in normal tissues in both structure and function. While normal blood vessels are characterized by orderly structures, tightly aligned endothelial cells, intact basement membranes, and stable blood flow, the tumor-associated vasculature typically exhibits structural disorganization, morphological distortion, incomplete vessel walls, and increased permeability.320 In metastatic tumors, increased glycolytic activity is closely associated with increased tumor blood vessel permeability.321 Inhibiting glycolysis in tumor endothelial cells can achieve tumor vessel normalization, which represents a novel approach to cancer therapy.322 Additionally, in cancerous tissues, large amounts of protons (H⁺) and lactate are produced by the metabolism of tumor cells and inflammatory cells, leading to local acidification.323 This low-pH microenvironment affects tumor cells and surrounding stromal cells, such as remodeling human dermal lymphatic endothelial cells, thereby promoting lymphatic metastasis.323 Notably, although the tumor neovasculature typically exhibits high permeability, it paradoxically poses a barrier to tumor-suppressive immune cell infiltration. Specifically, tumor cells can penetrate the vascular barrier more readily than normal cells, facilitating invasion and metastasis. In contrast, effector T cells, NK cells, and other antitumor immune cells struggle to effectively enter the tumor interior owing to the abnormal vascular structures and the selective barrier functions of endothelial cells.320,324

Circulation

High circulating tumor cell (CTC) counts are positively correlated with poor survival rates in patients with cancer.325 Although thousands of CTCs are shed into the bloodstream daily, most die rapidly, as they face two major survival threats: mechanical forces, including hemodynamic shear stress, and biological responses, such as immune system attacks.

To survive, CTCs must adopt survival strategies, with the formation of CTC clusters being the most critical. Ki67-positivity is more prevalent in CTC clusters than in CTCs.326 Notably, although EMT typically reduces intercellular adhesion and promotes tumor cell dissemination, lactate can paradoxically enhance CTC cluster formation and stability by upregulating CD44 expression and increasing cellular stemness.327,328 Therefore, lactate plays a dual regulatory role in both EMT and cell aggregation, and its function in the metastatic cascade should be interpreted in the context of temporal and microenvironmental dynamics. Moreover, CTCs can resist the shear stress induced by physiological blood flow, a property that may be partially attributed to EMT plasticity within CTC clusters.329

Platelets circulating in the blood of patients with malignant tumors are critical for CTC survival.330 Lactate induces the expression of adhesion molecules, such as integrins and CD44, on CTCs, increasing their binding capacity to platelets. These heterotypic CTC clusters protect CTCs from shear stress and shield tumor antigens, enabling them to evade recognition and elimination by NK cells.331 Moreover, the number of circulating NK cells is negatively correlated with serum LDH levels.332 Lactate can also regulate the release of prometastatic factors, such as TGF-β and PDGF, from platelets via the GPR81 receptor. Within the TME, platelets sustain high TGF-β activity through the GARP–TGF-β axis, continuously suppressing T cell functions.333 Therefore, combining antiplatelet therapies, such as aspirin and clopidogrel, may represent a promising strategy to increase immunotherapy efficacy.

Extravasation

Tumor cell extravasation involves multiple highly coordinated steps. First, a small subset of CTCs that survive in the bloodstream disseminate to distant organs via the circulation.334 Within microvascular networks, CTCs may be passively retained or mechanically trapped owing to slowed blood flow or the narrow diameter of capillaries. Additionally, CTCs can actively adhere to the endothelium through specific interactions with endothelial adhesion molecules, including sE-selectin, ICAM-1, and VCAM-1.335,336 ATP and lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) are two key effector molecules involved in endothelial barrier disruption and tumor invasiveness.337 CD97, an adhesive G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) expressed on the surface of tumor cells, plays a crucial role by directly activating platelets to release ATP and LPA. It forms a signaling complex with LPA receptors on endothelial cells, facilitating both transendothelial migration and metastatic dissemination.337 Lactate is highly associated with the invasive structures of extravasating tumor cells.338 For example, MCT4/CD147 colocalizes with invadopodia-specific markers, such as cortactin, suggesting their direct involvement in invadopodia formation. MCT4/CD147 colocalizes with MMP14 and is present within lysosomes (marked by LAMP1) and partially within autophagosomes (marked by LC3). These vesicles are wrapped by F-actin and transported to invadopodia via the cytoskeleton, indicating a critical synergistic role of lactate in the formation and function of invadopodia.338 Tumor cells also create an acidic microenvironment by exporting protons through Na⁺/H⁺ exchangers and H⁺/lactate cotransporters, which degrade the ECM and facilitate the formation of invasive protrusions.339 Notably, inhibiting tumor glycolysis significantly reduces the formation of invasive and lamellipodial protrusions.340,341

Cell colonization

Although CTCs can successfully extravasate into secondary organs, they still face formidable challenges in the new microenvironment. A vast majority fail to colonize due to incompatibility with the local niche or immune system clearance. Even after extravasation, metastasis remains a highly selective and inefficient process. However, unlike the complex steps and microenvironmental adaptations required for primary tumor formation, the survival and proliferation of disseminated tumor cells in secondary organs are relatively easy. This is because the distant microenvironment has already been remodeled by the primary tumor, rendering it more receptive to colonization. This concept aligns with the “seed and soil hypothesis,” first proposed by Stephen Paget in 1889, which posits that tumor cells (the “seeds”) can thrive only in favorable microenvironments (the “soil”).342 Building upon this theory, Lyden et al. introduced the concept of the “premetastatic niche” (PMN) in 2005. They suggested that the primary tumor systemically conditions distant organs by secreting exosomes, cytokines, and other factors, inducing local immunosuppression, increased vascular permeability, recruitment of bone marrow-derived cells, and ECM remodeling. Together, these processes establish a “pretransplantation microenvironment” that supports subsequent tumor cell colonization and expansion.343

In an HCC lung metastasis model, lactate promoted Rab7A lactylation in an MCT1-dependent manner, regulating the directional transport of multivesicular bodies and facilitating the formation of tumor-derived exosomes (TDEs).344 These TDEs are enriched in integrin β4 and ECM remodeling-associated proteins, contributing to the establishment of the PMN. Additionally, liver cancer cells activate glycolysis through ITGB4, leading to high lactate production, which in turn activates and recruits CAFs to help establish the PMN under hypoxic conditions, thereby promoting tumor metastasis.345 The acidic TME is not only confined to primary tumors but also extends to target organs before metastasis, such as the lungs. Matsui et al. reported that conditioned medium from breast cancer cells could induce acidification in the lungs. Specifically, tumor cells release exosomes enriched with glycolysis-related proteins into secondary sites, promoting PMN formation via increased glycolysis and lactate accumulation.346 Moreover, TDEs can induce macrophages to highly express PD-L1, resulting in an immunosuppressive phenotype.347 TLR2s on macrophages recognize TDEs and activate the downstream NF-κB signaling pathway, which increases glycolytic activity and further exacerbates lactate accumulation at secondary sites.347 CAFs also contribute to PMN acidification by upregulating MCT4.348 In CRC bone metastasis, lactate was identified as a key factor in shaping the bone metastatic niche. In preosteoclasts, lactate activates the PI3K‒AKT pathway, modulating the expression of two core factors: CXCL10 and cadherin-11. It recruits CD4⁺ T cells via CXCR3 signaling to secrete RANKL, promoting osteoclast differentiation. It also enhances collagen deposition, remodeling the fibrotic microenvironment and providing a scaffold conducive to tumor colonization in bone.349 Highly metastatic ovarian cancer cells can increase lactate production in mesothelial cells through P-cadherin signaling, providing abundant substrates for fueling de novo lipogenesis in the metastatic niche.350 Thus, lactate acts as a critical mediator linking the primary tumor to the microenvironment of distant metastatic sites, playing a key role in PMN formation; this significantly deepens our understanding of the mechanisms underlying tumor metastasis and provides insights for identifying novel therapeutic targets (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Tumorigenesis and progression. Recurrence

In patients with cancer, LDH levels can serve as an independent prognostic indicator of recurrence rates.351 Elevated lactate levels are also closely associated with increased recurrence rates in various cancers.352 A widely accepted explanation for tumor recurrence is the presence of dormant tumor cells; this process involves both the formation of dormant cells and their subsequent reactivation.353,354 Dormant tumor cells exist in a nonproliferative or low-proliferative state and can survive in the body for extended periods. Under the right conditions, they can be reawakened and lead to tumor relapse or distant metastasis.353,354 Notably, concepts such as cancer stem cells (CSCs) and drug-resistant cells are frequently discussed alongside tumor dormancy and recurrence. Although these cell types are associated with tumor persistence, they are biologically distinct and emphasize different aspects of cancer biology. CSCs are a subpopulation of tumor cells that can self-renew and differentiate into multiple lineages.355 Alternatively, drug-resistant cells are tumor cells that exhibit reduced sensitivity or complete resistance to anticancer drugs.356 Many CSCs possess the capacity for dormancy, allowing them to survive harsh microenvironmental conditions by entering a quiescent state, thereby evading immune surveillance and therapeutic pressure.357,358 Furthermore, tumor cell dormancy is closely linked to drug resistance, which is discussed in detail in the next section.

Lactate impacts tumor recurrence through multiple mechanisms. As discussed earlier, lactate plays a pivotal role in promoting distant metastasis. Most tumor cells that successfully extravasate into distant organs initially survive in a dormant state within the foreign microenvironment. Following a series of lactate-mediated adaptive regulatory processes, these tumor cells may transition from quiescence to activation, re-enter the proliferative cycle, and eventually form overt metastatic lesions. Moreover, lactate maintains CSC stemness and promotes the dedifferentiation of cancer cells into CSCs, increasing the risk of tumor recurrence. For example, lactate derived from CAFs negatively regulates the Hippo signaling pathway via the DLG5/CUL3/MST1 axis, promoting YAP1 nuclear localization and enhancing CSC-like phenotypes.359 In prostate cancer, CAF-derived lactate promotes collagen type I synthesis and activates the collagen hydroxylase P4HA1. Luigi et al. proposed the P4HA1–DDR1–STAT3 signaling axis as a key pathway driving tumor stemness and metastatic potential.360 Notably, although CSCs exhibit dormant properties, their intracellular lactate levels are significantly higher than those of typical tumor cells. Conversely, classical dormant tumor cells tend to maintain a low metabolic profile.

Previously, disseminated tumor cells were considered insensitive to chemotherapy owing to their quiescent state, as most chemotherapeutic agents preferentially target actively proliferating cells. However, even nondormant disseminated tumor cells can resist chemotherapy.361 This apparent contradiction in metabolic characteristics suggests that lactate may play a subtle and multifaceted role in regulating stem-like cell dormancy, warranting further investigation.

Drug resistance

Drug resistance affects all stages of tumor progression, posing a major challenge in current cancer therapy. It can be broadly classified into two categories: intrinsic (primary) and acquired (secondary).362 Primary resistance refers to tumors that exhibit minimal or no response to standard treatments, including chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy, from the start of treatment.363 In contrast, secondary resistance arises after an initial period of therapeutic efficacy, during which tumors gradually acquire resistance under ongoing selective treatment pressure, ultimately leading to disease progression or reccurence.364

In recent years, the relationship between lactate and tumor drug resistance has garnered significant attention. A complex interplay exists between lactate and drug resistance-related genes. Lactate accumulation can induce genomic instability, promoting mutations and the selective amplification of resistance-associated genes such as KRAS.365 Moreover, in tumor cells harboring resistance-related genetic alterations, lactate may further enhance treatment adaptability by reprogramming metabolic pathways. For example, KRAS mutations are commonly associated with resistance to EGFR-targeted therapies, including cetuximab.366,367 Richiardone et al. reported that KRAS-mutant cetuximab-resistant CRC cells (LIM1215 and OXCO2) exhibit significantly altered metabolism, including elevated glycolytic activity, and that inhibiting MCT1 significantly reduces lactate uptake and oxidation in LIM1215 cells. They suggested a potential strategy to overcome KRAS mutation-induced anti-EGFR resistance.368 Mutations in STK11 (also known as LKB1), a tumor suppressor gene, drive resistance to immunotherapy in KRAS-mutant lung adenocarcinoma. Moreover, targeting MCT4 or the lactate receptor GPR81 holds promise for enhancing immunotherapeutic responses in such contexts.369

Second, lactate can drive drug efflux, reducing intracellular drug concentrations. For example, lactate upregulates the expression of the deubiquitinase USP34 in HCC cells, increasing the expression of resistance-associated efflux proteins, such as MRP1 and P-gp, and contributes to both primary and acquired drug resistance.370,371

Lactate also acts as an antioxidant, helping tumor cells adapt to excessive oxidative stress. By reversing ROS-induced DNA/RNA damage, lactate mediates resistance to therapy.372 Furthermore, lactate can increase the expression or function of DNA repair-related genes, increasing the capacity of tumor cells to respond to chemotherapy-induced DNA damage.373 For example, NBS1 K388la enhances the formation and function of the MRN complex, promoting HR repair pathway activation.102 MRE11 K673la increases DNA-binding affinity, and inhibiting CBP or LDH improves tumor cell sensitivity to chemotherapy.96 Both L-lactate and D-lactate can inhibit HDACs, thereby increasing histone H3 and H4 acetylation, altering chromatin structure, and promoting the expression of DNA repair genes, such as LIG4, NBS1, and APTX.374 However, Watanabe et al. reported an opposing view, whereby excessive lactate production may suppress HR-related gene expression by reducing histone acetylation.375

Beyond its direct effects on tumor cells, lactate also promotes acquired drug resistance by modulating the TME. In TNBC, CAFs promote histone lactylation through lactate, upregulating ZFP64 expression and activating the GCH1/FTH1 axis, ultimately mediating resistance to doxorubicin.376 Using single-cell analysis, Chen et al. identified a novel CAF subset (CTHRC1⁺ CAFs) that enhances cancer cell glycolysis via the TGF-β/Smad3 pathway, establishing a CTHRC1/glycolysis/H3K18la positive feedback loop that drives EGFR-TKI resistance in lung cancer. Notably, this loop is disrupted by the natural compound gambogenic acid, which sensitizes tumors to EGFR-TKIs and offers a promising therapeutic strategy for overcoming resistance.377

In summary, lactate plays dual roles in malignant tumor progression and therapeutic resistance. Specifically, it significantly enhances the malignant potential of tumor cells under extreme conditions, such as hypoxia and acidosis, by inducing genomic instability, activating oncogenic signaling pathways, and remodeling the TME.365 This contributes to increased invasiveness and the adaptive evolution of cancer cells. Lactate also upregulates the expression of genes related to DNA damage repair and strengthens the capacity of tumor cells to repair chemotherapy-induced DNA lesions, improving their survival under therapeutic pressure.373 This seemingly contradictory phenomenon of “change” and “constancy” reflects the remarkable plasticity and adaptability of tumor cells within a complex microenvironment. Fundamentally, both processes represent the same evolutionary strategy—preserving survival advantages and driving sustained tumor progression.

Lactate-related signal transduction in cancer progression

In recent years, the role of lactate as a signaling molecule has gained widespread attention in tumor biology.378 Lactate alters the local microenvironment through diffusion. It also acts as a ligand with a direct role in signal transduction, participating in multilayered intracellular and extracellular regulatory networks. GPR81 (also known as HCAR1) is a classical GPCR for lactate. Its activation inhibits cAMP production and promotes tumor growth and immune suppression in various solid tumors.379 Another receptor, GPR132 (also known as G2A), senses lactate-induced acidic stress and regulates the polarization of immune cells, such as macrophages, indirectly shaping the tumor immune microenvironment and influencing cancer progression.380 In addition to its role in classical receptor-mediated pathways, lactate can also function intracellularly, stabilizing the protein NDRG3, preventing its degradation by PHD2, and subsequently activating the Raf-MEK-ERK signaling cascade to promote cell proliferation and angiogenesis. This establishes an HIF-independent mechanism for hypoxia adaptation.381 Lactate can also directly affect cell signaling and fate determination by modulating the posttranslational modifications of both histones and nonhistone proteins.149,382

PI3K/AKT pathway

The PI3K/AKT pathway, a frequently aberrantly activated signaling pathway in cancer, plays a crucial role in promoting cell proliferation, survival, and metabolic reprogramming. Tumors often upregulate lactate production by activating both the PI3K/AKT and HIF pathways.383,384 In human HCC cells, all three AKT isoforms, namely AKT1, AKT2, and AKT3, can independently increase the oxygen consumption rate, extracellular acidification rate, and lactate production by regulating pyruvate dehydrogenase.385 PI3K inhibition significantly reduces lactylation levels within tumor cells and reverses the immunosuppressive state of the TME.386 Histone lactylation promotes malignant progression by facilitating USP39 expression to target the PI3K/AKT/HIF-1 alpha signaling pathway in endometrial carcinoma.387 During tumor angiogenesis, lactate contributes to PI3K/AKT pathway activation through ligand-mediated stimulation of three receptor tyrosine kinases: Axl, TEK (Tie2), and VEGFR2.388 Therefore, further investigation into the relationship between lactate and the PI3K/AKT axis may provide new insights for developing targeted anticancer therapies.

Hippo pathway

The Hippo pathway primarily regulates the activity of the transcription factors YAP and TAZ, controlling contact inhibition, cell growth, and stemness maintenance.389 This pathway is commonly dysregulated in tumorigenesis. In hepatic metastasis originating from CRC, GPR37 promotes glycolysis and H3K18 lactylation by activating the Hippo pathway, suggesting its potential as a novel therapeutic target.390 However, Xie et al. reported that circXRN2 activates the Hippo pathway by inhibiting SPOP-mediated degradation of LATS1 and downregulating H3K18 lactylation and LCN2 expression. This ultimately suppresses glycolytic metabolism, cell proliferation, and metastatic potential in bladder cancer.382 These findings suggest that the Hippo pathway may play a dual regulatory role according to the tumor type or molecular context. AARS1 mediates the lactylation of multiple proteins involved in the Hippo pathway, including ACTG1, MOB1A, PPP1CA, YAP, and TEAD1.116,391 Furthermore, lactylation of the YAP–TEAD complex promotes AARS1 transcription and translation, forming a positive feedback loop between AARS1 and YAP–TEAD lactylation.116 Lactate upregulates CD38 expression through the OXPHOS-driven Hippo–TAZ pathway, promoting lung cancer cell invasion and metastasis.392 Thus, the lactate–Hippo signaling axis may serve as a crucial bridge linking metabolic states with tumor heterogeneity.

MAPK pathway

The MAPK pathway plays a central role in cellular stress responses, proliferation, and apoptosis regulation, and is classified into three major subgroups: ERK, JNK, and p38.393 Nucleolin upregulates MADD through an RNA splicing-dependent lactylation mechanism, promoting intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma development via the MAPK pathway.87

Under glucose-deprived conditions, p38, ERK1/2, and JNK phosphorylation significantly increase in pancreatic cancer cells, whereas L-lactate effectively maintains cell viability and reverses p38 MAPK-associated stress signaling.134 Under chronic inflammatory conditions, lipopolysaccharide induces PDK4 expression by activating the JNK pathway instead of p38 or ERK, thereby inhibiting the activity of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex and promoting lactate production.394 Within the TME, lactate alters ERK and p38 MAPK signaling cascades in DCs, impairing their antigen-presenting capacity.395 A novel small-molecule ion carrier, Lactrans-1, enhances lactate transmembrane transport through an MCT-independent mechanism, disrupting lactate homeostasis in cancer cells. Lactrans-1 activates p38-MAPK and JNK/SAPK oxidative stress responses and enhances the therapeutic efficacy of cisplatin.396 Therefore, distinct MAPK pathway subtypes have complex and precise interactions with lactate regulation. This underscores the need to thoroughly understand the mechanisms by which ERK, JNK, and p38 differentially regulate lactate production, transport, and function under various pathological conditions.

Wnt pathway

Wnt signaling is a highly conserved developmental pathway that plays a critical role in regulating stem cell properties, EMT, and tumor drug resistance. In tumors, Wnt pathway activation often leads to elevated aerobic glycolysis levels.397 Additionally, the Wnt pathway and c-Myc can independently and synergistically upregulate MCT1 expression, increasing intratumoral lactate accumulation.398 β-catenin is the central signaling molecule of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway.399 Hypoxia-induced β-catenin lactylation promotes cell proliferation and stemness in CRC through the Wnt signaling pathway.400 The lactate/ESM1/SCD1 axis can promote platinum resistance in ovarian cancer by activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and impairing the antitumor immunity of CD8⁺ T cells.401 Moreover, combined treatment with the inhibitors copanlisib, trametinib, and LGK974, targeting PI3K, MEK, and porcupine, respectively, suppresses the PI3K/AKT, MAPK, and Wnt/β-catenin pathways. In PTEN/p53 double-deficient GEM mouse prostate cancer cells, this combination significantly reduces both lactate levels and H3K18 lactylation, enhancing the phagocytic function of TAMs.397

In summary, aberrant activation of the Wnt pathway promotes tumor progression by increasing lactate production and accumulation, providing critical support for cancer development and immune evasion.

JAK–STAT pathway

The JAK–STAT pathway is a key signaling cascade that responds to cytokines and growth factors and plays a particularly significant role in immune regulation and inflammatory responses.

In TAMs, the STAT1/GSK-3β signaling axis functions as a critical immune regulatory pathway and is strongly modulated by lactate levels. This axis represents one of the primary mechanisms by which lactate mediates the phenotypic and functional reprogramming of macrophages.395 Moreover, H3K18la enhances the expression and function of METTL3, activating the JAK1–STAT3 pathway in tissue-infiltrating macrophages and contributing to tumor immune evasion.402 STAT5 can also indirectly regulate lactate production by modulating HIF-1α expression.403 Lactate acts as an epigenetic modulator in microglia, indirectly activating the JAK/STAT signaling pathway in astrocyte progenitors by upregulating LRRC15.404

Notch pathway

The Notch signaling pathway is a crucial regulator of cell fate and plays a key role in tumor cell differentiation, proliferation, and drug resistance. Its function is highly dependent on specific cell types and the microenvironment, regulating glycolysis and lactate production in a context-dependent manner. For example, in HCC, this pathway promotes glycolysis by upregulating the expression of hexokinase 2, thereby increasing lactate production, which supplies energy and confers a survival advantage to tumor cells.405 Conversely, in testicular Sertoli cells, Notch pathway activation inhibits glycolysis-related gene expression, reducing lactate supply and impairing spermatogenesis.406 In gastric cancer, USP24 upregulation stabilizes PLK1 through deubiquitination, which in turn activates the Notch1 signaling pathway to promote gastric cancer cell glycolysis and proliferation.407 In myeloid-derived suppressor cells and TAMs, Notch pathway activation suppresses transcription of the lactate transporter MCT2 via its downstream effector Hes1, resulting in decreased intracellular lactate levels.408

In summary, glycolytic gene regulation by the Notch signaling pathway clearly demonstrates temporal and tissue specificity.409,410

NF-κB pathway

The NF-κB pathway is a classic signaling cascade involved in inflammation and immune regulation. In CRC liver metastases, tumor tissues are enriched with bacteria, particularly Escherichia coli. Intratumoral E. coli+ promotes lactate production and induces RIG-I lactylation, which suppresses NF-κB signaling and weakens antitumoral immunity. This process leads to M2 macrophage polarization, enhanced Treg-mediated immunosuppression, and impaired CD8⁺ T cell function, ultimately promoting tumor metastasis and resistance to 5-fluorouracil chemotherapy.411 Additionally, lactate enhances histone H3K18 lactylation, increasing NF-κB p65 transcriptional activity and increasing TNFR2 expression in Treg cells. This further strengthens immunosuppression and accelerates tumor progression.412 Long-term treatment with enzalutamide can upregulate SLC4A4 expression, which promotes P53 lactylation via the NF-κB/STAT3 signaling pathway. This diminishes the tumor suppressor function of P53 and promotes enzalutamide resistance in prostate cancer cells.176 Thus, targeting SLC4A4 may offer a new strategy for overcoming drug resistance. GPR132-deficient NK92 cells exhibit increased cytotoxicity. GPR132 regulates NK cell function through the CSK/ZAP70/NF-κB signaling pathway, suggesting that GPR132 inhibition, particularly in the context of CAR-NK cell therapy, could serve as a promising immunotherapeutic strategy.248 Overall, lactate creates an immune-permissive environment for tumors by modulating the NF-κB pathway, enhancing their ability to evade immune surveillance. Thus, targeting the lactate–NF-κB axis may provide novel entry points for tumor immunotherapy.

TGF-β pathway

The TGF-β signaling pathway is widely involved in cell proliferation, differentiation, and immune regulation. It plays a dual role in cancer by functioning as both a tumor suppressor and promoter. CAFs enhance glycolysis and induce drug resistance in lung cancer cells by secreting CTHRC1, which activates the TGF-β/Smad3 pathway and upregulates hexokinase 2 transcription.377 Additionally, lactate can maintain Treg cell stability and function both in vivo and in vitro by upregulating FOXP3. Specifically, lactate-mediated MOESINK72la promotes the selective binding of MOESIN to TGF-β receptor I, increasing SMAD3 phosphorylation and FOXP3 transcription.244 A phase II clinical trial (NCT04856787) highlighted the potential of the combination of SHR-1701, a bifunctional protein that targets both PD-L1 and TGF-β, with bevacizumab and the XELOX regimen, as a first-line treatment for unresectable metastatic CRC.413 However, Wu et al. reported that TGF-β receptor II can inhibit the nuclear translocation of PKM2, a key glycolytic enzyme, in CAFs, impairing their metabolic support function and suppressing oral cancer growth.414 This lactate-mediated dual regulatory mechanism considerably influences tumor-associated immune responses.

Hedgehog pathway

The Hedgehog pathway is essential for embryonic development and stem cell regulation. Its aberrant activation in various tumors promotes cell proliferation and survival. In glioblastoma, lactate can activate the Sonic Hedgehog signaling pathway by upregulating IGFBP6 expression.415 Additionally, during HSC transdifferentiation, lactate acts as a downstream metabolite of Hedgehog signaling and a critical mediator that reinforces and sustains the fibrotic state.416

In summary, within the TME, lactate-driven signaling orchestrates a series of pathological processes, including metabolic reprogramming, increased invasiveness, immune evasion, angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling in tumor cells. Crosstalk between lactylation and acetylation

The interplay between lactylation and other posttranslational modifications, including acetylation, is crucial for tumor progression. Comprehensive research on these interactions has increasingly revealed their complex and dynamic crosstalk, providing new insights into combined therapies targeting both metabolism and epigenetics and expanding available anticancer strategies.

Structural and enzymatic overlap

The chemical formula of the lactyl group is CH₃CH(OH)CO–, whereas that of the acetyl group is CH₃CO–. Both are derivatives of aliphatic carboxylic acids formed by the removal of the hydroxyl group (–OH) from a carboxylic acid to generate an acyl structure (–C=O). This shared structural feature enables both groups to form stable covalent modifications with the ε-amino group of lysine residues in proteins via amide bonds. These modifications neutralize the positive charge of lysine, potentially affecting protein conformation and thereby altering protein function.

Notably, their shared acyl structure may explain why lactylation and acetylation share many of the same writers and erasers,417 suggesting common enzymatic regulation and potential interconnections and mutual influences between these two modifications. Although several known histone acetyltransferases, including p300/CBP, GCN5, HBO1, TIP60, KAT8, and TA1, can catalyze lactylation, p300 exhibits a particularly strong substrate preference for acetylation. This selectivity is largely associated with donor substrate bioavailability.193,418 Acetyl-CoA, the donor for acetylation, is ubiquitous in various cellular metabolic pathways. Its intracellular concentration is maintained at micromolar levels, enabling rapid responses to changes in metabolic states such as glucose metabolism and fatty acid oxidation.419 Conversely, lactyl-CoA, a potential donor for lactylation, is extremely difficult to detect directly in cells. Metabolomic data have indicated that the intracellular lac-CoA concentration is considerably low,420 its biosynthetic pathways are limited, and it may not even be the sole or primary donor for lactylation. This introduces uncertainty regarding the substrate origin of lactylation. Additionally, erasers of acetylation demonstrate substrate-specific preferences, further underscoring the differential regulation between the two types of modifications.420

Therefore, although acetylation and lactylation share some enzymatic machinery, they do not constitute completely overlapping modification networks. This highlights the importance of focusing on the specific regulatory mechanisms of lactylation in future research and understanding how it functions within the complex landscape of cellular metabolism.

Convergence of donor substrate metabolic pathways

Although acetylation and lactylation differ in their modification types and physiological functions, their donor substrates, acetyl-CoA and lactyl-CoA, exhibit highly intertwined biosynthetic pathways within the cellular metabolic network. This metabolic intersection offers regulatory links between these two modifications and underscores their sensitivity to cellular metabolic states.

First, both modifications trace their metabolic origins to glycolysis. Glucose is metabolized to pyruvate, which serves as a common precursor for both lactate and acetyl-CoA synthesis.115,419 Under aerobic conditions, pyruvate is predominantly converted into acetyl-CoA in the mitochondria and further metabolized via the TCA cycle. However, under anaerobic or quasianaerobic conditions, such as the Warburg effect in tumor cells) Pyruvate is preferentially converted to lactate, leading to intracellular lactate accumulation and subsequent increase in lactylation modifications. Second, the production of both acetyl-CoA and lactate as acyl donors is regulated by glycolytic flux, oxygen availability, and mitochondrial function.419 This metabolic dependence defines the conditional coupling of the two modifications. For example, under high-glucose or hypoxic conditions, increased glycolysis results in lactate accumulation, significantly increasing lactylation levels.201 Furthermore, mitochondrial dysfunction may limit acetyl-CoA production, leading to reduced acetylation levels.421 This metabolic shift could dynamically reconfigure the modification landscape.

In summary, although lactate and acetyl-CoA have distinct origins, their metabolic pathways are highly interconnected, reflecting substrate-level convergence. This mechanism explains the reciprocal transformation of their modification landscapes under specific conditions and provides a theoretical foundation for understanding how metabolism regulates chromatin accessibility and gene expression through diverse acyl modifications.

Functional competition or coordination?

Lactylation and acetylation are undoubtedly two distinct types of modifications. However, whether lactylation and acetylation function antagonistically or synergistically remains a subject of considerable debate. First, lactylation and acetylation share many common modification sites,422 suggesting a competitive relationship. However, both H3K18ac and H3K18la are associated with malignant tumor progression,397,423 suggesting a dynamic regulation and functional complementary relationship rather than merely a zero-sum relationship under certain physiological or pathological contexts. Second, although enhanced glycolysis promotes acyl donor production for both lactylation and acetylation, their responses to lactate signaling differ.422 Specifically, lactylation levels fluctuate with changes in lactate production, whereas acetylation remains unaffected, indicating that lactate concentration directly regulates lactylation. Nevertheless, Wagner et al. reported that both L-lactate and D-lactate can inhibit HDACs, increasing the acetylation of histones H3 and H4, which enhances the DNA repair capacity of breast cancer cells and contributes to anticancer drug resistance.374

Furthermore, these two modifications play different roles in macrophage polarization. Zhang et al. reported upregulated anaerobic glycolysis and lactate production during M2 polarization induction in macrophages, with a greater global change in lactylation than in acetylation.422 This suggests that lactylation may play a more crucial role in macrophage polarization. Moreover, histone lactylation is generally upregulated under hypoxic stress. Conversely, histone acetylation exhibits a bidirectional regulatory pattern, with some sites being upregulated and others being downregulated, suggesting a more refined and gene- or chromatin context-dependent regulatory mechanism.422

Future research directions

Future studies should systematically elucidate the regulatory relationship between lactylation and acetylation, particularly the mechanisms governing modification selection at shared lysine residues. Although lactylation and acetylation frequently occur at the same sites and are associated with similar biological processes, it is unclear whether they exhibit functional redundancy or activate distinct transcriptional programs by recruiting different “reader” proteins. These two modifications may ARISE at different time points, in different cell types, or under various pathological conditions, highlighting the need for high-spatiotemporal-resolution techniques for dynamic tracking of chromatin-associated changes.

As a posttranslational modification driven by a metabolic byproduct, lactylation is closely linked to the cellular metabolic state. Thus, future research may benefit from the integration of metabolomic, epigenomic, and transcriptomic data to construct a comprehensive “metabolism–epigenetics–function” regulatory network.

Clinical applications and treatments

In malignant tumors, high glycolysis levels establish an acidic microenvironment that promotes malignant proliferation and facilitates lactylation. This process enhances the infiltration of immunosuppressive cells and inhibits the antitumor activity of immune cells. Consequently, drugs targeting lactate may disrupt cancer cell metabolism and significantly increase the effectiveness of immunotherapy. Inhibiting lactate production

LDH inhibitors

LDH inhibitors are a class of drugs that target LDH activity. LDHA and LDHB are key metabolic enzymes that catalyze the bidirectional conversion of pyruvate and lactate, playing critical roles in glycolysis and lactate fermentation. Currently, inhibiting LDHA to reduce lactate production is the most widely applied approach for influencing cancer cell metabolism and growth.424 For example, GSK2837808A functions as an LDHA inhibitor by competing for the cofactor NADH, thus reducing E. coli-induced lactate levels in tumors and alleviating the burden of liver metastasis.411 However, its clinical application is limited by its low in vivo clearance rate.425 Moreover, GNE-140 inhibits cell growth by blocking the glycolytic pathway.426 Notably, in patients with cancer, LDHB expression is negatively correlated with survival, suggesting LDHB as another potential therapeutic target.427

PK inhibitors

PK catalyzes the conversion of phosphoenolpyruvate to pyruvate, generating an ATP molecule that directly supplies energy to tumor cells. PK exists in different isoforms, including L, R, M1, and M2. The M2 isoform (PKM2) is overexpressed in various cancers, including liver, colorectal, lung, and breast cancer.428 Inhibition of PKM2 activity induces an energy crisis and disrupts metabolism in cancer cells, leading to apoptosis or autophagy.429 Moreover, PKM2 inhibition may result in ROS accumulation, promoting programmed cell death in glycolysis-dependent cancer cells.430

Other inhibitors

Hexokinase is the first enzyme in the glycolytic pathway and is often overexpressed in malignant tumors. Currently, several hexokinase inhibitors have entered clinical trials. 2-DG is a glucose analog with a structure that closely resembles glucose, except for the absence of a hydroxyl group (–OH) at the second carbon position. This subtle structural difference allows 2-DG to be recognized and phosphorylated by hexokinase to form 2-DG-6-phosphate; however, it cannot proceed through the subsequent steps of glycolysis.431 Therefore, 2-DG is frequently used in cancer metabolism research as a glycolytic inhibitor. Lonidamine (LND), developed by Angelini Pharmaceuticals (Italy), was marketed in Italy in 1986 as an antitumor agent. It can inhibit hexokinase II, suppressing glycolysis and lactate production.432 However, LND also inhibits MCTs and the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier, thereby blocking lactate export and pyruvate uptake into mitochondria, resulting in intracellular lactate accumulation.433 Notably, the antitumor efficacy of LND monotherapy is modest and reversible, suggesting that LND may be more effective as a sensitizer in combination therapies rather than as a standalone treatment.434 Another agent under clinical investigation, RRx-001, exerts antitumor effects by inhibiting glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, reducing NADPH levels, disrupting redox homeostasis in tumor cells, and increasing cell sensitivity to oxidative stress.435

Inhibiting lactate shuttle

Lactate transport primarily relies on MCTs. MCTs, particularly MCT1 and MCT4, are key lactate transmembrane transporters. They are highly expressed in cancer cells, enabling adaptation to environments with elevated lactate production.436 Among various MCT inhibitors, MCT1 inhibitors, such as AZD3965, have been extensively studied and have entered clinical trials, demonstrating promising anticancer potential.437 In diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, BAY-8002 inhibits bidirectional lactate transport and modulates cell resistance.438 Syrosingopine, a clinically approved druge, exhibits anticancer effects on various malignancies, including myeloma,439 and holds potential as a future adjunct therapy for cancer. Although MCT4-specific inhibitors are relatively scarce, VB124 is an MCT4-specific inhibitor that exhibits strong antitumor activity against HCC.440

Inhibiting lactylation

Targeting enzymes involved in lactylation modifications

Within cells, epigenetic modifications influence gene expression by chemically modifying DNA, RNA and histones.441 “Writers” and “erasers” are central to epigenetic regulation. Writers add chemical modifications, such as methylation and lactylation, whereas erasers remove these modifications. Lactate, a metabolic byproduct, participates in epigenetic regulation through lactylation, affecting gene expression and tumor growth.442 Targeting lactylation-related writers and erasers can modulate gene expression in cancer cells, inhibiting their proliferation and survival. Inobrodib inhibits p300 function and has demonstrated efficacy in various cancers by reducing protein lactylation and reversing tumor malignancy.443,444 HDAC1-3 and SIRT1-3 act as erasers of lactylation. They can reverse global protein lactylation modifications and partially mitigate tumor growth and metastasis induced by high lactate levels.102,445 However, the specificities of writers and erasers remain unclear, as they regulate the modifications of numerous proteins. Thus, further research is required to elucidate the characteristics of these enzymes in different cellular environments and disease contexts.

Targeting lactylation sites

Compared with other treatments, the precise targeting of lactylation sites on proteins can more directly influence the epigenetic and metabolic regulatory pathways associated with the disease to achieve more effective therapeutic outcomes. As mentioned previously, the lactylation of RIG-I at K852 promotes liver metastasis in CRC.411 Through virtual screening, cellular validation, and animal validation, (7-(carboxymethyl)-10-methyl-10H-phenothiazin-2-yl) acetic acid was demonstrated to reduce RIG-I K852 lactylation levels in vitro.411 Additionally, inhibiting MRE11 K673la using the peptide K673-pe can enhance the effectiveness of chemotherapy.96 K673-pe specifically inhibits MRE11 K673 lactylation and interferes with the HR pathway involved in DNA damage repair. Notably, this peptide does not exhibit significant organ toxicity in mouse models, suggesting promising therapeutic potential and a favorable safety profile.96 In NSCLC, lactate produced by tumor cells promotes APOC2 lactylation at the K70 site, increasing its stability.446 This process leads to fatty acid release, causing Treg accumulation, which results in immune therapy resistance and tumor metastasis.446 Notably, an antibody targeting this lactylation modification (anti-APOC2K70-lac Ab) has been developed. It enhances the efficacy of PD-1 antibody therapy, offering a potential new strategy for combination immunotherapy.446

Precise targeting of specific sites represents an efficient and specific drug development strategy, offering effective therapeutic approaches for many complex diseases. However, successful targeting depends on a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms of the target, alongside a thorough consideration of factors such as drug tolerance and toxicity, making this approach pivotal for future cancer research.

Combinations with immunotherapy

Lactate-targeted therapy combined with immunotherapy is an emerging cancer treatment strategy aimed at enhancing the anticancer effects of the immune system by disrupting lactate metabolism in tumors. This approach leverages the critical role of lactate in cancer cell metabolism and the TME. Lactate produced by cancer cells supports cell growth and immune evasion by acidifying the microenvironment and suppressing immune cells.447 The efficacy of ICB is limited in acidic environments, especially under severely acidic conditions (pH < 5.5).301 LDH-IN-1 inhibits lactate metabolism in HCC cells and enhances the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy.244 PKM2-IN-1 can increase gemcitabine sensitivity in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma cells by inhibiting the β-catenin signaling pathway.448 Moreover, inhibiting lactate metabolism enhances the efficacy of tumor immunotherapy,449 making it a promising cancer treatment strategy for future clinical applications.

Combinations with targeted therapy

Targeted therapy, which achieves precision treatment by intervening in tumor cell-specific molecular mechanisms, has emerged as an important component in the treatment of various solid tumors. Compared with traditional chemotherapy, targeted therapy offers numerous advantages, including enhanced selectivity, decreased adverse effects, and more durable efficacy.450 Notably, inhibiting histone lactylation can significantly increase the sensitivity of malignant tumors to bevacizumab, a common first- and second-line antiangiogenic drug in CRC.26 Moreover, combining 2-DG and sorafenib effectively targets sorafenib-resistant cancer cells.451 In NSCLC, lactate promotes erlotinib resistance through multiple mechanisms.452 Further, epigallocatechin gallate effectively reverses acquired resistance to EGFR-TKIs by remodeling glycolytic metabolism, providing a promising combination strategy for treating NSCLC.453

In summary, although targeted therapy has significantly improved the prognosis of various tumors, drug resistance remains a major challenge. Current research focuses on metabolic interventions to increase the efficacy of targeted drugs, offering new insights and therapeutic strategies to overcome resistance.

Combinations with chemoradiotherapy

The TME of UM is characterized by high lactate levels, ROS deficiency, and hypoxia, limiting the efficacy of radiotherapy.454 To address these challenges, Yao et al. developed a novel nanomaterial, termed CoMnFe-layered double-oxide nanosheets, which sensitize tumors to radiotherapy by scavenging lactate and catalyzing the conversion of H₂O₂ into ROS.454 Additionally, mitochondrial lipid peroxidation induced by radiation therapy and the associated inhibition of tumor growth can be further enhanced by LDHB knockdown.455 Inhibiting RIG-I lactylation can increase the sensitivity of CRC cells to 5-fluorouracil both in vitro and in vivo.411 Moreover, lactate neutralization via sodium bicarbonate significantly reduces MRP1 expression, improving the efficacy of etoposide against NSCLC.456 However, current research on the targeting of lactate metabolism to enhance radio- and chemotherapy remains limited.457 Therefore, additional research is urgently needed to elucidate the mechanisms by which lactate metabolism contributes to therapeutic resistance and response, and thus inform the development of effective lactate-targeted combination treatment strategies (Fig. 7).Fig. 7Diagnosis and treatment of tumors. Conclusion and perspective

Research on lactate metabolism and lactate-derived lactylation, as core drivers of cancer initiation and progression, is gradually changing our traditional perception of lactate as a “metabolic waste product.” This review systematically examines the basic mechanisms of lactate metabolism, its abnormal manifestations in tumor cells, and the multiple roles of lactate in the TME. From the perspective of tumor initiation and progression, we reviewed the critical roles of lactate and lactylation modifications in transcriptional regulation, protein function modulation, immune evasion, and treatment resistance. The present review highlighted the role of lactate as an important mediator of signal transduction and epigenetic regulation, beyond its conventional function as an intermediate product of cellular metabolism.

Mechanistically, tumor cells reprogram their metabolism to increase glycolysis, even under aerobic conditions (i.e., the Warburg effect), significantly increasing lactate production. Subsequently, glutamine metabolism becomes an important source of lactate, supporting the energy and biosynthetic precursor demands of rapid tumor proliferation while promoting immune suppression, angiogenesis, and metastasis by acidifying the TME. Additionally, the lactate shuttle mechanism facilitates metabolic symbiosis between different regions of tumor cells.

Lactylation, a novel form of posttranslational protein modification, expands the connection between metabolic products and epigenetic regulation. The “writers” (such as p300, GCN5, and KAT8), “erasers” (such as HDACs and the SIRT family), and “readers” (such as Brg1 and DPF2) of lactylation constitute a highly dynamic regulatory system. These modifications are present on histones, regulating the open state and transcriptional activity of key genes. They also widely affect nonhistone proteins, influencing biological processes, including DNA repair, the cell cycle, and drug resistance. The differences in lactylation isomers, such as L-lactylation and D-lactylation, and their source pathways (enzymatic or nonenzymatic) also provide new insights into tumor molecular heterogeneity and therapeutic target development.

Notably, lactate and lactylation exhibit significant dynamics and duality across different stages of cancer. During tumor initiation, lactate accelerates carcinogenesis by inducing DNA mutations, impairing tumor suppressor gene function (such as p53 lactylation), and remodeling the immune microenvironment. During tumor expansion and invasion, it supports malignant clone selection and expansion by acidifying the microenvironment and driving immune tolerance. Moreover, during cancer treatment, lactylation confers drug resistance by enhancing DNA repair and inhibiting immune responses. Therefore, the regulatory network of lactate and lactylation has become a “gray area” in current cancer treatment, serving as both an important aspect of the tumor survival strategy and a key challenge in overcoming resistance and immune evasion. Currently, drug research targeting lactate and lactylation is advancing rapidly, with lactate targeting representing a potentially powerful strategy for enhancing the efficacy of existing cancer treatments.

Despite significant research progress, several issues remain. First, a comprehensive understanding of the specific substrates, precise sites, and functional consequences of lactylation modifications requires the use of advanced high-throughput screening and structural analysis tools. Second, the cross-regulatory mechanism between lactylation and acetylation remains unclear, and the potential existence of a “metabolic epigenetic code” remains unexplored. Third, the bidirectional relationship between lactate metabolism and the TME suggests that merely “inhibiting lactate production” may lead to side effects. Instead, precise regulation of the spatial expression and temporal activity of lactate represents a more promising strategy.

Accordingly, future research should focus on the following directions: (1) developing specific targeted drugs to inhibit lactate production and lactylation modifications and combine them with immunotherapy to create synergistic strategies; (2) screening biomarkers based onn lactylation profiles to achieve tumor typing and personalized treatment; (3) integrating multiomics data to reveal the heterogeneity of lactate and lactylation regulatory networks in different tumor types and treatment stages; and (4) establishing spatial metabolic maps at both the tissue and single-cell levels, thus enabling the dynamic visualization and intervention of TME metabolic reprogramming.

In conclusion, lactate metabolism and its posttranslational modification, lactylation, which is traditionally considered a metabolic byproduct, have gradually emerged as key focuses of cancer research. Research into their crucial roles in tumor initiation and progression has revolutionized our understanding of tumor metabolism, providing novel breakthroughs in precision medicine and targeted therapies. Through further systematic mechanistic exploration and translational research, the lactate–lactylation axis is expected to bridge basic science and clinical treatment, advancing cancer therapy into a new era of precision and dynamic regulation.
