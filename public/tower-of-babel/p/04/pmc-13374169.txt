Unraveling the mechanisms of PCOS: the interplay between gut microbiota and the immune system
Authors: Jun Liu, Li Wang, Min Jiang, Heqiu Yan, Guohui Zhang, Ningjing Li, Yuhong Zhao, Xinrong Du, Qin Zeng, Dongsheng Xiong, Libing He, Zhuoting Zhou, Mengjun Luo, Weixin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolycystic ovary syndrome(PCOS), a reproductive endocrine disorder that affects fertility in women of reproductive age, is characterized by high prevalence, diverse clinical manifestations, and unclear mechanisms. Elucidating its pathogenesis is of great significance for the treatment and prevention of PCOS. The gut microbiota, as a key modulator of human metabolism, plays a crucial role in PCOS through its interactions with the immune system. Gut microbiota and its metabolites not only serve as essential components of human metabolism but also act as critical regulators of the immune system. Therefore, this review focuses on the role of gut microbiota and the immune system in the pathogenesis of PCOS. It highlights the interactions between gut microbiota and its metabolites (such as lipopolysaccharides, branched-chain amino acids, and short-chain fatty acids) with the immune system, and their effects on key symptoms of PCOS, including inflammation, ovarian dysfunction, and insulin resistance. Furthermore, potential therapeutic strategies for alleviating PCOS are discussed, aiming to provide guidance for the treatment and prevention of PCOS.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Polycystic ovary syndrome, Immune system, Gut microbiota

Background

PCOS is a complex endocrine and metabolic disorder that significantly impairs women’s health and fertility, with its exact etiology remaining unclear. Recent studies have revealed that the gut microbiota and its metabolites, as well as the host immune system, play pivotal roles in the pathogenesis of PCOS. Therefore, this review conducted a systematic search and analysis using key terms such as “polycystic ovary syndrome,” “gut microbiota,” “immune system,” “cytokines,” “inflammation,” “hyperandrogenism,” “insulin resistance,” “obesity,” and “lipid metabolism disorders.” This review aims to explore the potential mechanisms by which the gut microbiota and its metabolites mediate PCOS development through immune system modulation, providing novel perspectives on the pathology of PCOS. Furthermore, with the advancements in microbiome-based therapies and immunomodulatory treatments, a deeper understanding of the gut microbiota-immune system axis in PCOS could contribute to the development of innovative therapeutic strategies. By integrating progress from gut microbiology, immunology, and endocrinology, this review seeks to enhance the multidimensional understanding of PCOS etiology and foster interdisciplinary research and collaboration in this field.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most common endocrine disorders in women of reproductive age, with a prevalence ranging from 5% to 18%. It is characterized by hyperandrogenism and polycystic ovarian morphology and is often accompanied by insulin resistance, obesity, metabolic syndrome, and psychological issues, severely impacting women’s fertility and quality of life [1]. The pathogenesis of PCOS is complex and is widely believed to be influenced by genetic, environmental, and hormonal factors. Imbalance in the gut microbiota composition also plays a significant role in the development of PCOS. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota leads to immune system dysfunction, which directly affects the ovarian immune microenvironment or disrupts normal endocrine function, triggering PCOS. However, research on the underlying mechanisms is limited, and further studies are needed to explore how the gut microbiota interacts with the immune system to mediate PCOS, guiding the development of new interventions.

PCOS is highly heritable and closely associated with genetic factors. Studies have identified susceptibility loci for PCOS on chromosomes 2p16.3, 2p21, and 9q33.3 [2]. Gene mutations in ERK-1/2 kinase can also lead to anovulation or excessive androgen secretion [3]. Additionally, PCOS patients exhibit significant Trp28Arg/Ile35Thr variations in the gene encoding the LH beta subunit (LHB) [4]. Obesity, insulin resistance, and hyperandrogenism, as core pathological features of PCOS, are interconnected and mutually influence each other. Obesity leads to the release of various inflammatory factors and free fatty acids from adipocytes, which interfere with insulin signaling and subsequently reduce insulin sensitivity. Hyperinsulinemia, induced by insulin resistance, not only directly stimulates ovarian granulosa cells and theca cells to synthesize androgens but also decreases serum levels of sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) by inhibiting its hepatic production, thereby increasing the concentration of free androgens in the blood. Elevated androgen levels further contribute to abnormal fat distribution, particularly the accumulation of fat in the abdominal region [5]. In addition, the environments to which the body, ovaries, and oocytes are exposed are closely related to the development of PCOS. These factors include prenatal testosterone excess, in utero conditions such as fetal malnutrition or small for gestational age, antiepileptic drugs (e.g., valproate), endocrine-disrupting chemicals (e.g., bisphenol A), environmental toxins (e.g., epoxy resins, polyvinyl chloride [PVC]), and glycotoxins (e.g., advanced glycation end products [AGEs]) [5–7]. Thus, the etiology of PCOS is broad and intricate. In recent years, the discovery of gut microbiota dysbiosis and immune system regulation has also been found to play a significant role in the pathogenesis of PCOS. This highlights the importance of exploring these factors further to identify more effective interventions in the future.

The intestinal microbiota refers to the collection of microorganisms residing in the human gut, which play a crucial role in maintaining normal physiological functions of the body [8]. The gut microbiota participates in metabolic regulation through various mechanisms, including the breakdown of nutrients absorbed by the host in the digestive tract [9]. Additionally, the gut microbiota and its metabolites play a vital role in maintaining immune system balance. They regulate inflammatory responses, induce cell maturation, and mediate autoimmune and allergic diseases [10]. Emerging evidence indicates that gut microbiota–derived signals serve as a converging upstream regulator of insulin resistance (IR), hyperandrogenism (HA), and immune dysregulation in PCOS. Microbial metabolites such as LPS, BCAAs, secondary bile acids and SCFAs influence insulin signaling, androgen biosynthesis and systemic inflammation through TLR4–NF-κB, mTOR, FXR–FGF15 and GPCR pathways (Fig. 3).IR promotes ovarian androgen production via compensatory hyperinsulinemia, while HA further impairs adipose and immune homeostasis, creating a vicious cycle that reinforces IR. Parallelly, immune activation (elevated Th17/Treg imbalance, macrophage M1 polarization) alters the ovarian microenvironment and folliculogenesis.

Gut microbiota and their metabolites regulate PCOS through the immune system. A. LPS binds to LBP, CD14, and MD-2, transferring to immune cells and interacting with TLR4. This activates NF-κB, leading to the expression and release of TNF-α and IL-6 in peripheral circulation and the ovaries. Additionally, LPS directly activates caspase-4 and caspase-5 in human macrophages, inducing GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis, releasing various inflammatory factors, and inhibiting the expression of the Esr2 gene in granulosa cells, thereby suppressing estrogen receptor β synthesis. B. SCFAs directly act on the GPR43 receptor on neutrophils, inducing chemotaxis and regulating degranulation while inhibiting NF-κB and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α. Activation of GPR43 also promotes macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 type to the anti-inflammatory M2 type, increasing IL-10 secretion. Furthermore, SCFAs inhibit HDACs, facilitating the transition of immune cells to an anti-inflammatory phenotype, leading to NF-κB inactivation in monocytes and neutrophils, and reducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF. C. SCFAs induce SIRT1 expression, which directly regulates the HPO axis, influencing folliculogenesis and ovulation. Butyrate, by inhibiting METTL3, reduces m6A modification of FOSL2 in KGN cells, thereby suppressing the expression of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α and mitigating inflammation. D. The gut microbiota regulates bile acid metabolism, leading to a decrease in GDCA levels. Reduced GDCA decreases IL-22 production from ILC3 via the cAMP-GATA3 pathway, with IL-22 improving insulin resistance and ovarian dysfunction in PCOS mice. Bile acids also directly activate the TGR5 receptor on immune cells through the cAMP-PKA pathway, leading to phosphorylation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, inhibiting the maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18, which may help alleviate inflammation in PCOS. E. α-Galactosyl glycerol derived from Bacteroides fragilis, synthesized through valine, leucine, and isoleucine, determines its structure and acts on the CD1d-NKT cell receptor axis, ultimately inducing the release of TNF-α and IL-6, which may exacerbate the inflammatory state in PCOS.

This review comprehensively summarizes the relationship between gut microbiota, its metabolites, and the development of PCOS, with the immune system serving as a mediator. In conclusion, based on existing research, the review provides insights into early risk prediction and therapeutic strategies for PCOS, focusing on the role of the gut microbiota through immune system modulation.

Gut microbiota, immune system, and PCOS

The role of gut microbiota in PCOS

There is a close relationship between PCOS and the gut microbiota. PCOS patients often experience gut microbiota dysbiosis, characterized by a reduction in beneficial bacteria, an increase in harmful bacteria, or an altered ratio of the two. This imbalance affects key symptoms of PCOS, such as obesity, insulin resistance, and hyperandrogenism, through multiple pathways.

Gut microbiota-induced lipid metabolism abnormalities (Obesity) in PCOS

Obesity is a risk factor for many endocrine and reproductive disorders. It causes structural and functional changes in adipose tissue, primarily characterized by adipocyte hypertrophy, leading to hyperinsulinemia, hyperlipidemia, and chronic low-grade inflammation [11]. Numerous studies have shown that PCOS patients exhibit significant lipid metabolism disorders, which have a strong causal relationship with their metabolic risk, affecting 50% to 80% of obese PCOS patients. In addition to inducing or exacerbating insulin resistance and hyperandrogenism, these lipid abnormalities also contribute to and worsen non-metabolic conditions, such as obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) [12–14]. Therefore, altering lipid metabolism through weight loss may serve as a therapeutic approach for PCOS patients. Currently, bariatric surgery is considered an experimental treatment. However, existing studies have shown that weight loss surgery can restore normal menstrual cycles and improve ovulation rates in PCOS patients [15]. A recent randomized controlled trial in women with PCOS found that patients undergoing bariatric surgery experienced a significant improvement in ovulation rates, along with marked improvements in glucose, insulin, and glycated hemoglobin levels, indicating better glucose and lipid metabolism. Additionally, androgen levels were reduced, and patients showed notable enhancements in psychological health and quality of life [14]. This indicates that modifying glucose and lipid metabolism in PCOS patients holds significant potential for improving fertility.

Changes in the gut microbiota often lead to various metabolic disorders, including obesity and lipid metabolism abnormalities (Fig. 1A). Studies have reported that in obese PCOS patients, the α-diversity of the gut microbiota is inversely correlated with body mass index (BMI) [16, 17]. In obese individuals, changes in the gut microbiota result in a decrease in short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) concentrations, which reduces leptin levels and accelerates weight gain. This also inhibits the activity of cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase (CYP7A1), affecting bile acid levels and subsequently impairing lipid and triglyceride metabolism, leading to lipid metabolism disorders in PCOS patients [18]. In individuals with obesity and PCOS, the overall richness and diversity of the gut microbiota are lower compared to lean PCOS patients. However, the abundance of certain bacterial genera, including Fusobacteria, Lachnoclostridium, Fusobacterium, Tyzzerella_4, Anaerofustis, Atopobium, and Scardovia, is significantly elevated [19, 20]. In individuals with obesity and PCOS, there is a notable imbalance in the ratio of Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes, two representative phyla of the gut microbiota. This Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes (F: B) ratio is significantly elevated and is associated with increased central adiposity and liver lipid metabolism markers, such as Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT)and hepatic fat fraction (HFF) [21]. Similar findings have been observed in mouse models of obesity and PCOS [22]. Additionally, a reduction in the proportion of Bacteroidia has been observed in PCOS patients with lipid metabolism disorders, such as elevated LDL cholesterol (LDL-C) [12]. This suggests that gut microbiota dysbiosis may contribute to lipid metabolism abnormalities in PCOS, leading to symptoms of obesity.

Gut microbiota-induced insulin resistance in PCOS

Insulin resistance (IR) refers to a reduction in the sensitivity of peripheral tissues to insulin due to interference with insulin receptors and their signaling pathways, which leads to a compensatory increase in insulin levels. Excess insulin stimulates insulin receptors in the follicles and pituitary, enhances the activity of insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) receptors in the ovaries, and promotes the production of androgens like testosterone. This contributes to PCOS-related pathological manifestations, such as menstrual irregularities, impaired ovulation, hirsutism, and inhibition of ovarian follicle growth and development. Additionally, insulin resistance exacerbates the severity of metabolic syndrome in PCOS [23, 24].

Gut microbiota dysbiosis is one of the key factors contributing to the development of PCOS and plays a crucial role in inducing insulin resistance (Fig. 1B). Studies have shown that oral administration of Bacteroides vulgatus can induce insulin resistance in experimental mice, disrupting their estrous cycle, ovarian morphology, and hormone profiles [25]. In PCOS patients with insulin resistance, the α-diversity of the gut microbiota is reduced, characterized by an increase in pro-inflammatory Bacteroides and a decrease in Prevotellaceae [26]. Additionally, in PCOS patients with insulin resistance, the relative abundance of Enterococcaceae, Ruminococcus, and Enterococcus is significantly elevated. The abundance of Enterococcus shows a positive correlation with the insulin resistance index (HOMA-IR). The secretion of Gelatinase E (GeIE) by Enterococcus faecalis can degrade glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), leading to abnormal insulin secretion. Meanwhile, the abundance of the dominant genus Prevotella is significantly reduced in the gut [27]. Similar changes have also been observed in rat models of PCOS with insulin resistance [28]. Ruminococcus gnavus has also been found to have a positive correlation with the insulin resistance index in PCOS patients. Additionally, in PCOS patients with type II diabetes or those exhibiting insulin resistance (impaired glucose tolerance), the abundance of the genus Dorea is increased [29, 30]. Moreover, transferring feces from PCOS patients to mice has resulted in insulin resistance in the recipient mice [25]. The aforementioned results suggest that gut microbiota may contribute to insulin resistance and other metabolic symptoms associated with PCOS. Mechanistically, the Bacteroidetes-bile acid-intestinal FXR signaling pathway may promote glucose metabolism disorders and induce insulin resistance in PCOS. The elevated levels of Bacteroidetes in PCOS enhance the metabolism of chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), leading to downregulation of intestinal FXR activation. CDCA can improve glucose metabolism and insulin resistance in mouse models of PCOS by activating FXR [31]. In conclusion, changes in the gut microbiota are significant contributors to the development of insulin resistance in patients with PCOS.

Gut microbiota-induced hyperandrogenism in PCOS

Hyperandrogenism is considered a core pathophysiological characteristic of PCOS(Fig. 1C). Elevated androgen levels hinder the development of follicles by altering the expression of oocytes, ovarian granulosa cells, and theca cells. For a long time, improving androgen levels has been an ideal therapeutic target in the treatment of PCOS. Many treatment strategies aim to reduce hyperandrogenemia in order to alleviate various clinical symptoms in PCOS patients. A recent study found that artemisinin can improve symptoms associated with PCOS [32]. Artemisinin can directly target the mitochondrial protease LONP1 to enhance its interaction with the CYP11A1 protein, leading to a reduction in CYP11A1 levels. As a key enzyme catalyzing androgen synthesis, CYP11A1 inhibition suppresses ovarian androgen production, thereby reducing hyperandrogenemia. This mechanism helps improve irregular estrous cycles, polycystic ovarian morphology, and low fertility in PCOS-like mice, while also enhancing the ovarian morphology and restoring normal menstrual cycles in PCOS patients. These findings suggest that artemisinin may serve as an effective treatment for PCOS, highlighting the central role of hyperandrogenism in the condition.

In PCOS, hyperandrogenism is closely associated with changes in the gut microbiota. Most PCOS animal models are often induced by directly or indirectly promoting elevated androgen levels in the animals, resulting in PCOS-like models that are typically accompanied by dysbiosis of the gut microbiota [33]. In mice induced with dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), after excluding factors related to hyperinsulinemia and insulin resistance, a significant increase in the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus was observed [34]. In PCOS mice induced with letrozole, the abundance of Enterococcus significantly decreased and showed a clear negative correlation with total testosterone levels. Following antibiotic intervention, there were significant changes in the abundance of Enterorhabdus [35]. Testosterone directly affects gut microbiota enzymes, such as altering β-glucuronidase activity, as it serves as a substrate for these enzymes. Additionally, changes in estrogen and androgen levels can indirectly modify gut function by activating steroid receptor signaling in the gastrointestinal tract, brain, and peripheral tissues, or by regulating gut immune functions [36, 37]. Moreover, in PCOS patients, variations in the gut microbiome’s genomic structure (SVs) and phylogenetic diversity are negatively correlated with testosterone levels and symptoms of hirsutism [38]. Furthermore, in rat models, gut microbiota intervention can significantly modulate the levels of sex steroids [39]. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from PCOS-like rats can also elevate serum testosterone levels in recipients [40]. Here, gut microbiota dysbiosis may disrupt the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis through the interplay between the enteric nervous system and the central nervous system, ultimately leading to increased androgen levels and metabolic and endocrine imbalances. Specifically, the genus Prevotella is notably associated with androgen levels; studies have shown a negative correlation between serum testosterone levels and the abundance of Prevotella in PCOS patients [19, 30, 41]. In contrast, another study found that in rat models, the abundance of Prevotella increased alongside the elevated levels of testosterone and androstenedione [39]. It can be speculated that the most significant reason for these discrepancies is the differences between species. The gut microbiota is shaped by various factors from birth, including genetic and environmental influences. Additionally, variations in the modeling methods, research designs, and living conditions of the rat models could also contribute to these findings. Nevertheless, these results indicate that Prevotella has an undeniable relationship with sex hormones in PCOS. Furthermore, in PCOS-like rat models, testosterone levels positively correlate with the abundance of Ruminococcus and Roseburia, while androstenedione is positively associated with Clostridiales, Firmicutes, and Ruminococcus. Notably, Clostridium scindens within the Clostridiales order can convert glucocorticoids into androgens, highlighting the complex interplay between gut microbiota and hormonal regulation in PCOS [42]. Thus, it is evident that gut microbiota plays a crucial role in the core pathological manifestations of PCOS. Current research has revealed specific microbial types associated with PCOS; however, there is a lack of evidence detailing the specific pathways through which these microbes exert their effects. Future studies focusing on these mechanisms will significantly contribute to the development of effective interventions for this condition.

The role of the immune system in PCOS

The immune system is an indispensable component of the human body, playing a critical role in maintaining normal physiological functions and responding to diseases caused by various internal and external environmental factors. PCOS is characterized by a systemic low-grade inflammatory state, and there is also varying degrees of dysregulation in the local immune microenvironment of the ovaries, which is one of the reasons for the polycystic appearance of the ovaries. In the pathophysiological processes of PCOS, immune cells, inflammatory cytokines, and regulatory mechanisms of the immune system all play significant roles.

The role of immune cells in PCOS

There is a close connection between the occurrence of PCOS and the immune system, particularly regarding the abnormal activity of immune cells (Fig. 2). Bioinformatics analyses indicate that central memory CD4+ T cells, central memory CD8+ T cells, effector memory CD4+ T cells, γδ T cells, and Th17 cells may play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of PCOS [43]. Furthermore, activated immune cells such as CD4+ T cells, eosinophils, B cells, γδ T cells, macrophages, and mast cells can lead to delayed or atretic follicle growth and ovulatory dysfunction. These immune cells exacerbate the pathological progression of PCOS by inhibiting the growth of KGN cells and promoting their apoptosis [44].

In a PCOS rat model (Fig. 2B), the changes in leukocytes across different tissues reveal a significant immune imbalance. There is an increase in pro-inflammatory Th17 cells in peripheral blood, spleen, and kidneys, while anti-inflammatory Treg cells are reduced [45]. This immune imbalance is not confined to local ovarian inflammation but is also accompanied by systemic immune dysregulation. For example, local granulocyte miR-379-5p significantly promotes macrophage polarization toward the M1 type in PCOS rats by targeting PDK1 and inhibiting the AKT pathway, while suppressing M2 polarization [46]. In the follicular fluid of PCOS patients, the ratios of CD3+ and CD8+ T cells are significantly lower than normal levels. Concurrently, the expression of CD69 and IFN-γ in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells is also significantly reduced. In contrast, the expression of the T cell activation marker PD-1 is significantly elevated, positively correlating with serum E2 levels and negatively correlating with IFN-γ expression [47] (Fig. 2A). These results suggest that the increased expression of PD-1 may affect follicular development, ovulation, and hormonal changes by inhibiting T cell activation, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of PCOS. In terms of systemic changes, the proportion and number of CD19+ B cells and naive B cells in the peripheral blood of PCOS patients are significantly elevated, while there are no significant changes in the proportions of memory B cells, plasma cells, and regulatory B cells [48]. Additionally, helper T cells (Th), Regulatory T cells (Tregs), and mucosal-associated invariant T cells (MAIT) show significant changes in PCOS patients (Fig. 2C). Studies indicate that the proportion of Tregs in the peripheral blood of PCOS patients is significantly reduced, potentially due to abnormalities in the IL-2 signaling pathway that lead to STAT5 pathway inhibition and excessive activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway, collectively suppressing Treg production and further exacerbating immune system imbalance [49]. Furthermore, the proportion of Tfh2 cells is significantly increased, while the proportion of Tfr cells is significantly decreased. The elevated Tfh2 cells show a positive correlation with testosterone levels. This may exacerbate the immune inflammatory response by aiding B cells in producing antibodies, promoting B cell proliferation and differentiation, and facilitating the generation of autoantibodies [50]. In PCOS patients, there is a significant reduction in CD8+ MAIT cells and CD38+ CD8+ MAIT cells, which may lead to metabolic dysfunction, hormonal imbalances, and abnormal weight, as well as trigger chronic inflammation that contributes to the pathological processes of PCOS [51]. Moreover, the significant reduction of CD3+ MAIT cells in peripheral blood is negatively correlated with BMI, HOMA-IR index, AMH levels, and the elevated IL-17 levels in PCOS patients [52]. These findings suggest that MAIT cells play a crucial role in the metabolic dysfunction and immune response associated with PCOS.

Chronic low-grade inflammation is a core pathophysiological feature of PCOS and is closely linked to the regulation of cytokines within the immune system. Under normal physiological conditions, moderate inflammatory stress supports oocyte growth and development. However, in PCOS, the imbalance of pro-inflammatory cytokines is closely associated with ovarian dysfunction, altered steroidogenesis, and delayed follicular maturation. In the follicular fluid of PCOS patients, the levels of Th1-related inflammatory and immune-regulatory cytokines, such as IFN-γ and IL-2, are significantly elevated [53]. Additionally, the levels of the inflammatory chemokine CCL5 in peripheral blood are increased, which is closely related to hyperandrogenemia and insulin resistance [54]. Inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-18 regulate ovarian function by altering the local microenvironment, thereby triggering PCOS-related symptoms such as elevated androgen levels and insulin resistance [43, 55, 56]. In particular, IL-18 expression is often elevated in PCOS patients, while increased levels of pro-inflammatory factors such as macrophage-derived TNF-α can affect insulin signaling, leading to insulin resistance and directly stimulating the synthesis and secretion of androgens [57, 58]. TNF-α further promotes the expression of IL-17 A in cooperation with IL-23, resulting in an increase in lymphocyte and macrophage populations and triggering the secretion of additional inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 [59]. Additionally, elevated TNF-α increases free fatty acids, reduces insulin signaling, inhibits glucose transport, and decreases the expression of glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4), thereby exacerbating insulin resistance. It may also induce apoptosis in preantral granulosa cells, thickening the follicular theca layer, leading to a reduction in granulosa cell numbers and impairing communication and metabolism between granulosa cells and oocytes, ultimately resulting in abnormal follicular development or ovulatory dysfunction [56].

The role of immune regulatory mechanisms in PCOS

Epigenetics studies the heritable regulatory mechanisms of gene expression or phenotypic changes without alterations to the DNA sequence. The main mechanisms include DNA methylation, histone modification, chromatin remodeling, and the role of non-coding RNAs. These mechanisms have profound effects on cellular functions and individual development [60]. In PCOS patients, epigenetic studies have shown a significant reduction in global DNA methylation levels in immune cells, including monocytes, helper T cells, cytotoxic T cells, and B cells [61].A study analyzing leukocytes from PCOS patients found a significant increase in the methylation levels of the PPARGC1A gene promoter, while mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) content was significantly reduced [62]. Moreover, the methylation level of the PPARGC1A promoter is significantly positively correlated with PCOS indicators such as BMI, waist circumference, insulin levels, and the HOMA-IR, while mtDNA content shows a significant negative correlation with insulin and HOMA-IR. This suggests that PPARGC1A promoter methylation and mtDNA content may serve as potential biomarkers for PCOS, and the abnormal methylation of the PPARGC1A promoter could lead to metabolic dysfunction in PCOS patients by affecting mitochondrial function. Although these two studies focus on different aspects—one on global DNA methylation levels in immune cells and the other on the relationship between promoter methylation in leukocytes and disease phenotype—they both indicate that the epigenetic mechanisms of immune cells play a significant role in the pathogenesis of PCOS.

Telomeres are specialized nucleoprotein structures at the ends of chromosomes, composed of repetitive short DNA sequences and associated proteins. Their primary function is to protect chromosome ends, preventing them from fusing with other chromosome ends, thereby maintaining genomic stability. Telomere length gradually shortens with each cell division, a process closely associated with cellular aging and individual lifespan [63]. In early studies investigating the correlation between leukocyte telomere length (LTL) and PCOS, researchers found that LTL in 698 Chinese PCOS patients was significantly shorter than that of 611 healthy control women, and this shortening of LTL was closely associated with PCOS [64]. This shortening may be due to elevated oxidative stress levels caused by excess androgens, insulin resistance, and obesity, which in turn exacerbates endocrine and metabolic disturbances in PCOS patients. Another study explored the effects of metformin treatment on LTL in PCOS patients, finding that metformin may protect LTL by reducing oxidative stress and inflammatory responses through the activation of the AMPK pathway [65]. However, a recent comprehensive cross-sectional and longitudinal study conducted in Northern Finland involving PCOS patients aged 31 and 46 years included samples of 190 and 207 PCOS patients, respectively, alongside 1,054 and 1,324 control participants [66]. The results showed no significant differences in average LTL between PCOS patients and controls at either age. While the LTL of the control group significantly shortened from ages 31 to 46, the change in LTL for PCOS patients was not significant, suggesting that the rate of telomere shortening in PCOS patients may be slower and that other mechanisms may be protecting LTL. This finding contrasts with earlier studies and may relate to differences in sample size and the geographic origin of the patients. Therefore, further research is needed to explore the potential mechanisms underlying the relationship between PCOS and LTL, particularly concerning inflammation, oxidative stress, and hormone exposure.

Gut microbiota and their metabolites regulate PCOS through the immune system

In the development of PCOS, both gut microbiota and the immune system play crucial roles. Gut microbiota and their metabolites can modulate the state of the immune system, affecting its function and thereby participating in the pathogenesis of PCOS. This influence may involve specific genera and species of bacteria, as well as metabolites produced jointly by the gut microbiota and the host.

Gut microbiota and the immune system

The gut microbiota is one of the largest ecosystems of host symbionts, playing a critical role in maintaining gut homeostasis and immune system function. Microbes and their metabolites can enter host cells through the intestinal mucosal epithelial layer, subsequently influencing the immune system, either maintaining a normal immune state or promoting the development of diseases [67–70]. Components of microbes, such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and peptidoglycans, can be recognized by receptors like TLR4, PGLYRPs, TLRs, and NLRs, thereby participating in inflammatory immune responses. For instance, peptidoglycan can activate the phagocytic activity of neutrophils after entering the bloodstream from the gut [68]. Lipoteichoic acid (LTA) induces different immune responses by modulating TLR2, while flagellin acts as a TLR5 agonist, activating the MyD88-dependent signaling pathway to promote antibody production and immune memory. Additionally, SCFAs like butyrate and propionate enhance the differentiation and proliferation of Tregs. Tryptophan metabolites such as indole, 3-methylindole, and serotonin can bind to the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) to regulate immune cell activity, while other metabolites like branched-chain amino acids and trimethylamine also indirectly affect the immune system [67]. The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in regulating inflammatory responses, with certain symbiotic bacteria exhibiting anti-inflammatory effects. For instance, Bacteroides fragilis produces polysaccharide A, which can inhibit the production of IL-17 and enhance the activity of intestinal Tregs, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects [10, 68, 71]. On the other hand, gut microbiota can also induce inflammatory responses through specific mechanisms. For example, taurine produced by the gut microbiota in mice can enhance the activity of the NLRP6 inflammasome, leading to the secretion of IL-18. In contrast, histamine and spermine, which are produced jointly by the host and gut microbiota, can inhibit the secretion of IL-18 [72]. Additionally, the DNA of gut bacteria can enhance the activity of the AIM2 inflammasome, resulting in increased IL-18 secretion and decreased expression of IL-22 binding protein (IL-22BP), thereby exacerbating inflammatory responses [58]. The gut microbiota not only plays a role in inflammatory regulation but also has a key role in the formation and maturation of immune cells. After birth, gut microbiota and their metabolites interact with the host immune system to promote normal immune development. Microbial-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) released by gut microbiota guide lymphoid tissue-inducing cells (LTi) to migrate to mesenteric lymph nodes (MLN) and Peyer’s patches (PPs), stimulating the development of these immune organs and promoting the maturation and distribution of immune cells. These cells also migrate to intestinal mucosal crypts, supporting the formation and maturation of isolated lymphoid follicles (ILFs) and gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT). Furthermore, LTi cells are involved in the maturation of dendritic cells, B cells, and T cells, as well as in immune responses [69]. Therefore, considering the roles of the immune system and gut microbiota in the development of PCOS, a deeper investigation into how specific bacterial species regulate immune cells or immune pathways may provide new insights for the diagnosis and treatment of PCOS.

Immune regulation in polycystic ovary syndrome mediated by gut microbiota–derived metabolites

The gut microbiota participates in the development of PCOS through immune system regulation. In PCOS patients, Bacteroides vulgatus and Prevotella copri, which have relatively high species-level abundance, are most closely associated with the immune system [30]. In PCOS rats, fasting insulin levels show significant correlations with specific bacterial genera and inflammatory markers, such as IL-6 and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (HS-CRP). Ruminococcaceae UCG-003 and Lachnospiraceae UCG-008 are positively correlated with both inflammatory markers and fasting insulin levels, whereas increased abundance of Alloprevotella is often associated with reduced levels of IL-6 and HS-CRP [73]. Gut dysbiosis in PCOS increases intestinal permeability, allowing LPS to enter the bloodstream, which subsequently elevates fasting blood glucose and insulin levels [74]. Additionally, the gut microbiota is closely associated with PCOS through the regulation of various metabolites [75, 76]. In PCOS, the gut microbiota interacts with the host through metabolites produced directly or indirectly, which may originate from bacteria or result from the transformation of food or host substrates by gut microbes. These metabolites serve as a bridge linking the gut microbiota to the host immune system and shared metabolic pathways. Key metabolites involved include LPS, branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), SCFAs, bile acids, trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), and glucuronide-sulfatase and β-glucuronidase derivatives (GUSBD).

Lipopolysaccharides(LPS)

In patients with PCOS, dysbiosis of the gut microbiota leads to the translocation of LPS from Gram-negative bacteria into systemic circulation. On one hand, immune activation triggered by LPS impairs insulin receptor function, resulting in elevated serum insulin levels. On the other, LPS is transported to immune cells via binding to LPS-binding protein (LBP), CD14, and myeloid differentiation factor (MD-2), where it binds Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on the cell surface. This interaction activates downstream signaling pathways that promote the release of inflammatory cytokines. This process drives macrophages accumulated in tissues to polarize from an M2 to an M1 phenotype, further stimulating the expression and release of TNF-α and IL-6 in peripheral circulation and ovarian tissues via the TLR4-NF-κB signaling pathway (Fig. 3A). Additionally, macrophages can recognize LPS via nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptors, thereby activating the innate immune system and generating proinflammatory cytokines, leading to insulin resistance and associated pathological features. Macrophage-released interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) enhances macrophage function, while a high-fat diet significantly increases circulating LPS levels, exacerbating this inflammation and promoting androgen production in the ovaries, disrupting normal follicle formation [23, 25, 65, 77, 78]. Furthermore, LPS induces macrophage-dependent pyroptosis in the ovaries via Gasdermin D (GSDMD), disrupting estrogen production and promoting granulosa cell apoptosis. This interference affects communication between granulosa cells and oocytes. Notably, GSDMD knockout significantly improves ovulatory dysfunction in PCOS [65]. LPS entering the host circulation can directly activate caspase-4 and caspase-5 in human macrophages, as well as caspase-11 in mice, leading to inflammatory cell death known as pyroptosis. This process in macrophages suppresses the expression of the Esr2 gene in granulosa cells, thereby disrupting gonadotropin stimulation of follicular maturation. GSDMD-dependent macrophage pyroptosis triggers the release of cytokines like IL-1β and IL-18, or damages cytoplasmic membrane components (e.g., receptor complexes, connexins, and synapses), impairing communication between granulosa cells and oocytes, ultimately hindering oocyte development. Additionally, GSDMD-dependent macrophage pyroptosis leads to sepsis, inhibiting the transcription and translation of genes related to estrogen synthesis and granulosa cell apoptosis, reducing estrogen and estrogen receptor β synthesis, inducing granulosa cell apoptosis, and resulting in follicular atresia [78] (Fig. 3A). In summary, LPS produced by the gut microbiota contributes to the persistent inflammatory state in PCOS pathogenesis by modulating inflammatory responses and inducing macrophage pyroptosis, thereby impeding oocyte development, maturation, and release.

Short-chain fatty acids(SCFAs)

SCFAs are the primary metabolic products of the gut microbiota, primarily comprising acetate, propionate, and butyrate. They are produced through the fermentation of non-digestible carbohydrates (NDCs) by gut microbes, with key bacteria involved in SCFA production including Bacteroidetes, Akkermansia muciniphila, Ruminococcus bromii, Eubacterium rectale, and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii. SCFAs are closely associated with maintaining gut integrity, lipid metabolism, immune regulation, and anti-inflammatory functions [77, 78]. In patients with PCOS, an increase in Streptococcaceae and a decrease in Rikenellaceae are associated with reduced butyrate levels [79]. Additionally, the abundance of four SCFA-producing gut microbes—Anaerococcus spp., Odoribacter spp., Roseburia spp., and Ruminococcus bromii—is also lower in PCOS patients. Studies indicate that SCFAs produced by Bifidobacteria or other probiotics can significantly improve hyperglycemia and insulin resistance in patients with PCOS [80].These studies suggest that SCFAs produced by the gut microbiota may have beneficial effects on patients with PCOS.

SCFAs not only alter energy metabolism but also act as mediators between the gut microbiota and the immune system, influencing host immune function. SCFAs exert anti-inflammatory effects by activating PPAR-γ in the liver and muscle or by directly modulating peripheral dendritic cells (DCs) and T cells to induce systemic anti-inflammatory responses. Additionally, SCFAs can increase the number of Tregs, regulate the production of interleukins (ILs), and modulate immune cells by binding to free fatty acid receptors (FFARs) in the G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) family [18, 58, 81]. SCFAs activate four distinct FFARs on the cell membrane, including FFAR2 (GPR43), FFAR3 (GPR41), and hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 2 (HCA2/GPR109a). GPR43 is primarily expressed in epithelial and immune cells of the gastrointestinal tract, GPR41 in the lamina propria of the colon, spleen, lymph nodes, and polymorphonuclear leukocytes, while GPR109a is found in colonic epithelial cells and immune cells such as macrophages, monocytes, and neutrophils. When SCFAs act directly on immune cells, they activate GPR43 on neutrophils, inducing chemotaxis and regulating degranulation, while suppressing NF-κB and inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6 and TNF-α). Additionally, through GPR43 activation, SCFAs promote macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, increasing IL-10 secretion by macrophages and alleviating the low-grade inflammation characteristic of PCOS (Fig. 3B). In intestinal cells, SCFAs activate GPR41, GPR43, and GPR109a, subsequently triggering the RAS, protein kinase A, PI3K, and ERK1/2 signaling pathways. These pathways regulate the expression of key immune and inflammatory mediators such as IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, CXCL1, and CXCL2 via transcription factors like ATF2 [58, 77]. SCFAs can also trigger glucagon-like peptide (GLP) secretion through GPR43, acting on pancreatic P cells to regulate insulin secretion in the body. This modulation plays a role in addressing insulin resistance in PCOS and subsequent metabolic syndrome [82]. This mechanism may help improve the obesity phenotype associated with PCOS. Additionally, SCFAs induce the expression of SIRT1, a member of the sirtuin family of histone deacetylases, which plays a crucial role in insulin secretion, glucose metabolism, and immune responses. In immune regulation, SCFAs promote immune tolerance and anti-inflammatory cell phenotypes by inhibiting histone deacetylases (HDACs), particularly fostering Tregs. This process ultimately leads to NF-κB inactivation in monocytes and neutrophils and a reduction in the production of pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF. SIRT1 also directly impacts ovarian regulation by modulating the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis, supporting essential ovarian functions and promoting the estrous cycle. The absence of SIRT1 disrupts the estrous cycle [83, 84]. Butyrate, a key member of SCFAs, improves LPS-induced apoptosis and glucose metabolism disorders in KGN cells via the PPAR-γ/GLUT4 pathway, achieved by inhibiting the activity of methyltransferase METTL3. Studies indicate elevated expression of METTL3 and METTL14 in PCOS patients, leading to an increase in m6A modifications that affect the m6A methylation level of FOXO3, thereby hindering communication between granulosa cells and oocytes and resulting in impaired oocyte maturation and release. On the other hand, butyrate inhibits METTL3, reducing m6A modifications of FOSL2 in KGN cells, which suppresses the expression of inflammatory factors IL-6 and TNF-α. Additionally, butyrate lowers the expression of the inflammasome NLRP3, ultimately alleviating the inflammatory state in PCOS granulosa cells [79, 85] (Fig]. 3C). In summary, SCFAs produced by the gut microbiota play a significant role in modulating the immune system, thereby contributing to the pathophysiology of PCOS.

Branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs)

BCAAs, primarily leucine, isoleucine, and valine, are essential amino acids that the human body cannot synthesize on its own and must be obtained through diet or synthesized by gut microbiota. Studies have shown that BCAAs are key fasting serum metabolites associated with IR and metabolic syndrome [86]. BCAAs are significantly correlated with the m77etabolic syndrome commonly observed in patients with PCOS. In a high-fat diet-induced mouse model, BCAA levels were positively associated with the abundance of gut microbiota such as Ileibacterium and Turicibacter [87]. In terms of insulin sensitivity, non-targeted serum metabolomics studies have revealed two metabolite clusters associated with IR and metabolic syndrome, termed the IR and insulin sensitivity (IS) metabotypes. The IR metabotype includes amino acids and BCAAs. Research shows that microbial functional modules positively correlated with IR involve BCAA synthases, while those negatively correlated involve BCAA transport systems. Microbial functional modules driven primarily by P. copri and B. vulgatus are positively correlated with HOMA-IR, increasing serum BCAA concentrations and being linked to IR. Further research has revealed that P. copri is the primary strain promoting gut microbial synthesis of BCAAs and is associated with IR. An increased abundance of P. copri exacerbates glucose intolerance [86]. In studies on fatty liver and glucose tolerance, it was found that serum BCAA levels were higher in the high-fat diet group compared to the normal diet group. Correlation analysis showed that BCAA production was mainly driven by bacterial groups such as Clostridiales, Streptococcaceae, Clostridiaceae, and Prevotellaceae. In particular, the genus Prevotella was positively correlated with circulating BCAA levels and the HOMA-IR index [88]. Further validation confirmed the close association between IR in PCOS and BCAAs produced by the gut microbiota.

In the immune system, changes in BCAAs produced by the gut microbiota directly influence the structure of microbial metabolites, thereby regulating the host’s immune response. Alpha-galactosylceramides produced by Bacteroides fragilis contain branched-chain fatty acids, which can be synthesized from the deamination of valine, leucine, and isoleucine, determining their structure. This compound can inhibit the proliferation of natural killer T cells (NKT cells) in the colon and antagonize their function. Additionally, structural analogs constructed in vitro exert immune regulatory functions through the CD1d-NKT cell receptor axis. In mice treated with these analogs, immune regulatory genes such as IL-2, IL-4, and IL-13, as well as genes like ERG1-3, LRRC32, and SOCS1/CISH, were significantly upregulated. This leads to effects mediated through signaling pathways, including TNF-α/NF-κB, IL-2/STAT5, and IL-6/JAK/STAT3 [89] (Fig. 3D). TNF-α and IL-6 play significant roles in the inflammatory state of PCOS. Additionally, studies have shown that Bergenin can regulate the BCAA-mediated mTOR/p70S6K signaling pathway by inhibiting the abundance of Bacteroides vulgatus, thereby improving the inflammatory state. The increase in BCAAs not only elevates the activity of myeloperoxidase (MPO) but also promotes elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, while reducing the levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, thus exacerbating the inflammatory response [90]. These changes may be closely related to the inflammatory state in PCOS; therefore, BCAAs might regulate the occurrence of PCOS through these pathways. However, deeper mechanisms need to be further investigated to confirm this.

Bile acids

Bile acids are synthesized in the liver through a series of enzymatic reactions, referred to as primary bile acids. Newly synthesized bile acids conjugate with glycine or taurine in hepatocytes to form conjugated bile acids. These conjugated bile acids are secreted into the small intestine, where they aid in the digestion and absorption of fats and are subsequently converted into secondary bile acids. These secondary bile acids are reabsorbed at the end of the ileum and returned to the liver via the portal vein, thus forming the enterohepatic circulation [91]. Studies have shown that conjugated primary bile acids in PCOS patients are positively correlated with serum androgen levels, including serum total testosterone (TT) and androstenedione levels. Additionally, the levels of primary bile acids capable of binding with glycine and taurine are significantly higher in individuals with PCOS compared to the normal population [92]. The levels of glycochenodeoxycholic acid (GDCA) and taurochenodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) are significantly reduced. Within the gut, Bacteroides vulgatus (B. vulgatus) has been identified as an important microorganism regulating bile acid metabolism, as B. vulgatus promotes the depolymerization of conjugated bile acids, thereby increasing androgen levels. In animal experiments, TUDCA and GDCA can increase the levels of IL-22 mRNA in the gut. However, B. vulgatus from PCOS patients can lead to a reduction in TUDCA and GDCA by regulating bile acid metabolism. The decrease in GDCA affects the levels of IL-22 produced by innate lymphoid cells (ILC3) through the cAMP-GATA3 signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting its production and improving symptoms such as insulin resistance and ovarian dysfunction in PCOS mice [25, 93]. Therefore, the gut microbiota can participate in the regulation and generation of IL-22 through bile acid metabolism, thereby impacting ovarian function and insulin sensitivity in PCOS. Additionally, bile acids in the gut can activate the membrane receptor TGR5, initiating the cAMP-PKA signaling pathway. The activated PKA kinase induces the ubiquitination and phosphorylation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, inhibiting its activation. This process helps prevent metabolic disorders and blocks the maturation and release of caspase-1-dependent pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-1β and IL-18) triggered by NLRP3 (Fig. 3E). In vivo experiments indicate that bile acids can improve metabolic disorders and inflammation associated with type 2 diabetes (or insulin resistance) through this pathway [94]. It is noteworthy that this mechanism bears similarities to the metabolic disorders observed in PCOS animal models. Therefore, the inflammatory response associated with granulosa cell apoptosis and insulin resistance in PCOS may also be improved through this pathway. Additionally, research has found that resistant starch (RS) increases the production of secondary bile acids by affecting gut microbiota such as R. bromii, B. adolescentis, and B. vulgatus, thereby alleviating obesity and insulin resistance. This effect is closely related to the reduced abundance of bile salt hydrolase (BSH) genes, with BSH expression being significantly negatively correlated with B. vulgatus. Moreover, B. adolescentis is positively correlated with serum ANGPTL4. Bile acids play a crucial role in lipid metabolism, and these mechanisms are significant for alleviating the common insulin resistance and obesity in PCOS patients [95, 96]. Furthermore, gut microbiota participate in the host’s bile acid metabolism through various pathways, thereby influencing cytokines and inflammasomes within the immune system. This interaction plays a significant role in the ovarian immune microenvironment and core symptoms of PCOS patients.

β-Glucuronidase(GUSB)

β-glucuronidase (GUSB), a member of the glycoside hydrolase family 2 (GH2), plays a crucial regulatory role in estrogen metabolism by hydrolyzing β-glucuronides to release glucuronic acid and other compounds [97]. The gut microbiota, typically comprising Gram-negative bacteria, promotes the enterohepatic circulation of estrogen metabolites in the host by secreting GUSB, which hydrolyzes estrogen glucuronides [98]. In patients with PCOS, GUSB activity levels are elevated [99]. tudies have identified Parabacteroides johnsonii and Paraprevotella clara as the primary bacteria enriched in GUSB. Pharmacological clearance of these strains can reduce GUSB activity, inhibit the intestinal BAs-FXR signaling pathway, and increase ileal GLP-1 secretion, thereby preventing hyperglycemia [100]. Lowering GUSB activity to enhance GLP-1 secretion is significant in improving insulin resistance in PCOS. Additionally, in in vitro cell experiments, GUSB expression in M2 macrophages was found to be three times higher than that in M1 macrophages. GUSB can stimulate intracellular calcium ion release into the extracellular space, activating and regulating macrophages. Following GUSB treatment, the expression levels of CD68, Arg-1, IL-10, CD206, and CD163 in M0 macrophages, as well as the concentrations of NGF and TNF-α, were significantly increased [101].The elevation of these immune cell receptors and inflammatory factors plays a significant role in the inflammatory state of PCOS. This suggests that the increased activity of GUSB can promote estrogen metabolism and contribute to the inflammatory state that leads to the development of PCOS.

Other gut microbiota-related metabolites

The metabolites produced by the gut microbiota are diverse, with other major metabolites associated with PCOS including trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), tryptophan, and vitamin B12. TMAO is derived from the conversion of trimethylamine (TMA), which results from the microbial and host metabolism of choline and L-carnitine found in red meat, eggs, and fish. It is one of the important products of microbiota-host symbiotic metabolism. TMAO can reduce lipogenesis and lipid accumulation, improve insulin secretion, restore glucose tolerance, and alleviate endoplasmic reticulum stress associated with inflammation and insulin resistance [102, 103]. In vitro experiments have shown that TMAO decreases the levels of Bacteroidaceae and the overall metabolic potential of the microbiota while increasing the levels of pro-inflammatory Enterobacteriaceae and lipopolysaccharides, thereby exacerbating the inflammatory response. Furthermore, TMAO also elevates the levels of inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and TNF-α in the immune system, while reducing the levels of anti-inflammatory factors like IL-10 and IL-4 [104]. Similar to the changes observed in the immune system of PCOS, these findings suggest that TMAO may play a role in the occurrence and progression of PCOS within the immune system. Tryptophan, an essential aromatic amino acid, can be metabolized by gut microbes, such as Lactobacilli, into indole and its derivatives. These derivatives act as ligands for the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), stimulating ILC3s to produce IL-22 and increasing the number of intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs) in the gut. Furthermore, defects in the AHR pathway can lead to a reduction in GLP-1, which plays a significant role in insulin resistance and low-grade inflammation in PCOS [84, 103]. Vitamin B12, a regulator of gut microbiota, is primarily produced by Cetobacterium and serves as a key factor in enhancing the host’s intestinal resistance. It strengthens the interaction between gut microbiota and the tight junctions of the intestinal barrier, thereby improving the host’s resistance to pathogenic infections [105]. This may reduce the entry of harmful gut microorganisms and their metabolites (such as LPS) into the circulatory system, thereby preventing or alleviating immune system dysfunction associated with PCOS. Furthermore, certain components of the gut microbiota (such as PSA and N-formyl peptide moieties) as well as metabolites like imidazole propionate (IMP) and polyamines play significant roles in inducing insulin resistance, serving as markers of low-grade inflammation, maintaining the intestinal epithelial barrier, and interacting with the immune system. These components may play important roles in the pathogenesis of PCOS mediated through the immune system [84, 103]. Overall, the gut microbiota and its associated metabolites play a crucial role in immune regulation, thus having a significant impact on the occurrence and development of PCOS.

Future directions and prospects

Challenges in diagnosis and treatment

The etiology and pathological mechanisms of PCOS are complex, involving genetic diversity and the interaction between genes and the environment. Although it is known that PCOS patients commonly exhibit insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome, characterized primarily by elevated androgen levels, the specific mechanisms underlying insulin resistance and hyperandrogenemia remain unclear. The relationship between PCOS, gut microbiota, and the complexity of the immune system also presents challenges for diagnosis and treatment. PCOS is closely associated with chronic low-grade inflammation, where abnormalities in immune cells play a significant role. However, the specific regulatory molecular mechanisms that are critical in PCOS require further exploration, posing a challenge for developing immunomodulatory therapies based on the immune system. Additionally, epigenetic changes in immune cells and abnormalities in telomere length have been observed in PCOS, but the specific roles and clinical significance of these indicators remain unclear. This adds to the complexity of using these metrics for early diagnosis and prognostic assessment of PCOS. The diversity of the gut microbiota is reflected in individual variations and its relationship with metabolic disorders. While there is some overall similarity in the composition and function of gut microbiota among different PCOS patients, there remains substantial inter-individual variability. The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in PCOS-related lipid metabolism abnormalities, insulin resistance, and hyperandrogenism, yet the specific mechanisms are complex and variable, involving multiple bacterial species, various metabolites, and signaling pathways. Consequently, further research is needed to improve the diagnosis and treatment of PCOS.

In the treatment of PCOS, current first-line therapies primarily focus on dietary interventions and the management of related complications, such as anovulatory uterine bleeding, skin issues (including hirsutism, acne, and alopecia), metabolic disorders (type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular diseases), and anovulatory infertility [5]. However, due to the multifactorial etiology and multi-system impacts of PCOS, single treatment methods often fail to achieve optimal results. Therefore, developing a multi-target comprehensive treatment strategy that considers factors such as hormone regulation, metabolic control, immune modulation, and gut microbiota regulation is particularly necessary. Moreover, in the context of personalized and precision medicine, the symptoms and conditions of PCOS patients vary significantly, necessitating the formulation of individualized treatment plans that consider multiple factors. Among these, the interactions between gut microbiota and its metabolites with the immune system are crucial. Metabolites such as SCFAs, BCAAs and LPS influence the host immune system through various mechanisms, regulating immune cell function and inflammatory responses. These complex mechanisms increase the difficulty of researching and understanding their specific roles in PCOS. Current diagnostic and treatment methods have not comprehensively addressed these aspects, making it vital to explore these mechanisms further to develop more precise and individualized therapeutic strategies. Specifically, although an increasing body of evidence has demonstrated that the gut microbiota and its metabolites contribute to the onset and progression of PCOS through multiple immunoregulatory mechanisms, several critical challenges still impede the translation of these fundamental findings into clinical practice. First, the gut microbiota exhibits marked interindividual variability, and no unified reference standard has yet been established for clinical application. PCOS patients display substantial microbial heterogeneity, with pronounced differences observed across clinical subtypes such as obese, insulin-resistant, and hyperandrogenic phenotypes [106]. Moreover, consistent “core pathogenic taxa” distinguishing PCOS from healthy individuals have not been identified, and a clinically actionable, PCOS-specific microbial signature has not been defined. Consequently, current microbiome profiles cannot yet support reproducible, clinically operable diagnostic, subclassification, or therapeutic decision-making. Second, the causal relationships between microbial–immune signaling and PCOS pathophysiology remain insufficiently clarified. Although several mechanistic pathways, such as the LPS–TLR4 axis, BCAAs–mTOR signaling, and bile acid–IL-22 interactions, have been proposed, most evidence to date remains correlative rather than causal. In addition, it is still unclear whether immune dysregulation (Th17/Treg imbalance, M1/M2 macrophage polarization) precedes or follows microbial alterations, further limiting the development of mechanism-based precision therapies. Third, methodologies for assessing microbiota composition, microbial metabolites (LPS, SCFAs, BCAAs, TMAO), and immune parameters (MAIT cells, Th17/Treg ratios) lack standardization and harmonized reference ranges, preventing their use as reliable diagnostic biomarkers or predictors of therapeutic response. The inherent complexity and dynamic nature of the immune microenvironment also constrain the feasibility of immune-targeted interventions. Although PCOS is frequently characterized by Th17/Treg disequilibrium, M1/M2 macrophage skewing, and local ovarian immune disturbances involving aberrant cytokine and chemokine production, immune homeostasis fluctuates substantially under the influence of hormonal status, adiposity, and lifestyle factors. While immune-targeted therapies such as IL-6 or TNF-α blockade may offer potential therapeutic benefits, their safety and risk–benefit profiles require careful evaluation [107, 108]. Taken together, future studies should integrate genomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics, and metagenomics to construct more refined molecular subtype models of PCOS, and large-scale, longitudinal clinical trials will be essential for validating the efficacy and safety of microbiota-based targeted interventions before they can be incorporated into routine clinical management.

Early diagnosis of PCOS, metabolic risk prediction, and multi-omics-based diagnostic strategies

The metabolic syndrome phenotype of PCOS is closely linked to gut microbiota dysbiosis. The co-metabolites produced by the gut microbiota and the host can often serve as precursors to the heterogeneity observed in metabolic syndrome phenotypes. For instance, co-metabolites such as trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), phenylacetylglutamine (PAG), and hippurate have been identified as predictive features in the outcomes of diabetes and obesity, demonstrating a strong potential to predict metabolic risks associated with PCOS [102]. Among these, trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) can also predict the risk of cardiovascular disease, while BCAAs are associated with an increased risk of type 2 diabetes (T2D). Moreover, the application of metagenomic sequencing has revealed more details about the relationship between microbial genes and functions and the risk of metabolic diseases. This has significant implications for achieving precise predictions related to gut microbiota-associated diseases, facilitating early diagnosis of these conditions [109]. Identifying efficient, non-invasive, and easily collectible biomarkers for early diagnosis of PCOS would represent a significant breakthrough in early treatment and improvement of fertility. Gut microbiota possesses these characteristics and is now regarded as a potential diagnostic biomarker for various metabolic diseases. A typical example is a study that utilized a machine learning classifier based on the gut microbiome metagenome, combined with multivariate analysis and characterization of gut microbiota, which successfully differentiated patients with type 2 diabetes (insulin resistance) from healthy individuals [110]. By analyzing the microbiome structure in fecal samples and integrating artificial intelligence techniques to develop classifier models, early diagnosis and prediction of PCOS may be achievable before the appearance of pathological features in the ovaries. Additionally, microbial-related metabolites or active molecules detected in serum, such as SCFAs, BCAAs and secondary bile acids, may also become potential biomarkers for future PCOS, providing supportive diagnostic and predictive capabilities [109]. Given the regulatory role of the gut microbiota on the immune system and its relationship with changes in PCOS, certain specific types of immune cells and immune factors are also expected to serve as metabolic predictors and diagnostic biomarkers for PCOS. Therefore, risk prediction and early diagnosis of PCOS should not be limited to a single indicator or system; rather, it should integrate clinical features, blood parameters, genetic testing results, imaging examinations, gut microbiota characteristics, and the aforementioned potential biomarkers to develop a multifactorial risk assessment model. This approach will enable a more comprehensive and accurate early prediction and diagnosis of PCOS, providing patients with opportunities for early intervention and personalized treatment. We would also like to emphasize that although gut microbiota–derived metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), indole-3-propionic acid (IPA), and secondary bile acids, have been mechanistically linked to insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, and hormonal dysregulation in PCOS, and thus represent promising candidates for molecular biomarkers and therapeutic targets, current evidence remains largely based on small- to medium-sized clinical studies and preclinical mechanistic investigations. Considerable inconsistencies persist across studies regarding the directionality and magnitude of these metabolic alterations, and their levels are influenced by multiple confounding factors, including dietary patterns, body weight, medication use, and genetic background. In addition, accurate quantification of SCFAs, IPA, and related metabolites requires advanced analytical platforms such as GC–MS or LC–MS/MS, which are not widely available in most routine clinical laboratories. These issues limit the reproducibility and reliability of such metabolites as stable clinical indicators. The relatively high cost of testing further restricts their feasibility in general health assessments or during the initial clinical evaluation of patients with suspected PCOS. Therefore, at present, we deem that these microbial metabolites are not suitable to be incorporated as standalone biomarkers in routine PCOS diagnostic workflows. Instead, they are more appropriately positioned as complementary molecular indicators for refining diagnostic models or for monitoring therapeutic responses during interventions such as probiotic supplementation, dietary modulation, or lifestyle-based metabolic management. As analytical technologies continue to advance, testing costs decline, and large multicenter clinical cohorts provide more robust validation, these metabolites may ultimately be integrated into individualized PCOS management frameworks, offering valuable molecular-level insights for precision diagnosis and tailored treatment strategies.

Moreover, given the substantial genetic heterogeneity of PCOS, the associated genetic variants are highly complex and widely distributed. With the rapid advancement of transcriptomics, single-cell sequencing, metabolomics, and microbiome sciences, multi-omics integration has provided critical breakthroughs for elucidating the systemic mechanisms of PCOS and improving diagnostic accuracy. Recent genomic investigations, particularly large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) involving hundreds of thousands of samples, have identified nearly one hundred independent loci associated with PCOS, many of which were previously unreported. These findings have refined the genetic architecture of PCOS, revealed its polygenic risk structure, and highlighted the potential involvement of pathways such as AMH and PPARG in disease pathogenesis. Importantly, these genetic discoveries have facilitated the development of polygenic risk score (PRS) models, laying a theoretical foundation for genetically-informed risk prediction and molecular subtyping [111]. Concurrently, transcriptomic analyses and integrative multi-omics approaches have markedly advanced the discovery of molecular biomarkers and mechanistic insights in PCOS. Through RNA sequencing, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), and machine-learning–based feature extraction, numerous differentially expressed genes have been identified in granulosa and cumulus cells of PCOS patients, alongside regulatory networks involving immunity, metabolism, cell cycle pathways, and intracellular signaling. Notably, several key genes, such as CLDN11, HLA-DMA, TAB 3, and LYN, exhibit strong diagnostic potential and are closely linked to IL-17, MAPK, and mTOR pathways, suggesting that they may serve as central nodes in PCOS pathophysiology. Furthermore, integrated transcriptomics and machine-learning analyses have identified hub genes associated with metabolic comorbidities, including DPYSL4, FOS, TRIB1, and ZNF331, indicating that the molecular features of PCOS extend beyond reproductive axis dysregulation to encompass systemic metabolic and inflammatory abnormalities [112, 113]. Collectively, genomic studies demonstrate that the susceptibility architecture of PCOS is not confined to the reproductive system but instead involves broader regulation of systemic homeostasis, thereby facilitating identification of high-risk individuals, including women with latent susceptibility who may progress to overt PCOS later in life [114–116]. Transcriptomics provides a complementary layer of information by capturing “active disease pathways,” reflecting real-time biological activity. Stable transcriptomic signatures, such as upregulated inflammatory signaling, impaired energy metabolism, and dysregulated steroidogenesis, have been consistently observed across ovarian tissue, adipose tissue, and immune cells of PCOS patients. Single-cell transcriptomics further reveals disruptions in the ovarian microenvironment, including impaired communication among granulosa cells, theca cells, and immune cells. In parallel, microbiome and immunomics analyses offer phenotypic mechanistic evidence, demonstrating that gut microbial signals can regulate key pathways such as LPS–TLR4, BCAA–mTOR, bile acid–IL-22, and SCFA–GPR43, ultimately contributing to Th17/Treg imbalance, M1/M2 macrophage polarization, and increased IL-6/TNF-α levels. These findings underscore that multi-omics integration effectively bridges genetic predisposition with active disease processes, enabling the identification of distinct PCOS subtypes, the detection of early metabolic and immunological perturbations, and the mapping of cross-omics regulatory networks that facilitate biomarker discovery and therapeutic target identification [117]. Taken together, incorporating genomic, transcriptomic, and microbiome–immune features into diagnostic frameworks holds considerable promise for transcending traditional phenotype-based diagnostic paradigms and establishing a robust foundation for the development of precision medicine in PCOS.

Exploring new therapeutic pathways for PCOS by leveraging gut microbiota and the immune system

Existing research has revealed specific dysbiosis patterns of gut microbiota in PCOS patients and indicated that the use of prebiotics and probiotics is one of the important therapeutic measures. In the future, finding effective probiotics, prebiotics, or performing fecal transplants to restore gut microbiota balance may enable precise microbiome therapy. However, the currently known beneficial bacterial strains are limited, and acquiring and validating more effective strains requires significant time and effort. Utilizing artificial intelligence and big data analysis technologies can integrate a large amount of genomic, microbiomic, and clinical data to identify characteristic patterns and key regulatory mechanisms in PCOS patients, thereby discovering more effective beneficial strains to address this challenge. Researchers have developed an online bioinformatics tool for the rapid screening of probiotics by analyzing the oligonucleotide composition features of microbial genomics combined with machine learning algorithms to predict and select probiotics at the strain level: iProbiotics [118](free access:http://bioinfor.imu.edu.cn/iprobiotics). It predicts the probability of bacterial genera as potential probiotics based on the input genomic sequences. Additionally, another tool developed this year, called metaProbiotics, utilizes an artificial intelligence language model for the rapid detection of probiotic populations in metagenomes (available for free download at https://github.com/zhenchengfang/metaProbiotics) [119]. Compared to iProbiotics, this tool offers more robust performance and broader applicability. Utilizing metaProbiotics, certain bacterial genera that commonly do not reveal probiotics, such as Butyricicoccus, Parabacteroides, Phocaeicola, Agathobaculum, Alistipes, Collinsella, and Ruminococcus, have been predicted to possess probiotic properties to some extent. These bacteria have been verified to exert beneficial effects through various mechanisms, including the production of SCFAs, involvement in immune modulation, metabolic regulation, and intestinal barrier protection. The use of this tool has also revealed that probiotics exhibit heterogeneity across different disease types. Future research directions include establishing a PCOS-related probiotic model based on probiotic community structures and host factors, combined with big data analysis, to enhance personalized probiotic interventions. Exploring more bacterial genera may further identify connections related to the onset of PCOS. Furthermore, the gut microbiota exhibits substantial interindividual variability, reflected not only in differences in microbial diversity and core taxa abundance but also in marked heterogeneity in functional metabolic capacity. Recent studies have classified the gut microbiota of PCOS patients into several representative enterotypes, such as Prevotella-rich, Bacteroides-rich, and Ruminococcus/butyrate-producing types [120, 121]. These enterotypes display distinct metabolic and immunological features and are closely associated with the severity of insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, and androgen imbalance in PCOS. Therefore, such enterotype-specific characteristics may profoundly influence patient responses to particular therapeutic strategies, providing valuable insights for individualized treatment in PCOS. Specifically, individuals with a Bacteroides-rich enterotype tend to benefit more from probiotic supplementation and high-fiber dietary interventions, whereas those with a Prevotella-rich enterotype often respond better to low-carbohydrate and anti-inflammatory dietary patterns. In addition, the therapeutic efficacy of metformin appears to be modulated by gut microbiota composition, with its glucose-lowering and metabolic benefits partially mediated through key taxa such as Akkermansia [122]. Distinct inflammatory profiles driven by different enterotypes may also influence patient responsiveness to anti-inflammatory therapies. Overall, individual variability in gut microbiota not only shapes metabolic and hormonal phenotypes in PCOS but also substantially affects patients’ responses to dietary modulation, probiotics, pharmacological treatment, and immune-targeted interventions. As a result, “enterotype-based personalized therapy” is emerging as a promising direction for precision medicine in PCOS. With the continued accumulation of metagenomic, metabolomic, and immunomic data, incorporating microbial enterotypes into PCOS subclassification frameworks may significantly enhance treatment matching and help transition clinical management from empirical interventions toward predictable, quantifiable, and mechanism-informed precision therapeutics.

The gut microbiota can regulate the occurrence of PCOS through the immune system, and various components within the immune system are expected to become novel biomarkers for PCOS. By transplanting gut microbiota into germ-free mice, representative bacterial strains that have effects on the immune system can be identified through changes in immune phenotypes and their transmission. This involves constructing a collection of clonal strains from the donor community, which can then be divided into subsets for validation and analysis to identify specific effect strains [123]. This provides a new perspective for studying the interactions among gut microbiota, the immune system, and PCOS. Moreover, immunomodulators, targeted immune cell therapies, and epigenetic regulation of immune cells hold significant potential for treating PCOS. Achieving precision medicine requires research into how specific beneficial bacteria, probiotics, and prebiotics can intervene in the immune system, thereby opening new avenues for PCOS treatment. In the future, the treatment of PCOS will undoubtedly result from a multidisciplinary integrated approach that combines endocrinology, immunology, and microbiology. By considering the interactions among multiple systems and employing combined therapeutic methods (such as hormonal regulation, metabolic control, gut microbiota modulation, and immunoregulation), it will be possible to comprehensively improve patients’ symptoms and provide greater benefits for those with PCOS. However, although substantial evidence indicates that modulation of the gut microbiota can significantly influence PCOS-related phenotypes, rendering microbial ecological regulation a promising new therapeutic direction. Clinical randomized controlled trials have already shown that probiotics, prebiotics, and synbiotics can improve insulin resistance, lipid profiles, and hormonal parameters with good tolerability, several critical barriers still prevent microbiota-based interventions from being incorporated into current PCOS management guidelines [124, 125].First, the existing level of evidence remains insufficient to support guideline-level recommendations. Most clinical trials involving probiotics or synbiotics are characterized by small sample sizes, short intervention durations, and limited population diversity, often conducted within a single region or ethnic group. Large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up are still lacking, making it difficult to establish the long-term efficacy and safety required for inclusion in evidence-based clinical guidelines. Second, microbiota-targeted interventions currently lack standardized protocols regarding dosage, strain composition, duration of administration, and delivery modality. Considerable methodological heterogeneity across studies hinders reproducibility, clinical translation, and the development of guideline-ready recommendations. Third, safety and regulatory concerns remain substantial obstacles, particularly for ecological-level interventions such as fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT). Issues such as regulatory classification, biosafety evaluation, donor screening criteria, and the unknown long-term consequences of FMT have not yet been fully resolved, further limiting its broader clinical adoption. Taken together, these limitations suggest that microbiota-based approaches are unlikely to become “standard therapies” in PCOS guidelines in the near term. Nevertheless, their potential as adjunctive treatments or components of individualized management strategies is increasingly evident. With the accumulation of high-quality clinical evidence, the establishment of standardized intervention frameworks, and the integration of microbial data into metabolic–immune phenotyping models, microbiota-based therapies may ultimately be incorporated into future PCOS management guidelines, particularly within comprehensive treatment programs that combine lifestyle interventions ( dietary modification, probiotic supplementation) with metabolic improvement strategies.

Conclusions

The pathogenesis of PCOS is extremely complex. In the past decade, the influence of gut microbiota on PCOS has gradually gained attention from researchers, particularly concerning its regulation of the immune system, which plays a crucial role in the development of PCOS. Gut microbiota and their metabolites can modulate the immune system through various mechanisms, thereby affecting the occurrence and progression of PCOS. This article provides an in-depth review of the processes and specific mechanisms by which gut microbiota and their metabolites influence PCOS via the immune system. It also discusses the challenges in the diagnosis and treatment of PCOS and proposes solutions to these challenges while outlining future research directions. These summaries provide a solid theoretical foundation for further understanding the complex etiology of PCOS and serve as an important reference for future clinical research and therapeutic strategies.
