Background {6a}

Stroke is characterized by high disability rates. Evidence indicates that [1] approximately 80% of stroke survivors worldwide develop functional impairments in extremities, with over half exhibiting persistent upper limb or hand dysfunction beyond 6 months post-onset. These deficits significantly compromise activities of daily living, making post-stroke upper limb rehabilitation a critical research priority in neurological recovery.

The mirror neuron system (MNS) was discovered by Rizzolatti et al. [2], the mirror network in the human brain involves the Broca area the ventral premotor cortex, the lower part of the central precentral gyrus (PreCG), the posterior part of the inferior frontal gyrus, the supramarginal gyrus, the insula, the amygdala, and the prefrontal cortex [3]. Many scholars have established treatment methods based on mirror neuron theory, including mirror therapy (MT), action observation therapy, motor imagery (MI), and GMI. GMI is a staged and progressive rehabilitation technique that combines MI and MT in a stepwise manner, which activates the cortex in stages and reduces cortical inhibition, promoting cortical reorganization and functional recovery [4]. Some studies suggest that GMI may be a sequential treatment approach that can enhance the sensorimotor cortex [5].

It is well known that the motor and sensory cortices of the brain are adjacent and closely connected. Research has confirmed that peripheral nerve stimulation in stroke patients can modulate the excitability of primary motor cortex (M1) through existing cortical-cortical connections between primary somatosensory cortex (S1) and M1 or through direct projections from thalamic nuclei [6]. Some scholars have found that in the rubber hand experiment, merely visually capturing a rubber hand placed in an anatomically consistent manner was sufficient to trigger visual-proprioceptive integration without synchronous brushing [7], indicating that sensory observation can induce visual-proprioceptive integration. Mikkel et al. [8] demonstrated that the input of visual signals may promote the activation of S1 and lead to increased excitability of M1, resulting in changes in brain plasticity. Studies have shown that sensory observation activates bilateral PreCG, left supplementary motor area, right postcentral gyrus (PoCG), left inferior parietal gyrus, and other regions, with greater clustering activation in PreCG than in PoCG, suggesting that SO may regulate the sensorimotor network by activating the MNS [9]. It has been found that mirror neurons are both motor neurons and sensory neurons [10]. While MNS-based therapies (e.g., MT, AOT) demonstrate efficacy in post-stroke upper limb rehabilitation, critical gaps remain: (1) Sequential Activation Uncertainty: Limited evidence exists on whether sequential activation of sensory mirror neurons followed by motor mirror neurons enhances functional recovery. (2) Clinical Implementation Barriers: Current protocols require minimal auditory interference and sustained high attentional engagement, severely constraining real-world applicability.

VR technology generates immersive environments that enable real-time bidirectional interaction between limb movements and multisensory feedback [11]. Research demonstrates VR-based stroke rehabilitation significantly enhances patients’ body ownership and limb position sense, with this perceptual enhancement directly improving motor functional outcomes [12]. Destaw B. et al. [13] used functional neuroimaging to confirm that VR-augmented upper limb therapy strengthens functional connectivity in motor-associated brain regions. This reorganization reflects critical cortical remodeling in lesioned areas, showing strong correlation with recovered motor function. Simultaneous EEG recordings revealed increased alpha-band power during VR training, indicating enhanced attentional focus and optimized cognitive resource allocation [14]. Critically, VR facilitates neurorehabilitation through dual mechanisms reinforced embodiment perception (body ownership/spatial localization) [12] and augmented attentional modulation [14].

Objective {7}

Hypothesis 1: Integrating SO components into GMI creates a novel therapeutic approach that strengthens functional coherence between somatosensory and motor cortices, potentially improving upper limb motor function recovery in post-stroke patients. Hypothesis 2: Compared to conventional mirror neuron therapies requiring extreme attentional focus and auditory-controlled environments, VR training boosts therapeutic efficacy by increasing engagement through multisensory immersion, optimizing attentional resource allocation and enabling simultaneous one-to-multiple training protocols.

This study aims to investigate the clinical efficacy and neurophysiological mechanisms of the proposed interventions, specifically targeting enhanced treatment response rates and clinical outcomes in stroke upper limb rehabilitation through the integration of SO and VR technologies with GMI. Concurrently, our methodology is designed to (1) establish a reduced-exertion intervention model by minimizing physical demands and (2) ensure comprehensive applicability across all upper limb dysfunction stages (Brunstrom stages I–VI). Furthermore, the implementation of a one-to-multiple training paradigm optimizes resource allocation, creating an efficient rehabilitation pathway.

Intervention description {11a}

All participants will receive standardized pharmacotherapy and evidence-based conventional rehabilitation comprising five core therapeutic modules: (1) therapeutic positioning strategies, (2) occupation-based interventions, (3)comprehensive hemiparesis management, (4) graded hand function training using task-specific approaches, and (5) physical agent modalities (e.g., neuromuscular electrical stimulation). Certified rehabilitation therapists with ≥3 years of specialized neurological experience will deliver twice-daily 40-min sessions, administered 5 days/week over 4 weeks. Rigorous pre-trial protocol standardization includes: (1) therapist competency certification through Objective Structured Clinical Examinations achieving ≥85% adherence scores, and (2) treatment fidelity monitoring throughout the intervention period.

Within an acoustically controlled environment (<35 dB ambient noise verified by Type 1 sound meter), therapists administer the three-stage protocol using standardized digital interfaces. Participants seated in ergonomic chairs view training materials on 11-inch backlit tablets at 60 cm viewing distance. The first stage implements Laterality Judgment (2 min), employs the image database (200 validated limb images at 0°/45°/90°/180° rotations), where therapists randomly present stimuli using computerized randomization algorithms, asking the patient to judge whether the unilateral limb in the image belongs to the left or right side. After completing the judgment of all images, the average response time of the patient for each image and the accuracy rate of identification are manually recorded. The second stage implements MI (9 min), defined as the cognitive simulation of action without physical execution. Utilizing multimodal stimuli (video demonstrations), therapists guide patients to mentally rehearse movements of their affected limb while observing presented content. This kinesthetic imagery phase specifically targets premotor cortex activation. The third stage employs MT (9 min), wherein patients perform actions with their unaffected limb while observing real-time mirror-reflected movements of both limbs displayed on calibrated screens. Concurrently, patients engage in synchronized motor imitation through this visual feedback loop, leveraging mirror neuron systems to stimulate bilateral cortical activation. GMI protocol is administered in 20-min daily sessions, 5 days weekly across a standardized 4-week intervention period.

The SO-GMI therapy represents a novel sensorimotor integration paradigm that incorporates SO components into Stage II of conventional GMI, thereby establishing a four-phase intervention protocol. SO entails the cognitive simulation of unimplemented sensory inputs, wherein patients attentively view videos depicting tactile brushing stimuli administered to various segments of their affected limb. The standardized protocol comprises Phase I) Laterality Judgment (2 min), Phase II) SO (6 min; viewing site-specific brushing sequences), Phase III) MI (6 min; kinesthetic rehearsal of observed movements), and Phase IV) MT (6 min; bilateral motor synchronization via mirrored feedback). Administered in 20-min sessions (excluding 2-min inter-phase transitions) 5 weekdays weekly across a 4-week intervention period, this regimen delivers precisely timed neuroplasticity-targeted exposure.

VR-based rehabilitation interventions will be administered in acoustically controlled environments (<35 dB ambient noise), with participants seated in adjustable ergonomic chairs maintaining 30° elbow flexion. Workstation height will be calibrated to position the interactive surface at umbilical level to optimize upper limb range of motion. A 32-inch 4 K display (3840 × 2160 resolution, 60 cm height-adjusted) will be positioned at 1.5 m (56° horizontal viewing angle), supplemented by the Percro HIM VR headset (Beijing Noitom Technology Ltd.). Certified occupational or physical therapists holding specialized neurorehabilitation certification will guide participants through protocol execution. Using this immersive platform, subjects will be alternatively assigned to perform either GMI or SO-GMI protocols. Sessions will be conducted in 20-min daily increments, 5 days weekly over a standardized 4-week intervention period.

Outcomes {12}

Primary outcome

FMA-UE was employed to assess upper limb motor function, quantifying motor control, coordination, and reflex activity following stroke. This scale is a strongly recommended [15] and validated [2] outcome measure for evaluating post-stroke motor impairment in both clinical and research contexts [16]. The assessment comprises 33 standardized tasks, each scored on a 3-point ordinal scale: 0 (cannot perform), 1 (partially performs), and 2 (fully performs). The total score ranges from 0 to 66, with higher scores indicating better motor function. All assessments were administered by trained therapists or raters adhering to standardized procedures.

FMA-UE scores (presented as mean ± standard deviation) were compared within each group at three time points: baseline, 2 weeks, and 4 weeks of treatment. The primary endpoint of the study was the score at 4 weeks. The mean difference in score changes from baseline to 4 weeks was compared between the two groups to evaluate the intervention effect.

Secondary outcome measures

The change in outcome value for each measure was calculated. For scale-based measures (ARAT, MBI), within-group results are presented as mean ± standard deviation at baseline, 2 weeks, and 4 weeks. The between-group difference in the change from baseline to the 4-week time point was compared for each outcome to determine the intervention’s effect.

Sample size {14}

The FMA-UE served as the primary efficacy endpoint, with anticipated scores derived from prior clinical evidence [12] indicating a mean of 48.82 ± 8.32 for the VR training group versus 43.47 ± 6.65 for the control group. Using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (v26.0; IBM Corp) with α = 0.05 and power = 0.8, the initial sample size calculation yielded 24 participants (12 per arm). Accounting for a conservative 20% attrition rate based on rehabilitation trial benchmarks, the final required sample size was determined as 15 participants per group (four groups), resulting in a total sample size of 60.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

Randomization will be performed using computer-generated random numbers created by SPSS software (v26.0; IBM Corp). Immediately following participant informed consent, an independent investigator with no prior patient contact will assign each subject a sequentially numbered code corresponding to the predetermined intervention allocation via concealed implementation. This methodology ensures allocation concealment throughout the trial.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

A designated personnel uninvolved in clinical care will exclusively manage the assignment of specific random numbers to participants. This individual performs no other trial-related roles beyond number allocation, ensuring the randomization process occurs without involvement or awareness by other parties.

Implementation {16c}

Upon confirmation of participant eligibility, unique identifiers will be assigned based on stroke subtype (ischemic/hemorrhagic) and time since onset. Blinded assessors will conduct longitudinal assessments at all predefined timepoints, with evaluation results sealed and secured until database lock. Treating therapists will deliver protocol-specific interventions strictly per assigned allocation groups.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

Due to therapeutic limitations that preclude double-blinding. An independent researcher unaffiliated with patient assessments oversees blinding and randomization procedures. Outcome assessors and statisticians remain blinded throughout the trial. Two experienced therapists administer group-specific interventions in physically separate treatment zones to prevent interaction; these therapists shall not disclose allocation details to patients, assessors, or statistical personnel. The blinded independent researcher conducts data management and final statistical analysis. Blinded assessors perform evaluations in a standardized quiet environment using only patient identifiers (name, age, randomized ID).

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

Clinical emergencies requiring immediate intervention (e.g., seizure, acute cerebrovascular event) necessitate unblinding to guide critical care management. When assessors suspect compromised blinding due to protocol deviations (e.g., accidental disclosure by patients), verification and documentation must occur. For participants withdrawing from the study, attending physicians receive full disclosure of prior intervention types to facilitate continuity of standard rehabilitation therapy. Unblinding is mandated in these predefined circumstances per trial safety protocols.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

Primary efficacy analysis will employ intention-to-treat linear mixed-effects models with repeated measures (baseline, 4-week, 12-week). Fugl-Meyer Assessment Upper Extremity (FMA-UE) scores serve as the primary endpoint, analyzed via analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) adjusting for baseline scores and stroke type. Between-group comparisons will use Tukey’s HSD with Bonferroni correction (α = 0.0125). Secondary outcomes (WMFT, MAL, fMRI connectivity) will undergo multivariate ANOVA. Subgroup analyses will examine Brunnstrom Stage I-III patients using moderation models. Effect sizes will be reported as partial eta-squared (η < sup > 2 </sup > < sub > p </sub >) with 95% confidence intervals. Sample size (n = 15/group) provides 80% power (β = 0.2) to detect moderate effects (f = 0.25, α = 0.05) in G-Power 3.1. Missing data will be handled via multiple imputation chained equations. Statistical significance is defined as p < 0.05 (two-tailed) using SPSS (v26.0; IBM Corp).

In the rs-fMRI analysis, laterality indices (LI) for both the bilateral M1 and bilateral S1 were calculated. The LI was computed according to the following formulae [19]:

LI-M1=(Number of activated voxels in the ipsilesional M1-Number of activated voxels in the contralesional M1)/(Number of activated voxels in the ipsilesional M1+Number of activated voxels in the contralesional M1).

LI-S1=(Number of activated voxels in the ipsilesional S1-Number of activated voxels in the contralesional S1)/(Number of activated voxels in the ipsilesional S1+Number of activated voxels in the contralesional S1) [20].

Consequently, the LI value ranges from −1 to 1. An LI of −1 indicates exclusive connectivity in the contralesional hemisphere (relative to the lesion), an LI of 1 indicates exclusive connectivity in the ipsilesional hemisphere, and an LI of 0 indicates perfectly symmetrical bilateral connectivity. Thus, the LI serves as a quantitative metric to assess the symmetry of functional connectivity patterns.

Discussion

GMI relies on action observation to activate the MNS [2], utilizing visual input to simulate movement intention and induce neuroplastic reorganization in the premotor cortex and M1 [16]. Observation (or imagination) of grasping actions enhances corticospinal excitability targeting hand muscles through activation of motor cortical networks [17]. This process essentially constitutes “offline encoding” of motor information—where the brain encodes identical movement patterns without physical execution—establishing a neuromotor template for relearning movements [18]. Post-stroke somatosensory impairments frequently disrupt motor planning [20]. The MNS responds not only to actions themselves but also to associated sensory consequences. fMRI evidence demonstrates that observing tactile stimuli activates neural networks overlapping with actual touch and motor preparation—including the primary (SI) and secondary somatosensory cortices (SII),and premotor cortex (BA6)—forming “sensorimotor shared representations” [19, 20]. During observation of touch events (e.g., object-hand/object-object interactions), SII activates selectively through automated simulation via shared neural circuitry [21]. This activation results from anticipatory encoding of goal-directed actions [22], where motor goals (e.g., stable grasping) integrate tactile properties of objects [23]. fMRI reveals enhanced functional connectivity between the dorsal premotor cortex (dPMC) and SII when observing goal-relevant sensory input [24], suggesting sensory information optimizes motor command generation. Consequently,our experimental design employs VR to deliver ecologically valid SO stimuli (e.g., visualizing icy object contact), which may drive cross-modal reorganization in somatosensory-motor integration cortices. We propose augmenting GMI therapy with SO to enhance MNS “observation-execution matching” pathways. Post-training fMRI will evaluate corresponding cortical activation patterns and connectivity changes.

Studies have demonstrated that observing videos of hand-object touch activates the primary somatosensory cortex (SI) with specificity to content [25], eliciting neural activity resembling actual tactile processing [26]. Research further indicates [27] that early enhancement of somatosensory evoked potentials (SEPs)—whether after the 30th or 60th trial—shows statistically significant positive correlations (correlation coefficient r > 0.4, corrected p < 0.05) with later behavioral improvements. Subjects exhibiting greater early increases in SI excitability subsequently demonstrated more substantial overall behavioral gains. Changes in SI excitability serve as a critical biomarker during early human motor learning,preceding alterations in motor cortex (M1) activity temporally and predicting subsequent learning efficacy. Using transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) to measure motor-evoked potentials, SI has been shown to modulate corticospinal output (i.e., increased MEP amplitude) via corticocortical projections influencing M1 [28]. Accordingly, our experimental design prioritizes SO before graded motor imagery training, aiming to leverage early sensory cortex activation to amplify the efficacy of subsequent motor imagery and mirror therapy. We will employ resting-state fMRI to validate the hypothesized sensory-motor cortical connectivity and determine whether modified immersive virtual reality training further enhances clinical outcomes and promotes functional brain recovery.

A limitation of this study is its inclusion of SO therapy within GMI without distinct subgroup analyses differentiating patients with versus without somatosensory impairments. Sensory function baselines were not formally assessed prior to intervention. Future research should stratify participants by pre-intervention sensory status and implement protocol-adapted SO paradigms. This approach will determine whether the combined intervention differentially promotes sensory recovery in stroke survivors with somatosensory deficits and clarify its mechanistic role in neural reorganization.
