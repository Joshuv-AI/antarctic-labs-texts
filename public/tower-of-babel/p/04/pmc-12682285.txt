SEAMAN: A climate odyssey through synthesized earth monthly anomalies
Authors: Simone Bregaglio, Maria Roberta Bruno, Roberta Calone, Giulia Carriero, Eleonora Del Cavallo, Giovanni Maria Poggi, Eugenio Rossi, Natan Sinigaglia, Sofia Bajocco
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryEffectively communicating the facts of climate change is essential for raising awareness and inspiring meaningful responses. However, the complexity of climate data and analytic methods is often inaccessible to the public. The arts and humanities offer alternative approaches to empower communication by enriching scientific representation. In this context, SEAMAN (Synthesized EArth Monthly Anomalies) is a software translating NASA-POWER climate data (1984–2024) into a nocturnal seascape digital imagery. Moving sea waves reflect temperature trends through their variability in color, movement, and sound, the moon’s size and position visualize precipitation rates, and the stars represent aridity patterns. As a unified tableau, these elements form a dynamic metaphor of climate anomalies, generating a unique and constantly changing digital canvas. Users can explore climate trends, download data, and introduce changes in precipitation and temperature. SEAMAN aims to help educators, researchers, and decision-makers engage with diverse audiences and communicate the urgency of collective climate action.

Introduction

Traditional methods of presenting scientific data are often limited in their efficacy to convey messages to non-specialist audiences.1,2 The consequent communication gap has significant implications for climate change policy,3 as it can lead to skepticism, disengagement, or even resistance to the intended messages,4 undermining collective efforts to reduce global warming.5,6 Scientists are searching for alternative means of representation that diverge from the social, cultural, and epistemological paradigms that have shaped dominant narratives about climate change.7 Digital technologies, the humanities, and the arts are increasingly incorporated into climate communication to better engage with lay audiences.8,9 Pedagogical videogames10 and other artistic initiatives inspired by the climate crisis11 share the mission to overcome the sender-receiver communication model, which considers the public as a passive recipient of information.12

Open-access, global datasets such as those of the NASA-POWER and Copernicus hubs are valuable resources for understanding climate change, yet meaningful public access to this information remains limited.13,14 While these platforms provide essential climate data for scientific research, they are challenging for non-experts to engage with and require significant technical knowledge for data manipulation, analysis, and interpretation.15,16 To bridge this communication gap, it is therefore crucial to develop new formats for elaborating these datasets that resonate with the broader public.17,18 In this context, new trends have emerged that promote creative and engaging approaches to environmental data use. Notably, international geoscience app competitions, such as the NASA International Space Apps Challenge, as well as hackathons organized by universities and private companies (e.g., UT Austin, University of Calgary, and CloudFerro Geospatial Innovation Challenge) have catalyzed the development of tools that combine scientific data with compelling visual narratives, interactive design, and user-centric functionality. These initiatives emphasize open access, reproducibility, rapid prototyping, and public engagement, and reflect a broader shift toward democratizing climate information through accessible interfaces and immersive formats.

Research has shown that aesthetically compelling messaging can influence risk perception and reinforce public engagement, but pessimistic or fear-based framing may also lead to anxiety or inaction when it is not accompanied by hopeful narratives or a sense of agency.19,20 Consequently, the role of visual and auditory artworks in fostering emotional engagement and constructive dialogue around the climate crisis is increasingly recognized.21 While integrating creativity with scientific rigor can produce meaningful metaphors to make complex data resonate emotionally,22 there are also inherent risks of misrepresentation. The overdramatization of future climate scenarios, for example, may cause either undue alarm or skepticism about the underlying science.23 Likewise, oversimplifying a message can strip away essential nuances, leading to misunderstandings of climate change complexity and uncertainty.24 Therefore, while creativity and aesthetic presentation strategies can translate climate data into relatable narratives for the public, it is equally important to ensure scientific accuracy and to frame messages that inspire hope and agency.19,25

We developed SEAMAN (Synthesized EArth Monthly ANomalies), a software that allows users to navigate the Earth’s climate across space and time. SEAMAN maps global, monthly climate data onto a digital canvas, in which a nocturnal seascape is immersed in a synthesized auditory environment. Besides providing standard climate classification and basic chart visualizations, this tool translates temperature and precipitation anomalies into interactive symbols accompanied by poetry generated with an open-source large language model. We propose SEAMAN to elaborate original climate data through simple metaphorical and imaginative elements, with the ultimate purpose of illuminating unprecedented climate trends.

Results

User engagement and communicative impact

SEAMAN’s approach to climate visualization and interaction is designed to engage a broad range of users. In educational settings, it provides young generations with a hands-on, exploratory tool to illustrate regional climate change impacts. By enabling students to both visualize and hear the variability of climate anomalies, SEAMAN supports more meaningful and lasting learning experiences. Educators can use the software to contextualize regional climate trends and stimulate discussion around adaptation and mitigation. For science communicators and cultural institutions, SEAMAN offers an alternative way to raise public awareness, transforming climate data into emotionally resonant symbols that make the crisis more relatable and urgent. Although not intended as a substitute for technical climate analysis, researchers may benefit from SEAMAN’s intuitive interface as a complementary means of exploring year-to-year anomalies, comparing diverse climatic zones, and accessing NASA-POWER data in a visual and downloadable format. At the same time, the project has a clear scope and limits: SEAMAN emphasizes the experiential understanding of medium-term anomalies rather than long-term trend analysis or policy guidance. By embedding scientific data in a metaphorical and interactive environment, it complements existing climate communication strategies and helps bridge the gap between technical information and public perception. As users navigate floating stars and the moon over dynamic sea waves, they encounter both constancy and flux in the natural world, deepening their understanding of the climate trends that define the Anthropocene (Video S1).

Comparative climate variability: Extreme cold and hot years in Rome

SEAMAN classifies Rome within the Köppen-Geiger climate class “Csa,” typifying a Mediterranean climate with mild winters and hot summers. NASA-POWER data from 1984 to 2024 showed that the average annual temperature was 16°C and cumulative precipitation was 684 mm. These values are displayed near the sea and moon icons in the Navigation Pane (Figures 1A and 1C). The sea poem generated by the language model reflects this situation: “Roma Capitale, 1984/Foggy dawn, with chill in the air./A year that shunned the summer’s fire/Cool and wet, with no desire./Summer’s warmth, a distant hum,/Rain-soaked streets, a melancholy drum./The sky swept tears, in endless pour/A marked departure, from the norm score.” The Representation Pane visually and sonically echoes these climatic conditions (Figure 1B). The sea surface, composed mainly of white and light blue waves, languidly flows from right to left, symbolizing colder-than-average temperatures. November and December stand out with deeper blue waves that shift to orange-red near the top of the digital sea, reflecting close-to-normal temperatures (maximum and minimum temperatures of 17.3°C and 9.3°C in November, and 13°C and 4.9°C in December, respectively; Figure 1A). Monthly histograms in the Navigation Pane further confirm this pattern, contrasting slightly less in these months. The SEAMAN stars form an almost regular arc in the sky, with the aridity indicator increasing from January to July and then decreasing until December. Spikes range from 5 in February and December to 11 in June and July. Regular contours around the stars symbolize the absence of temperature anomalies, while large halos indicate positive precipitation anomalies, especially in February (111 mm), May (74 mm), August (58 mm), and September (127 mm). The large, greenish, high moon emphasizes substantial precipitation, with greener rings marking months with significant rainfall surpluses. The minimalistic soundscape features a slow kick drum at 86 beats per minute (bpm) and a soft, atmospheric synth with no discernible melody. The background remains dark, with only a few stars creating a cold ambiance, consistent with the climate data. In stark contrast, the Navigation Pane portrays an extraordinary shift in Rome’s climate in 2022, one of the hottest and driest years on record (Figure 1C). Maximum and minimum temperatures reached the 82nd and 73rd percentiles, respectively, with precipitation significantly below the historical average (37th percentile. Figure 1C). The sea poem reads: “Roma Capitale, 2022/a sweltering summer, sunbeams shine through./Temps soared high, 35° and more/Drought-stricken days, with nary a shore./Rainfall scarce, 520 mm did fall/A far cry from norms, hear the desert’s call./Summer’s warmth lingers, winter’s chill delay,/A year of contrasts, in Rome’s own way.” The Representation Pane (Figure 3D) transforms accordingly, with tempestuous orange and red waves symbolizing above-average temperatures, especially in the middle, corresponding to summer months. Monthly histograms confirm this trend (Figure 1C), with lighter colors peaking in July, when extreme conditions were evident (33.4°C and 20.3°C for maximum and minimum temperatures, respectively). SEAMAN’s constellation forms an uneven arc in 2022, with stars from January to April positioned higher than in 1984, indicating a greater evapotranspiration deficit. These stars show distorted contours and more spikes, corresponding to negative temperature anomalies, especially from May to July. Stars do not have halos, reflecting low precipitation, except in August (28 mm) and September (103 mm), where rainfall exceeded the norms. In 2022, the moon appears smaller and lower on the canvas, with faint, green-to-pink concentric rings, signaling a severe lack of precipitation. The sky is a lighter blue, with more white stars, indicating temperatures in the highest percentiles. The soundscape intensifies accordingly, with a faster drum beat at 122 bpm and layered, distorted synths, adding a sense of urgency and tension to the dramatic climate shift in Rome.

In autumn and winter, sea waves are dense and icy, moving slowly from right to left, symbolizing lower-than-average temperatures during these seasons. From the late 1990s to 2009 (average maximum and minimum temperatures of 26°C and 14.5°C, and annual precipitation at 240 mm), however, the Representation Pane shows a shift toward faster and ample blue-to-orange hues, with white waves nearly disappearing, signaling increased minimum and maximum temperatures. The sky visually strengthens this warming shift, with white stars appearing brighter and more dense, especially in 1998, 1999, and 2008. During this period, alternating years of exceptionally high (1986, 1991, 1994, 1997) and low (1984, 1989, 1993, 1995, 1998, 1999) precipitation rates indicate increased year-to-year fluctuations. SEAMAN’s monthly stars show little variation in these years, reflecting the region’s consistently arid environment, with the aridity indicator hovering around 1 for most months. In the winter months, however, when most of the region’s rainfall occurs, the stars are surrounded by ample halos, indicating brief periods of high precipitation. This subtle contrast emphasizes the area’s overall dryness, interrupted by seasonal rainfall.

A more pronounced warming trend emerges in 2010-24 (average maximum and minimum temperature of 26.3°C and 15.1°C, and annual precipitation at 417 mm). The SEAMAN seascape becomes dominated by intense orange waves, representing increasing temperature anomalies. The waves move faster and are larger in amplitude, indicating a rise in minimum and maximum temperatures. The digital canvases in 2010, 2016, 2021, 2023, and 2024 are particularly striking, with much lighter blue skies and almost entirely orange waves, reflecting the increased frequency of exceptionally hot years. Over the last 15 years, the moon has shown greater year-to-year variations and generally increased precipitation rates, with 2015, 2018, and 2020-24 featuring exceptionally large, green moons, positioned high in the sky, signaling substantial increases in annual rainfall. The SEAMAN soundscapes evolve alongside these climate changes. Early years are characterized by slow rhythms and simple synth backgrounds. As heat intensifies, the soundscape becomes more intense, layered, and distorted, with faster electronic drums and articulated melodic loops to symbolize the increased climatic instability. Palestine’s climate data from 1984 to 2024 clearly demonstrates a trend toward hotter, more volatile conditions. When combined with the region’s political challenges, these findings underscore the pressing need for sustainable climate solutions to address both environmental and humanitarian crises.

Demonstration and audience feedback

To evaluate the communicative potential and user engagement of SEAMAN, we conducted two demonstration sessions with distinct audiences. The first demonstration took place in the Master’s course Monitoring and Management of the Agroecosystem (MSc in Agricultural Sciences and Technologies, University of Florence), where 30 students attended a 30-min lecture introducing SEAMAN’s conceptual background, technical structure, and artistic approach. Following the presentation, students participated in a live demonstration of the tool and were invited to ask questions, particularly regarding the selection of locations and interpretative choices. At the end of the session, they completed a digital survey assessing their first impressions, emotional responses, and perceptions of SEAMAN’s effectiveness as a communication tool. Out of 24 survey responses, the majority described the tool’s design as “creative” (16), followed by “intuitive” (5), with only 2 participants finding it “confusing” and 1 describing it as “technical.” Emotional reactions were largely positive: 11 respondents reported feeling “inspired”, 10 “surprised”, 6 “engaged”, and others noted feelings of being “thoughtful” (4), “hopeful” (3), or “worried” (7). Only 2 participants felt “upset” and none were “indifferent”. Participants rated the clarity of SEAMAN’s climate message (via visuals, sound, and poetic text) at 4.1/5, ease of interaction at 4.0/5, and its overall communicative effectiveness compared to more traditional materials (e.g., articles, graphs) at 4.3/5 (Table 1). The second evaluation took place at the European Geosciences Union (EGU) General Assembly in Vienna, during a dedicated session on art and science. SEAMAN was presented in an 8-min talk to a multidisciplinary audience of researchers, artists, and communicators. The discussion that followed focused on the tool’s metaphorical language and multisensory interface as a means of representing complex data. Discussion

SEAMAN presents the year-to-year variations in global, monthly temperature and precipitation anomalies, defined as deviations from the climatic norm, according to NASA-POWER gridded data at 0.5° resolution (1984–2024), a globally available gridded climate product developed primarily for agroclimatic applications. This dataset was chosen because it combines global coverage with moderate spatial resolution (∼0.5 ° × 0.5 °), which is adequate for visualizing regional climate anomalies, and provides an exceptionally streamlined API that enables fast, reliable, and user-friendly access. By contrast, alternatives such as ERA5 offer finer spatial detail (∼0.1°) but require more complex access procedures, longer retrieval times, and queuing systems, which are less suitable for the interactive and publicly accessible framework developed here. The monthly scale has often been used to analyze climate series, as it captures short-term variability and facilitates climate patterns identification.28,29 SEAMAN uses the Z-score approach30 to compute monthly climate anomalies as percentiles with respect to the reference series. Values above the 50th percentile indicate warmer or wetter conditions than the long-term mean, and vice versa.31,32 Analogously, aridity patterns have been analyzed at a monthly scale, as done by widely used indices such as the De Martonne index,33 the Aridity Index34 and the Standardized Precipitation-Evapotranspiration Index.35 Unlike these metrics, SEAMAN relies on a synthetic aridity indicator ranging from −1 to 1, computed as the ratio of the difference between monthly precipitation and reference evapotranspiration (ET0) to their sum.36 This index enables comparing monthly aridity trends across diverse locations regardless of climate zones, making it particularly effective for capturing variations in humid and arid environments. Using the Hargreaves formula37 to estimate ET0 enables real-time applications due to computation simplicity and minimal input requirements,38 despite being less accurate than the standard FAO-56 Penman–Monteith method.39

The distinctive trait of SEAMAN, however, lies neither in the climate data used nor in its analysis, but in the modes in which these datasets are made accessible, presented, and translated into visual and auditory metaphors. SEAMAN streamlines access to NASA-POWER data, which are retrieved by a basic mapping tool from any location with delays of no more than 12 s, with a standard internet connection. The first graphical interface (the Navigation Pane) geo-codes the location and describes its climate according to Köppen and Geiger,40 the still most widely used classification nearly a century after its creation.41,42 The Pane is complemented by monthly histograms displaying temperature and precipitation norms, overlayed by point markers with light and dark contrasts, highlighting monthly anomalies for a selected year. The only non-scientific element in the Navigation Pane is the sea poem generated by an open-source language model, a tool increasingly used to synthesize knowledge in scientific contexts43 and to create poetry in computational creative tasks.44

While the Navigation Pane adheres to scientific information, the second component of the graphical user interface, the Representation Pane, introduces a metaphoric dimension, in which the same climate data are transformed into visual and auditory expressions. This process aims at translating “not intrinsically photogenic numbers”45 into evocative narratives, fostering an emotional connection to climate anomalies. The choice of a nocturnal seascape as the core visual metaphor is deeply rooted in literary, artistic, and symbolic traditions. Across cultures and epochs, the sea has served as a symbol of mystery, self-reflection, and exploration.46 From Homer’s Odyssey, where the sea is a pathway of peril and revelation, through Romantic poets, who used it to mirror the depths of human emotions, to Impressionists, who depicted its ever-shifting rhythms as a proxy of the flux of nature, the sea has carried a durable iconographic meaning. This imagery also resonates within psychoanalytic and religious frameworks, where the sea evokes both the unconscious and spiritual transcendence.47 In climate change communication, the sea is a particularly prominent tòpos conveying a dual message. On one hand, it is the natural element most impacted by global warming effects such as ice-melting, sea-level rise, marine heatwaves, coral bleaching, and ocean acidification.48 On the other, the sea symbolizes resilience and regeneration, reminding both the vastness of planetary systems and the potential for adaptive responses.7,49 The sea also symbolizes the perilous journey of climate migrants, who risk treacherous waters in search of safety, with the sea serving both as a threat and a symbol of hope, offering the promise of survival and a new beginning.50,51 Within this conceptual framework, SEAMAN encodes monthly temperature anomalies through floating waves whose color and speed mimic their magnitude. Reddish, fast-moving, turbulent waves signal stronger positive anomalies, i.e., months that are significantly warmer than the long-term norm. Conversely, slow or still blue waves depict colder-than-average months. The color scheme is conceptually aligned with climate change artworks such as the Warming Stripes,52 which use a progression from blue to red to depict rising temperatures in a direct and intuitive way. Recent work has shown how this visual metaphor can be extended beyond surface air temperatures to reveal warming in the upper ocean and atmosphere and further adapted into “Warning Stripes” that project possible future trajectories under different emission scenarios.53 This reinforces the communicative strength of the blue-to-red gradient in conveying both the observed progression of warming and the urgency of future risks, while SEAMAN adapts this palette by associating white with cold extremes as the icy color, blue with median conditions, and orange-red with hot anomalies. The twelve-monthly stars in SEAMAN’s seascapes draw a constellation tied to the annual aridity pattern of a specific location. Drawing on the historical role of constellations in maritime navigation,54 users can observe the stars’ positions, shapes, and colors to interpret intra-annual climate variability. Scientifically, each star’s color corresponds to the synthetic aridity indicator, which ranges from −1 (indicating a substantial surplus of precipitation relative to evapotranspiration) to 1 (indicating a pronounced evapotranspiration deficit). Their vertical positioning visually encodes this gradient: drier months are plotted higher in the sky, while wetter months appear closer to the sea level. Users can interact with the stars by playing them via keyboard input, triggering associated sound cues that reflect the underlying data. This multisensory interaction enables a representation that is both scientifically grounded and metaphorically resonant, bridging quantitative precision with experiential engagement.

Additional meteorological information is embedded in the stars’ form. A soft, diffuse halo surrounding each star represents the percentile rank of monthly precipitation, with brighter and broader halos corresponding to wetter conditions. This design loosely echoes the expressive luminosity of Van Gogh’s Starry Night, where celestial forms seem to pulse and swirl with emotional intensity.55 Distorted contours depict temperature anomalies, and the stars visually shift over time, echoing David Bowie’s Space Oddity (“the stars look very different today”). These deviations symbolize extreme departures from climatic norms, with irregular outlines intended to evoke a sense of instability and disruption. This metaphorical deformation highlights the unpredictability of changing climate patterns in a way that may elicit emotional engagement. The moon has an equally crucial role in the visual landscape, portraying the precipitation regime. Concentric rings reflect each month’s share of the total annual precipitation, producing a visual summary of seasonal rainfall distribution. Scientifically, this choice draws on empirical studies that associate lunar gravitational forces with variations in precipitation patterns and rates,56,57,58 as well as by the cultural association of the moon with tides and water cycles.59 A large, green moon, suggestive of vegetation flourishing on the lunar surface,60 floats high in the sky to signal the prevalence of positive monthly precipitation anomalies. In contrast, a low-hanging, small, pink moon subtly echoes Nick Drake’s Pink Moon, evoking the arid beauty of barren landscapes and symbolizing dry conditions and precipitation deficits. By integrating climate data with evocative symbols such as the dark sky that brightens with temperature anomalies and fills with small white stars,7 SEAMAN seeks to represent climatic variations and trends with both scientific accuracy and interpretive depth. Temperature anomalies are mapped across multiple modalities: the color and speed of sea waves, the brightness of the sky, and changes in the auditory environment, including increased beat tempo and sound distortion. While we recognize that assigning more than one representation to a single variable may initially challenge interpretation, this design choice reflects the centrality of temperature in climate discourse. Reinforcing the same signal through different visual and auditory cues enhances perceptual salience, emotional resonance, and intuitive comprehension, especially for audiences less familiar with traditional scientific graphs.

Inherent differences between regional climates are presented in a unique but ever evolving seascape which introduces another symbolic layer to the digital representation, i.e., egalitarianism in the face of climate change.61 This visual metaphor reflects the notion that all humans are navigating the same planetary climate, despite the large regional variability and extreme scales of climate conditions.62 In this regard, the sea, often regarded as a shared, universal symbol, joins the regions it separates.63 It has no country and belongs to everyone,64 erasing distinctions between individuals and serving as a powerful reminder that humanity shares the same existential challenge in facing the climate crisis. The interaction layer is equally important in SEAMAN as it positions users as active participants, fostering the importance of individual actions to mitigate or exacerbate global warming, and the consequent responsibility and awareness.

Users can navigate through time and space from the Navigation Pane and simulate changes in temperature and precipitation, with their effects rendered dynamically in the Representation Pane. For instance, users can observe and listen to the simulated impact of a +1.5°C global temperature rise, a widely recognized threshold identified by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change.62 This interaction is intentionally tied to recent years, allowing users to intuitively grasp how moderate warming levels could have affected places and seasons they may personally recall. The capacity to visualize the effects of climate change, even in a metaphoric representation, imparts a tangible sense of engagement, as do popular video games (Future Delta65) and card games (Carbon City Zero66). Sonification enhances the user’s sensory experience and emotional connection to the data and has been shown to be effective for conveying scientific information in an intuitive and impactful way, particularly for non-expert audiences.67,68 In SEAMAN, changes in temperature and precipitation are accompanied by corresponding shifts and sound distortion, creating an auditory landscape that reflects the intensity and nature of climate anomalies. This multisensory approach reinforces an emotional and intellectual connection to the data, making the climate message more visceral and immediate.69 While SEAMAN is primarily designed for non-specialist audiences such as students and educators, it can also serve researchers as a complementary tool for exploratory visualization and science communication, rather than as a substitute for technical analysis. As climate communication increasingly seeks ways to engage diverse audiences, SEAMAN’s combination of interactivity, visual metaphors, and sonification offers an innovative platform, fostering a deeper understanding of global climate trends. Although SEAMAN has so far only been demonstrated in a few group settings (i.e., a classroom environment and a scientific conference) these occasions revealed its potential for collective engagement and dialogue. In this context, the Visual Thinking Strategies (VTS) framework (https://vtshome.org) offers a relevant model for fostering shared interpretation and communication around visual artifacts. Exploring such structured, group-based approaches could meaningfully extend SEAMAN’s impact in science communication. While our approach is aimed at “non-specialist audiences”, we recognize that this category includes individuals with diverse backgrounds differing in age, educational level, cultural context, and personal experience, all of which shape how climate content is perceived. Artistic and metaphorical visualizations can evoke a range of interpretations, and their reception may vary widely across demographic groups. Acknowledging this variability is essential to refining SEAMAN’s communicative strategies and adapting future iterations to better suit specific educational and cultural contexts.

Limitations of the study

While SEAMAN introduces an alternative approach to climate communication through multi-sensory and artistic interaction, several limitations should be acknowledged. These concerns both the tool’s current implementation and the underlying climate data on which it relies. First, SEAMAN’s accessibility is constrained by its technical architecture. Built with the vvvv visual programming environment, it is currently available only as a Windows executable and distributed via GitHub. Although the source code is open-source and free to use, this delivery format may present usability barriers for non-technical audiences, such as educators or decision-makers unfamiliar with GitHub or lacking access to compatible systems. To address this, we have deposited a stable release on Zenodo70 and included a demonstration video, screenshots, and step-by-step guidance to support onboarding for non-specialists. We also propose workarounds for MacOS users (e.g., virtual machines) and are exploring web-native alternatives (e.g., p5.js or Three.js) that would enable browser-based interaction and broader accessibility in future developments. Second, the complexity of SEAMAN’s visual and auditory metaphors may raise questions about communicative clarity when compared to minimalist formats such as the widely adopted warming stripes.52 While the climate stripes convey long-term warming trends with immediate visual impact, SEAMAN seeks to complement this approach with a more exploratory, immersive experience. Accordingly, SEAMAN is primarily intended for educational and outreach contexts such as university classrooms, secondary and primary school activities, and public exhibitions, where guided use can enhance understanding, encourage dialogue, and foster meaningful engagement with climate data. The tool also retains utility for researchers interested in spatiotemporal patterns of climate anomalies, offering both intuitive visualization and direct access to NASA-POWER data for further analysis. At the same time, it is important to acknowledge limitations in the underlying dataset. SEAMAN is built on NASA-POWER, a globally available gridded product developed for agroclimatic applications (NASA-Power: https://power.larc.nasa.gov/). Its moderate spatial resolution (∼0.5 ° × 0.5 °) allows for consistent global coverage and interoperability but may not capture fine-scale variability, particularly in heterogeneous landscapes or regions with complex topography. Comparisons with higher-resolution datasets such as ERA5 generally show good agreement for broader patterns but reveal local discrepancies, especially during extreme events.71 While ERA5 could theoretically serve as an alternative input, its more complex access procedures and slower data retrieval make it less practical for an interactive communication tool. For the purposes of experiential climate engagement, NASA-POWER therefore represents a pragmatic balance between resolution, accessibility, and usability. The dataset used in SEAMAN spans the years 1984–2024, and we acknowledge that this relatively short temporal frame may limit interpretations of long-term climate variability. However, the 40-year period analyzed aligns with the World Meteorological Organization’s standard climatological baselines, typically defined in 30-year intervals. Our observations of year-to-year variations, particularly in the post-2015 period, are consistent with existing literature identifying this time frame as one marked by intensified regional climate signals and internal variability.72 In this context, SEAMAN is not intended to analyze long-term climate trends but rather to explore medium-term fluctuations and anomaly patterns that characterize recent decades. However, SEAMAN’s intended use is not predictive or operational but communicative; thus, the emphasis is on accessibility and consistency rather than fine-grained precision. A further limitation concerns the simplified way in which users can manually adjust annual temperature and precipitation series. These modifications are applied as uniform monthly correction factors, enabling users to explore the sensitivity of the representation pane to these hypothetical changes. However, such adjustments can generate scenarios that are not physically plausible: for example, a +1.5°C increase in temperature combined with a 200 mm decrease in precipitation may not align with projections from regional or global climate models. This practice is analogous to the incremental or synthetic scenarios described by the IPCC, in which climate variables are altered by plausible but essentially arbitrary amounts for sensitivity testing, rather than to represent realistic trajectories derived from coupled models.73 While useful for illustrative purposes, such simplifications ignore the coupled dynamics and uncertainty ranges that characterize the global climate system.62 The intention of this functionality is therefore not to provide scientifically realistic projections, but to offer an intuitive and experiential means for non-specialists to engage with “what-if” explorations. Integrating model-based scenarios would be a valuable direction for future work, though it lies beyond the scope of the present implementation. Finally, while initial surveys conducted during two public demonstrations suggest positive engagement and usability, further systematic evaluation is warranted. Planned extensions include additional user testing with diverse audiences and a possible transition toward online deployment, which would enable broader dissemination and feedback. These efforts align with growing calls in science communication for interdisciplinary, emotionally resonant tools that can make abstract climate data more relatable and actionable.74 While our design choices were guided by intuitive and subjective metaphors, we acknowledge that their psychological and aesthetic impact has not been formally evaluated. The visual language was developed to reinforce intuitive associations (e.g., still, pale waves resembling ice; dynamic red ones suggesting heat), and sonification elements were similarly mapped to aridity and temperature gradients. However, we recognize that a systematic psychological or design-based assessment of these elements could enhance future iterations of SEAMAN. We see this as a promising direction for interdisciplinary collaboration, particularly with experts in visual cognition, environmental psychology, or data aesthetics.

Method details

Overview

SEAMAN (Synthesized EArth Monthly ANomalies) is a standalone, dual-interface climate visualization tool developed in VL, the visual programming language of the vvvv environment (https://vvvv.org/), which operates within the.NET ecosystem. VL is a node-based programming language designed for visual dataflow, real-time interaction, and multimedia applications, while vvvv provides the development environment widely used in interactive art, design, and prototyping. SEAMAN transforms NASA-POWER gridded climate data (1984–2024) into immersive visual and auditory metaphors aimed at supporting science communication and education. The name SEAMAN is the acronym for Synthesized EArth Monthly Anomalies and was chosen for its mnemonic value and to metaphorically evoke the idea of navigating a shared global climate, with no intended gender connotation. The software is composed of two main panes: the Navigation Pane, which provides an interface for map interaction, geoclimatic metadata, and textual output, and the Representation Pane, which renders the selected climate information as a dynamic seascape. Each visual and sound element corresponds to a specific climate variable or anomaly, mapped using percentile-based statistics to express deviation from historical norms. Monthly data are retrieved through APIs and converted into percentiles using standard Z-score transformations. The results are then encoded into interactive elements allowing users to explore changes over time or simulate future scenarios. The visual seascape is complemented by a generative soundscape whose tempo and layering respond to annual temperature anomalies, enhancing the emotional and experiential dimension of climate data interpretation. The tool has been developed by a multidisciplinary team of researchers at the GeoModel Lab of the CREA Research Centre for Agriculture and Environment (Italy), bringing together expertise in climate science, data analysis, music composition, and visual design. Iterative prototyping was conducted within the team, followed by informal testing with users from various disciplinary backgrounds to refine interface logic and improve interpretability. The project also benefited from the collaboration of visual and sound artist Natan Sinigaglia, who contributed significantly to the refinement of SEAMAN’s aesthetic and interactive elements. The software is freely accessible via GitHub (https://github.com/GeoModelLab/seaman) and Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15774839), enabling broad dissemination and reproducibility. A dedicated project website (https://geomodellab.github.io/seaman) has also been created to provide immediate access to user documentation, including installation guidance, interface descriptions, and a section on limitations. To support onboarding, SEAMAN includes a built-in tutorial that appears on first launch and guides users through how climate variables are mapped to visual and auditory elements. Figures S1–S4 provide a walkthrough of the elements; Figure S5 details user interactions; and Figure S6 maps the visual and auditory representations to the underlying climate variables. Additionally, a video presentation is available at https://vimeo.com/1041078640?share=copy, offering an in-depth explanation of SEAMAN’s interface, features and underlying logic. Modena (Italy, 44.3° N; 10.8° E) was selected as a proof-of-concept site to demonstrate SEAMAN general presentation (Figures S3 and S4).

The Navigation Pane

The Navigation Pane (Figure S7) is black and white and allows users to select locations on an interactive map, displayed using an equidistant cylindrical projection with the standard parallel at the equator (Figure S7, panel a). Upon georeferencing, three web Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) are called upon for the latitude and longitude of the selected site. The first API is the reverse geocoding service from OpenWeather (https://openweathermap.org/api/geocoding-api), which retrieves basic geographic and administrative data about a location, including the country, region, and city. The second API is the Köppen-Geiger (KG) climate classification from MAPresso (https://climate.mapresso.com/api/koeppen/?lat=lat&lon=lon), which provides the 3-letter KG climate type code and its description. The outputs of these two APIs are rendered in the Navigation Pane as text close to the sea and moon icons (Figure S7, panel c). These figures are used to give information on the average annual temperature (°C) and precipitation (mm) from the climatic norm, pointing at the visual metaphors used in the Representation Pane. The third API is the NASA POWER monthly, which retrieves weather data for the selected coordinates from 1984 to 2024 (https://power.larc.nasa.gov/docs/services/api/temporal/monthly/). Climate variables extracted include the monthly average 2-meter-high air temperature maximum (T2M_MAX, °C) and minimum (T2M_MIN, °C), the bias-corrected monthly cumulated precipitation at the Earth’s surface (PRECTOTCORR_SUM, mm), and the monthly sum of extraterrestrial radiation (TOA, W m-2). Once the data have been received, the monthly reference evapotranspiration (ET0) is computed using the Hargreaves formula,37 which requires TOA radiation, along with T2M_MAX and T2M_MIN as inputs. The ET0 and the PRECTOTCORR_SUM are used to derive a monthly aridity indicator36 based on the formula (ET0 – PRECTOTCORR) / (ET0 + PRECTOTCORR). This indicator, ranging from -1 to 1, provides insight into the balance between precipitation and evapotranspiration, with negative values indicating an evapotranspirative deficit and positive values indicating a surplus. Temperature and precipitation data for a user-selected year are then used to derive monthly percentiles, i.e., anomalies with respect to norms. First, the mean (μ) and standard deviation (σ) of monthly temperature and precipitation over the entire 41-year period are calculated, and used to compute a Z-score using the monthly x value of temperature or precipitation, i.e., (x- μ)/ σ. Then, the Z-score is converted to a percentile using a polynomial approximation to the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the normal distribution. This approach is commonly employed in computational statistics to approximate the CDF when precision is not critical, as it provides a balance between accuracy and efficiency.75 User modifications to temperature and precipitation are applied as correction factors to the monthly series, and the implementation and impact of these adjustments are described in the Visuals section. The specific year used for the comparison is set through a slider, whose update triggers the update of three histograms reporting monthly anomalies of minimum and maximum temperature and precipitation (Figure S7, panel d). The x-axis categories are months, and the displayed values are normalized from the minimum to the maximum value for each location to highlight monthly patterns in the same location, rather than regional climatic differences. Monthly data from the selected year are shown as points overlapped to the histogram norm, and their values are reported as labels. The magnitude of the anomaly determines the brightness of the histogram, with dark and light representing low and high monthly percentiles. The last element of the Navigation Pane is the short sea-poem produced by the virtual assistant of the Llama open-source large language model, through API (https://openrouter.ai/models?q=api). Llama is informed by these calculations and asked to produce a poem resembling the information, displayed in the Navigation Pane (Figure S7, panel e). The prompt message, sent using the “assistant” role, is: “You are SeaMan, an honest, concise and creative climate assistant. Based on the climate data provided, respond with a short rhyming poem (max 2 stanzas, no more than 200 characters). Focus on monthly anomalies and how they differ to the norm. Avoid excessive use of numbers but emphasize the feeling of the temperature and precipitation changes. Start each poem with the location and year as part of the poem itself. TONE: Capture the emotional essence of climate anomalies and their impact. Use imaginative metaphors and language related to the seascape, sky, and environment. Do not simply list data but instead interpret it creatively. Do not do incipit, start in medias res.” Then, the Llama API is called with the “user” role with the following message, “The weather in [location name]. The climatic norm is: average temperature = [Tavg] and precipitation = [average precipitation]. Now we are in [Year] and the maximum monthly temperatures: [Monthly maximum temperatures, comma separated] minimum temperatures: [Monthly minimum temperatures, comma separated] precipitations: [Monthly precipitation, comma separated]. This year the average Tmax with respect to the climatic norm was [average maximum temperature], percentile [maximum temperature percentile] Tmin was [average minimum temperature] °C, percentile [minimum temperature percentile] and Precipitation was [annual precipitation] mm, percentile [precipitation percentile].”

The Representation Pane

The SEAMAN Representation Pane features a digital seascape composed of four primary elements: the sea, the moon, the stars, and the sky (Figure S8). These elements are designed to convey synthetic information on climate and anomalies, transforming NASA-POWER data into visual and auditory metaphors. The sea is synthesized by 24 monthly waves, 12 each for maximum and minimum temperature anomalies (Figure S8, panel a). The waves are color coded to indicate the severity of these anomalies, with low percentiles with respect to the norm ranging from white (0th percentile) to blue (50th percentile), and from blue to yellow and orange (from 50th to 100th percentile). Temperature anomalies below the 50th percentiles determine a right-to-left movement of the sea waves, and those above are indicated by left-to-right movement. The waves are arranged in a bottom-up gradient from January to December. A simplex noise algorithm governs the movement of each wave, with the noise frequency correlating with the specific anomaly. The moon is on the right side of the sky and reflects the annual precipitation. It is rendered as a light circle composed of twelve concentric rings (Figure S8, panel b), whose width indicates the relative contribution of monthly cumulated precipitation to the annual precipitation (mm). The rings are color coded based on the percentile anomaly, ranging from pink (0th percentile, minimal precipitation) to green (100th percentile, significantly above-average precipitation). The size and position of the moon reflect the ratio between the cumulative annual rainfall and the climatic norm. The moon’s movement is controlled by a position damper, ensuring smooth transitions between years, and reshaping for a visually coherent experience.

Twelve stars are evenly spaced in the sky from January (left) to December (right), representing monthly aridity indicator values (Figure S8 panel c). Their colors range from blue (indicating an aridity value of -1, or excess precipitation relative to ET0) to yellow (value of +1, or pronounced evapotranspiration deficit). The stars’ vertical position reflects the aridity indicator: wetter months appear closer to the sea horizon, while drier ones are positioned higher in the sky. The user is informed about the value of the aridity indicator, and of maximum and minimum monthly temperature, precipitation, and ET0 by hovering over the stars with the mouse. Each star is surrounded by a blurry halo proportional to the monthly precipitation percentile, visually emphasizing wetter conditions. Additionally, the number of spikes (ranging from 5 to 11) and the degree of distortion of each star’s contour correspond to temperature anomalies: regular, symmetrical contours represent temperatures near or below the climatic average, while irregular and deformed shapes reflect warm anomalies. Stars’ movements also feature a damping effect, ensuring a smooth shift over time. The sky is colored according to average minimum and maximum temperature anomalies, ranging from a dark black (0th percentile) to blue sky (100th percentile). The sky is filled with an increasing number of white stars according to increasing temperature anomalies (Figure S8, panel d). Users can increase or decrease annual temperature (°C) and precipitation (mm) by pressing ‘S’ (sea) and ‘M’ (moon) together with the ‘up’ or ‘down’ arrow keys. Users can also interactively modify annual temperature and precipitation values within SEAMAN. These adjustments are implemented as simple offset factors (ΔT, ΔP) applied uniformly across the 12 monthly baseline series extracted from the NASA-POWER dataset for the selected year. These modified values are then propagated through the computational pipeline: the modified temperature values are used to recompute reference evapotranspiration (ET0) using the Hargreaves formula, and the modified precipitation values are combined with ET0 to recalculate the monthly aridity indicator. The resulting adjusted climate indicators are immediately reflected in the visual and auditory representations (histograms, sky stars, and sonification), allowing users to explore how hypothetical shifts in annual temperature or precipitation affect both anomalies and derived indices. This approach does not replace formal climate projections but provides an accessible and immediate means to experiment with “what if” scenarios and to perceive the sensitivity of climate indicators to changes in key variables. Raw data from NASA-POWER are also downloaded as .csv file by pressing ‘D’ key and saved in the ‘csvData’ folder. By default, SEAMAN opens at Chernobyl in 1986, a year symbolically linked to environmental risk and historical climate relevance.

The SEAMAN’s soundscape dynamically adjusts based upon temperature anomalies, blending multiple drum and synth loops. The basic elements are a dance kick drum in a 4/4 rhythm and a 4-chord sequence (F#- D B D) inspired by Frankie Hi-NRG’s ‘Quelli che benpensano’, an Italian artist committed to promoting individual actions against climate change through popular initiatives as ‘M’illumino di meno’ (https://www.rai.it/milluminodimeno/). The tempo varies from 80 bpm (0th percentile) to 130 bpm (100th percentile), mirroring temperature fluctuations. Drum loops gradually fill by increasing the volumes of multiple tracks downloaded from the Looperman website (https://www.looperman.com/) according to increased temperature anomalies. Analogously, melodic synth layers have been developed with the VL.Audio library and are activated progressively. Average annual temperature higher than the mean (50th percentile) activates two arpeggios in the 2nd and 3rd octaves. The stars component provides an auditory representation of the aridity indicator and can be played by the user pressing keys from 1 (January) to 0 (October), and then with the ‘O’ and ‘P’ keys for November and December. Temperature anomalies add distortions to the star notes, which are mapped to the -1 to +1 range of the aridity indicator, divided into equal intervals from F# (5th octave) to A (6th octave) following the F# minor diatonic scale.

Case study selection

To illustrate SEAMAN’s potential across varied climatic and socio-environmental contexts, we selected three representative case studies: Rome, Palestine, and Tierra del Fuego. Rome, situated in the central Mediterranean basin, is located within a well-known global climate change hotspot where warming, heat extremes, and precipitation variability are intensifying.76 Its climate characterized by hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters,77 makes it ideal to explore the visual and auditory encoding of climatic extremes. Including Palestine, particularly the Gaza Strip, as one of SEAMAN’s case studies is a deliberate choice to highlight the intersection of environmental crisis and historical injustice. In addition to severe climate vulnerability due to semi-arid conditions, acute water stress, and restricted adaptive capacity,78,79 the current situation is further exacerbated by ongoing geopolitical instability and humanitarian crisis.80,81,82 Escalating environmental and humanitarian crisis in this region underscores the urgent need for tools that not only convey climate data but also foster ethical awareness and emotional engagement. Lastly, Tierra del Fuego in southern Argentina offers a unique perspective from the Southern Hemisphere, with reversed seasonal cycles and subpolar conditions. This contrast allows users to experience how the same data metaphors are expressed under different climate regimes, especially regarding aridity patterns. These three regions offer diversity in climate type, hemispheric orientation, and sociopolitical context, providing the framework for evaluating SEAMAN’s effectiveness.
