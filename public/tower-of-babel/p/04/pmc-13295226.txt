Determinants of mental health service utilization among young adults with anxiety disorders
Authors: Siyeon Ko, Hyekyung Woo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnxiety disorders are among the most prevalent mental health issues faced by young adults, who experience the highest lifetime prevalence of these conditions. Despite the increasing rates of anxiety disorders, mental health service utilization within this demographic remains notably low. This study aims to analyze the factors influencing mental health service utilization among young adults with anxiety disorders, distinguishing between early young adults (19–29 years) and late young adults (30–39 years), to propose tailored support strategies.MethodsData from the 2021 National Mental Health Survey of Korea, which included 395 adults diagnosed with anxiety disorders, were analyzed. Complex sample logistic regression was used to assess how sociodemographic characteristics, health behaviors, and comorbid mental health issues impact service utilization.ResultsAmong early young adults, higher education (odds ratio [OR] = 6.57), being married (OR = 7.02), and suicidal thoughts (OR = 29.42) were significantly associated with increased mental health service utilization. For late young adults, suicidal thoughts (OR = 10.79) and post-traumatic stress disorder (OR = 15.87) were significant predictors of higher service use, while alcohol use disorder was associated with lower service utilization (OR = 0.07).ConclusionsThis study highlights the need to prioritize support for early young adults with lower education, single status, and suicidal thoughts, as well as late young adults with alcohol use disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, or suicidal thoughts. Implementing these strategies may enhance mental health management and improve the overall quality of life for young adults.

Introduction

Although there is no universally accepted definition of young adulthood, and the age range used to define young adults varies across social, policy, and research contexts, previous Korean studies have often defined young adults as individuals aged 19–39 years [1, 2]. This period is a critical period of psychological and social development, during which mental health issues can significantly impact overall health and quality of life [3]. In recent years, young adults have increasingly experienced mental health challenges due to various socioeconomic and transitional stressors, including difficulties related to employment, independent living, and social isolation [1, 4, 5]. In Korea, the 2023 depressive mood prevalence rate was 16.3% among adults in their 20s and 11.6% among those in their 30s, and treated cases of depression and anxiety disorders increased by 127.1% and 86.8% among people in their 20s, and 67.3% and 44.5% among those in their 30s, between 2017 and 2021 [6–8]. Anxiety disorders are the most prevalent mental health problems in young adulthood, with the highest rates within this age group compared to others, and a rapidly increasing trend [7, 9]. Young adults with anxiety disorders may benefit from a range of professional mental health services, including services from psychiatrists, other mental health professionals, and non-psychiatrist physicians. However, without timely access to these services, anxiety disorders are at high risk of becoming chronic and are often accompanied by other mental health issues, such as depression, highlighting the importance of timely and effective intervention [10].

Despite the crucial role of such services in promoting individual health and societal well-being during young adulthood [11, 12], young adults show lower awareness and demand for these services compared to other age groups. This results in service utilization rates that fall short of the actual prevalence of mental health problems [9, 13–15]. Mental health support for young adults is often embedded within general adult mental health services, with limited segmentation according to the specific characteristics and needs of this population. In Korea, university counseling centers have long served as a major pathway for mental health support among young adults; however, this service structure may limit opportunities for those outside academic settings to access institutional mental health support [16]. Accordingly, accessible support pathways that facilitate early identification of mental health problems and linkage to appropriate services are increasingly needed for diverse groups of young adults, including those outside academic settings [17]. In this context, digital mental health platforms, including mobile applications and web-based self-assessment tools, have expanded in recent years and are gaining attention as complementary pathways that may improve service accessibility and facilitate early linkage to appropriate support among young adults [18, 19].

The causes and symptoms of mental health problems, such as anxiety disorders, can vary by age, sex, and life stage, requiring tailored mental health services [20]. Young adults experience significant life transitions, such as entering university, seeking employment, becoming independent, marrying, and raising children, which necessitates an approach that accounts for the diversity within this group [21, 22]. Young adults people aged 19–29 often experience anxiety related to academic competition and job preparation, while young adults people aged 30–39 are more likely to face anxiety due to new responsibilities, such as job stability, marriage, and childcare [23–26].

Previous studies have predominantly focused on the general adult population, failing to adequately capture the unique characteristics of young adulthood. Consequently, findings have often been inconsistent or contradictory. For example, some studies have reported that females and individuals with higher education levels are more likely to utilize mental health services, while others find no significant impact from these factors [27–30]. Similarly, some studies have suggested that frequent alcohol consumption and lack of physical activity reduce the likelihood of using mental health services, while other studies have reported no significant influence of these behaviors on service utilization [29, 31, 32]. In addition, while some studies have found no association between suicidal thoughts and mental health service utilization, others have indicated that individuals with suicidal thoughts or post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) are more likely to seek these services [31, 33, 34]. These conflicting results may arise from a lack of analysis that considers the life cycle of young adulthood. Without accounting for the diverse developmental stages and mental health needs within this group, there is a risk of underestimating or overestimating the characteristics of certain age groups or experiences [35]. Therefore, it is essential to examine factors previously reported to be associated with mental health service utilization while considering the life cycle characteristics of young adulthood [36].

This study aims to analyze the key factors influencing the utilization of mental health services among young adults with anxiety disorders, with particular attention to the life cycle characteristics of young adults. For the purposes of this study, young adults are categorized into two distinct groups: early young adults (ages 19–29 years) and late young adults (ages 30–39 years). Through this analysis, our findings provide foundational data essential for developing tailored mental health support strategies appropriate for each age group.

Methods

Research data and subjects

We used microdata from the 2021 National Mental Health Survey of Korea, conducted by the Ministry of Health and Welfare. The survey, overseen by the National Center for Mental Health, has been conducted every 5 years since its inception in 2001. It includes data from one-on-one interviews and self-administered questionnaires assessing the prevalence of mental disorders, associated risk factors, and sociodemographic distribution within the community. Mental disorders in this survey were assessed using the Korean version of the Composite International Diagnostic Interview (K-CIDI), which is based on DSM-IV diagnostic criteria [37]. The 2021 survey employed a stratified sampling method, collecting data from 5,511 individuals aged 18–79 years across Korea. For this study, participants younger than 19 years or older than 39 years were excluded. Among those aged 19–39 years, only young adults classified as having a lifetime anxiety disorder based on the K-CIDI-derived variable (Fig. 1). Use of these data was approved by the National Center for Mental Health, and the study was granted exemption from review by the Institutional Review Board of Kongju National University (KNU_IRB_2024–039).

Measurement variables

Dependent variable

The dependent variable was the utilization of mental health services, as measured via the 2021 National Mental Health Survey of Korea. This variable reflects lifetime mental health service utilization, including services from psychiatrists, other mental health professionals, and non-psychiatrist medical doctors. Responses were categorized as either “Yes” or “No.”

Independent variables

The independent variables were selected to capture the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants. These variables included sex, age, household size, education level, marital status, employment status, and income level. Sex was classified as male or female. Age was categorized into 19–24 years and 25–29 years for early young adults, and 30–34 years and 35–39 years for late young adults, to more closely examine within-group heterogeneity. Household size was divided into 1 person, 2 people, and 3 or more people. Education level was categorized into middle school, high school, and university or higher. Marital status was classified as single or married, employment activity as “yes” or “no,” and income level as “below median income” or “above median income.”

To evaluate health behaviors, we included variables such as health satisfaction, subjective quality of life, vigorous physical activity, moderate physical activity, and online gaming. Health satisfaction was classified as “dissatisfied” or “satisfied,” while subjective quality of life was categorized as “poor” or “good.” Vigorous and moderate physical activity were categorized as none, 1–3 days per week, or 4 or more days per week. Online gaming was classified as either “yes” or “no.”

To assess comorbid mental health problems, we examined variables reflecting the lifetime prevalence of suicidal thoughts, PTSD, and alcohol use disorder. All three variables were classified as either “yes” or “no.”

Statistical analysis

Data were extracted using a complex sample design that incorporated stratification variables, clustering variables, and weighting factors. After generating a complex sample plan file, statistical analyses were performed. To explore the sociodemographic characteristics, health behaviors, and comorbid mental health problems, as well as their associations with mental health service utilization among early and late young adults, complex sample frequency analysis and complex sample cross-tabulation analysis were conducted. Chi-square tests were used for the cross-tabulation analysis to assess the relationships between variables, using two-tailed tests. To identify factors influencing mental health service utilization in these groups, complex sample logistic regression analysis was performed. All independent variables were entered simultaneously into the complex sample logistic regression models, and multicollinearity was assessed. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0.

Results

The sociodemographic characteristics, health behaviors, and comorbidities associated with mental health service utilization among early and late young adults were analyzed (Tables 1 and 2). Overall, 27 of 165 early young adults (17.9%) utilized mental health services, compared to 26 of 230 late young adults (10.9%).

Among early young adults, females were significantly more likely to utilize services(p = 0.049). Those aged 19–24 years also showed higher utilization rates than those aged 25–29 years(p < 0.001). In addition, early young adults who were dissatisfied with their health were more likely to use services than those satisfied with their health(p = 0.033). A particularly strong association was observed for those with suicidal thoughts, who had a significantly higher utilization rate compared to those without suicidal thoughts (p < 0.001).

For late young adults, no significant sex differences were observed in service utilization. However, those aged 30–34 years had higher utilization rates than those aged 35–39 years(p < 0.001). Similar to early young adults, late young adults dissatisfied with their health were more likely to utilize services, and those with suicidal thoughts were significantly more likely to use mental health services (p < 0.001). Unlike early young adults, late young adults who were not economically active had higher utilization rates than those who were economically active (p = 0.048). Among comorbid mental health issues, late young adults with PTSD had significantly higher rates than those without the disorder(p < 0.001). In addition, late young adults without alcohol use disorder were more likely to use mental health services than late young adults with the disorder(p = 0.013).

Characteristics associated with mental health service utilization in late young adults with anxiety disorders

The results of complex sample logistic regression analysis are presented in Table 3. Among early young adults, significant differences were observed by sex, education level, marital status, and the presence of suicidal thoughts. Females were 4.58 times more likely to utilize services than males (odds ratio [OR] = 4.58, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.19–17.66). Those with a high school education were 6.57 times more than those with only a middle school education (OR = 6.57, 95% CI = 1.36–31.64). Married early young adults had a 7.02 times higher likelihood (OR = 7.02, 95% CI = 1.06–46.16). Regarding health behaviors, those who engaged in vigorous physical activity 1–3 days per week were 8.02 times more likely than those who did not (OR = 8.02, 95% CI = 1.22–52.43). Finally, early young adults with suicidal thoughts were 29.42 times more likely to utilize such services than those without such thoughts (OR = 29.42, 95% CI = 7.6–108.86).

Among late young adults, females were 4.28 times more likely to utilize mental health services compared to males (OR = 4.28, 95% CI = 1.23–14.99). However, differences in other sociodemographic variables were not statistically significant. In terms of health behaviors, late young adults who engaged in vigorous physical activity 1–3 days per week were 9.43 times more likely (OR = 9.43, 95% CI = 1.83–48.42). Among comorbid mental health issues, all variables showed statistically significant associations. Late young adults with suicidal thoughts were 10.79 times more likely (OR = 10.79, 95% CI = 3.14–37.11) and those with PTSD were 15.87 times more likely (OR = 15.87, 95% CI = 3.10–81.03). Conversely, those with alcohol use disorder were 0.07 times less likely to utilize such services (OR = 0.07, 95% CI = 0.01–0.45).

Factors influencing mental health service utilization among early and late young adults with anxiety disorders

Discussion

We explored the factors influencing mental health service utilization among early and late young adults with anxiety disorders. Although suicidal thoughts showed a strong association with service utilization in both age groups, the overall pattern of significant factors differed by developmental stage. Sociodemographic factors appeared to be relatively more salient among early young adults, whereas mental health-related comorbidities were more prominent among late young adults. Notably, early young adults were more likely to utilize services than late young adults, with younger individuals in both groups exhibiting higher utilization rates. This trend may reflect the pressures and anxieties associated with the transition to independence, employment, and financial challenges that early young adults face, which makes them more inclined to seek support [32, 36, 38, 39]. By contrast, late young adults may encounter barriers to accessing services due to time constraints imposed by work and family responsibilities [40]. These findings highlight the need to consider differences in factors associated with mental health service utilization between early and late young adults.

For early young adults, educational level and the presence of suicidal thoughts emerged as significant factors influencing service utilization. Those who graduated from high school were more likely to use services than individuals with only a middle school education, aligning with previous research suggesting that higher education levels increase the likelihood of service utilization [41]. This suggests that a higher level of education may enhance awareness of mental health issues and the importance of seeking services, thereby promoting utilization [42]. In addition, early young adults with suicidal thoughts were more likely to access services compared to those without such thoughts. This contrasts with previous studies reporting a decrease in the intention to seek services as suicidal ideation intensifies [28]. The expansion of community-based suicide-prevention programs in Korea, particularly those targeting early young adults, may explain this finding [43, 44]. These programs likely identify at-risk early young adults early and actively guide them toward services. It is also possible that the growing availability of digital mental health platforms may contribute to increased service accessibility, potentially facilitating linkage to appropriate services among young adults [45, 46]. Although further research is needed to explore the impact of these social changes on service utilization trends, it is crucial to continue enhancing access to mental health services for early young adults with suicidal thoughts. Therefore, strengthening both community-based programs and accessible service pathways, including digital platforms, should be considered to enable early young adults to seek professional help in a timely manner.

By contrast, for late young adults, comorbid mental health problems were identified as major factors influencing service utilization. Those with suicidal thoughts and PTSD were more likely to utilize them, whereas those with comorbid alcohol use disorder were less likely. This aligns with previous studies suggesting that individuals with alcohol use disorder may rely on alcohol to alleviate anxiety symptoms rather than seeking professional services [47, 48]. Because alcohol use disorder often develops during young adulthood, and late young adults exhibit higher drinking rates and are more exposed to alcohol-related problems, this can lead to long-term dependence on alcohol, exacerbating anxiety disorders [49, 50]. Therefore, it is essential to develop targeted mental health services for individuals with specific health behaviors, such as alcohol use disorder. This can be achieved by raising awareness of the risks associated with alcohol use and its impact on anxiety disorders, and by encouraging those affected to seek professional treatment and support. In addition, late young adults engaged in economic activities tend to have lower service utilization rates, consistent with research suggesting that limited time availability due to work reduces the likelihood of seeking services [40]. However, another perspective suggests that economic activity may enhance self-esteem and quality of life, potentially leading to a decreased need for such services [51, 52]. Given these considerations, it is important to establish flexible service-delivery options, such as expanding evening or online counseling services, to ensure that late young adults with time constraints can maintain the positive effects of economic activity on mental health while still accessing necessary services [53–55].

Unlike late young adults, early young adults who were married were more likely to utilize services compared to single counterparts. This contrasts with previous research, which suggests that marriage does not significantly promote such service utilization [51]. However, it implies that marriage may act as a facilitating factor for service use, specifically in early young adults. Particularly for married individuals in the early stages of marriage without children, the emotional support provided by a spouse appears to positively influence the likelihood of seeking services [56]. As early young adults transition into later adulthood, the increasing anxiety and responsibilities associated with balancing work and family life, particularly after having children, may contribute to a lower prioritization of service utilization [57]. Moreover, previous studies on middle-aged and elderly populations have indicated that marriage positively impacts mental health, suggesting that the stability gained in both family and work life with age may also lead to a decrease in service utilization [58, 59]. To better understand the impact of marriage on mental health service utilization among early and late young adults, further research is needed to explore the effects of having children and the evolving dynamics of family roles. Based on these findings, mental health services for early young adults should emphasize strengthening support programs during significant life transitions, such as marriage. For late young adults, policy efforts should focus on supporting mental health through the appropriate implementation of measures such as parental leave, flexible working arrangements, and initiatives aimed at balancing work and family life.

This study had several limitations. First, due to its cross-sectional design and the use of a lifetime anxiety disorder variable, it was challenging to identify changes over time or establish causal relationships. Second, the self-reported nature of the survey data may have introduced biases, such as memory errors or social desirability bias. Third, although previous studies have suggested that clinical and social relationship-related factors may be associated with mental health service utilization, some potentially relevant factors, such as depressive disorders and social relationship-related variables, were not included in the present analysis due to limitations in the available variables and statistical considerations. Future studies are needed to examine these factors to provide a more comprehensive understanding of mental health service utilization among young adults. Nevertheless, the significance of this study lies in the distinction between early and late young adults, allowing for the identification of key factors influencing mental health service utilization. Our findings provide a foundational basis for the development of tailored mental health support strategies, which can significantly contribute toward the formulation of public health policies that improve the mental health of young adults. Such strategies are expected to establish a foundation for early prevention and treatment of mental health problems through customized interventions.

Conclusion

We found significant differences in the key factors influencing mental health service utilization between early young adults and late young adults with anxiety disorders. For early young adults, priority should be given to individuals with lower education levels, those who are single, and those with suicidal thoughts, with a focus on providing accessible social support. For late young adults, key support targets should include those with comorbid alcohol use disorder, those with suicidal thoughts, those with PTSD, and those who are economically active. It is essential to explore flexible service access strategies that consider time constraints and provide psychological support for these individuals. Our results emphasize the importance of identifying groups within the life cycle that require priority support and the need for focused policy support aimed at these groups. Such strategies should help to effectively manage mental health problems among young adults and contribute to improving their overall quality of life.
