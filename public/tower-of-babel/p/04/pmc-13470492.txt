Ancient plume-reinforced lithosphere modulates present-day crustal fluid migration and deformation
Authors: Zikun Zhou, Nian Yu, Xin Li, Sheng Zhang, Tianqi Wang, Wenxin Kong, Tianyang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding how ancient mantle plume residues modify lithospheric structure and influence tectonic deformation remains challenging, especially in plume-peripheral regions where basalts are not exposed at the surface. Here, we present magnetotelluric imaging across the Huayingshan Fault in the outer zone of the Permian Emeishan large igneous province, China. The resistivity model reveals a vertically extensive high-resistivity structure beneath the fault that coincides with high seismic velocities and high density, and is most consistently interpreted as the crystallized product of mantle-derived basaltic melts from the Emeishan plume. We use upper-mantle-scale geodynamic simulations to examine how plume-driven melting and plume-derived material interact with lithospheric heterogeneity. While these models do not explicitly simulate small-scale melt extraction or crustal emplacement, they suggest that pre-existing lithospheric heterogeneity can shape the lateral redistribution of plume-derived material. We infer that residual plume material crystallized in situ within the fault zone formed a low-permeability, mechanically strong lithospheric barrier that may impede or redirect crustal-fluid migration. This plume-reinforced lithosphere may promote stress localization on its northwestern side, where present-day earthquakes are preferentially clustered. Together, these results suggest that plume-modified lithosphere can preserve a long-lived structural imprint that modulates crustal-fluid migration and continental deformation.Subject terms: Geophysics, Seismology, Geology

Introduction

The mechanical behavior and tectonic evolution of the continental lithosphere are strongly affected by ancient mantle processes1. Mantle plumes have played a key role in Earth’s evolution, with their intense thermal anomalies triggering rapid and voluminous mantle upwelling and melting, which can significantly modify lithospheric architecture2,3. This process generates voluminous basaltic volcanism and large igneous provinces (LIPs)4–6, and can modify the regional stress field and lithosphere–asthenosphere coupling due to melt extraction and thermo-chemical reworking7,8. Although the main phase of mantle plume activity is geologically short-lived9, residual plume-derived material can persist in the lithosphere for hundreds of millions of years8. These “fossil plumes” include refractory residues10, high-pressure cumulates11, and volatile-rich channels12 that can continue to affect plate interactions under the present-day thermal and tectonic conditions7. Identifying such inherited structures and understanding their interactions with ongoing geodynamic processes is key to understanding the long-term effects of mantle plumes on lithospheric architecture and controls on present-day seismicity and fluid transport.

The Emeishan LIP (ELIP) in southwestern China is a globally significant mantle-plume-type LIP that formed in response to an intense late Permian (ca. 260 Ma) mantle plume event13 (Fig. 1a). This plume activity caused voluminous basaltic eruptions, extensive magmatic intrusions, and mantle metasomatism, and potentially generated high-pressure crystallized residues and/or thermally altered zones in the deep crust14. Plume–lithosphere interactions can thermally weaken or chemically modify the mantle15. As the thermal anomaly dissipates during continental drift, melt depletion and dehydration induced by the plume can further rheologically reinforce the lithosphere8. Ancient plume activity can leave long-lived structural imprints within the continental lithosphere and control its mechanical response to subsequent tectonic processes16.

The subsurface fate of the mantle plume and its potential effects on lithospheric strength are well constrained in the inner zone of the ELIP17. Seismic velocity and magnetotelluric (MT) resistivity models reveal a vertically continuous high-velocity and high-resistivity structure extending to depths greater than 150 km, interpreted as the cooled and crystallized remnant of an ancient mantle plume18. The strengthened lithospheric mantle modulates the crustal and mantle flow19. In contrast, the Huayingshan Fault (HYSF), located at the tectonic boundary between the Yangtze Craton and Chuan–Dian diamond block, is positioned in the outer zone of the ELIP17. In this region, the surface basalts have a patchy distribution (Fig. 1b), and it is unclear whether this region has been strongly affected by the ancient mantle plume.

Here, we integrate three-dimensional magnetotelluric imaging with thermo-mechanical geodynamic modeling to investigate how the Emeishan mantle plume modified continental lithospheric architecture and whether remnants of this ancient plume are preserved beneath the outer zone of the large igneous province. We aim to image fossil plume-derived structures within the lithosphere and to evaluate how pre-existing lithospheric heterogeneity governed plume–lithosphere interaction and the lateral migration of plume-derived magmas. By linking geophysical observations with geodynamic simulations, we further assess how inherited plume-related structures may continue to influence lithospheric strength, fluid migration, and present-day crustal deformation, thereby constraining the long-term geodynamic influence of ancient mantle plume events within continental interiors.

Results

Broadband MT data were collected in 2022 and 2025 from 103 MT stations (Fig. 1c) surrounding the HYSF. High-quality impedance estimates were obtained at periods of 0.003 to 1818 s for most stations, based on recordings longer than 72 h (Supplementary Fig. 1). The reconstructed resistivity model revealed a pronounced electrical contrast in the crustal structure beneath the Sichuan Basin, HYSF, and the eastern Sichuan fold-and-thrust belt (Fig. 2). The most striking model feature is a vertically extensive, high-resistivity structure located directly beneath the HYSF, with a maximum resistivity value exceeding 10,000 Ω·m and an average resistivity of ~600 Ω·m. This structure exhibits a near-vertical columnar geometry extending from the shallow crust to well below the Moho, indicating a deep mantle origin. At shallow depths, the high-resistivity anomaly beneath the HYSF spans a lateral distance of 20–30 km (Fig. 2a, b). With increasing depth, its across-strike width broadens gradually (Fig. 2c–f). Given the spatial coincidence of this resistivity structure and the surface trace of the HYSF, we define this feature as R.

In contrast, the adjacent domains to the west (Sichuan Basin) and east (Eastern Sichuan Fold-and-Thrust Belt) are characterized by widespread low-resistivity anomalies in the upper–mid crust. These low-resistivity zones, referred to as C1 (beneath the Sichuan Basin) and C2 (beneath the fold-and-thrust belt), exhibit quasi-horizontal geometries with resistivities of 1–30 Ω·m. The thickness of the conductors reaches ~10 km beneath the Sichuan Basin and >20 km beneath the fold-and-thrust belt. Seismicity is concentrated mainly within the conductive layer C1, west of R, whereas the high-resistivity body R itself and the region immediately to its east are characterized by sparse seismicity or seismic gaps (Fig. 2c–f). This spatial pattern highlights a strong association between the high-resistivity structure R and zones of reduced seismic deformation, indicating a pronounced mechanical contrast relative to the surrounding crust. Details of the data acquisition, processing, quality assessment (Supplementary Figs. 1–3), model inversion (Supplementary Figs. 4–11), and sensitivity tests (Supplementary Figs. 12–14) are provided in Methods and Supplementary Information.

Discussion

Nature of the resistivity anomalies

MT data are inherently more sensitive to low-resistivity structures20; consequently, high-resistivity features have received less attention. This is particularly true in regions such as along the margin of the Yangtze Craton, where similar anomalies have been interpreted either as unmodified ancient lithospheric blocks21 or foundered lithospheric fragments22. However, cratonic lithosphere, due to its strong melt depletion, is typically characterized by low density and positive buoyancy23. The close spatial correlation between the high-resistivity anomaly R and a positive density anomaly (Fig. 1c) makes an interpretation solely in terms of buoyant cratonic lithosphere unlikely. Moreover, anomaly R exhibits a vertically extensive yet laterally localized geometry, forming a sharp contrast with the surrounding low-resistivity crust and extending from the surface into the lithospheric mantle (Fig. 2). Such a geometry differs from that typically expected for laterally continuous, unmodified cratonic lithosphere or uniformly thickened lithosphere produced by regional compression24. Crustal resistivity is controlled by the composition, temperature, and microstructure25,26. Although the study area lies in the outer zone of the ELIP (Fig. 1b), the thermal anomaly associated with the Permian mantle plume had dissipated by the Mesozoic27, and the present-day surface heat flow is comparable to that of surrounding regions28 (Fig. 1c). Therefore, anomalously low temperatures cannot explain the observed high resistivity. Similarly, grain growth at mid–lower crustal depths is unlikely to simultaneously produce the observed increases in seismic velocity and resistivity29,30. Instead, the most plausible explanation for the high-resistivity anomaly R is a compositional contrast with the adjacent crust. The electrical properties of R are strongly in correspondence with other geophysical parameters. For example, the high-resistivity region coincides with a zone of increased Vp and Vs in seismic images31 (Fig. 2b), suggesting that R represents a coherent, mechanically strong block. A statistical comparison between shear-wave velocity and resistivity models further reveals a robust positive correlation between high velocities and high resistivities within R, reinforcing the interpretation that these anomalies reflect a common physical origin rather than independent artifacts (Supplementary Figs. 15, 16). Furthermore, results from multi-observable probabilistic inversions indicate that the lithosphere beneath this region has elevated Mg# values32 (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 17), which is consistent with a history of melt extraction linked to ancient mantle plume activity. Such a geochemical signature is compatible with mantle plume–related magmatic processes, but is less readily explained by tectonic thickening or unmodified lithospheric mantle alone.

Collectively, these observations are consistent with R representing a residual body produced by in situ cooling and crystallization of mantle-derived magmas. Although extensive basaltic outcrops are absent within the R region (Fig. 1b), Emeishan basalts have been identified in boreholes to the northeast and southwest of the study area (Fig. 2a, b). This indicates that plume-related magmatism was not restricted to surface eruptions, but also involved widespread subsurface emplacement beyond basaltic outcrops. The resistivity, seismic velocities and density characteristics of R are consistent with those of structures imaged in the inner ELIP, where plume-related magmatic additions are well established18,19. This correspondence supports the interpretation of R as a dense, dry and mechanically strong mafic–ultramafic residual body associated with Emeishan plume magmatism. This interpretation is not unique, however, because comparable geophysical signatures could also arise from lower-crustal eclogitization or mafic underplating unrelated to ELIP magmatism. In the absence of direct petrological or geochemical constraints from R, including xenolith, thermobarometric, or in situ compositional data, we therefore treat a plume-related origin as the best-supported interpretation within present observational limits, rather than a uniquely required explanation.

Several potential mechanisms can account for the low-resistivity anomalies in the upper–mid crust. In arc volcanic settings, such anomalies are commonly attributed to volatile-bearing melts33–35. In contrast, within intraplate settings, horizontally extensive low-resistivity layers associated with compressional volcanic systems are generally interpreted to be the result of recent or ongoing magmatic underplating25,36. However, outside of volcanic regions, the formation of laterally continuous low-resistivity horizons (i.e., extending for tens of kilometers) is more plausibly explained by interconnected saline fluids within pore space or by the presence of graphite and sulfide networks37–40. Based on the surface heat flow in the Huayingshan region and the depth of anomaly C1, we estimate the temperature is ~500 °C, which is well below the solidus of crustal minerals41 and rules out the presence of significant melt. Although graphite or metallic sulfides can contribute to enhanced electrical conductivity, they generally exert limited influence on seismic velocities and are unlikely to generate pronounced velocity anomalies. In contrast, C1 is characterized by reductions in both Vp and Vs, with a stronger decrease in Vs, resulting in an elevated Vp/Vs ratio31 (Supplementary Fig. 15). Such a pattern is more consistent with the presence of crustal fluids, which can simultaneously reduce seismic velocities and resistivity, providing a self-consistent explanation beyond that of melts, graphite, or sulfide phases. This interpretation is further supported by the extremely low 3He/4He ratios (<0.016Ra42) measured at the C1 location, which indicate a dominantly crustal fluid source and effectively rule out a significant contribution from mantle-derived volatiles. Under salinity conditions typical of crustal fluids in continental settings, the resistivity anomaly C1 is interpreted as a saline fluid layer with an estimated volume fraction of ~6 vol.% (3 wt.% salinity), based on the relationship between reduced seismic velocities and inferred porosity. The inferred fluid fraction is based on geologically reasonable ranges of temperature, salinity, host-rock resistivity, and connectivity exponent commonly used for continental crustal settings. It should therefore be viewed as a constrained, but still parameter-dependent, estimate rather than a unique value. Details of the brine-fraction calculations are provided in the Methods and Supplementary Information (Supplementary Figs. 18, 19). In addition, the depth extent of C1 (to ~25 km) strongly suggests it is linked to deep crustal or upper mantle processes, rather than shallow meteoric water circulation. The spatial distribution of surface hot springs provides additional field evidence for the presence of saline fluids associated with C143 (Fig. 2a). The resistivity image reveals that C1 has a well-defined resistive lower boundary that is confined to the upper–mid crust, whereas C2 has a more diffuse lower boundary, indicating a deeper, mantle-derived origin (Fig. 2). In addition, the detachment structures that formed by folding and thrusting in the region of C2 might also contribute to its pronounced low-resistivity anomaly. There is a lack of seismicity above C2; consequently, we do not discuss this feature further.

Formation of the high-resistivity feature

The high-resistivity structure R is interpreted as a fossil magmatic intrusion formed by in situ cooling and crystallization of basaltic melts emplaced in the ELIP. To investigate the origin and migration of this magmatism, we conducted two-dimensional thermo-mechanical simulations of plume–lithosphere interactions using Underworld244 (Fig. 3). Although plume–lithosphere interactions and plume-related melt migration are inherently three-dimensional processes, the principal mechanism investigated here, namely buoyancy-driven plume ascent and the consequent asymmetric distribution of plume material arising from lateral contrasts in lithospheric structure, is governed primarily by vertical and cross-sectional dynamics. These processes are therefore amenable to robust investigation within a two-dimensional modeling framework45. The reference model assumed that laterally homogeneous lithosphere (120 km thick) was underlain by asthenosphere that had a hot mantle plume at its base. The initial material configuration consisted of 25 km-thick silicic upper crust, 25 km-thick mafic lower crust, and underlying mantle consisting of dry olivine. The physical properties of the materials used in the numerical experiments were taken from refs. 46–48 (Supplementary Table 1). Within the subcontinental lithospheric mantle (SCLM), we introduced three discrete vertical weak zones, each approximately 10 km in width. In the interiors of ancient cratons, such localized weak domains may represent hydrated mantle regions. Their presence does not imply the existence of throughgoing faults; rather, they indicate mechanically weakened loci within the SCLM, where deformation is more readily initiated in response to external perturbations49. Within the lithosphere, a 10-km-thick horizontal weak layer was incorporated to represent the widely recognized mid-lithospheric discontinuity50. At the base of the lithosphere, we prescribed a localized high-density domain to represent compositionally densified lithospheric mantle, as suggested by recent geodynamic studies on secular craton evolution and lithospheric instability50. Such dense mantle domains may develop through melt-related metasomatism or the cumulative effects of long-term deformation and can govern both lithospheric stability and the dynamics of mantle–lithosphere interaction. The thermal anomaly of the mantle plume was represented by a circular region with an initial radius of 100 km and a temperature 300 K higher than the ambient mantle15,51–53. In the reference case, the rheologically weak lithospheric mantle was assigned a viscosity that was 0.001 times that of the surrounding mantle9,54. These parameters are broadly comparable to those used in recent studies that investigated lithospheric delamination caused by mantle plumes54,55.

To assess the robustness of our results, we conducted a series of sensitivity tests in which both the viscosity contrast and the geometry of the weak zone were systematically varied. These experiments demonstrate that, although the rate of plume material migration may change, the fundamental behavior of localized plume-material accumulation, lithospheric delamination, and plume transport pathways remains stable across the tested parameter space (Supplementary Figs. 20–22). Furthermore, the integrated melt volume generated in the reference model (~2.6 × 106 km3) is broadly consistent with independent geological estimates of magma production associated with the ELIP (Supplementary Fig. 23). Details of the governing equations, rheological formulations, boundary conditions, and partial melting calculations are provided in the Methods and Supplementary Information.

Our simulations show that ascent of a hot, buoyant mantle plume induces partial melting at the lithosphere–asthenosphere boundary. The resulting low-viscosity plume material and its melts accumulate and migrate laterally at the base of the lithosphere (Fig. 3b1, c1, and d1). When plume material encounters regions of reduced lithospheric strength, it becomes preferentially channelized along these pre-existing weak zones, which act as conduits for hot, buoyant material and facilitate penetration of plume-derived material into the overlying lithospheric mantle45,54. Progressive thermal and mechanical erosion of the lithospheric mantle by this plume-derived material promotes localized gravitational instability, ultimately leading to lithospheric mantle delamination and thinning56,57. In turn, lithospheric thinning further enhances the channelized flow of hot material, establishing a positive feedback between lithospheric weakening and plume-material transport58–60. Small-scale mantle flow induced by lithospheric foundering further promotes the lateral redistribution of plume material, reinforcing asymmetric plume material spreading beneath the lithosphere61. In contrast to the symmetric, mushroom-shaped spreading of plume heads predicted for laterally homogeneous lithosphere9,57, our simulations exhibit pronounced asymmetric migration of plume material and melt, governed by lateral variations in mechanical properties within the lithospheric mantle beneath the Huayingshan Fault system. These rheologically weakened zones not only reorganize the regional stress field but also control the pathways and loci of plume-material accumulation and melt focusing (Fig. 3d).

Melt-rich plume material progressively erodes the base of the lithosphere (Fig. 3d2) and, guided by the layered zones or secondary faults, ascends episodically to the surface to form LIP magmatism62–64. The predicted asymmetric melt distribution is consistent with the paleogeographic reconstructions of Late Permian–Early Triassic ELIP magmatism65. This pattern suggests that the juxtaposition of plume-induced melting and lithospheric weak zones provided an efficient mechanism for magma generation and localization. Following magma emplacement, prolonged stagnation and crystallization of magmas in the crust and uppermost lithosphere would have allowed volatile components (e.g., H2O, CO2, and H2S) to dissipate38, leaving behind dense, cold volatile-depleted residues characterized by a high electrical resistivity. Compared with the vertically focused melts that ascended near the plume center66, magmas migrating laterally beneath faults are more likely to form horizontal lattice-preferred mineral orientations. The crystalline framework of cumulates and metamorphic assemblages (e.g., garnet–olivine-rich eclogite) would increase the shear-wave velocity67. Together, these processes provide a plausible explanation for the high-resistivity, high-velocity and low Vp/Vs structure observed in the crust, as independently imaged by present-day MT and seismic observations.

It should be noted that the geodynamic simulations are also intended as mechanism tests rather than a full reconstruction of the natural system. Our modeling focuses on upper-mantle–scale dynamics governing the asymmetric migration of plume material controlled by lithospheric heterogeneity. Although regions of partial melting are explicitly tracked in the simulations, processes of melt extraction, percolation, and dyke propagation are not incorporated. Consequently, the inferred strengthening of the overlying crust above plume-material accumulation zones, resulting from cooling and crystallization of plume-derived melts, represents a geological interpretation based on the thermo-mechanical context of the models rather than a directly simulated process.

Influence of a fossil mantle plume on present-day fluid migration and seismicity

Remnants of ancient mantle plumes not only preserve a record of thermochemical events in Earth’s interior, but also may exert a long-lived effect on present-day geodynamic and tectonic processes. Our conductivity model reveals that the high-resistivity anomaly (R) coincides with the central axis of the Huayingshan Fault and is flanked by crustal regions with markedly lower resistivity, indicative of fluid-rich crust, likely sourced from tectonically induced metamorphic dehydration of the basin basement in response to the eastward extrusion of the Tibetan Plateau42,68,69. The geodetic observations of surface GPS velocities (Fig. 1c) document a pronounced eastward to southeastward extrusion of the crust, which provides the regional tectonic forcing necessary for sustained fluid production, pressure accumulation, and redistribution within the upper–mid crust. The presence of the dense, high-resistivity structure likely acts as an impermeable and mechanically strong lithospheric barrier. This plume-derived lithospheric block, characterized by a high strength and low permeability, not only obstructs fluid migration19, but also promotes fluid accumulation and an increase in pore pressure to its west, thereby reducing effective normal stress on surrounding faults. However, permeability values, pore-pressure evolution, and three-dimensional fluid-flow pathways are not directly modeled here. The proposed barrier effect should therefore be regarded as an inferred mechanism supported by the combined geophysical, geodetic, and seismic observations, rather than a directly simulated hydromechanical process. Future coupled fluid-flow and geomechanical modeling would be needed to quantify how permeability contrasts around R control pressure accumulation, fluid redistribution and earthquake triggering. Consequently, stress localization and brittle failure are enhanced along its margins70,71, providing a plausible mechanism for frequent fluid-related seismicity on the western side of the Huayingshan Fault39,72. The deflection of the regional maximum horizontal compressive stress (SHmax) on the western side of the high-resistivity structure R (Fig. 2b), together with persistently low GPS strain rates coincident with R (Supplementary Fig. 24), indicates that this structure has a substantially higher effective shear modulus and mechanical stiffness than the surrounding lithosphere, and behaves as a coherent, mechanically strong block. This rigid block, which we interpret as a vestige of plume activity, may also act as a mechanical buttress against the eastward extrusion of Tibetan crustal material. This impedes the tectonic forces from the plateau and modulates both the distribution of seismicity and style of regional deformation. The asymmetric earthquake distribution across the fault provides strong evidence for this mechanism (Fig. 1c). Moreover, this plume-derived modulation on seismicity is not confined to our study area. Across the ELIP, seismicity systematically avoids the interiors of basalt-dominated regions and instead concentrates along their margins and adjacent major fault zones, where strong lateral contrasts in lithospheric strength are expected (Supplementary Fig. 25). This pattern highlights a fundamental, yet poorly recognized process, in that fossil mantle plume material in the lithosphere can exert a protracted control on subsequent tectonic deformation. Our findings suggest that ancient plume events result in lithospheric-scale geological “barriers” that modulate crustal fluid migration and seismicity over timescales of hundreds of millions of years (Fig. 4).

Our results reveal a pronounced high-resistivity anomaly beneath the HYSF. Its elevated seismic velocities and density indicate this anomaly is best explained as the remnant of an ancient mantle plume preserved within the lithosphere. Geodynamic simulations indicate that pre-existing lithospheric heterogeneity promoted asymmetric lateral migration of plume material and melt generation, leading to lithospheric delamination and localized magma accumulation. Although melt extraction and crustal emplacement are not explicitly modeled, the combined observations are consistent with in situ cooling and crystallization of plume-derived material, producing a dense, dehydrated, and mechanically strong lithospheric domain. This process can account for both the observed spatial pattern of the ELIP and the development of rigid crustal domains. The fossil plume material continues to affect tectonic processes. It may behave as a relatively low-permeability and mechanically strong barrier that impedes fluid migration west of the HYSF, resulting in elevated fluid pressures and fluid-driven seismicity. The rigidity of this block localizes stress and focuses earthquakes along the western side of the fault. This process is a mechanism by which ancient mantle plume material regulates crustal deformation. Our findings highlight the enduring effects of plume–lithosphere interactions. Ancient mantle plume processes can form lithospheric-scale heterogeneities that modulate fluid migration, fault mechanics, and regional deformation over hundreds of millions of years.

Methods

Magnetotelluric data

The Magnetotelluric (MT) data used in this study were collected from a total of 103 MT stations (Fig. 1c, blue triangles) surrounding the HYSF in 2022 and 2025. The average station spacing across the study area is approximately 15 km, with a wider spacing of ~20 km toward the periphery, and a denser network established in the central region, where additional measurements were acquired at intervals of about 7 km. All sites were carefully located away from towns, roads, villages, and power lines to minimize anthropogenic noise and ensure high-quality data acquisition. High quality impedance estimates were obtained within the period range of 320 Hz–1818 s for most stations from recordings greater than 72 hours in length. Typical sounding curves are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1. The data curves and pre-inversion analyses are provided in Supplementary Information.

Modeling and inversion

We inverted the full-impedance data from 103 sites (Fig. 1c) to construct the preferred three-dimensional resistivity model using the modular electromagnetic inversion system (ModEM)73. The model consists of 94 × 106 laterally uniform cells, each 2.5 km wide in both latitude and longitude, surrounded by 8 padding cells with widths increasing by a factor of 1.3. In the vertical direction, the model comprises 50 layers, with thicknesses increasing by a factor of 1.15 from 30 m at the surface downward, yielding a total depth of ~217 km. The entire model volume spans 190 × 210 × 217 km and thus contains 110 × 122 × 50 cells (Supplementary Fig. 4). The inversion was initialized with a homogeneous half-space of 100 Ω·m and employed an optimized smoothing parameter of 0.3 in all three directions (x, y, z). Full-impedance data were logarithmically sampled at 20 periods between 320 Hz and 1818 s, with error floors set to 5% of |ZxyZyx|1/2 for off-diagonal and 10% of |ZxyZyx|1/2 for diagonal components. After 93 iterations, the inversion achieved a normalized root-mean-square (nRMS) misfit of 2.26. Topographic effects were not considered in the inversion. Given that the seismic events are predominantly clustered in the southwest, we have restricted the main-text figures to this specific sub-region to better highlight the interplay between electrical heterogeneities and seismicity. A comprehensive visualization of the inversion model across the entire survey area is available in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Fig. 5). For details on inversion parameters, data fitting, sensitivity tests of the resistivity anomalies, and additional information, please refer to the Supplementary Information.

Model setup

Our two-dimensional thermo-mechanical model spans 1200 km ×400 km and is discretized into 300 × 100 finite elements, populated by 750,000 Lagrangian particles (Fig. 3a). The computational domain comprises five physical layers: a 20 km-thick sticky-air layer, an upper and lower crust, and the lithospheric and asthenospheric mantles. The total crustal thickness is 50 km, evenly divided between the upper and lower crust. Crustal rheology follows a wet quartz flow law79, whereas both lithospheric and asthenospheric mantles are represented by dry olivine rheology47. Within the SCLM, we introduced multiple discrete vertical weak zones, each approximately 10 km in width, representing mechanically weakened loci that are preferentially susceptible to deformation under external perturbation. A 10-km-thick horizontal weak layer was additionally incorporated within the lithosphere to represent the widely recognized mid-lithospheric discontinuity. To capture lithosphere-scale weakness, we prescribe a 400 km-wide weak zone within the lithospheric mantle to simulate a major fault system. At the base of the asthenosphere, a semicircular thermal anomaly with a radius of 100 km and a temperature excess of 300 K relative to the ambient mantle is imposed to represent the initial mantle plume. Detailed rheological parameters are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The initial geotherm corresponds to a steady-state conductive thermal profile derived from the heat conduction equation under fixed boundary temperatures of 0 °C at the surface and 1300 °C at the base of the lithosphere, ensuring thermal equilibrium prior to model evolution80. Temperatures within the sub-lithospheric mantle were initialized using an adiabatic gradient of 0.5 °C/km for regions exceeding this temperature. The sticky-air layer serves as a free-surface approximation to allow topographic adjustment.
