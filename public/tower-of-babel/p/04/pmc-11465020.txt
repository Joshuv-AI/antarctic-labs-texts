Exploring the role of ADAMTSL2 across multiple cancer types: A pan-cancer analysis and validated in colorectal cancer
Authors: Qing-xin Yu, Rui-cheng Wu, Jie Wang, Zhou-Ting Tuo, Jun Yang, Yong-ping Zhang, Jing Jin, Quan Yuan, Chun-nian Wang, De-chao Feng, Deng-xiong Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent studies have established a correlation between ADAMTSL2 (ADAMTS-like 2) and the development of various cancers. Introduction

ADAMTS (a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs) proteases, zinc metalloendopeptidases predominantly targeting extracellular matrix (ECM) components, play crucial roles in various biological processes [1]. ADAMTSL2 (ADAMTS-like 2) is a notable member of the ADAMTS protein superfamily, playing roles in skeletal myogenesis regulation and implicated in Geleophysic dysplasia type 1 (GD1), a rare autosomal recessive disorder affecting the skeletal system [2, 3]. Recent studies have begun to shed light on ADAMTSL2's potential role in tumorigenesis, with observations linking its expression levels to prognosis in lung cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, and breast cancer [4–6]. However, a comprehensive understanding of ADAMTSL2's function and mechanism in tumorigenesis remains elusive.

Cancer continues to exert a substantial toll on public health, resulting in numerous fatalities each year [7, 8]. As our population ages, the burdens—physical, economic, mental, and political—imposed by cancer are poised to escalate [9]. Despite the availability of numerous cancer treatment drugs in clinical practice, their efficacy remains debated. There is still a need for the development of more effective new drugs and more precise targeted therapies [10]. Currently, publicly funded cancer genomics databases serve as invaluable resources in pan-cancer investigations [11, 12]. Leveraging these databases in conjunction with diverse genomics analyses facilitates a more comprehensive and profound understanding of the mechanisms driving tumor initiation and progression [13]. Pan-cancer analyses are crucial in cancer research as they help identify common molecular features and mechanisms across different tumor types, offering insights that could lead to broader therapeutic applications and the discovery of universal biomarkers [14, 15]. Additionally, pan-cancer analyses can more precisely guide clinical treatment based on predictive outcomes, helping to avoid overtreatment that might lead to resistant cancer phenotypes [16, 17].

Despite limited previous evidence suggesting that ADAMTSL2 may be involved in tumor development and progression, its role in cancer remains underexplored. We hypothesize that ADAMTSL2 may play a key role in the initiation and progression of various cancers and could serve as a potential prognostic biomarker and immunotherapy target in human malignancies. Therefore, our investigation delved into the ADAMTSL2 gene across a spectrum of cancers, employing diverse analyses to explore its role in cancer pathogenesis, clinical prognosis, regulatory networks, and immune landscapes utilizing data from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Furthermore, we conducted IHC experiments to investigate ADAMTSL2 expression in COADREAD and its correlation with clinicopathological parameters. This study contributes to a deeper understanding of ADAMTSL2 expression and its potential function across various cancers, and explore its prospects as a prognostic and immunological biomarker for malignancies, with colorectal cancer as an example.

Materials and methods

Differential and prognostic analysis

UCSC database (https://xenabrowser.net/) provided tumor and normal tissue data, including expression data and clinical data [18, 19]. Our pan-cancer analysis utilized curated data from various cancer types available in the UCSC database, which was extracted from TCGA. The sample with 0 expression or follow-up time less than 30 days were excluded. Then, a log2 (x + 0.001) transformation was performed on each expression quality, and a sample size of less than 10 cancer type would be eliminated [14]. Furthermore, differential significance analyses were assessed by the differential expression and unpaired Wilcoxon rank sum and signed rank tests.

Based on the median value of ADAMTSL2 expression, patients were divided into high and low expression groups. The prognostic value of ADAMTSL2 in pan-cancer was analyzed with the Cox proportional hazards regression model established by using overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), disease-free interval (DFI) and progression-free interval (PFI) indictors. Moreover, Unpaired Wilcoxon rank sum and signed rank tests and Kruskal test were used to assess the relationship between gene expression and clinical characteristics.

Tumor heterogeneity and stemness analysis

We assessed tumor heterogeneity by calculating tumor mutation burden (TMB), mutant-allele tumor heterogeneity (MATH), tumor ploidy, tumor purity, loss of heterozygosity (LOH), neoantigen (NEO), and homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) by using the Sangerbox platform (http://sangerbox.com) [20–22]. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient was used to compare the microsatellite instability (MSI) scores between groups with high and low ADAMTSL2 expression [23]. The tumor stemness was tested via tumor methylation and mRNA expression signatures. Spearman analysis compared six indexes (including DNA methylation based (DNAss), differentially methylated probes-based (DMPss), enhancer elements/DNA methylation-based (ENHss), RNA expression-based (RNAss), epigenetically regulated DNA methylation-based (EREG-METHss) and epigenetically regulated RNA methylation-based (EREG-METHss) between the high and low ADAMTSL2 expression groups.

Analysis of RNA modifications, mutation landscape, and tumor microenvironment

Firstly, we explored the expression of 36 stimulatory, 24 heterogeneous checkpoints, and 150 immune regulatory genes in high and low ADAMTSL2 groups [21]. Then, we assessed the correlation between ADAMTSL2 expression and 10 m1A genes, 13 m5C genes and 21 m6A genes expression. Each simple gene mutation was calculated by the Sangerbox based on the nucleotide variation dataset [22]. Of these, we exhibited the gene expression and mutations of BCLA, COAD, and STAD. To evaluate the difference in mutation frequency among each group of samples, the chi-square test was employed.

The Timer tool in the R package “IOBR” is used through the Sangerbox platform to explore the situation of immune cells in the tumor microenvironment [22, 24, 25]. Furthermore, the potential function of ADAMTSL2 in drug sensitivity was measured through GSCALite (the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) and the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP)) in pan-cancer [26].

Patients and clinical specimens

A total of 120 COADREAD patients who underwent surgery in our hospital from January 1, 2019 to December 31, 2019 were enrolled. All patients were pathologically diagnosed as intestinal adenocarcinoma, had no history of other malignant tumors, were younger than 18 years old or had a postoperative survival of less than 30 days, did not receive preoperative chemoradiotherapy and had complete clinical data. Clinical features were also collected, such as age, sex, TNM stages and so on. Clinical stages were determined according to the criteria of the 8th edition of the AJCC/UICC system (AJCC, American Joint Committee on Cancer/UICC, Union for International Cancer Control). Our study was approved by the Ethical Committee of our institution, and the ethics review was conducted (The ethical approval number: YJ-NBPC-LW2024-106). All patients provided written informed consent.

Immunohistochemical analysis

IHC was performed on paraffin-embedded 3 μm sections. Following deparaffinization with xylene and rehydration with a gradient of alcohol, antigen retrieval was carried out through heating in citrate buffer. To quench endogenous peroxidase activity, 3% H2O2 was applied, followed by blocking with 5% BSA. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with a primary antibody against ADAMTSL2 (diluted at 1:800, Cat. No: P07779, ProMab Biotechnology, USA) for 1.5 h at room temperature. After three washes, the sections were covered with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 30 min. Thereafter, specimens were stained with 3′,3-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride, counterstained with hematoxylin. After alcohol dehydration, xylene vitrification and neutral gum seal, sections were photographed using Leica 2500 microscope.

To semiquantitatively represent the ADAMTSL2 immunostaining results, evaluable sections were classified based on the proportions and intensity of cell staining [27, 28]. The proportion of stained cells were scored as 0 (no stained cells), 1 (1–20% of cancer cells were positively stained), 2 (21–50% were positively stained) and 3 (> 50% cells were positively stained). The intensity of the staining was recorded as 0 (no staining), 1 (light yellow), 2 (brownish yellow) and 3 (brown). IHC scores were calculated by multiplying the scores for proportion of stained cells by the scores of the staining intensity. IHC scores ≥ 4 were defined as high expression and < 4 as low expression. Two pathologists independently scored all stained sections.

Statistical analysis

One-way ANOVA or the Mann‒Whitney U test was employed to analyze three or more continuous variables based on the normality and quality of variances of the data. Student’s t test calculated quantitative data in two groups. All analyzed data are displayed as the standard deviation (SD). Pan-cancer data is analyzed through the Sangerbox platform [22]. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistical significance. ns, P ≥ 0.05; *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001.

Results

Differential expression and clinical characteristics of ADAMTSL2

Compared to adjacent non-cancerous tissues, ADAMTSL2 exhibited significant differential expression in various human cancers. Specifically, we observed significant upregulation in 9 types of tumors, including most digestive system tumors such as ESCA, STAD, COAD and READ, while downregulation was noted in 10 types of tumors, such as LUAD, HNSC, GBM and KICH (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, we analyzed the association of ADAMTSL2 in different cancers with gender, age, clinical stage, tumor grade, T stage, N stage, and M stage groups (Supplement Fig. 1A–E). The results showed that ADAMTSL2 expression was significantly correlated with tumor stage and T stage in COADREAD, PAAD and KIRC (Fig. 1B, C).

Prognosis and diagnosis value of ADAMTSL2 in pan-cancer

To explore the possible prognostic value of ADAMTSL2, we examined the relationship between ADAMTSL2 expression and prognosis in patients with various cancers. As shown in the forest plot, ADAMTSL2 expression was positively correlated with the hazard ratio of OS, DSS, DFI and PFI for ESCA and COADREAD (p < 0.05), indicating that ADAMTSL2 may be a risk factor for patients with the above digestive system tumors. However, ADAMTSL2 expression was negatively associated with risk ratios of OS, DSS, and PFI in KIRC, suggesting that ADAMTSL2 may be a protective factor for KIRC (p < 0.05) and the effect of ADAMTSL2 in different tumors is inconsistent (Fig. 1D, E; Supplement Fig. 1F, G).

Relationship of ADAMTSL2 with tumor heterogeneity and stemness

We further investigated the correlation between the expression level of ADAMTSL2 and tumor heterogeneity and stemness (Fig. 2, Supplement Fig. 2 and Supplement Table 1). MSI and NEO are indicators of tumor response to immunotherapy. Our study revealed significant correlations between ADAMTSL2 expression levels and MSI status in COAD (r = − 0.37, p < 0.001) and STAD (r = − 0.33, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). In terms of NEO, we observed negative correlations between ADAMTSL2 expression and in COAD (r = − 0.31, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). However, in terms of MATH, we observed positive correlations between ADAMTSL2 expression and in COAD (r = 0.188, p < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). Additionally, we found a positive correlation between ADAMTSL2 expression and tumor purity in KIPAN (r = − 0.34, p < 0.001) and ACC (r = − 0.32, p < 0.01). In terms among DNAss, EREG-MATHss, DMPss and ENHss, we found a positive correlation between ADAMTSL2 mRNA expression and TGCT, PCPG and DLBC (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2E–H).

Mutation analysis

Next, we analyzed the ADAMTSL2 mutation status in pan-cancer. As shown in Fig. 3A, ADAMTSL2 alteration was observed in 25 cancers, with a mutation frequency of 5.0% for COAD, 3.5% for UCS, 2.9% for SKCM and 2.9% for SKCM. In BLCA, significant gene mutations includingTP53, KMT2D, SYNE1, SYNE2 and DNAH7 were observed between high- and low- ADAMTSL2 expression groups (Fig. 3B). APC, TP53, MUC16, SYNE1 and TTN were found to have a significant difference in COAD (Fig. 3C). Similarly, KDM5C, SLITRK and ABCC exhibited a markable variation in KIRC (Fig. 3D).

Relationship between ADAMTSL2 expression and immunomodulatory, RNA modifications and tumor microenvironment

Our results show that ADAMTSL2 expression is significantly positively correlated with many RNA-modified genes in many malignant tumors, such as urinary and digestive tumors (Fig. 4A). According to the results of TIMER analysis, the expression of ADAMTSL2 was positively correlated with several immune cells in urinary tumors, such as PAAD, LUSC and PRAD (Fig. 4B).

Similarly, ADAMTSL2 was positively associated with abundant immune checkpoints in PAAD, ESCA, and COAD (Fig. 4D).

Validation of the clinical value of ADAMTSL2 in COADREAD

ADAMTSL2 was different in colorectal cancer and pan-cancer, and ADAMTSL2 expression was correlated with the prognosis of colorectal cancer patients. Moreover, elevated ADAMTSL2 mRNA and higher mutation rate have been observed in COADREAD patients. Therefore, we conducted immunohistochemistry experiments on tumor tissues from these patients to further explore the relationship between ADAMTSL2 protein expression and their clinicopathological parameters. Based on immunostaining intensity, the level of ADAMTSL2 protein in the tumor tissues and the surrounding nontumor tissues was scored. ADAMTSL2 was observed to be primarily localized in the cytoplasm of colorectal tumor cells (Fig. 5A, B), and much higher immunostaining for ADAMTSL2 protein was observed in most of the tumor tissues of COADREAD patients in both paired (Fig. 5C) and unpaired (Fig. 5D) comparisons.

Discussion

ADAMTS proteases are zinc metalloendopeptidases, most of whose substrates are extracellular matrix (ECM) components [1]. ADAMTSL2 (ADAMTS-like 2), located on chromosome 9q34, is a member of ADAMTS proteins superfamily [2]. ADAMTSL2 is a secreted protein in the extracellular matrix, and is regulated during skeletal myogenesis [2]. Mutations of ADAMTSL2 cause Geleophysic dysplasia type 1 (GD1), a rare autosomal recessive disorder [3]. GD1 primarily affects bones, joints and skin, and cardiac and respiratory dysfunction, often leading to early mortality [1]. In recent years, studies have suggested that ADAMTSL2 is related to the occurrence and development of tumors. Lee et al. found that ADAMTSL2 expression was low in lung cancer tumors compared to normal sites, and that low ADAMTSL2 expression was associated with poor prognosis [4]. Another study indicated that ADAMTSL2 might be one of hub genes implicated in hepatocellular carcinoma prognosis and immune infiltration [5]. Tang et al. introduced ADAMTSL2 as a component of the disulfide collapse-related score, which could be a novel risk factor for predicting breast cancer prognosis [6].

So far, little is known about the potential function and mechanism of ADAMTSL2 in tumors. In this study, we indicated that the role of ADAMTSL2 in cancer is not confined to a single tumor type. We conducted a comprehensive and multifaceted analysis of ADAMTSL2 expression in pan-cancer patients, including its correlation with clinical characteristics, tumor heterogeneity, stemness, genetic mutations, RNA-modifying genes and immune infiltration. In addition, the expression of ADAMTSL2 protein in COADREAD patient tissues was verified by IHC.

By investigating the expression-based panoramic picture of ADAMTSL2 in different cancer types, we found that the expression of ADAMTSL2 was significantly upregulated in several digestive system cancers, including COADREAD, ESCA, and STAD, when compared to their corresponding normal tissues. While was significantly downregulated in some cancer types, such as LUAD. This finding is consistent with the existing studies that have reported [4]. Remarkably, the expression of ADAMTSL2 increased with the progression of tumor stage and T stage in COADREAD and PAAD. All these results strongly suggested that ADAMTSL2 might act as a risk factor for tumor progression, especially in digestive tract tumors.

Colorectal cancer ranks as the third most common malignancy worldwide and is the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, and the most common histological classification of colorectal cancer is adenocarcinoma [29, 30]. Currently, treatment for colorectal cancer primarily involves radical resection and chemotherapy [31]. However, surgical resection is invasive and costly, while some COADREAD patients experience resistance to chemotherapy or immunotherapy [32]. Chemotherapy resistance in cancer patients is associated with various factors, including changes in the immune microenvironment and the presence of tumor heterogeneity [33, 34]. Analyzing tumor heterogeneity is critical for studying tumor growth, metastasis, apoptosis, and drug resistance, and has important implications for identifying potential therapies [14, 35, 36]. MATH is a novel, non-biased, quantitative method to assess intra-tumor heterogeneity based on tumor next generation exome sequencing [36, 37]. Our study demonstrated a positive correlation between the expression level of ADAMTSL2 and MATH in COAD. Studies suggest that tumors with high MATH scores are associated with an increased risk of metastases, reduced immune cell infiltration, diminished immune response activation, and lower survival rates [38, 39]. Besides, NEO is indicator of tumor response to immunotherapy. And low NEO expression underlies early immune escape in colorectal cancer [40, 41]. Therefore, our results may serve as a valuable reference for the selection of immunotherapy approaches. Therefore, our results may provide a valuable reference for metastasis risk assessment, survival prediction and immunotherapy selection.

Specific gene mutations are often potential biomarkers for interpreting a patient’s prognosis and also help predict the effect of drug therapy on cancer patients [42, 43]. Somatic mutations are crucial in the development of COADREAD [44, 45]. The TTN gene transcribes mRNA that encodes the protein titin. Mutations in TTN have been linked to a variety of diseases, such as dilated cardiomyopathy, centronuclear myopathy, and LUSC [46–48]. One study showed that TTN mutations were inversely associated with prognosis in COADREAD patients [44]. The results of Liu et al. show that patients with the TTN/OBSCN “Double-Hit” phenotype have better prognosis, stronger tumor immune response, and superior immunotherapy outcomes compared to other phenotypes [49]. Our study suggests that different expressions of ADAMTSL2 in COADREAD may be associated with different mutations of TTN, indicating that the expression of ADAMTSL2 may also be correlated with immunotherapy effects in COADREAD patients.

Given that ADAMTSL2 is associated with colorectal cancer in various analyses, we conducted immunohistochemical tests on 120 patients with colorectal cancer in our institution. The results showed that ADAMTSL2 expression was correlated with T stage, N stage, M stage, presence of perineural invasion and vascular tumor thrombus. Additionally, the high ADAMTSL2 group had shorter OS and progression-free survival times compared to the low ADAMTSL2 group. These results suggested that ADAM may be a prognostic marker and a new therapeutic target for COADREAD.

However, it must be acknowledged that our work has certain limitations. First, the reliability of our analysis results may be slightly affected by differences between databases. Moreover, our study is based on the analysis of public databases and, while it covers a variety of tumor types, further experiments are needed to clarify the specific roles and mechanisms of ADAMTSL2 in different tumors. Furthermore, as this was a retrospective study with a relatively small patient sample, there was a possibility of result bias, which necessitates validation through larger-scale experimental studies.

This study not only reveals the role of ADAMTSL2 in the development and progression of tumors but also comprehensively assesses its potential as a prognostic marker and therapeutic target from various perspectives. In particular, the abnormal overexpression of ADAMTSL2 in digestive system tumors and the relationship between expression and prognosis, such as esophageal cancer, are worthy of further experimental verification and exploration. The results of IHC analysis and bioinformatics studies in colorectal cancer patients showed that ADAMTSL2 might be a good guide to stratification of patients based on prediction of prognosis and treatment effect. Patients with low ADAMTSL2 expression may have longer survival, while immunotherapy for patients with high ADAMTSL2 expression may have better therapeutic outcomes. This study laid the foundation for the future development of ADAMTSL2 as a potential therapeutic target, and further research is needed to fully explore and validate its role in cancer treatment.

Conclusions

In summary, we conducted a comprehensive pan-cancer analysis of ADAMTSL2 and we demonstrated that ADAMTSL2 may serve as a novel prognostic biomarker and immunotherapy target in COADREAD.
