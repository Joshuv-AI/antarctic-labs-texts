Research advances in mushroom umami: substance characteristics, multidimensional attributes, umami peptide screening, and umami assessment
Authors: Xianpu Wang, Honggao Liu, Jieqing Li, Yuanzhong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mushrooms have become an indispensable food worldwide due to their high nutritional value and unique flavor profile, with umami being their most distinctive taste characteristic.This article introduces various umami substances in mushrooms and their characteristics, including proteins, umami peptides, amino acids, organic acids, and 5′-nucleotides. And reviewing umami receptors and umami synergy. In addition, we summarized various factors affecting the umami of mushrooms, focusing on various processing methods and storage methods, among others. The process of screening and identification of mushroom umami peptides is summarized, including machine learning and molecular docking, which are effective methods for high-throughput screening of umami peptides, as well as a variety of taste evaluation methods that have been applied to the evaluation of umami. For mushrooms, more attention should be paid to umami peptides and intrinsic factors influencing umami taste in the future, which will help the development of mushroom food products.

Introduction

Mushrooms, which are the macroscopic fruiting bodies of certain fungi, primarily belong to the phyla Basidiomycota and Ascomycota (Rizzo et al., 2021; Wijayawardene et al., 2024). They exhibit a rich variety and possess significant commercial value. It is estimated that there are more than 14,000 species of mushrooms, of which about 3000 are considered edible, and 270 have therapeutic potential (El Sheikha and Hu, 2018). Mushrooms have long been used as a functional and nutritious food due to they are low in calories and fat, rich supply of carbohydrates, protein, lipid, mineral, and vitamin content, rich in micronutrients that are beneficial to health and have been the human diet for thousands of years (Wang et al., 2014; Huang et al., 2022). Mushrooms are regarded as both food and medicine by people in different geographical regions of the world (Rizzo et al., 2021), and they are now increasingly recognized as a food and medicine continuum (Yao et al., 2023). Mushrooms are not merely rich in macronutrients and micronutrients, but also in a variety of bioactive components, including polysaccharides, dietary fiber, ergosterols, flavonoids, proteins, peptides, terpenoids, and polyphenols, which not only endow mushrooms with high nutritional value but also with high bioactivity (Kalač, 2009; Yan et al., 2018; Khursheed et al., 2020; Peer and Kuehnelt, 2024; Rousta et al., 2024). The ability to treat and prevent several chronic diseases such as cancer, diabetes, cardiovascular and obesity when used as part of the daily diet are functions that are played by the nutrients and bioactive compounds in mushrooms (Roncero-Ramos and Delgado-Andrade, 2017; Sun et al., 2020; Khursheed et al., 2020; Sunil and Xu, 2022).

Nowadays, mushrooms are playing an increasingly important role in the food industry, serving as a high-quality alternative to animal protein (Roncero-Ramos and Delgado-Andrade, 2017; Pastrana-Pastrana et al., 2025). Advances in cultivation and processing technologies have significantly enhanced the yield and nutritional quality of mushroom protein (Ayimbila and Keawsompong, 2023; Wang et al., 2023). Replacing meat with mushrooms not only meets human nutritional needs but also reduces reliance on animal husbandry, enabling more efficient and cost-effective production while minimizing environmental impact (González et al., 2020). Mushrooms excel both nutritionally and in terms of flavor, helping to mitigate the health risks associated with excessive animal protein consumption (Pérez-Montes et al., 2021; Gou et al., 2025). Their umami characteristics stem from abundant glutamic acid, aspartic acid, and 5′-nucleotides (Aisala et al., 2018), which directly influence food choice preferences (Sun et al., 2020) and reflect nutritional value (Wang et al., 2020). This makes mushrooms more readily accepted by consumers compared to other plant-based proteins (Hess et al., 2017).

Umami, recognized as the fifth basic taste following sweet, salty, bitter, and sour, was first identified in kelp broth (Ikeda, 2002). Since then, research on umami has proliferated. The umami taste in mushrooms is mainly provided by free amino acids, umami peptides, 5′-nucleotides, and organic acids (Li et al., 2014; Hu et al., 2020). A systematic summary of umami substances in mushrooms holds paramount importance for the development and research of mushroom foods. Taste contains information about food and plays a key role in chemical perception (Wang et al., 2020). Current research has focused predominantly on the taste information of umami substances, while largely overlooking the association between their intrinsic properties and nutritional or bioactive functions. Therefore it is important to study the link between umami and the nutritional value and bioactivity of mushrooms. Umami peptides are readily found in the hydrolysates of mushrooms. Summarizing the conformational relationships and roles of umami peptides in mushrooms can help to study umami peptides from mushrooms in depth. In the food production process, various drying and cooking methods are used to process mushrooms (Zheng et al., 2023), and different enzymes can be used to hydrolyze the proteins of mushrooms to enhance the flavor of mushroom products. Additionally, different storage methods are employed during food storage. All these factors can affect the umami characteristics of mushrooms.So it is crucial to deeply explore these factors in the processing and production of mushroom foods in terms of umami. With the advancement of machine learning and deep learning, their integration with molecular docking enables high-throughput screening of umami peptides (Gu et al., 2024a), marking that mushroom umami peptide screening has entered the era of high-throughput screening (Xu et al., 2024). In recent years, umami peptides from mushrooms have been continuously identified, but no one has systematically summarized the screening process for mushroom umami peptides. Summarizing new methods is crucial for the screening of mushroom ummi peptides. Umami perception is based on chemosensory transduction mechanisms, where taste information is acquired through molecular recognition by receptors (Wang et al., 2020). The umami taste of mushrooms should be accurately evaluated, with diverse assessment methods applicable across studies. Notably, machine learning-based sensory prediction models are emerging and hold promise for future application in umami evaluation.

In this study, mushroom umami substances are presented. For umami peptides, we reviewed the Structure-activity relationships of umami peptides by summarizing umami peptide studies in mushrooms. We also summarized the mechanisms of umami receptors and, building on this foundation, systematized the synergistic effects between umami substances—including receptor-mediated interactions—and the salt-reduction benefits derived from umami synergy. In addition, various factors affecting the freshness and flavor of mushrooms were described. Finally, the paper discusses the methods of screening and identification of umami peptides and umami evaluation of mushrooms. At present, the research on the umami flavor of mushrooms has become a hot topic in the food industry, necessitating a systematic synthesis of existing findings to provide a reference for in-depth research and application of umami in mushrooms.

Characteristics and functions of mushroom umami substances

A large amount of umami substances naturally exist in mushrooms. (Kong et al., 2019). Umami occupies a vital position in the flavor composition of mushrooms, which makes it a traditional seasoning and popular among consumers. The mushroom umami has a great market potential due to the characteristics and importance. More and more mushrooms are now being analyzed for their umami components and further extracted, Hericium erinaceus, Pleurotus eryngii, Agrocybe aegerita, Lentinus edodes, Cordyceps flower, Antler fungus, Agaricus blazei murill (Yang et al., 2022), Pleurotus cystidiosus, Agrocybe cylindracea, Agaricus blazei, Pleurotus eryngii, Coprinus comatus (Zhang et al., 2018), Suillus granulatus (Zhao et al., 2020), Stropharia rugoso-annulata (Hu et al., 2020), Lactarius camphoratus、Boletus edulis, Cantharellus cibarius, Craterellus tubaeformis (Manninen et al., 2018), Morchella sextelata (Gao et al., 2021), and Morel Mushrooms (Rotzoll et al., 2006), among others.

The low-molecular-weight water-soluble compounds are the primary contributors to the umami taste in mushrooms, which include free amino acids, such as the MSG-like amino acids Glu and Asp, and flavorful nucleotides, 5′-nucleotides (Gao et al., 2021). All of these umami substances are involved in the production of umami in mushrooms. Organic acids, such as succinic acid, have umami properties, as well as citric and malic acids assist in enhancing the umami taste in mushrooms (Li et al., 2014; Hu et al., 2020). Furthermore, the umami peptides existing in the hydrolysate of mushroom protein possess the characteristics of imparting umami taste or enhancing the umami taste in mushrooms (Zhang et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2023).

Umami is the recognition of amino acids and oligopeptides, and this taste is related to the protein content in food (Wang et al., 2020). The umami components in mushrooms are integral parts of their distinctive umami taste and also have the function of essential nutrients and bioactive substances, such as proteins, peptides, and amino acids. As functional food components, they address dual human requirements by satisfying nutritional needs and gustatory preferences, while their bioactive properties demonstrate exceptional potential in health preservation and therapeutic interventions for chronic diseases. Umami substances also possess biological activity. Some studies have shown that umami molecules, such as umami peptides, nucleotides, and organic acids, exhibit antiviral biological activity and virus-inhibitory activity (Cui et al., 2025a). However, there are fewer studies on the bioactivity of umami substances, especially in mushrooms. As one of the possible major food products in the future, It is necessary to study the role of flavor in the bioactivity of mushrooms.

Protein - the precursor of umami

Protein is an important macronutrient for maintaining the optimal functioning of the human body and for influencing the nutritional value that ultimately determines the quality of food. Mushrooms are a sustainable source for the production of high-quality proteins due to their lower cost and ease of processing compared to meat (González et al., 2020; Kumar et al., 2022; Mayirnao et al., 2025). The human preference for umami perception may originate from its physiological association with protein metabolism, as the intensity of umami often indicates a high protein content in food, thereby contributing to the fulfillment of the body's nutritional requirements (Zhu et al., 2023). A study employed linear regression to analyze the association between taste intensity and nutritional components, and the results indicated that umami was positively correlated with protein content (Martin & Issanchou). As a protein source, mushrooms can readily meet the protein quantities specified in recommended dietary intakes (Wang and Zhao, 2023). Simultaneously satisfying the dual demand for umami perception and nutrition. Compared to plant proteins, mushroom proteins typically possess complete essential amino acid profiles. When serving as animal protein substitutes, plant proteins often lack one or multiple essential amino acids and generally exhibit lower quality. This distinctive characteristic enables mushroom proteins to fulfill dietary requirements better while offering greater economic advantages compared to both animal and plant protein sources (Ayimbila and Keawsompong, 2023). The fact that peptides and amino acids, as products of hydrolysis from proteins, are produced in large quantities when mushrooms are used as a food, means that mushrooms are richer in flavor layers and more palatable than plants.

Umami peptides

In mushrooms, peptides include not just bioactive peptides. Umami peptides are characterized by specific structural configurations and are bioactive compounds capable of imparting distinct umami characteristics to food matrices (Zhang et al., 2019a). These taste-active molecules typically exist as oligopeptides composed of amino acids interconnected through peptide bonds, functioning as critical intermediates in both protein degradation pathways and amino acid synthesis processes. Particularly in mushroom-derived products, umami peptides play pivotal roles in flavor enhancement while demonstrating exceptional processing adaptability. Their unique molecular interactions substantially contribute to achieving harmonious flavor integration, refined taste profiles, and velvety mouthfeel characteristics in final food products (Zhao et al., 2019). Studies have shown that umami peptides possess unique characteristics. After binding with taste receptors, they do not trigger taste perception but merely enhance the umami taste of food (Jiang et al., 2023). Flavor peptides in mushrooms.

Umami peptides are biologically active and can exert active effects on the human body. There are two ways for umami peptides to exert bioactivity, one is that the umami peptides themselves have bioactivity, for example, Zhang et al. (2024) peptides from Huangshan floral mushroom water by ultrafiltration have antioxidant activity, and the peptides with 1–3 kDa have the best umami taste and the highest antioxidant activity. And a study has screened out umami peptides with angiotensin I-converting enzyme and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitory activities from Stropharia rugosoannulata (Li et al., 2024). Another type of umami peptide exerts its biological activity after hydrolysis in the human body. Hao et al. (2020) systematically compiled structural data of identified umami peptides, revealing a predominant incorporation of hydrophobic amino acids (Gly, Ala, Val, Leu, Ile, Pro, Phe, Trp, Met) in these bioactive sequences. The researchers hypothesized that such hydrophobic residues may correlate with angiotensin I-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitory potency, while simultaneously observing the enrichment of antioxidative amino acids (Pro, Tyr, Trp, Phe) in a subset of umami-active peptides. Notably, leveraging pepsin's cleavage preference for hydrophobic residues, the study postulated that gastrointestinal digestion of these peptides could yield bioactive fragments with enhanced physiological relevance. Through in silico simulation of gastrointestinal metabolism, three umami peptides (CC, CCNK, HCHT) were identified to retain dual functionalities—ACE inhibition and antioxidant capacity—post-digestion.

The structure-activity relationship of umami peptides

The relationship between the amino acid composition of umami peptides and umami taste

Peptides are composed of amino acids, and different amino acid sequences can have an important effect on peptides. Zhuang et al. (2016) demonstrated through a comparative analysis of peptide solutions and their constituent amino acid mixtures that the taste profile of oligopeptides is determined by their specific amino acid sequence rather than merely by the presence of individual amino acid components. The study highlighted that the mere presence of a particular amino acid within a peptide does not exclusively dictate the flavor profile of the peptide itself. Most umami peptides contain hydrophilic amino acids, which generally contribute to the expression of umami taste. For instance, umami amino acids (UAAs) such as Glu and Asp have an umami taste themselves and directly contribute to the umami of peptides. Sweet amino acids such as Ser, Ala, Gly, and Thr do not have an umami taste themselves, but they can modulate the sweetness of peptides and enhance the umami of peptides (Zhuang et al., 2016). Hydrophobic amino acids, such as Leu, Met, and Phe, usually have a bitter taste and can affect the expression of umami (Zhang et al., 2021).

The umami characteristics of peptides are influenced by their bioactive fragments and amino acid species. Studies have indicated that the umami potency specifically correlates with the Glu and Asp residues in peptide sequences (Iwaniak and Minkiewicz, 2016). Ramalingam et al. (2019) demonstrated that peptides derived from the self-condensation of Glu, Gln, Asp, or Asn, or from their conjugation with Thr, Ser, Ala, Gly, or Met, exhibit umami taste properties. The key residues in the umami receptor T1R1 can well recognize acidic amino acids, enabling umami peptides to obtain higher affinity and thus increase umami (Toda et al., 2013; Huang et al., 2025). For short-chain peptides (dipeptides and tripeptides) and umami peptides with 4–7 amino acid residues, when they contain Asp or Glu residues, regardless of their position in the peptide (N-terminal, C-terminal or in the middle), the residues Asp and Glu are key active sites (Wang et al., 2022). Furthermore, in longer peptides (those exceeding 7 amino acids), despite the complexity and heterogeneity of their amino acid composition leading to diverse active site profiles, Asp and Glu remain the predominant functional residues (Wang et al., 2022). Consistently, studies have indicated that for short-chain peptides, umami taste is largely governed by amino acid composition, with umami-specific residues (Asp and Glu) being indispensable (Li et al., 2015). Notably, even in the absence of Glu or Asp, Arg or Lys at the C-terminus consistently functions as an active site in peptides (Wang et al., 2022).

The molecular weight and spatial structure of umami peptides in relation to umami taste

Research demonstrates that peptides with molecular weights below 3 kDa demonstrate strong correlations with umami and salty characteristics, whereas those within the 3–10 kDa range predominantly associate with bitter and astringent sensory profiles (Gao et al., 2021). Additionally, umami intensity is inversely correlated with the molecular weight distribution of peptides. Peptides with a molecular weight below 3 kDa constitute the key taste-active fraction, particularly contributing to umami perception (Amin et al., 2020). During the hydrolysis of mushroom proteins, medium-length peptides become predominant at a certain stage. Peptides within this molecular weight range tend to expose hydrophobic amino acid residues, thereby eliciting bitter taste activity (Gao et al., 2021). Zhang et al. (2024) reported analogous findings, showing that ultrafiltered polypeptides from enzymatic hydrolysates—specifically peptides <1 kDa and 1–3 kDa fractions—displayed distinct umami characteristics. Furthermore, Wang et al. (2022) revealed through analysis of 205 umami peptides that short-chain peptides composed of 2–3 amino acids accounted for 44 % of the total umami-active sequences. Generally, when the molecular weight of a peptide is excessively high, its complex spatial structure may hinder the access of taste-active motifs to taste receptors, resulting in minimal contribution to taste perception (Gao et al., 2021). Nonetheless, recent molecular dynamics research has demonstrated that the length of umami peptides is significantly negatively correlated with the free energy of binding calculated by MM/PBSA and SMD. The longer the length of the umami peptide, the greater the negative the calculated free energy of binding, the higher the molecular weight, and the higher the affinity between the umami peptide and the receptor (Meng et al., 2024). In the reality of umami perception, the length of the umami peptide does not play a key role. Li et al. (2020) manifested that umami peptides comprising four or more amino acid residues exhibit no positive correlation in connection with their umami intensity and the chain length of amino acid residues in the peptide structure in the research. According to Tables 1 and it can be seen that both short-chain and long-chain peptides can exhibit umami, which indicates that the molecular weight of umami peptides is not the decisive factor influencing the perception of umami taste. It is just one of the important factors affecting the umami taste.

Gustatory properties of long-chain peptides are governed by tertiary conformations rather than primary sequences (Su et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2024). As the peptide chain elongates, the spatial structure of the peptide exerts a progressively stronger influence on umami taste. The increase in its length significantly enhances the complexity of receptor recognition of the peptide, with contributing factors encompassing the types of amino acids constituting the peptide, their positional arrangement, the properties of amino acids distributed outside the 3D spatial structure, and their side chain groups (Li et al., 2015). The sequence of amino acids can affect the umami taste of peptides. For instance, peptides with acidic amino acids at the N-terminal and basic amino acids at the C-terminal exhibit diverse taste characteristics, the dipeptide composed of glutamic acid and lysine, with glutamic acid at the N-terminal, has an umami taste; conversely, it lacks umami taste when glutamic acid is not at the N-terminal (Su et al., 2024).

Amino acids

Amino acids, the basic units of peptides and proteins, are present in large quantities in mushrooms in free form and are highly relevant to flavour and biological activity. Moreover, the catabolic metabolism of these amino acids has a very close influence on the flavor and aroma of mushrooms (Ardö, 2006). Different components of free amino acids (FAAs) have distinct flavor contributions and contribute significantly to the freshness and sweetness of mushrooms. MSG-like FAAs include L-Aspartic and L-Glutamic; sweet FAAs include L-Alanine, Glycine, L-Serine, and L-Threonine; Bitter FAAs L-Arginine, L-Histidine, L-Isoleucine, L-Leucine, L-Methionone, L-Phenylalanine, L-Tryptophan, L-Tyrosine and L-Valine (Manninen et al., 2018). FAAs flavor profiles vary depending on their structural features such as size, the chirality of the alpha carbon, charge, functional groups of the side chains, and hydrophobicity, and their flavor attributes have been classified as sweet, umami, bitter, and tasteless (Chen et al., 2015; Kawai et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2009). The flavor mechanism of FAAs is a five-membered ring structure formed by electrostatic interaction between two functional groups, NH3+ and COO-, which can be perceived by fresh taste receptors (Ardö, 2006). Moreover, research indicates that the amino acids in mushrooms also enhance their aroma, such as dimethyl trisulfide and 3-methylthiopropanal produced by the Maillard reaction between Met and nucleic acids, which imparts a meaty flavor to mushrooms (Yang et al., 2025). Additionally, amino acids themselves possess biological activity. Tryptophan, a precursor to neurotransmitters such as melatonin and serotonin, which regulate mood and circadian rhythms, is abundant in mushrooms (López et al., 2025).

Nucleotide

Non-protein nitrogenous compounds in mushrooms, such as purine bases, also contribute to their overall flavor profile (Phan et al., 2018). Flavorful nucleotides are a typical source of flavor in mushrooms. Consist of 5′-adenosine monophosphate (5′- AMP), 5′-inosine monophosphate (5′-IMP), 5′-cytosine monophosphate (5′-CMP), 5′-guanosine monophosphate (5′-GMP), 5′-xanthosine monophosphate (5′-XMP) and 5′-uridine monophosphate (5′-UMP) (Yang et al., 2022; Samarasiri and Chen, 2024). However, among 5′-nucleotides and their derivatives, only purine nucleotide exhibit umami taste, whereas pyrimidine nucleotides lack this flavor property (Zhao et al., 2019). When the hydroxyl group of the phosphate ester undergoes deprotonation, the resulting nucleotide exhibits umami taste, whereas esterification or amidation of the hydroxyl group eliminates this flavor property (Zhao et al., 2019).The 5′-IMP, 5′-GMP, and 5′-AMP, mentioned above, have an umami profile, whereas 5′-UMP and 5′-CMP, two nucleotides that do not do well for their freshness, and can only function by helping to accentuate umami through synergistic interactions with MSG (Yamaguchi, 1991; Arai et al., 1972; Zhao et al., 2019).

Organic acids

Organic acids and their derivatives impart acidic and astringent flavors to edible mushrooms, thus they also contribute to the development of complex and distinctive flavors in edible mushrooms. Moreover, succinic acid and its derivatives in mushrooms contribute to umami (Hadar and Dosoretz, 1991).When succinic acid combines with other organic acids such as citric acid, its umami taste can be enhanced (Zhao et al., 2019). Organic acids also play important biological roles in mushrooms. For instance, malic acid, citric acid and succinic acid have antioxidant properties and can prevent the enzymatic darkening process during the harvesting, marketing, storage and processing of mushrooms (Jabłońska-Ryś et al., 2022).

Receptors and multifunctionality of umami

Umami receptors

The five basic tastes are mediated through the activation of taste receptor cells, which are organized into taste buds distributed across various papillae on the epithelial surfaces of the tongue and palate (Behrens et al., 2011). Studies on ultrastructure and patterns of gene expression and cellular function have revealed that each taste bud can be categorized into three distinct anatomical types of TRCs based on their different functions: Type I, Type II, and Type III (Murray, 1986). Bitter, sweet, and umami stimuli are detected by Type II cells, which serve as the predominant cellular mediators in both taste discrimination and signal transduction (Zhang et al., 2003; DeFazio et al., 2006; Ahmad and Dalziel, 2020). Acidic stimuli are transduced through Type III cells, which form chemical synapses with afferent nerve fibers to mediate taste perception via G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) signaling pathways (Ye et al., 2016; Roper and Chaudhari, 2017). The cellular mechanism underlying salty taste detection remains uncharacterized within taste bud cells (Chandrashekar et al., 2010). Humans perceive umami as an indicator of protein content in food (Zhu et al., 2023), studies have shown that umami is positively correlated with protein content (Martin and Issanchou, 2019), so the recognition of umami by taste receptors is important for protein acquisition in humans.

The perception of different tastes depends on the corresponding receptors, and umami has its specific receptor. Within the type 1 taste receptor (T1R) family, the T1R2/T1R3 and T1R1/T1R3 heterodimeric complexes mediate sweet and umami taste perception, respectively (Nuemket et al., 2017). Sweet and umami compounds are transduced by a subset of class C G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), including T1R1, T1R2, and T1R3. The umami sensation is initiated by ligand binding to the T1R1/T1R3 heterodimer, a receptor complex within the T1R family. This recognition process involves the extracellular ligand-binding domain (LBD), which detects umami substances such as amino acids, peptides, and nucleotides (Li et al., 2002). Residues N69, D147, R151, A170, S172, S276, and R277 in the T1R1 receptor have been identified as critical for umami peptide binding (Wang et al., 2022). Similar to other G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), T1R1 and T1R3 exhibit a canonical seven-transmembrane (7TM) helical domain. Additionally, they possess a large extracellular Venus flytrap (VFT) domain, which serves as the primary binding site for prototypical ligands. Initial structural models hypothesized that members of this receptor family adopt homodimeric conformations in their active states (Temussi, 2009). Numerous studies have investigated the interaction of umami with the receptor T1R1/T1R3 using a variety of techniques, a consensus has been reached, umami compounds bind to the ligand-binding pocket of the T1R1 VFT domain, inducing conformational changes in the receptor protein that stabilize the T1R1-VFT complex in a closed conformation. (Meng et al., 2024), with T1R3 playing a main supporting role (Pin et al., 2003). In addition, of the peptides studied by Dang et al. (2019a), only the dipeptide was able to bind to T1R1, interacting with the Ser303 and Arg344 residues. All of the peptides analyzed were able to bind to T1R3, interacting with the Glu277 and Arg303 residues in the salty and sour peptides. The diversity of taste transduction mechanisms has been shown to highlight the complexity of the taste pathway. Certainly, for the umami peptides produced by different species of mushrooms, the recognition sites of the receptors will have unique patterns. For example, for Stropharia rugosoannulata, ASP147, and ASP219 are the key amino acid residues for the recognition of umami peptides by T1R1, and ARG64, GLU45, and GLU48 are the key amino acid residues for the recognition of umami peptides by T1R3 (Li et al., 2015). In a mouse experiment, mice could still perceive umami after being knocked out of the gene encoding G proteins, suggesting that the signaling of umami is not entirely dependent on G proteins to mediate and that the mechanism of umami transduction needs to be further elucidated (Kim et al., 2014).

Other than T1R1-T1R3, umami taste receptors also exist in taste bud cells. The eight genes encoding metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluRs) are categorized into three distinct groups based on phylogenetic analysis of sequence homology, pharmacological profiling, and functional coupling to intracellular signaling cascades: Class I mGluR includes mGluR5 and mGluR1; Class II mGluR includes mGluR2 and mGluR3; Class III mGluR includes mGluR4, mGluR6, mGluR7, and mGluR8. Among these, there are two umami taste receptors: the N-terminal truncated taste-specific variants of mGluR1 and mGluR4, both of which are activated by glutamate concentrations in food (Chaudhari et al., 2000, 2009; Kunishima et al., 2000; San Gabriel et al., 2009).

Synergy

Among the five basic tastes, the most distinctive feature of umami is its synergistic effect (Zhang et al., 2008). Natural and processed foods contain various types of umami and other flavor compounds. The different combinations of these simple compounds can cause subtle differences in the perception of umami. New compounds are constantly being discovered, which may not only act as potent umami compounds but also enhance the umami of known compounds (Beksan et al., 2003). When umami compounds are present in food, they interact with other components in the food (Baryłko-Pikielna and Kostyra, 2007). Sweetness, umami, and bitterness are mediated by G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) (Chandrashekar et al., 2010). The umami and sweet taste receptors exhibit functional interrelatedness, both originating from the T1R (taste receptor type 1) family proteins. These chemosensory signals synergistically mediate the induction of taxis behavioral responses in organisms (Chandrashekar et al., 2006). The synergistic umami effect of Glu and 5′-nucleotides with umami receptor proteins may be due to the spatial structure of the umami receptor proteins (Escriche et al., 2012). This mentioned above indicating that the mechanism of influencing receptor proteins and thereby influencing the synergistic effect is universal.

Nucleotides can increase the palatability of certain foods, and the morphology among flavor nucleotides and umami constituents greatly increases the palatability of mushrooms (Baryłko-Pikielna and Kostyra, 2007; Tsai et al., 2009). The 5′-nucleotides, such as IMP, are present in small amounts together with glutamate synergistically to enhance umami (Roper and Chaudhari, 2017). L-glutamate and 5 ′-nucleotide is highly synergistic, and its L-Glu umami is greatly enhanced by this synergy. Purine nucleotides such as IMP and GMP improve the action of the T1R1/T1R3 receptor, whose binding site to the receptor is located in the extracellular structural domain of T1R1 (Zhang et al., 2008). Zhang et al. (2008) developed a model of the umami receptor binding with Glu and IMP based on fluorescence staining. Through homology modeling and crystal structure analysis, they detected the concentration of Ca2+ and verified and summarized the relationship between the synergistic effect of Glu and IMP and the VFT of T1R1.

Umami peptides can enhance the umami taste of MSG solutions within a certain intensity range. Dang et al. (2019b) employed an electronic tongue to assess the taste intensity and synergy of 36 peptides and confirmed the taste intensity of the peptides through sensory analysis. They screened the peptides and ranked the synergy between the peptides and MSG. Similarly, Xu et al. (2024) found that ARG-277 in T1R1 plays a major role in the binding of umami peptides to T1R1-VFT when exploring the synergistic effects of umami peptides selected from Agaricus bisporus with MSG or IMP. IMP and MSG bind to receptor proteins, causing conformational changes and resulting in synergy (Bellisle, 1999). Dang et al. (2019a) developed a new bivariate model of T1R1/T1R3 molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation. Through this model, they found that three specific changes occurred in energy and conformation when MSG was added between umami peptides and T1R1/T1R3, thereby observing and verifying the synergy between umami peptides and MSG and exploring the interaction forces during the process. They also confirmed the synergy between umami peptides and MSG again using an electronic tongue. Yang et al. (2021) demonstrated that archetypal kokumi-tasting γ-glutamyl has a strong affinity for the T1R3 receptor of MSG, and typical kokumi-tasting γ-glutamyl has a significant umami-enhancing effect on MSG. Among them, γ-E-γ-EV was investigated and found to potentially have the strongest umami-enhancing effect. With the discovery of new umami enhancers, more interactions between new umami enhancers and umami receptors need to be further explored.

The synergy of umami substances does enhance umami, but it can also act as a salty enhancer. Amino acids (e.g., glutamate, arginine, and histidine) can activate umami receptors such as T1R1/T1R3 or mGluR, which produce umami in low-sodium environments, leading to an enhanced perception of saltiness (Sood et al., 2024). Enzymatic digests obtained from mushrooms have been shown to be viable as salt enhancers for flavorings. Yang et al. (2022) demonstrated that supplementation with Antler fungus-derived umami enzymatic hydrolysate exhibited concentration-dependent synergistic potentiation of saline perception in differentially concentrated sodium chloride solutions. Chen et al. (2024) investigated the umami enhancement effect of the umami peptide of Stropharia rugosoannulata, and found that SGCVNEL, which was initially regarded as weakly salty, exhibited enhanced salty taste when mixed with salt, which was analyzed to be a possible structural change. However, no studies have been conducted to determine the effect of salt-enhancing umami substances on enhancing salty taste by interacting with salty receptors, and it is unknown which receptors are activated to determine the overall salty taste of food (Sood et al., 2024). In response to the salty-enhancing effects of mushrooms in the daily diet, the mechanisms by which umami substances synergistically enhance salty taste and the structural changes in umami peptides during synergistic effects with salty taste can be further investigated.

Factors affecting the umami taste of mushrooms

In the selection process of mushroom food, the same type of mushrooms cultivated in different ways are usually chosen. In the food production process, various drying and cooking methods are used to process mushrooms, and different enzymes can be used to hydrolyze the proteins of mushrooms to enhance the flavor of products. Additionally, distinct storage methods are employed during food storage. All these factors can affect the umami characteristics of mushrooms. The factors influencing the umami of mushrooms are diverse, so it is crucial to deeply explore these factors in the processing and production of mushroom foods in terms of umami.The Equivalent Umami Concentration (EUC) value serves as a key indicator for evaluating the umami intensity of mushrooms and is commonly used to assess the impact of processing methods on their umami flavor (Wang et al., 2024).

The influence of cultivation methods on the umami of mushrooms

Studies have shown that wild and cultivated mushrooms differ in the intensity of several sensory descriptors and exhibit umami differences (Aisala et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2020). This also indicates that cultivation methods can influence the umami taste of mushrooms. This view has been further supported by research demonstrating that soil-cover cultivation results in higher nutritional value and a stronger umami-sweet flavor in cultivated Pleurotus eryngii (Song et al., 2025). Additionally, studies comparing cultivated and wild varieties such as Cantharellus cibarius and Craterellus tubaeformis have revealed sensory differences between cultivated and wild mushrooms, with cultivated mushrooms exhibiting higher flavor intensity (Aisala et al., 2018).

Effect of cooking methods on umami of mushroom

Edible mushrooms appear on people's dining tables in various cooking styles, and the variations in cooking methods exert an influence on the umami taste. Blanching is a common cooking method that involves boiling food in hot water, which can eliminate bitterness and astringency in food ingredients' taste (Liburdi et al., 2019). This can reduce bitterness's antagonistic effect on umami in mushrooms, thereby enhancing the umami taste. Rotola-Pukkila et al. (2019) five different mushroom varieties, namely Agaricus bisporus, Lactarius trivialis, Cantharellus cibarius, Cantharellus tubaeformis and Suillus variegatus, were subjected to sous vide (SV) cooking at 90 °C. It was found that the bitter amino acids decreased during the cooking process. Agaricus bisporus was cooked at four different temperatures (60 °C, 70 °C, 80 °C and 90 °C), and it was discovered that the concentrations of 5′-AMP and 5′-GMP significantly increased after heat treatment. The mushroom samples treated at 70 °C had the highest concentrations of total 5′-nucleotides and flavor 5′-nucleotides (Rotola-Pukkila et al., 2019).

Effect of varied drying methods on umami of mushroom

Drying is one of the oldest and most effective preservation techniques in the food industry (Dong et al., 2017), especially used for the preservation treatment of mushrooms. The drying technology reduces moisture activity, inhibits microbial reproduction, blunts enzyme activity, and prevents spoilage (Das and Arora, 2018; Wang et al., 2023). Natural air drying (NAD), hot air drying (HAD), fluidized bed drying (FBD), infrared drying (IRD), microwave drying (MD), microwave vacuum drying (MVD), vacuum drying (VD), freeze-drying (FD), vacuum-freeze-drying (VFD), and freeze-drying combined with microwave-vacuum-drying (FMVD) are nowadays used for mushroom drying treatments (Pei et al., 2014b; Zhang et al., 2018; Tian et al., 2016; Walde et al., 2006). Each drying technique has unique advantages and limitations, and the effects on the nutritional properties or microstructure of flavor characteristics of mushrooms may vary under different drying methods.

These mushrooms, such as shiitake mushrooms (Tian et al., 2016), Pleurotus eryngii (Li et al., 2015), Coprinus comatus (Ren et al., 2022), Flammulina velutipes (Wang et al., 2023), Agaricus bisporus (Pei et al., 2014a), Hypsizygus marmoreus (Wang et al., 2024), Stropharia rugoso-annulata (Hu et al., 2020), Stropharia rugosoannulata (Liu et al., 2022), and Suillus granulatus (Zhao et al., 2020) under different drying techniques have distinct effects on umami. HAD can increase the content of UAAs and organic acids in mushrooms, preserve the 5′-nucleotides and soluble sugars in mushrooms at a relatively high level, and have a good equivalent umami concentration (EUC) value (Hu et al., 2020). The FAAs and organic acid contents in mushrooms can be well preserved in VD, and significantly retain the content of UAAs (Glu and Asp), thus having a relatively high EUC value (Zhao et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2023). MVD has a better ability to promote protein decomposition than HAD, with the concentration of FAAs after drying significantly higher than that in fresh mushrooms, which can increase the content of organic acids in mushrooms and help maintain their unique flavor (Tian et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2022). NAD leads to the degradation of umami compounds in mushrooms, thereby reducing their umami taste (Wang et al., 2024). FMVD can effectively preserve MSG-like amino acids in mushrooms, and after drying, it has an EUC value similar to that of fresh mushrooms (Pei et al., 2014a). IRD treatment significantly increases the EUC value of mushrooms, which is beneficial for the preservation of umami compounds in mushrooms (Ren et al., 2022). MD significantly reduced the levels of free amino acids and organic acids in mushrooms, and its EUC value remained relatively low when compared with other drying methods (Li et al., 2015; Ren et al., 2022).

In summary, hot drying treatment, which promotes proteolysis within the mushrooms and increases the FAAs content, MVD possessed the highest concentration of UAAs. However, during the drying process, proteins break down into amino acids, which promotes the Maillard reaction between amino acids and sugars (Hu et al., 2020). This leads to a reduction in umami amino acids, and the increase in temperature also affects 5′-nucleotides, causing their levels to decrease (Yang et al., 2024b). Therefore, both time and temperature need to be controlled during the drying and processing of mushrooms. Low-temperature drying and vacuum drying treatments result in a compound content in mushrooms that is closer to that of fresh mushrooms, which is attributed to the minimal structural shrinkage of mushrooms during freeze-drying (Huang et al., 2021). The new drying techniques (FMVD, IRD) were more capable of preserving and enhancing the freshness of mushrooms and better protecting their flavor compound content. FMVD combines two drying methods, which shortens the drying time compared to using a single drying approach and better preserves the activity of flavor-contributing amino acids (Pei et al., 2014a). IRD offers distinct advantages, as during the drying process, energy penetrates to a very shallow depth within the material and is then converted into heat. Additionally, its compatibility makes it an excellent choice for combination with other drying methods (Huang et al., 2021). MD is less suitable than other drying methods for processing mushrooms when the goal is to preserve their umami flavor. Drying methods can also be used in combination with other techniques to improve mushroom flavor, Li et al. (2024) dried Lentinus edodes separately by combining different drying techniques with cellulase hydrolysis and found that the combination of HAD with cellulase hydrolysis produced significantly high levels of concentrations of UAAs and 5′-nucleotides. The development of new drying techniques in combination with other processing technologies may be used to effectively develop functional foods with potential health-promoting properties and to improve people's perception of mushroom drying and subsequent processing. More attention should be paid to the mechanism of action of the Maillard reaction in mushrooms, which will facilitate further investigation of umami as well as other flavors of mushrooms.

Effect of storage means on umami of mushroom

Fresh mushrooms have strong cellular activity and metabolic processes. During post-harvest storage, they encounter problems such as cap development, browning, insect infestation, and softening, all of which can lead to a loss of quality. These problems not only significantly shorten the shelf life of mushrooms, but also reduce their economic value. Therefore, proper storage of fresh mushrooms is essential. The quality and flavor characteristics of mushrooms can be effectively maintained by using effective storage methods. The methods for storing mushrooms can be divided into two major categories: physical treatments such as Non-thermal plasma (NTP), packaging, and latent heat storage, etc. On the other hands chemical processes such as antioxidants, ozone, and edible coatings, etc. (Xia et al., 2023a).

Refrigeration effectively preserves the freshness of mushrooms. Zhang et al. (2019b) investigated the effects of different storage temperatures on Pleurotus geesteranusand found that mushrooms stored at 5 °C exhibited a higher EUC, along with increased levels of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), adenosine diphosphate (ADP), and adenosine monophosphate (AMP). Their study revealed that AMP serves as a link between umami perception and energy metabolism, showing a significant positive correlation with both EUC and umami intensity as measured by an electronic tongue at 5 °C. In contrast, mushrooms stored at 20 °C demonstrated a rapid decline in umami-related compounds over time, ultimately leading to spoilage, whereas those stored at 10, 5, and 0 °C maintained better quality. For dried mushrooms, there are also pre-treatments to enhance mushroom quality. Xu et al. (2019a) conducted pre-drying of dried shiitake mushrooms at different temperatures (45, 55, 65, and 75 °C) and found that enzyme activity of shiitake mushrooms was high throughout the pre-drying process, which was favorable for the formation of flavor compounds.

Feng et al. (2023) performed light treatments on Suillus granulatus, including red, yellow, green, blue, and white light, light intensity was 3500 lx and the illumination lasted for three days. Under blue light treatment, the contents of UAAs and 5′-nucleotides are higher, along with higher EUC and electronic tongue scores. This suggests that light treatment can improve the flavor characteristics of dried mushrooms. Xia et al. (2023b) developed 1- Methylcyclopropene (1-MCP), which reduced ethylene peaks, increased Glu content, and activated energy metabolism-related enzymes to increase ATP and Energy Charge (EC), thus enhancing umami in Pleurotus geesteranus. Li et al. (2021) developed a water-based Phase change material (PCM) that can maintain low temperatures by absorbing latent heat through a phase change, and this PCM showed better results in maintaining low-temperature conditions compared to ice. It was applied to almond mushrooms for five days of post-harvest storage at room temperature and was found to improve Gln accumulation for almond mushroom umami, and the increase in Gln enhanced its flavor profile. Liu et al. (2021) developed a storage method using Oudemansiella radicata (ORWP) wrapped around fresh shiitake mushrooms. They found that many of the qualities of ORWP-wrapped mushrooms were improved during the storage process. Many qualities were significantly enhanced during storage, including increased concentrations of superoxide dismutase and catalase, and for mushroom umami, higher concentrations of UAAs,5′-nucleotides, and 1-octen-3-ol.

The umami substances of mushrooms can be maintained or enhanced during storage by influencing enzyme activity, improving energy status and metabolism, etc. The production and processing of mushroom foods require well-stored raw materials, so effective storage methods must be adopted to preserve their umami taste. Effective storage methods can improve the umami taste and quality of mushrooms. Further research and application of new storage methods will help enhance the market competitiveness of mushroom products and meet consumers' demands for high-quality food.

Effect of enzymes on umami of mushroom

Umami substances in mushrooms, such as UAAs and umami peptides, can be extracted from the large protein molecules in mushrooms through enzymatic hydrolysis. For instance, the protein in shiitake mushrooms can be degraded by cellulase and protease into free peptides and free amino acids (Zhao et al., 2019). Enzymatic hydrolysis is a method that operates under mild and controlled conditions while avoiding the generation of toxic degradation byproducts (Feng et al., 2017), which is of great advantage for the food processing of mushroom products. The products of enzymatic hydrolysis of mushroom proteins vary with different enzymes, so it is necessary to study the optimal enzymes corresponding to different types of mushrooms.

Studies have found that the type of enzyme is crucial. Under the same total enzyme concentration, the combined use of Neutrase and Flavourzyme yielded Morchella sextelata hydrolysates with higher nutritional value and improved taste. Moreover, under optimal parameters, this enzymatic approach was more conducive to generating a stronger umami taste and reducing bitterness (Gao et al., 2021). Seven enzyme groups (the composition of the enzyme groups is provided in the Supplementary Materials) were used to hydrolyze the proteins of Huangshan floral mushroom to obtain protein hydrolysates, among which the dispase hydrolysate was selected as the most effective enzyme hydrolysis with the best flavor profile and the highest umami (Zhang et al., 2024). Enzymatic hydrolysis is a method to obtain umami peptides and serve as a potential future stable source of umami peptides for mushrooms.

Umami and energy metabolism

There is a relationship between umami compounds and energy metabolism, and this relationship has received extensive attention and research. Zhang et al. (2019b) found that a higher energy state contributes to umami retention during mushroom storage. The energy state is highly correlated with the postharvest quality of mushrooms and is a core factor of their quality, and an imbalance in the energy state can lead to accelerated deterioration of mushrooms having a poor impact on their freshness and umami (Yang et al., 2019; Xia et al., 2023b). Yan et al. (2020) investigated the impacts of nanocomposite packaging materials (Nano-PM) on flavor components and mitochondrial energy metabolism in post-harvest white Hypsizygus marmoreus (WHM) and concluded that the taste elements of mushrooms may be susceptible to post-harvest energy metabolism.

5′-nucleotides include adenosine AMP, GMP, IMP, and XMP; UAAs include Glu and Asp. These umami substances are not only the main sources of umami taste but also important energy metabolites in the metabolic process of mushrooms (Sun et al., 2020). The energy in mushrooms mainly comes from the Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas (EMP), pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), and tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA). Umami substances are closely related to the metabolism of mushrooms. For example, the umami amino acid Glu is a key regulatory factor in carbon-nitrogen balance, and its metabolic products can directly participate in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and energy metabolism (Li et al., 2021).

Investigations have revealed that the energy state is linked to the umami taste of edible mushrooms at the molecular level. For instance, as the spores of Lentinula edodes are released, 15 genes involved in energy metabolism are restrictedly downregulated, among which LecpdP1 and LeAK are responsible for converting ATP to AMP. These two genes are the key genes linking energy status to umami taste (Xia et al., 2023c). However, there are too few studies on mushroom genes related to energy status, and molecular research in this area may enhance the influence of cultivation on the umami taste of mushrooms.

Screening of mushroom umami peptides

The advancement of small-molecule peptide studies, coupled with the integration of computational approaches and bioinformatics, has revolutionized the development of innovative methodologies for peptide discovery and optimization. The in silico digestion approach markedly reduces the temporal span between raw material processing and peptide synthesis, thereby expediting progress in umami peptide discovery studies (Qi et al., 2022). The conventional approach for screening umami peptides from identified peptides relies on structure-activity relationship analysis or the frequency of bioactive fragments (Bu et al., 2021; Szymczak et al., 2023). Nevertheless, this methodology faces significant challenges in high-throughput sequencing applications. Recently, the integration of machine learning with molecular docking has emerged as a promising technique, offering both high-throughput capacity and rapid processing advantages, enabling preliminary screening of umami peptides within established peptide libraries (Charoenkwan et al., 2020; Qi et al., 2022).

Chromatography and mass spectrometry

Ultrafiltration and various chromatographic techniques are the most commonly used for the separation and purification of umami peptides (Fig. 1) (Hu et al., 2024). They are often employed in the screening process, such as gel filtration chromatography (GFC), gel permeation chromatography (GPC), ion-exchange column chromatography (IEC), fast protein liquid chromatography, and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Peptides separated in chromatography are further analyzed in Mass spectrometry (MS) after ultrafiltration (Fig. 1) (Chen et al., 2023). To identifing peptide tastes it is also necessary to screen the peptides analyzed by MS against databases such as the UniProt protein database (https://www.uniprot.org/) to match the peptide amino acid sequences (Liang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022b).Fig. 1Purification, separation, and identification of umami peptides by chromatography and mass spectrometry.Fig. 1

Kong et al. (2019) separated the dried shiitake components by ultrafiltration, GFC and reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC), followed by organoleptic evaluations (grading and flavor dilution analyses) and e-tongue analyses of isolated peptides to isolate and characterize the umami peptides, two triple peptides (Gly-Cys-Gys-Gys-Gys-Gys-Gys-Gys-Gys), and two triple peptides (Gly-Cys-Gys-Gys-Gys-Gys). Xu et al. (2019b) identified tasty peptides by ultrafiltration, gel filtration chromatography, and PR-HPLC separation of straw mushrooms by ultra-performance liquid chromatography time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UPLC-Q-TOF-MS) in combination with sensory evaluation, among which ASNMSDL (Ala-Ser-Asn-Met-Ser-Asp-Leu) and LQPLNAH (Leu-Gln-Pro-Leu-Asn-Ala-His) were umami. Compared to the traditional column chromatography method used for peptide identification, the mass spectrometry-based peptidomics approach demonstrates advantages including simpler pretreatment procedures, enhanced screening capabilities, and rapid and effective screening and identification of novel peptides (Kong et al., 2019).

While conventional identification strategies demonstrate accuracy, they remain time- and labor-intensive, with the potential loss of umami-active peptides that are excluded from the most bioactive fractions during separation processes (Qi et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023). Although chromatographic techniques provide quantitative chemical analysis for umami peptide screening, they fail to characterize the holistic taste profile of peptides, necessitating subsequent umami evaluation of isolated peptides for effective screening. The combination of existing techniques alone proves insufficient for high-throughput screening of umami peptides, demanding the integration of novel technologies to drive methodological advancements.

Machine learning and deep learning

The emergence of the post-genomic epoch has driven exponential expansion in novel peptide sequence discovery. Computer techniques (machine learning and deep learning) for fast and accurate identification of gustatory peptides (Fig. 2) play a crucial role in assisting analysis techniques. The synergistic integration of proteomics and computational analysis constitutes a powerful method for high-precision identification and efficient screening of umami peptides (Zhang et al., 2022b; Guo et al., 2024). Machine learning (ML) analyzes large amounts of data information converts it into useful knowledge, and can generate predictive and decision-making information suitable for various application scenarios. Computer-aided virtual screening enables rapid bioactivity prediction of large chemical libraries through high-throughput computational methodologies (Spaggiari et al., 2020). Deep learning (DL), as a subset of artificial intelligence, excels in autonomously extracting discriminative features from extensive datasets, enabling predictive modeling and classification of uncharacterized samples. This self-evolving paradigm demonstrates exceptional capabilities in uncovering latent structure-property relationships, computational efficiency, and predictive accuracy through its end-to-end learning architecture, which facilitates automated feature extraction directly from raw peptide sequences. In recent years, this methodology has emerged as a transformative approach to bioactive peptide prediction and functional analysis (Greener and Kandathil, 2022; Su et al., 2024). The continuous development of DL algorithms in recent years, such as convolutional neural network (CNN), long short-term memory (LSTM), inception network, and Transformer, has led to the proposal of data-driven methods in many fields, including the screening and prediction of peptides (Wang et al., 2022; Qi et al., 2023). Homology modeling, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics

The method of computer simulation has been widely applied in fields such as materials science, computational biology, and computational chemistry. The development and popularization of bioinformatics have led to the emergence of molecular docking as a drug discovery technology, which has been widely used in the food industry to evaluate and screen umami peptides (Qi et al., 2022). Combining molecular simulation results with sensory evaluation to study the mechanism of umami perception is a trend in umami research (Zhang et al., 2023b; Meng et al., 2024). Virtual screening methods based on computer algorithms can be roughly divided into two types: one is ligand-based (when using information about known ligands), and the other is structure-based (when using information about the binding site of the target protein) (Spaggiari et al., 2020). The molecular docking technology used in the screening of umami peptides is calculated based on the homology modeling of umami receptors. As the structure of taste receptors has not been fully resolved so far, an alternative approach is needed. Computational simulation can be used to generate 3D structural models of taste receptors (Spaggiari et al., 2020). Homology modeling and molecular docking serve as robust computational approaches for investigating ligand-receptor interactions. The homology modeling protocol enables accurate construction of three-dimensional structures for T1R1/T1R3 heterodimers, while molecular docking simulations reveal molecular-level interactions between umami peptides and their cognate receptors (Fig. 3). The umami peptide taste perception mechanism can be elucidated through analyzing the interaction between umami peptides and their receptors (Chen et al., 2022). Direct modeling of the T1R1/T1R3 receptor is not possible without its crystal structure, so homology modeling is used to construct the structure of T1R1/T1R3. The glutamate-binding domains of T1R1 and mGluR1 are highly conserved. Therefore, the "closed-open/active" conformation of mGluR1 (PDB ID: 1EWK) can serve as one of the templates for modeling the VFT domain of T1R1 (Mouritsen and Khandelia, 2012; Nuemket et al., 2017; Dang et al., 2019a). In addition to using the mGluR1 structure, mGluR3, and mGluR7 have also been employed to construct the T1R1 model (Zhang et al., 2008; Acher et al., 2011). Moreover, some studies have built homology models of the T1R1/T1R3 complex based on the sweet taste receptor of fish (T1R2a/T1R3) (Yu et al., 2023).Fig. 3Molecular docking of the umami peptide (KCGQ) with T1R1/T1R3, the umami peptide (KCGQ) borrowed in the image is from Song et al. Molecular docking allows the perception of umami by simulating the molecular recognition between umami ligands and taste receptors (Spaggiari et al., 2020). The interaction strength between umami receptors and peptides can be quantitatively assessed through homology modeling and molecular docking calculations, thus enabling the screening of bioactive peptides (Hao et al., 2024), as well as the exploration of interaction forces and strengths between screened umami peptides and umami receptors. Ligand-binding domains in umami taste transduction can be predicted through molecular docking simulations, while the underlying molecular mechanisms can be elucidated (Shen et al., 2023). Generally, umami ligands bind to amino acid residue sites in the VFT of T1R1/T1R3, and the forces between them and T1R1-VFT receptors are mainly hydrogen bonding, π-stacking, and hydrophobic interactions, and umami peptides show affinity through the strength of their interactions with umami receptors (Xu et al., 2024).

Evaluation of umami taste

The primary objectives of umami evaluation can be broadly categorized into two types: the first is flavor analysis, which is largely concerned with compounds related to umami in food, aiming to determine the flavor characteristics of these relevant compounds; the second is sensory analysis, which determines the sensory attributes and other properties of food (Piggott et al., 1998).

The flavor analysis of mushrooms relies on the measurement of both volatile and non-volatile compounds in food, among which taste is directly or indirectly influenced by non-volatile compounds. Data on non-volatile compounds are typically obtained through analytical instruments such as liquid chromatography and automatic amino acid analyzers. Sensory data are generally provided by sensory evaluation panels, which assign scores to the sensory attributes of food (Yu et al., 2018a). Mimicking the unique properties of biological taste has inspired the development of odor and flavor analysis equipment (Son & Park, 2018), meeting the current demands for umami evaluation. Umami assessment can be categorized into four methods (Fig. 4), including chemical analysis, human sensory evaluation, electronic taste sensors, and AI-based sensory prediction. Chemical indicators

The umami compounds identified in mushrooms, which are all non-volatile, can be assessed using two key parameters: Taste activity value (TAV) and EUC (Wang et al., 2020). Most of the evaluations on the influence of processing methods on the umami of mushrooms are conducted by calculating the TAV values and EUC values.

TAV is conventionally employed to evaluate the contribution of individual taste-active compounds, such as free amino acids, nucleotides, organic acids, and related compounds, to taste perception. Higher TAV values demonstrate greater flavor contribution from the respective compound (Wang et al., 2023). Human sensory evaluation

The human sensory evaluation of food is mediated by the detection of various chemical compounds through receptors in the nasal and oral cavities, which generate neural signals transmitted to the brain. These sensory inputs are subsequently processed and analyzed through either empirical knowledge or neural network-based pattern recognition mechanisms (Ha et al., 2015). Organoleptic assessment serves as a validated and widely implemented methodology for gustatory characterization, where a person performs product detection and analysis through the responses of the senses of sight, hearing, smell, touch, and taste to qualitatively and quantitatively analyze the taste of food (Sun et al., 2020). Human sensory evaluation serves as the conventional method for taste assessment, encompassing six distinct methodologies for determining the umami intensity of umami solutions. Incorporating quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA), two-alternative forced choice (2-AFC), three-alternative forced choice (3-AFC), taste dilution analysis (TDA), comparative taste dilution analysis (cTDA), and taste recognition threshold concentrations (TRTC) (Wang et al., 2020). 2-AFC and 3-AFC are used to determine the presence of umami. QDA (Quantitative Descriptive Analysis) is a method of descriptive analysis (DA) (Murray et al., 2001) used to specifically characterize the attributes of umami. However, this method imposes stricter requirements on sensory panel members. TDA and cTDA are the most commonly used methods for umami assessment. For example, Li et al. (2024) utilized TDA to evaluate umami peptides screened from Stropharia rugosoannulata. TRTC is used to determine the minimum concentration at which a taste can be detected.

Human sensory evaluation holds a significant position in the food industry and is irreplaceable in current food research and the food industry. It is simple and quick, and can directly reflect the external quality of the food, usually, sensory evaluation is conducted by professionally trained group members. However, due to its high subjectivity and cost, it has some obvious disadvantages (Ghasemi-Varnamkhasti et al., 2018). Human sensory evaluation is easily influenced by subjective factors, making it difficult to provide accurate data, and its objectivity and reproducibility are poor. In addition, some foods may have potential toxicity, especially for mushrooms. Furthermore, although human sensory evaluation remains crucial in the food industry, certain food products may pose potential toxicity concerns, particularly in the case of mushrooms. Consequently, the implementation of advanced analytical techniques is required to replace human-based assessments, thereby enhancing the reliability and reproducibility of food quality evaluations through accurate umami intensity quantification and standardized assessment protocols. This methodological advancement is of paramount importance for the sustainable development of the food industry.

Electronic tongue

Artificial taste sensors have undergone continuous advancement, with intelligent sensory systems emerging as bio-inspired technologies that mimic human gustatory perception. A novel electrochemical sensor array capable of replicating human tongue functionality has been developed, enabling comprehensive evaluation of taste profiles in both standardized solutions and complex food matrices - a technology formally designated as the electronic tongue (e-tongue) (Lee et al., 2018).

The e-tongue is mainly composed of sensor arrays, signal processing, and pattern recognition systems, the sensor arrays are like human sensory organs, responding to the samples and obtaining the response signals; signal processing transmits the response signal and performs simple pre-processing on the signal, just like the human nervous system; the final signal is processed by a computer system, which further analyzes and processes the signal, just like the human brain, in order to obtain the results of the taste characteristics of the evaluated object (Dang et al., 2019b; Lu et al., 2022). The e-tongue is an analytical instrument utilizing appropriate models and statistical methods for qualitative and quantitative detection (Hao et al., 2024). Compared with conventional chemical analytical methods, this technology demonstrates significant advantages including high sensitivity, simplified operation procedures, reduced equipment requirements, and cost-effectiveness (Ghasemi-Varnamkhasti et al., 2018). Available new biosensor e-tongues are instruments consisting of a single biosensor or a series of biosensors that provide a broad and holistic response to the analyzed species and are flat and equipped with chemometric tools that enable the extraction and interpretation of meaningful data from complex data (Cetó et al., 2016).

With the definition of the e-tongue by The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC), this modern instrument is rapidly evolving and is being applied to flavor evaluation and identification of food products and other research applications (Di Natale et al., 1997; Winquist et al., 1997; Sehra et al., 2004; Tian et al., 2007; Wu et al., 2020). The advancement of e-tongue technology has progressively refined the measurement of umami taste, bringing it closer to true value. This development significantly aids in the future assessment of umami in mushrooms and the screening of umami compounds within them. Given the growing demand for high-throughput screening of umami peptides, there is an urgent need to develop receptor-based bionic taste sensors to facilitate rapid and accurate identification of umami peptides (Li et al., 2024).

Sensors based on biological recognition elements

With continuous breakthroughs in research on the taste system, specific biosensors for gustatory-related analysis have been developed. Biosensors, owing to their high sensitivity and specificity, exhibit extensive applicability across multiple stages and domains in food production systems (Zhang et al., 2020). Biosensors can trigger taste signals through the binding activities between selective taste receptors and taste substances, thus providing the possibility for the development of artificial taste sensors that imitate the human taste system. The gustatory system comprises a hierarchical neural network extending from the tongue to the cerebral gustatory cortex, functionally encompassing taste perception, signal transduction, and neural processing of gustatory information (Nelson et al., 2001; Yarmolinsky et al., 2009). The mammalian gustatory system is categorized into four hierarchical levels based on the sequential perception of taste, with functional units across all levels serving as sensing elements in taste system-inspired biosensors (Zhang et al., 2020).

Biological sensor elements are the core component in biosensor technology, a biologically-derived recognition component originating from diverse biological sources including gustatory cells, tissues, neuronal networks, and enzymatic systems, as well as emerging advanced materials such as functionalized nanomaterials and microfluidic chips. Identification elements demonstrate exceptional capability in generating biomimetic analytical signals characterized by enhanced sensitivity and molecular selectivity (Lu et al., 2017). The recent development of a novel e-tongue technology, grounded in biological taste-sensing mechanisms, employs strategic immobilization of diversified lipid compounds onto polyvinyl chloride (PVC)-based sensor substrates. This innovative system transduces molecular interactions between various lipid species and taste-specific substances (corresponding to sweet, sour, bitter, salty, and umami modalities) into quantifiable membrane potential signals. These electrochemical responses undergo advanced pattern recognition analysis, enabling precise discrimination of flavor attributes and comprehensive evaluation of organoleptic quality parameters in analyte samples (Wu et al., 2020). Moreover, current evidence reveals significant limitations in biosensor technology. For instance, the majority of biosensors struggle to maintain stable performance after prolonged storage periods. Furthermore, the output of the sensing elements of biosensors is always very low, resulting in prohibitively high manufacturing costs. These technical constraints collectively indicate that biosensors currently lack feasibility for large-scale manufacturing and practical implementation (Wasilewski et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020).

Sensory prediction based on ML and DL

Although taste sensors for taste perception can now accurately assess taste attributes and overall quality characteristics, further progress on sensors is difficult due to some technical and material issues. Therefore, more models for evaluating umami intensity should be developed (Qi et al., 2022). Additionally, since taste originates from the interaction between taste receptors in the mouth and chemical taste agents, and is highly dependent on the physicochemical properties of food components, machine learning and deep learning can classify and predict the taste of compounds by utilizing their molecular structures (Androutsos et al., 2024).

The relationship between flavor components and sensory properties is not necessarily linear, and linear regression methods may not be able to account for the nonlinear relationship between human senses and food properties (Krishnamurthy et al., 2007). Thus, there is a need to construct models for nonlinear relationships. Androutsos et al. (2024) developed a multiclass predictor that can separate bitter, sweet, and umami from other taste sensations, which opens up possibilities in the field of multisensory perception by integrating vision, touch, smell, and taste to comprehensively characterize flavor perception. Artificial Neural Network (ANN) has also been used to explore the nonlinear relationship between sensory properties and consumer perception in food products (Ikeda et al., 2004). The advantage of ANN is mainly in its ability to estimate any linear or nonlinear function without prior transformation. However, ANN its 'black box' nature is a major drawback of its use as a regression tool, making useful key information inaccessible (Yu et al., 2018b). In the new generation of artificial networks, spiking neural network (SNN), which contains more biological features than ANN, Jia et al. (2022) improved SNN based on Motiftopology and Reward-Learning for multisensory integration targeting sensory prediction. A sensory evaluation model based on human EEG was constructed, which is based on common spatial patterns, and a support vector machine algorithm for feature extraction and classification, and wavelet transform was applied to the data processing. The final model predicted the fresh and sweet flavors with satisfactory results (Li et al., 2025).

Conclusions

This review summarizes the current research status on umami taste in mushrooms, covering the characteristics and multidimensional properties of umami substances, the screening of umami peptides, and the evaluation of umami. Umami compounds in mushrooms not only provide taste perception but also serve as nutrients and possess bioactivity, indicating that the nutritional value of mushrooms can be reflected through their umami properties. Umami peptides, as key taste-contributing components, exhibit activity influenced by their amino acid composition, molecular weight, and spatial structure.Various factors, including cultivation methods, processing techniques, storage conditions, and types of hydrolases, can alter the content of umami substances in mushrooms, thereby affecting their umami taste. A notable feature of umami is its synergistic effect, which is largely associated with umami receptors. Specifically, the umami receptor (T1R1/T1R3) interacts with various umami substances (such as amino acids, umami peptides, and nucleotides). Moreover, due to the shared membership in the T1r receptor family, many sweet substances can also be recognized by the umami receptor. Current research focuses on the dynamic changes in umami components in mushrooms and the identification of novel umami peptides. The emergence and development of technologies such as machine learning, deep learning, homology modeling, and molecular docking have accelerated the screening and identification of mushroom-derived umami peptides, enabling high-throughput screening. However, sensory evaluation is still required to validate the taste intensity of identified umami peptides. The integration of traditional taste assessment with sensory prediction will form a critical foundation for future flavor substance analysis and screening. Furthermore, multi-sensory integrated flavor evaluation holds significant potential in the processing of mushroom-based foods.

Future perspectives

The relationship of umami with nutritional value and bioactivity should be further investigated, with the study of umami peptides being exceedingly significant. In the future, we should explore the bioactivity of the identified umami peptides and find the relationship between the structure of umami peptides and umami taste and bioactivity, as well as the relationship between umami taste intensity and bioactivity. This is also a direction for future research on mushroom proteins and peptides, which is related to the development of mushroom food. The genetics of umami are also understudied. In the future, the causes of umami should be studied in detail, and a substantial number of genes related to the production of umami compounds should be identified or investigated. Further research on umami receptors will also help the progress of umami peptide screening, and the screening and identification of umami peptides similarly facilitate the study of umami receptor-ligand interaction mechanisms. Research and application of new processing methods and storage means will help to improve the market competitiveness of mushroom products and to meet consumer demand for high-quality food, and the development of more environmentally friendly and effective processing means to promote umami enhancement in mushrooms is warranted.

The computational screening of mushroom umami peptides necessitates the development of enhanced predictive models to facilitate the discovery of novel umami peptides. This advancement will accelerate investigations into structure-activity relationships and bioactivity profiles of umami peptides, thereby facilitating mushroom-based food product development. However, the synthetic pathways of many umami substances within mushrooms are speculative in nature and remain understudied when it comes to screening and identifying umami substances. Notably, research on post-translational modifications of umami peptides remains insufficient. Zhang et al. (2022a) investigated Amadori rearrangement products (ARP) of peptides, revealing that the RFPHADF-ARP derived from bitter peptide RFPHADF exhibits T1R3-binding capability and enhances conformational stability of umami receptor complexes. Furthermore, Cui et al. (2025b) proposed a potential cyclization strategy for engineering novel umami peptides. Subsequent research should prioritize elucidating structure-umami intensity correlations and modification-induced flavor enhancement mechanisms, which are fundamental for the de novo design of umami peptides. The integration of machine learning and deep learning in sensory prediction presents innovative opportunities for rational food design, enabling pre-determination of flavor profiles and the development of engineered complementary dietary formulations to augment traditional medicinal treatments.
