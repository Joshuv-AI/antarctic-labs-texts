Femoral Tunnel Position Thresholds Associated With Functional Outcomes After Medial Patellofemoral Ligament Reconstruction
Authors: Byeong-Hun Jeon, Min Jung, Kwangho Chung, Hyun-Soo Moon, Se-Han Jung, Jusung Lee, Sung-Hwan Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:While numerous studies have examined the effects of femoral tunnel malposition in medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) reconstruction (MPFLR), the extent to which deviation from the anatomic femoral insertion can be tolerated before patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are adversely affected has not been clearly established.Purpose:To evaluate postoperative improvements in PROMs after MPFLR, determine outcome-based cutoffs for femoral tunnel placement using 3-dimensional imaging, and assess the effects of directional deviations.Study Design:Cohort study, Level of evidence 3.Methods:This retrospective study included 50 knees that underwent MPFLR between 2011 and 2023, with or without concomitant tibial tubercle osteotomy. PROMs (Lysholm, Tegner, International Knee Documentation Committee [IKDC], Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score [KOOS], and Kujala) were collected pre- and postoperatively, and minimal clinically important differences (MCIDs) were calculated using a distribution-based method. Femoral tunnel positions were analyzed in 37 knees using postoperative computed tomography. Pearson correlation and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses identified tunnel deviation thresholds associated with clinically meaningful improvements, and directional analyses assessed anterior-posterior and proximal-distal variations.Results:All PROMs significantly improved after surgery, with MCID achievement rates of up to 68%. Femoral tunnel deviation was negatively correlated with the IKDC, KOOS, and Kujala scores. ROC analysis identified a 7.2-mm cutoff for predicting meaningful IKDC improvement (area under the curve = 0.892). Directional analysis showed no significant differences between anterior and posterior placements; however, a distal deviation >4.5 mm was associated with significantly poorer IKDC, KOOS pain, and KOOS sports outcomes.Conclusion:This study establishes a PROM-based cutoff for femoral tunnel positioning in MPFLR. A deviation of >7.2 mm from the anatomic insertion significantly compromises functional improvement, and excessive distal displacement has a particularly detrimental effect on outcomes.Keywords: femoral tunnel position, medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction, minimum clinically important difference, patellar instability

Recurrent patellar dislocation (RPD) is a common and disabling condition, particularly in adolescents and young adults engaged in sports. The incidence of acute patellar dislocation is estimated at approximately 23 per 100,000 person-years, and up to 40% of patients may develop recurrent instability after the first dislocation, with substantially higher recurrence rates reported in high-risk populations.4,17,25 Recurrent instability is associated with pain, functional impairment, reduced quality of life, and an increased risk of patellofemoral osteoarthritis. 1

The medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) is the primary passive restraint of lateral patellar translation,2,11,22 and its reconstruction has become the foundational surgical technique for restoring patellofemoral stability. Among several technical factors, femoral tunnel position is widely regarded as one of the most critical determinants of successful outcomes,7,13,20 although some studies have reported a minimal clinical impact.3,14,23 Biomechanical studies have shown that nonanatomic fixation can overload the medial patellar cartilage or, conversely, create graft laxity and instability.10,20 Although these effects have been well characterized mechanically, the association between the tunnel position and patient-reported functional recovery remains unclear.

Previous studies examining the influence of femoral tunnel placement on surgical outcomes have primarily relied on empirical or distance-based parameters, such as tunnel diameter and experimental offsets, to assess positional accuracy.6,13,19,21,23,27 Consequently, to our knowledge, no widely accepted outcome-based definition exists for the permissible tunnel placement margin in MPFL reconstruction (MPFLR). Furthermore, biomechanical studies suggest that errors along the proximal-distal axis exert a greater influence on graft length change and patellofemoral loading than anterior-posterior displacement. 10 However, clinical evidence remains inconclusive, with some reports of poorer results after proximal displacement 7 and others finding no effect of anteroposterior (AP) variation. 26 Therefore, this study's purpose was 3-fold: (1) to evaluate postoperative improvement after MPFLR using multiple patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) regarding the minimal clinically important difference (MCID); (2) to determine an outcome-based definition of the optimal displacement for femoral tunnel placement; and (3) to investigate the directional effects of femoral tunnel malposition on PROMs and clinical outcomes. We hypothesized that deviations from the anatomic femoral insertion would adversely affect postoperative PROMs, with vertical deviation expected to have a greater impact than AP displacement.

Methods

Patients

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Severance Hospital (IRB No. 4-2025-1417), and the requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective study design. This retrospective cohort study included patients who underwent MPFLR for RPD between March 2011 and August 2023 at our institution. A total of 63 knees (62 patients) were initially identified. Patients were included if they (1) experienced at least 2 episodes of RPD before surgical stabilization, (2) had closed physes confirmed on plain radiographs, (3) underwent isolated MPFLR or MPFLR with concomitant medializing tibial tubercle osteotomy (TTO), (4) had complete clinical and radiological data, or (5) had a minimum follow-up of 2 years. The exclusion criteria included patients who (1) underwent revision MPFLR, (2) underwent concomitant procedures such as medial closing wedge osteotomy or cartilage restoration surgery (eg, microfracture or autologous chondrocyte implantation), (3) experienced combined multiligament injuries or patellar fracture, or (4) were lost to follow-up within 2 years.

Surgical Technique and Postoperative Rehabilitation

All procedures were performed by a single senior orthopaedic surgeon (S.H.K.) in accordance with a uniform MPFLR protocol. Before reconstruction, an autogenous semitendinosus tendon graft was harvested. A small oblique incision was made over the pes anserinus, and the semitendinosus tendon was identified near its distal insertion. The accessory fascial band was then carefully released. Using an open-loop tendon stripper, the tendon was first detached from its tibial insertion and subsequently harvested proximal to the musculotendinous junction. The harvested tendon was folded and prepared on a back table to create a doubled graft measuring approximately 5 mm in diameter. Both free ends were then whipstitched using Ethibond (Ethicon Inc). Patients with a computed tomography (CT)-based tibial tubercle-trochlear groove distance of >20 mm were considered to have bony malalignment and underwent TTO. The degree of medialization was individualized based on the patient's anatomy and patellar tracking. Arthroscopic lateral retinacular release was selectively performed based on intraoperative assessment of patellar eversion and lateral retinacular tightness.

After the completion of any indicated bony realignment, a medial parapatellar incision was made to expose the medial retinaculum and prepare the medial aspect of the patella. Two suture anchors (Y-Knot RC, ConMed) were positioned along the superior two-thirds of the patella, and the autogenous semitendinosus graft was secured to these anchors with nonabsorbable sutures, leaving both free ends for subsequent femoral passage.

The femoral attachment site was identified by palpation of the adductor tubercle and medial femoral epicondyle to ensure placement between these 2 anatomic structures and further confirmed under fluoroscopic guidance according to the radiographic landmarks. 18 Care was taken to obtain a true lateral fluoroscopic view during femoral tunnel localization. A 5-mm femoral tunnel was uniformly created in all cases, and femoral fixation was performed at 15° of knee flexion using a 6-mm bioabsorbable interference screw (GENESYS Matryx, ConMed). After graft passage, tension was applied while assessing patellar tracking. Care was taken to avoid overconstraint by ensuring that the patella could be translated laterally by approximately 1 quadrant at the time of fixation. Partial weightbearing ambulation with an extension-lock brace was permitted for the first 6 weeks after surgery. During the initial 2 weeks, the brace was maintained in full extension, and the range of motion (ROM) was restricted. From 2 to 4 weeks postoperatively, ROM exercises were initiated with a target of 60° of flexion, progressing to 90° by 6 weeks, and 120° thereafter. The brace was discontinued after 6 weeks once sufficient quadriceps control was achieved. All patients regained full ROM by 3 months, and a return to sports activities was permitted at 6 months postoperatively.

Clinical Outcome Evaluation

Clinical outcomes were assessed using PROMs, including the International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC), Kujala, Lysholm, Tegner, and Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS). All PROMs were obtained preoperatively and at the latest follow-up (minimum of 2 years postoperatively). The patients completed standardized questionnaires during routine outpatient visits, and the results were documented in electronic medical records.

The MCID was determined using a distribution-based method, defined as 50% of the standard deviation of the change from pre- to postoperative scores for each PROM.5,16,24

Radiographic Evaluation

Postoperative CT scans were available for 37 knees and used for 3-dimensional (3D) reconstruction. Immediate postoperative CT scans were routinely obtained as part of the institutional postoperative evaluation protocol during the study period, after implementation of routine CT assessment. They were not performed specifically for this study. Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine data were processed using the Mimics software (Version 17; Materialise) to generate 3D bone models. 15 Using the 3-matic software (Version 17; Materialise), a true lateral projection of the distal femur was established by superimposing the posterior aspects of the femoral condyles.

The center of the femoral tunnel was identified on a true lateral image. The anatomic femoral insertion of the MPFL was determined according to LaPrade et al. 12 The tunnel position was then evaluated by measuring the linear distance to the anatomic point and by evaluating its location in X–Y coordinates on the true lateral projection, where the Y-axis was defined as the line connecting the adductor tubercle and medial epicondyle, the origin was set at the anatomic MPFL insertion, and the X-axis was drawn perpendicular to the Y-axis through the origin (Figure 1).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the R software, Version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Clinical outcomes before and after surgery were compared using paired t tests for normally distributed data and Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed data.

The association between femoral tunnel placement and postoperative PROMs was evaluated using the Pearson correlation test. Variables that demonstrated statistically significant and clinically relevant correlations with PROM improvement were selected for further analysis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was then conducted on these selected variables using the achievement of the MCID for each PROM as a dichotomous outcome. The optimal cutoff value for femoral tunnel placement was defined at the point maximizing the area under the curve (AUC).8,9

Directional analyses were performed to assess the potential influence of tunnel malpositioning. Tunnels were categorized according to the direction of displacement along the AP and proximal versus distal axes relative to the anatomic point. Subgroup analyses were performed to examine how displacement in each direction was related to postoperative clinical outcomes, and further gradation along each axis was analyzed to evaluate whether increasing displacement magnitude was systematically associated with PROMs. Patients with tunnels in the proximal region were excluded from the subgroup analysis because of the small sample size and the distinct nonisometric behavior associated with proximal tunnel placement, as reported in previous studies. 7

Measurements (distance, angle) were performed by 2 independent observers (B.H.J. and J.L.) trained as orthopaedic surgeons in sports medicine and knee surgery. Each parameter was measured twice by these surgeons, with a minimum interval of 4 weeks, and they were blinded to each other's measurements and to their previous measurements. Intra- and interobserver reliability were assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient. Interobserver reliability ranged from 0.89 to 0.93, and intraobserver reliability from 0.86 to 0.91.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Between March 2011 and August 2023, a total of 63 knees (62 patients) underwent MPFLR with or without concomitant TTO for RPD. Three knees (3 patients) were excluded because of revision MPFLR (1 patient), concomitant medial closing wedge osteotomy (1 patient), or autologous chondrocyte implantation (1 patient). Of the remaining 60 knees (59 patients), 10 knees (10 patients) were excluded because they were lost to follow-up within a minimum of 2 years.

After applying these criteria, 50 knees (49 patients) were included in the analysis of patient-reported outcomes. Among these, 37 knees (36 patients) with available postoperative CT scans were included in the femoral tunnel position analysis (Figure 2).

PROMs and MCID Achievement

At the final follow-up, all PROMs showed significant improvement compared with the preoperative values (P < .001 for all comparisons), except for KOOS Symptoms (P = .002) and KOOS Pain (P = .050). Cutoff Value for Clinically Relevant Improvement

Pearson correlation test revealed significant negative correlations between femoral tunnel distance and several PROMs, including IKDC (r = −0.548; P < .001), KOOS pain (r = −0.449; P = .005), KOOS ADL (r = −0.439; P = .007), KOOS sports (r = −0.360; P = .029), and Kujala score (r = −0.419; P = .010), indicating that increased femoral tunnel deviation was associated with poorer clinical outcomes (Table 3).

Subgroup Analysis of Femoral Tunnel Orientation and Distal Deviation

A subgroup analysis was performed to evaluate the influence of tunnel position and distal deviation. Four tunnels located proximal to the reference point were excluded from this analysis. When patients were stratified by tunnel direction across the adductor tubercle-medial epicondyle (AT-ME) line into anterior (n = 18) and posterior (n = 15) groups, no statistically significant differences were observed in any PROMs (all P > .05) (Figure 5). However, when assessing vertical deviation along the AT-ME line, Pearson correlation analysis demonstrated that greater distal deviation was significantly associated with lower IKDC (r = −0.400, P = .021), KOOS ADL (r = −0.352; P = .045), and KOOS Sports (r = −0.421; P = .015) scores. ROC curve analysis identified a cutoff value of 4.5 mm, with the highest discriminative ability observed for IKDC (AUC = 0.815) (Figure 6). When the patients were dichotomized at 4.5 mm into distal (>4.5 mm, n = 14) and proximal (≤4.5 mm, n = 19) groups, the distal group showed significantly poorer outcomes for IKDC (P = .015), KOOS Pain (P = .026), and KOOS Sports (P = .027) scores than the proximal group (Figure 7).

Discussion

This study's primary findings were that a greater femoral tunnel deviation from the anatomic insertion was associated with inferior postoperative PROMs. ROC analysis identified a 7.2-mm cutoff for suboptimal outcomes, and distal deviation >4.5 mm resulted in significantly poorer IKDC, KOOS Pain, and KOOS Sports scores than proximal placement. These results support our hypothesis that although overall tunnel deviation compromises outcomes, vertical deviation has a relatively greater impact than AP displacement.

The MCID values calculated in our cohort (≈9 for Kujala, ≈11 for Lysholm, and ≈10 for IKDC) were highly consistent with those reported by Qiao et al 16 and Walsh et al. 24 This finding reinforces the external validity of these findings. Whereas previous studies primarily focused on defining absolute MCID thresholds, this analysis incorporated MCID achievement rates that ranged from approximately 38% to 68% across PROMs. Although these rates may appear modest, this approach provides complementary insights by shifting the focus from mean score improvements to the proportion of patients who experienced a clinically meaningful benefit.

Previous investigations proposed various thresholds to define femoral tunnel malposition; however, these were largely based on empirical judgments. Hopper et al 6 and Tscholl et al 23 suggested that a deviation of ≥10 mm from the anatomic site should be considered malposition, a definition grounded primarily in clinical experience. Servien et al 19 and Maione et al 13 recommended a cutoff of ≥7 mm from the Schöttle point, reasoning that this distance approximated the typical femoral tunnel diameter and therefore represented a clinically relevant margin of error. Zhang et al 27 reported a threshold of ≥5 mm from the Schöttle point, drawing on cadaveric findings by Stephen et al that a 5-mm offset altered graft isometry and patellofemoral mechanics. In contrast, the present study derived a 7.2-mm cutoff using ROC curve analysis directly linked to PROM-based MCID achievement, thereby establishing a scientifically rigorous and clinically meaningful threshold. Importantly, this PROM-driven cutoff not only reflects functional recovery from the patient's perspective but also converges with the ranges reported in earlier anatomic and biomechanical studies (5-10 mm). This concordance suggests that previous empirical definitions were not arbitrary but were validated by patient-reported outcome data, reinforcing the appropriateness of 7.2 mm as a clinically relevant reference threshold.

In the subgroup analysis of femoral tunnel position, 4 tunnels located proximal to the reference point were excluded. This decision was based on 2 considerations: (1) Hu et al 7 have already demonstrated that proximal femoral tunnel malposition leads to inferior clinical outcomes due to marked nonisometry, and (2) the small sample size (n = 4) precluded meaningful statistical analysis. After excluding these cases, subgroup analysis confirmed that AP placement did not significantly influence PROMs, whereas increased distal deviation was associated with poorer outcomes. These findings align with those of Yoon et al, 26 who reported no significant differences in PROMs between anterior and posterior tunnel placements. However, when the femoral tunnel was analyzed specifically along the proximal-distal axis, increased distal deviation was associated with inferior PROMs, with statistically significant differences observed beyond the 4.5 mm threshold. This pattern is consistent with the cadaveric study of Kernkamp et al, 10 who demonstrated that proximal-distal displacement has a more profound impact on MPFL anisometry than AP variation. Altogether, these results suggest that although 7.2 mm may represent a clinically relevant reference threshold, the acceptable range for femoral tunnel positioning may not be perfectly circular, but rather asymmetrical with increased sensitivity to distal deviation. Nevertheless, confirming this hypothesis requires further studies with larger patient cohorts.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. Although PROM analysis was performed on 50 knees, only 37 knees with available postoperative CT scans were included in the tunnel position analysis, potentially reducing statistical power. Nevertheless, standardized imaging and measurement protocols were used to ensure data consistency and reliability. Furthermore, all procedures were performed by an expert surgeon, resulting in femoral tunnel placements clustered near the anatomic MPFL insertion, primarily located between the adductor tubercle and medial epicondyle. Consequently, the tunnel positions across the full range of possible directions cannot be comprehensively assessed. Furthermore, the MCID values were calculated using a distribution-based method rather than anchor-based methods, which may limit clinical interpretability. However, the derived thresholds were consistent with those reported in similar populations. Some patients underwent concomitant TTO, and chondral lesions were present in a subset. Although no statistically significant differences in postoperative PROM improvements were observed by TTO status or the presence of chondral lesions, these subgroup analyses may have been underpowered to detect subtle differences due to the relatively limited sample size and variability in outcome measures. Therefore, the potential confounding effects of concomitant bony realignment procedures and cartilage pathology cannot be completely excluded. In addition, minor concomitant procedures, such as lateral release and loose body removal, were performed selectively based on intraoperative findings. Although no postoperative redislocations or major complications were observed during a minimum 2-year follow-up period, the potential influence of these concomitant procedures on postoperative PROMs cannot be completely excluded. Future studies should include larger and more heterogeneous cohorts to validate and refine the cutoff thresholds, and the use of anchor-based MCID determination would provide a more robust link between statistical improvement and patient-perceived outcomes.

Conclusion

This study established a PROM-based cutoff value for femoral tunnel positioning in MPFLR. A deviation of >7.2 mm from the anatomic insertion significantly compromises functional improvement, and excessive distal displacement has a particularly detrimental effect on outcomes.
