Health advocates to address social adversity in pediatric liver transplantation: A clinical trial
Authors: Sharad I. Wadhwani, Bethany Reyna, Andrea Huerta, John C. Bucuvalas, Laura M. Gottlieb, Catherine Lee, Courtney R. Lyles, Sue J. Rhee, Amy M. Shui, Jennifer C. Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Children undergoing liver transplantation experience socioeconomic disparities in long-term outcomes, partly due to unaddressed material economic hardships such as food insecurity and housing instability. Health Advocates—trained lay health workers who assist families in navigating social and medical systems—may mitigate these disparities. We pilot-tested a Health Advocate intervention (NCT05700799) to assess feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary effects on caregiver experience and clinical outcomes in pediatric liver transplant recipients.Methods:In this single-center study, 18 caregivers/child dyads participated in a 90-day Health Advocate program; they were provided weekly support from trained navigators. Navigators helped to identify and connect participants with community-based resources and communicated reported social risks to the transplant team.Results:The patients’ median age at transplant was 1.1 (0.7, 4.6) and the median age at study enrollment was 8.6 (4.3, 13.1) years; 83% remained in the intervention until their needs were met or the end of the 12-week intervention. Caregivers reported improvement in comfort discussing social risks with the transplant team and improved overall care experience (both median 1-point change). Clinical parameters, including the Medication Level Variability Index, trended toward improvements. Qualitative analysis demonstrated high enthusiasm for the intervention, with themes of enhanced communication, reduced stress, and practical assistance addressing diverse social needs.Conclusions:A Health Advocate intervention for pediatric liver transplant families was feasible, acceptable, and associated with improved caregiver experiences. These preliminary findings support testing this intervention in a multi-center effectiveness and implementation trial to evaluate its impact on improving longer-term transplant outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Pediatric liver transplantation is the only life-saving intervention for children with end-stage liver disease. While there have been tremendous surgical advances over the past 50 years, long-term outcomes remain sub-optimal.1,2 Moreover, racial and socioeconomic disparities pervade the transplant continuum, starting pre-transplant and extending through long-term survival.3–6 These disparities may be explained, in part, by the added material economic hardships experienced by families with limited financial means. In the previous work, we have found that households experiencing material economic hardship (eg, food insecurity, housing instability, transportation challenges, or utility needs) are at increased risk of T-cell-mediated rejection within the first year following liver transplantation.7,8 In addition, we have identified several aspects of transplant care that exacerbate economic hardship—for example, the compounded stress of lost wages, increased out-of-pocket expenses, and increased caregiving responsibilities—and others that affect the ability of the healthcare teams to address those stressors, including variable social worker availability and engagement.9,10

Health Advocates, or lay healthcare members with no formal medical training, have been used in other healthcare settings as a strategy to address healthcare disparities.11,12 These team members can help families identify and connect with community-based resources, navigate their medical care, and alert the medical team to specific hardships that the family may be experiencing. In pediatric primary care, for example, this model has resulted in improved health outcomes and decreased healthcare utilization.11–13 To adapt this program for pediatric liver transplant recipients, we conducted in-depth qualitative interviews with patient caregivers and transplant team members to identify gaps in the current care paradigm, and then we engaged them in a set of human-centered design focus groups to develop the intervention components of a pediatric liver transplant-specific health advocate intervention (Figure 1).

Conceptual model of the Health Advocate role. The conceptual model highlights how the Health Advocate supports caregivers and transplant teams.

In this study, we piloted the new program to assess the feasibility and acceptability of the refined Health Advocate program to support children and families after liver transplant (HEAL-Tx Pilot, NCT: 05700799). We organized the program evaluation around the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework, an implementation science framework for evaluating especially suitable for evaluating complex health system interventions. 14

METHODS

This study was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul; this study was reviewed by the University of California, San Francisco IRB (22-36504) and all participants provided written informed consent. This was a single-center pilot study at the University of California San Francisco (UCSF). The clinicians on the pediatric liver transplant team at UCSF include 6 hepatologists, a surgical director, a nurse practitioner, a nurse coordinator, and a social worker. Neither the transplant program nor the children’s hospital has any existing navigation programs (eg, a cultural navigator program).

Health Advocate intervention

Health Advocate model adaptation

We engaged transplant stakeholders (ie, clinicians and caregivers) in a set of human-centered design focus groups to adapt an established Health Advocate program to meet the needs of transplant families. 15 The original Health Advocate intervention was developed by Laura M. Gottlieb and is currently implemented at a large safety-net pediatric primary care practice. Key adaptations included: (1) helping families identify both community-based and hospital-based resources, and (2) alerting the transplant team to the social risks families are experiencing.

Health Advocate training

Prior to enrolling participants, our Health Advocate (Bethany Reyna, a clinical research coordinator) underwent training with the supervisor of the established Health Advocate program to learn how the Health Advocates support families in identifying and connecting with community-based resources. This training included a 1-hour one-on-one session with the supervisor, a 2-hour overview of the established Health Advocate program, workflow, and resource navigation tools, a 1-hour shadowing session with an established Health Advocate, and a 1-hour meeting with a social work manager to review escalation procedures, reportable events, and approaches to navigating patient situations.

Health Advocate intervention overview

The pilot Health Advocate intervention lasted 90 days and focused on (1) building rapport with the caregiver, (2) identifying and connecting families with community-based resources to address existing hardships, and (3) alerting the transplant team to new hardships the family was encountering. At the start of the intervention, the Health Advocate conducted an intake interview to understand each family’s social risks and assets. Then, the Health Advocate used existing information about community resources to provide tailored support based on identified risks. Examples included finding neighborhood food pantries, helping families complete Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) applications, and finding free legal aid. After the initial consultation, the Health Advocate called families weekly to identify ongoing needs and help families address those needs. The Health Advocate would also communicate updates to the clinical team on the families’ needs so they could provide additional support as necessary (eg, writing a letter of medical necessity).

Participants

We intentionally kept our inclusion criteria broad because we wanted to maximize enrollment rate to refine the intervention and identify implementation challenges. Therefore, we did not specifically select patients on the basis of any specific social risk factor. Rather, we enrolled patients based on a holistic assessment of benefit. Weekly, we reviewed the upcoming patients seen in the outpatient clinic for liver transplant follow-up. We reviewed the results of the clinic-based Accountable Healthcare Communities 10-question social risk screening. We approached families with reported social risks (ie, food insecurity, housing instability, transportation challenges, and/or utility resource needs) or those whom the transplant team believed could be a good fit. Inclusion criteria included: (1) pediatric liver transplant recipients <18 years of age at the time of enrollment, (2) parent/legal guardian able to provide informed consent and willing to participate, and (3) transplant team identified as having specific hardships that the Health Advocate could support. Exclusion criteria included: (1) the child being a ward of the state, (2) the family being non-English/non-Spanish speaking, (3) non-US residents, and (4) the liver recipient being older than 18 years at the time of enrollment.

Data collection procedures

We collected qualitative and quantitative data during the intervention. At the start of the intervention, caregivers completed a survey and an in-depth interview, which both helped the Health Advocate understand their social risks and assets and served as baseline research data. Caregiver-reported survey measures included select questions on patient experience (from the National Survey for Children’s Health), experience with social care, social risk assessment (using the Accountable Healthcare Communities tool and single financial strain question), and the global assessment of child health question. The research team conducted in-depth interviews at 30, 60, and 90 days during the intervention. A clinical research coordinator (CRC) (Andrea Huerta), who was not serving as a Health Advocate, conducted all in-depth interviews to encourage honest dialogue. Participant caregivers completed a post-intervention survey at the conclusion of the intervention. EHR data from the pediatric patient were extracted 6 months after enrollment in the study.

Study measures

Intervention intensity and fidelity

We kept detailed journal entries each time the Health Advocate contacted a participant, a community-based resource, or a transplant team member on behalf of a particular participant. The Health Advocate also documented the purpose of each contact.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was changes in caregiver experiences with social risk discussions. We asked about their overall experience discussing their social risks with the transplant team on a 5-point Likert scale. We also summed questions (each with a 5-point Likert scale response) on (1) overall experience, (2) how comfortable parent felt, (3) how respected the parent felt, and (4) whether it was a good use of time to discuss social and economic issues, where the minimum score was 4 and the maximum score was 20, and higher values indicated more favorable experiences. These questions were derived from qualitative data on factors driving patient experiences of social care. 16

Blood biomarker changes

We measured objective laboratory measures, including changes in the Medication Level Variability Index (MLVI) and changes in alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT).

Qualitative interviews

We collected qualitative assessments of intervention effectiveness and specific Health Advocate functions most helpful to caregivers and transplant team members based on interviews with caregiver participants throughout the intervention.

Analysis plan

The primary aim of this pilot study was to pilot-test the feasibility and acceptability of the Health Advocate intervention. Since this pilot study was not intended to test the hypothesis that the intervention improved parent-reported or patient health outcomes, nor provide effect size estimates, we did not report p-values.18,19 For survey data, we developed Sankey diagrams to show changes in response from pre-intervention to post-intervention.

Qualitative interviews were audio recorded and transcribed (or translated and transcribed for Spanish-speaking families) using a professional transcription service (TranscriptionWing). We structured a qualitative assessment around RE-AIM, an implementation science framework. First, we generated a codebook with example quotes for each construct. The primary qualitative codes from this framework were “effectiveness”, “adoption”, and “implementation”. Two clinical research coordinators (Bethany Reyna and Andrea Huerta) coded all interview transcripts and Health Advocate journey entries. After they each separately coded a transcript, they would meet to discuss the codes and adjudicate any disagreements. After all transcripts were coded, we thematically analyzed each code to identify themes. We met as a group to discuss emerging themes. All qualitative data were analyzed using Dedoose.

This study was approved by the UCSF IRB (22-36504), and all caregiver participants provided informed consent.

RESULTS

We recruited N=18 caregiver/patient dyads (Table 1). The patients were a median of 1.1 years at the time of their liver transplant, and a median of 8.6 years at the time of enrollment in this pilot study; therefore, participants in this intervention were long-term pediatric liver transplant recipients. Most of the patients were female, of “other” race, and of Hispanic ethnicity. The median ALT and GGT at time of enrollment were 25 (IQR 19, 42) and 34 (IQR 16, 44), respectively. Of the 18 caregiver/patient dyads that consented to enrollment, 15 (83%) stayed in the intervention for the entire 12-week duration. Those who left the study early withdrew before intervention activities commenced (N=2) or were lost to follow-up after 11 weeks of intervention (N=1). Intervention intensity and fidelity

The Health Advocate made a median of 12 (IQR 10, 18) calls to each caregiver. The Health Advocate made a median of 13 (IQR 6, 17) resource referrals per participant, and 94% of caregivers established a connection with the resource. The Health Advocate alerted the clinical team to concerns she identified for 81% of participants, and the clinical team adjusted care plans based on that information for 44% of the participants.

Changes in experience discussing social risks

For overall experience, we observed improvements, with before and after median scores of 4 and 5, respectively. For the summed responses to (1) overall experience, (2) how comfortable the parent felt, (3) how respected the parent felt, and (4) whether it was a good use of time to discuss social and economic issues, we found an improvement from before (16.5; IQR 13, 18) and after (17.5; IQR 14, 20) the intervention, respectively.

Changes in caregiver experience

Sensitivity to caregiver needs

The median composite score decreased from 8 (IQR 5, 11) pre-intervention to 6 (IQR 5, 8) post-intervention, indicating improvement in caregiver experience.

Sharad decision-making

Before and after intervention, the median composite scores were 4 (IQR 3, 6) and 3 (IQR 3, 4), respectively, with lower scores reflecting more positive experiences.

Overall perceptions of child health

The median pre-intervention and post-intervention caregiver perception of overall child health was 3 (IQR 2, 3) and 2 (IQR 1, 3), respectively, with lower scores indicating better perceived health.

DISCUSSION

We developed and pilot-tested a Health Advocate intervention tailored to the needs of families living post-liver transplant to address families’ experiences of social adversity. We observed improvements in all quantitative parent-reported and clinical outcomes study measures, suggesting that such an intervention could have health benefits. Qualitative data from caregivers supported the quantitative findings, revealing high levels of intervention acceptability. Moreover, we demonstrated that implementing the Health Advocate program and study procedures associated with program assessment was highly feasible. These results suggest that providing targeted supports using Health Advocates to address social adversity may be a viable strategy to improve pediatric liver transplant outcomes, laying the research groundwork for a larger, multi-center randomized clinical trial to test this intervention in the unique context of liver transplantation.

The qualitative feedback from this pilot was overwhelmingly positive. Many of the caregivers commented that they wished this program were available to them earlier in the transplant process. The demands on families over the first year of transplant are much higher than in later years—Health Advocates could be particularly helpful during this period, and this feedback should be incorporated into future work. These qualitative data suggest the program works by (1) addressing unmet social needs, (2) addressing health system complexity, and (3) providing emotional support to caregivers. This finding mirrors reports in the literature documenting that social care may improve health outcomes by strengthening emotional support and trust with the clinical team.20–24 The Health Advocate customized their intervention strategy to the needs of each individual participant. Some examples of the support provided included (1) finding and attending a legal aid phone call for a landlord dispute, (2) helping a parent format a resume and find a job, (3) locating neighborhood food banks, (4) finding free immigration services, and (5) attending a meeting with a child’s school to provide context for the Individual Education Plan (IEP). While much of the support provided did not require advanced training, we estimate the Health Advocates spent an average of 3.8 hours per participant in needs assessment, in addition to gathering and providing information about support services.

Despite the intensive nature of the intervention, this strategy could be a cost-effective solution to addressing disparities in the transplant system. In this pilot, the Health Advocate provided several supports that could yield large cost savings to both health systems and payors by facilitating timely access to healthcare. For example, the added support in scheduling a ride to a clinic appointment for families experiencing transportation challenges could reduce no-show rates, improve clinic efficiency, and result in cost savings to the health system. Similarly, helping families navigate challenges with immunosuppression refills could lead to improved adherence and reduced risk of immunosuppression-related complications (eg, T-cell-mediated rejection—TCMR), which would result in cost savings to health insurers. Our Health Advocate was a college graduate with no advanced medical training and with an entry-level job title. In contrast, social workers have advanced degrees with associated higher salaries, and our qualitative interviews with social workers uncovered that they often feel overburdened by their workloads.9,10 With the Health Advocate spending about 4 hours per participant, this model could offer a low-cost and scalable approach to providing social care support for families throughout the transplant process. Therefore, we expect the Health Advocate role to complement rather than replace or extend social work support. Future cost–benefit analyses to health systems and payors could identify strategies for funding this and similar interventions.

We previously found that children living with household social adversity experience increased risk of adverse outcomes. 7 We co-produced this Health Advocate program specifically with and for families of children with recent liver transplants using rigorous human-centered design methods. 15 In contrast to other navigation programs focused solely on healthcare navigation, the resulting program piloted in this study focused on helping families address social adversity (eg, food insecurity or housing instability) in addition to helping families manage challenges they faced navigating the healthcare system. Evidence for the addition of social care navigation (in addition to healthcare system navigation) suggests that it leads to improved health outcomes in primary care settings.11–13,25 However, evidence of similar programs focused on addressing social adversity in subspecialty pediatrics remains limited, 26 but emerging data suggest they could improve clinical outcomes.27,28 Integrating social care into pediatric hepatology offers an opportunity to provide holistic care, in line with integrative hepatology.29–31

Given the wide variability in transplant team structures and resources, multi-center effectiveness and implementation data will be essential before recommending that the Health Advocates be scaled. Since starting this pilot, we have identified specific social risks (ie, food insecurity, housing instability, transportation challenges, and utility needs) at the time of transplant that predict later risk of T-cell-mediated rejection and increased healthcare utilization over the 2 years following liver transplantation.7,32 Future studies of intervention effectiveness should specifically target recruitment for these high-risk families to better understand this intervention’s ability to address disparities. Moreover, future studies should include outcomes important to a broad range of stakeholders. Here, we observed decreases in MLVI, an objective biomarker of immunosuppression adherence that predicts risk of TCMR, 33 across the study period. Using the SIREN social care logical model as a guide, 20 future studies could include clinical outcomes (eg, TCMR) and parent-reported outcomes (eg, caregiver burden) that are consistent with this theory of effectiveness. Moreover, in focus groups we held with our community advisory board to design future studies, parents identified child quality of life, burden of morbidity (eg, number of TCMR episodes), and markers of liver inflammation as priority outcomes they wish to see measured. These results underscore the importance of designing future studies specifically to assess the effectiveness of this intervention on priority outcomes.

This study should be interpreted in the context of the following limitations. First, this was a pilot study that was not powered to detect clinically meaningful differences in clinical outcomes. The intention was to measure the feasibility and acceptability of the study. We nonetheless observed improvements in all parent-reported and clinical outcomes. Second, we used broad enrollment criteria based on subjective assessments of who would “benefit” rather than selecting the highest risk patients. Our goal was to increase the generalizability of this intervention before widespread testing. Future studies could include high-risk groups, such as children with household material economic hardships, as this may optimize the power to detect improvements in clinical outcomes. 7 Third, many caregivers reported favorable survey responses before the intervention, potentially creating a ceiling effect for the caregiver-reported outcomes. That we still observed improvements in these outcomes suggests that the intervention could be a viable strategy for improving parent/patient experience. Finally, social desirability bias in pre-post responses could limit interpretability. However, we took several steps to minimize this bias, including having a second CRC who was responsible for obtaining consent and measuring outcomes.

We found that this pilot intervention was feasible to implement and evaluate. Qualitative feedback from participants suggested strong intervention acceptability. Despite the small number of participants, we also saw positive impacts across all quantitative outcome measures. Well-powered studies should test the effectiveness of this intervention in reducing transplant-related complications.
