Engagement and cognitive load of upper‐year medical trainees during mixed reality–enhanced dissection
Authors: Geoffroy P J C Noel, Isabella Xiao, Maher Chaouachi, Alexandru Ilie, Jeremy O'Brien, Sean C McWatt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMixed reality (MR) offers a way to visualize and manipulate complex digital objects in three dimensions, which is particularly beneficial for human anatomy. However, implementing MR effectively requires a deep understanding of its effects on cognitive processes. The purpose of this study was to evaluate cognitive markers of students' engagement and cognitive load while they used MR technology to overlay donor‐specific diagnostic imaging onto the corresponding body donors in a fourth‐year medical elective course. During two separate dissection sessions, each participant (n = 12) used the imaging on (1) a head‐mounted Microsoft HoloLens and (2) an Apple iPad to examine the underlying anatomy of their body donor before beginning dissection. During each activity, participants wore portable five‐lead electroencephalographic (EEG) devices to collect cognitive processing data. Separate indexes were calculated from those data to quantify engagement (engagement index; EI) and cognitive load (theta‐alpha ratio; TAR), which were compared between HoloLens and iPad usage. Mean EI calculated from EEG data collected while using the HoloLens (0.499 ± 0.038) was significantly higher than the mean EI while using an iPad (0.297 ± 0.037; p = 0.002). Conversely, the mean TAR calculated from EEG data collected while using the HoloLens (1.508 ± 0.047) was significantly lower than that collected while using an iPad (1.813 ± 0.071; p = 0.012). These results indicate that the use of HoloLens to superimpose radiographic images onto a human body donor during dissection is significantly more engaging and requires less cognitive effort than the same task on an iPad.Keywords: augmented reality, cognitive load, electroencephalography, engagement, extended reality, medical education, medical imaging, mixed reality

INTRODUCTION

Human anatomy is a fundamental subject in medical and health sciences education. Despite this, the hours attributed to human anatomy education in North American medical curricula have declined in recent years due to the increasing breadth and depth of knowledge that today's medical students are required to learn. 1 , 2 This has encouraged widespread integration of various basic sciences courses in order to maximize efficiency in the use of the curricular time that remains. 3 One area of medical education that is particularly well‐suited for integration with human anatomy teaching is medical imaging. 4 , 5 , 6 However, the interpretation of three‐dimensional (3D) images from modalities such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) requires the ability to visually navigate anatomical structures through mental rotation. 7 Modern 3D digital interfaces may offer advantages over traditional two‐dimensional (2D) screens by enhancing students' ability to visualize structure rotations and therefore more easily interpret such images. 8 , 9 , 10

Extended reality (XR) is an inclusive term for technology that enables immersive digital environments such as virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), and mixed reality (MR). These technologies have garnered great interest among anatomy educators due to their highly visuospatial nature and their potential use for the integration of 3D imaging during laboratory‐based teaching. 11 , 12 , 13 Of particular interest to educators and administrators alike are interactive modalities such as MR, which can be used in concert with traditional learning activities. 14 While VR introduces the user to an entirely digitally constructed virtual space, AR can incorporate elements of virtual reality such as 3D digital images and computer‐generated models into the user's view of their surrounding physical environment, and MR can enhance the AR format by allowing the user to interact with the digital elements as if they were real‐world objects. Accordingly, MR can be incorporated into existing anatomy education approaches, such as dissection‐ or prosection‐based laboratory sessions, as an interactive supplement. For example, students can use MR to digitally superimpose 3D medical imaging, like MRI or CT scans, onto human body donors to assist in 3D visualization and guide their dissections.

The use of XR technologies in teaching can also offer unique advantages for student engagement by igniting their motivation to spend more time using the XR environment and, as a result, encourage them to interact more deeply with the educational content. 15 , 16 High levels of engagement are important for students to maximize their interactions with the learning materials and tasks, which ultimately benefits the quality of their learning outcomes. However, in addition to engagement, medical courses that utilize XR technologies should also consider how they operate within cognitive load theory. 17

Cognitive load theory describes learning as a process of integrating new information from the learner's limited “working” memory into their long‐term memory. 18 It is divided into three domains: intrinsic load, which pertains to the complexity of the material itself; germane load, which encompasses the learner's prior knowledge, motivation, and mental effort required to process new information; and extraneous load, which describes the irrelevant information in a task that distracts from learning. 18 Given the fixed nature of intrinsic load, the goal is to minimize extraneous load such that germane processing can be maximized. 18 Given the visuospatial nature of XR modalities and their novelty in anatomy education, the impact that they have on students' cognitive load is important to assess when incorporating them into a learning environment. 19

A major challenge that comes with using metrics like engagement and cognitive load as measurements of the effectiveness of XR is quantifying them. Other than some survey‐based studies, 20 , 21 , 22 little research has been conducted to assess the effect of XR on cognitive load in anatomy education. While survey‐based data provide important contributions to the scientific assessment and validation of new technologies in teaching, they rely upon accurate self‐reporting from students, which is inherently subjective and potentially biased. Alternatively, biometric data collected in real‐time during students' use of XR technologies, like MR headsets, can be a much more reliable option for quantifying nuanced phenomena like engagement and cognitive load. Some physiological measures that can be used to gauge these metrics include electroencephalography (EEG), functional near‐infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), and gaze tracking measurements. 23 , 24 One non‐invasive approach for measuring brain activity is EEG, which can record electrical signals generated by the brain through electrodes placed on the scalp. The recorded electrical waveforms have frequencies that can be interpreted to represent different mental processes. 25 In humans, these frequencies include delta (≤4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), and beta (≥13 Hz) waves, though the frequency ranges included in each are variably defined in the literature. 25 Since the amplitudes of these frequencies increase or decrease independently according to the subject's cognitive activity, indices can be calculated from these data to represent changes in cognitive states like engagement or cognitive load. 26 , 27 , 28 Specifically, the ratio of beta wave amplitude to the sum of alpha and theta wave amplitudes (engagement index; EI) can be used to quantify task engagement, 29 and the ratio of theta wave amplitude to alpha wave amplitude (theta‐alpha ratio; TAR) has been correlated to task‐dependent changes in cognitive load. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33

Thus, the present study aimed to use EEG to quantify the engagement and cognitive load associated with using MR versus touchscreen tablets to integrate radiographic imaging into dissection‐based anatomy learning in a four‐week intensive (approximately 30 h/week) fourth‐year medical selective, Anatomy for Surgeons (AFS), at McGill University. Students' cognitive activity was recorded using EEG, analyzed for engagement (quantified by EI) and cognitive load (quantified by TAR), and compared between two learning tasks: (1) using a Microsoft HoloLens MR device to superimpose donor‐specific radiographic images onto the body donor during dissection, versus (2) using a control resource, the Apple iPad to view the donor‐specific radiographic images on a 2D touch screen during their dissection.

It was hypothesized that the MR‐assisted method would elicit higher engagement and impose lower cognitive load than the control method. It indeed addresses several of Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning principles 34 : the spatial contiguity principle, as corresponding radiological and physical anatomical structures are presented in integrated rather than separated spaces; the temporal contiguity principle, as digital information and physical dissection occur simultaneously; and the coherence principle, as extraneous details are eliminated. By overlaying digital radiological information directly onto the cadaver, MR technology minimizes the split‐attention effect—a cognitive phenomenon that occurs when learners must mentally integrate multiple sources of information presented in different spatial locations, which consumes valuable cognitive resources that could otherwise be directed toward meaningful learning.

Findings from this study will be used to better elucidate the actual impact of MR use on cognitive processing in anatomy laboratory‐based learning.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from McGill University's Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences Institutional Review Board in Montreal, Québec, Canada (A12‐E86‐19A), and all participants provided informed consent before data collection began. The educational design of the course examined in this study required the procurement and use of medical imaging specific to the human body donors used for dissection from the Dossier Santé Québec (DSQ)—a system in the province of Québec, Canada, that stores patients' medical imaging and health information for use by select healthcare professionals. Therefore, written permission to access and retrieve premortem donor‐specific diagnostic imaging from the DSQ was obtained directly from the 6 body donors used in the study (if prior to death) or from their respective power of attorney. Before presenting any of the diagnostic images to the students, they were anonymized by a McGill University faculty member (J.O.) who is responsible for overseeing the use of such images. Once the course began, all anonymized premortem imaging collected from the DSQ and acquired within the two years preceding the death of each donor was made available to students enrolled in AFS, but only within the human anatomy laboratory. 5 Donors were selected according to the extent of premortem imaging available so participants in the study had access to a broad range of imaging.

Study population

All 4th year medical students registered in the full‐time four‐week‐long AFS course during the Winter 2020 (n = 30) were invited to participate in this study. Written consent was collected from 16 students in total (53.3%). All participants were invited to complete an online questionnaire to collect demographic data like age, sex, and previous experience with touchscreen and XR devices (Table 1).

Mixed reality software

To conduct this study, ten iPad devices were available for radiographic image display on a 2D screen, and four Microsoft HoloLens devices were available for stereoscopic radiographic image overlay in MR. The iPad and HoloLens devices utilized the Novarad picture archiving and communication system (NovaPACS) image review station and OpenSight system (Novarad® Healthcare Enterprise Imaging, American Fork, UT), which is a U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)‐approved MR program for Microsoft HoloLens with 510(k) clearance for use in pre‐operative surgical planning. 35

The diagnostic images from the selected body donors were taken premortem, within a two‐year period prior to death, to ensure high quality as demonstrated in the literature. 36 These images were CT scans, MRI, with and without contrast, acquired from various scanners over time, and formatted for digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) and uploaded to the NovaPACS platform. The resolution of each scan was not considered in the study. The scans from each donor were manually retrieved from NovaPACS on encrypted WiFi via the OpenSight system, launched using the OpenSight application, and displayed on either the 2D iPad screens or as a 3D virtual hologram in MR using the Microsoft HoloLens devices. When using the iPad devices as a reference, participants could rotate and manipulate the images on the 2D touch screen, but they could only align the images with the body donor mentally. When using the HoloLens, however, participants were able to manually superimpose the digital images onto the body donor using a series of hand gestures to align the hologram to anatomical contours and body landmarks, as described in previous studies. 35 , 37 , 38 Participants could then use the projected donor‐specific diagnostic imaging to examine their body donor, scrolling through different planes of view and examining the images from different physical perspectives by moving their head or body positioning, as if the images were real‐world objects (Figure 1).

To allow the research team to monitor what the Microsoft HoloLens user was seeing in real time, a television screen (60″ Panasonic TH60PF30; Kadoma, Japan) was equipped with a Microsoft Wireless Display Adaptor (Model P3Q‐00001; Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA) via the HDMI port, which allowed for screen mirroring from the HoloLens device to the television screen.

Data collection equipment

All EEG readings during participants' use of the iPad and HoloLens devices were recorded using a Muse EEG headband (InteraXon Inc.; Toronto, Canada) as illustrated in Figure 2A. Twelve Muse headbands were available for the collection of EEG. These headbands are commercially available, light, portable, and simple to use devices that contain bilateral anterior frontal dry‐electrode leads (AF7 and AF8), bilateral conductive temporo‐parietal rubber ear sensors (TP9 and TP10), and three reference sensors (FPz, FP1, and FP2) (Figure 2B), making them more suited for data collection in authentic environments than multi‐channel wet‐electrode machines. 39 During each session with either the HoloLens or iPad devices, students wore a Muse headband while orienting and interacting with the CT imaging interface to supplement their dissections. The device transmitted the recordings of cortical activity through Bluetooth via the Muse Direct smartphone application. The output sampling rate is 220 Hz, with 2 μV (RMS) noise. Active noise suppression is achieved with a FPz‐FP1‐FP2 feedback configuration using centrally positioned frontal sensors. The input range of AC coupled signal (low cutoff at 1 Hz) is 2 mV. This application allowed the research team to monitor the quality of the connection throughout each trial using a proprietary color‐coded band that changed depending on the level of connectivity. It also allowed the research team to store the recordings from each trial on a cloud‐based storage system and export the raw data for analysis.

(A) Participant wearing both Microsoft HoloLens and Muse EEG headband. (B) The 7‐sensor montage is capable of producing bipolar readings using FPz (center of forehead), FP1 (left forehead), FP2 (right forehead) as the references for AF7 (anterior frontal left), TP9 (temporo‐parietal left), AF8 (anterior frontal right), TP10 (temporo‐parietal right).

Protocol

On the first day of the course, an out‐of‐laboratory EEG trial was conducted to collect the participants' baseline cortical activity without any stimuli. For this, each participant sat in a classroom while wearing a Muse EEG headband, and a one‐minute recording was made at rest. Specifically, participants were instructed to sit in a comfortable position with their eyes closed and to breathe calmly while attempting to clear their mind of any thoughts they may be having. Subsequently, participants were then asked to repeatedly close and open their eyes to mimic common artifacts produced by those movements, move their heads to mimic artifacts produced during dissection, and open and close their jaws to mimic artifacts produced when talking. Those recordings were used in the signal processing of the in‐laboratory recordings.

On the first day of the course, students were permitted to practice using the HoloLens and iPad devices during a one‐hour session in the laboratory supervised by members of the research team. The goal of this session was to ensure that all students had some experience using the devices prior to data collection, thereby minimizing bias from previous experiences with MR or touch screens and reducing the impact of the user interface on data related to cognitive load. Since all participants were familiar with how to use the touch screens on the iPad devices to interact with the images, the main focus of the session was on using the HoloLens devices to navigate an MR environment with which the participants had little to no experience. During this session, participants learned how to use the voice and gesture commands to open the imaging interface and interact with the CT images (zoom, rotate, move, etc.) within the MR environment. Guidance was offered from the research team as necessary, and all participants were able to practice with the device until they were satisfied with their ability to use it effectively.

For the remainder of the course, the iPads and HoloLens devices containing donor imaging were available for students to use while planning and executing their dissections. EEG data were collected from both cerebral hemispheres during a focused 4–7 min period immediately prior to the first incision in the dissection procedure. This specific timeframe was selected to examine the cognitive processes involved in pre‐dissection anatomical visualization, a critical planning phase where participants mentally construct spatial relationships before physical intervention. By restricting our EEG analysis to this pre‐incision phase, we controlled for variability in overall session length and specific anatomical regions being studied (e.g., upper limb versus head and neck), thereby isolating the effect of visualization technology on spatial understanding of anatomy prior to dissection, using either the iPad (control) or HoloLens (experimental) modality. The participants therefore started some of their dissections using the images displayed on an iPad as a reference and others while viewing the images in an MR environment with the HoloLens. When using either modality, the participants donned a Muse EEG headband to record their cortical activity. Thus, each participant had one EEG recording of their first use of an iPad and one EEG recording of their first use of the HoloLens, each collected during their approach to a new dissection.

At the end of the course, a questionnaire adapted from 40 was also used to collect the participants' general perceptions of the images used, the integration of diagnostic imaging and dissection‐based anatomy education, and the use of iPads and HoloLens devices as learning tools in the anatomy laboratory on seven‐point Likert‐type scales. 36

Processing of EEG data

Frontal EEG recording has been shown to be sufficient to assess cognitive load 25 , 41 ; therefore, the Muse headband was deemed to be a sufficient data collection tool for this study. The data recorded from the anterior frontal leads (AF7 and AF8) were imported into the MATLAB toolbox (MathWorks; Natick, MA), EEGLab, 42 for processing. First, a 1 Hz high‐pass filter was applied to remove artifacts caused by skin potentials. Second, a 60 Hz low‐pass filter was used to remove non‐physiological frequencies (electrical interferences). Third, artifacts such as eye blink, tongue, and neck movements were removed through visual inspection and adaptive finite impulse response filter using the reference signals recorded during the baseline exercise. Lastly, independent component analysis was used to remove components that correlate with the identified artifact. Poor quality channels or noisy segments with amplitude exceeding 100 μV were also removed.

The data were then separated into one‐second epochs with 0.5 seconds of overlap. Fast Fourier transform was applied to each channel to generate frequency bins with a width of 1 Hz for each epoch. Absolute spectral power (μV2) of each frequency band (theta: 4–8 Hz, alpha: 8–13 Hz, and beta: 13–30 Hz) of each segment was computed and normalized by z‐score calculation.

After the initial processing, EI and TAR were computed for each epoch. Several studies demonstrate that alpha waves decrease in amplitude under conditions with higher cognitive loads, while theta waves increase under those same conditions. 33 , 43 The ratio of theta to alpha wave amplitudes can, therefore, be used as a reliable index to represent overall cognitive load. 33 , 44 Further, engagement has been associated with increases in beta wave amplitude relative to alpha and theta wave activity, allowing EI to be calculated by dividing the amplitude of beta waves by the sum of the amplitudes of the alpha and theta waves. 29 As its name suggests, this index is often used to quantify the wearer's mental stimulation, or engagement, during a given task. 29 Accordingly, these two ratios were calculated in this study to provide a more objective and nuanced examination of the participants' cognitive processes than would be available through the use of only one metric or self‐reported data alone.

Although EEG amplitudes are recorded in microvolts (μV), EI and TAR are expressed as unitless ratios. Once calculated for each epoch, a median filter and a moving average filter were applied. The median filter was run across four epochs, whereas the moving average filter was run across 15 epochs.

Data analysis

All data were compiled using Microsoft Excel version 2304 (Redmond, WA) and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software version 26 (Armonk, NY). Because not all students who participated in the EEG collection completed the survey (and vice versa), these data were analyzed separately. The EI and TAR calculated from the EEG recordings under the baseline, iPad, and HoloLens conditions were averaged across all participants. These data were examined for outliers, normality, and sphericity, then two one‐way repeated measures analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were run to examine the differences in (1) mean EI and (2) mean TAR between each trial condition (baseline versus iPad versus HoloLens). No violations of normality or sphericity were detected for either dataset. Two outliers were identified in the EI data, but ANOVAs run with and without those outliers yielded near identical results; therefore, the outliers were retained in the final analyses.

Test significance was declared at p ≤ 0.05 for all tests. All data is presented as the mean and its standard error unless otherwise stated.

For the survey data, since they were collected via seven‐point Likert‐type scales, they represent non‐parametric ordinal data and were analyzed accordingly. The medians of the responses to the questions pertaining to their perceptions of the iPads versus the HoloLens were compared using the related‐samples sign test, as they were within‐subject comparisons.

RESULTS

Electroencephalographic data

In total, 12 students (40.0% of class) participated in all EEG trials for the study (baseline, iPad, and HoloLens). Demographic data including mean age, sex, and previous MR experience is outlined in Table 1.

The one‐way repeated measures ANOVA run to examine the differences in mean EI collected during the baseline (0.210 ± 0.013), iPad (0.297 ± 0.037), and HoloLens (0.499 ± 0.038) conditions detected a significant main effect [F(2,22) = 35.22, p < 0.001, partial η 2 = 0.762; Figure 3]. Pairwise comparisons indicated that the mean EI calculated from EEG data collected under the iPad condition was significantly higher than that collected during the baseline trial (p = 0.042). Also, mean EI calculated from EEG data collected under the HoloLens condition was significantly higher than the mean EI at baseline (p < 0.001) and while using an iPad (p = 0.002). These results indicate that the use of the iPad and HoloLens both increased students' engagement relative to baseline, but the use of the HoloLens to superimpose CT images onto a human body donor during dissection was significantly more engaging than examining the same images on a 2D iPad screen while dissecting.

DISCUSSION

Anatomy educators have always been interested in new emerging technologies to implement in the training of future health professionals. In this study, the use of MR to superimpose radiological images while working with a human body donor was investigated using EEG to measure brain activity related to engagement and cognitive load. The results of this study demonstrated that the use of MR reduced cognitive load and increased engagement when compared to the use of an iPad to view the same images.

Technology and cognitive load

Modern approaches to health sciences education are evolving with the increasing accessibility, affordability, and advancement of mixed reality (MR) technologies. Technologies like MR allow the user to understand, explore, and appreciate spatial relationships. 21 , 45 , 46 The results from this study support previous reports from Henssen et al. 20 , Küçük et al. 21 , and Li et al. 47 , which used subjective measures (self‐reported data) of cognitive load. Li et al. 47 hypothesized that allowing surgeons to intuitively learn the unique anatomical structures of their patients while using MR in the surgical field could relieve stress during the operation and increase confidence in their surgical process. Similarly, using MR in clinical training helped lower the learning curve associated with ventricular targeting among residents at different levels of postgraduate training. 48 Further, among medical students, using MR helped to improve the accuracy of inexperienced students when inserting a thoracolumbar pedicle screw. 49

Technology and engagement

The current study also found that learning in MR was significantly more engaging than examining the same medical images on a 2D touchscreen. This was a similar finding to those of previous studies which have found that AR helped to present content in an integrated and interactive form, while also decreasing the learners' cognitive load. 20 , 21 Other studies that investigated the use of AR concluded that users enjoyed using the resource and that it helped to increase their motivation to learn. 50 , 51 Similar to the findings of this study, others reported that using AR helped provide a more engaging and effective learning method while maintaining an enjoyable learning environment. 52 , 53 When comparing XR modalities to other traditional teaching methods, studies have also shown that AR increased motivation to learn and was considered to be more interesting than the less immersive traditional tools. 54 , 55 , 56 On top of the aforementioned studies that reported on the benefits of using AR from a students' perspective, others documented the utility of AR to enhance the users' spatial comprehension and provided them with the ability to better visualize abstract structures in 3D. 21 , 53 , 55 These findings demonstrate that using XR modalities like AR and MR can help users improve their understanding and ability to learn in comparison to traditional methods. 55 , 56 , 57

The findings suggest a complex interplay between engagement and cognitive load, which, while distinct constructs, appear to be interconnected in the context of Mixed Reality‐based anatomy education. The authors propose that two complementary mechanisms are at work in explaining these results. First, the novelty and interactive nature of the MR technology likely stimulated higher levels of student engagement compared to conventional approaches. Second, and perhaps more significantly from a pedagogical perspective, the MR modality appears to have effectively reduced extraneous cognitive load typically associated with the iPad‐based visualization method. This reduction can be attributed to the mitigation of the split‐attention effect, as MR technology spatially aligns digital radiological information with the physical specimen, eliminating the need for mental integration of separate information sources. This alignment directly corresponds with Mayer's principles of multimedia learning, particularly the spatial contiguity principle, which emphasizes the importance of presenting corresponding words and images in proximity to facilitate more efficient cognitive processing. By addressing these principles, MR technology may optimize the limited capacity of working memory, allowing students to allocate more cognitive resources to germane processing essential for developing robust anatomical knowledge structures.

Limitations

The benefit of using head‐mounted devices for MR is that they provide the user the ability to freely view the virtual world directly superimposed onto the real world, enhancing spatial awareness of the field of view while in the setting of an anatomical laboratory or in an operating room. While the HoloLens has stood out as the most widely used MR device, 14 , 50 , 51 , 58 , 59 , 60 , 61 , 62 , 63 , 64 , 65 our study did not compare other head‐mounted MR devices with different image display or interaction capabilities.

There were also many challenges related to the collection of real‐time physiological data in an authentic learning environment for this study that impose far greater limitations on the generalizability of the data in this study. For one, the EEG devices used for physiological measurement were required to be used in combination with MR headsets, as physiological data collection and MR use happened simultaneously. Most EEG, fNIRS, and eye‐tracking devices are not designed to fit under an MR headset and often require complex setups. These factors may have led to increased difficulty with connectivity and introduced noise artifacts to the signal. However, EEG data recorded with Muse compared to data recorded with analogous electrodes from a more standard 10–10 electrode EEG device showed minimal differences, 66 and a high degree of care was taken to minimize noise in the data from movement artifacts. Dry and gel‐based electrode EEG devices have also been shown to provide comparable signal quality and signal information content under mobile conditions, 39 so the authors are confident in the quality and reliability of the signals obtained.

A notable limitation of this study relates to temporal variability in the learning process. In prioritizing an authentic learning environment, the authors encountered minor, yet unavoidable differences in time spent on tasks due to individual student characteristics and anatomical region complexity. While the focused pre‐incision EEG data collection helped standardize the initial visualization phase, the authors acknowledge that different participants likely experienced varying cognitive timelines while interacting with the technologies. The decision to use averaged values for each task was a methodological compromise intended to minimize the impact of these temporal differences and focus statistical comparisons specifically on the modality (HoloLens vs. iPad). This approach, while allowing the authors to maintain ecological validity, may not fully capture the nuanced temporal dynamics of learning or account for potential EEG amplitude changes that typically occur with increasing familiarity. Future studies might explore methods for temporal normalization that preserve authentic learning contexts while enabling more granular analysis of cognitive load patterns over time.

Another limitation of this study is the inability to analyze potential order effects between participants who used the HoloLens first versus those who began with the iPad. While the possibility exists that prior exposure to one modality might influence subsequent performance with the other, our sample size did not provide sufficient statistical power to conduct a meaningful sub‐analysis examining this specific effect. Future research with larger participant cohorts should consider counterbalancing designs that can specifically isolate and quantify the impact of technology sequence on learning outcomes and neurophysiological measures.

Finally, a key limitation of this study lies in real‐time physiological data collection during a single educational encounter. Unlike conventional experimental paradigms in cognitive neuroscience or physiology, which permit multiple trials and controlled repetition to enhance signal‐to‐noise ratios, dissection‐based learning offers only a single, unrepeatable opportunity to capture neural dynamics as they unfold in an authentic setting. While this limits the extent to which artifacts and variability can be statistically normalized, it also strengthens the ecological validity of our findings by preserving the natural complexity and emotional salience of the learning environment.

CONCLUSION

The findings of this study highlight the benefits of using MR in anatomy education. The use of MR was associated with reductions in students' cognitive load when using radiological images to enhance anatomical dissection and increased their engagement in the task, which is critical for learning. Future studies should investigate the impact of using MR and other forms of XR on academic performance and the acquisition of clinical skills.
