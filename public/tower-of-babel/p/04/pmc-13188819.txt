Hemodynamic disturbances in vertebral artery dominance: a potential pathogenic mechanism in acute isolated pontine infarction
Authors: Yanquan Yuan, Shan Wei, Shijie Xu, Jiaohao Li, Yao Zeng, Yutian Zhang, Yuxin Wang, Zhiyong Huang, Xiaolong Lin, Chun Xiao, You Li, Linfa Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIsolated pontine infarction (IPI) is closely linked to the hemodynamic properties of the vertebral artery. However, the underlying pathogenic mechanisms remain insufficiently defined. This study was designed to explore the contribution of vertebral artery hemodynamic disturbances to the development of IPI and identify the optimal hemodynamic cut-off values for predicting IPI risk in patients with vertebral artery dominance (VAD).Materials and methodsA total of 381 individuals were enrolled. These included 205 patients with acute IPI and 176 control individuals without cerebrovascular disease who were hospitalized during the same period. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were used to determine infarct location and evaluate the diameter and curvature of the vertebral arteries and basilar artery (BA). Hemodynamic parameters of the vertebral arteries were measured using color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to identify the optimal cut-off values of dominant-side hemodynamic parameters for predicting IPI risk in patients with VAD. Appropriate statistical analyses were used for intergroup comparisons and correlation assessments.ResultsA total of 321 (84.25%) of the 381 enrolled subjects were diagnosed with VAD. Among these, patients with IPI had a significantly lower dominant-side end-diastolic velocity (EDV, 22.72 ± 7.29 cm/s) compared with controls. In contrast, the dominant-side resistance index (RI, 0.61 ± 0.07), pulsatility index (PI, 1.06 ± 0.21), and the peak systolic velocity/end-diastolic velocity ratio (PSV/EDV, 2.70 ± 0.61) were all significantly elevated. Subgroup analysis revealed that patients with paramedian pontine infarction showed similarly pronounced differences in dominant-side EDV (22.64 ± 7.26 cm/s), RI (0.62 ± 0.07), PI (1.07 ± 0.23), and PSV/EDV (2.75 ± 0.69) relative to the controls. Among the 192 patients with both VAD and BA curvature, 130 (67.7%) demonstrated BA curvature contralateral to the dominant vertebral artery (Spearman rank correlation, r = − 0.296, P < 0.0001). Control subjects with BA curvature showed no significant dominant-side hemodynamic abnormalities compared with VAD control subjects without BA curvature (P > 0.05). ROC curve analysis identified the optimal cut-off values for key dominant-side hemodynamic parameters: EDV ≤ 20.50 cm/s, RI ≥ 0.57, PI ≥ 0.93 and PSV/EDV ≥ 2.35.ConclusionThese findings indicate a strong association between VAD and acute IPI, mediated by significant disturbances in vertebral artery hemodynamics. A reduced EDV, accompanied by increased RI, PI, and PSV/EDV, was observed consistently, with the most marked alterations occurring in the paramedian infarction subtype. BA curvature does not represent an independent risk factor for IPI, but is merely a morphological mediator that exacerbates VAD-related hemodynamic disturbances. The identification of cut-off values of dominant-side hemodynamic parameters provides a foundation for the early identification of hemodynamic risk, thereby enabling targeted interventions for VAD-related IPI.

Introduction

According to 2021 epidemiological data, ischemic stroke remains a major global health burden, with a prevalence of 819.5 per 100,000 individuals, an incidence of 92.4 per 100,000, and a mortality rate of 44.2 per 100,000, ranking as the third leading cause of disease burden worldwide [1]. Isolated pontine infarction (IPI) accounts for approximately 7‒15% of all acute ischemic strokes [2], and early neurological deterioration occurs in approximately 14‒35% of affected patients [3, 4]. While localization of IPI lesions and their responsible vascular territories are typically identifiable, prior investigations have focused primarily on arterial stenosis or occlusion, often neglecting the hemodynamic consequences of vascular morphological variations [5].

The distribution of IPI is closely related to the hemodynamic characteristics of the vertebrobasilar system [6], although its precise pathogenic mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Vertebral artery dominance (VAD) is a common anatomical variation of the posterior circulation, occurring in approximately 68.9‒94% of the general population [7]. Although VAD is traditionally regarded as a benign congenital variant, accumulating clinical evidence suggests that it is an independent risk factor for posterior circulation infarction [8]. Patients with VAD show a higher incidence of infarction within territories supplied by the basilar artery (BA) and posterior inferior cerebellar arteries, and clinical outcomes appear to correlate with the degree of asymmetry in the vertebral artery diameter [9]. Despite these observations, studies specifically examining how VAD induces hemodynamic alterations in pontine infarction are limited, and there are no clinically actionable cut-off values for hemodynamic parameters to predict IPI risk in patients with VAD. Therefore, further investigation is needed to clarify the role of vertebral artery hemodynamic disturbances in the pathogenesis of IPI and identify optimal hemodynamic cut-off values for risk stratification, to improve early risk stratification and targeted prevention strategies for IPI.

Materials and methods

Participant recruitment

This investigation adopted a case–control study design. A total of 381 hospitalized individuals were enrolled, including 205 patients diagnosed with IPI and 176 participants without cerebrovascular disease. All subjects were admitted to the neurology departments of Huizhou Central People’s Hospital and Huizhou Third People’s Hospital from August 2020 to December 2022. The diagnosis of IPI was established according to the Trial of ORG 10,172 in the Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) classification and was specifically categorized as the small-vessel occlusion subtype [10]. Diagnostic confirmation was based on a comprehensive evaluation, incorporating clinical presentation, neurological examination, and neuroimaging findings. Patients were excluded from the study if they had a history of transient ischemic attack, coronary artery disease, intracerebral hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage, chronic infectious diseases, malignant tumors, hematological disorders, immune-related diseases, or systemic inflammatory disorders. The control group consisted of individuals without cerebrovascular disease who were matched to the IPI group by age, sex, and ethnicity and were recruited during the same period. The exclusion criteria for the control group similarly included a history of cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, infections, cancer, hematological disorders, immune-related diseases, or systemic inflammatory disorders. The study protocol conformed to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Huizhou Third People’s Hospital (Approval No.: SY Ethics [2021] No. 28) and Huizhou Central People’s Hospital (Approval No.: kyll2023029). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Imaging analyses

All patients underwent head and neck computed tomography angiography (CTA) using a 128-slice gemstone spectral CT scanner. The scanning parameters included a tube current of 300 mA, a tube voltage of 120 kV, and a slice thickness of 1 mm. All subjects were placed in the supine position with their heads and necks firmly immobilized and the neck fully exposed. Iohexol contrast medium was administered intravenously, and scanning was performed from the aortic arch to the intracranial arteries in a craniocaudal direction. The scanning data were post-processed, and three-dimensional reconstruction was performed. Patients with IPI and VAD were identified and classified based on the CTA findings. A total of 176 patients with VAD-associated pontine infarction were included and subsequently stratified into four subgroups according to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) characteristics: Group A, paramedian type (supplied by the paramedian arteries, n = 88); Group B, ventrolateral type (supplied by the short circumferential arteries, n = 36); Group C, tegmental type (supplied by the long circumferential arteries, n = 18); and Group D, mixed type, involving two or more of the above infarction patterns (n = 34). In addition, a VAD group without cerebrovascular disease, comprising 145 individuals, was established. All enrolled subjects underwent vascular ultrasonography of the bilateral vertebral and basilar arteries. For the assessment of VAD, the junction at which the bilateral vertebral arteries converge to form the BA was defined as the reference origin. The diameter of the vertebral artery was measured at three consecutive segments at 3-mm intervals at the V2 segment, and the mean value was calculated. A side-to-side difference in diameter of ≥ 0.3 mm was used to define vertebral artery dominance, with the larger vessel designated as the dominant artery [11]. The length of the BA was defined as the straight-line distance from the origin of the artery to its termination, while the curvature was quantified as the perpendicular distance from the point of maximal deviation to the straight line connecting the origin and termination of the BA [12]. Based on the location of this maximal deviation, BA morphology was categorized as left-curved, right-curved, or straight. Dominant and non-dominant vertebral arteries were identified from integrated CTA findings, and bilateral hemodynamic parameters were measured using color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI). CDFI was performed in patients with IPI within 72 h of symptom onset, alongside CTA and MRI, using a Hitachi Arietta 70 color Doppler ultrasound diagnostic system equipped with a 2–12 MHz linear array transducer. Measurements were specifically focused on the V2 segments of the vertebral arteries, with the scanning depth set to 20–50 mm. The Doppler angle was maintained at ≤ 60° during measurement, and the gain was adjusted to ensure clear delineation of the blood flow spectral waveform. The recorded hemodynamic indices included the peak systolic velocity (PSV), end-diastolic velocity (EDV), mean velocity (Vm), resistance index (RI), pulsatility index (PI), and the PSV/EDV ratio. All the hemodynamic parameters were measured manually and independently by two experienced senior attending physicians specialized in vascular neurology. If the discrepancy between the measurements obtained by the two physicians exceeded 10%, a third senior chief physician was appointed as a reviewer. Ultimately, either the consensus result or the mean value of the original measurements was adopted, thereby mitigating operator-dependent subjective errors. Our Doppler measurements of vertebral artery hemodynamic parameters (PSV, EDV, RI, and PI) were performed according to standardized methodology [13, 14].

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were first assessed for normality and homogeneity of variance. Data that met normal distribution criteria are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared between two groups using the independent-samples t-test. Skewed variables are reported as median (interquartile range) and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to adjust for potential confounding factors, including sex, age, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, smoking, and stroke history. For RI and PI values, the odds ratio (OR) was estimated for a 0.01-unit increase in each index, rather than the default 1-unit increase. Associations between variables were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to determine the optimal cut-off values of the dominant-side hemodynamic parameters (PSV, EDV, Vm, RI, PI, PSV/EDV) for predicting IPI in patients with VAD, with Youden’s J index used to select the optimal cut-off point. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of each parameter. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant demographics

Baseline characteristics were compared between the 176 patients in the IPI group and the 145 participants in the control group to identify potential risk factors for IPI among individuals with VAD (Table 1). Within the VAD population, male sex, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, prior stroke history, smoking status, elevated total cholesterol (CHO), and increased low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels were significantly more prevalent in the IPI group (P < 0.05). In contrast, no significant differences were observed between the groups with respect to age, alcohol consumption, triglyceride (TG) levels, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), or uric acid (UA) (P > 0.05).

Hemodynamic comparisons between the IPI and control groups

Hemodynamic parameters of the dominant and non-dominant vertebral arteries were compared between the IPI and control groups (Table 2). On the dominant side, the IPI group demonstrated significantly lower EDV relative to the controls (22.72 ± 7.29 vs. 25.17 ± 5.26 cm/s, P = 0.016). Conversely, RI (0.61 ± 0.07 vs. 0.59 ± 0.06, P = 0.003), PI (1.06 ± 0.21 vs. 0.97 ± 0.16, P = 0.002), and the PSV/EDV ratio (2.70 ± 0.61 vs. 2.47 ± 0.38, P = 0.002) were significantly higher in the IPI group. No significant differences were observed in PSV and Vm between the two groups (P > 0.05). On the non-dominant side, no statistically significant differences were identified in PSV, EDV, Vm, RI, PI, or PSV/EDV between the IPI and control groups (P > 0.05). After adjustment for potential confounders (sex, age, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, smoking, and stroke history) in the multivariate logistic regression analysis, the significant differences in dominant-side EDV, RI, PI and PSV/EDV remained statistically significant (P < 0.05).

Hemodynamic comparisons for IPI subtypes and control participants

Hemodynamic parameters were compared between participants with different IPI subtypes and the controls. It was observed that the most pronounced hemodynamic changes occurred in the paramedian infarction subtype (n = 88, Table 3). After adjustment for confounding factors, the EDV values on the dominant side were significantly reduced relative to those in the control group (22.64 ± 7.26 vs. 25.17 ± 5.26 cm/s, P = 0.047), while the RI (0.62 ± 0.07 vs. 0.59 ± 0.06, P = 0.008), PI (1.07 ± 0.23 vs. 0.97 ± 0.16, P = 0.005), and PSV/EDV ratio (2.75 ± 0.69 vs. 2.47 ± 0.38, P = 0.005) were significantly increased (P < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups with respect to PSV or Vm (P > 0.05). Notably, on the non-dominant side, the PSV/EDV ratio was significantly higher in the paramedian subgroup compared with the controls (2.94 ± 0.68 vs. 2.71 ± 0.55, P = 0.018), while this increase was not found in the other subtypes.

A total of 321 individuals with VAD were identified in the study population, of whom 192 exhibited BA curvature (Table 7). In patients with left-sided VAD (n = 137), the BA curved toward the left in 41 cases and toward the right in 96 cases. Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed a significant negative association between the dominant side and direction of BA curvature (r = − 0.296, P < 0.0001).

Notably, EDV exhibited the highest AUC (0.64) among all parameters, while RI, PI, and PSV/EDV showed consistent diagnostic efficacy (AUC = 0.63). Due to the inherent limitations of using single parameters in the assessment of sensitivity and specificity (e.g., EDV has high specificity but low sensitivity, while PI has high sensitivity but low specificity), the combination of EDV ≤ 20.50 cm/s + PI ≥ 0.93 was found to be the preferred strategy for the precise stratification of IPI risk in patients with VAD in clinical practice.

Discussion

Isolated pontine infarction represents a clinically important subtype of posterior circulation stroke, with its pathogenesis being closely linked to anatomical variations and hemodynamic abnormalities within the vertebrobasilar arterial system [15, 16]. Increasing evidence indicates that VAD is not simply a benign congenital variant but rather an independent risk factor for posterior circulation infarction [8, 17]. In particular, patients with concomitant hypertension and VAD appear especially susceptible to disturbances in the cerebral blood flow [18]. This study focused on VAD as a key anatomical factor and systematically evaluated its associated hemodynamic alterations by assessing baseline clinical characteristics, vertebral artery flow parameters, and BA morphology in patients with acute IPI. The optimal cut-off values of hemodynamic parameters in the prediction of IPI risk in patients with VAD were also determined. The findings consistently demonstrated the significance of reduced EDV, accompanied by increased RI, PI, and PSV/EDV, with the most pronounced changes apparent in the paramedian infarction subtype. Additionally, a significant spatial correlation was observed between the curvature of the BA and VAD, with BA curvature having a pro-ischemic effect only when combined with VAD-related hemodynamic disturbances. These results offer clinically relevant evidence for improved risk stratification, early identification, and targeted prevention strategies for IPI in individuals with VAD.

This study examined vertebral artery hemodynamics in patients with VAD by directly comparing flow-related parameters between individuals with IPI and those without cerebrovascular disease. Several clinically meaningful hemodynamic alterations were identified. Specifically, on the side of the dominant vertebral artery, patients in the IPI group exhibited significantly reduced EDV, whereas the RI, PI, and PSV/EDV ratio were significantly increased compared with the values of the control participants. By focusing on hemodynamic differences within the VAD population rather than merely contrasting VAD and non-VAD individuals [19], this study extended the findings of prior investigations with closer alignment with the needs of risk stratification. Monitoring dynamic changes in blood flow in patients with established VAD, rather than relying solely on anatomical dominance, enabled a more precise identification of individuals at elevated risk for IPI. From a pathophysiological perspective, the observed reductions in EDV reflect a decreased regional perfusion reserve within the vertebrobasilar circulation. In particular, reduced diastolic flow likely compromises oxygen and energy delivery to the pontine tissue during cardiac diastole. Due to the critical role of the pons within the brainstem and its limited tolerance of ischemia and hypoxia, sustained reductions in perfusion may predispose this region to infarction. The concomitant increases in RI and PI indicated heightened downstream vascular resistance and reduced arterial compliance. This high-resistance hemodynamic state may further exacerbate perfusion deficits, leading to the establishment of a deleterious feedback loop characterized by progressive reductions in blood flow and vascular elasticity [20]. Additionally, the elevated PSV/EDV ratio suggested increased flow pulsatility, which may intensify mechanical stress on the vascular wall and increase the likelihood of thrombus formation within the perforating arteries. Notably, this study highlighted the clinical relevance of both PI and EDV, which were markedly altered in patients with IPI, indicating that the combination of these two parameters has significant value in clinical risk assessment. Prior studies have associated increased PI with adverse stroke outcomes and neurological deterioration [21, 22]. Consistent with these findings, the present results suggested that PI may serve not only as a predictive marker for IPI risk in patients with VAD but also as a potential indicator of prognosis, thereby offering complementary value for both risk stratification and outcome assessment in clinical practice.

The results of the subgroup analyses revealed substantial heterogeneity in hemodynamic patterns among the different IPI subtypes. In the paramedian infarction subtype, EDV on the dominant side was significantly reduced, accompanied by increases in RI, PI, and PSV/EDV. On the non-dominant side, the PSV/EDV ratio was elevated, making this subtype the most hemodynamically distinct. The ventrolateral subtype demonstrated increased RI, PI, and PSV/EDV exclusively on the dominant side. In contrast, no significant hemodynamic alterations were detected in the tegmental subtype. Patients with mixed-type infarctions exhibited reductions in PSV, EDV, and Vm on the dominant side, whereas the non-dominant side showed no significant hemodynamic changes. These subtype-specific differences are likely attributable to the anatomical characteristics of the arterial supply to distinct pontine regions. Paramedian infarctions are supplied by the terminal perforating arteries with minimal collateral support, rendering them particularly sensitive to hemodynamic disturbances. Ventrolateral regions receive blood from short circumferential arteries with limited collateral capacity, whereas tegmental regions are supplied by long circumferential arteries with extensive collateral networks, conferring greater tolerance to flow alterations. Mixed-type infarctions involve multiple vascular territories, resulting in more complex hemodynamic changes. Collectively, these findings provide hemodynamic evidence supporting a refined classification and mechanistic interpretation of IPI subtypes. Continuous monitoring of vertebral artery flow parameters using CDFI may therefore assist in the localization of infarction, elucidation of underlying mechanisms, and the guidance of individualized therapeutic strategies.

Among the 192 patients with VAD who also exhibited BA curvature, 67.7% demonstrated curvature toward the side contralateral to the dominant vertebral artery. This spatial relationship was likely attributable to asymmetrical flow velocities and pressure gradients between the bilateral vertebral arteries in the setting of VAD [12, 23]. Persistent shear forces and directional pressure at the vertebrobasilar junction may have imposed uneven mechanical stress on the BA wall, causing displacement toward the side with lower resistance and ultimately resulting in vascular curvature. Notably, subjects with BA curvature showed no significant hemodynamic abnormalities compared with VAD subjects without BA curvature, which confirmed that BA curvature itself is not an independent risk factor for IPI as its pro-ischemic effect is only manifested when combined with VAD-related hemodynamic disturbances. Following BA deformation, its perforating branches, particularly the terminal arteries supplying the paramedian pontine region, are prone to thinning due to tensile forces generated by curvature, a vulnerability amplified by their characteristic right-angled origin. This structural configuration likely reduces downstream perfusion. At the same time, localized reductions in flow velocity at the curvature site may promote blood stasis, platelet aggregation, and thrombosis [24, 25]. These mechanisms provide a plausible explanation for the predominance of paramedian infarctions among patients with BA curvature, as the terminal perforators lack collateral circulation and are therefore highly susceptible to hemodynamic instability. In contrast, the tegmental regions have greater collateralization provided by the circumferential arteries [26]. Furthermore, BA curvature may alter blood flow direction and distribution, exacerbating regional perfusion imbalances within the pons [27]. Together, these observations offered a novel anatomical and hemodynamic framework for understanding IPI classification, diagnosis, and pathogenesis.

This study also identified the optimal cut-off values of dominant-side hemodynamic parameters as EDV ≤ 20.50 cm/s, RI ≥ 0.57, PI ≥ 0.93, and PSV/EDV ≥ 2.35. PI was found to be an important clinical indicator in IPI risk assessment with an AUC of 0.63, while EDV is a key parameter reflecting diastolic cerebral perfusion and showed the highest AUC value (0.64) among all the indices. Currently, there are no universally accepted normal reference ranges for hemodynamic parameters in the V2 segments of the vertebral arteries due to variations in multiple factors, including ultrasound equipment, probe frequency, and patient characteristics. Thus, the cut-off values identified in this study based on the VAD population have important clinical implications for IPI risk stratification. The combined application of multiple parameters, such as EDV ≤ 20.50 cm/s + PI ≥ 0.93, is recommended for precise risk stratification, thereby compensating for sensitivity and specificity limitations associated with the use of a single parameter, and thereby enhancing the accuracy of IPI risk prediction.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the case-control design precludes definitive causal inference between VAD-related hemodynamic disturbances and IPI, underscoring the need for validation through prospective cohort studies. Second, long-term follow-up was not performed, preventing an assessment of the prognostic implications of hemodynamic parameters. Future investigations should incorporate larger sample sizes, longitudinal follow-up, and more comprehensive multivariate regression models to further clarify the relationships between vertebral artery hemodynamics and IPI risk in patients with VAD.

Conclusion

This study comprehensively characterized the relationship between vertebral artery hemodynamic alterations and different IPI subtypes in patients with VAD, and identified the optimal cut-off values of dominant-side hemodynamic parameters for IPI risk prediction. The findings establish a foundation for the early identification of hemodynamic risk and targeted interventions for VAD-related IPI. The combined application of multiple parameters is key to precise IPI risk stratification in patients with VAD in clinical practice. In addition, this study demonstrates that VAD exhibits a significant spatial correlation with BA curvature, although the latter was not an independent risk factor for IPI but merely a morphological mediator related to asymmetric vertebrobasilar hemodynamics. BA curvature was observed to exacerbate VAD-related hemodynamic disturbances, and paramedian pontine infarction is particularly vulnerable due to the anatomical features of its supplying arteries. Clinically, patients with VAD can be stratified into low- and high-risk groups using the combined parameter criteria. The incorporation of CDFI-based monitoring of these hemodynamic parameters together with aggressive management of modifiable risk factors, such as hypertension, diabetes, and smoking, may facilitate earlier detection and more precise prevention of VAD-related IPI in high-risk individuals.
