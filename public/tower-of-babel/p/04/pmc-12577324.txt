Layer by layer nanocoating of chlorhexidine-loaded micelles on polymethyl methacrylate denture base
Authors: Pattarasuda Luanareesatit, Norased Nasongkla, Chuchai Anunmana, Trinuch Eiampongpaiboon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDenture stomatitis is a prevalent oral infection commonly affecting denture wearers with inadequate oral hygiene cares. This susceptibility stems from the porous and rough nature of denture bases, which readily harbor bacteria and promote plaque accumulation. This study aimed to modify the surface of denture bases with a chlorhexidine (CHX) coating to promote antimicrobial activity against Candida albicans.MethodsDenture bases were coated with chlorhexidine-loaded poly(ethylene glycol)-block-poly(ε-caprolactone) micelles (CHX-micelles) using the solvent evaporation method. The surface was coated using a layer-by-layer (LbL) dip coating process involving CHX-micelles and poly(acrylic acid) (PAA). The total number of LbL coating cycles was adjusted, and the coating thickness, drug content, CHX-release, antimicrobial activity, and toxicity of the coated denture base were evaluated. One-way ANOVA and Two-way ANOVA were performed. Statistical significance is defined as p < 0.05.ResultsThe optimized coated denture base, with 90 layers, achieved a maximum coating thickness of 13.33 ± 2.41 μm and a CHX content of 23.64 ± 4.71 µg/cm2. This coating exhibited sustained CHX release over 14 days. Even with a non-polished side, the coated surface displayed a reduced contact angle, which would decrease fungal adhesion. The adherence of C. albicans to the surface was also significantly reduced by the antifungal activity of CHX-micelles coated denture base, and this activity sustained for 14 days. Whereas free CHX decreased cell viability to less than 80% with 50, 75, and 100% of extracted solution, CHX-micelles contributed more than 85% of cell viability, comparable to non-coated denture base.ConclusionThe development of antimicrobial denture bases using this coating method has shown promising results. The optimized coating effectively prolonged CHX release, inhibited fungal growth, and maintained biocompatibility.

Introduction

Denture stomatitis, a fungal infection linked to poor oral hygiene, frequently affects denture wearers. To combat this issue, researchers are exploring ways to develop infection-resistant dentures. Poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) resin has been a mainstay in dentistry for several years as a denture base material due to its workability and esthetics. However, it suffers from inherent porosity and roughness that harbor bacteria and plaque [1, 2]. Furthermore, the rough, unpolishable surface further encourages colonization and enhances plaque formation on teeth. Preventing these processes is crucial to avoid denture-related stomatitis [2, 3].

Modifying the denture base’s surface has been proposed to prevent or reduce bacterial adhesion. Hydrophobic interactions play a crucial role in the attachment of Candida albicans to denture surfaces. This strong adherence stems from the hydrophobic nature of both the PMMA denture base and the C. albicans cell surface. Consequently, various research efforts have focused on altering the antibacterial properties of PMMA by rendering the denture base hydrophilic to minimize hydrophobic interactions and by incorporating fillers into the denture composition or coating the denture surface with antimicrobial agents [4]. Studies have shown that incorporating chlorhexidine (CHX) into dentures or coating their surfaces with it effectively reduces biofilm formation and holds promise for preventing denture stomatitis [5]– [6]. This finding necessitates further research and development to optimize the incorporation of CHX into denture bases. The present study was to investigate the properties of CHX-micelles, surface properties of a denture base coated with CHX-loaded micelle as well as their antifungal activity and cytotoxicity effect.

Methods

Denture base specimen preparation

The heat polymerized PMMA denture base materials (Rodex, RODONT S.r.l – Milan, Italy) were mixed according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The disk-shaped specimens (10 mm in diameter and 2 mm in thickness) were prepared by placing the dough into stone molds and pressed with a hydraulic instrument and polymerized at 70 °C for 2 h. All specimens were initially machined using a tungsten carbide cutter, followed by fine machining and smoothing with burs. The surfaces were then polished with 1000, 2500, and 5000 grit alumina paper.

Chlorhexidine nanoparticles preparation and characterization

The CHX micelles were prepared by the following methods [7]. Poly(ethylene glycol)-block-poly(ε-caprolactone) (PEG -b-PCL) (NanoPolyPEG, Co. Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand) micelles were prepared using a solvent evaporation method. PEG-b-PCL and CHX (Evonik Nutrition & Care GmbH, Germany) were dissolved in tetrahydrofuran (THF) (RCI Labscan, Bangkok, Thailand) at a weight ratio of 1:9. The solution was dropwise added to distilled water under sonication with a probe at an amplitude of 80% for 2 min. The solution was then subjected to solvent evaporation on a shaker for 6 h. Finally, the CHX micelles were separated from free CHX by centrifugal filtration (Vivaspin, Littleton, MA, USA) (MW cutoff = 50 kDa). The CHX micelles were characterized by dynamic light scattering (Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern, UK) for size, polydispersity (PDI), and zeta potential. HPLC conditions were performed on a C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (Deionized water (DI) with 0.1% formic acid (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) and solvent B (97% Acetonitrile (ACN) (RCI Labscan, Bangkok, Thailand), 3% DI with 0.1% formic acid). The flow rate was 1.0 ml/min, the injection volume was 10 µl, and the column temperature was maintained at 35 °C. The detector was set to measure at 265 nm. Each experimental group was repeated three times independently.

Surface coating and characterization

Denture base specimens were incorporated with CHX-loaded micelle solution (0.4 mg/ml) by using layer-by-layer (LbL) dip-coating technique (dip coating machine; IET Solution Co. Ltd. with controller GOT 1000, Thailand). To determine the maximum drug loading in this study, specimens were divided randomly into four groups and the coating cycles were performed among 30, 60, 90, and 120 cycles (n = 3). Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM) (JEOL JSM − 6610 F, Tokyo, Japan) was used to characterize the surface topography and the thickness of the coated specimens.

In vitro release of CHX from coated denture base

The amount of CHX loading in each specimen was extracted and quantified by using HPLC analysis. Furthermore, the releasing of CHX from coated specimen was also investigated periodically (n = 3). Denture base specimens were immersed in 2 ml artificial saliva solution (pH 6.3) and on incubator shaker (New Brunswick Scientific G25, NJ, USA) at 37 °C and 90 rpm. The releasing solutions were collected and replaced with new artificial saliva at selected times (3 h, 6 h, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 days). Collected solutions were analyzed by HPLC. The column oven was set at 35 °C, and the detector was set at 265 nm. Contact angle test

The contact angle (CA) of 2 µL of DI was also measured using a video-based system (OCA 20; DataPhysics Instruments GmbH, Filderstadt, Germany) on both the polished and non-polished sides of the coated and uncoated surfaces (n = 3).

Antimicrobial activity tests

The antimicrobial activity of coated denture base (90 cycles) against C. albicans (ATCC 10231, Manassas, Virginia, USA) were investigated by using agar disc diffusion assay and microbial adhesion method. The tests were triplicated independently.

C. albicans suspension prepared in Sabouraud dextrose broth (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Sparks, MD, USA) and adjusted the turbidity to 0.5 McFarland standard (McFarland densitometer, Biosan Medical-Biological Research & Technologies, GIBTHAI CO LTD, Bangkok, Thailand). The 100 µL of diluted inoculum was spread on Sabouraud dextrose agar plates (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Sparks, MD, USA). Coated and non-coated denture bases disc were placed on the agar. Paper discs without solution and with 5% v/v of 2% chlorhexidine gluconate solution (Mdent, Bangkok, Thailand) were placed and used as negative and positive controls, respectively. All specimens were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The inhibition zone was measured at day 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14. The mean inhibition zone were calculated. After measuring the zone, the denture base specimens were transferred to new agar plate.

Microbial adhesion of C. albicans on coated and uncoated specimens was evaluated in a culture solution composed of Sabouraud dextrose broth and sterile artificial saliva (1:9 v/v). Culture solution was replaced daily. Collected solutions at day 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 were spread on Sabouraud dextrose agar plates and then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Also, at the same period of time, uncoated and coated specimens were placed in new sterile artificial saliva and sonicated at 60% amplitude for 1 min to collect fungal bound to the surface. After that, the solution was spread onto Sabouraud dextrose agar plates and then incubated at same condition. Colony forming unit (CFU) were counted. Fungal concentration was log-transformed. Each experimental group was repeated three times independently (n = 3).

Cytotoxicity test

An in vitro cytotoxicity test was conducted on a coated denture base in accordance with ISO 10993-5 standards [8]. The sample groups included a non-coated denture base, a coated denture base with CHX-micelles, and free CHX, which were all extracted following the ISO 10993-12 protocol [9]. Cytotoxicity assays were performed using L929 cells (ATCC # CCL-1, Manassas, VA, USA) and MTT assay (Sigma-Aldrich, St. louis, MO, USA). The absorbance at 570 nm was measured. Cell viability was determined compared to the control. Each experimental group was repeated three times independently (n = 3).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using PASW Statistics software (IBM Corp. Released 2019. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0, Armonk, NY, USA). Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s test were performed to determine data normality and variance homogeneity. The significance level for all tests was set at α = 0.05.

According to the characterization of the coating, antimicrobial activity, and cytotoxicity tests. One-way ANOVA and Tukey’s multiple comparison test were performed to analyze the CHX contents, thickness, inhibition zones, and the percentages of cell viability. Whereas, for the contact angle tests, Two-way ANOVA and Tukey’s multiple comparison test were performed to compare contact angles among groups.

In vitro release of CHX from coated denture base

The sustained release of chlorhexidine (CHX) from micelles deposited on denture bases over 14 days (Fig. 3). Initially, a rapid release phase occurred, with 67% (19.75 ± 0.72 µg/ml) of CHX released within the first two days. Subsequently, a sustained-release phase dominated, with a steady release rate of 1.26 ± 0.27 µg/ml/day observed between days 4 and 14.

Microbial adhesion were also examined from both collected solution and denture base surface of coated group compared to non-coated group. The CFU was counted at day 1 to day 14 and the log (CFU/ml) was calculated (Fig. 6). The results showed that coated denture bases could significantly inhibit C. albicans growth in both collected solution and denture base surface compared to uncoated groups (p < 0.05). On day 1, the coated base showed 1000-fold greater inhibition (< 10 CFU/ml), maintaining lower bacterial counts even after 14 days, the coated group (< 105 CFU/ml) showed significantly microbial adhesion compared to uncoated group (> 106 CFU/ml) (p < 0.05). In the collected solution, similar patterns were seen, at day 14 with the coated group showing lower microbial counts (< 10⁵ CFU/mL) than the uncoated group (> 10⁷ CFU/mL) (p < 0.05). Cytotoxicity test

In Fig. 7, at extracted concentrations of 100, 75, 50, and 25%, CHX-micelle coated denture base maintained L929 cell viability at 85.28%, 87.49%, 89.31%, and 93.35%, respectively. These percentages of cell viability were similar to non-coated, which remain more than 85%. Nevertheless, free CHX group demonstrated significant differences of the cell survival when compared to the others. Their percentages of cell viability were below 80% at doses of 50, 75, and 100% of extracted solution (p < 0.05). Notably, denture base coated with CHX-micelles and non-coated denture base both exhibited low cytotoxicity, indicating that the free CHX itself contributed to the apparent increase in cytotoxicity.

Discussion

One promising strategy involves coating the denture surface with antimicrobial agents [10, 11]. Our research delved into a micelle-based approach, successfully encapsulating CHX within polymeric micelles through solvent evaporation methods. These micelles possess several attributes that enhance their effectiveness, including a uniform size, positively charged surface, and nanoscale structure. Furthermore, the absence of interlayer interactions within the micelles supports their antifungal potential [7].

This study aimed to extend the CHX release profile by tailoring denture base specimens with higher CHX loading. A layer-by-layer method deposited CHX micelles onto the base, creating a bilayer with poly(acrylic acid) (PAA). While Fig. 2 showed varying CHX amounts and thicknesses across different coating cycles, FE-SEM analysis (Fig. 1A and B) clearly demonstrated the formation of a uniform and smooth CHX micelle coating on the denture base, confirming the effectiveness of the layer-by-layer dip coating technique. The proportional increase in coating thickness and CHX loading up to 90 cycles suggests that repeated deposition enhances drug incorporation and surface coverage (Fig. 1C-F). Surprisingly, the 120-cycle coating was thinner, likely due to extended deposition time and potential peeling(Fig. 1G). This indicates that 90 cycles are an optimal number of coating cycles to maximize CHX loading and thickness which is comparable to Srisang et al.‘s coated catheters [12]. This configuration was chosen for further studies.

Although this study did not directly evaluate the effect of the CHX coating on denture retention, the coating thickness measured (13.3 ± 2.4 μm) is minimal. While factors like base adaptability, border seal, and salivary properties are crucial for retention [13], research shows a gap persists between the denture base and tissue even in the conventional and digitally processed complete denture [14]. It might assume that the thin CHX coating is unlikely to substantially impact retention. However, further studies specifically evaluating the effect of such coatings on retention would help clarify this potential effect.

Preferably, denture surfaces could release antimicrobial agents in two phases: an initial burst followed by sustained, long-term release. This happens because drug molecules both bound to and present on the coating surface are released quickly, followed by gradual diffusion and hydrolysis processes releasing the remaining drug through the polymer matrix. An initial burst release of antimicrobial agents from the coated denture base is the ideal release profile. These phases, governed by the diffusion of the drug through the polymer matrix or pores, enable controlled and prolonged-term drug release [15, 16]. The crosslinking process slowed down the short-term CHX release, which was anticipated to occur within a week. In this study, we investigated the release of CHX from coated denture bases for antifungal purposes. To mimic the oral cavity environment, the release of CHX from coated denture bases was evaluated in artificial saliva. The results showed the release profiles of CHX from CHX micelles on coated denture bases from day 0 to day 14. During the initial phase (day 0 to day 2), the cumulative CHX release rate reached 67%. This finding aligns with the results reported by Srisang et al. [12]. The controlled release of CHX in this system occurred specifically during the second phase (days 4–14), when the drug release rate became steady and consistent. This release behavior is governed by diffusion through the polymer matrix, where CHX molecules gradually migrate from the inner matrix to the surface and into the surrounding environment. The release is controlled through several mechanisms, including the encapsulation of CHX within micelles, crosslinking of the polymer coating, and the inherent properties of the polymeric network, which together regulate the porosity and diffusion pathways. These structural characteristics slow the release of CHX, enabling sustained delivery over time. Additionally, the incomplete release within 14 days further indicates a slow, sustained diffusion process typical of such systems. Notably, approximately 12% of CHX remains unreleased, suggesting the possibility of prolonged CHX release beyond 14 days. In line with Hiraishi’s study demonstrating CHX release for up to 40 days [17], further investigations are necessary to confirm our results.

In addition, wettability, which determines how easily a liquid spread on a surface, plays a crucial role in microbial adhesion [18]. In denture care, this principle is particularly relevant when considering the adhesion of C. albicans, a fungus responsible for denture stomatitis. The fungus adheres more readily to hydrophobic surfaces, especially in its hyphae form, where its cell wall becomes more water-repellent. Our approach aimed to mitigate this adhesion by modifying the surface properties of dentures. The strategy, based on the idea that hydrophilic surfaces repel microbes, involved coating the denture base with materials promoting a water-loving surface [19, 20]. The effectiveness of this modification is reflected in the dramatically reduced wetting angles observed in both polished and non-polished groups (Fig. 4A).

Fungal adhesion on denture surfaces often causes denture stomatitis. Chlorhexidine digluconate mouth rinses at 0.12% effectively treat this condition, and studies by E.B. Türker demonstrate their benefits in maintaining good dental hygiene and managing palatal inflammation [21–23]. Building on this concept, researchers have explored coating denture surfaces with antimicrobial agents for sustained release and reduced microbial growth [7, 24, 25]. Our study examined the effectiveness of CHX micelles as a denture base coating. Agar diffusion test showed a zone of inhibition around the coated base after incubating with C. albicans for 24 h (Fig. 5A). Also, analyzing the diameter of this inhibition zone (Fig. 5B) revealed it to be largest on the first day, gradually decreasing thereafter. This aligns with the observed CHX release profile from the coated base, characterized by an initial burst followed by sustained release. Additionally, a microbial adhesion tests from collected solution and on denture base surface were performed. These tests revealed significantly greater antifungal activity against C. albicans on the CHX-coated denture base compared to the uncoated control (Fig. 6). Notably, on the first day, the coated base demonstrated more than 1000-fold higher capacity to inhibit fungal growth compared to its uncoated counterpart. This potent antifungal effect persisted over time. Clinically, these findings imply that CHX coating not only inhibit fungal colonization on the denture surface but also reduces microbial load in the surrounding saliva, potentially lowering the risk of oral candidiasis in denture wearers. These findings strongly suggest that the sustained release of CHX from the coated surface effectively decreases fungal growth on denture bases, potentially offering long-term protection against denture stomatitis.

Moreover, comparing cell survival among non-coated, CHX-micelle coated denture bases and free CHX solutions revealed crucial differences. All samples incubated with the L929 cells and extracted solutions were obtained following ISO 10993-12 guidelines9 and diluted to varying concentrations (100%, 75%, 50%, 25%). Interestingly, denture bases coated with CHX micelles showed remarkable biocompatibility, maintaining cell viability exceeding 80% across all extracted concentrations. Similarly, an uncoated denture base exhibited high cell survival, aligning with previous finding [7]. In contrast, free CHX demonstrated significant cell cytotoxicity below 80% when the concentration was greater than 50%. Accordingly, CHX cytotoxic effect was known to increase with both concentration and exposure duration [26]. However, one study demonstrated that mouth rinses containing lower CHX concentrations (0.2%) exhibit reduced toxicity to human gingival fibroblasts [27]. These results demonstrated that controlled CHX release from micelle-coated denture bases significantly reduces cytotoxicity compared to free CHX, offering enhanced biocompatibility. Even upon complete release of CHX from the micelles, no significant cytotoxicity was observed. This suggests that the micelle formulation modulates the release profile of CHX, maintaining its concentration within a biocompatible range and preventing the burst exposure that typically leads to cytotoxic effects. This suggests a key advantage of encapsulating CHX in polymeric micelles: the ability to control its release and significantly minimize the toxic effects compared to free CHX, potentially offering a safer approach to denture stomatitis prevention.

Nevertheless, the occlusion and abrasive forces encountered during denture use may impact the coating’s integrity. Additionally, cleaning procedures and water immersion may affect the coating’s adhesive strength. Further research is necessary to enhance the bond strength between the coating layer and the denture base.

Conclusion

The layer-by-layer dip-coating technique effectively incorporated 90 layers of CHX micelles onto the denture base, resulting in a sustained release of CHX for nearly 14 days. The coated denture bases demonstrated potent antifungal activity, prolonged drug release, and minimal cytotoxicity to the L929 cells. These findings suggest that the CHX micelle-coated denture base could have the potential to effectively decrease denture stomatitis by suppressing fungal growth while minimizing adverse effects on oral tissues.
