Nursing Care Behaviours and Challenges Faced During Truth‐Telling: A Phenomenological Study
Authors: Ayşegül Yıldız İçigen, Elif Zeynep Meti̇n Gemici
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe process of truth‐telling in nursing, particularly in the fields of oncology and intensive care, is a critical yet complex process that encompasses ethical principles, effective communication and emotional support. It involves significant challenges during the transmission of difficult information to both patients and their families.ObjectiveThis study aimed to examine the care behaviours of nurses working in oncology and intensive care units during truth‐telling and to identify the challenges they experience in this process.MethodsThis study was conducted using the phenomenological design. Sixteen nurses from different hospitals in Türkiye were selected through purposive sampling. Maximum variation sampling was utilized to ensure the widest possible diversity among participants. In‐depth interviews were used for data collection, and the data were analysed using the traditional descriptive phenomenological approach. The study adhered to the COREQ checklist for qualitative research reporting.ResultsThe study was conducted with 16 nurses from various age groups working in oncology and intensive care clinics of different hospitals in Türkiye. Study data were analysed, and three themes were elicited: the first thing that comes to the Light of Truth, the Language of Care and the Thorn of the Rose.ConclusionIt was determined that nurses used care behaviours, such as understanding the individual during truth‐telling, empathic listening to give spiritual support, showing respect, establishing open communication, answering questions, relieving concerns, showing a friendly approach and therapeutic touch. Coping with the emotional responses of patients‐relatives/the family and their own emotions during truth‐telling was the most challenging situation for nurses.Keywords: ethics, intensive care, nursing, oncology, truth‐telling

Introduction

Truth‐telling is a challenging task that nurses frequently encounter while providing nursing care [1]. In this process, information regarding disease prognosis, treatment processes, life expectancy and the success of medical interventions is often communicated to patients and their families. Nurses, who spend the most time with patients and witness their most private moments, are the healthcare professionals closest to the patient within clinical settings, acting as trusted sources of information and support.

According to Article 15 of the Patient Rights Regulation in Türkiye, patients have the right to receive verbal or written information regarding their diagnosis, treatment methods, the potential benefits and risks of these methods and possible outcomes if no treatment is administered. This information must be provided by the ‘healthcare professional who will perform the intervention’, which is typically the physician [2]. According to the supplementary provisions of the regulation, even if the healthcare professional group changes, another trained healthcare worker may provide the information when necessary; however, the primary responsibility still lies with the physician. While the Nursing Regulation does not assign nurses direct responsibility for patient education, it does include roles such as supporting the physician's explanations, clarifying the provided information, answering patient questions and offering emotional support [2]. The Turkish Nurses Association's document titled ‘Ethical Principles and Responsibilities’ emphasizes that nurses must act in accordance with the ethical principles of non‐maleficence, respect for autonomy, confidentiality, justice and responsibility. In this context, nurses play an indirect yet significant role in the information‐sharing process by supporting the patient's understanding of their health condition and enhancing their emotional capacity to cope with the information provided [3]. Therefore, for nurses, truth‐telling is not just a factual disclosure—it is a relational, ethical and emotional interaction embedded in daily care.

At times, particularly when discussing the course of illness, treatment outcomes or life expectancy, nurses may face ethical dilemmas while responding to patients' inquiries regarding their health status [4]. These dilemmas may revolve around whether or how the truth should be conveyed. In recent years, there has been an increasing consensus that patients must be informed about medical realities, no matter how unfavourable they may be. The ethical codes of international nursing organizations such as the International Council of Nurses (ICN) and the American Nurses Association (ANA) emphasize that truth‐telling is an integral component of nursing care [5, 6].

The process of truth‐telling in nursing is often evaluated within the framework of Beauchamp and Childress's [7] four fundamental ethical principles widely recognized in medical and healthcare ethics: autonomy, non‐maleficence, beneficence and justice. According to this approach, nurses must provide accurate and sufficient information to respect patient autonomy, while also considering the principles of non‐maleficence and beneficence to deliver unfavourable information as appropriately as possible. A tendency to withhold the truth may undermine patient autonomy by impeding their participation in treatment planning and decision‐making. Therefore, truth‐telling is regarded as an ethical obligation.

The primary ethical justification for truth‐telling in nursing is the necessity of building a trust‐based relationship with the patient. Trust is a key factor that significantly influences the quality of nursing care and patient satisfaction [8]. The principles of truthfulness and candour are critically important for informing the patient, ensuring treatment compliance and promoting active participation in the care process. If a nurse avoids telling the truth or withholds information, it may foster feelings of distrust in the patient, thereby negatively affecting the quality of care.

There is no evidence in the literature suggesting that truth‐telling causes harm to patients. On the contrary, it is emphasized that even knowing bad news does not harm patients [9]. What matters is how nurses, utilizing their knowledge, skills and experience, employ certain caring behaviours when conveying the truth. Caring behaviours refer to the professional and therapeutic approaches nurses use in communication with patients. These behaviours are sometimes exhibited unconsciously. Prominent caring behaviours include being present, being available, maintaining openness and accessibility, therapeutic touch, active listening, acceptance, establishing eye contact, addressing the individual by their preferred name, providing comfort and smiling [10].

Many studies in the literature have addressed the challenges, importance and information‐sharing aspects of truth‐telling [1, 11]. However, studies conducted in Türkiye have predominantly focused on evaluating nursing services, approaches to terminally ill patients and delivering bad news [12, 13]. There appears to be a lack of studies specifically exploring the emotions and difficulties experienced by nurses while telling the truth to patients. Nevertheless, nurses' experiences in this process are highly valuable. Uncovering, interpreting and sharing these experiences could enhance awareness among nurses and contribute to the development of caring behaviours.

Truth‐telling is a fundamental aspect of nursing care that has been widely discussed in the literature, often emphasizing ethical principles, communication strategies and theoretical frameworks. However, while many studies address the conceptual importance of truth‐telling, there is a relative paucity of research focusing specifically on nurses' lived experiences during this complex process. Particularly lacking are in‐depth insights into the emotional challenges, ethical dilemmas and practical difficulties nurses face when conveying difficult health information to patients and their families. Addressing this gap is critical, as understanding nurses' firsthand experiences can inform the development of more effective support systems and training programs tailored to the realities of clinical practice. Therefore, this study aims to explore the caring behaviours nurses employ and the challenges they encounter while truth‐telling in oncology and intensive care settings, thereby contributing valuable knowledge to both nursing theory and practice.

Nurses working in oncology and intensive care units frequently care for patients with poor prognoses or life‐threatening conditions [14]. Consequently, it has been reported that healthcare professionals in oncology and intensive care settings face more frequent ethical challenges [15]. This study aims to examine the caring behaviours utilized by nurses working in oncology and intensive care units while delivering critical health information such as disease prognosis, treatment plans and life expectancy to patients, and to identify the difficulties they encounter during this process.

Methodology

Study Design

This study employed a phenomenological (qualitative) research design. It was conducted in accordance with the COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) guidelines for qualitative research reporting [16].

Participants

The study population consisted of nurses working in intensive care and oncology units in Türkiye. Maximum variation sampling, a purposive sampling method, was utilized to ensure the widest possible diversity among participants [17]. In this context, the hospitals where nurses were employed, their total years of professional experience and their specific units were considered as criteria for maximum variation. During participant recruitment, an invitation was sent to nursing services managers of hospitals operating in Türkiye via the email addresses listed on their official websites. Nursing services managers referred eligible and willing nurses who met the inclusion criteria to the researchers. Subsequently, researchers established direct contact with those nurses who agreed to participate. Three of the invited nurses declined the invitation, and five nurses who accepted the invitation could not participate in the study due to scheduling conflicts.

Creswell and Poth [18] and Polkinghorne [19] suggest including between 5 and 25 participants for phenomenological studies, while Dukes [20] recommends a range of 3−10 participants. Moreover, data saturation is considered achieved when no new information emerges during interviews [21]. Hennink and Kaiser [22] argue that data saturation typically occurs between the 9th and 17th interviews. In this study, interviews were conducted with 16 nurses until no new themes emerged, and data saturation was achieved.

Data Collection Instruments

Data were collected using a semi‐structured interview form that allowed participants greater freedom to express themselves and enabled the collection of rich and detailed data. The interview form included a total of 11 questions: six related to sociodemographic characteristics and five focused on experiences of truth‐telling. The questions were developed through a literature review and consultation with three subject matter experts.

The semi‐structured interview form was pilot‐tested with two nurses to assess the clarity and relevance of the questions. These pilot interviews were conducted prior to the main data collection and were excluded from the final analysis to maintain data consistency and rigour. Necessary revisions were made based on feedback obtained during the pilot phase. Sample interview questions included: ‘What does truth‐telling mean to you?’, ‘Could you describe an experience in which you had to tell the truth?’, ‘How did you feel during this process?’, ‘Have you ever found it difficult to tell the truth?’ and ‘Which nursing approaches do you use when telling the truth?’

Interviews

Data were collected between November 2022 and September 2023 through face‐to‐face in‐depth interviews using a semi‐structured interview guide. Interviews were conducted outside clinical settings in distraction‐free environments. For this purpose, study rooms in public libraries located in participants' cities were used as interview venues. There were no third parties present during the interview, aside from the researcher and the participant.

Each interview involved two stages: the first consisted of a brief introductory conversation to build rapport and mutual trust between the participant and the researcher. The second stage involved conducting the actual interview based on the semi‐structured form. Each session lasted approximately 30–35 min. To prevent data loss, interviews were audio‐recorded and supplemented by field notes taken during the sessions.

The interviews were conducted by two researchers: a faculty member with a PhD in Fundamentals of Nursing and a lecturer specialized in Public Health Nursing. Both researchers had formal training and prior experience in conducting qualitative research.

Data Analysis

The data were analysed following the traditional descriptive phenomenological approach. Researchers adhered to the phenomenological analysis steps to gain a deep understanding of participants' truth‐telling experiences. Initially, audio recordings and field notes were transcribed verbatim, and significant statements were identified. These statements were then broken down into meaning units, from which themes were generated. Great care was taken to reveal the essence of participants' experiences without reducing the data to superficial content categories.

Throughout this process, the principle of epoché (bracketing) was observed to minimize researcher bias, focusing solely on participants' narratives (Husserl 2012) [23]. Thus, the study aimed not only to perform content analysis but also to explore the phenomenological depth and meaning of participants' experiences. Data analysis was conducted manually by two independent coders. Initially, transcripts totalling 128 pages were created from audio recordings and notes. Themes and codes were then generated. Inter‐coder reliability was calculated using Miles and Huberman's [24] reliability formula and found to be 89%. As a result of the analysis, three main themes and corresponding subthemes were identified. No software programs were used during the data analysis process.

Credibility and Trustworthiness of Qualitative Data

To ensure credibility, the coding consistency between the two coders was calculated, and coding results were further validated by comparison with an independent external researcher. An inter‐coder agreement of 89% was achieved, which is considered a high level of reliability in qualitative research [24, 25].

Reliability was further supported by thoroughly documenting each phase of the data collection and analysis process, ensuring transparency and auditability. Confirmability was ensured by meticulously preserving raw data, coding schemes and analytic decisions to demonstrate that findings were grounded in participants' narratives, independent of researcher bias. These strategies align with the verification approaches recommended by Morse [26] to ensure rigour in qualitative research, thereby enhancing the study's trustworthiness.

Ethical Considerations

Prior to data collection, ethical approval was obtained from the Non‐Interventional Research Ethics Committee of the Local University (Approval No: E‐64577500‐050.99‐26978, Decision No: 22.18).

Researchers informed participants about the study's purpose and methodology before beginning the data collection process. Participants were made aware that they could withdraw from the study at any time without consequences. All participant data were kept confidential, anonymized through coding and used exclusively for scientific purposes. Audio recordings were made only after obtaining written informed consent from each participant. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The three themes derived from the data were developed simultaneously and complementarily, with no hierarchical order or difference in importance among them. Each theme reflects different aspects of the nurses' experiences with truth‐telling.

Theme 1: The Light of Truth

Nurses consistently emphasized the indispensable role of truth‐telling in clinical practice, especially in oncology and intensive care units where uncertainty and fear are prevalent. They described that when patients and families do not receive clear or sufficient information, mistrust and emotional distress often arise. Thus, truth‐based communication was viewed as a cornerstone for building therapeutic nurse–patient relationships and ensuring ethical care. In particular, nurses highlighted that adjusting the amount and depth of information to the patient's condition and readiness was critical to avoid confusion and loss of hope. This theme includes three sub‐themes: Care Based on Trust, Effective Communication and Participation in Care.

Sub‐Theme 1.1: Care Based on Trust

Nurses emphasized the importance of gaining patients' trust, noting that trust‐based care enhances the effectiveness of nursing interventions. They indicated that securing trust is pivotal for patients to comprehend their conditions accurately, believe in the nurse's information and avoid scepticism. Failure to establish trust was associated with reactive behaviours, negative emotions and noncompliance among patients. Trust was considered the foundation of effective nursing care. Nurses explained that, especially in ICUs, patients are often isolated from physicians and rely primarily on nurses for information. Several nurses reported that when honesty was compromised, patients displayed resistance, anxiety or even anger.

In the intensive care unit, often the only person a patient can directly trust is the nurse. Patients obtain information about their health, life, environment, and family primarily through the nurse. Therefore, trust‐based care is essential for patients to hold on to life.

(N7)

Explaining terminal care decisions truthfully ensures patients know that nothing is being hidden from them, which strengthens their trust.

(N3)

For patients to comply with nurses' instructions, they must first trust the nurse. To achieve this, patients must not be misled or deceived. For instance, when vital signs are abnormal, it is essential to tell the truth to maintain trust.

(N15)

This finding reveals that truth‐telling, in addition to being a communicative act, is an ethical responsibility that sustains trust in high‐stress care environments.

Sub‐Theme 1.2: Effective Communication

Nurses identified transparent and patient‐centred communication as essential for truth‐telling. They noted that communication strategies needed to be tailored to different patient groups and contexts. In ICUs, nonverbal methods such as touch, tone of voice and eye contact were particularly important when patients were intubated or unable to speak. In oncology, nurses stressed the importance of delivering complex medical information in a clear and emotionally sensitive manner.

In oncology, we must avoid overwhelming patients with unnecessary details. Focusing on what is most relevant helps them process information without losing hope.

(N6)

In communicating with patients, emphasizing critical points and simplifying complex details based on patients’ cognitive capacities is vital to enhance understanding.

(N8)

Avoiding medical jargon and using plain language ensures that patients comprehend their health information without unnecessary confusion.

(N11)

When telling the truth to critically ill patients, I try to simplify the message and use non‐verbal cues so they understand despite their limited ability to respond.

(N10)

This sub‐theme highlights that communication in truth‐telling is context‐specific, requiring both technical knowledge and emotional intelligence.

Sub‐Theme 1.3: Participation in Care

Truth‐telling was perceived as a way to promote patient and family involvement in care, encouraging informed decision‐making and patient autonomy. Nurses described that in oncology settings, transparent communication about treatment and prognosis encouraged patients to take an active role despite the challenges. In paediatrics, age‐appropriate explanations facilitated family collaboration.

Truth‐telling enables patients and their families to actively participate in treatment decisions, encouraging more conscious and effective involvement.

(N3)

When families are kept fully informed, they contribute more actively to decision‐making and cooperate with the care team.

(N11)

When patients understand their conditions, they tend to collaborate more and show greater effort during treatments, which may accelerate recovery.

(N1)

In oncology, from the moment of diagnosis, honest communication allows patients to gradually adapt and engage more actively in their treatment.

(N4)

This indicates that truth‐telling, in addition to supporting ethical principles, also strengthens the ability of patients and families to navigate complex care processes.

Theme 2: The Language of Care

Nurses described using a variety of caring behaviours when telling the truth, ranging from empathy and emotional support to spiritual care. Nurses reported that truth‐telling was inseparable from caring behaviours that provided emotional and spiritual support. They stressed that individualized approaches were necessary, particularly in sensitive contexts such as end‐of‐life care or paediatric oncology. Paediatric nurses noted the use of storytelling and play techniques, while nurses working with adults emphasized involving family members and maintaining a calm demeanour. This theme is categorized into three sub‐themes: Empathic Listening, Spirituality and Respect and Kindness.

Sub‐Theme 2.1: Empathic Listening

Empathic listening was seen as essential for recognizing patients' emotions and ensuring they felt heard. Nurses described that active listening reduced anxiety and encouraged open dialogue, even when the information delivered was distressing.

While disclosing difficult truths, I allow patients to express emotions without interruption. This makes them feel respected and less alone in their suffering.

(N16)

While telling the truth, I strive to understand my patients’ feelings and give them time. I often use smiles to ease their concerns.

(N8)

In one case, I sensed that a patient in the ICU was not only listening to my words but also observing my tone and body language. Answering his questions attentively made him feel better and underscored for me the importance of empathic listening.

(N12)

These findings indicate that empathic listening plays a pivotal role in the truth‐telling process by reducing patients' anxiety, fostering trust and ensuring that they feel valued and understood even in emotionally challenging situations.

Sub‐Theme 2.2: Spirituality

Spiritual care was emphasized as a vital part of holistic nursing during truth‐telling, especially when patients faced life‐threatening conditions. Nurses described offering spiritual reassurance as a way of maintaining dignity and hope in critical moments.

While telling the truth, I use a gentle and reassuring tone to provide emotional support to patients and their families.

(N15)

In intensive care, spiritual needs are often overlooked. Addressing spirituality makes care more holistic rather than mechanical.

(N9)

In oncology, patients and families greatly benefit from spiritual support. Spirituality is a crucial aspect of being human.

(N5)

These findings reveal that spiritual care in the process of truth‐telling not only provides emotional relief but also supports patients and families in maintaining hope and adapting to care with dignity.

Sub‐Theme 2.3: Respect and Kindness

Nurses reported that truth‐telling must always be delivered with respect and kindness to protect patients' dignity and psychological well‐being. They noted the importance of assessing readiness before disclosing sensitive information and balancing honesty with compassion.

Before discussing a new cancer diagnosis, I assess whether the patient is emotionally ready. If not, I postpone the conversation out of respect.

(N4)

When communicating difficult news, it is important not to destroy hope but to approach patients with sensitivity and care.

(N11)

When informing families about a patient's deterioration, it is crucial that they know all necessary interventions have been made and that the nursing staff will continue to support them.

(N2)

These findings suggest that truth‐telling is not solely about information transfer but also an ethical act of caring, deeply intertwined with empathy, spirituality and respect.

Theme 3: The Thorn of the Rose

This theme reflected the dual emotional burden of truth‐telling, encompassing both relief and distress. Nurses reported that while telling the truth often led to professional satisfaction and spiritual relief, it also generated sadness, anxiety and burnout when breaking bad news. This theme consists of three sub‐themes: Patient and Family Reactions, Relief and Emotional Burden.

Sub‐Theme 3.1: Patient and Family Reactions

Nurses described managing strong emotional reactions—such as anger, denial or grief—as one of the greatest challenges of truth‐telling. These reactions were particularly intense in ICU settings, where families faced sudden deterioration and in oncology, when patients confronted terminal diagnoses.

Coping with patients’ anger and sadness is difficult.

(N10)

The most challenging part is managing the emotional reactions of patients and families, especially since we often form strong emotional bonds with them.

(N6)

In oncology, some patients refuse to accept the truth and withdraw, which makes communication more difficult.

(N9)

Sometimes patients shut down, refuse to communicate, or cry when faced with the truth. In such cases, careful handling is required.

(N5)

Providing emotional support while managing patients' and families' emotional reactions is a crucial part of the truth‐telling process.

(N7)

These findings demonstrate that patient and family reactions constitute one of the most demanding aspects of truth‐telling, requiring nurses to balance honesty with emotional support in order to sustain communication and maintain therapeutic relationships in both oncology and intensive care contexts.

Sub‐Theme 3.2: Relief

Despite the challenges, nurses reported a profound sense of relief after being honest with patients and families. This relief stemmed from fulfilling their ethical duty and avoiding deception.

Regardless of the content, telling the truth to the patient instead of hiding it provides a great sense of relief.

(N3)

Whether positive or negative, after truth‐telling, I feel a sense of spiritual relief and professional satisfaction.

(N4)

I often make several plans on how to deliver the news, but once it's said, I feel lighter and more at ease.

(N8)

These findings suggest that, despite the emotional difficulty of disclosure, truth‐telling provides nurses with a sense of inner peace, ethical fulfilment and professional satisfaction, reinforcing their integrity in clinical practice.

Sub‐Theme 3.3: Emotional Burden

Nurses acknowledged that breaking bad news often led to emotional exhaustion, especially when they had developed close bonds with patients. They reported struggling to balance honesty with the desire to preserve hope.

Sometimes I experience burnout because of the constant tension between truth‐telling and maintaining compassion.

(N1)

The most difficult part is balancing the need to support families without completely extinguishing their hope, while also managing my own emotions.

(N14)

I feel sadness and anxiety because I understand how difficult this process is for patients and their families.

(N2)

Especially when delivering news related to a patient's death, I experience anxiety, sadness and tension.

(N16)

This theme underscores that while truth‐telling affirms professional integrity, it also exposes nurses to significant emotional strain, particularly in oncology and intensive care contexts.

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the experiences of nurses working in oncology and intensive care units regarding the process of truth‐telling to patients, the caring behaviours they employed and the challenges they encountered. The findings demonstrate that nurses prioritize trust‐based care, effective communication and patient participation during truth‐telling processes. It was evident that building trust with patients enhances the quality of care and patient satisfaction, and that adhering to ethical principles during truth‐telling supports patient autonomy [8, 27].

Unlike other healthcare professionals, nurses provide continuous care around the clock, which grants them a unique moral and ethical responsibility to engage in truth‐telling regarding patients' prognoses [28, 29]. This responsibility arises from the moral dimension of nursing care. Nurses are able to deliver high‐quality care when they establish trust‐based relationships with their patients [30].

The ICN, in its Code of Ethics for Nurses, emphasizes that nurses must provide honest and sufficient information about interventions and patient conditions. It underlines that nurses must uphold the principles of truthfulness and justice at all times to protect patients' rights and prevent errors in nursing care [5].

In this study, nurses expressed that truth‐telling is crucial for patients, and when patients do not receive sufficient or accurate information, they struggle to trust nurses. Therefore, effective communication rooted in truth is necessary for fostering strong patient‐nurse interactions. Other studies have also highlighted that truth‐telling serves as a means to build communication and trust, and that nurses must balance the ethical principles of beneficence and autonomy during this process [29, 30].

Several models have been developed to guide truth‐telling practices [31, 32, 33]. These models typically address stages such as preparing a suitable environment, assessing patients' perceptions, providing information and empathetically recognizing patients' emotional reactions—mostly in the context of delivering bad news. Fundamentally, these models emphasize preparation, communication and outcome evaluation. In the present study, nurses reported employing caring behaviours consistent with these models, such as empathic listening, demonstrating respect, engaging in open communication, delivering information clearly and understandably, providing emotional and spiritual support, using a kind and reassuring tone, answering questions, alleviating concerns, building rapport and practicing therapeutic touch.

Importantly, the findings of this study highlight that the most challenging aspects of truth‐telling for nurses occurred in two main contexts: breaking bad news about terminal diagnoses in oncology and communicating sudden deteriorations in intensive care. Nurses frequently reported internal conflict between preserving hope and maintaining honesty, which significantly increased their emotional burden. While oncology nurses often struggled with the gradual disclosure of prognosis over time, ICU nurses faced urgent and unexpected situations requiring immediate communication with families under intense distress. These contextual differences emphasize the need for setting‐specific communication strategies tailored to each environment.

Consistent with prior literature, the nurses in this study described adapting their communication not only verbally but also through nonverbal cues such as tone of voice, eye contact and therapeutic touch, especially in ICU contexts where patients were intubated or unable to speak. In oncology, strategies such as assessing readiness, simplifying medical language and pacing disclosure were emphasized as essential for balancing honesty with compassion. These approaches underscore that truth‐telling is not a uniform practice but one that requires technical skill, emotional intelligence and ethical sensitivity.

Nurses working in oncology and intensive care units are particularly impacted by the challenges of truth‐telling due to the nature of their patients' conditions [29, 34]. Testoni et al. [1] reported that nurses experience significant emotional burdens during truth‐telling processes, including feelings of sadness, discouragement and anxiety over potentially failing to sufficiently assist patients. Similarly, this study found that while nurses felt relief and happiness after delivering good news, they experienced sadness, exhaustion and emotional distress when delivering bad news. Nonetheless, truth‐telling, whether positive or negative, ultimately led to a sense of relief and professional satisfaction, reinforcing their ethical commitment.

Patients and their families may experience emotions such as anger, distrust and disappointment during truth‐telling processes [8, 11]. Consistent with the literature, the nurses in this study reported emotional difficulties in managing both their own feelings and the emotional reactions of patients and their families. It is well known that nurses often struggle to manage patient and family responses in clinical settings [35]. Developing communication skills is essential for nurses to effectively navigate emotional reactions, and supportive work environments are necessary to protect nurses' emotional well‐being during these processes [35, 36]. This study further adds that balancing truth‐telling with emotional support is key to sustaining therapeutic relationships, particularly in high‐stress oncology and ICU settings.

Limitations

The main limitation of this study is that it reflects only the perspectives of nurses.

Future research could adopt a triadic approach by including the views of nurses, patients and family members to provide a more comprehensive understanding. Additionally, data were collected solely from nurses working in Türkiye, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

This study provides new insights into the caring behaviours employed by nurses and the challenges they encounter regarding the critical issue of ‘truth‐telling’ within the nurse‐patient relationship. Although informing patients about their diagnosis typically falls under the physician's responsibility, conveying truths beyond the diagnosis and managing patients' reactions after learning the truth are predominantly nursing responsibilities.

Thus, fulfilling this role represents a moral obligation within nursing care.

The findings indicate that nurses engage in caring behaviours such as empathic listening, demonstrating respect, maintaining open communication, answering questions, alleviating concerns, building rapport and practicing therapeutic touch in their efforts to understand and spiritually support individuals during truth‐telling processes.

The most significant difficulty reported by nurses was coping with the emotional reactions of patients and families, as well as managing their own emotional responses. Furthermore, this study contributes to the existing body of nursing knowledge by providing an in‐depth understanding of the caring behaviours and emotional challenges nurses experience during truth‐telling, particularly in critical care settings.
