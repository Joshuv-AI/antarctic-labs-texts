In Response to ‘Intraoperative Atrial Fibrillation: An Anesthetic Challenge – A Safe Outcome Saves the Day’
Authors: Praveen Kumar Neema, Nagarjuna Panidapu
Published: 2026

Editor,

We read with interest the case report, “Intraoperative Atrial Fibrillation: An Anesthetic Challenge—A Safe Outcome Saves the Day” published in the recent issue of Annals of Cardiac Anaesthesia.[1] We congratulate the authors on successfully managing the challenging intraoperative complication. The paper highlights valuable learning points, and we would like to further enhance its educational value considering current practice guidelines.

The patient, presented with bioprosthetic aortic and mitral valves, severely reduced the left ventricular function (EF 35%), and chronic AF with a controlled heart rate. The patient was receiving aspirin AV 75/20 mg and digoxin 0.25 mg daily. It should be noted that aspirin is only recommended in patients with a bioprosthetic valve in sinus rhythm.[23] In patients with chronic AF, anticoagulation with either vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) or direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) is indicated.[4] Presently, DOACs are preferred, and VKAs are indicated in patients with moderate to severe mitral stenosis or mechanical prosthesis. When these patients present for elective surgery, perioperative anticoagulation is an important concern. Periprocedural anticoagulation management is driven by two competing aims: minimizing the risk of thromboembolism by withholding the anticoagulant for the shortest possible time, and ensuring coagulation parameters are as close to normal as possible during the procedure to facilitate hemostasis and avoid intra- and postoperative bleeding.[5] Preoperative evaluation is aimed at assessing thromboembolic risk, which is guided by CHA2DS2-VASc score [(congestive heart failure, hypertension, age >75 years, diabetes mellitus, H/O Stroke, TIA, or thromboembolism, Vascular disease (H/O myocardial infarction or peripheral arterial disease), and female gender)].[6] Each criterion is given a score of one; age >75 years and history of stroke are given two points each; a score of >2 indicates high thromboembolic risk. Bridging anticoagulation with low-molecular weight heparin or unfractionated heparin can be administered to mitigate thromboembolic risk in high-risk patients during interruption of anticoagulation therapy. However, studies consistently showed an increased risk of bleeding without a difference in thromboembolic risk with this strategy.[7] Therefore, bridging is recommended only for those with very high risk of thromboembolism.[5] The BRIDGE trial demonstrated that interrupting warfarin without bridging anticoagulation was superior to bridging,[8] underscoring that routine bridging with low molecular weight heparin oe unfractionated heparin is not necessary. The evidence favors stopping warfarin for 5 days before surgery to allow the INR to normalize below 1.5 before incision, and restarting it 12–24 hour after surgery, if hemostasis is satisfactory. In patients managed with DOACs, the studies favor stopping them 48 hours before[9] and restarting them on the evening of the first postoperative day in low bleeding risk cases and between the second and third postoperative day in high bleeding risk patients. Patients managed with dabigatran, having a creatinine clearance of 50 ml/min, the half-life of dabigatran is 10–14 hour, the half-life increases with deteriorating renal functions.[10] It is recommended to stop dabigatran one day (30–36 hour) prior in patients with a creatinine clearance of 50 ml/min for a moderate risk surgical procedure and to resume 1–3 days after based on patient factors and the bleeding risk of the procedure.[10] Because of the devastating complication of epidural bleeding, neuraxial blockade is considered a high bleeding risk procedure. A transversus abdominis plane block or any regional nerve block is safer option than epidural block; moreover, it can be performed even in the presence of anticoagulation. Epidural block and lateral positioning with a kidney bridge likely worsened hypotension and were avoidable.

In the present patient, a high probability of perioperative rapid ventricular response AF should be anticipated; therefore, the presence of a left atrial (LA) clot should be ruled out by transesophageal echocardiography (TEE). The management of rapid ventricular response AF with hemodynamic instability (shock, hypotension, pulmonary edema, or ongoing ischemia) is not straightforward, and restoring hemodynamic stability is paramount. If time permits, the triggers of rapid AF: hypoxia, electrolyte imbalance (hypokalemia), discontinuing inotropic agents, and manafing acute pain, should be addressed. If direct current cardioversion (DCCV) is not immediately available, rate control can be attempted with esmolol or metoprolol (if blood pressure allows), or with amiodarone, (if hypotension is present or β-blockers are contraindicated). Achieving rhythm control is difficult in patients with rheumatic mitral stenosis because the rheumatic process causes progressive fibrosis and enlargement of the atria, fibrosis of the internodal and interatrial tracts, and damages the sinoatrial node.[11] If hemodynamic instability is life-threatening and requires DCCV, TEE to rule out a LA clot and intravenous anticoagulation with heparin 70 u/kg is indicated, if feasible and no major contraindication exists.[11] It is important to consider the possible risks of DCCV. Low flow states (hypotension) and low EF predispose to LA clot formation. The most critical risk is the embolization of preexisting atrial thrombi during the shock, which can lead to ischemic stroke, systemic embolism, or pulmonary embolism. The practice of anticoagulation before, during, and after DCCV is based on the post-cardioversion risks of thromboembolism, which arise from factors like preexisting thrombus, the restoration of sinus rhythm, atrial stunning post-cardioversion, and a transient prothrombotic state.[12]
