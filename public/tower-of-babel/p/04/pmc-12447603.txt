Liver fat mediates hippocampal-orbitofrontal reward circuit disruption and cognitive-emotional impairments in MASLD
Authors: Liqiang Wu, Ruoyu Tang, Yidan Liang, Yihan Jin, Kuanghui Xu, Xiaofei Chen, Junping Shi, Jie Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs a major chronic liver condition, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is linked to numerous extrahepatic manifestations, including cognitive and emotional dysfunction. However, its neuroanatomical and functional brain correlates remain unclear. This study investigates cerebral alterations in MASLD by examining structural differences via voxel-based morphometry (VBM), along with functional features using resting-state functional connectivity (FC).Methods and patientsSeventy-four MASLD patients and sixty-two demographically matched healthy controls (HC) underwent structural MRI and resting-state fMRI. Group differences in gray matter volume (GMV) and FC were analyzed using VBM and seed-based FC methods. Correlations and mediation analyses were further used to assess the relationships among abnormal GMV, altered resting-state FC and clinical indicators in MASLD individuals.ResultsPatients with MASLD exhibited reduced GMV in the bilateral hippocampus, left parahippocampal gyrus, left middle temporal gyrus, right fusiform gyrus, right supplementary motor area (SMA), and right cerebellum. Importantly, liver fat content fully mediated the effects of hippocampal atrophy on cognitive-emotional symptoms. Seed-based FC analysis further revealed decreased connectivity between the right hippocampus and the right orbitofrontal cortex. This reduction in connectivity was also associated with worse cognitive function and a higher waist-to-hip ratio.ConclusionMASLD-related brain atrophy and functional disruption are linked to visceral adiposity and neuropsychiatric deficits. The mediating role of liver fat highlights the importance of dual-pathway interventions that target both metabolic health and neural protection, potentially reducing long-term neurocognitive disability burdens and enabling accessible risk stratification in practice.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), updated from the earlier term non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is diagnosed based on the detection of hepatic steatosis in individuals who also exhibit one or more cardiometabolic abnormalities [1, 2]. The global escalation of obesity and metabolic disorders has been accompanied by a consistent increase in MASLD cases, currently estimated to affect nearly one-third of the world’s population [3]. Recognized as a significant driver of chronic liver pathology, MASLD may advance to liver cirrhosis and even hepatocellular carcinoma, posing a substantial threat to patients’ overall health status and daily life quality and exacerbating the burden of the disease [4, 5]. The pathophysiology of MASLD involves complex metabolic disorders that are intimately linked to insulin resistance, obesity, dyslipidemia, and oxidative stress [6], which are also proven risk factors for extrahepatic diseases closely associated with cognitive impairment, such as atherosclerosis, neurodegenerative diseases, and diabetes mellitus [7, 8]. Evidence from preclinical studies together with clinical investigations indicates that the liver-brain axis is a pathophysiological mechanism and therapeutic target for metabolic and neurocognitive impairment caused by liver diseases [9, 10]. Frequent occurrence of cognitive decline and dementia among MASLD patients has been extensively described, and recent studies have also found that depression and anxiety are at high risk of comorbidity with MASLD [11–13]. Depression comorbidities in MASLD are strongly correlated with the degree of hepatocyte ballooning [14]. Notably, MASLD persisted as a standalone predictor of cognitive deterioration, anxiety, as well as depressive symptoms, after adjustment for potential confounders including cardiovascular conditions [15]. However, it remains unclear what the neural mechanisms underlying these associations are.

Retrospective analyses of large-scale population cohorts using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have indicated that MASLD correlates with a reduction in total brain volume (TBV), thinning of the cortex in regions involved in cognitive function (particularly the hippocampus and precuneus), as well as an increased risk of white matter hyperintensities. Notably, the severity of these neuroimaging alterations shows significant correlations with histological features including hepatic steatosis grade, lobular inflammation, and ballooning degeneration [9, 16–18]. These studies evaluated overall gray and white matter volumes both globally (i.e., total volumes across the entire brain) or regional volumes in region of interests (ROIs) selected based on prior hypotheses. Voxel-based morphometry (VBM) automates the analysis of macrostructural neuroanatomical images by primarily quantifying gray matter volume (GMV). This approach controls for individual anatomical differences and facilitates integration into standard clinical practice owing to its rapid processing [19]. To date, however, few studies have utilized the VBM technique to detect abnormal subtle morphological differences across the entire brain in patients with MASLD. Beyond this, emerging evidence from neurodegenerative research suggests that structural abnormalities alone cannot fully account for clinical symptom profiles [20, 21], as cognitive-emotional impairments fundamentally arise from disrupted interregional connectivity rather than isolated regional damage [22, 23]. Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) allows for the exploration of these interactions by measuring functional connectivity (FC), particularly through seed-based approaches that assess synchronized activity across various cerebral regions [24]. This FC approach provides a complementary perspective to the structural changes identified by VBM. Previous studies have shown that regions with altered GMV often exhibit abnormal FC with other regions [25, 26]. Therefore, exploring the FC of structurally altered regions identified by VBM could help identify vulnerable brain networks earlier and offer deeper insights into the neural basis underlying cognitive and affective manifestations associated with MASLD. Nevertheless, the FC correlates of structural changes in MASLD remain unexplored.

In previous studies, liver fat quantification was mainly dependent on ultrasound [27], with occasional confirmation by invasive liver biopsies [18]. However, the former is operator-dependent, and patient acceptance of the latter is low. Proton density fat fraction (PDFF), an objective and non-invasive biomarker derived from advanced quantitative MRI, demonstrates a strong correlation with liver biopsy results for quantifying hepatic steatosis. Recent research has highlighted accelerated brain aging and worsening cognitive function in middle-aged MASLD individuals, with these changes being linked to Alzheimer’s disease and cerebrovascular pathologies [28]. Thus, neuroimaging-based early identification of cognitive impairment-associated cerebral abnormalities among younger and middle-aged MASLD patients is critical for timely intervention. Furthermore, investigating younger individuals without confounding cerebral small vessel disease risk factors is necessary to pinpoint the unique effects of MASLD on cerebral integrity.

To fill this research gap, the current study primarily integrates VBM and rs-fMRI for the investigation of structural and functional cerebral variations in young-to-middle-aged adult patients with MASLD, specifically those without risk factors for cerebral small vessel disease. By combining these approaches, this study aims to (1) identify regions with GMV changes across the whole brain in MASLD using an unbiased VBM approach and characterize the correlations between these regions and whole-brain voxels in terms of spontaneous neural activity properties; (2) explore the relationships between these neuroimaging alterations, hepatic steatosis severity, and clinical symptom profiles. The present work offers a novel integrated structural and FC investigation of the brain in the early and middle adult MASLD population studied, thereby providing novel perspectives on the neural mechanisms underlying its neuropsychiatric comorbidities.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study included 74 individuals meeting the criteria fordiagnosed with MASLD and 62 healthy controls (HC) matched on age and sex. MASLD cases were recruited from the Liver and Metabolic Diseases Clinic of the Hangzhou Normal University Affiliated Hospital, whereas HC participants were obtained from the hospital’s health examination center. Prior to participation, all subjects signed consent documents following detailed explanations of the study protocols. Ethical approval was secured from the institutional review board (Approval code: [2019 (E2) -HS-02]), and the study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Diagnosis and clinical assessment

The identification of MASLD followed the 2018 guidelines proposed by the Hepatology Society of China, in conjunction with the 2023 multisociety Delphi consensus statement [1]. Diagnostic assessment comprised a thorough review of medical history, physical examination, biochemical testing, and evaluation of hepatic steatosis using MRI-PDFF [29]. All enrolled MASLD patients were newly diagnosed and met treatment-naïve criteria, having no history of MASLD-related pharmacotherapy. They were free from any metabolic disorder medications (including lipid-lowering, antihypertensive, or glucose-lowering agents) for at least 3 months prior to enrollment. For HC, abdominal ultrasonography, computed tomography, or MRI was performed to rule out fatty liver. Participants were included according to the following criteria: (1) age 18 to 60 years; (2) a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score no lower than 24 using the Chinese version; (3) no previous or current diagnoses of psychiatric or neurological conditions (including epilepsy, cerebrovascular accidents, traumatic brain injury, or cranial neurosurgery); hepatic diseases (such as hepatic encephalopathy or other chronic liver conditions); or some metabolic disorders (including diabetes and hypertension); and (4) right-hand dominance verified through the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory [30].

All subjects underwent a multidimensional neuropsychological assessment battery conducted by two psychiatrists in a quiet environment. The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) was selected for its demonstrated sensitivity in detecting mild cognitive impairment, validated utility in metabolic disorders including NAFLD and diabetes [31, 32], and efficient evaluation of visuospatial, memory, and executive functions - all particularly relevant for identifying subtle cognitive deficits in MASLD patients [32]. The Digit Span Test (DST) [33] was adopted to assess working memory function. The Trail Making Test (TMT) [34], which includes two parts (A and B), was used to evaluate attention and executive function, respectively. The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HAD) contains14 items that are organized into dual subscales designed to measure symptoms of anxiety and depression [35].

Weight, height, and abdominal circumference measurements were taken for all subjects. On the day scheduled for brain MRI, venous blood was collected under fasting conditions from each patient to determine fasting glucose, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), insulin concentration, and hepatic function indicators (such as alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST]). The homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was computed as (fasting glucos [mmol/L] × fasting insulin [µIU/mL])/22.5. Furthermore, within the week preceding brain imaging, participants underwent quantitative liver MRI (qMRI) using a six-echo Dixon protocol, and corresponding qMRI measurements were documented.

MRI image acquisition

All participants underwent MRI examinations on a 3.0 Tesla GE scanner (Milwaukee, WI, USA) at the hospital’s MRI Center. During scanning, participants lay on their backs with their eyes closed while remaining awake. Soft padding and earplugs were applied to limit head movement and acoustic interference. High-definition structural images providing entire brain coverage were obtained using the three-dimensional spoiled gradient recall (3D-SPGR) protocol. The imaging parameters included: a repetition time (TR) of 8.16 ms, echo time (TE) of 3.18 ms, 8° flip angle, a square field of view (FOV) measuring 256 × 256 mm², 256 × 256 acquisition matrix, 1 mm slice thickness, and 176 slices acquired contiguously. For functional imaging, an echo-planar imaging sequence was employed with a TR of 2000 ms, TE of 30 ms, flip angle of 90°, matrix size of 64 × 64, FOV of 192 × 192 mm, 4 mm slice thickness, and an inter-slice gap of zero. Furthermore, additional sequences, including diffusion-weighted and T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery were obtained to screen for structural anomalies and organic lesions.

All MASLD patients underwent abdominal MRI examinations on a 1.5 Tesla Siemens Magnetom Avanto scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Germany). The entire liver was imaged using a non-contrast, breath-hold, 3D gradient recalled six-echo sequence. The principal parameters for the pulse sequence included a TR fixed at 15.60 ms, TEs of 2.38, 4.76, 7.14, 9.52, 11.90, and 13.90 ms, flip angle of 5°, FOV set to 320 × 320 mm, slice thickness at 4.0 mm, and an inter-slice interval of 0.8 mm.

Data analysis

Liver qMRI data processing

Liver qMRI data analysis was performed by a board-certified radiologist under blinded conditions regarding all subjects’ details. The MRI-PDFF values were determined using MRI workstation software. For this process, ROIs were manually delineated on segment VI of the hepatic PDFF map, carefully avoiding adjacent anatomical features and sizable vascular structures.

VBM preprocessing

Before VBM preprocessing, a visual inspection of T1-weighted images was conducted to identify any significant anatomical abnormalities. The processing steps were carried out using the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT 12) operating within the Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM 12) framework (Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, UK) in a MATLAB environment. Individual T1-weighted images were first reoriented to the anterior commissure-posterior commissure reference plane. Image registration, spatial standardization, and intensity modulation were subsequently performed using the Diffeomorphic Anatomical Registration using Exponentiated Lie Algebra (DARTEL) algorithm for high-dimensional diffeomorphic mapping [36]. A study-specific template was created through iterative warping of individual segmented maps to an average tissue probability map template. Following this, the normalized images underwent intensity adjustment using Jacobian scaling factors and were transformed into Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space. Subsequently, these processed images were smoothed with an isotropic Gaussian kernel of 8 mm full-width at half-maximum to enhance their clarity.

Preprocessing of rs-fMRI data

Rs-fMRI datasets were initially processed employing the Data Processing and Analysis for Brain Imaging toolkit (DPABI, available at http://rfmri.org/DPABI) operating under SPM12. The workflow encompassed several key steps: DICOM-to-NIFTI file conversion, discarding the initial 10 time points to allow for magnetic equilibration, subsequent slice time correction, and realignment for head motion. Each participant’s structural images were coregistered to the motion-corrected mean functional image and partitioned into GM, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) compartments. Spatial normalization was achieved via DARTEL algorithm. This was followed by spatial smoothing with an isotropic Gaussian kernel and temporal band-pass filtering within the 0.01–0.08 Hz frequency range. Nuisance regression was finally performed utilizing Friston 24-parameter model to control for head movement, as well as signals from white matter and CSF.

Seed-based FC analysis

Based on VBM results and their correlation with clinical indicators, as well as its established structural vulnerability in MASLD and role in metabolic-cognitive integration [18, 37], the right hippocampus was selected as a spherical seed ROI for subsequent FC analysis. Specifically, via the DPABI software, a spherical region with a 5-mm radius was delineated in the standard MNI space. The sphere was centered on the peak coordinates (MNI: 22.5, −15, −7.5) identified within the right hippocampus from the VBM results. The temporal average of the signal within the ROI was derived, and Pearson’s r values were computed between this seed reference and each voxel throughout the entire brain to generate voxel-wise FC maps. Finally, all correlation values were transformed to z-scores using Fisher’s z-transformation to approximate a normal distribution for subsequent analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses of demographic characteristics, clinical metrics, and neuropsychological evaluation results were carried out in IBM SPSS Statistics 24.0 (Chicago, USA). Categorical variable comparisons between groups were evaluated with the Pearson chi-square test. Normally distributed continuous measures were analyzed with two-tailed independent-samples t-tests, whereas non-parametric variables were examined using the Mann-Whitney U test. A significance threshold was set at P < 0.05.

Intergroup comparisons of GMV were conducted via two-sample t-tests implemented in the DPABI toolbox, with age, sex, years of education, and total intracranial volume (TIV) included as covariates. Multiple comparisons were corrected using a Gaussian random field (GRF) correction, with a voxel-level threshold of P < 0.001 and a cluster-level threshold of P < 0.05. For anatomically discrepant regions, partial correlation analyses were conducted to probe the associations among GMV, neuropsychological scale scores, blood biochemical markers, and MRI-PDFF values in MASLD patients, controlling for confounding effects of age, sex, and educational attainment. Additionally, the PROCESS macro was employed to perform mediation analysis on clinical indicators, scale scores, and the brain regions significantly correlated with them. Statistical significance was assessed through 1000 bootstrap iterations, employing a threshold of P = 0.05.

To assess between-group disparities in FC, two-tailed independent samples t-tests were implemented through the DPABI platform incorporating age, sex, and educational attainment as confounding variables. Multiple comparisons were corrected using GRF theory, with thresholds set at P < 0.001 (voxel level) and P < 0.05 (cluster level). Furthermore, partial correlation analyses were carried out to examine the relationships linking significant hippocampal connectivity values with clinical indicators and neuropsychological scale scores, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Clinical demographic results

The demographic data and neuropsychological scale scores of all participants, as well as blood indicators and quantitative liver fat values in MASLD patients are depicted in Table 1. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups on measures of sex, age, or education level (all P > 0.05). In comparison to HC, MASLD individuals showed markedly higher body mass index (BMI) and waist-to-hip ratio (P < 0.001). Additionally, MASLD patients exhibited decreased DST and MoCA scores, and increased depression and anxiety symptoms (P < 0.05). The MRI-PDFF values of MASLD patients indicated abnormal hepatic fat deposition, with values exceeding 5%. In terms of biochemical data, significant impairments in liver function were evident, as indicated by elevated levels of ALT and AST. There was also a marked presence of insulin resistance, indicated by high insulin levels and HOMA-IR scores. None of the participants fulfilled the diagnostic threshold for diabetes, although some displayed impaired fasting glucose levels. Additionally, MASLD patients exhibited significant dyslipidemia, as evidenced by distinct abnormalities in total cholesterol, triglyceride, and low-density lipoprotein profiles, indicating impaired lipid metabolism.

Correlation analysis results

Partial correlation analysis indicated that among patients with MASLD, diminished GMV in the right hippocampus exhibited a significant positive association with DST scores (r = 0.241, P = 0.043). In contrast, its significant inverse correlations were observed with TMT-B, HAD, PDFF, and HOMA-IR (r = −0.292, P = 0.014; r = −0.277, P = 0.019; r = −0.352, P = 0.003; r = −0.358, P = 0.006, respectively). Additionally, Reduced GMV in the left parahippocampal gyrus demonstrated a robust positive association with DST performance (r = 0.273, P = 0.021). Conversely, reduced GMV in the right fusiform gyrus demonstrated a significant negative correlation with waist-to-hip ratio (r = −0.296, P = 0.012). Furthermore, FC values between the right hippocampus and right OFC demonstrated a positive association with MoCA scores (r = 0.274, P = 0.039) while exhibiting an inverse relationship with waist-to-hip ratio (r = −0.266, P = 0.046). Bivariate scatterplots depicting the aforementioned associations are provided in Figs. 2B–C and 3.

Mediation analysis

Mediation analysis indicated that liver fat content was a significant mediator of the relationship between GMV values in the right hippocampus and both cognitive decline and negative mood in MASLD patients. Specifically, MRI-PDFF significantly mediated the relationships between right hippocampal GMV and DST (a = −107.223, P = 0.003; b= −0.181, P < 0.001; c’= 6.674, P = 0.574; standardized indirect effect = 19.351, P = 0.011, 95% CI: 7.739, 35.164), TMT_B (a = −107.223, P = 0.003; b = 1.458, P = 0.003; c’ = −211.494, P = 0.154; standardized indirect effect = −156.320, P = 0.034, 95% CI: −326.682, −53.220) and HAD (a = −107.223, P = 0.003; b = 0.144, P = 0.001; c’ = −14.608, P = 0.243; standardized indirect effect = −15.433, P = 0.022, 95% CI: −31.959, −5.104). Discussion

This study aimed to explore both structural and functional brain changes in patients with MASLD. For structural alterations, it found consistent syndromic atrophy in the temporal lobe (particularly the hippocampus) and motor regions. This atrophy was significantly linked to cognitive decline and negative mood, with hepatic fat content statistically validated as a full mediator in these associations. For functional assessments, whole-brain connectivity mapping using a right hippocampal seed-based approach revealed reduced FC between the right hippocampus and right OFC. Importantly, this reduction in FC was significantly correlated with cognitive function and abdominal obesity.

The hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus are core components of the reward circuit, commonly referred to as the hippocampal complex, and are located within the medial temporal lobe [38]. This structure is traditionally associated with learning and memory. Additionally, it is implicated in sensory-motor integration and modulation of emotional behavior, influencing the activity of prefrontal regions related to inhibitory control by regulating the release of dopamine in the ventral striatum [39]. Hippocampal complex atrophy has been extensively reported in obese populations and is implicated in the modulation of satiety and cognitive dysfunction concomitant with food cravings [40, 41]. A previous longitudinal study has shown that a higher baseline BMI and waist circumference are positively correlated with hippocampal atrophy [42]. These findings suggest a mutual influence and a vicious cycle between hippocampal atrophy and obesity as well as related cognitive impairments. Reductions in hippocampal volume have also been found in cohort studies targeting MASLD, but the measurements used were to delineate and calculate this area through automatic boundary tracking, without considering individualized differences in TBV [18, 43]. Based on the VBM approach, the current research identified a marked reduction in GMV within the hippocampal complex among middle-aged and older individuals with MASLD, and this reduction was significantly associated with cognitive decline, anxiety and depression symptoms, liver fat accumulation, and insulin resistance. In particular, the mediation analysis demonstrated that visceral fat accumulation served as an intermediary link between hippocampal atrophy and cognitive-emotional impairments. These results highlight visceral adiposity as a modifiable risk factor, suggesting that interventions targeting abdominal fat reduction may not only improve metabolic parameters but also mitigate hippocampal neurodegeneration [44]. This finding further supports the existence of structural vulnerability in the hippocampal complex among MASLD patients [18]. The link between abdominal adiposity and exacerbated cognitive-emotional impairments in the presence of hippocampal atrophy merits deeper exploration as a possible focus for clinical assessment and therapeutic strategies.

The OFC, a critical component of the prefrontal region, serves as a key regulator of reward evaluation and higher-order cognitive regulation [45–47]. Recent studies have shown that alterations in prefrontal cortical activity in patients with MASLD may coincide with advancement of the condition and cognitive impairment [48]. As a vital component of the reward circuitry [49], the OFC interacts with the hippocampus in decision-making and cognitive processing, making their relationship particularly relevant in the context of neurocognitive alterations associated with MASLD. Weakened interaction could reflect an impaired ability to integrate memory-based predictions with reward evaluation, resulting in difficulties with cognitive flexibility and emotional regulation. Furthermore, given that the OFC is involved in value-based learning, its functional decoupling from the hippocampus may exacerbate reward-processing deficits, potentially explaining the increased susceptibility to cognitive and affective disturbances observed in patients with MASLD [50, 51]. This study revealed reduced FC values between the hippocampus and OFC in MASLD patients, and this attenuated connectivity was positively associated with cognitive function. This finding indicates that the functional decoupling of the hippocampal-OFC circuit may precede structural atrophy, potentially serving as an early neurobiological marker of MASLD-related cognitive impairment. Furthermore, reduced FC was negatively correlated with waist-to-hip ratio, suggesting an association between increased visceral fat and both hippocampal atrophy and disrupted hippocampus-OFC connectivity. Alterations in hippocampal morphology and connectivity within the reward circuit may be linked to cognitive impairment [52]. This dual association highlights the importance of combinatorial therapies that target both metabolic dysfunction and neural circuit plasticity [53, 54].

The fusiform gyrus, situated within the ventral temporal cortex, is involved not only in processing visual shape information but also in the processing of food rewards [55, 56]. During reward responses triggered by visual stimuli, such as viewing pleasurable objects, activation of the fusiform gyrus plays a key role in the reward circuitry [57]. In obese patients, the right fusiform gyrus exhibits cortical thinning, which correlates with an enhanced response to food-related cues [58, 59]. The middle temporal gyrus constitutes a pivotal node within the network mediating affective and sensory cognition [60]. Emerging evidence indicates a substantiated linkage connecting middle-aged MASLD and dementia-related pathological markers, including a thinner temporal lobe cortex and a lower clearance rate of beta-amyloid protein [61]. In parallel, a reduction in the GMV of these additional temporal lobe cortices was also observed in subjects diagnosed with MASLD in this investigation. Notably, atrophy in the right fusiform gyrus was inversely link to the waist-to-hip ratio, suggesting that this region may represent a vulnerable region in individuals with obesity, particularly among those with abdominal obesity.

As a key component of the sensorimotor network, the cerebellum and the SMA play crucial roles in motor control, sensory guidance, higher cognitive functions, and feeding behaviors [62–64]. The cerebellum has been proven to be one of the brain regions that is most vulnerable to damage in patients with obesity and early-stage chronic liver disease [65]. Earlier research has demonstrated that decreased GMV within the cerebellar hemispheres correlates with metabolic dysfunction, including insulin resistance, a condition commonly observed in obesity and diabetes [66, 67]. These findings imply that motor rehabilitation programs (e.g., coordinative exercise training) may not only improve physical function but also enhance cerebellar plasticity in MASLD patients [68]. In animal models, high-calorie diets have been shown to impair motor and sensory functions [69], highlighting the vulnerability of this network to metabolic stress. Moreover, in both obese individuals and those striving to regulate their eating patterns, lower GMV within the SMA correlates with a compromised self-control ability, subsequently giving rise to overeating habits, which in turn becomes a significant factor in the development of obesity [70, 71]. In light of this, degeneration within the sensorimotor network also serves as a critical factor in obesity-related metabolic brain injury.

Study strengths and limitations

This study leverages a multimodal neuroimaging approach combining VBM and seed-based FC analysis to comprehensively characterize cerebral alterations in MASLD, overcoming single - modality investigation limitations. Focusing on a young-to-middle-aged MASLD cohort excluding cerebrovascular risk factors and obvious metabolic confounders ensures the observed changes are more credibly specific to MASLD. Additionally, statistically robust mediation analysis with appropriate covariate adjustment supports inferences regarding potential causal pathways. However, several limitations of this study should be noted. The modest sample size may limit the generalizability and stability of the results. Although major metabolic confounders were excluded, unmeasured factors such as genetic predisposition, subclinical inflammation, or lifestyle variables may contribute to both liver and brain changes. The sample consisted largely of individuals with mild-to-moderate steatosis, limiting insight into severe MASLD subgroups. Furthermore, the lack of detailed dietary assessment represents an important omission given the established influence of nutrition on MASLD progression [72]. Finally, the study’s observational, cross-sectional design inherently restricts causative interpretation. While mediation analysis suggests potential pathways, these reflect associations and cannot establish temporal precedence. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are necessary to establish causal relationships and validate the findings.

Conclusion

This study reveals that MASLD patients exhibit medial temporal lobe atrophy and disrupted hippocampal-orbitofrontal connectivity, patterns that are associated with cognitive-emotional deficits and visceral adiposity. Crucially, liver fat content fully mediates the relationship between hippocampal structural damage and neuropsychiatric symptoms. These findings necessitate integrating routine cognitive-affective screening (e.g., MoCA) into standard MASLD management, and to consider brain MRI for symptomatic patients to detect early neural compromise. Clinicians should adopt dual-pathway strategies combining metabolic control through liver fat reduction with neuroprotective approaches. Future research should validate whether metabolic interventions can reverse these neural alterations, establish hippocampal-orbitofrontal connectivity as a biomarker of treatment response, and delineate the mechanistic interplay within the liver-brain axis in this pathophysiology.
