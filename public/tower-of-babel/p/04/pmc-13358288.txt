Measurement agreement between a portable and a conventional impulse oscillometry system: a two-centre method-comparison study
Authors: Shihua Yao, Rui Zhang, Bingxia Chen, Linchong Huang, Kuiqing Lin, Lingling Ji, Zhongping Wu, Dongfei Huang, Minqi Zhu, Yanqing Xie, Beilan Shen, Liping Zhong, Jinping Zheng, Fang Chen, Yi Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImpulse oscillometry (IOS) enables tidal-breathing assessment of airway mechanics and is useful in patients with limited cooperation. With the growing use of portable IOS devices for bedside and community pulmonary function assessment, agreement with established laboratory systems requires evaluation.ObjectiveTo assess measurement agreement and clinical classification agreement between the portable IOS 600 and the conventional Jaeger MasterScreen-IOS.MethodsIn this prospective two-centre method-comparison study with randomised measurement-sequence allocation, adult and paediatric participants underwent IOS measurement using both devices during a single visit. Core parameters (respiratory impedance at 5 Hz (Z5), resistance at 5 Hz (R5), resistance at 20 Hz (R20), reactance at 5 Hz (X5), reactance area and resonant frequency (Fres)) and secondary parameters were analysed. Interdevice agreement was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), Bland-Altman analysis and coefficients of variation. Clinical classification agreement for core parameters was evaluated using Gwet’s agreement coefficient 1 (AC1).ResultsOf 200 enrolled participants, 164 were included in the final paired analysis. Core IOS parameters showed high interdevice agreement, with ICCs ranging from 0.902 to 0.961. Bland-Altman analysis demonstrated small mean interdevice differences; however, proportional bias was observed for Z5, R5 and Fres, indicating that agreement at markedly abnormal values should be interpreted cautiously. Coefficients of variation did not differ significantly between devices. Clinical classification agreement was strong to almost perfect, with Gwet’s AC1 values of 0.90, 0.92, 0.91 and 0.96 for Z5, R5, R20 and X5, respectively. Adult-only and exploratory disease-subgroup analyses showed generally consistent agreement estimates. Most secondary resistance parameters showed strong-to-almost-perfect agreement, whereas reactance at 15 Hz and reactance at 25 Hz showed lower agreement.ConclusionsThe portable IOS 600 demonstrated high measurement agreement and clinical classification agreement with the MasterScreen-IOS for core IOS parameters under standardised testing conditions. Because the final cohort was predominantly adult, these findings are primarily applicable to adult participants. IOS 600 may serve as a complementary tool for airway function assessment, although further validation is needed in paediatric, disease-specific, bedside, primary care and community settings.Keywords: Respiratory Function Test, Respiratory Measurement, Equipment Evaluations

Introduction

Respiratory oscillometry enables effort-independent assessment of respiratory system impedance during quiet tidal breathing. Impedance comprises resistance and reactance components, and parameters measured at different frequencies provide complementary information on respiratory mechanics. Low-frequency indices such as respiratory impedance at 5 Hz (Z5), resistance at 5 Hz (R5), reactance at 5 Hz (X5), reactance area (AX) and resonant frequency (Fres) are commonly used to assess total and peripheral airway mechanics, whereas higher-frequency resistance parameters mainly reflect more central airway behaviour.1 2

Because oscillometry does not require forced expiratory manoeuvres or breath-holding, it is useful as a complementary pulmonary function test in children, older adults, frail patients and patients with dyspnoea or limited cooperation who may have difficulty completing spirometry, diffusing capacity testing or body plethysmography.3,5 This has encouraged the development of portable oscillometry systems for bedside, primary-care and community-based assessment.

However, oscillometry results cannot be assumed to be interchangeable across devices. Differences in hardware design, signal generation, spectral analysis and data-processing algorithms may produce systematic interdevice differences. Previous comparisons between impulse oscillometry and airwave oscillometry systems have shown that resistance and reactance values may differ across platforms, particularly under higher mechanical loads or in patients with more severe obstructive disease.6,9 Therefore, direct method-comparison studies are needed before measurements obtained from portable systems are interpreted alongside those from established laboratory-based systems.

The portable UBreath impulse oscillometry (IOS) 600 (e-LinkCare Meditech, China) applies externally generated oscillatory pressure signals during quiet tidal breathing and derives respiratory impedance from the relationship between oscillatory pressure and flow response. The device reports commonly used oscillometry parameters, including Z5, R5, resistance at 20 Hz (R20), X5, AX, Fres and frequency-specific resistance/reactance indices up to 35 Hz. Because waveform generation, spectral decomposition and filtering algorithms are device-specific, the measurement agreement between the IOS 600 and MasterScreen-IOS requires empirical evaluation. In this prospective two-centre method-comparison study, we therefore assessed measurement agreement and clinical classification agreement between the portable IOS 600 and the conventional MasterScreen-IOS for core and secondary IOS parameters.

Methods and participants

This prospective two-centre method-comparison study was not registered as a clinical trial because randomisation was used only to determine the order of measurements with the two IOS devices. Participants were not allocated to treatment groups, diagnostic-management strategies or any change in clinical care.

All participants completed sequential IOS measurements using both the MasterScreen-IOS system and the IOS 600 device during a single study visit, with the measurement order determined by a randomised sequence to minimise potential order effects.

From April 2025 to October 2025, participants were consecutively recruited at two centres: The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University and The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University. Patients attending respiratory clinics and healthcare staff volunteers were eligible for inclusion. The planned sample size was 200 participants, with no prespecified restrictions on age, sex, pulmonary health status or disease severity. Participants were eligible if they had no contraindications to IOS measurements and were able to complete IOS testing according to predefined quality-control criteria.

Participants were stratified according to the presence or absence of documented pulmonary disease into a pulmonary disease group and a non-pulmonary group. The pulmonary disease group included participants with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), interstitial lung disease, asthma, lung tumours, bronchiectasis or other pulmonary diseases. The non-pulmonary group comprised healthy participants and participants with heterogeneous non-pulmonary conditions or non-respiratory indications for pulmonary function testing, all without documented pulmonary disorders. Clinical diagnostic categories were extracted from diagnoses recorded on pulmonary function test requisition forms. These forms did not contain structured comorbidity fields, and non-pulmonary diagnoses were not consistently coded into prespecified diagnostic categories. Heart failure status was also not a predefined case-report-form variable and was not systematically captured. Therefore, detailed non-pulmonary diagnostic categories and heart failure status were not included in the analysis.

Although IOS measurements were collected prospectively, current pulmonary medication, acute exacerbation status and recent disease stability were not predefined case-report-form variables and were not consistently recorded on pulmonary function test requisition forms across the two centres. These variables were therefore not included in the analysis.

Measurement sequence allocation

To minimise potential order effects, a simple random method was used to allocate the measurement sequence, determining whether participants underwent IOS measurements first with the Jaeger MasterScreen-IOS or the IOS 600. A computer-generated random number list (1 or 2) was created by an independent investigator using SPSS V.26.0. A value of 1 indicated that measurements were initiated with the MasterScreen-IOS, whereas a value of 2 indicated initiation with the IOS 600.

Participants were not informed of the measurement sequence during data acquisition. After completion of all measurements, participants were informed of their pulmonary function results obtained from both devices.

Quality control

Before study initiation, both IOS systems underwent technical inspection and quality assurance procedures performed by certified technicians from the respective manufacturers or service providers. To ensure high-quality measurements, daily calibration was performed for both devices in accordance with the 2020 European Respiratory Society (ERS) Technical Standards for Respiratory Oscillometry.1

Before each testing day, environmental parameters such as barometric pressure and room temperature were calibrated. Subsequently, volume calibration was performed using a standard 3 L syringe with three flow rates for both devices. Impedance calibration was performed using three standard mechanical impedance loads (0.2, 0.5 and 1.0 kPa·s/L) provided by the manufacturers, in accordance with the 2020 ERS Technical Standards for Respiratory Oscillometry.

All IOS measurements were performed by nine experienced pulmonary function technologists. For each participant, measurements obtained with both devices were conducted by the same technologist to minimise operator-related variability. All procedures strictly followed the 2020 ERS Technical Standards for Respiratory Oscillometry.1

Device signal generation and specifications

According to the manufacturer’s technical documentation, the IOS 600 uses a loudspeaker-driven impulse oscillometry approach, in which pressure impulses are generated by a loudspeaker and superimposed on the participant’s quiet tidal breathing. These broadband pressure impulses are analysed across 5–35 Hz to derive respiratory impedance. The manufacturer’s user manual further states that the main unit contains a gas oscillation source, flow sensor and pressure sampling port, and that respiratory impedance is calculated from the ratio of oscillatory pressure to the corresponding flow response during quiet tidal breathing. The IOS module supports respiratory impedance testing and bronchodilator testing, with outputs including Z5, R5, R20, X5, AX, Fres and frequency-specific resistance and reactance indices up to 35 Hz. Because detailed waveform shaping, spectral decomposition, filtering and data-processing algorithms are device-specific, we treated the IOS 600 and MasterScreen-IOS as distinct device platforms and evaluated empirical measurement agreement rather than assuming technical equivalence.

Measurement protocol

IOS measurements were performed using both the Jaeger MasterScreen-IOS and the IOS 600 systems. Before testing, participants rested quietly for at least 5 min to ensure stable baseline conditions, and both devices were confirmed to be properly zeroed and stabilised.

During measurements, participants were seated upright with the head slightly extended, wore a nose clip and sealed their lips tightly around the mouthpiece. Participants were instructed to support their cheeks firmly using their palms or fingers, with the thumbs placed under the mandible to stabilise the floor of the mouth, and to breathe calmly and evenly at tidal volume (VT). Data acquisition was initiated once stable and regular breathing was observed by the technician. For participants unable to support their cheeks or tolerate a nose clip, assistance was provided by the technician or a caregiver.

All IOS measurements were performed in accordance with the 2020 ERS Technical Standards for Respiratory Oscillometry.1 Quality control criteria were defined in the following sections.

Acceptability criteria

Measurement duration ≥30 s for adults and ≥16 s for children.

Data acquisition included at least three stable and regular breathing cycles with consistent VT and respiratory frequency. Breathing cycles affected by air leaks (resulting in interruptions or shifts in the volume signal) or artefacts caused by tongue obstruction, glottic closure or vocalisation were excluded.

Repeatability criteria

The coefficient of variation (CV) of R5 across three acceptable measurements was required to be ≤10% for adults and ≤15% for children. For each participant and device, the CV was calculated across the three acceptable measurements and participant-level CVs were subsequently summarised for analysis.

If quality control criteria were met, the mean values of three acceptable measurements were used for analysis. Measurements not meeting quality control criteria were excluded from further analysis.

To minimise potential carryover effects and allow participants to return to a stable respiratory state, participants rested for approximately 10–20 min after completing all measurements on the first device before undergoing measurements on the second device.

Measurement parameters

Comparison of measured values

Core measurement parameters included Z5, R5, R20, X5, Fres and AX. Secondary measurement parameters included VT, resistance at 10 Hz (R10), 15 Hz (R15), 25 Hz (R25), 30 Hz (R30) and 35 Hz (R35), as well as reactance at 10 Hz (X10), 15 Hz (X15), 20 Hz (X20), 25 Hz (X25), 30 Hz (X30) and 35 Hz (X35).

Comparison of clinical classification

To evaluate clinical interpretability, core IOS parameters obtained from both devices were categorised according to the 2020 ERS Technical Standards for Respiratory Oscillometry and the Chinese Technical Standards for Pulmonary Function Testing—Impulse Oscillometry.1 10

Per cent predicted values were calculated using the reference equations recommended in the Chinese Technical Standards for Pulmonary Function Testing—Impulse Oscillometry. The same reference equations were embedded in both devices, resulting in identical predicted values for the same participant. Therefore, clinical classification was based on a unified set of predicted values and guideline-recommended thresholds for both systems.

For adults, Z5 values <0.5 kPa·s/L were considered normal, whereas higher values were considered abnormal. R5 and R20 values <150% of predicted were considered normal. X5 values greater than the predicted value minus 0.2 kPa·s/L were considered normal.

For children, Z5, R5 and R20 values <120% of predicted were considered normal, whereas higher values were considered abnormal. X5 values greater than the predicted value minus 0.2 kPa·s/L were considered normal.

Core IOS parameters were categorised as normal or abnormal according to guideline-recommended thresholds, and interdevice agreement in clinical classification was evaluated using Gwet’s agreement coefficient 1 (AC1) .

Proportional bias was assessed by regressing interdevice differences (IOS 600−MasterScreen-IOS) on paired-measurement means. Because the paediatric subgroup was small in the final analytic cohort, paediatric-specific agreement estimates were considered statistically unstable and were not calculated. Instead, an adult-only sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate whether inclusion of paediatric participants influenced the primary agreement estimates. Exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted according to pulmonary disease status and major diagnostic categories and were interpreted descriptively because of small and imbalanced subgroup sizes. Agreement strength for intraclass correlation (ICC) and Gwet’s AC1 was interpreted according to McHugh’s classification.11

Statistical analyses and sample size calculation

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (V.4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Bland-Altman plots were generated using MedCalc Statistical Software V.20.010, 64-bit (MedCalc Software) for graphical presentation; proportional bias analyses and other statistical analyses were performed using R software. Data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean±SD, whereas non-normally distributed variables are presented as median (IQR).

Agreement between devices for continuous measurements was evaluated using Bland-Altman analysis and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC; two-way mixed-effects model, absolute agreement). For Bland-Altman analysis, the mean paired difference was interpreted as bias, and the 95% limits of agreement were calculated as mean paired difference ±1.96 SD of the paired differences. Differences were calculated as IOS 600−MasterScreen-IOS. Because no clinically validated, parameter-specific acceptability thresholds were prespecified for these two IOS systems, the limits of agreement were interpreted descriptively together with proportional bias analysis and clinical classification agreement, rather than dichotomised as acceptable or unacceptable. Agreement strength for ICC and Gwet’s AC1, but not Bland-Altman limits of agreement, was interpreted according to McHugh’s classification. Differences in CV for core parameters were assessed using paired t-tests for normally distributed data or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normally distributed data. Agreement for categorical clinical classifications was evaluated using Gwet’s AC1 coefficient, which is less influenced by prevalence and marginal distribution than Cohen’s κ and provides more stable agreement estimates in imbalanced categories.12 A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sample size estimation for the primary agreement analysis was based on an expected ICC of 0.85, with two observations per subject, a CI width of 0.09 and a 95% CI of 0.80 to 0.89. Under these assumptions, a minimum of 152 participants was required. Allowing for an anticipated attrition rate of 20%–30%, the target sample size was set at 200 participants.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. All participants were recruited as subjects for impulse oscillometry measurements, and no patient or public representatives contributed to the study design, data collection, statistical analysis, manuscript preparation or dissemination strategies.

Results

Of 200 enrolled participants, 36 (18%) were excluded because paired measurements did not meet quality-control criteria in at least one device, leaving 164 participants for the final analysis (online supplemental figure 1). The numbers of participants failing the predefined R5 repeatability criterion were comparable between devices: 20 for the IOS 600 and 22 for the MasterScreen-IOS. Device-specific failure counts were not mutually exclusive, as six participants failed the criterion on both devices.

Agreement analysis of core IOS parameters

ICC analysis

Agreement between the two devices for core IOS parameters was evaluated using ICC analysis. The ICC values (95% CIs) for Z5, R5, R20, X5, AX and Fres were 0.952 (0.936 to 0.965), 0.928 (0.903 to 0.947), 0.902 (0.863 to 0.930), 0.947 (0.924 to 0.963), 0.961 (0.948 to 0.971) and 0.957 (0.941 to 0.968), respectively. All core parameters demonstrated almost perfect agreement according to McHugh’s classification (table 2). Only 11 participants were aged <18 years; therefore, formal paediatric-specific agreement estimates were not calculated. In the adult-only sensitivity analysis (n=153), agreement estimates remained consistent with the primary analysis, with ICCs ranging from 0.885 to 0.960 for core IOS parameters, indicating strong-to-almost-perfect agreement (online supplemental table 1). Exploratory subgroup analyses were performed according to broad clinical category and major pulmonary disease subgroups. Agreement estimates remained generally consistent in both pulmonary and non-pulmonary subgroups. Among the major pulmonary disease subgroups, ICCs for core IOS parameters ranged from 0.896 to 0.966 in COPD/emphysema, from 0.903 to 0.969 in interstitial lung disease and from 0.893 to 0.979 in asthma (online supplemental table 4).

Bland-Altman analysis

Bland-Altman plots for the core IOS parameters are shown in figure 1. The central line in each plot represents the mean paired difference, that is, the Bland-Altman bias, calculated as IOS 600−MasterScreen-IOS. Bland-Altman analysis showed small mean interdevice differences for the core IOS parameters. The mean paired differences were −0.004 kPa·s/L for Z5, −0.010 kPa·s/L for R5, −0.010 kPa·s/L for R20, −0.012 kPa·s/L for X5, 0.017 kPa/L for AX and −0.220 Hz for Fres. Numerical labels displayed in figure 1 were rounded for graphical presentation, whereas exact bias and limits-of-agreement values are provided in online supplemental table 2. Proportional bias analysis indicated significant measurement-range-related trends for Z5, R5 and Fres, whereas no significant proportional bias was observed for R20, X5 or AX.

Coefficient of variation analysis

The CVs for core IOS parameters obtained using both devices are presented in online supplemental table 3. Median CV values were comparable between devices for all core parameters, and no statistically significant differences were observed (p > 0.05 for all comparisons).

Clinical classification agreement

Agreement analysis of secondary IOS parameters

ICC analysis for secondary IOS parameters demonstrated variable levels of agreement. ICC values for VT, R10, R15, R25, R30 and R35 ranged from 0.799 to 0.922. Reactance parameters X10, X20 and X35 showed ICC values above 0.75, whereas X15 and X25 demonstrated relatively low agreement. Detailed results are provided in table 2.

Discussion

In this two-centre method-comparison study with a randomised measurement sequence, we systematically compared measurements obtained using the portable IOS 600 device and the conventional MasterScreen-IOS system across core and secondary IOS parameters. By applying complementary agreement and repeatability approaches—including ICC analysis, Bland-Altman analysis, CV assessment and Gwet’s AC1 for clinical classification—we evaluated interdevice agreement from both continuous-measurement and clinical-classification perspectives. Our findings demonstrate high agreement and stability between the two systems for core IOS parameters, supporting the reliability of the portable IOS 600 in clinical airway function assessment. Importantly, ICC was interpreted as a measure of relative agreement and rank agreement under an absolute-agreement model, rather than as evidence of full device interchangeability. Absolute agreement and potential clinical comparability were evaluated primarily using Bland-Altman bias and limits of agreement, proportional bias analysis and clinical classification agreement.

Agreement and clinical interpretability of core IOS parameters

All core IOS parameters (Z5, R5, R20, X5, AX and Fres) demonstrated strong-to-almost-perfect agreement between devices based on ICC analysis. Bland-Altman analysis showed small mean interdevice differences, although proportional bias was observed for Z5, R5 and Fres. These findings suggest good overall agreement under standardised testing conditions, while indicating that measurement-range-related differences, particularly at higher resistance values, should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, no significant differences were observed in CV, indicating comparable measurement repeatability and stability between devices.

To further reflect real-world clinical applicability, we assessed agreement in clinical classification for core parameters. Strong-to-almost-perfect agreement in categorical classification was observed for Z5, R5, R20 and X5 using Gwet’s AC1 coefficient, suggesting that the portable IOS 600 provides clinically interpretable results broadly consistent with those obtained using a conventional IOS system. These findings indicate that, for commonly used core IOS parameters, measurements obtained with the IOS 600 showed close agreement with those obtained using the MasterScreen-IOS.

Furthermore, consistent agreement in categorical classifications was observed in both pulmonary and non-pulmonary subgroups. This finding indicates that, when standard guideline-recommended thresholds are applied, the two devices are likely to result in similar clinical interpretations, supporting the practical comparability of core IOS parameters in routine airway function assessment.

The adult-only sensitivity analysis showed that agreement estimates remained consistent after excluding participants aged <18 years, supporting the robustness of the main findings in the adult population. In addition, exploratory subgroup analyses further supported the agreement of the main findings across broad clinical categories and major pulmonary disease subgroups. Agreement remained strong to almost perfect in both pulmonary and non-pulmonary groups, and similar patterns were observed in COPD/emphysema, interstitial lung disease and asthma. However, these analyses should be interpreted cautiously because several diagnostic categories were small and imbalanced in size. Therefore, the subgroup results should be considered descriptive rather than definitive evidence of disease-specific interchangeability.

Therefore, we retained paediatric participants in the primary cohort to reflect the enrolled study population, but the findings should be regarded as primarily driven by adult participants. The paediatric data were insufficient for age-specific agreement conclusions.

Interpretation of Bland-Altman findings and measurement-range limitations

The Bland-Altman plots indicated that observations were mainly concentrated within the physiological or mildly abnormal range. Visual inspection suggested possible measurement-range-related trends for several parameters, particularly at higher values where observations were sparse. Formal proportional bias analysis confirmed significant proportional bias for Z5, R5 and Fres, but not for R20, X5 or AX. Therefore, although agreement was generally high for core parameters, the limited number of participants with markedly abnormal resistance or reactance values restricts the ability to conclude full interchangeability across the entire pathological spectrum.

This limitation is consistent with previous interdevice and phantom-load studies in respiratory oscillometry. Even among systems using respiratory oscillometry, differences in pressure-signal generation, measurement-head design, calibration procedures and proprietary signal-processing algorithms may contribute to systematic interdevice differences. Dandurand et al used active mechanical test loads to compare oscillometers based on different technical implementations, including IOS and AOS, and found load-dependent deviations in respiratory system resistance and respiratory system reactance.7 Lundblad et al further compared IOS with an AOS system and reported that agreement decreased with increasing COPD severity and that IOS resistance measurements deviated from known resistance values at higher phantom loads.6 Kuo et al compared IOS and AOS in patients with asthma and COPD and reported better agreement for resistance than for reactance parameters.8 More recently, Suter et al reported strong agreement for resistance ratios but device-dependent differences in reactance ratios when comparing IOS and AOS in patients with asthma and COPD.9 Taken together, these findings support cautious interpretation of interdevice agreement at higher pathological values and reinforce that high ICC values alone should not be interpreted as evidence of full device interchangeability.

Agreement of secondary IOS parameters

For secondary IOS parameters, most resistance indices demonstrated strong-to-almost-perfect agreement, whereas VT and X30 showed moderate agreement and X15 and X25 showed minimal agreement.

This finding is consistent with the known frequency-dependent characteristics of respiratory system reactance. From a physiological and mechanical perspective, reactance in the intermediate frequency range (15–25 Hz) lies near the transition zone where elastic and inertial components contribute similarly but in opposite directions, making these parameters highly sensitive to breathing pattern variations, signal acquisition and spectral resolution.2 The 2020 ERS Technical Standards for Respiratory Oscillometry emphasise that differences in hardware design, signal generation, spectral analysis and digital filtering across IOS systems may lead to systematic discrepancies in frequency-dependent impedance measurements, particularly for reactance parameters.1

Furthermore, this frequency range is often close to the individual resonant frequency (Fres), where reactance changes rapidly with frequency. Small systematic differences between devices may therefore be amplified, resulting in reduced agreement for X15 and X25. Previous interdevice comparison studies have consistently reported higher agreement for resistance parameters than for reactance parameters.213,15

From a statistical perspective, the relatively small absolute values of X15 and X25 and their proximity to zero may also lead to underestimation of ICC values, contributing to apparently lower agreement.16 Importantly, reduced agreement for these parameters may reflect intrinsic frequency-dependent measurement characteristics and device-specific implementation differences, rather than necessarily indicating inadequate overall measurement reliability.

Moreover, X15 and X25 are not core parameters recommended for routine clinical interpretation in current ERS or Chinese technical guidelines. Clinical assessment of airway function primarily relies on core parameters such as Z5, X5, AX, Fres, R5 and R20. In the present study, all clinically relevant core parameters demonstrated a high level of agreement, supporting the clinical applicability of the portable IOS 600 despite lower agreement observed for some intermediate-frequency reactance parameters.17

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, only two devices of each IOS system were used, which limited assessment of interdevice variability across multiple hardware units. Future studies with larger sample sizes and multiple devices of each type are warranted to further evaluate agreement across device instances.

Second, although exploratory subgroup analyses were performed according to pulmonary disease status and major diagnostic categories, several disease-specific subgroups were small and imbalanced and severity-stratified analyses were not feasible. Therefore, these subgroup findings should be interpreted descriptively rather than as definitive evidence of disease-specific interdevice agreement or interchangeability. These factors may limit the generalisability of the findings to specific patient populations. In addition, paediatric participants accounted for only 7% of the final analytic cohort. Although adult-only sensitivity analysis showed findings consistent with the overall analysis, the study was not powered to assess paediatric-specific interdevice agreement. Moreover, within the non-pulmonary group, detailed non-pulmonary diagnoses and heart failure status were not systematically recorded or coded. Therefore, we could not reliably summarise non-pulmonary diagnostic categories or assess whether unrecorded cardiovascular or systemic comorbidities influenced IOS measurements.

Third, current pulmonary treatment, acute exacerbation status and recent disease stability were not systematically recorded because these variables were not predefined case-report-form items and were not consistently captured on pulmonary function test requisition forms across the two centres. Therefore, we could not assess whether medication use or acute clinical instability influenced interdevice agreement.

Fourth, this study was conducted in two tertiary centres by experienced pulmonary function technologists under standardised testing conditions. Therefore, the generalisability of the findings to primary care, community-based, bedside or operator-variable settings remains to be established.

Finally, long-term agreement and repeatability between devices were not assessed. Future studies incorporating larger cohorts, multiple disease subgroups and longitudinal measurements are needed to further validate the performance of the portable IOS 600 in long-term clinical applications.

Conclusion

In conclusion, under the measurement conditions and participant population investigated, the portable IOS 600 demonstrated high measurement agreement and clinical classification agreement with the conventional MasterScreen-IOS system for core IOS parameters. Most secondary resistance parameters also showed strong-to-almost-perfect ICC-based agreement, whereas some intermediate-frequency reactance parameters showed lower agreement. Given its portability and operational flexibility, the IOS 600 may serve as a complementary tool to conventional laboratory-based IOS systems. Further validation in community, primary care, bedside, paediatric and disease-specific settings is warranted before broader clinical implementation.
