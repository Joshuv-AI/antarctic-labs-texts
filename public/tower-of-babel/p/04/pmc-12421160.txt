Acute gastroenteritis and associated factors among under-five children visiting public hospitals in Jigjiga City, Somali Region, Ethiopia: a hospital-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Mahlet Ameha Getahun, Liyew Mekonnen Ayehubizu, Abdirahman Mohamed Ahmed, Seid Yimam Ali, Ahmed Moumin Abdilahi, Mekonin Meskelu Shegere
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThe study aims to assess the magnitude of acute gastroenteritis and associated factors among under-five children visiting public hospitals in Jigjiga City, Somali Region, Ethiopia.DesignA hospital-based cross-sectional study design was used to carry out the study. We then employed a systematic random sampling technique through face-to-face interviews to gather the data. A structured questionnaire consisting of socio-demographic, behavioural, environmental and clinical factors was developed after reviewing relevant literature.SettingThe study was conducted in public hospitals located in Jigjiga City, Somali Region, Ethiopia.ParticipantsA total of 353 under-five children visiting public hospitals were involved in the study.Outcome measuresThe primary outcome of the study was the prevalence of acute gastroenteritis.ResultThe 2-week prevalence of acute gastroenteritis among under-five children was 24.6% (95% CI 23.4% to 25.9%). The study found strong links between acute gastroenteritis and having a household greater than five (adjusted OR (AOR)=2.94, 95% CI 1.55 to 5.60), an unprotected source of drinking water (AOR=2.74, 95% CI 1.29 to 5.82), unimproved latrine facilities (AOR=3.15, 95% CI 1.26 to 7.78), the presence of faeces around the latrine (AOR=3.53, 95% CI 1.77 to 7.03) and mothers or caregivers’ history of diarrhoea over the past 2 weeks (AOR=6.23, 95% CI 3.22 to 12.06).ConclusionThe overall prevalence of acute gastroenteritis in Jigjiga City was relatively high in the 2-week period. Having a household greater than five, an unprotected source of drinking water and unimproved latrine facilities was a significant predictor of acute gastroenteritis. Additionally, the presence of faeces around the latrines and the history of diarrhoea in mothers or caregivers over the past 2 weeks were strongly linked to acute gastroenteritis.Keywords: Gastrointestinal infections; Hospitals, Public; Child

Introduction

Acute gastroenteritis (AGE) is characterised by inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract, mainly the stomach and small intestines.1 Bacterial, viral and parasitic organisms, primarily spread by faeces-contaminated water, cause AGE.2 Diarrhoeal disease is the second-leading cause of death among children under 5 years of age globally and accounts for almost one in five child deaths, or about 1.5 million each year.3 In low- and middle-income countries, diarrhoea is a common cause of mortality among children aged <5 years, with an estimated 2 million deaths annually.4 In Ethiopia, 23% of all under-five deaths—more than 70 000 children a year—are due to diarrhoea.5

38.4% and 93% of children less than 5 years of age in Ethiopia do not have access to improved water and sanitation, respectively. The number one region in Ethiopia that does not have access to improved water is the Somali Region, which reaches 58.3%.6 In Ethiopia, where access to clean water and sanitation infrastructure is limited in many regions, AGE continues to pose a considerable burden on child health.7

The prevalence of AGE varies across regions, and numerous studies have contributed valuable insights into its occurrence. The prevalence of AGE was 15.86% in East Africa,8 10.4% in the USA,9 7.3% in India,10 46.1% in Gaza,11 26.7% in Rwanda12 and 20.4% in Tanzania.13 In Ethiopia, the prevalence of AGE was 11% in Northeastern Ethiopia,14 23% in South Omo Zone,15 14.5% in Western Ethiopia,16 23.5% in Dire Dawa17 and 28.7% in Arsi Robe.18

In addition to the varying prevalence rates of AGE across regions, several factors have been identified as key contributors to its occurrence, particularly among children under five. Sociodemographic factors such as child age,10 14 19 20 male children,10 21 the presence of two or more under-five children at home,14 low economic status10 19 20 and low educational status8 12 19 were associated with AGE. Furthermore, behavioural factors such as a lack of rotavirus vaccination,15 starting complementary foods before 6 months, a lack of handwashing facilities near latrines and suboptimal handwashing practices at critical times were linked to AGE.16 A strong link was found between AGE and reliance on an unprotected drinking water source14 15 18 21 22 and faeces around the pit hole, slab or floor of the latrine.1013,15 23 Moreover, a mother’s or caretaker’s history of diarrhoea in the last 2 weeks15 23 greatly increased the risk of AGE.

To date, there have been few studies conducted nationwide to establish the prevalence of under-five diarrhoea and identify associated factors in Ethiopia. However, recent studies have failed to address particular risk factors, such as cultural practices, malnutrition, lack of immunisation or limited access to healthcare services. The exclusive population-based study conducted in Jigjiga City is outdated and does not accurately reflect the current status of AGE in this population.24 The demographic shifts, rapid urbanisation unique to Jigjiga City, improvements or setbacks in healthcare infrastructure and cultural practices, and the possible emergence of new determinants of AGE may necessitate further investigation. Therefore, the study aims to determine the magnitude and associated factors of AGE among under-five children in the Jigjiga City administration.

Methods

Study design, area and period

A hospital-based cross-sectional study design was employed. The study was conducted from 1 March to 30 May 2024, in public hospitals found in Jigjiga City. Namely, Sheik Hassan Yabare Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (SHYCSH), Karamara General Hospital (KGH) and Jigjiga Primary Hospital (JPH), Jigjiga, East Ethiopia. Jigjiga City is located 626 km east of Addis Ababa and has an estimated total population of 257 613. The city has one comprehensive specialised, one general, one primary and one private hospital.25 26

Source and study population

The source population comprised all under-five children brought to three governmental hospitals in Jigjiga City, while the study population consisted of randomly selected under-five children from these hospitals.

Eligibility criteria

All under-five children who were brought to the three governmental hospitals during the study period were eligible, whereas those under-five children who were critically ill and had not lived in the Somali Region for at least 6 months prior to the data collection time were excluded from the study. Additionally, when the child and caretaker lived in different settings, and if the child’s family was not present during the hospital visit, that child was excluded from the study. The next eligible child was then considered.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula based on the following assumptions: the prevalence of AGE at public hospitals in Southwest Ethiopia was 34.53%,22 with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. The estimated total population during the study period was 3600 (N). The maximum sample size determined by these values for each objective was 347. Since the source population was under 10 000, a finite population correction formula was applied, resulting in a working sample size of 316. A 10% non-response rate was factored in, leading to a final sample size of 353.

Sampling technique

A systematic random sampling technique was employed. The sampling interval (k) was determined by dividing the number of patients visiting public hospitals during the data collection period (3600, 1860 at SHYCSH, 1170 at KGH and 570 at JPH), which was roughly estimated based on the number of patients attending under five outpatient departments in the preceding months.

The sample size was proportionally allocated to each hospital: 181 at SHYCSH, 103 at KGH and 69 at JPH. The first patient was selected using a lottery method, and subsequently, every 10th patient was included in the study according to their order of visit. If a participant selected through systematic sampling was found to be ineligible, the next eligible child was considered for inclusion.

Data collection techniques and tools

The data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire developed after reviewing relevant literature.14 19 22 The outcome variable, AGE, was assessed using this questionnaire. Explanatory variables such as sociodemographic factors (child’s age, sex, birth order, residency, mother’s age, education, occupation, marital status, household size, house ownership, income, etc), behavioural factors (maternal handwashing over the past 2 weeks, feeding practices and Rota vaccination, etc), environmental factors (drinking water source, water availability, latrine type, proximity, sanitation conditions and waste management, etc over the last 2 weeks) and clinical factors (child and mother or caretaker diarrhoea over the past 2 weeks) were measured (online supplemental appendix I).

Data quality management

The questionnaire was initially prepared in English, then translated into Somali and Amharic, with language experts translating it back into English for consistency. A pretest was conducted on 5% of the sample at Kabribeyah Primary Hospital to refine the questionnaire. Two data collectors and one supervisor, all nurses fluent in Somali and Amharic, were recruited from each hospital. They underwent 2 days of training on study objectives, data collection methods, ethics, consent and confidentiality. The data were usually collected during working days of the week, from Monday to Friday, from 08:00 to 18:00. The collected data were checked for completeness and consistency daily, with each questionnaire coded by investigators until data analysis.

Data entry, processing and analysis

The data were checked manually for completeness, edited, coded and entered into Epi Data V.4.6, then exported to SPSS V.26.0 for analysis.27 Descriptive statistics, such as frequency and percentage, were used and presented in tables. A bivariate analysis was done to evaluate individual associations between each explanatory variable and the outcome variable. All explanatory variables that demonstrated an association with the outcome variable with p values of <0.25 were candidates for multivariate analysis. Subsequently, a multicollinearity test was done and indicated that there was no multicollinearity among the independent variables, as all variance inflation factor values were below 5. Finally, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed to determine independent predictors, with statistical significance set at p<0.05. Concurrently, the Hosmer and Lemeshow test indicated a p value >0.05 and a relatively small χ² value, which showed the model fits the data well.

Results

Socioeconomic characteristics

The study included 353 under-five children seeking healthcare at three public hospitals, with a 100% response rate. Of the participants, 128 (36.3%) were between 25 and 59 months, and 96 (27.2%) were between 13 and 24 months. Half (182, 51.6%) were male, and over half (192, 54.4%) lived in urban areas. Most mothers or caregivers (316, 89.5%) were married, but over half (208, 58.9%) had no formal education. A majority (270, 76.5%) were unemployed, and nearly half (173, 49%) had a middle-level monthly income (table 1).

Environmental factors

Over half of the participants, 195 (55.2%), relied on unprotected sources, and 200 (56.7%) lacked an adequate water supply. Prevalence of AGE

The 2-week prevalence of AGE among under-five children was found to be 87 (24.6% (95% CI 23.4% to 25.9%)). Moreover, 79 (22.4%) of the mothers or caregivers reported having diarrhoea over the past 2 weeks.

Factors associated with AGE among under-five children

In the bivariate analysis (p<0.25), AGE in under-five children was significantly associated with residency, mom educational status, house ownership, monthly income, caregiver handwashing at critical times, breastfeeding status and Rota vaccination. Environmental factors, including inadequate household water, unprotected drinking sources, unimproved latrines, proximity of latrines to homes, flies around latrines and faeces in the compound, were also significant. Additionally, faeces near the latrine, uncollected solid waste and recent caregiver diarrhoea were associated with the condition. Other variables showed no significant association (table 4).

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis at p value <0.05 and backward selection method, household size >5, the source of drinking water, the types of latrine facilities, the presence of faeces around the pit hole, slab or floor of the latrine, and mothers or caregivers’ history of diarrhoea over the past 2 weeks were significant and independent predictors of AGE among under-five children at governmental hospitals in Jigjiga, Ethiopia (online supplemental appendix II).

According to this study, children living in families with more than five households were three times more likely to have AGE as compared with those living in families with five or fewer households (adjusted OR (AOR)=2.94, 95% CI 1.55 to 5.60). Similarly, participants who had reported an unprotected source of drinking water were 2.74 times more likely to have AGE as compared with those who had reported a protected source of drinking water (AOR=2.74, 95% CI 1.29 to 5.82). Moreover, participants whose parents had reported unimproved latrine facilities were three times more likely to have AGE as compared with those participants whose parents had reported an improved latrine facility (AOR=3.15, 95% CI 1.26 to 7.78).

Participants who had reported the presence of faeces around the pit hole, slab or floor of the latrine were 3.53 times more likely to have AGE as compared with those participants who denied the presence of faeces around the pit hole, slab or floor of the latrine (AOR=3.53, 95% CI 1.77 to 7.03). Furthermore, participants whose mothers or caregivers had a history of diarrhoea over the past 2 weeks were six times more likely to have AGE as compared with those participants whose mothers or caregivers had no history of diarrhoea over the past 2 weeks (AOR=6.23, 95% CI 3.22 to 12.06).

Several variables initially showing a statistically significant association with AGE, including residency, maternal education, house ownership, income, handwashing practices, breastfeeding and Rota vaccination, lost significance in the final model. Likewise, environmental factors such as inadequate water supply, latrine proximity, the presence of flies, faeces in the compound and uncollected solid waste did not retain a significant association (table 4).

Discussion

This study assessed the magnitude of AGE and its associated factors among under-five children seeking healthcare services at governmental hospitals. According to the findings of this study, the overall prevalence of AGE among under-five children was 24.6% (95% CI 23.4% to 25.9%) over a 2-week period. This finding was consistent with the study conducted in some parts of Ethiopia: Dire Dawa, 23%17 and South Omo Zone, 23.5%.15 The findings of this study were also in line with those of the studies conducted in Rwanda, 26.7%12 and Burundi, 22.84%.19

On the other hand, the findings of this study were relatively lower than the institution-based cross-sectional study conducted in Robe Didea General Hospital and southwest Ethiopia, 34.53% and 28.7%, respectively.18 22 Moreover, the findings of this study were lower than those of a community-based cross-sectional study conducted in the Gaza Strip, 46.1%.11 The high prevalence in Gaza may be due to the fact that the study was conducted at the end of the summer season and directly after the last Israeli operation (aggression) on the Gaza Strip in 2014, where 25% of people were displaced and stayed at United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East, governmental schools, non-hygienic places such as garages, or even poorly prepared places.11

Conversely, the results of this study were higher than the 2016 Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey report, where 12% of under-five children had a diarrhoeal episode 2 weeks before the survey.32 The findings of this study were also higher than those studies conducted in Madagascar, 8.16%,8 USA, 10.4%,9 India, 7.3%,10 Northeastern Ethiopia, 11%14 and West Ethiopia, 14.5%.16 The higher prevalence in this study may be attributed to the fact that the study was conducted shortly after the diarrhoea outbreak in the Somali Region.33

This study found that factors such as household size exceeding five, unprotected sources of drinking water, unimproved latrine facilities, the presence of faeces around the pit hole, slab or floor of the latrine, and the history of diarrhoea by mothers or caregivers over the past 2 weeks were significant and independent predictors of AGE in multivariable logistic regression analysis, with a p value of less than 0.05 using the backward selection method.

The exploration of AGE reveals a compelling relationship between household size and the prevalence of this condition, underscoring the critical role of family dynamics in health outcomes. The data indicate that families exceeding five members are three times more susceptible to gastroenteritis, highlighting a potential area for public health intervention. This study’s findings aligned with a Northeastern Ethiopian study that identified the presence of more children in a household as a contributing factor.14 This suggests that larger families may face increased risk factors for AGE, possibly due to the challenges of maintaining hygiene in more crowded living conditions.

Another important predictor of AGE was the unprotected source of drinking water. Participants who had reported an unprotected source of drinking water were 2.74 times more likely to have AGE as compared with those who had reported a protected source of drinking water. This may be associated with the fact that 58.3% of under-five children in the Somali Region do not have access to improved water.6 A similar finding was reported in the study conducted in Northeastern Ethiopia, South Omo Zone, Arsi Robe, Sub-Saharan Africa and Southwest Ethiopia that reliance on an unimproved drinking water source was linked with diarrhoea.14 15 18 21 22 The association between unimproved water sources and gastroenteritis underscores the need for targeted interventions to improve water quality and access in low-resource settings like Somali Regional State.

Additionally, the types of latrine facilities were found to be a significant predictor of AGE. Participants who had reported unimproved latrine facilities were three times more likely to have AGE as compared with those participants who had reported an improved latrine facility. This result was in line with research from the Kamashi district in Western Ethiopia and Sub-Saharan Africa that found a substantial correlation between acute diarrhoea, poor latrine hygiene and a lack of handwashing facilities near latrines and toilets.16 21 Therefore, by enhancing sanitation infrastructure and promoting hygiene education, there is potential to reduce the incidence of acute gastroenteritis.

Furthermore, the presence of faeces around the pit hole, slab or floor of the latrine was significantly associated with AGE. Accordingly, participants who had reported the presence of faeces around the pit hole, slab or floor of the latrine were 3.53 times more likely to have AGE as compared with those participants who denied the presence of faeces around the pit hole, slab or floor of the latrine. A study in India, Tanzania, Northeastern Ethiopia, South Omo Zone, Southern Ethiopia and Harar reported a similar finding, strongly linking the presence of faeces around the pit hole, slab or floor of the latrine to diarrhoeal illness.1013,15 23 This correlation suggests that unsanitary conditions, such as the accumulation of faeces, may increase the risk of spreading pathogens that cause AGE. Therefore, maintaining cleanliness in latrine areas is crucial for preventing such infections.

Moreover, there was a strong correlation between the history of diarrhoea in mothers or caregivers over the past 2 weeks and AGE. Thus, participants whose mothers or caregivers had a history of diarrhoea over the past 2 weeks were six times more likely to have AGE than those participants whose mothers or caregivers had no history of diarrhoea over the past 2 weeks. This finding was in agreement with the study conducted in Benna Tsemay District, South Omo Zone, Southern Ethiopia and Harar town, Harari region, where a child’s vulnerability to diarrhoea was found to be associated with the mother’s or caretaker’s recent history of diarrhoea within the past 2 weeks.15 23 This suggests that recent gastrointestinal issues in mothers or caregivers may increase the risk of AGE in their children, potentially due to shared environmental factors or transmission of pathogens.

The study identified several independent variables potentially associated with AGE in children under five. However, the cross-sectional design of the study does not establish a cause-and-effect relationship among these variables. Additionally, there was no laboratory investigation to further explore the aetiology of AGE. Nevertheless, the study was conducted in a multicentre setting and assessed multiple variables that other studies did not address. Consequently, the findings of this study can provide baseline information about the prevalence of acute gastroenteritis and its associated factors among children under five in a resource-limited setting like Jigjiga City.

Conclusion

Nearly a quarter of children had AGE over the 2-week period. Factors such as household size greater than five, unprotected source of drinking water, unimproved latrine facilities, the presence of faeces around the latrines and the history of diarrhoea in mothers or caregivers over the past 2 weeks were significant and independent predictors of AGE. Therefore, enhancing the drinking water source by offering a safe supply and raising public awareness of family planning options may help lower the morbidity and mortality associated with AGE. Furthermore, developing better latrine facilities and stressing appropriate sanitation behaviours, such as cleaning and using latrines properly, can help lower the incidence of AGE in under-five children. Additionally, it is imperative to reduce the occurrence of AGE by optimising maternal and child healthcare services and disseminating health information to mothers or caregivers regarding appropriate child feeding habits, particularly during episodes of diarrhoea.
