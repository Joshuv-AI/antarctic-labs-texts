C3 Glomerulonephritis Associated With Anti-complement Factor B Antibodies Following Anti-cancer Treatment With Pembrolizumab
Authors: Persia Stroppou, Lucia Asaelof, Alexia Panayides, Maria Arsali, Michalis Spartalis, Sophia Kevrekidou, Themis Christofi, Theophanis Theophanous, Constantinos Michaelides, Kevin J Marchbank, Kyproula Dimitriou, David Kavanagh, Nicos Mitsides
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractComplement 3 (C3) glomerulonephritis (C3GN) is a rare diagnosis based on predominant C3 glomerular deposition relative to other immune reactants on kidney biopsy. It is typically a membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis with or without crescents. Causes are heterogenous, ranging from genetic defects to acquired autoimmunity against complement factors. A 78-year-old male presented with stage 3 acute kidney injury and microscopic hematuria on the background of a diarrheal illness, urinary tract infection and 2 years treatment with pembrolizumab for metastatic bladder cancer. Kidney histology revealed a crescentic C3GN with intratubular red blood cell casts. Complement analysis showed an acquired autoimmunity with circulating factor B antibodies. Despite treatment with corticosteroids, plasma exchange, cyclophosphamide and eculizumab he remained dialysis-dependent. This is an unusually aggressive case of C3GN. PD-1 inhibitors, such as pembrolizumab, are known to predispose to loss of tolerance and generation of autoantibodies. In this case, factor B autoantibodies were detected in association with C3GN.Index Words: C3 glomerulonephritis, glomerulonephritis, pembrolizumab

C3GN is a rare glomerular diagnosis based on predominant C3 deposition on kidney biopsy.1,2 The histology of C3GN is usually a membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN) and, in cases, crescents can be seen.3 These tend to be few and small but crescentic rapidly progressing glomerulonephritis (RPGN) without MPGN has been reported.3 C3GN results from different pathological processes involving inherent or acquired abnormalities of the alternative pathway (AP) of complement resulting in increased complement activation1 accounting for the diversity in clinical presentation, histology and response to treatment.1,2 Genetic causes including mutations of complement factor H (CFH), I (CFI), B (CFB), CFH-related5 (CFHR5) and complement C3 are rare. Acquired autoimmunity against factor FH, FI, FB and C3bBb, C3bBbC3b, C4bC2a, C4bC2aC3b, is collectively common, but their genesis is less well understood.1,2,4,5 There have been links to other autoimmune conditions,6,7 postinfective complement dysregulation and autoimmunity,8, 9, 10 as well as autoimmunity triggered by pharmacological agents and immune pathway modulators such as anti-cancer therapies.11,12 Identifying potential pathological pathways and their response to treatment would help improve outcomes in C3GN.4 We present an unusual case of C3GN, in its severity, presentation, and histology. We discuss potential factors linked to its development and findings that can help our understanding of acquired autoimmunity against key complement factors.

He had a medical history of chronic kidney disease stage 3, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and T3N1 bladder cancer diagnosed 5 years earlier. After a combination of gemcitabine and cisplatin chemotherapy, he had a metastatic relapse (lung, liver, and penis) one year later and commenced on radiotherapy and pembrolizumab. He completed 2 years of treatment with good response. He remained on surveillance with no further treatment or disease progression for 2 years before this episode.

On admission he was hemodynamically stable and oligoanuric and was treated for a urinary tract infection (UTI) (Enterococcus faecalis and Klebsiella pneumoniae) with ciprofloxacin and vancomycin based on antibiotic sensitivities. He had no recent exposure to other potentially nephrotoxic medications or contrast agents. Ultrasound imaging of the urinary tract was normal. He was treated as a prerenal AKI with no response and subsequently required support with hemodialysis.

Serum complement levels were not measured on admission, but he had a negative renal auto-antibody screen and serum protein electrophoresis (Table 1). Kidney biopsy was deferred (due to UTI) and empirical intravenous methylprednisolone was started to cover for interstitial nephritis, (the most common renal complication of pembrolizumab) although considerable time elapsed since the last pembrolizumab use. His management was complicated by an upper gastrointestinal bleed (GI), leading to steroid treatment discontinuation. A gastroscopy revealed a bleeding peptic ulcer in the anterior wall of the duodenal bulb. Tissue biopsies showed edematous but otherwise normal duodenal tissue.

The kidney biopsy showed a crescentic RPGN (Fig 1). With the immunofluorescence (IMF) pending he had plasma exchange (PEX) and one dose of cyclophosphamide. He received a total of 16 sessions of PEX. C3 level was low with normal C4 levels. At the same time, he had a positive cryoglobulin sample. This was negative on subsequent samples with no evidence of cold agglutinins.

Kidney biopsy findings: 10 glomeruli, 3 of which were globally sclerosed. The remaining 7 showed crescents; 5 cellular and 2 fibrocellular. Two crescentic formations were accompanied by fibrinoid necrosis (One shown in panel A). Approximately 30% of interstitial fibrosis and atrophy with areas of acute tubular injury. Many tubules contained red cell casts (shown by arrows in panel B). Arterioles showed severe hyalinization. No evidence of thrombotic microangiopathy were seen. Immunofluorescence (IMF) showed C3 2+ with negative IgG, IgA and fibrinogen and +/- IgM and C1q (panel C). Electron microscopy (EM) showed collapsed capillary loops with thick basement membranes and electron-dense deposits in subendothelial and mesangial areas (shown by arrows in panel D). Some of the subendothelial deposits extended and occupied the entire capillary lumen.

Following the kidney biopsy findings a complement blood panel screen was performed. Soluble C5b-9 levels were not checked as the samples were shipped abroad for analysis, and in these circumstances C5b-9 levels can be challenging to measure. He was started on eculizumab and completed 3 months of treatment with no response. He remains dialysis-dependent.

Discussion

Following the re-classification of MPGN some cases were labelled C3G based on the predominance of C3 deposits on kidney biopsy.1,2 The C3G is further subdivided into dense deposit disease (DDD) and C3GN based on whether the dense immune deposits on electron microscopy are mesangial/subendothelial or predominantly intramembranous along the glomerular basement membrane.1 Although DDD has a clear association with C3 nephritic factor, C3GN is a less described, more heterogeneous category. Accounting for the rarity of C3GN there are many gaps in our understanding of its pathogenesis. We present a case of an elderly man with stage 3 AKI and a possible link to his anticancer treatment, the PD-1 inhibitor pembrolizumab.22

The kidney biopsy showed RPGN and, with IMF pending, the differential diagnoses included ANCA negative vasculitis or anti-GBM disease with negative circulating antibodies, both reported to complicate pembrolizumab use.23,24 Thus, PEX and cyclophosphamide were initiated. IMF showed C3 immune deposits without IgG, pointing towards C3GN. This was confirmed by electron microscopy. Severe crescentic C3GN (>50%) without features of MPGN has been reported, although in much younger patients.3 We resorted to using the monoclonal humanized antibody, eculizumab, that has been used in C3GN with varying degrees of success25,26 with no response, likely reflecting the extensive glomerular involvement.

The genetic screen did not reveal any inherent abnormalities of the complement AP although the patient did appear to have one copy of CFHR1 and CFHR4.27,28 This is usually due a deletion of the CFHR1-CFHR4 genes. In some cases, this deletion is accompanied by a CFHR3::CFHR4 hybrid gene that has been previously reported in patients with atypical heamolytic uremic syndrome27 and C3G28 However, its pathological significance is undetermined.

The C3GN here was associated with a suspected acquired deregulation of the AP in the presence of anti-FB autoantibodies. FB and its active fragment Bb are important for complement activation. Bb comprises 2 protein domains: a von Willebrand type A domain, which associates to C3b to form the C3 convertase C3bBb, and a serine proteinase domain that cleaves C3.29 Anti-FB autoantibodies have been reported in immune complex MPGN and lead to overactivity of C3 convertase and activation of the alternative pathway.30 They are more often described in children and young adults with post-infective GN (PIGN), irrespective of C3 dominance.8,29,30 Differentiating between PIGN and other causes of C3GN can be difficult, but the clinical course can often help.10 By far, the most common organisms associated with PIGN are streptococci but other organisms have been linked.9,29 Our case had 2 infective sources, a diarrhoeal illness immediately prior to admission (no stool cultures) and a UTI. However, the timeline from infection to presentation was too short for PIGN, ASO titer was negative, and UTI is not commonly associated with PIGN. Moreover, PIGN tends to follow a self-limited course with a transient drop in C3, potentially reflecting autoantibody level elevation (when associated with anti-FB), followed by recovery.29,30 This is associated with a drop in anti-FB titres. Unfortunately, no follow-up sample was available in this gentleman.

Anti-FB autoantibodies have also been reported in infection-related cryoglobulinemic C3GN in endocarditis.8 Cryoglobulins are associated with malignancy,31,32 PD-1 inhibitors, including pembrolizumab33, 34, 35 and PIGN. In our patient cryoglobulins were initially detected although the methodology used in our hospital does not allow identification of the cryoglobulin type or quantification. There were no extra-renal features of a cryoglobulinemic vasculitis, no IgG deposits on IMF, or typical cryoglobulinaemic changes on kidney biopsy.8 There was no evidence on endoscopy or gastric biopsy to support that the upper GI bleed he experienced was due to cryoglobulinemic vasculitis.

PD-1 inhibitors have been linked with C3GN,22, 23, 24 being the third most common glomerular pathology complicating their use (11% of cases).24 To date, 6 cases have been reported, mostly with pembrolizumab, all presenting with AKI, microscopic hematuria, and MPGN.11,23 One case reported had remarkable red blood cell (RBC) casts within the renal tubules.11 All cases were on PD-1 inhibitors at the onset of C3GN.11,23 Most cases have been on PD-1 inhibitors < 6 months with the exception of the case with the RBC cast (18 months).11,23 Four cases showed partial recovery with steroid treatment alone, but the case with the RBC casts resulted in end-stage kidney disease despite treatment with cyclophosphamide11,23 possibly reflecting the aggressive nature of the C3GN, similar to our case. Our patient was on a PD-1 inhibitor for 2 years but had been off treatment for 2 years preceding this presentation. Although this might feel a long time Planned Death-1/PD-1 inhibitors interfere with apoptotic mechanisms and can alter permanently immune regulatory pathways. There have been reports of late immunological adverse events, especially in those with prolonged periods of treatment,36 and glomerular disease has been reported up to 6 months after discontinuation,37 we are not aware of another case presenting after 2 years.

C3G is predominantly a disease of the AP of complement, and as such, treatment with the terminal complement pathway inhibitor eculizumab has largely proven ineffective.2 Similarly, a phase 2 trial of the C5aR inhibitor avacopan also failed.38 Recently AP inhibition, in the form of the FB inhibitor iptacopan and the C3/C3b inhibitor pegcetacoplan has proven effective in phase 239,40 and 3 trials41,42of C3G offering targeted therapy to patients with C3G.

In summary, we present an unusually aggressive case of RPGN with predominantly C3 glomerular deposition and evidence of complement AP activation in the presence of anti-FB antibodies. It is never possible to identify causality when describing a real-world case, especially when there are many possible triggers and associations. PD-1 plays a key role in maintaining immune tolerance, and inhibitors are known to predispose to the generation of autoantibodies. We hypothesise this resulted in the generation of autoantibodies to FB with complement dysregulation that led to this unusually severe C3G. Successful clinical trials of AP complement inhibitors in C3G point to a more targeted treatment approach in the future.
