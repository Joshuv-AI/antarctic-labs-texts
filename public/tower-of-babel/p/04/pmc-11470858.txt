Green synthesis of self-oriented flower-like Ag@Ag2O nanostructures functionalized with L-Tryptophan for colorimetric simultaneous determination of ultra-trace level of thiamin and riboflavin
Authors: Maryam Abbasi Tarighat, Zahra Khosravani, Gholamreza Abdi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The study focuses on the green synthesis of Ag@Ag2O nanostructures using Padina algae extract and functionalizing them with L-tryptophan to enhance their properties as a colorimetric sensor for simultaneous detection of ultra-trace levels of thiamin and riboflavin. The nanostructures are characterized using techniques like XRD, FESEM, FTIR, TEM, AFM, and DLS to understand their morphology, structure, and interactions with target molecules. FESEM analysis revealed the hierarchical flower-like Ag@Ag2O nanostructures. The TEM image shows the formation of core-shell nanostructures. Also, DLS analysis and surface zeta potential spectra illustrated the aggregated nature of fabricated nanocomposites in the presence of vitamins. The study is the first to report simultaneous determination of thiamin and riboflavin using a colorimetric sensor based on Ag@Ag2O-L-Try nanocomposites using partial leas square (PLS). The dynamic range of thiamin and riboflavin was achieved in 0.1 mol L−1 acetate buffer pH 4 and the ratio Ag@Ag2O: L-try 1:1. The Ag@Ag2O-L-Try sensor exhibited two linear ranges of 0.1- 1.0 and 3-350 µMol L− 1 for riboflavin and a linear range 3.0–60 µMol L− 1 for thiamin. Also, low detection limit of 1.92 µMol L− 1 and 0.048 µMol L− 1 was obtained for riboflavin and thiamin, respectively. The results indicated that the success of the method depends on the selective and sensitive colorimetric assay of the sensor along with the simultaneous determination by the PLS algorithm. Hence, the proposed technique can be used for the accurate and precise determination of vitamins in different pharmaceutical syrup and tablet samples.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00726-024-03406-5.

Introduction

Vitamins are a category of organic compounds that are regarded as essential micronutrients. They are not made by the human body and, therefore, must be consumed daily as foods and supplements. They are typically devoid of energy but they are essential for the body’s healthy functioning as well as metabolism, protection against infections, growth, and development. Numerous illnesses are frequently brought on by a vitamin absence in the human body. Because the various forms of vitamin B all dissolve in water, they cannot be retained by the body and must be consumed often to prevent deficiency.

Thiamin, often known as vitamin B1 (VB1), protects against disorders with the heart, stomach, intestines, muscles, neurological system, and brain. Additionally, it affects how electrolytes enter and exit muscle and nerve cells. Anorexia, irritability, confusion, seizures, shortness of breath, and problems with short-term memory are some of the initial indicators of B1 insufficiency. It aids in the prevention of diseases like beriberi, which cause problems with the heart, nerves, and digestive system (Mrowicka et al. 2023).

Riboflavin, often known as vitamin B2 (VB2), is a nutrient that can be taken as a supplement or found naturally in the diet. Small amounts of riboflavin can be produced by gut bacteria, but they are not in sufficient quantities to satisfy the nutritional requirements. Riboflavin is an essential part of coenzymes that support cell development, energy production, and the metabolism of lipids, hormones, and drugs. Since the body does not keep the majority of riboflavin, excess levels are expelled in the urine. During children growth and pregnancy in women, the body needs more vitamin B2. Symptoms of a vitamin B2 absence include vision problems, neurological system problems, wounds, or inflammation of the skin (Mosegaard et al. 2020).

Today, simultaneous determination and quantification of these vitamins from various matrices represents a significant challenge. Traditionally, microbiological tests were used to analyze individual vitamins (Sharma et al. 2019), but developments of liquid chromatography (LC) technology have led to approaches that are more accurate and precise. There have been studies utilizing reversed-phase (RP) liquid chromatography with diode array detection (Zhao et al. 2004), electrochemical sensor (Khanmohammadi et al. 2023), UV-Vis detection (Heudi et al. 2005; Taghizade et al. 2021) and fluorescence detection (Gatti and Gioia 2005), as well as electrochemical/coulometric for different type of vitamin detection and measurements (Donato et al. 2012).

Mass spectrometry (MS), is particularly useful for quantifying vitamins in trace levels. So, several works have been proposed with the couple of HPLC and mass spectroscopy (Chen et al. 2006), and HPLC-isotope dilution mass spectrometry (Huang et al. 2010) and HPLC- MS-MS(Zia et al. 2023). HPLC-thin layer chromatography (Panahi et al. 2011) and supercritical fluid chromatography (Taguchi et al. 2014) has been suggested. In addition to chromatographic methods, the determination of vitamins has been performed employing capillary electrophoresis (da Silva et al. 2013) chemiluminescence (Kamruzzaman et al. 2013), and fluorometric analysis (Chen et al. 2015). HPLC and MS techniques are expensive equipment and require high operator skill. HPLC is a time-consuming analysis with a small sample throughput that also requires a large volume of hazardous solvents. Capillary electrophoresis is mainly used when only small amounts of a sample are available. The electrochemical techniques provided advantages in terms of their simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and fast response time. The determination of water-soluble vitamins using electrochemistry has the main drawbacks of low electrode stability and the necessity of electrode surface modification. Also, for species reduction, these techniques require high overvoltage and produce higher background currents. (Antherjanam et al. 2021; Tomčik et al. 2004).

Chemometric techniques are very useful for increasing the interpretation, accuracy, and predictive ability of data, and can improves sensor performance. Sensor technologies can attain greater sensitivity, selectivity, and dependability by utilizing these sophisticated statistical and mathematical methodologies, which increases their effectiveness in a range of analytical applications. Connection of chemometrics with sensor systems has created new opportunities for precise and accurate analysis in different areas including food safety, environmental monitoring, pharmaceutical and medical diagnostics.

Simultaneous determination using sensors involves the detection and quantification of multiple analytes in a single measurement. Chemometric methods play a crucial role in achieving accurate and reliable simultaneous determination by analyzing complex sensor data. Key chemometric methods used in this context include principal component analysis (PCA)(Ameku et al. 2021), cluster Analysis(Xu et al. 2022), Ppartial least squares regression (PLSR)(Tarighat and Naamdar 2024), artificial neural networks(Wang et al. 2019), support vector machines (Liu et al. 2022), genetic algorithms (Mendes and Carvalho 2005) and wavelet transformation(Tarighat et al. 2022b).

Metal nanoparticles have drawn wide attention for the sensing and quantitation of different species in various analytical, environmental, and biological fields. It is due to their unique qualities, such as their quantum effects, optical and electrical capabilities, biocompatibility, and long-term stability. Without the use of any additional equipment, their excellent optical characteristics exhibit color change that is even visible to the naked eye. This effect is connected to their special localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR). These sensors utilize LSPR, which is caused by the surrounding of surface plasmons in thin metallic layers when light is stimulated on the surface of conductive nanoparticles (Masson 2020). Hence, the resonance condition is produced when free electrons collectively oscillate in the electric field of incoming light due to the LSPR phenomenon. Particularly, LSPR bands in the visible part of the electromagnetic spectrum have been demonstrated by silver nanoparticles. This optical property serves as the foundation for colorimetric detection because it allows for the detection of color changes in colloid suspensions where the intensity, frequency, and location of the LSPR bands strongly depend on the size, shape, surface coating, dielectric environment, and aggregation state of the metal nanoparticles (Mauriz 2020). Colorimetric sensors have demonstrated great analytical capabilities in terms of low cost, simple and fast response, high reproducibility, and high efficiency using simple and available equipment.

To enhance the functionality and performance of AgNPs or nanocomposites for colorimetric sensing, surface modification is a vital aspect. Biomolecule immobilization, functionalization, polymer coating, molecular imprinting, self-assembly(Kulkarni et al. 2022), pH-responsive coatings, surface charge modification, bioconjugation methods, surface characterization, and environmental concerns are some of the techniques used(Uddin et al. 2022).

Techniques for functionalization include carboxylation, amino modification, silanization, and thiolation(Singh et al. 2023; Uddin et al. 2023). Polymer coatings improve dispersibility, stability, and biocompatibility (Kulkarni et al. 2022). The process of immobilizing proteins, enzymes, or antibodies onto a surface to enable selective detection is known as biomolecule immobilization (Mahmoudpour et al. 2021; Iriarte‑Mesa et al. 2020). Target analytes can bind selectively to molecularly imprinted polymer layers created via molecular imprinting(Bagheri et al. 2018).

A variety of techniques, employing various nanoparticles as reagents, including gold (Lin et al. 2018; Duenchay et al. 2020), functionalized multiwall carbon nanotube (Brahman et al. 2013), Graphene oxide (Fooladi et al. 2020), copper nanoclusters (Luo et al. 2015), silver nanoparticles (Khalkho et al. 2020; Kingslin et al. 2022), and molecular imprinting based SPR sensors (Çimen and Denizli 2020) for determining vitamin VB1 or VB2 have been published. Our literature review shows that simultaneous determination of these vitamins in various matrices has not been performed.

Utilizing algae extract to create Ag@Ag2O nanocomposites from AgNO3 is a sustainable and environmentally friendly approach that minimizes the usage of toxic chemicals. Because the extract doesn’t have harmful effects, it can be used in biomedical research because it includes biocompatible components. Biomolecules found in algae extract also improve colloidal stability. It could have an impact on the nanocomposite’s morphology, leading to controllable particle size and shape. Additionally, the extract may improve surface plasmon resonance and other optical qualities that make it appropriate for sensing applications. Algae extract is inexpensive, easy to use in moderate reaction conditions, and has a little influence on the environment. Furthermore, capping agents are frequently not required in the presence of algal extract in order to stop nanoparticle aggregation(Tarighat and Naamdar 2024).

The vital role of vitamins in maintaining human health cannot be ignored. Their deficiency causes a variety of adverse effects on humans. Hence, the accurate quantification of water-soluble vitamins in supplements and pharmaceutical preparations has become a major challenge. The proposed method could realize quick LSPR analysis with a wide linear dynamic ranges and low detection limits for VB1 and VB2. Keeping these points, the present study aims to (i) rapid, easy, precise and eco-friendly one pot synthesis of Ag@Ag2O core-shell, (II) characterization of silver hybrid nanostructure and (III) application of Ag@Ag2O functionalized with L-Tryptophan (L-Try) as a selective, sensitive and accurate colorimetric sensor (Ag@Ag2O-L-Try) for simultaneous determination of VB1 and VB2. To our knowledge, it is the first report of synthesized hierarchical flower-like Ag@Ag2O-L-Ttry core-shell using algae extract for simultaneous determination of ultra-trace level of target vitamins in different supplement and pharmaceutical products. Different characteristic techniques were used to characterize the Ag@Ag2O and Ag@Ag2O-L-Try surface to understand the interaction between sensors and targets.

Experimental

Materials and methods

Water-soluble vitamins (-)-riboflavin (98%) and thiamine (99%) were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (Gillingham, Dorset). Ethanol, nicotinic acid, uric acid, pyridoxine hydrochloride, glutamine, adrenaline, silver nitrate, sodium acetate and 98% acetic acid were acquired from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany).

Apparatus

A pH/mV meter (Model Metrohm 713, Switzerland) is employed. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM-SEM model TTESCAN MIRAIII, SEM-EDX Electron Microscopy (FEI/Philips XL 30 Eindhoven, NL) was used to examine the micrographs of the nanocomposite. For the FTIR measurement, a Bruker Vector 22 FT-IR with a transmittance mode spectral range of 4100 to 400 cm-1 was utilized. A spectrophotometer made by Analytica Jena was used to measure the UV-Vis spectra. The spectra were recorded using a quartz cell measuring 1.0 cm, a scan rate of 100 nm min-1, and a slit width of 2.0 mm. The captured spectra were digitized one data point per nanometer. To capture an electron microscope picture, an accelerating voltage of 80 kV was applied to the Zeiss-EM10C.

Collection of algae samples

Algae Padina sp. samples were collected from the seashores of the Persian Gulf. The collected samples were washed with sea water and transferred to the laboratory in a plastic bag. In order to remove surface contamination, the collected samples were then washed several times with double-distilled water. The collected brown algae were allowed to dry at room temperature for two weeks. After that, dried samples were ground and kept in a refrigerator for future use.

Preparing algae extract

The following process was used for extracting proteins and secondary metabolites from algae powder. First, 15 g of algae powder is weighed and transferred to a 500-mL beaker. Distilled water is added to the beaker, and the mixture is stirred for 1 h (without heat) to hydrate and disperse. Then, the uniform mixture is heated to 80 °C for 20 min, allowing for the extraction of proteins and secondary metabolites. The solution is then cooled to room temperature and filtered through Whatman filter paper. Filtration is performed to separate solid residues from the liquid extract. The resulting filtrate contains the extracted proteins and secondary metabolites. The obtained solution can be mixed with a silver nitrate solution to facilitate the reduction of Ag+ ions and formation of Ag nanoparticles.

Green synthesis of Ag@Ag2O NCs

The synthesis of silver nanoparticles using algae extract was done in several steps as follows. First, 0.0237 g silver nitrate (AgNO₃) was weighed and transferred it to a 100-mL volumetric flask.

After adding distilled water a 0.237 mg/mL AgNO₃ solution was created. The reaction mixture was then prepared by mixing 80 mL of the prepared silver nitrate solution and 20 mL of the previously prepared algae aqueous extract. The solution was then transferred to a 250-mL conical flask, covered with aluminum foil to prevent photodegradation. After 2 h, the pH of solution was adjusted by adding 0.1 mol L− 1 NaOH solution to the solution, continuously was stirred until pH reached to 10.7. Then, the color change from colorless to dark brown was observed, which indicated the formation of Ag@Ag2ONCs. The solution is then maintained at 70 °C for an additional 48 h to ensure complete reduction of silver ions and Ag@Ag2O nanocomposite formation(Tarighat and Naamdar 2024).

During the synthesis procedure, a pH of 10.7 was used to promote the production and stability of Ag@Ag2O nanocomposites. Because of the effective reduction of silver ions to Ag nanoparticles made possible by this pH, the nanoparticles were stable and did not aggregate or precipitate. Elevated pH levels may cause dissolution and agglomeration of nanoparticles. In order to balance a stable and efficient nanoparticle creation, the pH was adjusted experimentally; raising it could not have the desired effects or may have adverse effects. At acidic and neutral pH levels, the AgNPs had been exhibited destabilization and higher rates of aggregation while in the alkaline conditions, the particles had been stabilized due to the presence of hydroxyl ions. In conclusion, based on the advantages observed in the stability and performance of silver nanoparticles under pH-controlled conditions, pH 10.7 appears advantageous for the synthesis of Ag@Ag2O nanocomposites (Impact of pH on the stability, dissolution and aggregation kinetics of silver nanoparticles).

Synthesis of Ag@Ag2O L-Try sensor

To prepare an L-Tryptophan solution, 0.015 g was weighted and transferred to 25 mL volumetric flask. The L-Tryptophan dissolved in distilled water and mixed well until a clear solution formed. Then Ag@Ag2O solution was mixed with equal volume of L-Tryptophan solutions in a clean beaker. The beaker was placed on a magnetic stirrer and stirred at 750 rpm for one hour to ensure thorough mixing.

Results and discussion

Optical characterization of the Ag@Ag2O NCs nanostructure

The primary indication that the noble metal nanoparticles in the solution have been successfully biosynthesized is a color change. The silver nitrate solution’s color has changed from colorless to light yellow after 20 min of the addition of algae extract (Scheme 1). A second color transition from bright yellow to brown occurred after four hours. Although it’s possible that the production of Ag2O nanoparticles is partly responsible for the deep brown color, the quick color change from colorless-yellow to brown proves that silver ions (Ag+) were bio-reduced to metallic AgNPs. It may be declared that the extract’s active secondary metabolites and proteins act as capping agents in silver nanoparticle synthesis and prevent aggregation (Dhoondia and Chakraborty 2012). Hence, the synthesized nanostructure would disperse effectively in water, and a stable colloidal suspension would be formed, as shown in Scheme 1. Figure 1 shows the absorption spectra in the 200–800 nm range related to the synthesized nanostructure. At the beginning of the synthesis was the only peak observed at 420 nm, but with the passage of time, a new shoulder appeared in wavelength 260 nm. By observing the optical absorbance with a broad band around 430 nm, the synthesis of AgNPs was approved (Fig. 1A). Thus, the absorption peak was at 420 nm was the characteristic peak of pure AgNPs, while the red shift of the composite peak indicated that the metallic particles were hybridized. Metal nanoparticles have free electrons in the valence layer. According to May’s theory, from the interaction of light with these electrons, an absorption band is obtained in the visible region, the shape and intensity of which depend on the size of the particles and how uniform they are, as well as the type of solvent and the aggregation of the particles. After the attachment of L-Try to the surface of the nanostructure, the color solution was changed. The UV-Vis spectrum of the sensor showed that the absorption spectrum was changed and the nanocomposite exhibited a distinctive absorption peak at 275 nm, which was different from the 270 nm peak of both the L-Try and the Ag@Ag2O nanostructures (Fig. 1B). Still, Plasmon resonance was revealed in the appearance of absorption peaks at 420 nm, and were responsible for the color of the colloidal nanocomposite solution. But some changes in the spectrum also reveal that the solution absorption peak can be made by the other particles available in the solution, too. Hence, incorporation of L-Try into the hybrid structure of nanocomposite rather than just coexisting in the solution has occurred. So, L-Try bonding to the surface of Ag@Ag2O is responsible for this color change and wavelength shift that a decrease of the absorption peak at 420 nm and increasing absorption has happened at 270 nm (Tarighat and Naamdar 2024).

When Ag@Ag2O NCs are linked to L-tryptophan (L-Try), the UV-Vis absorption spectrum is altered. Numerous variables, including as SPR, changes in the environment, interactions in charge transfer, bonding and complex formation, aggregation and size distribution, and solvent effects, are responsible for this shift. The local dielectric environment is changed when L-Try binds to the nanoparticles, which shifts the location of the SPR peak and changes the absorption properties. The attachment of L-Try appears to have altered the plasmonic behavior, as indicated by the reduction in the absorption peak at 420 nm. The electron density on the surface of the nanoparticles may change due to charge transfer interactions between L-Try and the nanoparticles, changing the absorption characteristics. compared to the first nanoparticles, producing fresh peaks in absorption. novel electronic states or complexes that absorb light differently from the initial nanoparticles can develop via complex formation or bonding to the Ag@Ag2O NCs, leading to novel absorption peaks.

The COO- stretching peak, typically found at around 1679 cm− 1, is often associated with the asymmetric bending modes of N-H (indole) at approximately 1584 cm− 1 (Hou et al. 2014). The bands observed at approximately 3414 cm− 1 (N-H stretching in the amine group) and wide bands spanning around 3037, 2863, 2579, 2724, 2023, and 2071 cm− 1 correspond to vibrations in C-H stretching. Additionally, the bands at 1679 cm− 1, 1584 cm− 1, and 743 cm− 1 are linked to -C-C- stretching.

The peaks in the region between 3170 and 3409 cm− 1 were assigned to N-H stretching of amides. The main amides group, which is frequently present in the protein, is responsible for the absorption peak at 1620 cm− 1 due to C = O stretching and N–H bending vibrations. The stretching vibrations of C = C and C–C in the aromatic cycles is correlated with the absorption bands located approximately at 1790 cm− 1(Laouini et al. 2021; Ismail et al. 2018).

According to reported studies, metal-oxygen stretching frequencies usually appear in the range of 500 to 600 cm− 1. The vibrations of Ag-O and O-Ag-O are assigned peaks at 588 cm-1; this band confirms the formation of the Ag@Ag2O nanostructure. The combination of two bands attributed to the stretch and bend modes of Ag-O and Ag-O-Ag creates the wide band at 550 cm1 at modified Ag@Ag2O NCs. It is in accordance with previous reports about the synthesis of Ag2O nanoparticles using jackfruit trees (Chaudhary et al. 2020), octopus (Behzad et al. 2021) and papaya root extract (Forough and Farhadi 2010). The absorption band in the region of 1600 –1400 cm− 1 can be related to the symmetric and asymmetric stretching of the COO group and the symmetric and asymmetric bending bond of the free NH group, respectively. Also, absorption in the region of 1600 to 1470 cm− 1 is associated with C = C vibrations.

According to the presence of the absorbance bands seen around the 3400 cm − 1 and 1620 cm − 1, which assigned to the NH stretching vibrations of free NH group and C = C, the capping agent of the AgNPs may be amines or aromatic compound. This finding is in accordance with the previous results that reported polyols such as terpenoids, flavones and polysaccharides are responsible for the synthesis of nano-sized silver particles(Jegadeeswaran et al. 2012; Rajathi et al. 2012).

Also, the above results show that Ag@Ag2ONS was synthesized and the functional group was successfully placed on the surface of the nanostructure. There is no remarkable difference in the spectra of Ag@Ag2O NCs and Ag@Ag2O-L-Try displaying that the integrity of the amino acid is retained. L-Try spectrum is not visible in the FTIR spectrum of the Ag@Ag2O-L-Try, so it can be proposed that the carboxyl group adsorbed onto the Ag@Ag2O surface. According to the value of the pH point of zero charge (pHpzc) related to silver nanoparticles, which is 8.6, in lower amounts, the charge on the surface will be strongly positive, and in higher amounts, it will be negative. Therefore, the assumption of amino acid binding from the carboxylic acid group to the surface is approved.

Despite the possibility of L-tryptophan being present on the surface, this suggests that it does not interact chemically in a way that would change the nanocomposite’s crystal structure. Figure 2C shows the FESEM images of the Ag@Ag2ONCs using Padina algae in magnification of 10 micrometers. The morphology of silver nanoparticles is in the form of cubes and silver oxide nanoparticles are distributed in the form of clusters. The cubic -shaped of AgNPs are in agreement of XRD analysis and our previous work (Tarighat et al. 2022a).

TEM can be applied to the interpretation of the nanoparticle layer growth, their composition, shape, size, and morphology of synthesized nanostructures. The TEM image and histogram of particle distribution obtained from TEM analysis of Ag@Ag2O NCs are shown in Fig. 3A and b. The shape of the Ag core and shell Ag2O nanoparticles can be seen well. The figure shows that the Ag and Ag2O nanostructures are distributed between 4 and 22 nm and the maximum particle size is 11 nm. Figure 3C and D shows the TEM analysis and particle distribution histogram of Ag@Ag2O-L-Try nanocomposite. In this case, the distribution of particles is in the range of 2–30 nm, and the average distribution of particles is 17.5 nm. A comparison of TEM results shows that for the Ag@Ag2O nanostructure, the average hydrodynamic diameter is lower. Hence, the diameter of the coated nanostructures will be greater than that of the uncoated nanostructures. The growth in hydrodynamic diameter clearly suggested the presence of an L-Try amino acid coat layer on the surface of the modified nanostructure. An atomic force microscope (AFM) is used to investigate the properties and surface structure of materials in nanodimensions. The way a substance interacts with its surroundings is greatly influenced by its surface roughness, which is a part of its surface texture. Analyte adsorption on surfaces of a constructed nanocomposite is impacted by the interaction of surface physicochemical characteristics, including structure, hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity, charges, ridges, steps, and grooves. A comparison of AFM results related to 3D topography images of silver/silver oxide nanostructure alone (Fig. 4A and B) and nanostructure modified with tryptophan (Fig. 4C and D) can be seen in Fig. 4. AFM analysis confirmed the presence of silver/silver oxide at the nanoscale and shows useful morphological details regarding the roughness changes by modification of the nanostructure surface with tryptophan. The results estimate the average roughness value (Ra) for the Ag@Ag2O nanostructure to be 0.036 nm and for the Ag@Ag2O-L-Try sensor to be 5.05 nm. The standard deviation (Rq) reported for the nanostructure is alone 0.261 nm and for the Ag@Ag2O-L-Try sensor, 36.43 nm, which indicates an increase in roughness in the presence of L-tryptophan. Zeta potential analysis is a useful tool for determining the surface charge of nanocomposites as well as providing knowledge of the stability of individual nanoparticles. The value of zeta potential of Ag@Ag2O NCs, synthesized using algae extract, is in the range of -35 mV to + 10mV, which the average value of the stability of this nanoparticle is -14.5 (Fig. 4G). The zeta potential negative value shows that the stability of silver in aqueous solution. According to the Fig. 4H, the mean value of Ag@Ag2O -L-Try zeta potential, appeared in -23.9 mV while potential range is in -15 mV to -40 mV. The results indicate the proper stabilization of nanoparticles in the presence of tryptophan. In other words, functionalizing of Ag@Ag2ONCs using L-tryptophan causes electrostatic repulsion between particles, which ultimately leads to the stability and non-aggregation of individual nanoparticles.

Comparing this work with our previous work show that the synthesized Ag@Ag2O nanocomposite was found to have both metallic Ag and Ag2O phases, suggesting a hybrid structure. The nanocomposite’s particle size ranged from 4 to 22 nm, with an average size of 11 nm in this study, and 14–20 nm in previous one. The surface roughness and morphology changed with modification, with an average roughness of 10 nm for Ag@Ag2O NCs and 4.8 nm for Ag@Ag2O-β-CD NCs after modification. The size distribution varied between 20 nm and 80 nm, with an average size of 40 nm for Ag@Ag2O NCs. At previous work, after modification with β-CD, the size increased, and the average size became larger. Also, the zeta potential values indicated the stability of the nanoparticles, with a more negative value after modification, indicating better stability in presence of modifiers. Both studies demonstrate successful synthesis and modification of Ag@Ag2O nanocomposite, with slight variations in particle size, morphology, and stability.

Spectrophotometric determination of vitamins using Ag@Ag2O-L-Tyr sensor

The findings demonstrated that the fabricated composites were capable of producing stable colloidal suspensions and could disperse efficiently in water. As shown in Fig. 1, localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) has occurred in the form of absorption peaks at around 420 nm and was responsible for the color change of the colloidal solution. Hence, LSPR changes will be used for the simultaneous determination of vitamins. The spectra of VB1 and VB2 in the absence and presence of a colorimetric sensor are given in Fig. 5.

In order to check the interaction of the VB1 and VB2 with the fabricated sensor, the pure spectrum of vitamins was recorded in the range of 200–600 nm (Fig. 5A). As the figure shows, the vitamins do not have absorption spectra at these wavelengths. But their interaction with the modified nanostructure has led to an increase in absorption intensity in its LSPR region and caused a shift to lower wavelengths (blue shift) with respect to sensor spectra. It is due to the changes in the refractive index in the vicinity of the surface of the Ag@Ag2O nanostructure caused by the adsorption of analyte molecules. The spectra in the presence of vitamins are not similar, but there is a high overlap between their spectra. Hence, the colorimetric sensor measure is suitable for simultaneous determination of VB1 and VB2 using the partial least squares (PLS) approach.

Optimizing experimental conditions

In this study, parameters such as pH, volume of nanostructure, ratio of nanostructure to L-tryptophan and buffer concentration were optimized for simultaneous measurement of VB1 and VB2. The effective detection of these vitamins was achieved at pH = 4, a 1:1 ratio of nanoparticles and L-Try, and a volume of 500 µl of Ag@Ag2O-L-Try, which we will discuss in the following.

pH influence

The effect of pH in colorimetric sensing of VB1 and VB2 was investigated in Ag @Ag2O-L-Try NCs solution at 3.0, 4.0, 5.0, 6.0, 6.0 and 7.0. The absorbance changes at maximum wavelengths by fixed concentration of 1 × 10− 5 M versus media pHs were recorded. Figure 5B shows the results of pH influences. According to this figure, the highest sensitivity of vitamins was obtained at pH 4. L-Try with pKa1 = 2.38 and pKa2 = 9.39, and L-Tyr with pKa1=2.2 and pKa2=9.11 at lower pHs mainly present positive charges while negative charges are present at basic medium. In other words, at pH 4, amino acids have the characteristic of zwitterion, which could effectively induce the aggregation of nanocomposite.

Ratio of silver nanoparticles to L-Try

After determining pH = 4 as the optimal pH, the ratio of silver nanoparticles to L-tryptophan in the sensor was studied. So, different ratios of Ag@Ag2O: L-Try 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, and 1:4 at pH = 4 (constant volume of Ag@Ag2O and different concentration of L-Try) were investigated. Absorption changes at the wavelength of 276 nm corresponding to LSPR of Ag@Ag2O nanostructure were recorded. According to Fig. 5C, the maximum value of absorbance was obtained at 1:1 ratio of silver nanostructure and L-tryptophan.

Optimizing the amount of L-Try

The influence of different concentrations of tryptophan was investigated in the optimal ratio of 1:1 of silver nanoparticles and L-tryptophan at pH = 4. Therefore, volumes of 1000, 900, 700, 600 and 500 µL of stock solution of 3 × 10^-3 mol L− 1 were applied and their spectra were recorded. According to the Fig. 5d, the better sensitivity is obtainable at 500 µL.

For the determination of VB1 and VB2, the potential interferences of some amino acids and neurotransmitters were considered (see Fig. 2S). For this purpose, different amounts of selected species were transferred to 50 mol/L− 1 solutions of vitamins, and colorimetric detection was performed at optimum conditions. As shown in Fig. 2S; Table 4, no noticeable variations (approximately less than 10% relative error) were observed. Except glutamine, which was interfering at a 20-fold excess of concentration, and pyridoxine at a 2-fold excess of concentration for both vitamins. Uric acid also interfered at 20-fold and 1-fold excess concentrations for VB1 and VB2, respectively. Consequently, the developed colorimetric sensor may be used for the specific simultaneous measurement of vitamins in supplemental and medicinal products.

Proposed mechanism

According to reports, the pHpzc of AgNPs is 8.6, meaning that there is no net charge on the surface of the nanostructure at this pH. According to the pHpzc idea, the adsorbent surface will be mostly negatively charged at pH values above the pHpzc and will receive a net positive charge at pH values below the pHpzc. The adsorbent surface was strongly cationic for pH values below 8.6, whereas a pHpzc of the solution would yield a net positive charge(Dawodu et al. 2019; Tarighat and Naamdar 2024).

Tryptophan, with a carboxyl group (COOH) and an amino group (NH₂), has a pKa value of 2.38 and 9.39. At pH below 2.38, the carboxyl group is in its protonated form, while at pH between 2.38 and 9.39, the carboxyl group is deprotonated, and the amino group is protonated, making tryptophan a zwitterion. Tryptophan is zwitterion, so it can be attached to the nanoparticle surface from the protonated NH2 or COO−. From the appearance of functional groups in the FTIR spectra, it was concluded that tryptophan is attached to the surface of the nanostructure from the COO− group. Hence, due to the positive charge on the Ag@Ag2O surface, it is attached from the COO− group (Table 1).

Riboflavin, a complex molecule with multiple conjugated ring systems, acts as a zwitterion at physiological pH, having both positive and negative charges within the same molecule but an overall neutral charge. Its planar aromatic rings can interact with the indole ring of tryptophan through π-π stacking interactions, driven by the overlap of π-electron clouds from the aromatic systems.

Thiamine, another zwitterion at neutral pH, consists of a pyrimidine ring and a thiazole ring connected by a methylene bridge. It is also a zwitterion at neutral pH with protonated amino groups and a thiazole sulfur that can interact with other molecules. The thiazole ring of thiamine can interact with the NH₃⁺ group of tryptophan via sulfur’s lone pair electrons engaging in non-covalent bonding with the positively charged NH₃⁺. In summary, the Ag@Ag2O nanocomposites have a stable arrangement of tryptophan, riboflavin, and thiamine on the surface, driven by a combination of electrostatic, π-π, and hydrogen bonding interactions.The proposed mechanism is shown in Scheme 2. According to the result, tryptophan was used as a key component for modification of Ag@Ag2O NCs surface for simultaneous determining VB1 and VB2. For L-Try we expect the following effects. The Ag@Ag2O NCs surfaces may be coated with monolayer or multilayer tryptophan molecules, offering a stable and selective sensing platform. As a functionalizing agent it can provides specific binding sites for interaction with target analytes. It enables specific assessment of these molecules, enhancing sensitivity. L-Try enhances the nanocomposite’s selectivity towards these vitamins due to hydrogen bonding or π-π interactions. Hence, L-Try contributes to the nanocomposite’s stability, preventing aggregation and improving detection accuracy.

Simultaneous determination of vitamins by PLS approach

The absorption spectra of VB1 and VB2 after interaction with synthesized nanocomposite have strong spectral overlapping, as illustrated in Fig. 5A. These amino acids cannot be simultaneously determined by univariate techniques due to their high similarity level. In order to resolve the similarity of spectra, the PLS multivariate calibration technique was therefore suggested as a beneficial strategy in spectral analysis (Tarighat et al. 2015; Shojaeifard et al. 2021). In the simultaneous measurement of riboflavin and thiamine vitamins by the PLS method, forty-three samples were used, thirty-four samples were used as a calibration set and nine samples were used as prediction set (see Table 1S and 2S).

The absorption spectra ranged from 200 to 800 nm; however, the appropriate spectral portions for the quantitative study were chosen to be in the 220 to 520 nm range because this region contains the spectral information of the analyst. Additionally, it has been shown that pre-processing data to remove unnecessary information is effective and improves the capacity for prediction. The two most popular techniques for data pretreatment are mean centering and auto-scaling (Abbasi-Tarighat et al. 2023). Data scaling is often used to minimize or reduce additional sources of variance when the primary modeling tool does not sufficiently account for them (whether random or systematic). For the construction of the PLS model number of latent variables should be optimized as the first influencing variable. Through the use of cross-validation, an appropriate amount of significant latent variables for the PLS algorithm was found. The number that results in the lowest square error of cross-validation (RMSECV) would be an appropriate choice for the ideal number of factors. The RMSECV value was determined by comparing the predicted concentrations in each sample with the actual values. Equations from the literature were used to determine RMSECV.

A graph of the predictive residual error sum of squares (PRESS) versus the number of factors for L-Try and L-Tyr was obtained. These graphs indicated the minimum number of factors in the PLS model for simultaneous determination of VB1 and VB2, respectively. Using the squared correlation value (R2) and root mean square error of calibration (RMSECV), the optimal values for each component were determined (see Fig. 1S). The constructed model was used for the prediction of vitamins in synthetic mixtures. The predicted values and their RMSE values are given in Table 6. The RMSEP obtained at 1.1 and 0.90 shows the good ability of the method for simultaneous determination of vitamins in different binary mixtures. The correlation between the concentrations obtained by PLS and the real values was investigated. Figure 1S shows the obtained graphs using the predicted concentration of species with an R2 greater than 0.995, indicating that the model is highly accurate.

Conclusion

This research presenting a novel green method for creating and functionalizing nanostructures. The developed Ag@Ag2O-L-Try nanocomposite was utilized to construct a colorimetric sensor capable of simultaneous ultra-trace detection of VB1 and VB2. The sensor demonstrated excellent sensitivity, with 1.92 µmol L− 1 detection limits for VB1 and 0.048 µmol L− 1 detection limits for VB2. The linear detection ranges were 3.0 to 60.0 µmol L− 1 for VB1 and 3.0-350.0 µmol L− 1 for VB2, respectively. Furthermore, the sensor’s performance was greatly improved by the application of PLS regression, enabling precise and accurate measurement of both vitamins in a variety of samples. Also, the highly selectivity in the presence of common interfering substances has maintained accuracy and reliability of method. Overall, the Ag@Ag2O-L-Try nanocomposite sensor offering a cost-effective, eco-friendly, and highly efficient method for simultaneous vitamin detection, holds great promise for practical applications in pharmaceutical quality control and nutritional analysis.
