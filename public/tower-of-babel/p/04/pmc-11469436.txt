Exploring the complex information processes underlying plant behavior
Authors: A Novoplansky, GM Souza, ED Brenner, SC Bhatla, E Van Volkenburgh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNewly discovered plant behaviors, linked to historical observations, contemporary technologies, and emerging knowledge of signaling mechanisms, argue that plants utilize complex information processing systems. Plants are goal-oriented in an evolutionary and physiological sense; they demonstrate agency and learning. While most studies on plant plasticity, learning, and memory deal with the responsiveness of individual plants to resource availability and biotic stresses, adaptive information is often perceived from and coordinated with neighboring plants, while competition occurs for limited resources. Based on existing knowledge, technologies, and sustainability principles, climate-smart agricultural practices are now being adopted to enhance crop resilience and productivity. A deeper understanding of the dynamics of plant behavior offers a rich palette of potential amelioration strategies for improving the productivity and health of natural and agricultural ecosystems.KEYWORDS: Learning, memory, plant agency, plant behavior, plant communication

Plant behavior – a new field of inquiry

Plant Behavior is an emerging field of research arising from centuries-old observations of adaptive responsiveness and development in plants, invigorated by modern scientific methods. Over the past two decades, there has been a blossoming of reports on plant behavior in the scientific and lay press. Participation in this field is diverse, drawing on researchers from widely divergent fields and from around the globe. As we face rapid environmental degradation, climate change, human overpopulation, and challenges to our agricultural systems, it is crucial to incorporate diverse viewpoints to better understand plant behavior in natural and agricultural settings.

As a newly establishing field of research, Plant Behavior appears in the scientific literature as many initial but singlet reports describing plant behaviors. For a scientific topic as popular among the lay public as this one, these reports have fueled speculative essays and discussions about plant capabilities generated far afield from the science of plant biology. While all this activity could be useful in generating interest and funding of further research into how plants function, it is critical at this point to develop well-designed studies to test observations and hypotheses. Without scientifically based discussion, one-off reports of surprising plant behaviors will continue to be roundly criticized.

One problem that discourages some scientists from embracing this field as a legitimate realm within plant biology research is a lack of commonly accepted terminology to describe plant behaviors, their mechanisms, and their implications. The meanings of many terms being applied to plants need to be clarified: behavior, communication, sentience, cognition, intelligence, learning, memory, and agency. While these terms have been most used to describe human and animal traits, they can equally well apply to plants, as long as their meanings are clear and assumptions made evident. For example, learning is possible without dependence or presence of a central neural system (CNS), but often the presence of the latter is assumed, thus disqualifying plants from being able to learn.1 Another problem that needs to be addressed is the choice of experimental designs and protocols. Again, testing for traits in animals often relies on measuring movement (Box 1) or some neural responses. Such testing is not always pertinent to plant function. Plant-specific tests could be developed and used only if we intend to investigate plants without assuming them to be animal-like organisms. Finally, discussions of these traits as they pertain to plants have sometimes been dogmatic and exclusionary (e.g.1) Likely, discoveries of plant-specific ways of carrying out complex information processing and coordinating adaptive behaviors will be welcomed by biologists and philosophers working with animals and humans). As outliers among the investigated organisms, plants may shed light on questions and conundrums facing human understanding, such as the nature of decision-making, agency and intelligence.

Box 1: Movement in plantsMost frequently when asked to consider an organism’s behavior, scientists and laypersons alike think first of movement. From this perspective, plants are still and animals active. This view has contributed to our ignorance about how plants function, and the generalized state of plant blindness.18 Although plant behavior includes much more than movement, plants do move in subtle or striking ways. Over a century ago, Charles Darwin catalogued many of these behaviors in The Power of Movement in Plants.19 Most plant movements are slow relative to human perception. These include well-known growth movements such as photo- and gravi-tropism, leaf and petal unfolding, and nastic movements such as the twining of tendrils and vines. Bilaterally symmetrical flowers reorient their growth to achieve the ideal angle to attract pollinators.20 Generally, these movements are turgor-driven and regulated by hormone-mediated, cell wall-loosening which provides directionality. Many plant movements, however, are quite rapid. A striking example of behavior in flowers is the hammer orchid’s slamming its pollinia onto the back of a copulating wasp, enamored with its mimic flower. The confused wasp attempts to copulate with another flower thus ensuring out-crossing as pollen is transferred during the process. Famously, the Venus’ Fly Trap folds its leaf lobes to capture prey, and leaflets of the sensitive Mimosa fold upward when touched (both turgor-driven movements). Other rapid movements are based on mechanical energy stored in dried tissues, such as dispersal of spores and seeds flung out by the cracking open of drying pods and cones21 The Restless Plant).For research purposes, use of time-lapse photography is accelerating our understanding of plant movements. Notable are the popular website Plants in Motion developed in 2000 and spectacular films produced by Sir David Attenborough (e.g. The Green Planet, NOVA, 2022). For example, to ascertain directionality and possible agency, time-lapse photography is linked to data analysis systems to test hypotheses about vining.22–24 A recent study has demonstrated that thigmotropically induced stamen movement in Berberis julianae plants enhances insect flower visitation and increases pollination efficiency.25Analysis of movements, slow as they may be, in plants will provide many more unique behaviors for investigation. Aligning the movements with underlying physiological mechanisms may elucidate controls of plant behavior. However, plant behavior encompasses much more than movements.

What is plant behavior?

Consideration of behavior in plants requires an explanation of what we mean by behavior. We rely here on Arber’s2 notion that behavior is equivalent to acting, to put it simply: doing something. Silvertown and Gordon3 defined plant behavior as the response to signals, and this, along with Arber’s description, equates plant behavior with the phenomenon of phenotypic plasticity.4 Phenotypic plasticity can be broadly described by ‘norms of reactions’ as described by geneticists and ecologists, meaning the general responses of individual organisms to environmental conditions or stimuli.5–7 Plastic responsiveness is exhibited at a wide range of organizational hierarchies and at vastly variable spatiotemporal scales. These may be extremely rapid biochemical and physiological responses to local changes in growth conditions,8–10 all the way to greater, slower and longer-lasting developmental responses affecting resource allocation, organ- and whole-plant morphology and architecture.11 We therefore consider behavior synonymous to adaptive phenotypic plasticity.

While both plants and animals utilize various sensory capabilities and adapt with molecular and physiological responses, plant behavior stands out for its reliance on developmental plasticity. This includes indeterminate modular construction, ongoing organogenesis, and perpetual embryonic activity and totipotency. These unique features endow plants with remarkable diversity and extensive adaptive capabilities throughout most of their life cycle.

As plant behavior encompasses actions which depend on responses to meaningful and useful information, behavior is based on ‘processes’.12 Processes are sequences of interconnected actions that unfold over time. Hence, behavior is crucially time-dependent.13 Processes at different organizational scales exhibit a variety of rhythms and dynamics, from relatively regular circadian cycles to extremely complex and chaotic behaviors (examples see.14 The most well-understood rhythm in plants is the circadian clock cycle. This clock, governed by a genetic circuit,15 allows plants to anticipate and adapt to daily changes driven by light availability due to Earth’s rotation. These adaptations provide substantial biological advantage. A properly functioning circadian clock that aligns with the day-night cycle improves carbon fixation via photosynthesis, enabling faster growth in plants compared to those without a synchronized clock.16 On the other hand, irregular (chaotic-like) dynamics can confer more stability under changing environments. Plants exhibiting a more complex and unpredictable leaf gas exchange dynamics are more resilient after a drought stress period than plants showing a more regular CO2 assimilation dynamic.17 Therefore, understanding the emergence of complex plant behaviors is not restricted to the already enormous challenge of integrating different spatial scales, but also dynamic processes at various temporal scales and magnitudes. While science has developed powerful omics techniques for measuring networks of elements at different organizational hierarchies (genome, transcriptome, proteome, metabolome), the means for studying these networks over time as related to specific behaviors adds a much more challenging dimension of experimental and analytical complexity.13

How can plants have agency?

Common perception allows that plants might be functionally sophisticated, but they are merely reactive organisms without agency. The question of non-human, in this case plant agency, depends on one’s philosophical and epistemological perspective.26,27 According to classical Newtonian ontology, all observable phenomena can be reduced to the fate of passive objects and their movements. Inanimate objects can be repositioned, divided, or reshaped but remain unchanged unless acted upon by external forces. Identified by their form and position, properties of objects are intrinsic, absolute, and objective, unaffected by the presence of other objects or observers. The whole is merely the sum of its parts,28 and autonomous action and agency are prohibited unless a Cartesian body-mind dualism is assumed. However, such dualism challenges classical ontology, as the involvement of the mind contradicts pure object-based reasoning, implying that biological entities invariably lack agency.

Opposing the Newtonian ontology stands naturalistic metaphysics which states that biological entities are not merely self-assembled objects, such as crystals or snowflakes, in so far as they must constantly exchange energy and matter with their surroundings to stay far from thermodynamic equilibrium (death).29,30 In so doing, all biological entities, including plants, maintain homeostasis by integrating information from both their predetermined genetic program and their environment.28,31,32

Consistent with this worldview, a concept of biological agency has been defined as “the capacity of living systems at various levels to participate in their own development, maintenance, and function by regulating their structures and activities in response to conditions”.33,34 According to this definition, biological agency must not necessarily rely on a sophisticated CNS and can be readily found in non-human organisms such as plants and bacteria35–37 (see38 for a comprehensive review). Nevertheless, in traditional ontology, agency is only applicable to organisms with CNS capable of deliberate motility.39 Even if we assume such a restrictive sine qua non criterion for agency, the sedentary nature of plants does not prevent them from adaptive movement. In contrast to most animals, where development is carried out as part of an embryonically modeled plan according to standardized predetermined body plans, most plants are extremely plastic in their growth form, development, and shape. Utilizing their abilities to perceive and integrate a myriad of sensory inputs, perpetual juvenility, and open-ended modular form, plants maintain high levels of morphogenetical totipotency and adaptive plasticity. This allows them to regenerate readily following damage and continuously remodel their development and growth form while they actively and adaptively explore and exploit their heterogeneous environment in both time and space.40–43

Plant learning and behavior

While typically described in terms of organismal responsiveness to changing conditions, a larger view of plant behavior embraces organismal adaptive learning: the integration and propagation of information relevant to organismal performance in the service of genetic fitness.44

Though not in the eyes of Darwin himself but rather through the advocacy of his later proponents,45 neglect if not loathing of Lamarck’s concepts of evolution forced a dominant emphasis on natural selection due to gradual propagation of genetically encoded information via prior micro-evolutionary processes. Simple observations and even rudimentary experimentation with what we define today as gene-by-environment interactions were conducted ca. 2300 years ago by Theophrastus.46 Founders of quantitative genetics47 and their avid followers (e.g. Arthur48) marginalized the roles of sensory inputs and phenotypic responsiveness in evolutionary processes). This resulted in prolonged neglect of important concepts related to possible effects of lifetime experiences and development32,49,50 and of gene-environment interactions51,52 on evolutionary processes.

Despite a few attempts to revive the study of the effects of development on evolutionary processes, these lines of investigation remained neglected until recently.32,53 Among the early contributions to the field are the pioneering efforts of Ivan Schmalhausen54 to integrate developmental insights into evolutionary thinking, James Baldwin who introduced the concept of genetic accommodation,55 William Wheeler who discussed the “power of an organism to adapt action to requirement without the guidance of a hereditary method of adjustment”,56 and Conrad Waddington who described and experimentally demonstrated the groundbreaking concept of genetic assimilation.57 However, the superficial resemblance between these ideas and Lamarck’s theory tainted them in the eyes of many and kept them in the shadows for decades. These important concepts only returned into partial light following Bradshaw’s seminal paper on the significance of phenotypic plasticity,5 and its implications for not only the fate of individual organisms, but also for population genetics, community-level interactions and evolution.32,53,58–60

Spatiotemporal heterogeneity is one of the most fundamental attributes of any realm, a crucial challenge for biological existence, and the most significant driver of biological diversity.61–64 Even under hypothetically ideal environmental constancy, the mere physical presence of any object inherently and invariably creates a myriad of internal and external heterogeneities. This is especially true for developing systems, which experience these heterogeneities relevant to their own existence and performance at all organizational scales. These scales range from individual molecules, organelles, cells, and tissues to entire organisms, populations, and communities (e.g65,66) Interestingly, the common logical key element of all adaptive processes coping with heterogeneity can be directly attributed to one overarching process - learning. As there are different definitions of learning, we use here the one presented by Herbert Simon:67

… learning is any change in a system that allows it to perform better the second time on repetition of the same task … (p. 28).

though noting that the learned response could improve performance on repetition of similar, not merely the same, tasks.68 In addition, it is important to emphasize that regardless of the involved organism and specific mechanisms, learning is essentially relevant only in combination with memory (see below).

Learning is the foundation for behavior

Learning is a hallmark of biological existence at all scales and hierarchical levels. In its essence, all biological adaptations invariably rely on incorporation (i.e., learning) and retention (i.e., memory) of functionally relevant information. At the population level, information is acquired, filtered, and genetically stored as allelic frequencies and transferred via evolutionary processes without direct involvement or awareness of the affected organisms.69,70 However, the extent to which information garnered through evolutionary processes is relevant and potentially beneficial is limited by the range of past experiences and the probability of their reoccurrence in subsequent generations.

At fine-grained spatiotemporal scales relevant to specific individuals,61 adaptive responses are based on the organism’s abilities to perceive and respond to various challenges and opportunities, commonly referred to as phenotypic plasticity.5,59 Where autocorrelation between earlier and later life events is sufficiently tight, relevant information is readily learned by individual organisms. In these instances, information is assimilated and stored as plastic adaptations, and when adaptively appropriate can be readily transmitted via various maternal effects71,72 including various epigenetic processes.73 Such scenarios include elevated readiness, also referred to as priming, to variable challenges (e.g.74) In psychology, this type of learning is formally defined as sensitization via receptor modulation.75 Where primed organisms are resubjected to the learned challenge, they respond more rapidly and drastically, which could markedly increase their survival probability and fitness under challenging conditions74 Kessler and Mueller 2024). For example, repeated administration of various psychostimulants such as methamphetamine may lead to an increased sensitivity to these drugs.76 Alternatively, where long-term challenges are perceived, acclimation,77 or habituation78 are selected. In psychology, these processes are defined as desensitization or habituation.79 Importantly, such simple non-associative learning processes are ubiquitous among all life forms, regardless of their possession of a central nervous system (CNS), with detailed examples readily available in plants.80–86

As learning via genetic change or plastic responses typically requires substantial time, adaptive information must be relevant to the anticipated conditions under which the organism will eventually function.87,88 This consideration is ubiquitously crucial for any decision-making system, especially where the involved parties are engaged in strategic games such as competition, predation, or pathogen attack, where optimal responses are tightly dependent on the actions of counterpart players (e.g.89)

Different learning types and modes have various costs, rendering them adaptively viable only under particular circumstances. One of the important costs results from mismatches between the modified phenotype and the real-time conditions in which it functions. Both the products of intergenerational genetic differentiation and intragenerational phenotypic plasticity are based on past experiences, while selection maximizes future performance. The differences between genetic-deterministic and plastic-behavioral learning scenarios lie in the temporal resolution of response: lifetime intragenerational vs. intergenerational experiences. It can thus be inferred that both the potential gains from tight matchings and the potential costs of mismatches are expected to be significantly greater for evolutionary genetic learning than for phenotypically plastic learning.

Accordingly, instead of questioning whether plants can learn, a phenomenon well demonstrated in many taxa and across numerous organizational levels (for review see90) our attention should be directed toward the distinctive aspects of plant learning. How is plant learning different from other forms of biological learning? How can it be relevant and useful for understanding learning processes and implications in other CNS and non-CNS systems, or for the development of artificial learning systems?

While most studies on plant plasticity, learning and memory deal with responsiveness to resource availability and biotic stresses (e.g,91) adaptive information is often perceived from and coordinated with neighboring plants).92 For example, undamaged or unstressed plants rapidly respond to cueing from their herbivore-inflicted or drought-stressed neighbors.92 In some cases, cueing is further relayed through multiple unstressed plants, eliciting adaptive responses in distant yet unaffected plants in preparation for a probable forthcoming challenge74 Falik et al.93,94 Other studies demonstrated that interplant communication allows plants to increase flowering synchrony with their neighbors,95 and adaptively to modify flower type in response to the presence and density of potential reproductive partners.96

These examples demonstrate the possibility of social networking and learning in plants. More generally, they show that adaptive behavior, decision-making, and intelligence often rely on and emerge from communication among individuals, regardless of their neuronal capacity. Excellent examples of such systems have been shown in the movement patterning of bird flocks,97 fish schools,98 migrating locust swarms,99 ants,100 and very recently in plants.101 In these cases, tightly coordinated adaptive behaviors are based on individuals’ perception of other group members leading to an emergent collective behavior and intelligence of the group, regardless of the self-centered opportunistic motivations of its members (e.g.98,102).

Most of the few studies of collective decision-making in plants have been limited to cells or organs of the same individual (e.g.103) In one case, roots have been demonstrated mutually to affect the growth direction of their neighbors, leading to coordinated growth patterns resembling swarming behavior).104 However, plants can perceive and utilize valuable adaptive information from their neighbors even while competing with them for limited resources.83,94,95 Interestingly and importantly, the same general processes underlying collective interplant behaviors are responsible for learning and developmental plasticity in individual plants. While plant form and architecture are genetically determined, the fine details of organ development and positioning are governed by ontogenetically self-organizational processes relying on both direct and indirect interactions between organs of the same plant.105,106 Specifically, the fate of any given organ is dictated by its own vigor relative to the performance of other similar (redundant) organs on the same plant such as described above.107

Through reciprocal polar signaling of auxin and cytokinin, and somatic competition over the formation of new vascular strands, plants can allocate their limited resources to their most successful organs.106,108,109 As most plants, especially long-lived trees, develop a large excess of redundant organs, such somatic competition ensures that limiting resources are preferentially allocated to the most successful organs, typically those that are situated in the richest110 or most promising;84,111,112 resource patches. Importantly, these competitive processes help plants continuously shape their form and architecture according to changing conditions in ways that maximize their performance and fitness. This is based on integrated decisions made via the exchange of information and emergent collective learning and behaviors. This occurs regardless of the specific absolute sensory inputs and developmental experiences of each of their individual module and organs.113,114

Defining signals underlying plant behavior

Despite the human tendency to look for a central nervous system or ‘brain’ in organisms with agency, a central integrator of plant behavior has not yet been empirically defined. Localized and long-distance signaling systems have been intensively investigated but a whole plant signaling regulator remains obscure. Furthermore, few investigators have sought to find such an entity, let alone postulate its presence. At present, signaling in plants at the modular levels mostly appears as a cacophony of transmissions with increasing intensity, although our understanding of how these signals is coordinated is still rather particulated. In the absence of a recognizable central integrator, it is worth examining signals at the level of modules.

The multiscale modular organization of plants results in behaviors in response to the environment that may differ among organs (roots, shoots, leaves) or even among similar organs such as leaves positioned on different branches of the same plant, and across scales.42 The relationships between the different scales of organization appear to be non-linear, making it extremely difficult to determine the behavior of one scale in relation to another. This poses an enormous challenge to deterministic models that assume direct (linear) causal relationships between, for example, gene expression and physiological responses.115–117 This becomes even more difficult when plants face dynamic environments that are constantly changing.118

Much of this comes down to ascertaining the network of plumbing and wiring and sometimes “smoke signals” (volatile compounds) operating in and around plants. In plants, signals diffuse cell-to-cell and travel via bulk flow through vascular tissues. These signals include phytohormones and metabolites such as small proteins, sugars, amino acids, microRNA, among others. Other signals are airborne including organic volatile compounds (e.g119,120) Hydraulic cues, which include positive and negative pressure and shock waves, also play an important role, especially over long distances (e.g121–123). Bioelectric signals including action potentials, slow-wave potentials, and the electrome124–127 have regained attention because of their potential role in integrating physiological responses quickly throughout the entire plant body.

Recent literature suggests that in plants, pathways responding to systemic signals such as calcium, hydraulic, electric, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) are interconnected.128 Calcium and ROS signals are thought to enhance each other, with calcium affecting ROS production and ROS influencing the opening and closing of calcium channels. These signals might be linked with calcium-modulated electric signals. Additionally, mechanical sensors on calcium channels may connect hydraulic and calcium signals, although direct evidence is lacking.129 Other interactions involve RBOHs (Respiratory Burst Oxidase Homologs) which could influence pH and membrane potential, and thus affect electric and pH signals, as well as calcium signals through the regulation of specific enzymes. This network of pathways could enable plants to respond specifically to various stresses by coordinating systemic responses from the stress site to other parts of the plant.130–132 Crosstalk of local and systemic signals is also linked to phytohormone levels regulating local responses to stresses.133

A variety of environmental cues induce electrical signals throughout the symplast and/or apoplast, allowing local to long-distance communication channeling different signals in the plant body.124,134 To date, different kinds of electrical signal propagation have been reported in the plant realm: membrane or cell surface potentials, action potentials (APs), slow wave potentials (SWPs) and local electric potentials (LEPs), all of which are electrical activities involving ion (Ca+2, K+, Cl−, H+) imbalances across membranes. When occurring at the plasma membrane, signal-induced fluxes may lead to a voltage transient with a phase of fast depolarization of the cell membrane followed by a phase with variable rates of repolarization, depending on the electrical signal induced.135–137 Alternatively,138 describe a systemic potential (SP) induced after leaf wounding, characterized by hyperpolarization of the apoplast instead of a depolarization. Underlying these induced electrical signals, a non-evoked low voltage baseline electrical activity has been recorded by electrophytograms (EPG) at the level of plant tissues.139,140 Such complexity has inspired the concept of the “plant electrome”125 based on the “self-generated electrome” by De Loof.141 This concept describes the entirety of ionic dynamics across all scales of plant organization engendering a constant electrical activity. In plants, the electrome provides an apparent low-voltage electrical background which, upon external stimulation, generates spike-like events following a self-organized power-law distribution. Patterns of emerging events can be correlated to the whole plant physiological status.125,142

From knowledge to knowhow

A nontrivial yet highly valuable challenge is utilizing knowledge of plant behavior to improve applied practices.143 While a comprehensive discussion of the involved factors, players, and mechanisms belongs elsewhere (e.g144,145) here we attempt to codify a few general principles that could help harness the science of plant behavior to improve practices and decrease environmental impacts in agriculture.

Early yet not so humble beginnings

Since the earliest days of plant domestication, the selection of high-yielding plants inadvertently relied on both the genetic background and the responsiveness of selected plants to specific environments and growth conditions (Theophrastus146–148). Various plastic responses were readily utilized in ancient times to increase yields by manipulating plant development, morphology and architecture. Examples include soil preparation by manure fertilization as early as 12,000 years before present (YBP)149 and hoeing and plowing (Egypt, 4400–3900 YBP).150 Early utilization of plant regeneration properties included asexual propagation by bulb division (Babylon 5000 YBP);151 and air layering (China 4000 YBP). Cross-taxon grafting was practiced in the Mediterranean region and China starting 3000–4000 YBP.152 Increasing light perception by vine training was utilized in Egypt 2900 YBP,153 and girdling was used to improve fruit quality and yields in China 3000 YBP.154 Inducing plant miniaturization via repeated pruning and defoliation (penjing) was practiced in China 2500–3000 YBP,155 and increased fruit size by wounding is documented in the old testament (Eastern Mediterranean; 2800 YBP; Amos 7:14 (OT)156); but is known to have been practiced in Egypt even before that.157 Although none of these ancient practices relied on mechanistic knowledge of plant physiology or evolution, they demonstrate efficient utilization of both artificial selection and plant behavioral responsiveness for agricultural gains.

Among the early translations of modern plant science into agricultural practices was the discovery of photoperiodism.158 The simplicity of the phenomenological responses allowed a direct implementation of night interruption in horticultural systems where flowering timing was of commercial importance (for reviews see 159,160). The later discovery of phytochrome161,162 and its relevance to anticipatory plant responses to imminent light competition163 opened new possibilities for increasing plant performance and yields by manipulating R/FR ratios in agricultural settings. For example, the perception of end-of-day R/FR by field-grown soybean plants was significantly affected by the azimuth of the planted rows, which in turn affected both root-to-shoot resource allocation and yields.164 In another early study, manipulation of R/FR ratio in greenhouses was achieved by incorporating fluorescent dyes that converted some of the blue-green part of the spectrum into red light and increased R/FR, which in turn significantly increased fruit yields in tomatoes and flower production in roses.165

Nature vs. agriculture: potential gains from deceiving plants

The above examples demonstrate an important and highly useful aspect of information about plant responsiveness to environmental conditions. As virtually all adaptive responses are only relevant in the context of forthcoming rather than current conditions, plants have been naturally selected to respond to signals and cues sufficiently correlated with anticipated conditions (see Learning segment). This implies that external cueing could potentially elicit agriculturally desirable responses even when in partial contrast to prevailing growth conditions. For example, because competition for light is a critical determinant of plant performance and fitness, natural sun-loving plants are inevitably engaged in a competitive arms race whereby the only evolutionary stable strategy is to maximize shade avoidance and competitive responses (AKA shade-avoidance syndrome; SAS).166 This is accomplished via ever-increasing responsiveness to R/FR and other proximity cues at a dear economic cost of decreased allocation to agriculturally important roots, flowers, and fruits.167 Another revealing example of an inherent reliance on ‘pure information’ is demonstrated by reliance on hormone signaling. Slight changes in hormone levels and fluxes are ubiquitously utilized to communicate the states and dynamics of various physiological processes across long distances within the plant. This critical communication relies on tight correlations between the actual functional states and their hormonal proxies.113 These examples highlight the potential to elicit agriculturally desirable, developmental responses via non-resource cueing. Interestingly, this very concept has been utilized by various parasitic plants such as Cuscuta, Orobanche, and Striga168 and gall-forming insects for tens of millions of years. By hijacking their host’s signaling systems, these parasites deceive the hosts into diverting precious resources to their own benefit at obvious costs.169,170 Evolutionary considerations imply that such host-parasite systems, including agriculture in which humans are the parasites, can sustain under the radar of natural selection for many generations if it is sufficiently rare or its costs to the host are low.171

Prospective opportunities

Before discussing specific approaches, a general logical reservation should be discussed: it is conceivable that over millennia of domestication, the best possible genotypes have already been inadvertently selected for optimizing plant performance and maximizing yield production, thus leaving little or no margin for improvement by further tweaking of plant sensory modes and responsiveness. While this critical notion is logically valid, the above examples support the idea that there is still a significant potential for improvement for the following reasons: a) growth conditions, productivity intensity, and plant protection are vastly improved in modern agricultural systems, thus allowing for selective relaxation of important adaptations related to nutrition, competition, herbivory, pathogens, and other stressors; b) plants utilize multiple sensory and response modalities that are often redundant, inter-compensatory and epistatic; c) though operating for thousands of generations, artificial selection has been significantly short compared to natural selection.146 Accordingly, it is suggested that traditional artificial selection for agriculturally valuable traits still leaves significant potential for modern approaches to further increase plant productivity and decrease production costs and environmental impacts via the optimization of plant responsiveness.

Phenotypic versus genetic manipulations

Studies of plant behavior are closely aligned with the principles of modern agricultural practices, such as agroecology172 and permaculture.173 These practices could prioritize the optimization of specific crop taxa and environmental information, rather than simply maximizing plant production.174 Exogenous morphogenetical manipulations of plants in agricultural settings could rely on the elevated sensitivity of plants to exogenous non-resource cueing correlated with critical anticipated growth limitations such as light competition (e.g,175) pathogen attacks (e.g.176) or suitable time of reproduction. Practical protocols involve active manipulation of incoming spectral light composition, and photoperiod,177 which in intensive agricultural systems can be precisely controlled using LED lighting.178,179 Exogenous applications of plant hormones allow emulating endogenous signaling. Examples include GA applications to elicit stem elongation or flowering, ABA applications to induce osmotic or drought resistance, and various other metabolites to induce defenses against pathogens and herbivores (e.g180,181). A highly viable alternative or complementary approach is the selection and engineering of genotypes tailored for agriculturally desirable responsiveness, increased efficiency and optimized responses to multiple stressors under specific growth conditions (e.g.182,183) For example, as SAS involves increased resource allocation to competitive structures and increased canopy spacing at the expense of the production of commercially important organs such as roots, flowers, and fruits, the selection for and genetic manipulations of phyB inactivation could lead to increased agricultural yields.184,185 However, such ventures should be pursued with caution as drastic suppression of competitive responses could be accompanied by reduced ability to self-organize, resulting in suboptimal spacing and costly asymmetrical competition for light186,187,188).

No free meals: costs, limits and tradeoffs

One of the main challenges of maintaining or increasing agricultural production is related to the costs and limitations imposed by various tradeoffs on plant performance and yields.189–191 Phenotypic plasticity is an effective strategy for coping with variable conditions,192 a major facilitator of endurance and performance under suboptimal conditions (e.g193,194) and a vital bridge across genetically determined adaptive valleys.32,53,55,57 However, its adaptive value is strictly bounded by costs and limitations.195–197 In the context of plant behavior, the adaptive value of any response, and in agriculture – the implications of individual plant responses to the cumulative performance of entire stands – must be considered in the multi-dimensional contexts of its effects on alternative functions, environmental conditions, and developmental stages.198–200 Besides well-documented resource allocation tradeoffs between different functions, such as roots vs. shoots,201 vegetative vs. reproductive organs,202 seed size and numbers,203 and growth vs. defense,204,205 the adaptive value of behavioral decisions critically depends on multidimensional functional tradeoffs produced by intricate interactions of the plant’s genetic background, developmental history, and anticipated environmental challenges.

While behavioral traits could relax phenotypic constraints dictated by genetic linkage, pleiotropic and epistatic interactions,32,50,53 the evolution and adaptive value of these traits could in itself be limited by the very same genetic interactions.206 An important aspect of these limitations is the inherent genetic tendency of plants to prioritize their responsiveness according to their selective history in the wild, which might not coincide with the maximization of whole-stand production in agricultural systems. For example, SAS responses have been demonstrated to come at reduced defense against herbivory and pathogens, which might significantly limit plant protection and stand production in agricultural systems.183

Behavioral responses are significantly affected by earlier experiences and plastic moves.107 As noted above, the perception of forthcoming events allows for anticipative priming to forthcoming challenges such as herbivory,81 competition,167 and nutrient limitations,207 but these very responses may turn maladaptive and costly if conditions change.208 For example, early priming to anticipated drought may increase plant survival during a subsequent drought but lowered plant growth under eventual benign conditions.93,94 It is expected that increasingly larger mismatches between anticipatory behavioral responses and later growth conditions could develop if early behavioral moves limit later changes (e.g,107,209) or carried epigenetically across multiple generations during which the environmental conditions change.210 In this context, it is important to mention that anticipatory priming in response to early cues of forthcoming challenges are significantly less costly than full-blown responses to stressful conditions211 and might be utilized in cautionary protocols in agriculture when the probabilities of forthcoming stressful conditions and uncertain.212,213

These considerations imply that although integrated omics approaches and systems biology may allow deciphering key aspects of trait connectivity, interactions, and tradeoffs, reaching optimality in plant responsiveness to the ever-moving target of environmental challenges and character interactions will be a formidable challenge.200 It is predicted that future investigations into the intricate interactions between plant behaviors and environmental conditions will help reduce wasteful resource and time allocation to non-yield functions, increase plant resistance to various enemies at minimal performance costs, and further relax naturally selected traits that are still limiting plant performance in agricultural systems. In the context of plant behavior and learning, special emphasis should be placed on the development of genetic varieties and external cueing protocols that would allow plants to unlearn – remove memories of past experiences (e.g. epigenetic markers of previous stresses), and by doing so, rejuvenate their behavioral plasticity to the prevailing growth conditions.214

Conclusions

Plant Behavior as a new field of research in plant biology is exciting scientists, students, and lay persons alike to think again about how plants work. A significant contribution of the field is integration of levels of investigation and the development of new perspectives, paradigms and methods of investigation into functions hitherto not well recognized in plants including agency and learning.

Analysis of whole plant function that considers 3-D processes over time within and between plants at the physiological, genomic and electromic levels will eventually reveal how plants perform complex behaviors. Information gleaned by deciphering the interrelatedness of signaling pathways in plant behavioral responses could eventually be used to enhance crop resilience to risks of drought, pests, disease, and climate. Advances in agriculture with AI-driven and machine learning enhancements are currently being used to monitor pH, nutrient levels, temperature, and the amount and quality of light being received by plants in both experimental and agricultural systems.215 Farmers can monitor plants by precise location, focusing chemical treatment and thus limiting pollution and detrimental side effects of imprecise fertilization and pest control. Potentially, technological advances will be added based on recognized plant behaviors to environmental cues, both above and below-ground. Development of pure biochemicals and nanoparticles used as biostimulants could mitigate the effects of abiotic stress on plants via activation of mechanisms for reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging, osmolyte regulation and chlorophyll accumulation,216 and for disease control. Monitoring plant behavior to determine needed agricultural interventions could include using other new technologies – the Internet of Things (IoT)-based sensors and drone-based spectral images to generate real-time data on the soil and physiological status of target crops. These new technologies coupled with an intimate sense of plant processes in their own time over space will lead to the next revolution in both environmental and agronomic interventions.183

Plant Behavior researchers have borrowed and adapted concepts, questions, and challenges from human psychology, animal behavior, philosophy, economics, and more. At this juncture, insights from the study of plants are becoming thorough enough that they could fertilize the studies of decision-making, intelligence, and learning (see Box 2 for some examples). As molecular genetics in model plants has spurred new discoveries in human medicine, so might new discoveries in Plant Behavior do the same.

Box 2: What’s next?The value and beauty of the field of plant behavior rely on the delicate yet powerful meetings of minds, approaches, and methods from all walks of biological investigation. Mixed in are philosophy and even more so, old texts from the earliest of plant scientists, indigenous or trained, to the most complex, new, technologically revealed findings. This is because the field is evolving through an intersection of widely ranging ideas, findings, and epistemological interactions and syntheses. Accordingly, pointing out ‘required’ or ‘urgent’ areas of investigation could be presumptuous and counterproductive as science proceeds at its own pace. Instead, we present a few examples of currently evolving fronts that, in our opinion, could yield meaningful advances not only for deepening understanding of plant biology but also agricultural and conservation endeavors. Plant learning and memory: the study of plants’ abilities to integrate and recall adaptive information is in its preliminary stage but could profoundly change the ways we grasp biological adaptation. The nature of the field implies the employment of novel approaches adapted from but also potentially insightful for the fields of epistemology, information and set theories, neurology, psychology, theoretical physics, and machine learning.
