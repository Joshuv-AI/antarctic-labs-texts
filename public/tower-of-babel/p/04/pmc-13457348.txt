Differential presence of cerebral microbleeds in an amyloid mouse model compared with a CADASIL mouse model
Authors: Xiuli Yang, Yuguo Li, Adnan Bibic, Jiekang Wang, Mei Wan, Wenzhen Duan, Hanzhang Lu, Zhiliang Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTINTRODUCTIONCerebral microbleeds are commonly observed on susceptibility‐based magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in Alzheimer's disease (AD) and are often interpreted as markers of small‐vessel disease. However, how microbleed occurrence differs between amyloid‐associated vascular pathology and non‐amyloid vascular conditions remains incompletely understood. In this study, we performed a comparative analysis between an amyloidosis mouse model and a non‐amyloid arteriopathy cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) model to assess whether microbleed occurrence differs across these distinct vascular contexts.METHODSMultimodal in vivo MRI at 11.7T, including gradient echo (GRE), spin echo, and diffusion‐weighted imaging, was performed in 5xFAD mice and a CADASIL mouse model across 9 to 20 months of age. Ex vivo high‐resolution GRE imaging and Prussian blue staining were used to validate microbleeds. Quantitative analyses focused on microbleed counts, parenchymal and cerebrospinal fluid volumes, and regional apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC). In addition, blood‐brain barrier (BBB) integrity was assessed in the CADASIL mice.RESULTSBoth in vivo and ex vivo GRE MRI consistently revealed hippocampal microbleeds in 5xFAD mice, whereas no microbleeds were detected in CADASIL mice at any examined age. Microbleeds in 5xFAD mice occurred in the absence of brain atrophy or ventricular enlargement. ADC elevation was observed selectively in the midbrain of 5xFAD mice but not in other regions or in the CADASIL cohort. BBB permeability remained normal in CADASIL mice, indicating preserved vascular barrier integrity despite vascular smooth muscle cell loss.DISCUSSIONCerebral microbleeds emerged selectively in the amyloid model and were absent in a non‐amyloid arteriopathy driven by vascular smooth muscle cell degeneration, suggesting that microbleed occurrence may depend on the underlying pathology. These findings show consistency with the possibility that cerebral microbleeds may reflect disease‐associated vascular conditions, refining their interpretation as translational MRI biomarkers in AD.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, blood‐brain barrier, brain atrophy, microbleed, small‐vessel disease, susceptibility‐based imaging, ventricular enlargement

BACKGROUND

Microbleeds are increasingly recognized as a neuroimaging hallmark of cerebrovascular injury and can be sensitively detected using T2 *‐weighted or susceptibility‐weighted MRI. 1 , 2 In patients with Alzheimer's disease (AD), microbleed burden correlates with accelerated cognitive decline, disruption of neurovascular integrity, and increased risk of spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage, 3 underscoring their role as an imaging indicator of amyloid‐associated vascular fragility. Furthermore, microbleeds frequently co‐occur with parenchymal amyloid deposition, 4 neuroinflammation, 5 ventricular enlargement, 6 white matter hyperintensities, 7 and blood‐brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction, 8 linking them to broader neurovascular pathology rather than isolated hemorrhagic foci. Although cerebral microbleeds are frequently observed in AD, they are also reported in other conditions such as hypertensive arteriopathy and traumatic brain injury, 9 , 10 indicating that they are not specific to amyloid pathology.

Cerebral small‐vessel disease (SVD) represents a major contributor to dementia syndromes and encompasses diverse non‐amyloid vasculopathies, including hypertensive arteriopathy, cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL), and other inherited or sporadic vascular pathologies. 11 , 12 , 13 CADASIL is a hereditary SVD caused by mutations in the NOTCH3 gene (typically cysteine residues within the epidermal growth factor [EGF]‐like repeats), and the position of the mutation is correlated to the severity of disease progression. 14 , 15 These mutations lead to the accumulation of NOTCH3 receptor extracellular domains and the accumulation of granular osmophilic materials (GOMs) in the vasculature, especially in the cerebral vasculature, which is not consistently accompanied by loss of function of the NOTCH3 gene. 16 , 17 The relative contributions of loss‐of‐function versus toxic gain‐of‐function effects to pathological manifestation are still a matter of debate. There is a consensus on the neuroimaging presentation of CADASIL patients, including white matter hyperintensities and lacunar infarcts, for example. At present, a handful of mouse models have been developed to model different aspects of CADASIL by inserting a humanized NOTCH3 gene with different mutations in the cysteine rich EGF‐like repeats. 18 Existing CADASIL models recapitulate key aspects of the disease but may not fully represent its full complexity. 16

In the literature, a diverse range of animal models has been developed to study AD and SVD pathologies. 19 , 20 , 21 In this study, a 5xFAD model was used because it robustly recapitulates amyloid pathology and provides a relevant experimental context for examining microbleeds in association with amyloid‐related pathology. CADASIL was selected as a genetically defined model of non‐amyloid SVD, enabling comparison with amyloid‐associated vascular pathology under controlled experimental conditions. We performed a controlled comparison between 5xFAD mice and a CADASIL mouse model to assess whether microbleed occurrence differed across these distinct vascular contexts. 5xFAD mice were evaluated at 9 to 12 months, an age range characterized by established amyloid deposition 22 and emerging vascular injury, with microbleeds verified using histology and ex vivo MRI. CADASIL mice, modeling progressive non‐amyloid arteriopathy, were examined at 9, 13, and 20 months to capture potential age‐dependent vascular changes.

Experimental protocols for this study were approved by the Johns Hopkins Medical Institution Animal Care and Use Committee and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. Data reporting complied with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) 2.0 guidelines. All procedures were carefully designed to minimize discomfort and stress to the animals. Mice were housed in a quiet environment under a 12‐h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. A total of 81 mice (age: 9 to 20 months; body weight: 20 to 58 grams; 42 females [F], 39 males [M]) were investigated in this study. A 5xFAD model 22 (age: 9 to 12 months; n = 28; 16 F/12 M) were examined with multimodal MRI. The 5xFAD line (Jackson Laboratory, Stock No. 034848) was maintained on a congenic C57BL/6J background, and age‐ and sex‐matched C57BL/6J littermates served as controls. A CADASIL model 15 (age: 9/13/20 months; n = 53; 26F/27 M), a transgenic form of SVD, was examined for comparisons. CADASIL mice were obtained by cross‐breeding the 129‐Gt(ROSA)26Sortm2(NOTCH3*C455R)Sat/Mmjax line (Mutant Mouse Resource and Research Center, Stock No. 033000‐JAX; maintained on a 129S genetic background) with the SM22‐Cre driver line (Jackson Laboratory, Stock No. 017491; maintained on a C57BL/6J genetic background), carrying a mixed 129S/C57BL/6J background. 15 The CADASIL model was based on a conditional ROSA26 knock‐in of human NOTCH3 C455R and was not bred on an endogenous Notch3 knockout background; endogenous mouse Notch3 expression was preserved. This CADASIL line results in vascular smooth muscle cell‐specific expression of the NOTCH3 C455R mutation following Cre‐mediated excision of a LoxP‐flanked STOP cassette, as previously described. 15 Control mice consisted of age‐ and sex‐matched littermates carrying the SM22α‐Cre transgene but lacking the NOTCH3 C455R allele, thereby controlling for potential vascular effects associated with the Cre expression. Information of mouse numbers was detailed in Table S1. Experimental order was randomized across mice referencing to a reported scheme. 23

MRI experiments

An 11.7T Bruker Biospec system (Bruker, Ettlingen, Germany) with a horizontal bore and actively shielded pulsed‐field gradient (maximal intensity: 0.74T/m) was used for imaging. Data were acquired using a 72‐mm quadrature volume resonator as the transmitter and a four‐element (2 × 2) phased‐array coil as the receiver. The basic experimental setup followed that in previous reports. 24 , 25 Inhalational isoflurane was used as the anesthetic. 26 Isoflurane doses were carefully controlled and maintained consistently across animals, and all comparisons were performed under the consistent conditions. Experiments were terminated if respiration rates dropped below 50 breaths/min for more than 2 min.

Figure 1 illustrates our experimental design. Gradient echo (GRE) MRI was performed to assess microbleeds, while spin‐echo (SE) MRI was used to evaluate hyperintensities, parenchymal tissue volume, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) volume. 27 Diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI) was applied to examine microstructural tissue properties through the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC). 28 Experimental parameters are detailed in Table S2.

Schematic diagram of the study design.

All 5xFAD mice underwent in vivo GRE and SE scans, and brains from a subgroup (5 [1 F/4 M] WT, 5 [3 F/2 M] 5xFAD) were extracted for ex vivo GRE imaging. CADASIL mice received in vivo GRE scans; in its subgroup (20 months; 4 [2 F/2 M] WT, 8 [4 F/4 M] CADASIL), the in vivo GRE protocol for the 5xFAD model was used. GRE data of CADASIL mice were collected over an extended period during which imaging protocols underwent iterative refinement; consequently, certain acquisition parameters differed across age groups. In CADASIL mice, water‐extraction‐with‐phase‐contrast‐arterial‐spin‐tagging (WEPCAST) MRI was applied to measure permeability‐surface area product (PS), a marker for BBB integrity, following a reported protocol. 25 In addition, a reported phase‐contrast protocol 29 was used to estimate cerebral blood flow (CBF). Although isoflurane affected CBF, PS estimation was relatively robust to such variations, as increases in CBF would be accompanied by reductions in extraction fraction, thereby mitigating their combined effect on PS. 25 DWI scans 28 were performed in both 5xFAD and CADASIL models.

Histology and transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Preparation and analysis of brain sections followed our previous reports. 28 , 30 An iron stain kit (Catalog No.: ab150674; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), also known as Prussian blue stain, was used to validate the cerebral microbleed. 31 Staining procedures were performed in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Brain sections of an intracerebral hemorrhagic mouse from a separate study 32 were used as a positive reference.

Immunostaining was performed to visualize vessels (collagen IV staining), vascular smooth muscle cells (α‐smooth muscle actin [α‐SMA] staining), and amyloid plaque (6E10 staining). Primary antibodies included collagen IV (1:500, 2150–1470, Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), α‐SMA (1:250, C6198, MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA), and 6E10 (1:500, 803001, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). Fluorescently labeled samples were imaged using a Zeiss LSM 780 FCS confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Representative images were newly acquired for illustration purposes, whereas quantitative analyses were derived through re‐analysis of a previously established dataset from our prior study 24 using the same animal models.

TEM was performed to examine the presence of GOMs, a characteristic structural hallmark of CADASIL. Brain tissues were fixed and processed following standard protocols, and ultrathin sections (60 to 70 nm) were imaged to identify GOMs with a Thermo Fisher Scientific Talos L120C.

Data processing

All data processing was performed using custom MATLAB scripts (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). GRE images were visually inspected by two raters with discussion to identify hypointense spots considered microbleeds when their sizes exceeded two pixels. SE images were used to estimate CSF volume and parenchymal tissue volume following a reported protocol. 27 DWI processing followed reported procedures on a region‐of‐interest (ROI) basis.

Statistical analyses

Group‐wise comparisons were examined with Student's t‐test. Statistical details including t‐statistics, degrees of freedom, and p values were reported. The sex effect was evaluated using a linear regression model.

Linear mixed‐effects models were used to examine the genotype, age, sex, and genotype × age interaction on water extraction fraction, PS, and regional ADC of CADASIL mice. Effect estimate (denoted by β), 95% confidence interval (CI), and p value were reported.

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical details of analyses are presented in Table S3.

RESULTS

Microbleed arose in 5xFAD but not in CADASIL mice

Representative GRE images of WT and 5xFAD mice illustrate the typical image quality obtained (Figure 2A). Microbleeds were observed in the hippocampus of both female and male 5xFAD mice (Figure 2A). At the group level, 5xFAD mice exhibited a higher microbleed count than WT controls (p < 0.001; Figure 2B). No sex‐dependent difference was detected (p = 0.737). A small number of hypointense spots were occasionally observed in WT mice, which may reflect background vascular events associated with aging. 33 To validate the identification of microbleeds, Prussian blue staining was performed in representative mice. Given that MRI‐based detection was the primary outcome, histological staining was performed for confirmation rather than systematic quantification across all animals. Positive staining was observed around the dentate gyrus region of the hippocampus in 5xFAD mice (Figure 2C), consistent with MRI findings. Moreover, positive staining was predominantly observed in the hippocampus, with no consistent additional staining identified in other brain regions. No positive staining was found in matched WT controls (Figure 2C). Additionally, brain sections from an intracerebral hemorrhagic stroke mouse model 32 showed positive staining surrounding the hemorrhagic area (Figure S1), confirming the validity of staining procedure.

The ex vivo mouse brains were extracted and incubated in fomblin for imaging. Consequently, susceptibility‐discontinuity‐induced image distortion was reduced (Figure 3A). Slice‐by‐slice inspection confirmed good agreement in the identification of microhemorrhagic spots between in vivo and ex vivo datasets (Figure 3A). Owing to the enhanced through‐plane resolution, microbleed spots were better captured in the ex vivo GRE images. Microbleed counts from in vivo and ex vivo GRE were strongly correlated (p < 0.001; Figure 3B). Referencing to the ex vivo data, 5xFAD mice exhibited higher microbleed counts (p = 0.002; Figure 3C).

The CADASIL model was validated by the presence of GOMs on TEM (Figure S2). We examined the GRE images of WT and CADASIL mice at 9, 13, and 20 months of age and consistently found no hypointense spots. Representative images were displayed in Figure 4A. While no microbleeds were detected in CADASIL mice, we cannot exclude the presence of lesions below the detection threshold or at later stages not captured. Differences in genetic background (C57BL/6J vs 129S/C57BL/6J) may partly account for the presence of microbleeds in 5xFAD controls but not in CADASIL controls.

Reliable WEPCAST signals at the vein of Galen were achieved in the CADASIL model across all time points as marked by arrows (Figure 4B). Water extraction fraction exhibited an age effect (p = 0.028) but not a genotype (p = 0.911) or sex effect (p = 0.950) (Figure 4C). In a separate analysis for water extraction fraction, there was not a significant genotype × age effect (p = 0.709). No hyperintensity or volumetric changes detected in 5xFAD mice (9–12 months)

No additional hyperintense spots were observed outside the ventricular regions in the SE images (Figures 5A,B). There were no significant differences in parenchymal tissue volume (p = 0.613; Figure 5C) or CSF volume (p = 0.988; Figure 5D), indicating the absence of genotype‐dependent brain atrophy or ventricular enlargement. Separate linear regression analyses revealed no sex effect in parenchymal tissue volume (p = 0.862) or CSF volume (p = 0.188).

Diffusion alterations in 5xFAD and CADASIL models

Regional ADC comparisons between WT and 5xFAD mice are shown in Figure 6A. A significant difference in ADC was detected in the midbrain (p = 0.016; Figure 6B), whereas no significant differences were observed in the isocortex (p = 0.745; Figure 6C), hippocampus (p = 0.750; Figure 6D), thalamus (p = 0.742; Figure 6E), hypothalamus (p = 0.354; Figure 6F), or striatum (p = 0.544; Figure 6G). Linear regression analyses revealed no significant sex effect (p ≥ 0.083) across the tested regions.

The CADASIL model exhibited a significant age effect on ADC across all tested brain regions (p ≤ 0.036) except for the isocortex (p = 0.085) (Table S3 and Figure S3). The age effect is consistent with reported findings. 34 No significant genotype, sex, or genotype × age effect was observed (Table S3 and Figure S3).

In the 5xFAD model, the hippocampus exhibited microbleeds but normal ADC values, whereas the midbrain showed altered ADC without detectable microbleeds, suggesting a regional dissociation between microvascular rupture and diffusion‐derived microstructural changes. Moreover, the observed ADC alterations were associated with amyloidosis rather than NOTCH3‐associated pathology.

Pathological alterations in 5xFAD and CADASIL models

In representative mice, characteristic pathological features of the investigated models were confirmed. Clear amyloid plaque deposition was observed in the 5xFAD model across the isocortex, hippocampus, and thalamus (Figure 7A). Representative α‐SMA staining images from CADASIL and WT mice are shown for qualitative visualization (Figure 7B). At the group level, 5xFAD mice exhibited greater amyloid burden (p < 0.001; Figure 7C), while CADASIL mice showed reduced α‐SMA coverage (p = 0.017; Figure 7D), consistent with the characteristic pathological features of these models. DISCUSSION

In this study, we performed comparative analyses of MRI‐detected microstructural features in the 5xFAD and CADASIL models. Microbleeds were associated with amyloid pathology in the absence of hyperintensity, brain atrophy, and ventricular enlargement, but were not detected in the CADASIL model, which represents NOTCH3‐associated vascular pathology. These findings suggest an association between microbleed occurrence and vascular pathology in this experimental comparison; causal relationships remain to be established in future studies.

While the presence of microbleeds in amyloid models but not in CADASIL models have been described, 16 , 35 the present study extends prior work by providing a systematic, side‐by‐side comparison under controlled experimental conditions. This design enables direct evaluation of disease‐dependent differences and integration with complementary imaging markers, which cannot be readily inferred from studies focusing on individual models.

Our findings suggest model‐dependent differences in microbleed occurrence but do not establish microbleeds as amyloid‐specific markers or exclude their relevance to SVD. Instead, they support the interpretation that microbleed occurrence varies across vascular conditions and may reflect differences in underlying pathological context. In human studies, cerebral microbleeds in AD are frequently associated with CAA. 36 The 5xFAD model primarily recapitulates parenchymal amyloid deposition but exhibits limited CAA compared with other transgenic models (e.g., APP23). Therefore, the present data do not directly address CAA‐specific mechanisms or establish a causal relationship between vascular amyloid deposition and microbleed formation. Future studies using models with prominent CAA will be necessary to further clarify this relationship. Findings in CADASIL mice should be interpreted within the context of this specific model rather than generalized to SVD. This study provides a comparative, imaging‐based characterization of model‐dependent differences in microbleed occurrence. Mechanistic evaluation of microbleeds across SVD subtypes, direct comparison of the consequences of Aβ and NOTCH3 extracellular domain accumulation, and assessment of SVD contributions to CAA‐related hemorrhage were not addressed in the present comparative neuroimaging framework. We have demonstrated preliminary characterization on vascular pathology; however, future systematic assessment of vascular morphology, pericyte degeneration, perivascular inflammation, fibrinogen effusions in the perivascular space, or immune responses, which likely contribute to the microbleed formation, would add more mechanistic insight in model comparisons.

Previous clinical and neuropathological studies consistently showed that microbleeds in AD predominantly occurred in lobar regions, 37 , 38 whereas those in SVD typically localize to deep brain structures such as the basal ganglia, thalamus, and brainstem. 1 In the present study, microbleeds were detected in the 5xFAD model but were not observed in the CADASIL cohort, precluding assessment of spatial distribution in the latter. Therefore, while our findings are consistent with reported differences across disease contexts, they do not directly address regional patterns of microbleed localization in non‐amyloid SVD.

BBB disruption and cerebral microbleeds represent two interrelated yet distinct manifestations of vascular dysfunction. BBB permeability was assessed in CADASIL mice but not in 5xFAD mice in this study, as prior work demonstrated BBB disruption in 5xFAD mice using the same methodology. 39 While this implementation allowed targeted evaluation of CADASIL mice, it introduced an asymmetry in experimental design that limited direct comparison across models. The CADASIL model recapitulates key aspects of NOTCH3‐associated vascular pathology; the associated vascular alterations develop progressively over time 40 and are typically accompanied by limited immune activation compared to amyloid models. 41 This may allow for compensatory mechanisms that help maintain BBB stability under the conditions examined. In contrast, the 5xFAD model exhibits pronounced amyloid accumulation 42 and neuroinflammatory responses, 43 which may increase vascular vulnerability. Furthermore, species differences in vascular structure and resilience may also influence the detectability of microbleeds, potentially contributing to the discrepancy between the absence of detectable microbleeds in the CADASIL mice and their presence in human CADASIL patients.

AD develops through a complex cascade of interrelated pathological events 44 involving amyloid accumulation, neuroinflammation, vascular dysfunction, metabolic dysfunction, and neurodegeneration. 45 , 46 , 47 Recognizing microbleeds may serve not only as a diagnostic indicator of amyloid‐related vascular compromise but also as a potential marker for monitoring disease progression and evaluating vascular‐targeted therapeutic interventions.

In our data, ADC alterations and microbleeds appeared as distinct, non‐overlapping features. While ADC is sensitive to cytotoxic or degenerative processes within the parenchyma, 48 microbleeds mark localized vascular damage without substantial diffusion perturbation in the surrounding tissue. Notably, the spatial distribution of microbleeds and diffusion alterations exhibited a regional dissociation in the 5xFAD model. Microbleeds were predominantly observed in the hippocampus, whereas increased ADC was detected in the midbrain without accompanying microbleeds. This pattern suggests that susceptibility‐based and diffusion‐based MRI capture distinct aspects of tissue pathology. The lack of spatial overlap further indicates that these processes may occur independently or at different stages of disease progression. In 5xFAD mice, the regional distribution of ADC alterations may reflect differences in tissue vulnerability or vascular supply, and the selective localization of microbleeds in the hippocampus is consistent with the prior report of regionally heterogeneous vulnerability to amyloid‐associated vascular pathology, 49 although further studies are required to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

No significant sex differences were observed in microbleed occurrence or diffusion measures in this study, although the sample size may limit sensitivity to detect subtle sex‐dependent effects. Potential sex‐dependent differences warrant further investigation in larger cohorts.

Our findings should be interpreted within the context of several limitations. First, this study examined only a single amyloid model (5xFAD) and a single non‐amyloid arteriopathy model (CADASIL), caution is warranted in generalizing these findings to other forms of amyloidosis or the broader spectrum of SVD pathology. Meanwhile, current findings from mouse models provide comparative imaging evidence, while future studies are needed to evaluate their relevance across the heterogeneous spectrum of human SVD. Second, although high‐resolution GRE MRI allowed sensitive detection of microbleeds, smaller lesions below the spatial resolution threshold may have been missed. The ex vivo high‐resolution imaging was performed only in the 5xFAD cohort to validate positive findings; future studies incorporating ex vivo imaging in CADASIL models may further improve sensitivity for detecting rare or small lesions. Third, while our cross‐sectional design captures representative stages of amyloid and vascular pathology, longitudinal tracking would better delineate the temporal relationship among pathological events, e.g., BBB alterations and microbleed formation. Finally, the CADASIL model represents a specific NOTCH3‐associated arteriopathy and may not fully recapitulate the vascular pathology, disease progression, or microbleed manifestation observed in human CADASIL, limiting direct translatability of the findings. The 5xFAD model primarily recapitulates amyloid‐related changes without direct perturbation of NOTCH3 signaling; this difference limits direct mechanistic comparability between the models. Despite these constraints, this work provides a robust foundation for future translational efforts that integrate T2 *‐weighted MRI with complementary molecular or perfusion biomarkers to refine vascular phenotyping in AD and related disorders.

In summary, this study suggests that cerebral microbleeds emerge selectively in an amyloid mouse model but not in the non‐amyloid arteriopathy characterized by vascular smooth muscle cell loss, supporting the view that microbleeds may represent a disease‐context‐dependent imaging phenotype. These findings refine the interpretation of susceptibility‐based MRI markers in AD and underscore the importance of disease context when using microbleeds for imaging‐based phenotyping, vascular risk assessment, and/or translational study design.
