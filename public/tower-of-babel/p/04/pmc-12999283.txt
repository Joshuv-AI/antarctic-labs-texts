Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) due to candida tropicalis following total knee arthroplasty (TKA): A case report
Authors: Taraneh Razaviyoun, Seyed Jamal Hashemi, Azam Asadolahi, Hasti Kamali Sarvestani, Shahram Mahmoudi, Zahra Rafat, Hamed Samsami, Mohammadreza Salehi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFungal periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs) are uncommon and diagnostically challenging, frequently associated with delayed recognition, complex antimicrobial management, and poor outcomes. Candida tropicalis PJIs are particularly difficult to manage due to variable antifungal susceptibility patterns. In the present study, a 72-year-old male developed a polymicrobial PJI following total knee arthroplasty (TKA) despite the absence of classic predisposing risk factors. Early postoperative symptoms were nonspecific, leading to empirical antibacterial therapy and debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR) for presumed bacterial PJI. Persistent inflammation and radiographic changes prompted further investigation. Repeated synovial fluid analysis demonstrated elevated leukocyte counts with neutrophil predominance, and cultures yielded bacterial growth in addition to yeast. Targeted mycological cultures and molecular identification (ITS sequencing) confirmed Candida tropicalis. Antifungal susceptibility testing (CLSI M38-A2) revealed resistance to fluconazole, voriconazole, and caspofungin, with susceptibility to amphotericin B and itraconazole. Ongoing infection necessitated progression to the first stage of a two-stage exchange with prosthesis removal and spacer placement, followed by reimplantation (second stage). Clinical relapse after reimplantation was successfully managed with liposomal amphotericin B–based therapy, resulting in sustained clinical and laboratory improvement. This case highlights the difficulty of diagnosing fungal and polymicrobial PJI due to nonspecific clinical presentation and prolonged response to antibacterial therapy and shows the critical role of antifungal susceptibility testing in guiding treatment. Early incorporation of fungal diagnostics and standardized PJI management strategies is essential to optimize outcomes in complex cases involving Candida tropicalis.Keywords: Prosthetic joint infection, Mycoses, Candida tropicalis, Antifungal Therapy, Knee, Arthroplasty, Surgery

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is the current standard of care for moderate to severe osteoarthritis [1]. While complications are rare, prosthetic joint infections (PJIs) pose significant challenges, resulting in serious potential consequences from the surgery [1]. As the prevalence of TKA increases, the incidence of PJI is also expected to rise.

Over 98% of PJIs are caused by bacterial species, with Staphylococcus aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococci accounting for 50–60% of all cases. Streptococci and enterococci together contribute approximately 10% of PJIs. Fungal PJIs are considered atypical, occurring in about 1–2% of all cases, with Candida species being the most common fungi involved [2].

Patients experiencing fungal PJIs often exhibit impaired cellular immunity, primarily due to underlying issues such as malignant diseases, drug therapies (including corticosteroids, antineoplastic agents, and immunosuppressive drugs), the overuse or inappropriate use of antibiotics, indwelling catheters (either urinary or for parenteral hyperalimentation), diabetes, intravenous drug use, tuberculosis, and other acquired immunosuppressive conditions, all of which are linked to a higher frequency of mycotic infections [3], [4].

Fungal PJIs are challenging to treat, and effective treatment options are still not well-established [4]. The lack of reliable antifungal medications for both systemic and, particularly, local delivery complicates pathogen-directed treatment, highlighting the severity of the situation [4], [5], [6]. This report documents the first case of Candida tropicalis PJI in Iran.

Case study

A 72-year-old man underwent a right TKA in August 2022 for primary arthrosis. The patient had, no history of diabetes, no malignancy, no chronic kidney disease, no immunosuppressive therapy, and no intravenous drug use. Following a successful left knee TKA two years’ prior, the surgery and initial recovery proceeded without complications. However, two weeks prospectively, the patient began experiencing some discomfort which was attributed to a routine post-TKA recovery. By November 2022, significant swelling and inflammation at the surgical site prompted hospital admission. Laboratory tests revealed elevated levels of C-reactive protein (CRP) at 386 mg/ml (normal range up to 10), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) at 140 mm/h (normal range up to 20), and a reduced hemoglobin (HB) level at 7.5 mg/ml (normal range 9.6–15.9). Initial treatment involved debridement, antibiotics ( Teicoplanin (loading dose of 400 mg IV every 12 h for three doses, followed by 400 mg IV once daily for 14 days) and Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV twice daily for 14 days), and implant retention (DAIR) due to the presence of anemia and persistent wound issues. Following treatment, the patient was discharged with ongoing wound care and anemia management. Clindamycin 600 mg IV three times daily for 10 days and Amikacin 500 mg IV once daily for 10 days were switched to Teicoplanin (loading dose of 400 mg IV every 12 h for three doses, followed by 400 mg IV once daily for 14 days) and Amikacin: 500 mg IV once daily for 10 days as part of the revised treatment plan.

In January 2023, X-ray imaging of the affected knee revealed a lytic lesion with distinct boundaries in the lateral aspect of the right tibia plateau, suggestive of changes related to osteomyelitis (Fig. 1. a). Subsequent inflammatory marker tests showed elevated CRP levels of 360 mg/ml, ESR of 108 mm/h, and an improved Hb level of 10.5 mg/ml. A preoperative diagnostic arthrocentesis was performed to identify the cause of joint infection. The drained synovial fluid was sent to the clinical microbiological laboratory for culture. The results of the direct Gram and Giemsa staining of the synovial fluid sediment showed the presence of white blood cells (48,000 cells/µL), predominantly neutrophils (92%), and budding yeast (Fig. 2.a). Upon further incubation, the culture revealed the presence of bacteria (coagulase-negative Staphylococcus (Staphylococcus epidermidis)) on bacterial medium, such as Blood agar, Eosin Methylene Blue Agar, and Thioglycolate Broth, as well as yeast colonies on Sabourud's Chloramphenicol agar (SCA) and bacterial mediums.

Various mycological and molecular methods were employed to identify the yeast as Candida tropicalis, with a specific sequence number of area ITS1–5.8S rDNA-ITS2: PQ311667. Molecular identification using ITS sequencing was performed to: (1) confirm species-level identification of Candida tropicalis, (2) exclude closely related non-albicans Candida species, (3) support antifungal resistance interpretation.

Antifungal susceptibility testing (AFST) was performed according to CLSI M38-A2 standards, and interpretation was based on CLSI clinical breakpoints and epidemiological cutoff values. The isolate was found to be resistant to Voriconazole, Caspofungin, and Fluconazole, but sensitive to Amphotericin B and Itraconazole (All MIC values are reported in Table 2).

It should be noted that the patient initially declined major revision surgery due to advanced age and comorbid anemia. Also, conservative antifungal treatment strategy was attempted based on susceptibility results (Itraconazole 200 mg orally twice daily). During this period, the patient was monitored regularly with serial CRP, ESR, radiographic imaging, and clinical assessments. Although partial laboratory improvement was observed intermittently, inflammatory markers remained persistently elevated and radiographic signs of osteomyelitis progressed and persistent infection despite medical therapy ultimately necessitated surgical intervention. The likely reasons for failure of the initial antifungal regimen include high fungal burden, biofilm persistence on prosthetic material, documented resistance to caspofungin and azoles, and the polymicrobial nature of the infection. Although itraconazole was administered according to recommended dosing, therapeutic drug monitoring was not available at our center, which we acknowledge as a limitation. Relapse following reimplantation was most likely related to residual biofilm-associated fungal persistence.

The patient underwent rigorous debridement of infected and devitalized tissues on February 8, 2024, with the removal of total knee arthroplasty components. A spacer was inserted into the joint cavity after first stage of two-stage exchange. The patient received a combination of antibacterial and antifungal treatments, including cephalothin DC, Rifampin DC, Clindamycin 600 mg IV three times daily for 10 days, Amikacin 500 mg IV once daily for 10 days, and Caspofungin intravenous loading dose of 70 mg on day 1, followed by 50 mg once daily for a total duration of 6 weeks, followed by a maintenance dose of Caspofungin 50 mg once daily for a total duration of 6 weeks and subsequent 200 mg orally twice daily as step-down therapy for 12 weeks.

The use of caspofungin occurred before the final AFST report was available. It was initiated as empirical antifungal therapy due to high clinical suspicion of invasive Candida infection, broad-spectrum coverage of echinocandins in invasive candidiasis, favorable safety profile in elderly patients, and recommendation in IDSA (Infectious Diseases Society of America) guidelines for initial therapy in suspected non-albicans Candida infections pending susceptibility data.

Regular monitoring of renal function, liver function, anemia, and inflammatory markers was conducted to ensure the patient's well-being throughout the treatment process. The comprehensive treatment plan and close monitoring led to successful management of the joint infection caused by Candida tropicalis.

The patient experienced a significant improvement in knee inflammation and mobility after undergoing a series of treatments in 2024. In April, laboratory tests indicated that the inflammation in the knee area had subsided, with ESR tests below 10 mm/hr and no abnormalities in other tests. X-ray results also showed average joint space thickness with no bone lesions or deformities present (Fig. 1.b).

In July 2024, the patient underwent rigorous debridement of infected and devitalized tissues prior to reimplantation (second stage) of the right TKA (Scorpio NRG, posterior stabilized). Debridement and synovial fluid culture were obtained during the operation. Direct Gram and Giemsa stains of synovial fluid sediment showed many WBCs with predominately PMN and budding yeasts (Fig. 2.b), and cultures remained with few colonies of C. tropicalis (confirmed by mycology and molecular method) (Fig. 3). While Caspofungin IV 50 mg/day for 6 weeks, oral Itraconazole 200 mg twice daily for 12 weeks, and Vancomycin IV 1 g every 12 h for 4 weeks were prescribed, laboratory LFT and inflammatory tests were ordered. It should be noted, in this case antibacterial therapy was continued due to prior isolation of S. epidermidis, persistent inflammatory markers, and high risk of occult bacterial co-infection in fungal PJIs.

The patient was discharged. In October 2024 (after two months of TKA replacement), the ESR was increased to 40 mm. The soft tissue ultrasound around the right knee showed a 13 mm deep turbid fluid effusion, and an increase in synovium thickness was evident(Fig. 4).

Right Knee photograph after reimplantation (second stage) and treatment with Caspofungin and Itraconazole.

In November 21, 2024, the patient's treatment plan was modified to include Liposomal Amphotericin B (salvage therapy): 3–5 mg/kg/day intravenously (300 mg/day in this patient) for 4 weeks, Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV twice daily for 14 days, and Vancomycin IV 1 g every 12 h for 4 weeks, to be administered in the hospital for one month. An ultrasound of the soft tissue revealed a 13 mm deep turbid fluid in the right knee joint, along with a slight thickening of the synovium. The patient's ESR was measured at 40 mm and CRP at 76. By November, just six days after starting the new treatment, the patient showed significant improvement with ESR reducing to 15 and CRP to 36. A follow-up ultrasound of the right knee indicated a large effusion with a maximum thickness of 17 mm, internal echoes, and signs of synovitis in the supra patella area, along with mild subcutaneous edema. Antibacterial therapy was discontinued in December 2024, when the patient achieved a major milestone by being able to walk without the assistance of a walker for the first time in approximately two years. Discussion

This case highlighted the challenges encountered in managing post-operative complications following TKA. The etiology of knee total arthroplasty (KTA) infections worldwide is a growing concern due to the significant medical and economic impact they have [7], [8], [9]. Despite this, there is a lack of sufficient evidence regarding fungal PJI, making it a relatively understudied area in comparison to bacterial PJI [10]. This case highlights the difficulty of diagnosing fungal and polymicrobial PJI due to nonspecific clinical presentation and prolonged response to antibacterial therapy. Definitive diagnosis was achieved through a combination of synovial fluid analysis, prolonged fungal cultures on selective media, and molecular identification using ITS sequencing, underscoring the importance of multimodal diagnostic strategies.

Although our patient had no classic immunosuppressive conditions such as diabetes, malignancy, corticosteroid exposure, or immunosuppressive therapy, several predisposing factors may have contributed to the development of Candida tropicalis PJI. Advanced age, prior prosthetic implantation, multiple surgical interventions, prolonged exposure to broad-spectrum antibacterial therapy, and polymicrobial infection with Staphylococcus epidermidis likely disrupted local host defenses and facilitated fungal colonization and biofilm formation. Fungal PJIs are frequently healthcare-associated and may arise from perioperative contamination or secondary colonization following repeated antimicrobial pressure.

In the context of fungal prosthetic joint infections, Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) are emerging as more sensitive diagnostic options, with reported sensitivity of up to 90% [11], [12]. In this case, early infection (November 2022) was treated empirically as bacterial PJI, resulting in delayed fungal diagnosis. Whereas, mNGS could have identified Candida tropicalis earlier, detected polymicrobial co-infection reduced reliance on prolonged antibacterial therapy, and prevented progression of osteomyelitis and resistance evolution. Such an approach could potentially have shortened disease duration, reduced surgical burden, and minimized the development of multidrug resistance [11].

According to the IDSA guideline [13] and recent systematic reviews [4], [6], [10], the preferred management of Candida PJI involves a two-stage exchange arthroplasty combined with prolonged systemic antifungal therapy (at least 12 weeks), while Candida osteomyelitis may require 6–12 months of treatment depending on clinical response. In our case, antifungal susceptibility testing demonstrated resistance to fluconazole, voriconazole, and caspofungin, which precluded standard azole-based and echinocandin-based therapy and necessitated individualized therapy guided by susceptibility results. The patient ultimately required liposomal amphotericin B salvage therapy following relapse, achieving sustained clinical and laboratory remission. This case underscores the importance of early fungal diagnostics, AFST, and adherence to guideline-directed surgical management in complex Candida PJI.

Conclusion

The findings of this study highlight the importance of considering fungal infections, specifically C. tropicalis, in the diagnosis process of PJI. By incorporating fungal testing alongside bacterial exams, healthcare professionals can more effectively identify and treat both fungal infections and potential co-infections. The implementation of the AFST is crucial for improved outcomes in the treatment of fungal PJI. Overall, this study emphasizes the need for a comprehensive approach to diagnosing and treating PJI to achieve the best possible patient outcomes.
