Optimizing dehydration ratio and rate during the withering process enhances aroma quality of oolong tea
Authors: Binghong Liu, Jialin Chen, Cheng Ma, Zhaoyu Wang, Yanchun Zheng, Zihao Qiu, Jiahao Chen, Hongbo Zhao, Peng Zheng, Binmei Sun, Shaoqun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWithering plays a pivotal role in shaping the flavor and aroma quality of the final product, and dehydration ratio (DR) and dehydration rate (DRt) are key parameters during withering. In this study, the DR and DRt during solar withering and indoor withering (ID1) of Oolong tea were investigated, and the GC-MS and sensory evaluation were employed to assess tea aroma. The result showed that the DR and DRt of solar withering (DR 10%, DRt 16.67%/h) were significantly higher than those in ID1 (DR 1.3%, DRt 2.08%/h). Headspace solid-phase microextraction-gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC-MS) analysis and sensory evaluation revealed that increasing DR and DRt significantly elevated the total volatile content in withered leaves and promoted the conversion of aldehydes to alcohols and esters. Further experimental groups verified that DRt was critical and highly related to the development of aroma compounds instead of the DR. Orthogonal Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) identified seven key aroma compounds, including linalool, nerolidol, and indole, which showed strong positive correlations with DRt. Additionally, the samples with higher DRt (SL and ID3 treatments) substantially enhanced floral-fruity terpenoids and esters while reducing grassy aldehydes to achieve the highest aroma scores, dominated by floral and honey notes. These findings provide new insights into optimizing indoor withering parameters under rainy conditions, offering a practical solution to overcome climate-dependent limitations in traditional oolong tea processing. Subject terms: Biochemistry, Chemistry, Plant sciences

Introduction

Oolong tea, one of the six major tea categories, characterized by its unique shaking process and partial fermentation, develops a rich floral-fruity aroma and mellow taste1. In the eastern region of Guangdong Province in China, Lingtou Dancong tea (Camellia sinensis cv. Lingtou Dancong), as a distinctive oolong tea cultivar, is highly favored by consumers for its unique “floral and honey-like” aroma profile. Its quality formation heavily relies on the regulation of key postharvest processing steps2.

As a critical postharvest processing step, withering not only serves as the “first switch” to activate endogenous metabolism in tea leaves but also directly influences the metabolic orientation of aroma precursors by regulating water migration and cell membrane permeability3. In oolong tea processing, withering plays a pivotal role in shaping the flavor and aroma quality of the final tea product4. Studies have shown that dehydration ratio (DR), as a quantitative indicator of overall moisture loss during withering, significantly impacts the accumulation of aroma compounds such as terpenoids and aromatic alcohols5. However, systematic research on the regulatory mechanisms of dehydration rate (DRt) on aroma biosynthesis pathways remains lacking. Specifically, rapid DRt may activate the lipoxygenase (LOX) pathway by triggering jasmonic acid signaling, while slow DRt might enhance β-glucosidase activity to promote the release of free-form aroma compounds6.

Solar withering was used as an effective method to enhance floral-fruity aroma components during the production of Oolong tea7. However, Sunlight is not easily available during the tea production season, particularly in the rainy and humid spring climate of southern China8. Indoor withering, due to crude parameter control, often results in incomplete aroma conversion and residual grassy odors9. Previous studies have systematically explored the temperature and humidity influence on tea qualities during withering10. However, the impacts of solar withering on aroma transformation and quality characteristics remain poorly understood. Elucidating the influence of key water loss parameters on Lingtou Dancong tea quality will not only advance the understanding of aroma metabolism regulation but also provide critical theoretical and technical support for optimizing oolong tea processing.

This study investigated the DR and DRt during solar withering and indoor withering (ID1) of Oolong tea, and the GC-MS and sensory evaluation were employed to assess tea aroma. The result showed that the dehydration ratio and rate of solar withering (DR 10%, DRt 16.67%/h) were significantly higher than those in ID1 (DR 1.3%, DRt 2.08%/h), and with a great charming aroma. Further experimental groups verified that DRt was critical and highly related to the development of aroma compounds instead of the DR. The results provide a theoretical foundation for optimizing indoor withering protocols under rainy conditions, and reducing the industry’s traditional dependence on weather-dependent production methods.

Results

Effects of high DR and DRt under solar withering on aroma profiles of withered dancong tea leaves

A total of 44 volatile compounds were identified by HS-SPME-GC-MS, including 7 alcohols, 5 aldehydes, 10 esters, 1 heterocyclic compound (indole), 5 ketones, 4 oxides, 2 phenols, and 10 alkenes (Table S1). Both DR (10%) and DRt (16.67% h−1) in SL were higher than those in ID1 (DR = 1.3%, DRt = 2.08% h−1) (Fig. 1A). The total volatile content in SL reached 2219.5 μg/kg, which was significantly higher than that in ID1 and FL (P < 0.05), while no significant difference was observed between ID1 and FL (Fig. 1B). In comparison, SL exhibited higher DR and DRt values than ID1. Further categorical analysis revealed that ester content in SL increased to 1117.3 μg/kg (50.34% of total volatiles), alcohols rose to 689.2 μg/kg (31.05%), while aldehydes sharply declined to 39.8 μg/kg (1.79%). In contrast, under low DR and DRt conditions in ID1, the conversion of aldehydes to alcohols and esters was less pronounced (Fig. 1C and Table S7).

The content of linalool was highest in SL, reaching 271.99 μg/kg, an increase of 9.4% compared to FL (248.69 μg/kg), while it was 221.46 μg/kg in the ID1 group. The content of nerolidol significantly increased to 115.02 μg/kg in SL, an increase of 48% compared to FL (77.74 μg/kg), while it was 53.70 μg/kg in the ID1 group, less than half of that in SL. Indole was 99.41 μg/kg in FL, and its content decreased to 29.47 μg/kg and 34.79 μg/kg in SL and ID1 treatments, respectively (Fig. 2B).

Effects of Increasing DR and DRt during Indoor Withering on Aroma Profiles

Increasing the DR and DRt during indoor withering significantly enhances the volatile aroma compounds in withered leaves. The OPLS-DA model for indoor withered groups exhibited robust performance, achieving a 96.4% prediction rate for aroma profiles across three samples, and the model proved reliable for discriminating volatile compound profiles under varying dehydration conditions (Fig. S2A, B). When the DR of ID2 was elevated to 10% (DRt 2.01%/h, same level as ID1), the total volatile content increased to 1890.1 μg/kg, surpassing ID1. Further optimization of DRt in ID3 (DRt 3.55%/h) under the same DR elevated the total volatile content to 2103.9 μg/kg, significantly higher than both ID2 and ID1 (Fig. 3A, B). Categorical analysis revealed that alcohols, esters, heterocyclic compounds (indole), olefins, and alkenes were significantly increased (Fig. 3C and Table S7). (E)-2-hexenal (241.86 μg/kg) was exclusively detected in ID1, whereas (E)-3-hexen-1-ol emerged in ID2 (229.01 μg/kg) and ID3 (238.18 μg/kg) under elevated DR, mirroring SL patterns. Ester accumulation followed DR gradients: (Z)-3-hexenyl acetate increased from 348.69 μg/kg (ID1) to 618.86 μg/kg (ID2) and 525.09 μg/kg (ID3), while methyl salicylate rose from 57.38 μg/kg (ID1) to 148.42 μg/kg (ID2) and 157.14 μg/kg (ID3). Nerolidol exhibited an increase in ID3 (104.76 μg/kg) compared to ID1 (53.70 μg/kg) and ID2 (47.78 μg/kg), aligning with DRt-dependent terpenoid biosynthesis. Linalool and indole concentrations showed progressive enhancement with DR/DRt intensification, reaching 298.09 μg/kg (linalool, ID3) and 188.8 μg/kg (indole, ID3), respectively (Fig. 3D).

Analysis of volatile components in manufactured tea

To further analyze the volatile compounds in the manufactured tea, an OPLS-DA analysis was conducted on the manufactured tea samples from the SL, ID1, ID2, and ID3 (Fig. S3A). The OPLS-DA model was considered reliable based on R²and Q²values exceeding 0.5 (Fig. S3B, C). Nine compounds were with a VIP > 1(Table S6). OAV analysis found that 13 compounds exhibited OAV > 1, suggesting their pivotal role in shaping the distinctive aroma profile of Dancong tea. The calculated OAV values, combined with aroma classification from established databases, enabled categorization of the aromatic characteristics into six dimensions: floral, honey-like, grassy, fruity, woody, and roasted notes, as presented in Table S3. The aroma profile of SL is predominantly floral, with the floral dimension accounting for 97.15% of the total OAV, complemented by fruity, honey-like, roasted, woody, and herbal notes. The total OAV of floral compounds in SL reached 2017.25, significantly higher than that of fruity (total OAV = 32.26) and honey-like (total OAV = 14.16) notes (Table S3). The total OAV values for woody, roasted, and herbal dimensions were merely 7.53, 3.68, and 1.51, respectively. Within the floral dimension of SL finished tea, the primary contributing compounds were nerolidol, (E)-β-ionone, linalool, indole, methyl jasmonate, geraniol, and α-ionone, with OAV values of 1288.05, 584.77, 135, 2.75, 3.21, and 1.68, respectively. The woody dimension was mainly influenced by (Z)-linalool oxide and α-pinene, with OAV values of 5.91 and 1.27, while benzaldehyde, with an OAV of 3.68, was the dominant contributor to the roasted dimension. In the indoor withering treatment groups (ID1, ID2, and ID3), the aroma profiles of the manufactured tea were also predominantly floral, with the floral dimension accounting for over 90% of the total OAV, accompanied by fruity, honey-like, roasted, woody, and herbal notes. The total OAV values for the floral dimension in ID1, ID2, and ID3 were 1320.18, 1406.89, and 1650.3, respectively. Notably, the floral OAV of ID2 was significantly higher than that of ID1, while ID3 exhibited the highest floral OAV, surpassing both ID1 and ID2, though it did not reach the level observed in SL. The key compounds contributing to the floral dimension in the indoor withering treatments were consistent with those in the sunlight withering group, including nerolidol, (E)-β-ionone, linalool, indole, methyl jasmonate, geraniol, and α-ionone, except for α-ionone in ID2, which had an OAV below 1. It is noteworthy that in the indoor withering group, ID3 exhibited higher OAV values for numerous aroma compounds compared to ID1 and ID2.

Comprehensive OAV analysis revealed that floral notes constitute the dominant aromatic dimension of Dancong tea, demonstrating the most pronounced sensory intensity, followed by fruity, honey-like, roasted, and woody characteristics (Fig. 5). The chart reveals that SL finished tea exhibited the strongest performance in the floral and honey-like dimensions, significantly outperforming other treatments, while ID3 ranked second in these dimensions. However, ID3 demonstrated superior performance in other aroma dimensions, such as fruity, roasted, and woody notes (Fig. 5).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that increasing DR to 10% significantly promotes the metabolic conversion of aldehydes to alcohols and esters in withered tea. (E)-2-hexenal, also known as “leaf aldehyde”, contributes grassy and fruity notes to tea11. Notably, (E)-2-hexenal was predominantly detected in low-DR treatments (FL and ID1) but was undetectable once DR reached 10% (ID2 and ID3). (E)-3-hexenol, an isomer of leaf alcohol, exhibits floral and fresh aromas in tea and was exclusively detected in SL, ID2, and ID3 groups12,13. Multivariate correlation analysis revealed a highly significant negative correlation between (E)-2-hexenal and DR, while (E)-3-hexenol showed a highly significant positive correlation with DR, suggesting a regulatory association between DR and its metabolic conversion. Both (E)-2-hexenal and (E)-3-hexenol are oxidation products of free fatty acids (e.g., α-linolenic acid) via the lipoxygenase (LOX) pathway, and serve as intermediates for the synthesis of other aroma compounds such as (Z)-3-hexenyl acetate and methyl salicylate, playing a crucial role in shaping the aroma quality of withered tea during subsequent Dancong tea processing14. This conversion process may be associated with changes in cell membrane permeability: increased water loss accelerates lipid peroxidation in cell membranes14, releasing α-linolenic acid that is converted into C6 aldehydes via the lipoxygenase (LOX) pathway15. These aldehydes are subsequently transformed into alcohols and esters through the action of alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) and alcohol acyltransferase (AAT)16. These findings align with those in oolong tea17, but further highlight the importance of achieving a DR of approximately 10% in driving this metabolic transition. Regarding ester metabolism, the accumulation of (Z)-3-hexenyl acetate and methyl salicylate exhibited a highly significant positive correlation with DR. As a major aroma component in oolong tea18,19, (Z)-3-hexenyl acetate contributes fresh and tender aromas to tea13. When DR reached 10%, their contents increased to 647.17 μg/kg and 227.65 μg/kg, respectively. The activation theory of the mevalonate (MVA) pathway proposed by Rodríguez-Concepción, M and Boronat, A20 partially explains this phenomenon: DR-induced membrane disruption may enhance the localized interaction between AAT and substrates21, though further proteomic validation is required to confirm specific enzyme dynamics. This finding has practical implications for addressing the “residual grassy odor” issue in traditional indoor withering. The metabolic response of terpenoids further highlights the regulatory specificity of DR. Linalool content reached 271.99 μg/kg at 10% DR, representing a 9.4% increase compared to fresh leaves. This elevation is linked to enhanced farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP) synthesis in the MVA pathway20.

This study revealed that increasing DRt significantly enhances the accumulation of terpenoids. Linalool, an important aroma compound in oolong tea1, serves as a key component of floral notes in oolong tea22 and plays a crucial role in shaping the aroma quality of Dancong tea. Linalool and its oxides exist as volatile compounds in semi-fermented and fermented teas, accounting for over half of the total volatiles6. In addition to linalool, several other terpenoid compounds, including nerolidol and ionones, play critical roles in defining the aroma quality of oolong tea. Nerolidol, a sesquiterpene alcohol with a characteristic jasmine-like floral aroma23,24, exhibited high odor activity values (OAVs) and showed a strong positive correlation with dehydration rate (DRt), indicating its importance in the development of floral notes under rapid withering conditions. Similarly, ionones, such as (E)-β-ionone and α-ionone, derived from carotenoid degradation, possess extremely low odor thresholds and contribute violet-like and sweet floral aromas even at low concentrations25. These compounds were identified as key contributors to the floral dimension in the OAV analysis and significantly influenced the overall sensory profile of Dancong tea. Therefore, terpenoid metabolism, encompassing both monoterpenes and norisoprenoids, plays a comprehensive and indispensable role in the formation of the characteristic floral-fruity aroma of oolong tea. This phenomenon is closely related to stress-induced activation of the jasmonic acid signaling pathway. Rapid dehydration may induce osmotic stress, which has been reported to upregulate terpene synthase (TPS) gene expression promotes the conversion of farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP) to nerolidol. Notably, although ID3 did not rely on light exposure, its stress intensity may partially mimic certain biochemical effects observed during solar withering. The indole content in ID3 (188.8 μg/kg) was 5.4 times higher than that in the SL, suggesting that rapid dehydration may be associated with enhanced indole accumulation, potentially involving the tryptophan decarboxylase (TDC) pathway. Further analysis revealed that the DR exhibits specificity in regulating the metabolism of aroma precursors. The accumulation of (Z)-3-hexenyl acetate showed a highly significant positive correlation with DRt, reaching 647.17 μg/kg in SL samples. Its synthesis may be regulated by blue light radiation-induced CsCHAT1 gene expression26. However, the mechanistic differences between solar withering and indoor high DR treatments lie in the types of stress: the former relies on light-temperature synergistic effects, while the latter activates stress signaling via pure physical dehydration, resulting in more efficient indole synthesis in the ID3 group27,28. Although rapid dehydration can significantly increase terpenoid content, its molecular regulatory network remains to be fully elucidated. For instance, the spatiotemporal expression relationship between the jasmonic acid signaling pathway and TPS genes remains unclear. Future research should integrate transcriptomics and metabolic flux analysis to clarify the quantitative effects of DRt on membrane permeability changes and key enzyme activities, while developing intelligent control systems to achieve precise parameter optimization.

In this study, the quantitative advantages of aroma compounds conferred by DR and DRt during the withering stage were retained in the final manufactured tea. The total aroma content in SL-manufactured tea reached 1176 μg/kg, while the ID3 exhibited 1181 μg/kg, showing comparable levels between the two treatments. However, the compositional profiles of individual compounds in manufactured tea differed from those in withered leaves. Previous studies have demonstrated that the shaking process in oolong tea production imposes dual stresses of low temperature and mechanical damage, activating stress-responsive genes and enhancing biosynthetic pathways of characteristic aroma compounds such as indole, jasmine lactone, nerolidol, and β-ionone, thereby elevating the tea’s aroma profile1,29–31. Subsequent non-enzymatic processes, including fixation, rolling, and baking, further modify the chemical compositions, which may contribute to the observed differences between withered leaves and manufactured tea. This phenomenon may also be attributed to SL upregulating genes associated with α-linolenic acid metabolism and terpenoid biosynthesis, resulting in greater accumulation of aroma precursors compared to indoor withering. These precursors confer advantages for synthesizing key aroma substances like 3-hexenal, 3-hexenol, 3-hexenyl esters, and jasmonates during subsequent processing stages15. Alcohols accumulated during withering were oxidized to corresponding aldehydes during the shaking process29, while thermally stable esters were preserved through the fixation step22. Notably, the odor activity value (OAV) of indole in manufactured tea increased 3.2-fold compared to withered leaves, indicating enzymatic reactions during post-processing liberated bound aroma precursors. OAV analysis revealed that SL exhibited optimal performance in floral and honey-like dimensions, significantly outperforming other treatments, while ID3 ranked second. This confirms solar-withered Dancong tea’s superiority in floral and honey attributes over indoor-withered counterparts, consistent with prior findings32. Within the floral dimension, SL showed significantly higher OAVs for indole, nerolidol, and methyl jasmonate than ID1, ID2, and ID3, whereas (E)-β-ionone and linalool peaked in ID3. The aroma profile of Dancong manufactured tea predominantly featured floral-fruity notes with honey nuances, aligning with established research22. Intriguingly, although ID3 achieved 98% of SL’s total aroma content through accelerated dehydration, its floral OAV remained 12% lower. This discrepancy may be partially attributed to the absence of photochemical reactions associated with solar withering33. These findings suggest future process optimization should integrate physical dehydration with photochemical regulation, potentially through developing LED-assisted indoor withering systems that mimic solar spectral components. This study systematically elucidates, for the first time, the persistent effects of withering parameters on manufactured tea quality, providing critical data for establishing a coordinated “withering-shaking” regulatory framework. The integration of intelligent control systems for real-time monitoring of environmental parameters (temperature, humidity, light quality) with metabolic pathway modulation strategies may provide a useful reference for improving precision control in tea processing, and could address efficiency improvements and quality consistency challenges in modern tea production.

In conclusion, this study utilized HS-SPME-GC-MS combined with sensory evaluation and multivariate statistical analysis to comparatively analyze aroma quality components in Dancong tea leaves during the withering stage under different DR and DRt. The results demonstrated that increasing DR and DRt effectively improved the aroma quality of withered tea leaves. Specifically, DRt showed a significant positive correlation with the total volatile compound content, while DR showed a highly significant positive correlation. Key volatile compounds, including (Z)-3-hexenyl acetate, (E)-2-hexenal, (E)-3-hexenol, methyl salicylate, indole, linalool, and nerolidol, were identified as critical discriminators (VIP > 1) for aroma profiles under varying DRt treatments. Notably, elevated DR promoted the metabolic conversion of aldehydes to alcohols and esters, particularly the transformation of (E)-2-hexenal to (E)-3-hexenol. Additionally, DR showed a highly significant positive correlation with (Z)-3-hexenyl acetate and methyl salicylate accumulation. Floral and fruity aroma components characteristic of oolong tea, such as nerolidol, linalool, and indole, displayed significant positive correlations with DRt. Crucially, achieving a 10% DR combined with an accelerated DRt significantly increased the content of key floral-fruity aroma precursors in withered tea leaves.

Methods

Chemical reagents

Alkane standard solution (C8–C40) for calculating linear retention indices (RI) was supplied by TanMo Quality Testing Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The internal standard solution was prepared using dichloromethane prior to use34. Ultrapure water was generated using a Barnstead GenPure Pro system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The reference standard ethyl decanoate was purchased from Shanghai YuanYe Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Manufacturing process and sample collection of tea

Fresh leaves of Lingtou Dancong tea (FL) were harvested in 2023 from Pingxi Village, Raoping County, Chaozhou, Guangdong Province (23.93°N, 116.70°E), following the standard plucking criterion of first three leaves and buds. Totally 10 kg of tea leaves were collected and then divided into five equal parts for five independent treatments.

To systematically evaluate the independent effects of dehydration ratio (DR) and dehydration rate (DRt) on aroma formation, two withering approaches were designed: solar withering (SL) and indoor withering (ID), with three variants. SL (temperature 29.5 °C, light intensity 31000 ± 2000 Lux) represented natural solar withering, where withering was terminated when DR reached 10% (achieved within 36 min). ID treatments (temperature 28.8 °C, light intensity 500 ± 50 Lux) included: ID1 - duration identical to SL (36 min) but DR only 1.3%; ID2 - extended duration until DR reached 10% (298 min) to isolate the effect of DR; ID3 - forced air circulation to accelerate DRt, achieving 10% DR in 173 min, thereby mimicking the DRt of SL under indoor conditions. This design allowed us to disentangle the contributions of DR and DRt to volatile compound dynamics. DR was continuously monitored using a gravimetric method35.

The withered leaves were then processed into manufactured tea by experienced tea masters using traditional Lingtou Dancong tea methods, as displayed in Fig. 6. Both the withered and manufactured tea were subjected to the following assays.

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis of volatile compounds

Volatile compounds in tea samples were extracted using headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME). Fresh tea leaves were ground into powder under liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C prior to analysis. For fresh tea leaves (FL, untreated), 0.2 g of powder was placed in a 20 mL headspace vial, while 2 g of manufactured tea powder was used in a 40 mL vial.

Each sample was mixed with 5 mL of saturated sodium chloride solution and 5 µL of ethyl decanoate (8.64 μg/mL, in dichloromethane) used as an internal standard, then sealed with aluminum foil. Headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) was performed using a divinylbenzene/carboxen/polydimethylsiloxane (DVB/CAR/PDMS) fiber (50/30 µm inner diameter, 2 cm length) (Supelco, Darmstadt, Germany), as in our previous methods34. Briefly, samples were equilibrated 15 min at 80 °C to allow partitioning of volatile compounds between the sample matrix and the headspace prior to analysis. Then, the fiber was inserted into the headspace vial, and extraction proceeded for 40 min at 80 °C. After extraction, the SPME fiber was injected into the GC inlet for 3 min at 250 °C.

GC-MS analysis was performed on an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph coupled with a 5977A mass spectrometer. Separation was achieved using an HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) with high-purity helium as the carrier gas (1.0 mL/min in splitless mode). The temperature program was as follows: initially held at 50 °C for 1 min, ramped at 5 °C/min to 220 °C, and held for 5 min. The MS parameters included an ion source temperature of 230 °C, electron ionization energy of 70 eV, mass scan range of m/z 30–400, and a solvent delay of 4 min. Three technical replicates were analyzed per sample.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory analysis was conducted on finished tea samples processed from differentially withered leaves. A panel of five tea evaluation experts performed the assessment according to the GB/T 23776-2018 Methodology for Sensory Evaluation of Tea. To be specific, tea was brewed at 100 °C and served at ~55 °C; evaluations were conducted in a standardized sensory room (GB/T 18797); data were collected using paper ballots and later digitized; panelists included 3 males and 2 females, aged 28–55. The evaluation criteria included appearance (20%), liquor color (5%), aroma (30%), taste (35%), and infused leaf (10%). Weighted scores were aggregated to generate a final composite score.

Statistical analysis

Raw data from three biological replicates were preprocessed using Microsoft Excel 2021. One-way and multifactor analysis of variance were conducted to assess significant differences among treatments with SPSS Statistics 24 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). Visualization tools included bar plots, stacked plots, radar charts, and heatmaps generated via Origin 2024 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). OPLS-DA and VIP were executed using SIMCA 14.1 (Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden). Additional heatmaps were constructed with TBtools 2.136 (Guangzhou, China).
