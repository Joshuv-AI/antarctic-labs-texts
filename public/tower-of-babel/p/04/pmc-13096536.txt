Prevalence of tobacco use in a cross-sectional survey of people initiating HIV care in a Chennai clinic
Authors: S Poongulali, Nancy A Rigotti, N Kumarasamy, Catherine Nagawa, B Faith, Gina Rae Kruse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives: We aimed to understand tobacco use and treatment patterns among adults starting care in a Chennai HIV clinic. Methods: Cross-sectional survey of adults initiating HIV care with biochemical verification of tobacco exposure. Results: Among 154 adult patients (10/2019-12/2021) initiating HIV care, 37.7% (n = 58) self-reported current tobacco use (13.6% [n = 21] smoking, 17.5% [n = 27] smokeless tobacco [SLT], 6.5% [n = 10] dual use) and 7.8% (n = 12) reported former use (5.8% [n = 9] smoking, 1.3% [n = 2] SLT, 0.6% [n = 1] dual), none reported using cessation medications, two reported counseling. Cotinine levels indicating current use varied by tobacco product (smoking: 66.7% had cotinine ≥ 50ng/ml; SLT: 70.4%; dual: 80.0%, no current tobacco: 44.8%, p = 0.02). Conclusion: Smoking and SLT use are common and underreported and evidence-based cessation treatment use was rare prior to HIV care initiation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42986-y.Keywords: Smoking, Smokeless tobacco, HIV, India, CessationSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Tobacco use and HIV are both major public health threats to low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where over 80% of tobacco users and 91% of people living with HIV (PWH) reside.1,2 With ART, it is estimated that PWH who use tobacco will be more likely to die of tobacco-attributable illness than of HIV-related complications.3 In LMICs the prevalence of tobacco use is 30% and the relative risk of tobacco use among PWH compared to people without HIV is approximately 1.4 for women and 1.5 for men and these self-reported prevalence estimates may underestimate the true prevalence.4–6 The impact of tobacco use in this population is substantial. A model projecting 10-year cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk using the TREAT Asia HIV Observational Database which includes LMICs such as India, China, Thailand, Malaysia, The Philippines, and Singapore, showed the annual rate of CVD events is projected to double from 2019 to 2028.7,8 Simulations of CVD reducing interventions showed smoking cessation produced the greatest reduction in CVD events compared to other interventions including blood pressure control, cholesterol lowering, and antiretroviral drug substitutions.7 Beyond CVD, tobacco use is also associated with increased risk for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma and recurrent respiratory infections among PWH.9 Tobacco use is also a leading contributor to cancer, especially lung cancer, anal cancer and some lymphomas.10 Studies have shown that entry into HIV care is an opportune time to address tobacco use with substantial numbers of patients reporting cessation in the initial months in care, and this may reduce tobacco-attributable harms.4,11,12.

Research examining tobacco use and cessation treatments among PWH have primarily been based in high income settings, and studies based in LMIC practice settings remain limited.13–20 High resource settings have important differences from LMIC settings in tobacco products, use patterns, and treatment accessibility and acceptability that may have implications for treatment and cessation. One such difference is the prevalence of smokeless tobacco or use of multiple tobacco products.21,22 Smokeless tobacco produces morbidity and mortality from cancer and cardiovascular disease and this burden falls primarily in LMICs.23,24 There is a need for more studies of smokeless tobacco or dual forms of tobacco both among PWH and among general populations in LMICs.25,26

In India, 39% of men and 4% of women report current tobacco use.27 The effects of this tobacco use are apparent in high rates of heart disease, stroke, and cancer, particularly head and neck cancer. In 2020, India had an estimated 135,929 new cases of lip and oral cavity cancer, making it the second most common cancer in India after breast (178,361).28 The tobacco epidemic in India intersects with the third largest HIV epidemic in the world with an estimated 2.54 million people living with HIV and 59,310 deaths due to HIV in 2024.29,30 The health threats to PWH on antiretroviral treatment in India include conditions caused or worsened by tobacco including TB,29 cardiovascular disease,31 pulmonary disease, and cancer.32.

The objectives of this study were to understand the patterns of tobacco use and cessation treatment among adults initiating HIV care in a single clinic in Chennai, India.

Methods

We surveyed patients starting care for HIV to explore tobacco use, perceived risks and cessation treatment practices.33 Surveys were conducted using REDCap in-person in English, Tamil or Telugu and measured self-reported tobacco use, use of cessation treatment, and beliefs about tobacco use.34 Survey participants were asked to provide samples for biochemical measurement of tobacco exposure using urine cotinine with levels ≥ 50ng/ml indicating current tobacco use.35 Cotinine is the most widely used biomarker of tobacco exposure with a half-life of about 16 h, making it useful as a measure of use of nicotine containing products, including tobacco, in the past 7 days.35 Biomarkers of tobacco use increase the accuracy of assessing tobacco use and prior studies have demonstrated under and over-reporting of tobacco use among PWH.4,36 To explore attitudes and beliefs about cessation treatment a subset of survey participants and HIV care providers were invited to participate in key informant interviews.

The project originally proposed a follow-up visit at 3 months to measure changes in tobacco use after HIV care initiation. The follow-up data collection was disrupted for much of the sample by the COVID-19 pandemic. Here we report only on baseline data collected at time of HIV care initiation in a cross-sectional analysis.

Sample

Adult patients (≥ 18 years) with confirmed HIV diagnosis who were initiating HIV care were recruited for the study at a single HIV care site in Chennai, India from October 2019 to December 2021. Individual patients were excluded if they were not able to understand English, Tamil or Telugu, the limited resources of this project prohibited creation of study materials beyond these three most common languages. All patients presenting for care were screened for eligibility using standard procedures for study recruitment at the site. Among survey participants, a subset were invited to participate in qualitative interviews to explore treatment beliefs and experiences, purposively selected to include patients reporting current and former use. Written informed consent detailing the study purpose, logistics, and confidentiality was obtained prior to all study activities.

We explored treatment experiences and beliefs of HIV care providers by inviting all HIV counselors and physicians at the site to participate in interviews. Two group interviews with providers, one for counselors and one for physicians to limit impacts of hierarchy by role, were conducted in-person and audiorecorded. Verbal consent detailing the study purpose, logistics and confidentiality was obtained prior to provider interviews.

Survey Measures

The in-person patient survey covered demographics, socioeconomic status, behavioral health, self-reported tobacco product use,37 intentions to quit,38 prior quit attempts, nicotine dependence,39,40 perceived risks of tobacco use,41 and use of cessation treatments (see Supplemental File). Participants were remunerated with 120 INR (Indian rupees) for each survey, an amount aligned with local practice and designed to compensate for time and lost wages.

Biochemical verification of tobacco use

Cotinine is a metabolite of nicotine that can be measured in the urine, saliva, and serum of people exposed to nicotine through personal tobacco use, environmental tobacco smoke or nicotine medications. We opted to measure urinary cotinine as a non-invasive option deemed locally acceptable to the Chennai-based study team. Urine samples for biochemical verification were collected at the time of the in-person survey using clean, airtight storage containers, refrigerated at 2–8 °C, then transported under refrigeration to the certified analytic laboratory for urinary cotinine quantification by ELISA. A cut-point of 50 ng/ml was used to differentiate personal tobacco use from environmental tobacco exposure.35 Participants were remunerated 120 INR per sample.

Statistical analyses and sample size

We calculated the prevalence of current tobacco use by product type. Tobacco product type was categorized as smoked tobacco only, smokeless tobacco only, or dual use of smoked and smokeless tobacco products. Among individuals reporting current tobacco use we compared demographics, socioeconomic, nicotine dependence, health beliefs and treatment use by tobacco type using chi-square and t-tests. We calculated the proportion of people with cotinine-based evidence of current tobacco use and compared proportions by self-reported tobacco type using a chi-square test. The mean cotinine values were calculated by tobacco type and compared by self-reported tobacco type using ANOVA. The laboratory did not quantify values > 500 ng/ml so those values were recoded as 500 ng/ml for analysis.

Our sample size was dictated by practical considerations and based on precision of tobacco use prevalence measurement. Calculations were done prior to the global COVID-19 pandemic. Based on local pre-pandemic patient volume we expected to enroll about 180 new patients during the study period, among whom we estimated approximately 45% (adjusted for underreporting)4,5 would have cotinine levels consistent with current tobacco use. Qualitative data collection

We used semi-structured interview guides tailored for each key informant type (patient and providers). Guides explored experiences with tobacco use treatment and the integration of cessation treatment within HIV care. Patient interviews were conducted by Indian research team staff in patients’ preferred language at the same time as survey administration on the date of HIV care initiation visit. Interviews were audiorecorded, transcribed and translated for analysis. Individuals were compensated 200 INR for interview participation.

Provider groups were moderated in English by a single US research team member. Interviews were audiorecorded and transcribed by a professional transcription service. Providers were not remunerated for participation in qualitative interviews in accordance with clinic policies.

Qualitative analysis

Using a framework analysis approach,42 two researchers individually reviewed transcripts to become familiar with the data. The team met to generate a coding structure that combined a priori codes derived from the interview guide topics and emergent themes. The team iteratively reviewed raw data, comparing codes and revising code definitions to reach a final codebook.43 All transcripts were double coded with this codebook using Nvivo version 14.44 Codes were then summarized and salient themes were synthesized.

Results

Between October 2019 to December 2021 our study recruited 154 adults (n = 104 men, n = 50 women) new to HIV care. This represented 32.2% of all newly registered patients during the study period (n = 477; n = 276 men, n = 193 women, n = 8 transgender adults). There was a pandemic related pause in recruitment from March 2020 through April 2021. In this sample, 37.7% (n = 58; n = 49 men, n = 9 women) self-reported current tobacco use (13.6% [n = 21] smoking, 17.5% [n = 27] smokeless tobacco [SLT], 6.5% [n = 10] dual use) and 7.8% (n = 12) reported former tobacco use (5.8% [n = 9] former smoking, 1.3% [n = 2] former SLT, 0.6% [n = 1] former dual use) (Fig. 1). Among those currently using tobacco, most were male and achieved primary school education or less and there were no significant differences in demographic or socioeconomic characteristics by tobacco type (Table 1). Individuals who reported dual use most often reported daily use, while about half of those using only smoked or only smokeless products reported daily use (smoking: 52.4% [n = 11], SLT: 48.1% [n = 13], dual: 70.0% [n = 7]). Quit intentions and beliefs about harms of tobacco differed by tobacco type with lower quit intentions (p = 0.01) and fewer perceived harms to their own health (p = 0.01) among those reporting use of smokeless tobacco only compared to those using only smoked tobacco (Table 2). In terms of treatment, no one reported prior use of cessation medications and two participants reported counseling (smoking: n = 1; SLT: n = 1).

Urine cotinine levels varied significantly by self-reported tobacco use (smoking: 66.7% had cotinine ≥ 50ng/ml; SLT: 70.4% ≥50ng/ml, dual: 80.0% ≥50ng/ml, no current tobacco: 44.8% ≥50ng/ml, p = 0.02; Table 3). The cotinine verifications revealed false positives, defined as those who self-reported current tobacco use and had a cotinine level less than the cut point (50ng/ml) indicating no current use, and false negatives, nearly half of those who reported no current use had a cotinine level consistent with current use. The sensitivity of self-report of any current tobacco use (smoking, smokeless or dual use) in detecting cotinine above the cut-point indicating current use is 48.8% (41/84) in this population. Specificity is higher at 75.7% (53/70). Among those with detectable cotinine (≥ 10 ng/ml), those reporting current smoking or dual use had the highest average cotinine levels (smoking: 472.7 ng/ml, SLT: 363.4, dual: 462.0, no current use 221.3, p < 0.0001, Table 3).

Qualitative themes

Themes from our qualitative analysis of interview data from 18 individuals (n = 13 men, n = 5 women), including patients (n = 12) and HIV care providers (n = 6) highlighted barriers and facilitators to cessation treatment use.

Barriers and facilitators to tobacco use

Barriers to quitting included links between tobacco and work environments, using tobacco to pass the time, stay alert, or cope with stress: “Whenever I feel restless and something bothers me, I immediately tend to smoke.”

Some patients described social contexts promoting tobacco use. Being around others who smoke was a barrier to quitting or a trigger to relapse. One participant described having quit for 4 months: “my childhood friend…offered me a cigarette for which I replied that I had quit smoking 4 months back out of great difficulty, and I did not want to taste it again. Yet, he compelled me, and I could not resist myself and I took that cigarette.”

Barriers and facilitators to tobacco cessation

Participants explained factors influencing their decision to quit. Factors cited as reasons to quit included motivations to improve their health and perceived risks of ongoing use. Although, some described perceived risks as insufficient to overcome their dependence: “I know that my body is getting damaged. Having known that, I continue my coughing and smoke.” Others noted the cost of tobacco as a ‘loss’: “This is a loss to me in all aspects. Am I going to get any benefit with this smoking? Loss of money and loss of health. People rebuke me for smoking.” Participants also described beliefs about the central role of motivation in driving behavior change: “One can stop it if he wants to stop it. One won’t stop it if you don’t wish to stop. That’s all.”

Some patients described the impact of provider advice: “I had quit smoking due to the very reason that I have HIV. Doctor advised me on this. I stopped it because of my HIV.” Others described social desirability influencing their interactions providers about tobacco: “We tell you [provider] ‘Ok, we won’t’ and again we start smoking after you leave.”

Patients reported beliefs that the use of tablets and gums were ineffective: “There is a chewing gum has been released to help stop it…All of them do not work at all. They are just for name’s sake” and “I heard about some tablets, and I tried them too. The reason is I sit unengaged and alone due to the kind of business I do…[smoking] has become a routine to my brain.”

Health care provider practices and beliefs

Tobacco use counseling was offered by providers in HIV care. Providers described counseling about benefits of quitting for the patient and their environment: “starting from prevention of non-communicable diseases, other lung diseases; they’re saving the world from pollution; family members; and then about the cost.” Providers also described positive influences: “good counseling to the family members really helps them for better care at home…especially in the cultural setting like India, where people live in a nuclear family with extended family members. They’re part of the care team.”

For patients who need extra assistance beyond counseling in the HIV clinic, HIV providers described referrals to addiction specialty services. The HIV care providers did not routinely make recommendations for pharmacotherapy, “If they are not able to stop despite our counseling, then we refer to the psychiatrist.” Providers also described cost as a barrier, “If they have to take lots of medication, then [cessation pharmacotherapy] is a little expensive.”

Discussion

Tobacco use in multiple forms is common among adults initiating care for HIV in Chennai, India and few patients have experience with cessation treatment at time of HIV treatment entry. Engagement in HIV care offers an opportunity to intervene on tobacco that HIV care providers capitalize on, offering advice.38.

Barriers to cessation treatment delivery include underreporting of tobacco use, which is common and possibly driven by social desirability. As has been found in other LMIC settings, the sensitivity of self-report of tobacco use to detect current use was limited.36 While there is little evidence showing that biochemical verification impacts tobacco cessation treatment response,45 it may impact tobacco use disclosure. Biochemical verification may not be available in many care settings and this may undermine efforts to engage patients in cessation treatment. In the absence of biochemical verification, efforts to address stigma around tobacco use and to ensure cessation treatments are accessible to PWH could promote disclosure of tobacco use.

Perceived risks are associated with health behaviors including smoking.46 In surveys, these perceptions varied by tobacco product with those using smokeless tobacco reporting less interest in quitting and less perceived harm compared to those who smoke. Prior work has shown healthcare provider advice to quit is associated with increased perceived risk of continued smoking among PWH.47 Our work suggests that cessation support strategies that address risk perceptions may benefit from tailoring to tobacco product type. Our qualitative findings suggest that for some individuals, knowledge of health risks may not enough to overcome dependence, these individuals may benefit from pharmacotherapy or more intensive counseling to address nicotine dependence.

It is notable that a substantial number of individuals who self-reported tobacco use had cotinine levels less than the cut-point that indicates current use. The sensitivity of self-reported tobacco use compared to urine cotinine levels indicating current use may represent barriers to disclosure or differences in use patterns in this population, such as intermittent or non-daily use.48 In high income settings, data suggests people with HIV who smoke intermittently may have an easier time quitting, making them a promising group to engage with cessation services.49 Treatment strategies for PWH should consider varied tobacco use patterns.

Our study has limitations. The study period overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic, and this affected our ability to achieve our target sample size due to local competing priorities. Given the small numbers achieved with some groups statistics should be interpreted with caution. The small numbers may affect the precision of our estimates. The small number of women represented among our individuals reporting current tobacco use limits our interpretation of their use patterns and beliefs. This observational study sought to understand standard tobacco treatment practices for those initiating HIV care in a single site. Additional studies or multi-site studies are needed to understand this issue in India more broadly.

Conclusions

In this LMIC HIV care setting, smoking and smokeless tobacco use are common and biochemical verification revealed both underreporting and overreporting of use. Intermittent use may explain overreporting. Biochemical verification may be needed to accurately classify tobacco use in this setting. Use of evidence-based cessation treatment is rare. Strategies to identify people in HIV care who use tobacco in order to offer treatment should incorporate these results. Treatment approaches may be most effective if they incorporate these use patterns and health beliefs of patients in an LMIC context.
