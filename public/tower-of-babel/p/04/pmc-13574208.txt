Racial‐Ethnic Differences of Community Vulnerability Impact on Pediatric Thyroid Cancer Diagnosis and Surveillance
Authors: David Fei‐Zhang, Meredith Osmulski, Christopher Puchi, Abhinav Talwar, Jorge Gutierrez, John Maddalozzo, Jill D'Souza, Anthony Sheyn, Daniel Chelius, Jeffrey Rastatter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess racial‐ethnic differences of community‐level social vulnerability impact on pediatric thyroid cancer diagnosis and surveillance.Study DesignRetrospective cohort.SettingUnited States.MethodsThis retrospective analysis of 4324 pediatric thyroid cancer patients diagnosed between 1975 and 2017 from the Surveillance‐Epidemiology‐End Results (SEER) data set assessed overall social vulnerability aggregated from 15 different social vulnerabilities of health of socioeconomic status, minority‐language status, household composition, and housing‐transportation from the social vulnerability index (SVI). Social vulnerability scores of each category were matched to patients across US counties. Across racial‐ethnic strata (non‐Hispanic‐white/non‐white), age‐adjusted regression analyses assessed surveillance period, lymph node metastasis, and number of primary tumors with increasing social vulnerability scores.ResultsFor non‐Hispanic‐white (n = 2666) and non‐white (n = 1658) pediatric thyroid cancer patients, surveillance times at the lowest and highest‐total SVI levels were higher among white (162.92 and 108.02 months, respectively) compared to non‐white patients (117.64; 96.96). For lymph node metastasis, increasing SVI was associated with increased occurrence among non‐white pediatric thyroid cancer patients (odds ratio [OR] 1.16, 95% CI 1.05‐1.27) but not among white patients (0.96, 0.89‐1.03). For multiple primary tumors on presentation, no significant differences were observed across both race‐ethnic groups.ConclusionIncreasing community‐level social vulnerability correlated with worse pediatric thyroid cancer surveillance, especially for non‐white pediatric thyroid cancer patients in diagnostic and care outcomes. Through assessing upstream and downstream effects of social vulnerability on pediatric thyroid cancer patients, this multilevel approach allows nuanced considerations of one's individual racial‐ethnic identity and their community to elucidate how non‐clinical factors have disproportionate effects on the diagnosis of pediatric thyroid cancers.Keywords: epidemiology, ethnicity, papillary thyroid carcinoma, pediatric, race, SEER, social determinants of health, social vulnerability index, thyroid cancer

Thyroid cancer remains one of the most prevalent endocrine malignancies present in children and adolescents, with incidence ever‐increasing over the past two decades. 1 , 2 Despite recent plateaus in prevalence and decreased incidence among adults diagnosed with thyroid cancers, the rising trends in pediatric thyroid cancer have elicited investigations assessing which clinical and non‐clinical factors differentiate these contrasting patterns of thyroid cancer onset. 3

Among these trends, pediatric thyroid cancers have remained one of the pediatric cancers with excellent prognoses, with disease‐specific survival exceeding 95%. 4 However, pediatric thyroid cancers have long been observed to have a non‐insignificant rate of locoregional or distal recurrences and/or new primary occurrences, ranging from 15% to 30% in the United States. 5 Faced with such risks, pediatric thyroid cancer patients normally undergo years of longitudinal follow‐up using imaging, laboratory testing and sampling, and periodic physical exams to inform whether more acute interventions are needed. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 These differences become more stark when considering racial‐ethnic demographics, as a higher preponderance of more severe pediatric thyroid cancers has been observed to occur among non‐white pediatric thyroid cancer patients compared to white pediatric thyroid cancer patients. 10 , 11

Several investigated mechanisms underlie these care burdens for pediatric thyroid cancer management. Among them, pediatric thyroid cancer patients are predisposed to factors that hinder their diagnosis and surveillance. As a result, these factors can lead towards disparities in overall prognosis. Previously identified drivers, namely individual insurance status, income level, and race‐ethnicity, have been the principal focuses of past studies into disparity‐inducing risk factors. 11 , 12 However, considerations of a broader set of risk factors, including those affecting both one's surrounding community and their individual status, have seldom been investigated among pediatric thyroid cancer patients.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)'s social vulnerability index (SVI) comprises a comprehensive, area‐level‐based large‐data tool for assessing a wide variety of non‐clinical factors ranging from socioeconomic status (SES), minority‐language status (ML), household composition (HH), disability status, and housing‐transportation (HT). Applications of this tool have seen increasing utility among numerous investigations into disparities of adult and pediatric oncologic conditions, 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 including adult thyroid cancers. 21 However, its use within multilevel approaches combining both considerations of the community‐ and individual‐level factors has been seldom performed, 22 , 23 especially in the context of pediatric thyroid cancers.

Thus, utilizing the SVI, this investigation seeks to assess how community‐level factors and individual‐level racial‐ethnicity status are associated with differences in the diagnosis and surveillance of pediatric thyroid cancer patients. We hypothesize that non‐white pediatric thyroid cancer patients would experience greater decreases in surveillance and higher diagnostic burdens (eg, lymph node extension of disease and/or multiple primaries on initial presentation) compared to (non‐Hispanic) white pediatric thyroid cancer patients.

Methods

Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines were adhered to throughout this study. Institutional review board exemption by Northwestern University per the investigators' institutional policies was granted due to the analyses utilizing publicly available, deidentified databases (STU00216871).

Databases

The SVI is a large‐data demographic tool generated and validated by the CDC. It comprises 15 area‐level social determinants of health (SDoH) factors merged into 4 SDoH themes: SES (poverty, unemployment, income level, and high school diploma status), minority status‐language (ML—minoritized racial or ethnic group and English proficiency), HH (household members aged <17 and >65 years, disability status, and single‐parent status), and HT (multiunit structure, mobile homes, crowding, no vehicle, and group quarters). Relative scores across these themes and a weighted total score aggregating from these four themes are calculated to describe the variation of vulnerability derived from the community's composition of these SDoH‐factors. Based on CDC‐SVI documentation, 24 total and theme‐SVI scores are differentially weighted across these SDoH‐factors based on dynamic sociodemographic and geographical contexts across all 72,158 US Census tracts. These are then scaled from 0 to 1, with 0 representing the 0th percentile (ie, lowest social vulnerability) to 1 representing the 100th percentile (ie, highest social vulnerability) in comparison to all other census tracts. These census tract scores were then upcoded to county‐level scores through population‐adjusted averages, which are then rescaled from 0 to 1 in a similar fashion as aforementioned (Figure 1).

The National Cancer Institute Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program (NCI SEER) holds national data of patient variables, clinicopathological characteristics, and treatment—prognostic measures pertaining to various malignancies. In particular, “months under surveillance” represents the length of active follow‐up a patient receives for their primary malignancy until their last interaction with a healthcare professional, whether they were lost to follow‐up or had a confirmed death. “Months of survival” represents the period for which a patient with a confirmed death is actively surveyed until their timepoint of death. Within SEER, multiple staging standards were utilized and recoded into a SEER‐designated combined staging variable. Specifically, various values based on iterations of American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) standards that comprised the SEER‐specific combined staging variable of “advanced” or “late‐staging” comprised “stage IV” (and “stage III” for anaplastic histology types), “distant [expansion],” or “distal [expansion]” under a SEER‐specific combined variable to indicate “advanced” or “late‐staging,” with other values under these valuables being categorized as “non‐advanced” or “early‐earlier staging (stages I‐III).” Primary surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy occurrence represents whether patients received their respective intervention for their primary malignancy.

Population Definitions

Thyroid cancers were identified from the NCI‐SEER data sets based on International Classification of Diseases for Oncology version 3.8 (ICD‐O‐3) tumor site codes (C73.9). Inclusion criteria were pediatric patients (<20 years) diagnosed between 1975 and 2017 with available geographic information for data linkage between databases.

Statistical Analysis

The surveillance period was analyzed based on total SVI score and theme subscores. The patient sample cohort was split into equivalently sized quintiles based on these SVI scores. These relative SVI quintiles were then delineated by “less than 20,” “20‐39.99,” “40‐59.99,” “60‐79.99,” and “80‐99.99,” representing their relative percentiles (eg, patients with the lowest SVI scores are grouped into the <20 quintile group, representing the lowest vulnerability). Among these total and theme quintiles, differences between the mean months surveyed for the lowest and highest quintiles were calculated. Trend significance was assessed by linear regression across all data points against relative SVI quintiles for months' surveyed (ie, not a trend through the baseline descriptive values), and violin plots were generated to assess relative sample distributions for months' surveyed along the contour widths within each relative SVI quintile while simultaneously measuring the median, interquartile range (IQR), and 1.5 × IQR with its inner boxplot. Means, standard deviations (SDs), and ranges for months surveyed per quintile were also calculated. The proportion of patients who were alive or lost to follow‐up versus dead was calculated per quintile. Means, SDs, and ranges for actual total SVI scores and theme subscores were calculated per relative quintile group.

Presence of lymph node metastasis and number of primary tumors at the time of diagnosis were analyzed with age‐adjusted logistic regressions across relative SVI quintiles per SVI category. Reference levels for logistic regression characteristics were set at the lowest relative SVI quintile (representing the lowest social vulnerability level) for each total and theme‐SVI category, with comparators set at increasing ordinal levels of relative SVI quintiles (representing increasing social vulnerability).

These analyses were stratified along classifications of racial‐ethnic identity based on individual patients' identity of (non‐Hispanic) white or non‐white (ie, Hispanic, black, Asian, Pacific Islander, Native American, and Alaskan Islander) status.

Statistical significance was set as P < .05. Two‐sided P values were reported for analyses. Analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Surveillance

With increasing levels of overall social vulnerability, as measured by the SVI score, pediatric thyroid cancer patients of both (non‐Hispanic) white and non‐white race‐ethnicity showed significant decreases in the surveillance period. White patients experienced surveillance period decreases from 162.92 months in the lowest total SVI quintile (ie, lowest overall social vulnerability) to 108.02 months in the highest‐total SVI quintile (P < .001) (Figures 1 and 2). Non‐white patients experienced surveillance period decreases from 117.64 months in the lowest total SVI quintile to 96.96 months in the highest quintile (P < .001) (Figures 3 and 4). Based on the specific magnitudes of associations for each SVI‐theme, SES‐vulnerabilities were the strongest associative factors identified in these surveillance decreases.

Diagnosis

Among the diagnostic outcomes assessed, increasing social vulnerability was selectively associated with poorer prognostic indicators on initial presentation for certain racial‐ethnic patients. For lymph node metastasis, increasing overall social vulnerability was associated with occurrence on initial presentation among non‐white pediatric thyroid cancer patients (odds ratio [OR] 1.16, 95% CI 1.05‐1.27) but not among (non‐Hispanic) white patients (Table 2). These trends with overall vulnerability were most strongly contributed by specific vulnerabilities in ML, followed closely by HT and then SES‐factors (Table 2). For multiple primary tumors on presentation, increasing overall social vulnerability was not significantly associated with increased occurrence for either racial‐ethnic subgroup (Table 2).

Discussion

Using the SVI for evaluating community‐level SDoH‐factors, pediatric thyroid cancer patients demonstrated substantial decreases in surveillance and diagnostic outcomes with increasing overall social vulnerability. These detrimental trends were primarily observed in decreased longitudinal surveillance periods and increased presence of lymph node metastasis on initial presentation, especially among those of non‐white race‐ethnicity compared to those who were of non‐Hispanic white race‐ethnicity. In assessing these findings across both community‐ and individual‐level factors, this principal investigation utilized comprehensive, multilevel modeling to comprehensively and contextually assess how a broad spectrum of SDoH‐vulnerabilities impart their effects on pediatric thyroid cancer patient diagnostic and surveillance outcomes.

Racial‐ethnic differences in prognostic and care access have long been investigated across the pediatric head‐neck cancer spectrum, including those related to pediatric thyroid cancers. 11 , 16 , 21 Although such disparities have primarily been identified within non‐white patient populations, particularly those of black, Asian, or Pacific Islander populations, 25 , 26 , 27 a majority of these investigations solely focused on race‐ethnicity as the non‐clinical predictive factor of interest. Through our present investigation, our findings were able to reaffirm these differences among non‐white pediatric thyroid cancer patients compared to white pediatric thyroid cancer patients. Moreover, unlike prior investigations, our usage of multilevel modeling showcased how varied community‐level SES‐vulnerabilities were the main drivers of this exacerbated difference when compared to other non‐clinical factors. These differences were mainly observed within the surveillance period, as the significant trends when regressing from the lowest to highest social vulnerability groups saw significant decreases in surveillance length across both racial‐ethnic subgroups. Furthermore, non‐white‐papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) patients showed markedly lower absolute months of surveillance when compared with non‐Hispanic white‐PTC patients of similar vulnerability status. These differences per our thematic analyses showcased SES‐related vulnerabilities being the highest‐magnitude contributors to this overall detriment, suggesting that non‐white patient populations experienced the effects of SES‐related vulnerabilities more so than white patient populations in their longitudinal surveillance. Given that prior investigations did not utilize multilevel modeling approaches incorporating both individual‐ and community‐level approaches, their aforementioned interpretations of racial‐ethnic identity and its purported effects were not assessed jointly with other non‐clinical factors. As such, our methodological approach and findings for upstream clinical outcomes of the surveillance period addressed these gaps by introducing a quantitative comparison between traditionally understood SDoH‐factors and their interactions with each other in the social environment.

Alongside aspects of the surveillance period, our findings pertaining to specific diagnostic mechanisms, such as lymph node metastasis on initial presentation, reveal gaps in the lack of timely care access driven by community‐level vulnerability. As minoritized thyroid cancer patients have long been purported to experience increased delays in diagnosis and care, 28 , 29 , 30 our findings confer with these observations by showcasing increasing occurrence of metastasis with increasing overall community‐level vulnerability. However, these effects were only observed among those of non‐white racial‐ethnic identity. Furthermore, they were driven by community‐level vulnerabilities related to ML, alongside HT and SES‐related vulnerabilities. Taken as a whole, these findings elucidate how the exacerbated effects of worsening social vulnerability may be driven primarily by these three aforementioned vulnerabilities while selectively associating with certain pediatric thyroid cancer patients based on individual‐level non‐white racial‐ethnic identity. Such findings related to all three components have been reported in several independent investigations pertaining to adult and pediatric thyroid cancer patients. 11 , 25 , 28 However, our cohesive, objective analysis advances beyond these past studies by interactively contextualizing how factors convey their effects. Nevertheless, such studies have utility in discussing hypothesized vulnerability‐induced, disparity‐driving mechanisms that may underlie our quantitative observations. These include purported interactions between minoritized individuals being immersed in communal‐cultural attitudes that shape decision‐making to delay receiving care, living in less economically opportunistic areas resulting in decreased education within their communities and subsequent lesser symptom recognition, and/or having housing insecurity and increased commute times leading to lesser availability to book health appointments. 31 , 32

Despite these robust findings, our results must be understood within the confines of favorable prognostic states of PTCs. With disease‐specific mortality limited to annual estimates of 5% in the United States, 33 , 34 closing this remaining gap has long been a debate of advancing diagnostic tools and treatment efficacy, addressing non‐clinical disparity‐causing determinants, or assigning statistical error. 7 , 13 , 35 In addition, these rates of mortality pale in comparison to the vastly overwhelming mortality rates across other, more deadly pediatric cancers. 36 So, pediatric thyroid cancer investigations such as this one related to diagnostic and care access disparities can elicit questions of their (limited) clinical utility. As multiple investigations have observed that 60% to 90% of broader clinical outcomes are dictated by non‐clinical drivers, 37 discussions regarding pediatric thyroid cancer diagnosis and surveillance must revolve around similar principles. Furthermore, as a testament to the numerous, already‐available innovations in pediatric thyroid cancer treatment and diagnosis, 38 these estimates of mortality have largely progressed and plateaued due to well‐established clinical guidelines and practices to warrant better outcomes for a majority of pediatric thyroid cancer patients who access care. 5 Thus, the pediatric thyroid cancer patients included in this mortality estimate who fall through the cracks may largely be due to possessing the SDoH‐vulnerabilities identified in our study. In turn, characterizing and, subsequently, addressing these factors most pertinent to pediatric thyroid cancer surveillance and diagnosis remains the last hurdle towards addressing this mortality gap.

The strengths of our study include the use of validated, large‐data tools for quantifying community‐level SDoH‐vulnerabilities, a nationally representative patient cohort, and comprehensive, multilevel modeling for assessing interactions between individual‐ and community‐level variables. However, our study must be considered within its limitations. Retrospective cohort investigations such as ours, which utilize inferential analyses, cannot purport causality. Despite the extent of broader SDoH‐factors considered within the SVI, it does not include all possible factors of interest that would be associated with non‐clinical‐related disparities of pediatric thyroid cancer care and diagnosis. The selective skewing of pediatric thyroid cancer patient demographics, including a higher proportion of female and non‐Hispanic white populations, may not be generalizable to the outcomes of all pediatric thyroid cancer patients. Availability of variables that may be of interest within diagnosis, including delays of treatment from time of diagnosis, AJCC‐TNM staging, and others, was not available. In addition, other clinicodemographic variables beyond the ones described in Table 1 would be of interest in more comprehensive, multilevel modeling or similar approaches in future investigations. These would require proprietary or private data sets, which were not accessible during this current study due to financial constraints.

Conclusion

By incorporating both individual‐ and community‐level SDoH‐factors in multilevel analyses across a national patient cohort, this study assessed how SDoH‐vulnerabilities affected pediatric thyroid cancer patients in their surveillance time and diagnostic outcomes. Although SDoH‐vulnerabilities overall significantly affected both white and non‐white patients, non‐white patients were uniquely exacerbated through experiencing lower surveillance times and increased presence of lymph node metastasis on initial presentation. Furthermore, these differences conferred by increasing overall SDoH‐vulnerability were contributed by a multitude of community‐level factors, primarily among themes of SES, ML, and HT access. With these nuanced interactions and quantitative comparisons, these contextualized vulnerabilities present future opportunities to focus increasingly limited public health resources towards addressing the most pertinent disparities for pediatric thyroid cancer patients. Ultimately, addressing both the individual and their surroundings will be instrumental towards ushering meaningful improvements for the diagnosis and care of all pediatric thyroid cancer patients.
