Associations of arterial oxygen partial pressure with all‑cause mortality in critically ill ischemic stroke patients: a retrospective cohort study from MIMIC IV 2.2
Authors: Kangda Zhang, Fa Liang, Youxuan Wu, Xinyan Wang, Xuan Hou, Zihui Zhang, Yun Yu, Yunzhen Wang, Ruquan Han
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs a supportive treatment, the effectiveness of oxygen therapy in ischemic stroke (IS) patients remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the relationships between arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) and both consciousness at discharge and all-cause mortality risk in ICU IS patients.MethodsBlood gas measurements for all patients diagnosed with IS were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database. Patients were classified into four groups based on their average PaO2 during the first ICU day: hypoxemia (PaO2 < 80 mmHg), normoxemia (PaO2 80–120 mmHg), mild hyperoxemia (PaO2 121–199 mmHg), and moderate/severe hyperoxemia (PaO2 ≥ 200 mmHg). The primary endpoint was 90-day all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included the level of consciousness at discharge, assessed by the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and 30-day all-cause mortality. Multivariate Cox regression and Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis were used to investigate the relationship between mean PaO2 and mortality, and to assess the nonlinear association between exposure and outcomes.ResultsThis study included a total of 946 IS patients. The cumulative incidence of 30-day and 90-day all-cause mortality increased with decreasing PaO2 levels. RCS analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship between PaO2 and the risk of 30-day all-cause mortality (nonlinear P < 0.0001, overall P < 0.0001), as well as a nonlinear association between PaO2 and 90-day all-cause mortality (nonlinear P < 0.0001, overall P < 0.0001). The results remained consistent after excluding the small subset of patients who received reperfusion therapy. Sensitivity analysis indicated that the favorable impact on survival tends to increase with the extended duration of elevated PaO2.ConclusionsFor IS patients who do not receive reperfusion therapy or whose recanalization status is unknown, a lower PaO2 early during ICU admission is considered an independent risk factor for short-term and recent mortality. Adjusting respiratory parameters to maintain supraphysiological levels of PaO2 appears to be beneficial for survival, although this finding requires further validation through additional studies.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-024-02750-z.

Background

Cerebrovascular disease (CVD) represents a significant public health challenge worldwide. According to the 2024 Heart Disease and Stroke Statistics Report issued by the American Heart Association (AHA), ischemic strokes (ISs) account for 87% of all stroke types, imposing a substantial burden on patients as well as on the socioeconomic fabric of society [1]. It is estimated that intensive care intervention is required for approximately 24% of IS patients, a patient subset characterized by pronounced cognitive deficits and intricate clinical profiles [2].

Hypoxia is frequently observed in the initial days following acute stroke and is correlated with further deterioration of neurological function and an elevated overall mortality rate [3, 4]. Among patients receiving treatment in the intensive care unit (ICU), oxygen therapy is widely recommended [5]. However, the impact of oxygenation targets (arterial partial pressure of oxygen [PaO2], peripheral oxygen saturation [SpO2], or fractional inspired oxygen [FiO2]) on clinical outcomes in critically ill patients remains unclear. Furthermore, evidence from several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) suggests that neither lower nor higher oxygenation targets significantly affect mortality rates or hospital stays among ICU patients [6–8]. Nevertheless, clinicians must consider both disease and individual characteristics. When employing various methodologies to generate evidence-based individualized treatment effect estimates, significant variations in treatment outcomes among different patients are often observed, even in critical care trials where the average treatment effect is inconspicuous [9]. During IS, the interstitial oxygen tension in the ischemic core decreases to nearly zero, while tissue oxygen pressure in the penumbra may fluctuate unevenly due to changes in perfusion, underscoring the pivotal role of hypoxic injury in acute ischemic stroke (AIS). A retrospective cohort study indicated that exposure to hyperoxia among stroke patients receiving mechanical ventilation in the ICU may be independently associated with in-hospital mortality [10]. In this study, IS patients constituted only 19% of all stroke patients. PaO2 is a critical determinant of oxygen delivery; theoretically, even in situations where red blood cells cannot circulate normally through capillaries, enhancing oxygen delivery to underperfused areas can still improve the survival rate of distal embolized tissue [11]. On the other hand, supplemental oxygen may also pose potential harmful effects due to pulmonary toxicity and the generation of reactive oxygen species that inflict damage [12, 13]. Several studies have focused on the variations in PaO2, reporting inconsistent effects of hyperoxemia in subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) and traumatic brain injury (TBI) [14, 15]. However, in the field of IS, clinical research on ventilation management is limited in scale.

We hypothesize that outcomes for cerebral infarction patients admitted to the ICU, such as level of consciousness at discharge and short-term mortality, are associated with systemic oxygenation. Building on this premise, we identified specific PaO2 thresholds that can guide clinical management, thereby enhancing clinicians’ understanding of respiratory care in IS patients.

Methods

Data sources

This study is based on a real-world investigation utilizing the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, version 2.2) database, which contains information on 50,920 patients admitted to the ICU at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) in Boston, Massachusetts, between 2008 and 2019. The patient information in this database has been deidentified, thus obviating the need for informed consent. The author (K.-D.Z.) was granted access to the database in January 2024 (Certificate Number: 60525611). The statistical reporting of this study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Cohort selection

According to the ICD 9/10, we initially identified 3,818 ICU admission records with a diagnosis of cerebral infarction. For patients with multiple admissions, only their first ICU stay was considered, resulting in 2,602 adult IS patients. The exclusion criteria were: (1) lack of available arterial blood gas results on the first day of ICU admission, and (2) an ICU stay of less than 24 h. Data extraction and exposure definition

The data extracted for the target cohort, including demographics, comorbidities, laboratory test results, all blood gas analysis results within the first 24 h post-ICU admission, disease severity scores, treatment information, and survival outcomes, were obtained using Structured Query Language (SQL) in Navicat Premium software (version 15.0.12). Blood gas data, sourced from real-world clinical records, lacked standardized measurement timing and frequency. We calculated the average blood gas parameters for IS patients during their first 24 h in the ICU. We calculated the mean arterial blood gas parameters of IS patients during the first 24 h in the ICU and classified the patients into four PaO2 groups based on existing literature [16, 17]: (1) hypoxemia, PaO2 < 80 mmHg; (2) normoxemia, PaO2 80–120 mmHg; (3) mild hyperoxemia, PaO2 121–199 mmHg; (4) moderate/severe hyperoxemia, PaO2 ≥ 200 mmHg.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the 90-day survival status, encompassing both in-hospital and postdischarge data. Secondary outcomes included 30-day survival status and discharge level of consciousness defined by the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score corresponding to the last “charttime” in the “gcs” table of the MIMIC-IV database’s “public” module.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution are described as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed using one-way ANOVA; otherwise, the data are summarized as the median with interquartile range (IQR) and were analyzed with the Kruskal‒Wallis test. Categorical data are presented as frequencies (percentages) and were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Variables with missing rates less than 20% are imputed using the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) method and random forest, while those with missing rates exceeding 20% are excluded from the analysis.

In this study, we used the Kaplan‒Meier method to estimate survival probabilities and tested for significance using the log-rank test for survival data. To further investigate the association between PaO2 and 30-day or 90-day survival, we constructed a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model and calculated the hazard ratio (HR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI). Additionally, to analyze the linear relationship between PaO2 and GCS score at discharge, we employed a multivariable linear regression model and calculated the regression coefficients and their 95% CIs. Regarding model construction, we sequentially included age, sex, admission GCS, and variables that showed significant differences between groups in the univariate analysis as confounders, resulting in four sequentially adjusted models. Prior to conducting multivariate regression analysis, a variance inflation factor (VIF) diagnostic is performed; a VIF value exceeding 5 indicates significant collinearity, suggesting the removal of the variable from the model. Model 1 unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, admission GCS, and body mass index (BMI). Model 3 was further adjusted for pH, glucose, platelet, hemoglobin, mechanical ventilation, oxygenation index (OI), and creatinine, in addition to Model 2. Model 4 was additionally adjusted for hypertension, diabetes, myocardial infarction, dementia, and ICU length of stay in addition to Model 3. To enhance the interpretability of the coefficients, we treated every 30-mmHg change in PaO2 as an analytical unit. Additionally, to explore the potential nonlinear relationship between the average PaO2 in the first 24 h and the outcome variable, we employed the RCS method. This approach is commonly used to model continuous variables by dividing the data range into intervals and fitting polynomials within each interval. It allows for capturing complex patterns in the data while maintaining smoothness and minimizing the risk of overfitting. We selected 3 to 5 knots based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC) to balance model flexibility and overfitting risk. The significance of the nonlinear relationship was evaluated using the Wald test.

To conduct sensitivity analysis, this study excluded a small group of patients who received reperfusion treatment and replicated the previous analysis. Additionally, we extracted blood gas data from the first 6 h, 12 h, and18 hours after admission to the ICU, and calculate the average PaO2 for each time interval. After adjusting for covariates in Model 4, we further explored the association between PaO2 and the study outcome within different time periods. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient was used to assess the correlation between the mean PaO2 and the number of blood gas measurements and ICU length of stay. All data processing and analysis were performed using R version 4.3.3. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was interpreted as indicating statistical significance.

Results

Cohort characteristics

The comparison of outcomes and characteristics between the research and excluded cohorts is provided in Supplementary Table 1. Overall, the excluded cohort exhibited better survival outcomes.

A total of 946 IS patients were enrolled for the exposure of interest, with a median age of 69.2 years. The cohort included 494 males, with a median ICU length of stay of 6.2 days. A relatively low proportion of the total population received reperfusion therapy (1.1% received intravenous alteplase thrombolysis, 3% received endovascular treatment). The baseline characteristics of the four groups are shown in Table 1. Patients with lower PaO2 levels are more likely to have comorbidities such as diabetes, myocardial infarction, and dementia. On the other hand, patients with higher PaO2 levels are often hypertensive and receive mechanical ventilation.

Association of PaO2 with consciousness at discharge

The distribution of GCS scores at discharge showed no difference among the four groups (Fig. 2a). After adjusting for potential confounders, changes in PaO2 were not associated with consciousness levels at discharge (Model 1: β = 0, 95% CI: -0.07 to 0.08; Model 2: β = 0, 95% CI: -0.07 to 0.07; Model 3: β = 0.04, 95% CI: -0.05 to 0.13; Model 4: β = 0.02, 95% CI: -0.07 to 0.11) (Table 2).

Association of PaO2 with all-cause mortality

We conducted a detailed analysis of the impact of varying PaO2 levels on the short-term and recent mortality rates of IS patients. Specifically, in the hypoxemia group, the mortality rates were 40.5% and 50% at 30 and 90 days, respectively; in the mild hyperoxemia group, the rates were 37.5% and 43.6%, respectively; and in the moderate/severe hyperoxemia group, the rates significantly decreased to 22% and 29.3%, respectively (Fig. 2b-c). K‒M survival analysis confirmed the relationship between PaO2 levels and mortality rates. Figure 3 displays the survival curves for patients with different PaO2 levels, showing that IS patients with hyperoxemia group had significantly lower all-cause mortality rates at 30 days (log rank P = 0.001) and 90 days (log rank P < 0.001) than did those with hypoxemia. Multivariate Cox regression analysis further substantiated PaO2 as an independent risk factor for 90-day mortality. Across the different models, the relationship between PaO2 and 90-day mortality risk remained consistent (Model 1: HR = 0.93, 95% CI = 0.89 to 0.97; Model 2: HR = 0.93, 95% CI = 0.89 to 0.97; Model 3: HR = 0.90, 95% CI = 0.84 to 0.95; Model 4: HR = 0.91, 95% CI = 0.85 to 0.97). For 30-day mortality, the analysis also revealed a significant correlation between initial ICU day PaO2 and the 30-day mortality rate (Model 1: HR = 0.93, 95% CI = 0.89 to 0.98; Model 2: HR = 0.93, 95% CI = 0.88 to 0.98; Model 3: HR = 0.88, 95% CI = 0.82 to 0.95; Model 4: HR = 0.88, 95% CI = 0.82 to 0.95) (Table 2).

Sensitivity and correlation analyses

The relationship between PaO2 levels and clinical prognosis was analyzed across different time intervals. As shown in Fig. 5, with the expansion of the time window for high PaO2, the hazard ratio for patient survival associated with PaO2 gradually decreases. This finding suggests that prompt and sustained oxygen therapy may hold significant potential for improving short-term survival rates in patients. Furthermore, Spearman rank correlation analysis revealed a significant correlation between PaO2 and the frequency of blood gas measurements (r = 0.232, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 1). While this suggests that more frequent monitoring may be related to better PaO2 management, it is important to note that this correlation does not imply a causal relationship. However, no significant correlation was found between PaO2 and total ICU stay duration (p = 0.825). After excluding patients who underwent reperfusion therapy, the analysis results for all indicators remained consistent with the preliminary analysis (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Figs. 2–3). Modifications to the imputation methods similarly did not alter the original results (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

In this cohort of 946 IS patients, derived from the MIMIC-IV database, we found that early hyperoxemia was independently associated with lower short-term and intermediate-term mortality. The relationship between all-cause mortality risk and PaO2 levels was nonlinear. Maintaining early PaO2 ≥ 140 mmHg in clinical management effectively reduced mortality risk, offering a new therapeutic strategy for IS patients. These findings are particularly applicable to cerebral infarction patients who did not undergo reperfusion therapy.

The key to treating AIS lies in salvaging the ischemic penumbra and halting the ischemic cascade. Currently validated early recanalization treatments for AIS mainly include intravenous thrombolysis with rt-PA and thrombectomy [18, 19]. With the advent of new-generation thrombectomy devices and breakthroughs in imaging technology, the time window-based concept is gradually being replaced by an individualized tissue window approach, thereby expanding the number of patients who can benefit from endovascular therapy (EVT). However, significant disparities exist globally in access to advanced healthcare, with low- and middle-income countries facing more severe challenges in the availability of perfusion treatments than do high-income countries [20]. Using China as an example, the rate of intravenous thrombolysis for AIS was 5.64%, and the rate of EVT was 1.45% during the 2019–2020 period, which was significantly lower than that of the United States during the same timeframe [21, 22]. For IS patients who are unable to receive timely reperfusion therapy, oxygen therapy—being cost-effective, widely applicable, and easy to administer—holds promise as an important component of ICU care protocols. This is especially relevant in low-resource settings, where the need for feasible and scalable interventions is more pressing.

Normobaric hyperoxia (NBO) can increase PaO2, enhancing the fraction of dissolved oxygen in the arterioles and thereby providing oxygen delivery to the penumbra region. Liu et al. reported that interstitial oxygen tension in the penumbra can be maintained close to preischemic normal values following NBO therapy [23]. Other neuroprotective mechanisms of oxygen therapy supported by animal experiments include increased cerebral blood flow/volume (CBF/CBV) in the penumbra, attenuation of hyperglycolysis, improvement of cerebral cellular metabolism, stabilization of the blood‒brain barrier, reduction of peri-infarct depolarization, and amelioration of inflammation [24, 25]. Rapid elevation of oxygen levels can immediately improve ischemic-hypoxic conditions, leading to clinical symptom relief in a short period. However, prolonged hyperoxia can result in harmful consequences; therefore, intermittent hyperoxia is recommended [26].

Currently, the actual efficacy of oxygen therapy in treating AIS remains controversial, largely depending on whether the responsible vessel is recanalized. Earlier studies, conducted in the era before the widespread use of reperfusion therapies, indicated that patients with severe AIS benefited more from high-concentration oxygen delivered via a Venturi mask [27]. The advantage of oxygen is its ability to rapidly diffuse into ischemic tissue. Before reperfusion therapy, oxygen therapy serves as a nonpharmacological method to “freeze” the penumbra by increasing the tissue oxygen tension in this region [28]. In a pilot RCT, Li et al. demonstrated that initiating NBO treatment as early as possible before reperfusion could maximize protection of the ischemic penumbra, reduce infarct size, and improve short- and long-term neurobehavioral outcomes in AIS patients [29]. Nonetheless, oxygen therapy has a dual effect. High oxygen levels during reperfusion can induce reactive oxygen species (ROS), exacerbating reperfusion injury [30]. A retrospective study by Yu et al. revealed that lower preoperative SpO2 was closely associated with an increased risk of poor outcomes, while the effect of postoperative SpO2 exhibited the opposite trend [31]. In research by Farag et al., it was observed that AIS patients who underwent EVT and had a time-weighted average (TWA) SpO2 ≤ 98.15% had lower in-hospital mortality rates than those with TWA-SpO2 values between 96.95% and 98.17%, although this study did not delve into the impact of SpO2 on 3-month adverse outcomes [32]. It is important to note that SpO2 tends to stabilize when PaO2 approaches 100 mmHg. This stabilization implies that using SpO2 as a surrogate marker for arterial oxygen concentration may be insufficient to reveal the true impact of total oxygen content on IS patients. For IS patients undergoing EVT, baseline hyperoxia (PaO2 > 120 mmHg) upon ICU admission appears to be associated with poorer functional outcomes [33]. Given that reoxygenation following cerebral ischemia is a prerequisite for ROS-induced neuronal damage, it is noteworthy that in this study, the majority of IS patients did not receive reperfusion therapy. Consequently, the potentially harmful effects of oxygen therapy are unlikely to manifest in this cohort. The outcomes following acute brain injury exhibit a U-shaped relationship with systemic oxygenation, where hyperoxia demonstrates a broader range of PaO2 levels before neuronal injury occurs compared to hypoxia [16]. In this cohort, a PaO2 range of 140 to 240 mmHg may be recommended for improved survival.

The efficacy of oxygen therapy is limited by the timing of the intervention. Animal studies have shown that delayed oxygen intervention does not confer neuroprotection [34]. In a pilot study, IS patients who had not received intravenous thrombolysis were randomly assigned to receive either high-flow NBO therapy (45 L/min) for 8 h or standard treatment. The primary endpoint was the infarct core assessed by diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI). The results indicated that patients in the high-oxygen therapy group had significantly reduced lesion volumes at 4 h, but no significant changes were observed at subsequent time points [35]. A systematic review did not confirm significant effects of NBO on functional recovery or mortality in patients with acute stroke (both ischemic and hemorrhagic). Notably, in a meta-analysis, only 2.3% of participants received NBO within 12 h of symptom onset [36]. The ongoing PROOF trial (NCT03500939) aims to assess the efficacy of NBO (≥ 40 L/min via face mask) in patients with hyperacute stroke (within 6 h of symptom onset), with the potential to elucidate its role as a neuroprotective bridge to reperfusion therapy [37]. If positive, early oxygen therapy will become an integral component of stroke unit care, thereby enhancing the overall quality of acute care for stroke patients. In this study, considering the variability in oxygen supplementation during ICU care and the differences in the duration of hyperoxia, we performed a time-segmented sensitivity analysis. Although the exact time from symptom onset to ICU admission remains unclear, preliminary evidence suggests that early oxygen therapy in the ICU is highly valuable for IS patients, and this value increases with the expansion of the time window.

Leveraging the extensive real-world data provided by the MIMIC-IV database may offer new insights into oxygen therapy for IS. However, our study was impacted by a high exclusion rate due to the absence of arterial blood gas data. Notably, the excluded patients demonstrated higher rates of reperfusion therapy, shorter ICU stays, higher GCS scores, and better survival outcomes. This highlights the importance of timely blood gas monitoring and optimized oxygen therapy strategies, particularly for critically ill stroke patients, to improve clinical outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective study, it is inevitably subject to selection bias and information bias. Although we adjusted for relevant confounding factors, the MIMIC-IV database is not specifically designed for neurocritical care patients, and some variables significantly associated with IS prognosis, such as infarct core volume and NIHSS score at admission (stroke severity), were not recorded. Variations in clinical management at institutions other than BIDMC may also affect the generalizability of the study findings. Second, blood gas analysis measurements lacked standardization and continuity, with varying measurement times and frequencies among patients, potentially impacting the results. Third, the choice of imputation method may impact the results, although we accounted for two different approaches in our analysis. Approximately 20% of the data on inhaled oxygen concentration is missing, hence the calculation of the OI heavily relies on statistical imputation techniques. PaO2 directly reflects the actual oxygen content in the blood, whereas the OI assesses lung oxygenation efficiency by considering both ventilator settings and blood oxygenation status. Fourth, numerous factors influence long-term outcomes, and this study revealed only an association, not causation, between PaO2 levels and short-term mortality in IS patients admitted to the ICU. Therefore, designing a prospective study that stratifies patients based on the success of reperfusion therapy to evaluate the causal relationship between PaO2 levels and stroke mortality would be valuable.

Conclusions

In patients with cerebral infarction of unknown recanalization status, early elevation of PaO2 is associated with a lower risk of mortality at 30 and 90 days. Therefore, dynamic monitoring of blood gas analysis to adjust oxygen therapy may help improve short-term and recent survival in these patients.
