Impact of socio-economic factors and lifestyle behaviours on the quality of life of diabetes patients in Markurdi Benue state Nigeria
Authors: Oluwafunke O. Akinbule, Faith Nguvan Tor-Anyiin, Sultan A. Adenusi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Diabetes mellitus significantly affects quality of life (QoL). Lifestyle factors, including diet and physical activity, play a crucial role in managing the disease. This study investigated the impact of socio-economic and lifestyle behaviors on the QoL of diabetes patients. A cross-sectional study was conducted among 571 type-2 diabetics. Data were collected on socio-economic factors, lifestyle behaviours (smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, dietary habits) using standard procedures. Anthropometry, blood pressure, glycated hemoglobin, and blood glucose levels were determined using standard procedures. QoL was assessed using the Audit of Diabetes-Dependent QoL questionnaire. Pearson’s Chi-square and analysis of variance were conducted at p < 0.05. A high prevalence of poor dietary habits (93.9%), physical inactivity (50.4%), and abdominal obesity (16.6%) were observed. Additionally, 30.0% and 8.2% respondents were alcohol drinkers, and tobacco smokers, respectively. Diabetes had a significant negative impact on QoL, with 39.6% of respondents reporting a poor QoL. Treatment options, dietary habits, and physical activity levels were found to be significant (p < 0.05) predictors of QoL. The greatest negative impact of diabetes was on respondents’ “freedom to eat”. Improving physical activity and dietary habits can significantly enhance the QoL. Tailored interventions targeting lifestyle factors are essential for optimizing disease management and improving patient outcomes.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus, a non-communicable disease, has emerged as a significant global health burden with increased risk of mortality and morbidity, particularly in developing countries1,2. The International Diabetes Federation estimates a staggering 463 million adults worldwide living with diabetes, with 19.4 million prevalence in sub-Saharan Africa and 1,702,900 cases in Nigeria3. In 2019, about 3.9 million Nigerian adults lived with diabetics and 6.0 million adults is projected to have diabetes by 20244.

Several lifestyle factors, including unhealthy diets, sedentary lifestyles, alcohol consumption, smoking, and obesity, have been identified as key contributors to diabetes risk4–6. While moderate alcohol intake has been linked to a reduced risk of type 2 diabetes (T2DM)7,8, smoking can negatively impact insulin sensitivity9,10. Additionally, unhealthy dietary habits and a sedentary lifestyle increase the risk of obesity, a primary cause of T2DM11,12. Similarly, unhealthy dietary habits have been linked to poor glycemic control13,14.

Beyond its physiological effects, diabetes can significantly impact patients’ quality of life (QoL). Individuals with diabetes often face physical, psychological, and social challenges that can negatively influence their overall well-being2,15. Given the chronic nature of T2DM, improving QoL is a critical aspect of treatment and care16–18.

QoL, as defined by the World Health Organization, encompasses an individual’s perception of their position in life within the context of their culture and value system19. It involves a complex interplay of physical health, psychological status, personal beliefs, social relationships, and other factors. Assessing QoL can provide valuable insights into patients’ ability to cope with diabetes20.

To effectively evaluate QoL in diabetes patients, disease-specific instruments are more effective than generic health-related measures21. While previous studies often relied on generic tools such as health-related QoL (HRQoL), and World Health Organisation QoL (WHOQoL) questionnaires these instruments may not fully capture the unique challenges of living with a chronic disease like diabetes.

For this reason, this study will utilize the Audit Diabetes-Dependent QoL (ADDQoL) questionnaire. As a diabetes-specific instrument, the ADDQoL is designed to directly assess the impact of diseases on a patient’s QoL. It moves beyond general health status to evaluate the specific burdens and benefits of living with diabetes. The ADDQoL focuses on how diabetes affects various aspects of daily life, including personal relationships, social activities, work, and feelings about the future. By concentrating on the disease-specific context, the ADDQoL provides a more nuanced and clinically relevant picture of a patient’s QoL, making it a more appropriate and sensitive tool for this study.

Despite this, there is a dearth of information on the influence of socioeconomic characteristics (specifically income), obesity, and treatment options on the QoL of diabetes patients in Nigeria. This information is necessary to assist health professionals in the efficient management of T2DM and develop targeted interventions to improve patient QoL. This study aims to address this knowledge gap by investigating these factors in Makurdi, Benue State.

Methodology

Study design

The study adopted a cross-sectional research design.

Study location

This study was conducted in Makurdi, Benue State. Makurdi is the state capital of Benue state, located in central Nigeria (North Central region), and part of the Middle Belt region of central Nigeria. Makurdi has two public tertiary hospitals (Federal Medical Centre, Makurdi, and Benue State University Teaching Hospital, Makurdi) and one secondary hospital (General Hospital, Makurdi, Benue State).

The minimum sample size was 438. It was rounded up to 571 to cater for attrition.

Sampling technique

The population size of diabetes patients in the three hospitals is 934. The number of respondents selected from each hospital was proportionate to the population size. Federal Medical Centre, Markurdi has a population of 564, Benue State University Teaching Hospital (BSUTH) has a population of 204 and General Hospital, Makurdi (GHM) has a population of 166 diabetic patients receiving treatment at these hospitals during the period of data collection. Respondents who were willing to participate in the study were selected using a simple random sampling technique. Patients who attended diabetes clinic on the day of data collection were asked to pick a ballot of 0 and 1. Only respondents who picked the ballot with 1 participated in the study. A total of 571 respondents were selected from the three hospitals. Three hundred and forty-five diabetic patients were selected from FMCM, 123 diabetic patients from BSUTH, and 101 diabetic patients from GHM.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible patients for this study were only male and female adults 18 years of age and above, out-patients, diagnosed with diabetes mellitus and receiving treatments in the selected hospitals for at least 6 months, not suffering from debilitating or life-threatening complications, were cognitively intact (as determined by their medical records), and willing to participate in the study. Respondent who did not give their consent, and had hearing problems were excluded from the study.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from the Federal Medical Centre, Makurdi, Benue State (FMH/FMC/HREC/108/VL. 1), BSUTH/MKD/HREC/2022/169) and Benue State Hospital Management Board (HMB/OFF/215/VOL. II/535). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Informed consent

Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s) before data collection. Only respondents who consented participated in the study. Data collected were used strictly for research purposes.

Respondents’ personal and clinical data

A semi-structured interviewer-administered questionnaire was used to obtain information on Patients’ characteristics. Respondents’ clinical characteristics were extracted from their case notes.

Biochemical data

The blood pressure (BP) of respondents was measured using a mercury sphygmomanometer KENZ model 605P on the left arm in a sitting position. It was measured twice at five-minute intervals, and the average was determined. The random blood glucose level of respondents was assessed using Accu-Check Active glucometer, model no: HM100005. The glycated hemoglobin was measured using the Tosoh G7 HPLC analyzer which was calibrated using the diabetes control and complications trial (DCCT) standard with a coefficient of variations < 2.5%. Blood pressure (systolic/diastolic pressure) was classified as normal (< 120/<80mmHg), pre-hypertension (120–139/80-89mmHg), hypertension stage 1 (140–159 / 90-99mmHg) and hypertension stage 2 (> 160/>100mmHg) according to the Joint National Committee on Prevention, Detection, Evaluation, and Treatment24, fasting blood glucose was classified as “Normal” (70-99 mg/dl), “Prediabetes” (100-125 mg/dl) and “Diabetes” (126 mg/dl or above)25 while the glycated hemoglobin was classified as normal (< 5.7%), pre-diabetes (5.7–6.4%) and diabetes (≥ 6.5%)26.

Respondents’ lifestyle behaviours

The lifestyle behaviours assessed were smoking patterns, alcohol drinking patterns, physical activity levels, dietary habits, overweight/gross obesity, and abdominal obesity.

Smoking and alcohol drinking status

The smoking and alcohol drinking patterns were assessed using a semi-structured interviewer-administered questionnaire.

Physical activity level

The physical activity of the patients was assessed using a 27-item International Physical Activity Questionnaire. The questionnaire has domains on job-related; transportation; housework, house maintenance, and caring for the family; and recreation, sport, and leisure-time physical activity. The questionnaire was scored based on a continuous variable score expressed as Metabolic Equivalent (MET) in minutes per week. MET level* minutes of activity* events per week. MET levels used were 3.3 METs (Walking), 4 METs (Moderate activity), and 8 METs (Vigorous activity). Total MET-min/week = (Walk METs*min*days) + (Mod METs*min*days) + Vig METs*min*days) and categorized as inactive (< 600 MET-min/week), minimally active (600–2999 MET-min/week) and heap active (> 3000 MET-min/week)27.

Dietary habit

Respondents’ dietary habit was assessed using a 4-point scale 18-item validated questionnaire with a reliability coefficient of 0.6628. Respondents were asked to identify how often a particular food item is eaten. These questions included how often the subject consumed foods from each portion of the food pyramid, as well as the consumption of beverages, and vitamin and mineral supplements. Food intake, dieting, and skipping meals were also examined. For this section, answers ranged from always (4) to never (1). Question numbers 2–4, 8–9, 14–15, and 17 were reversed scored. This means that if the subject answered the question with a 1, then he/she was awarded 4 points. All other questions were scored according to their value. Items were scored by summing up responses. The obtainable score ranged from 18 to 72. The higher the scores, the better the eating habits. The percentage from the highest score obtainable was calculated and classified as “excellent” (76–100%), “good” (51–75%), “fair” (26–50%) and “poor” (0–25%).

Overweight/obesity and abdominal obesity

Respondents’ body weight, height, and waist circumference were measured using standard procedures29. Body mass index was calculated and classified as > 25.0 kg/m2 and ≥ 25.0 kg/m2 using the WHO classification29. Waist circumference was used to assess abdominal obesity (i.e. accumulation of fat around the abdominal region). Waist circumference was classified as “Normal” ( below/equal to 102 cm and below/equal to 88 cm) and “High” (> 102 cm and > 88 cm) for males and females, respectively29.

Quality of life

Quality of life (QoL) of patients was assessed using a 21-item validated Audit of Diabetes-Dependent QoL (ADDQoL) questionnaire. The questionnaire comprises two general questions and 19-domain questions on the impact (ranging from − 3, the maximum negative impact of diabetes to + 1, the positive impact of diabetes) and importance (ranging from 3, very important to 0, not important at all) of diabetes. The 19-domain questions include: questions were leisure, working life, journeys, holidays, physical health, family life, friendship and social life, personal relationships, sex life, physical appearance, self-confidence, motivation, people’s reactions, feelings about the future, financial situation, living conditions, dependence on others, freedom to eat, and freedom to drink.

Each item was scored as Weighted impact score = impact rating (− 3 to + 1) * importance rating (0–3) = − 9 (maximum negative impact of diabetes) to + 3 (maximum positive impact of diabetes). The lower the WI score, the worse the aspect of life within the scope of a given domain. The average value of the weighted impact (AWI) score is also calculated for the whole scale. The AWI score is derived by dividing the sum of the weighted ratings by the number of applicable domains30.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Means, standard deviation, and interquartile range were used for continuous variables. Pearson’s Chi-square and t-test were used to determine significant associations and differences between variables at p =< 0.05. All statistical analysis was carried out using statistical product and service solution version 25.

Lifestyle behaviours and biochemical parameters of the respondents

About 8.2% of the patients were current smokers and 26.3% were lifestyle smokers. Also, 30.6% of the patients were current alcohol drinkers while 23.5% were lifetime alcohol drinkers. While current smoking was more prevalent in males (9.3%), lifestyle smoking was more prevalent in females (27.9%). On the other hand, current and lifestyle alcohol drinking (32.9 and 23.6%) was more prevalent in males. About 93.9% of the patients had poor dietary habits, which were more prevalent in females, while 6.1% had fair dietary habits which were more prevalent in females. Also, only 15.8% had heap physical activity level, which was predominant in males while about half (50.4%) were physically inactive, which was predominant in females. Only abdominal obesity significantly differed among the male and female respondents (p = 0.00). The fasting blood glucose level of the respondents indicated that 97.4% had diabetes while the Hemoglobin A1C indicated that 93.7% of the respondents had diabetes. In addition, 19.3% and 79.2% of patients had stage 1 and 2 hypertension, respectively based on their systolic pressure, while 7.4% and 92.5% had stage 1 and 2 hypertension, respectively based on their diastolic pressure. Similarly, 18.7% were overweight and 16.6% had abdominal obesity. Overweight and abdominal obesity were more prevalent in females (Table 3). About one-third of the respondents skip meals, 18.7% consume vegetables, 17.2% consume fruits, and 3.7% consume dairy products at least 3 times per week. On the other hand, about 22.5% consumed breakfast, 92.6% consumed carbonated beverages, and 99.2% consumed high-calorie snacks less than 3 times a week (Fig. 1).

Overall quality of life of diabetic patients by gender

Table 4 shows the overall quality of life of the respondents. T2DM had negatively impacted the health of 39.6% of the respondents, especially the male respondents. About 93.0% believed that their lives would have been much better if they had not had T2DM. The average ‘present QoL’ and weighted impact score for this population were − 0.23 and − 2.67, respectively suggesting a mean response of ‘neither good nor bad, and very much better quality of life if they did not have diabetes. There are significant differences (p < 0.00) between the present quality of life of male and female respondents, and their belief regarding their QoL of they did not have diabetes.

Quality of life of diabetic patients by gender

Table 5 shows the quality of life of diabetic patients by gender. In general, T2DM had the greatest negative impact (-2.6 ± 0.6) on ‘the freedom to eat, followed by ‘the financial situation’(-2.3 ± 1.0), while reactions of other people (-1.1 ± 1.1). Among the male respondents, T2DM had the greatest negative impact (-2.6 ± 0.6) on ‘freedom to eat, followed by ‘freedom to drink’(-2.5 ± 0.6) and financial situation (-2.3 ± 1.0), while reactions of other people (-1.0 ± 1.1). Among the female respondents, T2DM had the greatest negative impact (-2.6 ± 0.6) on ‘freedom to eat, followed by ‘financial situation’(-2.4 ± 1.0) while reactions of other people (-1.2 ± 1.1).

In general, after considering weighting, freedom to eat (-7.1 ± 2.2) was the most affected QoL domain, followed by the financial situation (me-6.8 ± 3.3) while reactions of other people (-2.8 ± 3.2) was the least. However, freedom to eat (7.1 ± 2.5) was the most affected QoL domain, followed by freedom to drink (-6.7 ± 2.6) while reactions of other people ( -2.7 ± 3.4) was the least among male respondents. On the other hand, freedom to eat (-7.1 ± 2.4) was the most affected QoL domain, followed by the financial situation (-6.9 ± 3.2), while reactions of other people (-3.3 ± 3.0) was the least in females.

In general, “freedom to eat” (2.7 ± 0.5), “living condition” (2.7 ± 0.5), and “financial situation” (2.7 ± 0.4) were rated most important while personal relationship (2.0 ± 1.0) and sex life (2.0 ± 1.1) were rated least important. However, in females, financial situation (2.8 ± 0.4) was rated most important.

Discussion

The study revealed that a significant proportion of respondents were older adults and a slight male predominance. This is consistent with established epidemiological patterns of type 2 diabetes mellitus globally and within sub-Saharan African4,31. T2DM is a disease of aging, and its rising prevalence is linked to demographic transitions in many countries32. The finding that socioeconomic factors were not significantly associated with gender in our cohort is particularly interesting. While many studies highlight gender-based socioeconomic disparities as drivers of health outcomes33,34, our results suggest that in this population, the burden of diabetes and its management challenges may transcend simple socioeconomic divides, pointing towards other contributing factors like cultural norms or gendered health-seeking behaviors.

The significant gaps in patient education and self-management skills observed in our study align with the known challenges of chronic disease care in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)35,36. The healthcare systems in these regions often struggle with providing continuous, patient-centred care, leading to a reliance on acute interventions rather than long-term management strategies. The alarmingly low rate of social support is a critical finding that warrants further investigation, as a robust body of literature demonstrates the pivotal role of family and community support in effective diabetes self-management37. A compelling finding from our study was that females had significantly higher rates of counseling and self-management skills compared to males. This is a crucial observation, as some research from similar settings suggests that women may be more engaged in healthcare seeking and adherence to treatment protocols for chronic conditions38,39. This contrasts with other studies that have found women to be disadvantaged in accessing healthcare, particularly for non-communicable diseases40,41. Our results suggest a need for gender-specific health promotion strategies to address the potential disengagement of male patients from essential self-care practices.

The high prevalence of sedentary work, poor dietary habits, and physical inactivity in our cohort is consistent with global epidemiological data linking these lifestyle factors to the burden of T2DM42,43. The shift away from traditional, healthier diets towards Westernized, high-calorie food patterns is a recognized phenomenon in many developing nations undergoing urbanization and economic change44.

Our finding of a significantly higher prevalence of abdominal obesity in females is particularly relevant. This observation is supported by a growing body of research identifying abdominal obesity as a key risk factor for cardiometabolic disease, with a potentially different distribution and impact between genders45,46. This highlights the need for targeted, gender-specific interventions for weight management.

The biochemical data, showing very high rates of uncontrolled diabetes and hypertension, corroborates findings from other studies in resource-limited settings where monitoring and medication adherence are often poor46,47. This underscores the urgent need for health system strengthening to improve access to medication and routine monitoring.

Our finding that diabetes has a significant negative impact on patient QoL is consistent with extensive global literature on the subject48–51. However, our study provides a more granular insight by identifying “freedom to eat” as the most negatively impacted QoL domain. This finding is mirrored in other studies using similar QoL instruments, which emphasize the psychological burden of constant dietary restrictions and the loss of social enjoyment associated with food52,53. The significant impact of the financial situation on QoL is also a well-documented challenge for diabetic patients, particularly in contexts where healthcare costs are a substantial out-of-pocket expense54. Finally, the strong association between patient education, self-management skills, and QoL in our study reinforces the central role of comprehensive patient-centered care. As shown in other research, empowering patients with knowledge and skills can improve not only clinical outcomes but also their overall perception of well-being55.

Implication of findings

This study highlights the need to Implement comprehensive education and support programs to address the lack of diabetes self-management skills, carbohydrate counting knowledge, and counselling. It also highlights the need to develop tailored interventions to address the specific needs of individuals with diabetes, considering factors such as age, gender, and socioeconomic status. Promotion of lifestyle changes, including healthy eating, regular physical activity, and smoking cessation, might improve glycemic control and QoL. In addition, the provision of comprehensive care that addresses both diabetes and associated complications such as hypertension is essential in the management of diabetes. Furthermore, the information provided by this study can be used to advocate for policies that support access to affordable healthcare, diabetes education, and healthy lifestyle resources.

Suggestion for further study

Further research is needed to explore the long-term impact of these factors on QoL, investigate the effectiveness of different interventions, and identify additional factors that may influence the QoL of individuals with diabetes.
