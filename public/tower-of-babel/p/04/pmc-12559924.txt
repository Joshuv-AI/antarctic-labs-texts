The Relationship of Prior Concussion and Contact Sport Exposure With Cortical Macro and Microstructure
Authors: Timothy B Meier, L Tugan Muftuler, Bryna D Goeckner, Nicholas Weyenberg, Daniel L Huber, Lezlie Y España, Anjishnu Banerjee, Andrew R Mayer, Benjamin L Brett
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChanges in cortical gray matter are a key feature of neurodegenerative diseases that have been linked with concussion and repetitive head impacts (RHIs). Prior evidence implicates prior concussion and RHI in reduced cortical thickness or volume in temporal and frontal regions, with results largely restricted to older retired contact sport athletes. Fewer studies have investigated similar associations in younger athletes or applied approaches to capture more subtle differences in gray matter earlier in the lifespan. The current study assessed the association of concussion and RHI with cortical macrostructure (cortical thickness, cortical surface area), and cortical microstructure (cortical mean diffusivity), the latter of which has been suggested to be an earlier marker of gray matter abnormalities in neurodegenerative diseases. A total of 207 otherwise healthy collegiate‐aged athletes completed semistructured interviews for concussion and sport participation history, as well as a magnetic resonance imaging session including anatomical and diffusion imaging (N = 205 with available diffusion data). Cortical surface area and cortical thickness were estimated using FreeSurfer; cortical mean diffusivity was calculated with correction for partial volume. Bayesian multilevel modeling was conducted on regions of interest derived from Desikan–Killiany Atlas parcellations to determine the association of the number of prior concussions and RHI (included in the same models) with each metric, controlling for sex, age, and intracranial volume (area only). There was strong evidence for a positive association between the number of prior concussions and cortical mean diffusivity throughout most of the cortex. In addition, there was strong evidence for a positive association of the number of prior concussions with cortical surface area across several regions. For cortical thickness, there was strong evidence of inverse associations between the number of prior concussions and anterior and medial temporal cortical regions only. In contrast, only weak to no evidence of associations between years of contact sport exposure, a proxy for RHI, and any cortical surface metric was observed. These results demonstrate that cortical diffusivity may represent a more sensitive metric of subtle, early structural changes associated with repetitive neurotrauma, and highlight the importance of efforts to reduce concussion risk in sport.Keywords: cortical mean diffusivity, cortical surface area, cortical thickness, mild traumatic brain injury, sport

Introduction

The cumulative and chronic effects of concussion, or mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI), and exposure to nonconcussive repetitive head impacts (RHIs) on brain health are of major public health concern given the large number of individuals that participate in contact and collision sports (Brett et al. 2025; Manley et al. 2017). Changes in cortical gray matter (GM) (i.e., atrophy) are one of the key features of neurodegenerative diseases to which concussion and RHI have been linked, including all‐cause dementia and chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) (Alosco et al. 2021; Bieniek et al. 2021; Ferreira et al. 2017). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides the opportunity to assess cortical GM structure in vivo, potentially serving as a biomarker for the identification of individuals with or at risk for these adverse outcomes observed in subsets of former athletes.

Multiple prior studies have reported abnormalities in cortical GM structure in retired contact sport athletes such as reduced cortical thickness (CTh) or volume in the medial and anterior temporal lobes, frontal cortex, and insula (Adler et al. 2018; Alosco et al. 2021; Arciniega et al. 2024; Lepage et al. 2019; Lesman‐Segev et al. 2019; Recht et al. 2024; Tremblay et al. 2013); though not all studies have observed consistent results or evidence of any differences (Turner et al. 2024; Wojtowicz et al. 2018; Zivadinov et al. 2018). Fewer studies have investigated if similar associations are present in younger, active athletes. American football players with a history of concussion had thinner ventromedial prefrontal cortex relative to non‐football controls and thinner right central sulcus and precentral gyrus relative to football players without prior concussion (Meier et al. 2016). Widespread thinner cortex, including frontal, temporal and insular cortices, was observed in male Australian football players relative to non‐collision sport athletes (Major et al. 2021), and a recent study reported that active elite rugby players had thinner cortex in the bilateral precentral sulcus relative to controls (Parker et al. 2023). As in the findings from retired athletes, not all studies have uniformly observed smaller/thinner volume/cortex (Brett et al. 2020; Churchill et al. 2017; Mills et al. 2020).

Despite this existing work, several gaps in knowledge still exist. First, most prior work has focused on group comparisons of structural measures between contact sport athletes with concussion history and healthy controls or noncontact athletes and has been unable to delineate the potential cumulative and chronic effects of frank concussion from exposure to RHI throughout the lifespan. Second, most existing studies have been limited to either active or retired male athletes, typically football players, and it is, therefore, unknown the extent to which findings to date are generalizable to female athletes or athletes from other sports. Third, most studies have studied cortical volume or thickness. Cortical volume is comprised of CTh, which reflects the amount of neuropil and/or number of cells, and cortical surface area (CSA), which reflects the number of cortical columns (Rakic 1988), and thus potentially captures different pathological and developmental effects (Panizzon et al. 2009). A limited number of studies have investigated CSA differences in the chronic stage following a single mTBI (Bajaj et al. 2018; Dall'Acqua et al. 2016; Li et al. 2022); associations of CSA with repeated concussion and RHI history have not been investigated.

A growing body of literature demonstrates that measuring the microstructure of the cortex using cortical mean diffusivity (CMD), which is inversely associated with but at least partially genetically distinct from CTh, may provide a more sensitive measure of early structural abnormalities than CTh and CSA (Elman et al. 2017; Sampedro and Kulisevsky 2022). For example, longitudinal elevations in CMD across several of the above noted regions were observed in a large cohort of amyloid‐negative cognitively impaired individuals relative to unimpaired individuals in the absence of CTh changes (Sun et al. 2024). CMD has also shown superior sensitivity to CTh in other clinical populations, with more widespread elevations in CMD relative to decreases in CTh observed in patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis with cognitive or behavioral impairment (Illán‐Gala et al. 2020), patients with the behavioral variant of frontotemporal dementia relative to controls (Illán‐Gala et al. 2020), and patients with primary progressive aphasia (including the earliest stages of the disease) (Illán‐Gala et al. 2022). The relationship between repetitive neurotrauma and patterns of CMD has yet to be investigated.

Accordingly, the current study used semistructured interviews to retrospectively determine the number of prior concussions and exposure to RHI across the lifespan in a cohort of otherwise healthy collegiate male and female athletes. The primary goal of this work was to characterize the relationship of prior concussion and years of contact sport exposure (as a proxy for RHI) with macro (CTh and CSA) and microstructural (CMD) GM metrics. Based on prior work, we hypothesized that prior concussion would be associated with thinner CTh and elevated CMD in frontal and temporal brain regions. Finally, subanalyses were conducted to determine if similar associations were seen in male and female athletes separately based on potential concussion‐related sex differences (Covassin et al. 2018; Koerte et al. 2020).

Materials and Methods

Participants

Collegiate‐aged athletes were recruited from the community as part of a study investigating the chronic effects of concussion and contact sport exposure (Project ChronEx). Inclusion criteria included ages 18–26, active involvement in an organized sport within the past 12 months, and proficiency in English. Exclusion criteria included self‐reported history of psychiatric, neurodevelopmental, neuroendocrine, neurological, or severe autoimmune disease; use of neuroactive medication; history of moderate or severe TBI; concussion within 6 months or incomplete recovery from a previous concussion; excessive alcohol or substance use (defined as a score of 2 or more on the CAGE Questions Adapted to Include Drug Use); contraindication to MRI or other study procedures; and injury/illness precluding participation. All aspects of the study were approved by the Medical College of Wisconsin Institutional Review Board; written informed consent was provided by all participants. CTh data from a subset of this final cohort have been previously reported in interim analyses (Meier et al. 2021).

Assessment of Prior Concussion and Contact Sport History

Participants completed semistructured interviews to capture a detailed history of total lifetime head injury and exposure to contact/collision sports (Amadon et al. 2023; Meier et al. 2021). The number of total concussions across the lifespan was retrospectively determined for each participant by research staff based on criteria consistent with the original 1993 definition of mTBI provided by the American Congress of Rehabilitation Medicine (herein referred to as the ACRM definition) (Kay et al. 1993). Injuries to the head or neck met criteria as a concussion if they were accompanied by any of the following: loss of consciousness (LOC; ≤ 30 min, if present), posttraumatic amnesia (PTA; ≤ 24 h, if present), retrograde amnesia (RGA), or alteration of mental status (AMS, defined as being dazed, confused, “had your bell rung”). Each participant's history of RHI exposure was estimated as the cumulative number of years of exposure across all contact/collision sports (football, rugby, wrestling, soccer, men's lacrosse, men's ice hockey, and combat sports).

MRI Data Parameters

MRI data were collected on a GE Healthcare (Waukesha, WI) SIGNA Premier 3 T scanner using a brain‐dedicated receive only 32‐element coil array (Nova Medical Inc., Wilmington, MA). A T 1‐weighted image was acquired using three‐dimensional magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) pulse sequence with field of view (FOV) = 240 mm, image matrix = 240 × 240, 188 axial slices, 1‐mm slice thickness, voxel size 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm, repetition time (TR) = 2279–2356 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.84–2.97 ms, inversion time = 900 ms, and flip angle = 8°. A T 2‐weighted image was also collected using three‐dimensional CUBE pulse sequence with FOV = 240 mm, image matrix = 240 × 240, 188 axial slices, 1‐mm slice thickness, voxel size 1 × 1 × 1 mm, TR = 3202 ms, TE = 86.34–93.14 ms, and flip angle = 90°. A multishell diffusion weighted image series was collected using a single‐shot spin‐echo EPI with TR = 7500 ms, TE = 74 ms, FOV = 220 mm, image matrix = 110 × 110, 62 axial slices, 2 mm slice thickness, voxel size 2 mm × 2 mm × 2 mm, flip angle = 90°, and in‐plane ASSET acceleration of 2. Ninety diffusion directions were evenly sampled across three b‐values (700, 1000, and 2800 s/mm2), with six nondiffusion weighted reference volumes collected.

MRI Processing and Derived Metrics

Anatomical scans (T 1‐weighted and T 2‐weighted) were processed in FreeSurfer 7.2 for cortical reconstruction, tissue segmentation, and the generation of CSA and CTh. CSA and mean CTh were extracted for each region‐of‐interest (ROI; n = 68) from the Desikan–Killiany Atlas parcellation (Desikan et al. 2006).

Diffusion scans were corrected for susceptibility‐induced distortions using the GE PROGRES feature at the scanner. The multishell diffusion scan was corrected for eddy currents and head motion with the replace outlier option using eddy_openmp from the FMRIB Software Library (FSL) (Andersson et al. 2016; Andersson and Sotiropoulos 2016; Smith et al. 2004). A brain mask was derived from a distortion‐corrected nondiffusion weighted image volume (b 0) using FSL's BET (Smith et al. 2004). Mean diffusivity (MD) was calculated from volumes with b = 1000 s/mm2 using FSL's DTIFit program (Jenkinson et al. 2012). The MD scalar was aligned to the T 1‐weighted image based on the resulting boundary‐based registration of the b 0 image to T 1 (bbregister) (Greve and Fischl 2009). GM and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) probability maps were obtained from a segmentation of the T 1 image in SPM12 (Ashburner and Friston 2005) and subsequently resampled to 2 mm isotropic space to allow voxel‐wise partial volume correction of MD using the CSF contamination model (Jeon et al. 2012; Koo et al. 2009). Negative MD values (i.e., those resulting from partial volume correction) were replaced with zero and remaining MD values scaled by 10,000. Each participant's partial volume corrected MD was projected to the surface with a 0.5 projection fraction and nearest neighbor interpolation to generate CMD. Finally, the nonzero mean of CMD was extracted from the Desikan–Killiany Atlas parcellations for each participant. CMD was selected as the diffusion metric of interest, as described above; however, results for other diffusion tensor metrics are reported in the Supporting Information for reference.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the sample characteristics using SPSS. Bayesian multilevel modeling (BML) on ROI was conducted using the region‐based‐analysis (RBA) program from AFNI for all analyses of cortical GM metrics (Chen et al. 2019, 2020). The BML provides an alternative to traditional “mass‐univariate analyses” that are based on null hypothesis significance testing across numerous ROI, where each classical hypothesis test for an additional ROI increases the odds of a false statistically significant outcome by chance, thereby leading to an increase in Type I error or its equivalent and thus requires an adjustment to control family‐wise error at a desired level (e.g., 0.05). The BML framework instead addresses this issue by not making statistical inferences for ROIs in isolation, but jointly. More specifically, the BML framework better accounts for the structure of the data by using information from all ROI within a single unified model to identify the probability of effects being nonzero simultaneously given the current model and data (Chen et al. 2019, 2020). For the primary goal of characterizing independent associations of concussion history and RHI with cortical structure, separate Bayesian models were fit for CSA, CTh, and CMD, with the number of prior concussions, years of contact sport exposure, age, and sex included in all models; intracranial volume was included as an additional covariate in the CSA models. Primary effects of interest included the number of prior concussions and years of contact sport exposure. Response variables were modeled using a student's t distribution; continuous variables were standardized per program recommendations. In addition, metrics were also scaled as necessary per program recommendations to avoid issues with convergence during Markov Chain Monte Carlo simulations, with results automatically adjusted back to the original scale. All models used four Markov chains with 5000 iterations per chain. Rhat statistics, posterior predictive checks, leave‐one‐out probability integral transformation checks, and leave‐one‐out Pareto smoothed importance sampling were used for diagnosis and assessment of model fit. The strength of evidence for the observed effects was assessed as the probability that the effect was positive conditional on the model and available data using the “p+” metric (e.g., p+ of 0.95 indicates a 95% probability of a positive association). Based on this metric, p+ values near 0.5 indicate no evidence of an effect, p+ values near 1 indicate strong evidence of positive effects, and p+ values near 0 indicate strong evidence for negative effects. All resultant p+ values are presented for transparency, but, for simplicity, only strong effects (> 0.90 or < 0.10) are explicitly described in the results section. Finally, sensitivity analyses were conducted with participants split by sex to determine whether similar effects were observed in male and female participants when considered separately. Sensitivity analyses were conducted as above with sex removed from the model.

Of these, structural MRI data (T 1 and T 2‐weighted scans) were not available for 5 participants, leaving n = 207 for CTh and CSA analyses. Two additional participants did not complete the diffusion scan, resulting in n = 205 for CMD analyses. Association of Concussion and RHI With CSA

The p+ values and the associated mean, standard deviation, and quantile intervals of the effects of prior concussion and years of contact sport exposure on all metrics for each ROI are shown in Figures 1, 2, 3 and Tables 2, 3, 4. For history of prior concussion, there was evidence for a positive association with CSA in multiple regions including bilateral superior frontal gyrus, precuneus cortex, inferior temporal gyrus, and fusiform gyrus; right caudal middle frontal gyrus, inferior parietal cortex, lateral occipital cortex, and middle temporal gyrus; and left rostral anterior cingulate cortex, lateral orbitofrontal cortex, supramarginal gyrus, and superior parietal cortex (Figure 1, Table 2). There were no regions showing evidence for an inverse association of prior concussion with CSA. There was no evidence for a strong association of years of contact sport exposure with CSA for any ROI, though weak evidence of positive associations was generally observed across the whole cortex.

Association of Concussion and RHI With CTh

There was strong evidence for an inverse relationship of the number of prior concussions with CTh in the bilateral temporal pole, bilateral entorhinal cortex, right parahippocampal gyrus, and right middle temporal gyrus; no regions showed strong evidence of a positive association (Figure 2, Table 3). There were no regions for which years of exposure were strongly associated with CTh, though as in the CSA analyses there was weak evidence of positive associations with CTh across the cortex.

Association of Concussion and RHI With CMD

There was a global positive association between the number of prior concussions and CMD across the cortex, with all but nine regions showing strong evidence of an effect (Figure 3, Table 4). There was no evidence of any associations between years of contact sport exposure and CMD. Supplementary analyses indicated similar relationships were present for metrics of axial and radial diffusivity with the number of prior concussions, which is expected given they represent constituent factors of CMD.

Split‐By‐Sex Analyses

Quantile intervals of the effects for the split‐by‐sex associations of prior concussion and contact sport exposure with each cortical metric in each ROI are visualized in Figures 4, 5, 6, colored based on p+ values. The associated mean and standard deviation of the effect, and p+ values for all ROI are presented in Tables S2–S7. Results for the split‐by‐sex analyses for CSA were generally similar to those observed in the combined sample (Figure 4, Tables S2 and S3). For male athletes, the number of prior concussions was associated with increased CSA in the bilateral superior frontal gyrus, supramarginal gyrus, inferior temporal gyrus, and fusiform gyrus; right inferior parietal cortex, middle temporal gyrus, and precuneus; and left superior parietal cortex and rostral middle frontal gyrus. For female athletes, the number of prior concussions was associated with greater CSA in the bilateral precuneus, right inferior temporal gyrus and middle temporal gyrus; and left superior frontal gyrus and lateral orbitofrontal cortex. As in the primary analyses, neither male nor female athletes showed strong evidence for an inverse association of concussion history with CSA and neither showed strong evidence of CSA associations with contact sport exposure.

When analyzed separately, both male and female athlete groups showed weak evidence for similar inverse associations between CTh and concussion that were observed in the overall sample (i.e., in medial temporal regions), though only the left temporal pole in male athletes exceeded the threshold for a strong association (Figure 5, Tables S4 and S5). Discussion

There is growing concern that repeated concussion and exposure to RHI may have adverse long‐term effects on brain health, such as subtle changes in GM structure or even atrophy similar to the pattern that has been reported in deceased athletes with advanced CTE (Alosco et al. 2021; Brett et al. 2025; Manley et al. 2017). In the current study, we observe that the number of prior concussions is associated with both macro and microstructural GM in collegiate‐aged male and female athletes with a varied history in sport participation. Individuals with a higher number of prior concussions had thinner cortex in medial temporal cortex (i.e., entorhinal cortex, parahippocampal gyrus), temporal pole, and right middle temporal gyrus, larger CSA across several frontal, parietal, and temporal regions, as well as widespread increases in CMD across nearly the entire cortex. In contrast, there was limited evidence for any associations of years of contact sport exposure with surface metrics. Finally, split‐by‐sex analyses found generally similar patterns in both male and female athletes when analyzed separately, with an additional finding of a positive association of years of contact sport exposure with CTh in female athletes.

To our knowledge, our observation of a positive association between the number of prior concussions and CMD across most of the cortex is the first demonstration of an association between concussion history and CMD. As stated above, emerging evidence demonstrates that CMD is a more sensitive, earlier measure of structural abnormalities across a variety of patient populations (e.g., cognitively impaired individuals, various neurodegenerative diseases) relative to CTh (Elman et al. 2017; Sampedro and Kulisevsky 2022; Sun et al. 2024). Our finding of increases in CMD (i.e., less restricted diffusion) largely in the absence of CTh differences could conceivably reflect abnormalities in cellular structure, such as reductions in dendritic density, (Elman et al. 2017; Kane et al. 2011) in the absence of measurable atrophy in CTh; however, there are a variety of potential pathophysiological explanations for this finding that have been associated with neurotrauma (e.g., increased perivascular spaces, vasogenic edema), (Kane et al. 2011; Lara et al. 2021) and we cannot definitely ascribe our findings to any one mechanism. The pattern of results (more concussions associated with increased diffusion) is also consistent with prior reports in subcortical GM and white matter (Goeckner et al. 2023; June et al. 2020; Major et al. 2021; Terry et al. 2019), though the potential pathophysiology driving these associations in different brain tissues may differ. Regardless of the underlying pathophysiology, our findings of widespread CMD associations with prior concussion, while associations with CTh are circumscribed to more restricted areas, highlight the possibility that CMD is sensitive to the cumulative effects of repeat concussion in otherwise healthy, young adults. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine if these associations are predictive of eventual macrostructural changes, similar to work in other disease populations (Sun et al. 2024).

Our finding of decreased CTh in medial and anterior temporal lobe is most directly comparable to prior work that has similarly investigated CTh associated with concussion and/or RHI, with most evidence in retired athletes. Thinner cortex in entorhinal cortex, parahippocampal gyrus, and temporal pole has been reported in multiple separate studies of retired football players (American or Australian rules) relative to various control groups, such as asymptomatic controls or noncollision sport athletes (e.g., track and field) (Adler et al. 2018; Arciniega et al. 2024; Goswami et al. 2016; Major et al. 2021), with one paper showing a similar inverse association of concussion history with CTh to the current findings (Adler et al. 2018). These results extend upon these prior findings by showing similar associations in active, asymptomatic male and female athletes with a varied sport history (i.e., in contrast to retired football athletes). Furthermore, the current study design allowed the disentanglement of the effects of concussion from years of contact sport exposure (as a measure of RHI), with evidence in this cohort that CTh in medial and anterior temporal regions is specifically sensitive to concussion history rather than RHI exposure.

Current results also demonstrate a positive association of concussion history with CSA across several regions of the cortex. Relative to CTh and cortical volume, few studies have investigated the effects of neurotrauma on CSA, though there is a report of elevated CSA acutely following mTBI (Li et al. 2022) as well as a report of increasing CSA chronically following mTBI (Bajaj et al. 2018). Increased CSA is most extensively studied in the context of neurodevelopment, with CSA increasing through the first decade of life before becoming relatively stable with subtle decreases through young adulthood (Amlien et al. 2016; Raznahan et al. 2011; Tamnes et al. 2017; Wierenga et al. 2014). The cumulative effects of concussion on CSA are thus superimposed on these dynamic changes in CSA development, and our findings raise the possibility that repeated concussions occurring during key developmental periods alter typical neurodevelopment of the cortex. Again, longitudinal studies are needed to better characterize the complex interrelationships among repeat concussion and neurodevelopment on cortical structure.

Nevertheless, when considered in aggregate, current results are suggestive of a specific pattern of cortical structure abnormalities associated with repeated concussion in healthy, adult athletes characterized by increased CSA, widespread elevations in CMD, and more localized decreases in CTh. A number of processes associated with the neurometabolic cascade following concussion, including altered neurotransmission, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and the associated metabolic crisis may lead to the above profile of cortical structure abnormalities associated with cumulative concussion (Giza and Hovda 2014). While a single or lower number of injuries may not be sufficient to produce chronic changes, the effects of cumulative concussion could aggregate to result in (micro)structural differences observed in the current study. We propose that repeated concussions experienced throughout the lifespan (up to young adulthood in this cohort) result in altered neurodevelopmental trajectories that are reflected as increased CSA. These effects are accompanied by subtle disruptions in microstructure indexed as increased CMD, resulting in more cortical columns that are less densely packed with neurites. For most of the cortex, these disruptions in microstructure occur in the absence of frank atrophy that is measurable as decreased CTh. In contrast, repeat concussion is associated with a localized thinner cortex in the anterior and medial temporal lobe, consistent with the known sensitivity of these regions to brain injury (Bigler 2007; McAllister 2011).

In contrast to the number of prior concussions, current results suggest that RHI exposure history in healthy collegiate athletes is not associated with cortical surface metrics. This is inconsistent with some prior findings of associations of RHI with cortical structure in retired athletes (Adler et al. 2018; Arciniega et al. 2024; Lepage et al. 2019), though as previously stated much of the prior work was unable to delineate the effects of concussion from RHI. It is possible that simple dose‐dependent associations of RHI with cortical structure metrics do not exist, and associations only emerge with advanced age or in conjunction with other key moderating health and lifestyle factors (Brett et al. 2025). We also cannot rule out, however, that other measures of RHI might be more strongly associated with cortical structure even in this younger cohort. Several proxies of RHI have been used in prior studies, including age of first exposure, group differences based on primary sport (i.e., contact versus noncontact), and even measures that calculate estimates based on published position‐specific head impact sensor data (though these are limited to football) (Adler et al. 2018; Kerr et al. 2015; Montenigro et al. 2017; Schultz et al. 2018; Stamm et al. 2015). Years of contact sport exposure, either cumulatively or focused on specific sports, have been previously used in prior studies in this and other cohorts (Gallo et al. 2022; Meier et al. 2025; Singh et al. 2014), and is also a key anchor for the proposed criteria for traumatic encephalopathy syndrome (Katz et al. 2021). Thus, it represents a relatively parsimonious method to estimate RHI that considers all prior contact sport history (Amadon et al. 2023).

Finally, growing evidence has highlighted sex as a potential moderator for the effects of head injury and impacts (Koerte et al. 2020; Sollmann et al. 2018; Valera et al. 2021), though limited prior studies focused on the cumulative effects of concussion/RHI on brain health have included female athletes. Split‐by‐sex analyses in the current study demonstrated broadly consistent findings across male and female participants, though prior concussion was more strongly associated with CSA in male athletes and more strongly associated with CMD in female athletes. As in the overall cohort, most observed associations were with prior concussion rather than years of exposure. The one exception, however, was evidence of increased CTh in multiple regions in female athletes that was not observed in male athletes or across the whole cohort. This female‐specific association with years of exposure for CTh could be due to differences in neck girth and strength between male and female athletes, which have previously been associated with quantified head kinematics during controlled soccer heading (Bretzin et al. 2017). These results highlight the need for additional studies in female athletes given the focus on male football players in the literature.

Strengths and Limitations

Current results should be interpreted within the context of the study. First, the current study utilized strict exclusion criteria, minimizing the possibility that observed results are driven by underlying comorbidities. This design, however, also prevents investigation of the role of key moderators that might increase risk for adverse effects of repeat concussion and RHI. Second, as stated above, it is possible that other proxies of RHI (e.g., age of first exposure) might be more sensitive than years of contact sport exposure used here. Third, advanced diffusion models could provide additional information about potential underlying pathology; however, current analyses focus on CMD given the body of literature highlighting this specific metric as an early indicator of structural abnormality. Furthermore, current analyses did not explicitly investigate whether certain demographic (or other) factors, such as age, moderate observed associations between concussion and/or RHI with cortical outcomes. This remains an important avenue for future investigation. The current study was restricted to athletes, and it is possible that the benefits of sport (e.g., exercise, social support and group membership) on brain health outweigh potential effects of concussion and/or RHI. Future studies could consider the inclusion of non‐athlete controls to more directly address this possibility. Finally, the current work is a cross‐sectional study of healthy collegiate‐aged athletes. Thus, we cannot identify causal relationships and the nature of the relationship between concussion and RHI with cortical surface metrics is unknown. Moreover, the current clinical relevance of the observed subtle associations with GM structure is unknown. Future studies are needed to determine if such differences progress over time or are potentially early indicators of risk for adverse outcomes later in life that have been previously reported in subsets of aged, former athletes.

Conclusion

Current results suggest that greater concussion history in healthy, collegiate‐aged athletes is associated with differences in cortical macro and microstructure. Changes in microstructure were more diffusely observed and may represent a more sensitive metric of subtle, early structural changes associated with repetitive neurotrauma. In contrast, there were limited associations of years of contact sport exposure with cortical structure. These findings highlight the importance of efforts to reduce concussion risk in sport.
