Influence of molecular structure and size of cyclic and spherical carbon allotropes on high-order harmonic generation efficiency: a TDDFT study
Authors: Vahid Darvishzadeh, Saeed Batebi, Hanif Hadipour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Among the variety of nanostructured materials, carbon allotropes play an important role. This study explores how molecular size and topology influence the HHG response of distinct carbon allotropes, including cyclic (cyclo[n]) and spherical (Cn) species using the density matrix method. Different structures lead to different harmonic polarizations depending on the geometric configuration of carbon atoms and molecular orientation. The three cyclo[n]carbon molecules have been chosen such that three different ring diameters are examined. Our results reveal a clear dependence of the harmonic yield on both molecular size and topology. Furthermore, varying the direction of the applied field with respect to the carbon ring affects the harmonic generation efficiency. The results indicate that an electric field oriented within the ring plane produces stronger harmonics. The efficiency of the carbon rings appears to be higher than that of the fullerene molecule. It seems that changing the propagation angle of the applied field in the fullerene molecule does not affect the harmonic generation, as this molecule is largely symmetric. These insights provide fundamental understanding and practical guidance for designing nanoscale materials with tunable ultrafast optical responses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-61070-z.

Introduction

The interaction of high-intensity laser light with atoms, molecules, or crystals can lead to many nonlinear phenomena, including tunneling ionization1, above-threshold ionization2, and high-order harmonics generation (HHG)3–6. Among these, HHG have gained significant attention because they play a crucial role in generating ultrashort pulses7. ultrashort pulse lasers can be used to probe ultrafast processes in chemistry and physics, such as electron dynamics in atoms and molecules7. The high harmonics generated can be used in advanced spectroscopic techniques, allowing scientists to explore the structural and dynamic properties of materials at unprecedented time scales6,8. Ultrashort pulses on the order of attoseconds are vital for studying atomic particles and their dynamics8. To achieve such pulses, it is essential to enhance the efficiency of high-order harmonics9. The type and structure of the material play an important role in this process, so HHG in carbon molecules has garnered significant attention9. In particular, cyclic and cage-like carbon allotropes such as the C20 ring and fullerene provide useful test systems for exploring how molecular geometry and size influence the characteristics of high-harmonic generation10. These structures are of special interest because their symmetry and geometric distribution of atomic centers can significantly influence the recombination matrix elements and the resulting features of the harmonic spectrum10.

Research indicates that carbon-based materials, particularly in the form of nanostructures, exhibit unique HHG properties. For instance, Zurrón-Cifuentes and Plaja highlight the role of molecular structure in enhancing HHG efficiency, suggesting that specific configurations can lead to increased harmonic yield11. Avetissian et al. further explore the interaction of intense laser fields with carbon molecules, demonstrating that the nonlinear response is crucial for efficient HHG, with carbon’s electronic properties facilitating this process12. Zhou et al. also emphasize the importance of how electrons move and behave in carbon-based systems. They explain that the interaction between electron correlation and external fields plays a major role in shaping the process of harmonic generation9. However, some challenges still exist. For example, researchers need to control experimental conditions very carefully to improve HHG in carbon materials. Yan et al. discuss these issues and explain the weaknesses of current methods13. In general, the studies show that carbon molecules have great potential for HHG, but they also identify several areas that require more research9,10,12. Carbon, owing to its versatile hybridization states (sp, sp2, and sp3), can form an extraordinary range of allotropes—from linear chains and rings to three-dimensional cages and networks. Among these, cyclic and spherical nanostructures such as cyclo[n]carbons and fullerenes have attracted tremendous attention due to their remarkable optical and electronic properties.

To elucidate the mechanism behind the generation of high-order harmonics, a widely recognized framework, referred to as the three-step model14, has been developed. This model provides a structured explanation of the process, breaking it down into three distinct phases: ionization, electron motion after liberation, and recombination15. In the initial phase, the intense electric field of an incident laser causes valence electrons in atoms to undergo a process known as tunnel ionization. This occurs when the laser field distorts the potential barrier that confines the electrons, allowing them to escape from the atom. Once the electrons have been ionized, they are influenced by the oscillating electric field of the laser. During this stage, the freed electrons experience a strong acceleration, gaining kinetic energy as they move away from their parent ions. The intensity and frequency of the laser play a crucial role in determining how much energy the electrons can accumulate during this phase. The changing direction of the electric field causes the electrons to oscillate back and forth, further enhancing their energy16,17.

In the final phase, some of these accelerated electrons can reverse their trajectory and recombine with their parent ions. This recombination allows the electrons to return to their original ground state. During this process, the electrons release the kinetic energy they have acquired, emitting it as high-frequency photons. This emission of light constitutes high-harmonic generation(HHG), where the emitted photons have frequencies that are integer multiples of the original laser frequency16,18,19.

In the generation of high‑order harmonics, two parameters are crucial: the efficiency of the process and the extension of the cutoff14. Both the efficiency and cutoff of HHG are strongly influenced by the molecular potential landscape, electronic correlation, and the symmetry of the target system9. To improve HHG performance, researchers have explored a wide range of materials, from gases to solids, including two‑dimensional nanostructures such as graphene12, dielectric hexagonal boron nitride20, and dielectric Motts21. Nevertheless, carbon allotropes such as carbon nanotubes12 and fullerene molecules12, along with their derivatives, continue to attract significant interest due to their ability to produce high harmonic yield and efficient high‑order harmonic generation.

Over the past two decades, first‑principles computational methods rooted in time‑dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) have been well established and widely applied to investigate a variety of nonlinear phenomena22,23. Various computational techniques have been created to examine nonlinear phenomena in the frequency domain, applicable to both molecules24 and solids25. Additionally, time-domain methods that solve the time-dependent Kohn–Sham (TDKS) equation, the basic equation of TDDFT, in real time have been developed to determine nonlinear polarizabilities for both molecules and solids. Different numerical approaches have been employed to solve the TDKS equation in real time using various basis sets. High-order harmonic generation (HHG) in molecular systems has attracted considerable interest due to its important applications in attosecond pulse generation and ultrafast spectroscopy17. These applications rely on achieving strong harmonic emission and broad harmonic spectra, which are strongly influenced by the electronic structure of the target molecules and directly determine the harmonic yield and overall conversion efficiency. In this context, our study clarifies how molecular size and ring topology affect both the strength and the cutoff position of the generated harmonics, providing useful insights for designing molecular systems optimized for HHG applications.

Building on these general considerations, it is also important to identify which structural and electronic features of carbon‑based molecules can most strongly influence their HHG response. Some previous studies, including those on cyclic systems such as cyclo[18]carbon, have demonstrated that delocalized π‑electrons in conjugated systems can significantly enhance nonlinear optical responses9. However, most investigations have focused on linear or planar carbon structures, while three-dimensional and topologically nontrivial configurations—such as cyclic and spherical allotropes—remain less explored. In this context, understanding how molecular geometry and size influence HHG efficiency in carbon allotropes offers crucial insights for developing nanoscale photonic and optoelectronic applications9. We generated high-order harmonics from cyclo[n]carbons molecules, like C18 and fullerene C20 using the time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT)26 method. We used SALMON package27 to perform our calculations. The core part of the code is the real-time, real-space calculation of the electron dynamics induced in molecules and solids by an external electric field solving the time-dependent Kohn–Sham equation28. We investigated changes in the efficiency of the harmonics by increasing the diameter of cyclo[n]carbon rings and also aimed to demonstrate which of the cyclo[n]carbons or C20 fullerene exhibited better efficiency.

In order to avoid these unphysical effects, a complex absorption potential (CAP)33 is incorporated, typically introduced as an imaginary term in the Hamiltonian. The wave packet is absorbed near the boundary by the CAP, thereby simulating an infinite domain. The amplitude of the outgoing wave packet is reduced to zero by the CAP, ensuring that reflections are prevented.

Results and discussions

In this article, we investigate the generation of high-order harmonics and their efficiency with increasing cyclo[n]carbon ring diameter. Furthermore, a comprehensive comparison has been conducted between the harmonic efficiency of cyclo[n]carbons and fullerene20 (C20) to assess the influence of the carbon atom arrangement on the overall efficiency. The findings of this research may contribute to a deeper understanding of the role of molecular structure in the performance of high-order harmonics.

To precisely simulate molecular dynamics and explore time-dependent phenomena, we developed a sophisticated numerical model that integrates the time-dependent Schrödinger equation (TDSE) with time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) using the SALMON package. This approach enables accurate modeling of electron dynamics and light-matter interactions in molecular and nanostructured systems. To investigate high-harmonic generation (HHG) in cyclic carbon molecules (C14, C18, C20, and C28), we conducted numerical simulations using advanced time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT). By coupling the time-dependent Schrödinger equation (TDSE) with TDDFT, we modeled the molecular response to monochromatic laser pulses, as described in Eqs. 4 and 5 (In accordance with the specifications provided below Fig. 1), to generate high-order harmonics and compared the results to elucidate the influence of molecular structure on harmonic yield.

We divided the results of the study below into three general sections, which we will discuss in the following.

Harmonic spectra of cyclic carbon allotropes

The molecular structures of the compounds studied in this article are presented in Fig. 2. As observed, the molecules C14, C18, and C2835–37 have cyclic structures, whereas the C20 molecule38 exhibits a nearly spherical structure. The C20 molecule with a dodecahedral cage structure is the smallest member of the fullerene family. Its carbon atoms are sp2 hybridized, forming 30 bonds (edges) in a highly curved, cage-like structure. This high curvature results in greater reactivity and lower stability compared to larger fullerenes. In the cyclo[n]carbons the C–C bond length is alternating between 1.22 ˚ A and 1.34 ˚ A35–37,39. The carbon allotropes utilized in this study have their centers positioned at the origin of the coordinate system, with all nuclei fixed in place, and their molecular structures constrained to the x–y plane. The coordinates of the carbon atoms and the precise specifications used in our simulation are provided in references 38 to 41.

For smaller rings such as C14, distinct even-order harmonics appear, attributable to the loss of perfect inversion symmetry in their electronic density distribution during laser excitation. As the ring size increases, the even-order components gradually weaken, indicating a restoration of near-symmetric charge oscillation.

This behavior agrees with known HHG selection rules: systems with broken inversion symmetry produce both even and odd harmonics, while symmetric systems generate only odd orders40. The spectral width and intensity of the harmonics also increase with increasing molecular radius, indicating stronger harmonic emission and the appearance of additional harmonic orders.

One factor that may influence the harmonic efficiency is the HOMO–LUMO gap. As shown in Table 1, this gap decreases with increasing ring diameter, which may help explain the higher efficiency observed for larger rings. A smaller gap may lower the energy required for electronic excitation to unoccupied states and could potentially enhance the harmonic response; nevertheless, under strong-field conditions, additional excitation channels may also contribute to the emission process40. According to Koopmans’ theorem41, the ionization potential can be approximated as the negative of the HOMO energy, implying that higher efficiency is generally associated with lower ionization potential, consistent with previous studies42,43.

Effect of laser polarization

Another critical factor influencing harmonic generation is the orientation of the external electric field. To examine anisotropy in HHG, we simulated both parallel and perpendicular laser polarization relative to the molecular axis (see Fig. 4). For cyclic systems, parallel polarization generates much stronger harmonic intensity, while perpendicular (i.e., along the z-axis) polarization suppresses high‑order peaks. This directional dependence is consistent with the planar π‑electron distribution and anisotropic bonding in ring structures, where the electron density is mainly confined to the molecular plane45. In fact, because the entire ring lies in the XY plane and fields applied parallel to this plane interact more strongly with the delocalized π‑orbitals45, the in‑plane alignment allows the field to drive electron oscillations more efficiently than the perpendicular (z‑axis) orientation, which induces weaker polarization due to the reduced out‑of‑plane polarizability45.

In contrast, spherical structures such as C20 exhibit nearly isotropic responses, reflecting their uniform charge distribution across the curved molecular surface. Such isotropy makes spherical carbon systems ideal candidates for generating stable, broadband harmonic radiation10. The similarity in harmonic spectra can thus act as a diagnostic tool for probing molecular symmetry, as symmetric molecules like C20 produce consistent harmonic outputs across different field orientations, unlike less symmetric structures.

The effect of molecular structure on spectral efficiency

Based on the harmonic spectra presented in Fig. 5, it is evident that the efficiency of harmonic generation in C28 exceeds that observed in fullerene20 (C20). This enhanced efficiency may be attributed to the different electron confinement in the ring-like C28 compared with the spherical C20, which likely influences the nonlinear optical response. Unlike cyclic systems, spherical allotropes exhibit predominantly odd-order harmonics with remarkably regular spacing. This pattern is consistent with their high symmetry and delocalized electronic orbitals, which suppress even-order harmonic emission.

Time–frequency analysis

High-harmonic generation (HHG) spectroscopy is a powerful tool for studying ultrafast electron dynamics, offering insights into the specific harmonics where emission takes place. However, these spectra alone do not provide information about the timing or temporal evolution of the emissions, which is a critical aspect for a complete understanding of the underlying processes. To gain a thorough perspective, it’s essential to examine both the spectral characteristics-related to the frequency of the harmonics-and the temporal characteristics, which reveal how these emissions develop over time. By applying time–frequency analysis, as described in Eq. 11, researchers can effectively probe the spatiotemporal structure of dipole acceleration. This approach allows for a detailed exploration of how the emission behaves across both time and space, providing a deeper and more nuanced understanding of the dynamics involved in HHG processes.

To accurately analyze the spatiotemporal distribution of a signal, it is crucial to multiply its Fourier transform by a temporal window function (WF). Various temporal window functions are used in spectral analysis, and in the field of HHG, studies43,46,47 have predominantly utilized the Gaussian type, known as the Gabor transform.

To better understand the temporal dynamics of HHG, a Gabor transform was applied to the dipole acceleration signal.

It appears that the molecular response of C28 to strong-field ionization processes48 causes the HHG response to split into two distinct regions: a primary plateau (20–40 eV) and a weaker plateau (50–60 eV). The primary plateau shows strong, stable intensity from short electron trajectories, which are less sensitive to laser variations. A weaker plateau arises from long trajectories, with reduced intensity due to scattering or absorption. Short trajectories dominate early dynamics, while long ones contribute later, causing interference at specific times49. This interference, linked to phase alignment, likely produces the two distinct peaks observed in the Gabor spectrum, reflecting the temporal dynamics of short and long trajectories. This temporal extension leads to smoother harmonic plateaus and emphasizes the link between topology, delocalization, and electron coherence.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that both topology and molecular size critically determine the nonlinear optical response of carbon allotropes. The results show that larger cyclo[n]carbon rings generate higher harmonic energies compared with smaller ones. The cutoff extension increases with molecular size, indicating that delocalization of π‑electrons and spatial confinement jointly control harmonic energy. Cyclic carbon rings exhibit even‑order harmonics due to their lower symmetry, while spherical cages produce dominant odd‑order harmonics consistent with inversion symmetry.

The study’s results also show that the efficiency of harmonic generation improves noticeably when the electric field is aligned along the x‑axis compared to the z‑axis. This difference in alignment highlights how crucial the direction of the electric field is for obtaining stronger harmonic yields. Through detailed Gabor analysis of the HHG process in C28, the study reveals the temporal distribution of emitted harmonics and the formation of a plateau with weaker higher‑order components. These insights emphasize the importance of both the size of the carbon ring and the proper alignment of the electric field and provide a clear framework for designing carbon‑based nanomaterials with tailored ultrafast optical properties.
