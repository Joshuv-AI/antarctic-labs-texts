Screening for potential skin benefits of edible botanical extracts on a model associating in-vitro intestinal barrier passage with ex vivo human skin
Authors: Damien Guillemet, Nathalie Pedretti, Nathalie Coussay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe aimed to set up a functional in vitro system, combining the Caco-2 cell line intestinal barrier passage with ex vivo human skin explant to evaluate the effects of edible ingredients on skin health through full transcriptomic and specific biomarker assessments. After validation of this model, we have investigated several polyphenols-rich botanical extracts, already used as oral supplements tested now for skin health potentials. Out of five extracts tested, four (Combination of Immortelle and Clove, Hibiscus, Cranberry, and Olive extracts) have showed modulations of a wide range of gene expressions related to antioxidant and detoxifying cell process in the skin tissue, along with a lipid synthesis reduction. Additionally, Hibiscus and Olive extracts showed a protective effect on lipid peroxidation by reducing the malondialdehyde (MDA) production, a marker proposed for oxidative status. These in-vitro model and data should enhance representativity of nutricosmetic potential but remain to be confirmed by in vivo trials.Keywords: Gene expression, Malondialdehyde, Skin, Nutricosmetic

Introduction

The skin, because of its critical location, is a biological barrier against environmental stressors such as chemical and physical environmental pollutants. The exposure of the skin to these pro-oxidant agents leads to the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the generation of bioactive molecules that can damage skin cells [1].

Skin aging is a natural and complex process influenced by two mechanisms, i.e., intrinsic aging (genetic, chronological) and extrinsic aging (photoaging) [2]. Typical extrinsic factors for skin aging are UV-exposure, cigarette smoke and other pollution factors [3]. These stressors could lead to oxidative stress caused by an imbalance between the production and accumulation of ROS, in cells and tissues, and the antioxidant defense mechanisms [4]. ROS are normally generated as by-products of oxygen metabolism and can play physiological roles (i.e., synthesis of some cellular structures and defense against pathogens). However, environmental stressors contribute to greatly increase ROS production, therefore leading to damage cellular components like proteins, lipids and DNA [5,6]. Among biomarkers of these processes, malondialdehyde (MDA) is widely used to monitor ROS-induced lipid peroxidation [7,8]. The damages on cellular components can affect various cell functions including energy metabolism or signal transduction pathways. Antioxidants have been shown to play a crucial role in neutralizing free radicals and preventing oxidative damages.

Throughout history, plant extracts have traditionally been used for medicinal purposes, and especially for the treatment of many skin diseases and the treatment or prevention of skin aging [2,9,10]. Many supplements, nutricosmetics and cosmetics are based on botanical ingredients. As they must survive several pro-oxidative processes as ultraviolet radiation (UVR) exposure, plants are rich in antioxidants [11] that give them the ability to modulate certain types of skin damages resulting from harmful environmental factors, including UVR and free radicals. Plants, like fruits and vegetables, contain a large amount of phytochemical compounds responsible for their medicinal properties, such as polyphenols, carotenoids, phytosterols and polysaccharides [10].

Among these phytochemical compounds, plant polyphenols are widely acknowledged for their health benefits, especially for the prevention of inflammatory and age-related diseases [12,13]. They are generally involved in defense against ultraviolet radiation or aggression by pathogens [14]. Quercetin, widely present in many fruits and vegetables (e.g. onions, apples, cranberries), has been reported for having several biological actions such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and anti-allergic activities [2,5,15]. Hydroxytyrosol (HT), mainly found in olive fruit and oil, is known for its powerful antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties including inhibition of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and nitric oxide (NO) production pathways. In vitro models, it has been demonstrated that hydroxytyrosol increases cell survival and reduces DNA damage in human keratinocytes exposed to UVB radiation [16,17].

Here we considered several botanical extracts already used as oral supplements for their known antioxidant activities and related health benefits. Olive extract, rich in hydroxytyrosol, is used for cardiovascular health as it can protect low-density lipoproteins (LDL) from oxidation [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. The combination of Immortelle and Clove extracts, rich in flavonoids, is already used for hepatoprotective and detoxifying action ([22]; Kazi et al., s. d.; [[23], [24], [25]]). The combination has shown superiority in activating Nrf2, the promotor of the Antioxidant Response Element (ARE) that triggers synthesis of natural cell defenses against free radicals [26]. Prickly pear fruit is rich in betalains, mainly indicaxanthin, a bioavailable pigment implicated in protection of blood LDL oxidation [27,28]. Hibiscus extract, rich in polyphenols, has been widely studied for its liver protection activity [29], antihypertensive and cardiovascular effects [30,31]. Cranberries, rich in phenolic bioactives and organic acids, have shown beneficial effects on recurrent urinary tract infections (UTI) (Al-Badr & Al-Shaikh, s. d.; [32]) and cardiovascular health [33]. Our cranberry extract is particularly rich in organic acids, i.e., quinic acid, citric acid and malic acid, that can also benefit skin health via antioxidant, exfoliating [34], anti-inflammatory and photoprotective effects [35].

One approach to counteract oxidative stress in skin is through dietary intake of antioxidant nutrients like vitamins A, C, E and selenium [36]. These antioxidants may lead to reduce ROS levels in skin cells and thus protect against oxidative damage. However, the absorption and metabolism of nutrients is a complex physiological process that takes place in various systems within the body. Recent studies suggest that oral administration of botanical extracts may be an effective way to deliver antioxidants to skin cells via systemic circulation mechanisms [37]. However, along the digestive track, orally administered products such as polyphenols undergo several chemical modifications and selective transporter systems before reaching the bloodstream and then being delivered to the targeted tissue. In addition other processes are also implicated in the bioavailability of orally administered products, such as their distribution to specific tissues, pharmacodynamics and their metabolization by host cells and microbiota [38]. In vitro models of the intestinal barrier have become increasingly useful for studying nutrient absorption and metabolism [39,40]. A well established in vitro model of intestinal absorption based on Caco-2 cells is commonly used for testing compound bioavailability after ingestion [26,41,42], as these epithelial cell-like cultures, especially in contiguous monolayer system, can simulate the intestinal mucosal barrier. Caco-2 cells also express and efflux proteins and enzymes capable of metabolizing molecules to ensure the chemical conversion of test substances [38]. Therefore, this Caco-2 cell barrier model could be appropriate to mimic at least partially, intestinal barrier passage and bioavailability of botanical extracts investigated for potential benefits in skin health after oral administration.

In this study, to address the inherent complexities of bioavailability of our extracts, we set up an in vitro system comprising a bi-phasic culture, combining the Caco-2 cell line intestinal barrier model with ex vivo human skin explant. Our model consists in applying edible ingredients, i.e., botanical extracts, onto a differentiated monolayer of Caco-2 epithelial cells, that mimics the mucosal absorptive wall in the intestine, to simulate their intestinal absorption and associated metabolization. Each ingredient was integrated into the apical compartment of inserts containing Caco-2 cells, and the basolateral medium (BM) was recovered later as representative for active and passive direct absorptions of compounds as well as metabolization of others by the epithelial cells and their subsequent release in the BM. Then, BM collected from Caco-2 treated with each botanical extract were integrated in culture medium disposed at the bottom location of ex vivo human explants of complete skin.

We first validated the model and then applied this bi-phasic culture model for the investigation of our extracts by identifying biological properties and pathways implicated in final putative benefits in skin tissue. As the potential beneficial effects of these extracts on skin health can be various and difficult to predict, they were screened on human skin samples using a full genome transcriptional analysis to identify biological processes or markers susceptible to support new properties specific to this tissue. To get deeper in the skin biological impact of the most promising ingredients for promotion of antioxidant cell processes, we finally measured their effect on lipid peroxidation by analyzing MDA production in skin explants treated by BM extracts.

The aims of this study were (i) to set up and validate a functional bi-phasic culture model to obtain released compounds from the passage of edible ingredients through intestinal cell line, and (ii) to compare the transcriptional impact and the capacity to reduce lipid peroxidation (measure of MDA) of these remaining compounds, as an interest for skin health.

Materials and methods

Botanical extraction process

Olive extract (OliOla™ 5 % clear organic, Nexira France) is a purified extract from fruit of Olea europea. First stage of extraction consists of common process applied for virgin oil extraction for food application (milling, trituration, decantation and centrifugation). From the previous stage the olive oil wastewater is recovered, and polyphenols are purified on adsorbent resin. Acacia fiber is used as carrier for spraydrying stage in order to obtain a powder and the product is characterized in hydroxytyrosol, tyrosol and polyphenols contents. The final yield ratio is 200∼250:1 from olive fruit, and final powder is standardized in 5 % of hydroxytrosol + tyrosol (HPLC method).

Combination of Immortelle and Clove extracts (Hepure™, Nexira France) are made from Helichrysum italicum flower and Syzygium aromaticum flower bud respectively. Both ingredients are processed through a solid/liquid extraction of dried raw materials in water with centrifugation. Ingredients are dried without carrier and combined together. The final product is characterized in biflorin, isobiflorin, leontopodic acid & isomers, and quercetin & derivatives contents. The final yield ratios are 18:1 from immortelle dried flower and 6:1 from clove flower bud. The final powder is standardized in minimum 20 % of total polyphenols (Folin-Ciocalteu method).

Prickly pear extract (Cactinea™, Nexira France) is extracted from Opuntia ficus-indica fruit. It consists of juice extraction process and a spraydrying stage with acacia gum as carrier. The final yield ratio is 5∼10:1 from cactus fruit, and final powder is standardized in minimum 500 ppm of indicaxanthin + betain content (septrophotometric method).

Hibiscus extract (Hibiscus extract 10 %, Nexira France) is extracted from Hibiscus sabdariffa flower. The process is a solid/liquid extraction of dried flowers in water with centrifugation. Maltodextrin is used as carrier for spraydrying stage in order to obtain a powder and the product is characterized in polyphenols content. The final yield ratio is 3∼4:1 from dried flower, and final powder is standardized in minimum 10 % of total polyphenols (septrophotometric method).

Cranberry extract (Nexira, France), is extracted from Vaccinium macrocarpon fruit. First stage of extraction consists of common process applied for juice fruit extraction (decantation and filtration). Recovered juice is purified on adsorbent resin. Acacia fiber is used as carrier for spraydrying stage in order to obtain a powder and the product is characterized in organic acids content (quininic, citric and malic acids). The final yield ratio is 12∼17:1 from fruit, and final powder is standardized in 17 % of total organic acids (HPLC method).

The ex vivo human skin samples were obtained by surgical surgery and were collected after informed consent. Immediately after surgery, each skin sample was placed in an appropriate transport medium specifically designed for tissue preservation to ensure its integrity until arrival at our facilities and subsequent use. The collection vial was stored at 4 °C from the sampling to its arrival and use of the sample a few hours later.

Caco-2 cell line was purchased from LGC standard SARL, ref. HTB-37, Donor #10064.

Determination of the non-cytotoxic concentrations of plant extracts on Caco-2

Caco-2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured for 48h at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 in supplemented DMEM (10 % iFCS, 1 % NEAA, 2 mM l-Glutamine, 1 % PS). The cells were then treated with eight concentrations of each plant extracts (ranging from 0.0046 to 10 mg/ml or 0.046–100 mg/ml depending on the extract; for each extract, the dried final powder was resuspended in culture medium before being diluted) and incubated for 24h. After treatment, the cells were incubated with WST-8 (highly water-soluble tetrazolium salt) reduced in a water-soluble orange colored product (formazan) by succinate dehydrogenase (mitochondrial enzyme). This transformation is proportional to the number of living cells and their metabolic activity. The optical density (OD) of the extracts at 450 nm was recorded with a spectrometer (VERSAmax, Molecular Devices) and the morphological aspect of the cells was observed with a microscope. The results were expressed as a percentage of viability compared to the untreated control. Each treatment was performed in triplicate, except the untreated control condition performed in sextuplicate. Depending on the cytotoxicity score obtained, optimal non cytotoxic concentrations were defined and used for the conduction of the subsequent in vitro trials.

Preparation of basolateral conditioned media (BM) on in vitro intestinal epithelial barrier model

The Caco-2 cells were previously cultured at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 in supplemented MEM (20 % iFCS, 1 % NEAA, 2 mM l-Glutamine, 1 % PS). The basolateral compartments of Millicell polycarbonate membrane cell culture inserts (0.6 cm2 inserts) were filled with the medium. Next, the cells were added in the apical compartments and grown for 14 days at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 to obtain confluent differentiated cell monolayers. The medium (basal and apical compartments) was renewed every 2 or 3 days. At D14, the medium was replaced by the medium less concentrated in iFCS (5 % iFCS) and the cells were incubated for 24 h. At D15, the integrity of the Caco-2 barrier was checked by measuring its Transepithelial Electrical Resistance (TEER; ≥1000 Ω cm2, internal validation criteria for a confluent and homogeneous Caco-2 barrier) with an ohmmeter. The culture medium in the basal compartment was then renewed and the culture medium in the apical compartment was replaced by culture medium containing or not (untreated control) the extract final powders to the pre-determined non-cytotoxic concentration of 5 mg/ml (Prickly pear extract), 1 mg/ml (Cranberry extract), 0.5 mg/ml (Olive extract and Hibiscus extract) or 0.25 mg/ml (combination of Immortelle and clove extracts). The cells were then incubated for 24 h. At the end of incubation (D16), the media from the basolateral compartments were collected (Caco-2 basolateral conditioned media: BM) to be used for the treatment of the skin explants. The Control (BM) condition corresponds to basolateral medium of Caco-2 cells not exposed to botanical extracts and is considered as the control condition to compare the effects of botanical extracts with. All experimental conditions were performed in triplicate and BM from each test condition were pooled before use as culture medium in the subsequent assays on skin explants.

Safety of untreated Caco-2 BM on ex vivo human skin

Upon receipt of the abdominal biopsy, the adipose tissue was removed, 8 mm-diameter punches were then performed in skin explants and incubated in supplemented DMEM (10 % iFCS, 1 % PS) for 24 h with the epidermis at the air-liquid interface.

The medium was then replaced by 10 % iFCS DMEM (skin culture medium) or Caco-2 BM (untreated control condition; 3 dilutions: pure, ½, ¼) and the skin explants were incubated for 72 h with treatment renewals after 24 and 48 h. Each condition was performed in monoplicate. At the end of incubation, one punch per test condition was placed in RNAlater™ stabilization solution at 4 °C overnight and then frozen at −20 °C until RNA extraction and gene expression profile analysis using microarray analysis.

Evaluation of treated Caco-2 BM on ex vivo human skin

A described in §2.5, 8 mm-diameter punches were performed in skin explants and incubated in supplemented DMEM (10 % iFCS, 1 % PS) for 24 h with the epidermis at the air-liquid interface.

The medium was then replaced by Caco-2 basolateral media collected in the basolateral compartment of Caco-2 cells treated or not (BM control) with the extracts. The skin explants were then incubated for 72 h with treatment renewals after 24 and 48 h. All experimental conditions were performed in quadruplicate (4 punches per condition). At the end of incubation, one punch per test condition was placed in RNAlater™ stabilization solution at 4 °C overnight and then frozen at −20 °C until RNA extraction, and the three remaining punches were dry frozen for lipid peroxidation analysis.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

The skin samples (punches) were homogenized with the Precellys-Cryolys homogenizer (Bertin technologies, France) and total RNA was extracted from each sample using NucleoSpin®RNA kit according to the supplier's instructions. The quality of total RNA was evaluated using capillary electrophoresis (Bioanalyzer 2100). The quantity of RNA was evaluated using a spectrophotometer (Synergy H1). From each RNA, a labeled and amplified anti-sens RNA (aRNA) was obtained using GeneChip 3′IVT PLUS Kit (Affymetrix). For each labeled and amplified aRNA sample, the profiles were evaluated before and after fragmentation using capillary electrophoresis (Bioanalyzer 2100, Agilent technologies).

Microarray analysis

Hybridization of fragmented aRNA onto Affymetrix® U219 chip (36,000 transcripts and variants) was performed in the GeneAtlas™ fluidics Affymetrix® hybridization station for 20 h at 45 °C. U219 chip was analyzed using the GeneAtlas™ Imaging station (Affymetrix® - resolution 2 μm) to generate fluorescence intensity data.

Raw signal intensities were obtained by Robust Multi-array Average method. Data were normalized with the Expression Console (Affymetrix®) software and a scatter plot representation of the modulated probesets was edited using Transcriptome Analysis Console (Affymetrix®). A probe set is a collection of probes designed to interrogate a given sequence of a gene. For data interpretation, the most important relative expression value obtained with one probe is considered to be representative of the corresponding gene.

Individual genes were considered to be expressed if relative expression values are >20. Genes were selected that meet the cut-off of absolute fold change ≥2 (for upregulated genes) and ≤0.5 (for downregulated genes). This approach ensured the identification of genes whose expression was significantly affected, deeming them worthy of further interpretation and analysis. Following the selection of these genes, they were subjected to a functional interpretation using bioinformatic tools, i.e., IPA 3.0 (Ingenuity Pathway Analysis, Ingenuity, QIAGEN, Redwood City, CA, USA) and DAVID (Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery [43,44], NIH, USA) to explore underlying biological and biochemical processes, pathways, and interactions related to these genes. The aim of this analysis was to provide a meaningful interpretation of the biological significance of the observed changes in gene expression profiles, therefore providing significant insights into the impact of the botanical extracts on skin health at the gene expression level.

Lipid peroxidation analysis

Skin samples were homogenized in aqueous solution. Then, saponification, acid hydrolysis and PFB ((Pentafluorobenzyl)hydroxylamine, hydrochloride) derivation were performed, followed by liquid/liquid extraction, with the organic phase dried under nitrogen at 50 °C. Residues were resuspended in 50 μl of hexane. Gas spectrometry and separation was achieved using a 30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm DB-5MS capillary column, and helium was used as a carrier gas at a constant flow of 1 ml/min. Injector (splitless mode) was set at 200 °C and transfer line temperature was set at 300 °C. Oven temperatures started with a 30 s hold at 50 °C, increased to 180 °C at 25 °C/min; then to 250 °C at 5 °C/min; to 300 °C at 25 °C/min, with a final hold for 5 min. Electronic impact was used using Selected Ion Monitoring (SIM) detection mode.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in tissues were quantified by dividing the MDA chromatographic peak area by the internal standard peak area and interpolating the result via comparison with a standard curve.

Statistical analysis

The results were presented as mean±Standard error of the mean. The inter-group comparisons of the experimental data were performed by an unpaired Student's t-test. It was considered significant when p < 0.05. Data were processed using GraphPad PRISM® 7.03 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

Validation of the bi-phasic culture system combining Caco-2 intestinal barrier model and ex vivo human skin

The cytotoxicity of the plant extracts on Caco-2 cells was assessed to avoid death interference and to determine the optimal concentrations to be used on Caco-2 intestinal barrier for each extract during the subsequent experiments. Caco-2 cell viability was assessed using WST-8 reduction assay after incubation with each plant extract for 24h. Depending on the percentage of viability obtained and the morphological observation of the cells, optimal non cytotoxic concentrations were defined and used for the conduction of the subsequent in vitro experiments (Table S1). In agreement with our results, the plant extracts were not toxic towards Caco-2 cells up to 5 mg/ml for Prickly pear extract, 1 mg/ml for Cranberry extract, 0.5 mg/ml for Olive extract and Hibiscus extract, and 0.25 mg/ml for the combination of Immortelle and clove extracts.

In parallel, the impact of three dilutions of Caco-2 basolateral medium (Control (BM); untreated control condition) was assessed on the transcriptional profile of ex vivo human skin to determine the optimal culture conditions for the subsequent evaluation of the effect of BM from Caco-2 incubated with the plant extracts (Extracts (BM)). The gene expression profile of the skin samples was investigated using a microarray analysis. Under our experimental conditions, the incubation of ex vivo human skin with Control (BM), used pure as a culture medium for 72 h, did not induce major modulations of the gene expression profile (Fig. 1). The modulations were of low amplitude and often on weakly expressed genes (only 14 upregulated genes and 18 downregulated genes). As dilutions ½ and ¼ of Control (BM) also very slightly modulated the transcriptional profile of the skin samples (data not shown), Control (BM) tested pure as a culture medium was selected for the evaluation of the plant extracts (BM).

Effect of Caco-2 basolateral conditioned medium (Control (BM)) used pure (100 %) on the gene expression profile of ex vivo human skin explants.

Effects of the plant extracts on transcriptional profile of ex vivo human skin

When analyzing the metadata, we observed the most modulated gene expression profile compared to Control (BM) following treatment with the combination of immortelle and clove extracts (594 genes) and the least following Hibiscus and Prickly pear treatments (197 and 102 genes, respectively) (Fig. 2). The other Extracts (BM), i.e., Olive and Cranberry extracts, modulated an intermediary number of genes (336 and 405 genes, respectively). Except for Hibiscus extract (BM), all Extracts (BM) mainly downregulated the expression of genes in ex vivo human skin. Despite the higher test concentration of Prickly pear extract (BM) compared to other plant extracts, it was not further investigated for potential skin health benefits in this model due to its lack in activity on genes modulations with extremely low number of implicated genes with low magnitudes of modulations. Consequently, the data set obtained from Prickly pear was not subjected to additional analyses.

Number of genes upregulated (Red) and downregulated (Blue) by the treatment of ex vivo skin explants with basolateral conditioned medium (BM) from Caco-2 treated with Olive extract, Hibiscus extract, Prickly pear extract, Combination of immortelle and clove extracts and Cranberry extracts – Results are shown versus explants treated with BM from non-treated Caco-2.

For the other extracts (BM), we submitted the data set to bioinformatics analysis tools (IPA and DAVID) to identify biological functions and processes significantly modulated by the treatments. The list was then curated manually by selecting only those related to skin health or physiology. Finally, for each biological function or process, only genes meeting the cut-off of absolute fold change ≥2 (for upregulated genes) and ≤0.5 (for downregulated genes) were considered of interest. Using this methodology of analysis, we identified two major processes or biological functions modulated following the treatment with Extracts (BM): 1) Genes expression for detoxification and protective enzymes against pollutant stressors and ROS was improved and 2) Genes expression related to synthesis and accumulation of lipids was inhibited.

We found that all Extracts (BM) showed antioxidant, anti-aging and detoxification activities as they upregulated the transcription of PON3, and genes coding for aldehyde dehydrogenases (ALDH2, ALDH3A1, ALDH3A2), metallothioneins (MT1G, MT1H, MT1X) and cytochromes (CYP3A5, CYP4B1) (Table 1 and Fig. 3). Comb. immortelle and clove extracts (BM) was the condition exhibiting the most effective antioxidant activity on this bi-phasic culture model as it upregulated a larger number of genes, including also GPX2 and GSTO2, with a higher fold change of their transcriptional expression.

Effect of the plant extracts on lipid peroxidation

Following the previous results on the gene expression profile of skin explants, we investigated the effect of Extracts (BM) on MDA (ie. lipid peroxidation) as a global marker of oxidative status (Fig. 6). Under our experimental conditions, the mean content of MDA in human skin explants cultured with Control (BM) condition was 194.8 pg/mg (±38.3). When treated with Extracts (BM), a decrease of MDA was observed. Olive extract (BM) and Hibiscus extract (BM) strongly and significantly decrease by 59 % and 78 % MDA contents in skin explants while the two other Extracts (BM), i.e., Combination of immortelle and clove extracts (BM) or Cranberry extract (BM), showed a lower and non-significant reducing effect on MDA production (49 % and 55 %, respectively).

Discussion

The nutricosmetic approach, while promising on skin health as demonstrated by clinical studies [45,46], remains weakly scientifically substantiated and in vitro studies are mainly performed by directly testing nutraceutical ingredients or products on ex vivo skin or in vitro skin cell models [47,48], neglecting to consider the in vivo bioavailability and metabolization of these products intended for oral administration. This oversight is largely due to the complexity of the interactions between nutritional ingredients and skin health, as well as the challenges of accurately measuring the effects of these ingredients at the skin level.

In the present study, we first set-up a functional bi-phasic in vitro model for the evaluation at the skin level of botanical ingredients intended for oral use after their passage and metabolization through a reconstructed intestinal barrier. Hence, this model was used to evaluate botanical extracts and identify their potential beneficial effects at the skin level using a transcriptional profile analysis and an evaluation of their protective effect against lipid peroxidation in the skin.

The first part of the model consisted of a Caco-2 monolayer to mimic selective intestinal passage, as in, the absorption of substances from the internal-side of the epithelial membrane, the partial metabolization of the substances by the epithelial cells, and the secretion of the metabolites as well as digestive enzymes into the external-milieu of the epithelial cells [38]. The second part of the model consisted of the evaluation on ex vivo human skin of the biological effects of the basolateral media collected after metabolization of the extracts after Caco-2 passage.

To verify the functionality of this bi-phasic model, several doses of the non-treated basolateral medium (BM) collected after Caco-2 passage were tested on ex vivo human skin. The innocuity of pure BM on ex vivo human skin was confirmed by the absence of significant modulations of the skin transcriptional profile.

After validation of our model, five plant extracts, i.e., Olive extract, Combination of Immortelle and Clove extracts, Prickly pear extract, Hibiscus extract and Cranberry extract, were then deposited, at non-cytotoxic concentrations, on the Caco-2 in vitro intestinal epithelial barrier and their corresponding basolateral media (BM) were then used as culture medium for ex vivo human skin to screen their potential beneficial effects on skin health using a metagenomic approach. Prickly Pear extract was the less effective as it did not modulate enough genes to expect benefit on skin health, therefore it was not further investigated in our study. Interestingly, the four other extracts demonstrated antioxidant and detoxifying activities through an upregulation of genes coding for enzymes involved in these processes. Among them, PON3 (paraoxonase 3), ALDH2 and ALDH3A1 (Aldehyde Dehydrogenases), MT1G, MT1H, MT1X (Metallothioneins), GPX2 (Glutathione Peroxidase 2) and GSTO2 (Glutathione S-transferase omega-2) are known to be involved or regulated by Nrf2 pathway. Modulation of transcription and gene expression has been found to play a significant role in the effect of phytonutrients on various cellular processes including the antioxidant defense mechanism and inflammatory processes. Two transcription systems, i.e., the antioxidant response element (ARE)/Nrf2 and NFκB pathway, are known to be modulated by phytonutrients although with contrasting effects [5,[49], [50], [51]]. To date, the Nrf2 pathway is considered the major mechanism of indirect antioxidant action of polyphenols in biological systems [47]. The ARE/Nrf2 system upregulates genes coding for antioxidant and detoxifying enzymes that neutralize harmful molecules in the skin, whereas NFκB upregulates genes involved in inflammatory processes, such as IL-6 and TNF-α release, and that are deleterious to the skin [52].

In our model, Olive extract, Combination of Immortelle and Clove extracts, Hibiscus extract and Cranberry extract also exhibited an inhibitory effect on lipid synthesis in ex vivo skin as they downregulated most of the genes involved in this process. This capacity of plant polyphenols to downregulate genes involved in lipid synthesis has already been reported on sebocytes and could be of interest for the treatment of acne vulgaris [53].

Interestingly, we also found that the four botanical extracts upregulated the expression of ASPN gene coding for asporin, which has been reported to be a critical regulator of collagen biosynthesis and assembly in the dermal matrix [54]. Recent studies suggest that ASPN could modulate the structural integrity of the skin [55], therefore, our findings of botanical extracts that improve ASPN gene expression indicate potential beneficial effects on skin structure. However, this finding will need further investigations.

To further investigate the antioxidant and detoxifying effect of the four selected botanical extracts, we analyzed their potential protective effect against lipid peroxidation in the skin explants through the analysis of MDA production, an oxidative stress biomarker. Lipids are an essential component of cell membranes and play a crucial role in maintaining skin barrier function. Lipid metabolism is also implicated in oxidative stress with peroxidation of lipids leading to the production of toxic compounds such as malondialdehyde (MDA). MDA has been shown to be elevated in various skin diseases and thus serves as a biomarker for lipid peroxidation and oxidative stressBinder et al., 2016; [56]. In our bi-phasic model, Hibiscus and Olive extracts showed a marked protective effect against lipid peroxidation as they strongly decreased MDA production in ex vivo skin. Cranberry and Combination of Immortelle and Clove extracts only showed a tendency to decrease MDA production.

Using our bi-phasic in vitro model, we demonstrated that our botanical extracts could have beneficial effects on skin health after their passage through a reconstructed intestinal barrier. In addition to their already known beneficial effects on the health of cardiovascular, hepatic and urinary systems, we have demonstrated that Olive extract, Combination of Immortelle and Clove extracts, Hibiscus extract and Cranberry extract can also have beneficial effects on skin cellular senescence through their effects on the expression of genes involved in or regulated by Nrf2 pathway and through the protection against lipid peroxidation. Other antioxidative parameters such as ROS content or antioxidative enzyme activity (superoxide dismutase and glutathione peroxidase) could be investigated using our bi-phasic in vitro model to confirm the efficacy of our extracts.

This innovative approach is of interest as it introduces a novel methodology enhancing the relevance and representativeness of preclinical studies in skin health applications, compared to standard in vitro models and where orally intended ingredient is directly applied on final targeted cell line. The bi-phasic model improves representation of in vivo conditions notably in the context of nutricosmetic ingredient. The insights gained from this work could potentially lead to the development of more effective and targeted nutricosmetic products, ultimately benefiting consumers and advancing the field of skin health.

However, our bi-phasic model remains a simplification of the in vivo route from oral intake to skin exposure. We may highlight different gaps which are not taken into account in this model: Even if some digestive enzymes are secreted from Caco-2 cells into apical compartment, they are limited, and the tested ingredient is not going through a complete digestion process (e.g. acidic medium, full panel of digestive enzymes, emulsion by bile salts) and subsequent recombination with food bolus is not reproduced here (e.g. protein binding). Main polyphenols are very resistant to digestion (catechins and their polymers) but some galloyl moieties can be hydrolyzed, changing the composition of what in vivo achieves small intestine and where sugar de/conjugation processes occur and also affect intestinal polyphenols bioavailability. Caco-2 cells provide a first stage of compound metabolization during ingredient passage, but the hepatic first-pass metabolism in vivo engages several structural changes of circulating compounds (e.g. de/phosphorylation of polyphenols). Organ distribution and pharmacokinetics into the body are also questionable. Finally, in vivo, non-digestible compounds achieve the colon where they could act on microbiota profile and/or function and be transformed in newly bioavailable compounds that penetrate the body and achieve blood stream.

Future model improvements could include a preliminary in vitro gastric digestion step and the use of a hepatic in vitro system. However, the biphasic model used in this study provides an improvement of in vitro prediction model in preclinical studies for skin health applications and paves the way for future research in this area.

Furthermore, as it should be of interest to validate the antioxidant, detoxifying and lipid peroxidation protective properties of ingredients at the skin level, we have decided to further investigate the effects at the skin level of Olive extract, Combination of Immortelle and Clove extracts and Hibiscus extract in an in vivo trial.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we set-up an innovative bi-phasic in vitro model combining a reconstructed intestinal barrier and ex vivo human skin for simulating the intestinal passage of edible botanical extracts assessed for skin benefits. After validation of this model notably using a full genome analysis to check the absence of significant modulations of the transcriptional profile of the skin, the effects of five botanical extracts were screened using this model. Among these extracts, Combination of Immortelle and Clove extracts, Olive extract, Hibiscus extract, and Cranberry extract all exhibited a large genes expression support for antioxidant and detoxifying properties in the skin. Combination of Immortelle and Clove extracts, Olive extract, and Cranberry extract exhibited a decrease in genes expressions implicated in lipid synthesis. The Combination of Immortelle and Clove extracts was the most active extract while Hibiscus extract was the least. As a biological consequence, Hibiscus extract and Olive extract exhibited a protective effect on oxidative marker (MDA) in skin explants treated under the same conditions.

However, additional analyses such as determination of ROS content and antioxidative enzymes using this model could confirm the cutaneous antioxidative properties of these extracts. Further robust in vivo trials including oral administration would also be necessary to validate the antioxidant, detoxifying and lipid peroxidation protective properties of our ingredients and related final benefits at the skin level.
