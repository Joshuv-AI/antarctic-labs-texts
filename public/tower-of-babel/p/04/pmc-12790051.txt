Association of Income Inequality and Sleep Duration With Cardiovascular Outcomes in China and the UK
Authors: Cong Li, Guangyao Hua, Yan Wang, Xue He, Jinghua Jiao, Xiaohong Yang, Honghua Yu, Lei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are a major global health issue, influenced by income level and sleep duration.ObjectivesThis study sought to assess the impact of income inequality and sleep duration on CVD outcomes in China and the United Kingdom.MethodsWe analyzed data from the China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB) (n = 486,131) and UK Biobank (UKB) (n = 403,858). Income level was categorized into low, medium, and high based on the annual household income, and sleep duration into short (<7 hours), optimal (7-9 hours), and long (>9 hours). Cox models estimated HRs for income level, sleep duration, and CVD outcomes.ResultsA total of 134,227 incident CVD cases and 16,852 deaths were identified. Low income was associated with higher CVD incidence and mortality in both cohorts (P < 0.001). Short and long sleep durations increased CVD incidence in both cohorts (P < 0.05), except for long sleep and ischemic heart disease in the CKB. Long sleep duration was associated with higher CVD mortality in both cohorts, whereas short sleep duration increased ischemic heart disease risk in the UKB. Compared with individuals of high income and optimal sleep duration, low income was associated with higher CVD risk across all sleep categories. Medium income raised CVD incidence risk in all sleep categories, with mortality associations varying by sleep duration between the CKB and the UKB.ConclusionsIncome level and sleep duration independently and jointly affect CVD incidence and mortality. Low income and suboptimal sleep duration are detrimental to cardiovascular health, highlighting the need for public health strategies to address income disparities and promote optimal sleep for CVD prevention.

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) continue to be a global public health challenge, which remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally, with significant variations in incidence and outcomes across different populations.1, 2, 3 Income serves as a critical socioeconomic determinant of health, with substantial empirical evidence demonstrating its influence on health disparities through multiple pathways, including differential access to health care resources and variations in health-related behaviors.4 Growing evidence indicated that the CVD burden is marked by significant inequities, with over 30% of cardiovascular deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries, and approximately 80% of CVD cases found in nations with medium or low gross national product per capita.5 Additionally, low income level has been directly associated with the morbidity of CVD and a heightened risk of CVD events.6 The link between socioeconomic status (particularly income level) and CVD is accentuated by the prevalence of biological, psychosocial, and behavioral risk factors among disadvantaged individuals.6

Sleep is vital to human health, with individuals spending approximately one-third of their lives asleep. It is increasingly recognized as a crucial lifestyle factor that can influence both CVD and mortality.7 In 2022, the American Heart Association's Life's Essential 8 score augmented the cardiovascular health framework through the inclusion of sleep duration as a key determinant,8 substantiating its role as a modifiable risk factor for CVD prevention. Inadequate sleep has been linked to increased cardiovascular risk due to reduced energy expenditure, heightened appetite, and altered glucose metabolism.9 Conversely, excessive sleep has also been associated with a higher risk of CVD.10,11 However, other studies reported contradictory findings.12,13 Therefore, findings are inconsistent, partly due to variations in study design, target populations, cultural background, and definitions of sleep duration.

A previous cross-sectional study using data from 8 cohorts across 4 European countries indicated that short sleep duration may be a potential mechanism underlying the association between social inequalities and ischemic heart diseases (IHDs).14 Despite ongoing research, the precise relationship among income level, sleep duration, and CVD events across diverse populations, particularly in countries with varying health care systems, cultural practices, and socioeconomic contexts, remains unresolved. Additionally, the joint associations between income level and sleep duration concerning CVD incidence and mortality require further investigation. Moreover, it is uncertain whether these findings hold consistently across subpopulations defined by age, sex, and region or ethnic groups.

This study aims to fill these gaps by investigating the associations among income level, sleep duration, and the incidence and mortality of CVD in 2 diverse populations, China and the United Kingdom. By leveraging data from large-scale population-based longitudinal studies, the China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB) and UK Biobank (UKB), in both countries, this research seeks to provide insights into how these factors interact and contribute to cardiovascular risk, potentially informing tailored public health strategies in different sociocultural contexts.

Methods

Study populations

This study used data from 2 large, prospective cohort studies including the CKB and the UKB. Details of the study design, survey methods, and population characteristics of the CKB and the UKB studies have been previously described elsewhere.15, 16, 17 Briefly, the CKB includes 512,724 participants aged 30 to 79 years from 10 diverse regions (5 urban and 5 rural) across China, recruited between 2004 and 2008. These regions were selected based on local disease patterns, exposure to key risk factors, population stability, quality of health registries, and local implementation capacity.15 Approximately one-third of those contacted responded and were subsequently enrolled in the study. Following the completion of the baseline survey, 2 follow-up surveys were conducted on 5%-6% of randomly selected surviving participants. The UKB is also a large population-based cohort consisting of 502,409 participants recruited in the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010. Of approximately 9.2 million individuals aged 40-69 years, living within a 25-mile (40 km) radius of 1 of 22 assessment centers across England, Wales, and Scotland, 5.5% took part in the baseline assessment. Both cohorts have extensive baseline data, including sociodemographic characteristics, medical history, and lifestyle factors, along with follow-up data on CVD events. In this study, we excluded the participants with a diagnosis of CVD at baseline, missing data for income level and sleep duration, or with sleep durations <4 or >12 hours to reduce implausible sleep durations.

Assessment of income level

Individual income level was classified using established cohort-specific thresholds for annual household income.18,19 In the CKB, the income level referred to the average total household income last year and was classified into 3 levels: low income (<20,000 Yuan), medium income (20,000-34,999 Yuan), and high income (≥35,000 Yuan). In the UKB, total household income before tax was obtained through questionnaires, and participants were classified into 3 levels: low income (<₤18,000), medium income (₤18,000-£51,999), and high income (≥₤52,000).

Sleep duration assessment

Sleep duration was assessed using standardized self-report questionnaires that have been widely employed and validated in both cohorts.20, 21, 22 In the CKB, participants self-reported their sleep duration at baseline by answering the question, "On average, how many hours of sleep do you get in a 24-hour period (including naps)?" Responses were recorded in 1-hour increments.23 In the UKB, sleep duration was also self-reported, with participants responding to the standardized question, "About how many hours of sleep do you get in every 24 hours? (Please include naps)," with answers provided in hours.24 If the duration of sleep varies significantly, individuals provide the average sleep duration for a 24-hour day over the past 4 weeks. The reported sleep durations were categorized into 3 groups: short (<7 hours), optimal (7-9 hours), and long (>9 hours). For the current study, we defined 7-9 hours of sleep as the reference group, following both the American Heart Association's Life's Essential 8 metrics and the American National Sleep Foundation guidelines.8,25

Incidence of CVD

In both the CKB and the UKB, CVD events were coded using the International Classification of Diseases-10th Revision, and the primary outcome events were occurring IHD (I20-I25), cerebrovascular diseases (I60-I69), and their composite endpoint (defined as the first occurrence of either condition).26,27

Mortality outcomes

Mortality data were obtained through linkage with national death registries, ensuring comprehensive and accurate tracking of mortality events in the CKB. Moreover, mortality status, date of death, and underlying primary cause of death were ascertained via data linkage to the National Health Service Information Center (England and Wales) and the National Health Service Central Register (Scotland) in the UKB. In both cohorts, mortality outcomes were classified using the International Classification of Diseases-Tenth Revision codes, encompassing all-cause mortality and cause-specific mortality (including IHD [I20-I25], cerebrovascular disease [I60-I69], and their composite CVD endpoint).

Covariates measurements

The covariates were obtained through questionnaires and physical examinations in both the CKB and the UKB. Information on covariates included sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, region, ethnicity, levels of education, household income, etc), lifestyle behaviors (smoking status, alcohol consumption, etc),28,29 self-rated health status,30,31 education, physical activity, body mass index, and medical history (hypertension and diabetes). Low, medium, and high level of education in the CKB refers to primary school and below, secondary school, and college and above, respectively.32 In the UKB, education was coded in 3 categories: high (college or university degree), medium (A/AS levels or equivalent, O levels/GCSEs or equivalent), and low (none of the aforementioned).33 For physical activity, the metabolic equivalent of task hours per day and metabolic equivalent of task minutes per week were calculated in the CKB and the UKB, respectively.

Statistical analysis

The follow-up period for each participant was calculated from the date of baseline examination to either the date of outcomes occurrence, the date of death, or the end of follow-up, whichever came first. Survival curves were constructed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate HRs and 95% CIs for CVD incidence and mortality associated with income level and sleep duration. The multivariate Cox proportional hazards analysis was performed using covariates that were significant in the univariate model. Incidence and mortality rates per 1,000 person-years were calculated. Proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using the Schoenfeld residual test. The assumption was satisfied for the primary exposures (income level and sleep duration), whereas covariates that violated the proportional hazards assumption were addressed through stratification. We then examined whether our results were only significant in a certain subgroup by age (<60 vs ≥60 years), sex, region (CKB only), ethnicity (UKB only), and prevalent hypertension. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings.

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA (version 17.0, StataCorp LP) and R (version 4.2.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Missing data patterns are detailed in Supplemental Table 1. For the UKB cohort, the missing values were handled using multiple imputations by chained equations. Statistical significance was set at a 2-sided P < 0.05.

Ethical approval

All participants provided written informed consent for the 2 studies. In the CKB, the study protocol was approved by the Oxford Tropical Research Ethics Committee (reference: 025–04) and the Ethics Review Committee of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (approval notice: 005/2004).34 Ethics approval for the UKB was obtained from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee (reference: 11/NW/0382, application no. 82906). This research has been conducted using the CKB resource (request no. DAR-2022-00236 and DAR-2024-00089). Publication of results does not require or imply approval by the membership of the CKB Collaborative Group.

Results

Population characteristics

Finally, 486,131 participants in the CKB, and 403,858 participants in the UKB were included in this study. The baseline characteristics of participants from CKB and UKB are comprehensively detailed in Supplemental Table 2. Additionally, characteristics of these cohorts stratified by income level and sleep duration are presented in Table 1 and Supplemental Table 3, respectively. The CKB cohort comprised 486,131 participants (mean age 51.51 years, 59.0% female), with income distribution: low (n = 277,271), medium (n = 120,553), and high (n = 88,307). The UKB cohort included 403,858 participants (mean age 55.91 years, 46.4% female), with income stratification: low (n = 87,795), medium (n = 209,313), and high (n = 106,750). Key demographic differences emerged between cohorts: low-income individuals were predominantly female in the CKB (vs male in the UKB) and more likely to reside rurally, be nonsmokers, have lower education, and be diabetes-free. Chinese low-income participants also showed lower alcohol consumption, poorer self-rated health, higher physical activity, and elevated body mass index. Incidence of CVD

Over a median 9.90-year follow-up (Q1-Q3: 8.83-10.98), the CKB cohort recorded 33,062 IHD and 58,768 cerebrovascular disease cases, whereas the UKB cohort documented 42,397 CVD events (29,631 IHD; 12,766 cerebrovascular) during a median 12.34-year follow-up (Q1-Q3: 11.4613.08), with both cohorts showing highest CVD incidence among low-income individuals combined with either short (CKB) or long (UKB) sleep duration (Supplemental Table 4). The Kaplan-Meier curves of CVD incidence for both cohorts are shown in the Supplemental Figures 1 and 2.

After full adjustment for demographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors, compared to those of high income, individuals with low and medium income remained significantly associated with increased risks of both IHD and cerebrovascular diseases in both cohorts, with particularly strong associations for cerebrovascular disease in the UKB cohort. Sleep duration patterns showed cohort-specific risks: in the CKB, short sleep was linked to higher IHD risk whereas both short and long sleep increased cerebrovascular risk; in the UKB, both sleep extremes elevated risks for both outcomes (Table 2). CVD-related mortality

The study documented 5,123 IHD deaths and 7,726 cerebrovascular deaths in the CKB (median follow-up 10.16 years; Q1-Q3: 9.22-11.13 years) and 2,762 IHD deaths and 1,241 cerebrovascular deaths in UKB (median follow-up 12.45 years; Q1-Q3: 11.72-13.15 years). Low-income participants showed the highest CVD mortality rates in both cohorts. Long sleep duration was associated with the highest mortality in the CKB, whereas the UKB exhibited higher cerebrovascular mortality among short sleepers. The Kaplan-Meier curves of CVD mortality for the CKB and the UKB are shown in Supplemental Figures 3 and 4.

Compared to high-income groups, both medium- and low-income groups were associated with increased IHD and cerebrovascular mortality in the CKB. In the UKB, medium and low income were associated with higher IHD mortality, whereas only low income was associated with increased cerebrovascular mortality. Sleep duration effects differed by cohort: long sleep was consistently associated with increased IHD and cerebrovascular mortality in both cohorts, whereas short sleep showed significant associations only with IHD mortality in the UKB (Table 2). The associations observed for composite CVD mortality mirrored those for IHD, with both medium and low income levels and prolonged sleep duration linked to higher mortality risk in both cohorts. In contrast, short sleep duration was associated with increased CVD mortality risk only in the UKB cohort (Supplemental Table 4).

The joint analyses revealed that low-income individuals exhibited significantly elevated risks of both IHD and cerebrovascular mortality across all sleep duration categories in both cohorts. A differential risk pattern emerged for medium-income individuals, where increased IHD mortality was observed across sleep durations except among long sleepers in the CKB. For cerebrovascular mortality, medium income was associated with increased risk across all sleep durations in both cohorts, except for suboptimal sleep durations in the UKB. High-income individuals exhibited no significant sleep duration–related mortality risks for either cardiovascular outcome in either cohort (Figure 2, Supplemental Table 5). The analysis of composite CVD mortality revealed that both low- and medium-income groups demonstrated consistently elevated mortality risks across all sleep duration categories in the cohorts. In contrast, high-income individuals showed no increased risk except for those with long sleep duration in the CKB cohort (Supplemental Table 6). The all-cause mortality analysis yielded findings consistent with CVD-specific results, demonstrating elevated risks among individuals with lower income levels or suboptimal sleep durations in both cohorts (Supplemental Table 7). Stratified analyses of subgroups

Stratified analyses by age, sex, region or ethnicity, showed that the associations of income level, sleep duration with CVD incidence and mortality were stronger in younger than in older, in female than in male, in rural than in urban, and in non-White than in White participants in both the CKB and the UKB (Supplemental Tables 9 to 11). Similar trends were found in the joint association analyses of income and sleep duration with incidence and mortality of CVD (Supplemental Tables 12 to 14).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses, excluding events that occurred within the first 2 years of follow-up, demonstrated the robustness of the results, which were consistent with the main analyses, affirming the stability and reliability of the findings (Supplemental Tables 15 and 16). Additionally, analyses that excluded participants with cancer or chronic respiratory disease at baseline showed similar trends to the main analyses (Supplemental Tables 17 and 18).

Discussion

This study examined the associations between income level, sleep duration, and the incidence and mortality of CVD using the data from 2 large population-based studies in China and the United Kingdom, which covered diverse geographic, socioeconomic, and cultural countries, and used standardized techniques in measuring exposures and outcomes. Our analysis revealed that both low income and abnormal sleep duration (short or long) were independently associated with increased risks of CVD incidence and mortality in both the CKB and UKB cohorts. Importantly, the combined effects of low income and abnormal sleep duration appeared to amplify these risks, particularly for IHD and cerebrovascular diseases (Central Illustration). Impact of Income Inequality and Sleep Duration on CVD Outcomes

This multicenter prospective study examined the impact of income inequality and sleep duration on cardiovascular disease (CVD) outcomes in China and the United Kingdom. Low income independently increased CVD incidence/mortality risks, whereas both short and long sleep durations elevated CVD incidence. Jointly, low income consistently raised CVD risks across all sleep durations, with medium income showing variable mortality associations. CKB = China Kadoorie Biobank; UKB = United Kingdom Biobank.

In the CKB cohort, individuals with low income exhibited a significantly higher risk of both IHD and cerebrovascular disease incidence, regardless of their sleep duration. Notably, short sleep duration was associated with an increased risk of IHD, which is in line with a previous report,35 whereas both short and long sleep durations were linked to elevated risks of cerebrovascular diseases. Several population-based studies in Western countries have consistently shown that each extreme of the sleep duration distribution has been associated with CVD and other adverse health outcomes, with more consistent associations observed for long sleep duration.10,36,37 In our study, these associations were even more pronounced when combined with low income, suggesting a synergistic effect that exacerbates cardiovascular risk. Similarly, in the UKB cohort, low income was consistently associated with higher risks of CVD incidence and mortality. Unlike in the CKB cohort, long sleep duration in the UKB cohort was associated with a greater risk of both IHD and cerebrovascular diseases compared to optimal sleep duration. This difference might reflect the distinct socioeconomic and health care contexts in the 2 countries, which could influence the impact of sleep duration on cardiovascular health.5,38 The observed association between prolonged sleep duration and cardiovascular risk may be explained through several potential mechanisms. First, this association might be mediated by concomitant factors including sleep fragmentation, daytime sleepiness, or depression—all of which are well-established cardiovascular risk factors.39,40 Additionally, emerging evidence has demonstrated associations between extended sleep duration and elevated levels of inflammatory markers, particularly C-reactive protein and fibrinogen, which may potentially contribute to cardiovascular pathogenesis.41,42 Furthermore, growing evidence suggests that prolonged sleep duration may adversely influence cardiovascular health through detrimental effects on vascular function, including increased arterial stiffness and abnormal blood pressure variability.43

Our findings in both CKB and UKB are consistent with previous research demonstrating the significant impact of socioeconomic status on cardiovascular health.44,45 Our study advances the evidence linking socioeconomic factors to cardiovascular risk, reinforcing the importance of addressing social determinants in prevention strategies.46 The elevated CVD burden among individuals with low income stems from a combination of biological, behavioral, and psychosocial risk factors that are more commonly found in disadvantaged populations.47,48 A deeper understanding of income level and an effective risk stratification are essential initial steps in identifying and customizing interventions to reduce CVD risk within the population. A study from England found that whereas IHD mortality has declined across all socioeconomic groups,49 the decline was most pronounced in the wealthiest group compared to those with lower socioeconomic status. Similar patterns were observed in Brazil, where high-income subjects exhibited faster improvements than their low-income counterparts.50 Additionally, several countries have reported a plateau in IHD mortality reductions among younger individuals with low income level. In both the United States and Scotland,51,52 this trend may be driven by growing disparities in lifestyle and diseases profile among disadvantaged populations.53 These findings raise concerns that, despite overall improvements in cardiovascular care, the socially advantaged are benefiting more, exacerbating health inequalities.

Our study emphasizes that sleep duration significantly mediates the relationship between income level and IHD, particularly within low- and medium-income groups. This aligns with previous findings from a European cohort study, which identified that inadequate sleep heightens CVD risk, particularly in individuals with lower adult socioeconomic status.14 In the European study, socioeconomic status was determined based on both paternal and adult occupational status, and self-reported sleep duration was classified as recommended (6-8.5 h/night), long (>8.5 h/night), or short (<6 h/night). The study focused on 2 cardiovascular outcomes: IHD and stroke incidence, without addressing CVD mortality. In contrast, our study extends beyond these parameters by examining both the incidence and mortality of IHD and cerebrovascular diseases across various socioeconomic status categories and exploring how sleep duration influences these associations. Additionally, we found that short and long sleep durations contribute differently to CVD risks across income groups, with long sleep also emerging as a significant risk factor for CVD mortality, offering a broader perspective than the European study.

The study revealed significant population-specific patterns in how high-income status modifies the cardiovascular risks associated with short sleep duration. Although short sleep duration did not confer additional IHD or cerebrovascular risk among high-income UK participants, Chinese individuals within this socioeconomic group maintained elevated risk for these outcomes. This divergence likely reflects fundamental differences in how socioeconomic advantage mediates health protection across populations.54, 55, 56 Several factors may contribute to explain these population differences. Potential ethnic variations in physiological responses to sleep deprivation could differentially affect cardiovascular stress pathways. Additionally, China's distinctive high-pressure professional environments may generate work-related stressors that attenuate the typical cardioprotective benefits of high socioeconomic status. Furthermore, systemic differences in health care access and preventive care use patterns between populations may modify the sleep-CVD relationship. Although our observational design cannot establish causality, these findings highlight the context-dependent nature of sleep-related cardiovascular risk, underscoring the importance of developing population-tailored prevention approaches that account for local socioeconomic and cultural contexts.

At present, we found that low income combined with either short or long sleep duration significantly increases CVD risk of incidence and mortality, particularly in younger individuals, female participants, rural residents, and non-White populations. Younger adults exhibited higher risk due to career-related stress and lifestyle neglect. Cardiovascular risk factors are highly prevalent among economically active young adults in Spain. Whereas an optimal lifestyle is strongly linked to a healthier cardiovascular profile, such a lifestyle is rare within this population.57 In addition, female participants showed greater risks likely due to the dual pressures of work and caregiving.58 Recent evidence indicates that women who care for family members or spouses with CVD may suffer an elevated risk of developing cardiac issues themselves.59 Cardiovascular health across the urban-rural divide is not clearly understood, but in individuals with socioeconomic inequalities, poor sleep patterns, and living in rural areas, elevating CVD risk may stem from limited health care access and strenuous labor, particularly in China. China has experienced unprecedented internal migration over the past 2 decades, with over 145 million rural residents moving to urban areas.60 This rapid urbanization has exacerbated health care disparities between urban and rural regions. In 2009, per capita health care expenditure in urban areas was 2,176.6 Yuan (351.8 USD), which is significantly higher than the 562.0 Yuan (90.8 USD) spent in rural areas.61 Additionally, the urban-rural gap is reflected in the number of health care providers, with 7.62 technicians per 1,000 people in cities compared to just 3.04 per 1,000 in rural regions.61 In the United Kingdom, non-White individuals seem to have systemic inequalities exacerbating their cardiovascular vulnerability. There are ethnic inequalities in health care use and care quality among people with multiple long-term conditions (eg, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and depression) in the United Kingdom.62,63 Evidence suggests that individuals from Black, Asian, and minority ethnic groups in the United Kingdom are at a higher risk of developing multiple long-term conditions compared to the White majority population.64 Our subgroup findings underscore the importance of implementing targeted public health interventions to reduce these disparities and enhance health outcomes among high-risk populations.

Study strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including the use of large, well-characterized cohorts with comprehensive data on income level, sleep duration, and cardiovascular outcomes. The comparison of 2 diverse populations provides unique insights into the role of sociocultural factors in shaping the associations among income, sleep, and CVD risk. However, there are also limitations. First, the reliance on self-reported sleep duration may introduce measurement bias, because individuals may not accurately recall their sleep habits. Meanwhile, this single-question measure cannot fully capture day-to-day sleep variability compared to objective methods. Several potentially important covariates, including chronic kidney disease status, obstructive sleep apnea diagnosis, specific medication use (eg, sedatives or opioids), and private health insurance status were unavailable in our data set. Future studies incorporating these factors could further elucidate these relationships. Second, apart from the contribution of sleep duration, we must acknowledge the role of other potential confounders or mediators of the socioeconomic gradient in CVD, including life-related factors, working hours, psychosocial exposures, and environmental factors, whose contribution was not examined in the current study. Third, the observational nature of the study precludes causal inferences, and residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out despite adjustment for a range of potential confounders. Fourth, the differences in follow-up periods between cohorts reflect their distinct initiation timelines, which may affect comparability, though time-to-event analyses account for this variation. Moreover, potential selection bias arising from demographic discrepancies in our cohort should be considered when assessing the generalizability of these findings. Fifth, although focusing on IHD and cerebrovascular events, we acknowledge peripheral arterial disease as another important cardiovascular outcome. Future studies including these endpoints would enable more comprehensive CVD risk assessment. Besides, our income thresholds followed cohort-specific protocols rather than national standards, which may affect cross-population comparisons. Finally, our assessment of socioeconomic position relied solely on household income as an ordered categorical variable, which provides limited precision about actual income levels. Whereas our study focused specifically on income level as the primary socioeconomic measure, we recognize that socioeconomic status is a multidimensional construct encompassing education, geographic location, and other factors. Although we adjusted for educational attainment and urban/rural residence in our analyses, the use of income alone may not fully capture the complexity of socioeconomic disadvantage. Future studies would benefit from incorporating composite socioeconomic status measures that integrate multiple socioeconomic indicators to better understand their collective impact on cardiovascular health.

Conclusions

This study underscores the significant impact of both individual socioeconomic status and sleep duration on the incidence and mortality of CVD in China and the United Kingdom. The findings highlight the need for public health strategies that address both socioeconomic disparities and sleep health as part of comprehensive CVD prevention efforts. Future research should explore the underlying mechanisms driving these associations, with a focus on identifying modifiable factors that could mitigate the heightened cardiovascular risks associated with low socioeconomic status and abnormal sleep duration.
