Perforated Cameron's Ulcer from Incarcerated Paraesophageal Hernia with Gastric Volvulus
Authors: Elsayed Elsafy, Shabaz Khan, Minghui Huang, Fiona Evans, Hongyi Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Cameron ulcers are linear erosions or ulcerations in the setting of large hiatal hernias with potential to lead to life-threatening complications. We report a rare complication of a Cameron ulcer in the setting of an incarcerated paraesophageal hernia with gastric volvulus.Case Description:An 84 year old female presented in shock from gastric perforation. Imaging demonstrated significant pneumoperitoneum, free fluid, and a large incarcerated paraesophageal hiatal hernia with gastric volvulus. The patient underwent diagnostic laparoscopy revealing a perforated Cameron ulcer with subsequent laparoscopic reduction of incarcerated parasesophageal hiatal hernia, repair of Cameron ulcer perforation, and abdominal washout. Postoperative course was complicated by multiple intra-abdominal collections, mediastinitis, venous thromboses, choledocholithiasis, and persistent leak from gastric perforation, ultimately transitioning her care to comfort measures only.Discussion:This case highlights a rare but severe manifestation of Cameron ulcers and underscores the importance of early recognition of complications associated with large paraesophageal hernias.

INTRODUCTION

Cameron ulcers are linear gastric mucosal ulcerations that occur within large hiatal hernias, first described by Cameron and Higgins in 1986.1 These lesions typically develop on the crests of gastric folds at the level of the diaphragmatic hiatus in the context of a sizable hiatal hernia. Hiatal hernias are broadly classified into sliding (Type I) and paraesophageal or rolling hernias (Types II–IV), with Cameron lesions reported in association with both subtypes, though more commonly in large paraesophageal hernias. Cameron ulcers are often an underrecognized cause of gastrointestinal blood loss, most commonly presenting as chronic occult bleeding leading to iron deficiency anemia in patients with large paraesophageal hernias.2,3 The pathogenesis is thought to involve mechanical trauma and repetitive shearing of the gastric mucosa at the hiatus, along with focal ischemia from compromised blood flow and exposure to gastric acid in the herniated segment.3 While many Cameron lesions remain asymptomatic or cause only indolent anemia, they can occasionally produce acute upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage—including rare cases of life-threatening bleeding—especially in the setting of giant hiatal hernias.4 In contrast, perforation of a Cameron ulcer is an exceedingly rare event. Only isolated cases of gastric ulcer perforation associated with hiatal hernias have been reported in the literature, and such cases often involve large, incarcerated paraesophageal hernias in elderly patients.5,6

Paraesophageal hernias (classified as Type II, III, or IV hiatal hernias) comprise a small minority of all hiatal hernias but carry a higher risk of serious complications due to incarceration and volvulus of the stomach.7 In an incarcerated paraesophageal hernia, a portion of the stomach becomes trapped within the chest, which can lead to organoaxial gastric volvulus and strangulation. It is important to distinguish paraesophageal hernia from gastric volvulus, as they represent overlapping but distinct pathological processes: the former describes herniation of the stomach through the diaphragmatic hiatus, whereas the latter refers to abnormal rotation of the stomach (most commonly organoaxial, less frequently mesenteroaxial) that may occur as a complication of a paraesophageal hernia. Acute gastric volvulus in this context may result in compromised blood supply to the herniated stomach, culminating in ischemic ulceration and, in extreme cases, perforation of the gastric wall.6 Here, we present a case of an 84-year-old female with an incarcerated paraesophageal hernia and organoaxial gastric volvulus, who developed a perforated Cameron ulcer. This case highlights a rare but life-threatening complication of giant hiatal hernias and underscores the importance of early recognition and intervention in patients with large paraesophageal hernias.

CASE DESCRIPTION

An 84-year-old female with a past medical history of anemia, aortic regurgitation, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and hypothyroidism presented with several days of abdominal pain and was found to be hypotensive by emergency medical services.

In the emergency department, she was found to be tachycardic, hypotensive, tachypneic and hypothermic, and had abdominal distension with diffuse peritonitis. Initial labs were notable for lactate of 9.9 mmol/L, anion gap metabolic acidosis with an anion gap of 24, serum creatinine of 2.25 mg/dL, pro-B-type natriuretic peptide of 7,201 pg/ml and normal leukocyte level. Computed tomography (CT) of chest, abdomen, and pelvis on arrival showed significant pneumoperitoneum with free fluid in the abdomen, pelvis, and within the hiatal hernia sac, a large incarcerated paraesophageal hiatal hernia and significant mural thickening edema of the gastric antrum, ultimately raising suspicion for possible gastric ulcer perforation (Figure 1).

(A) Axial CT on presentation demonstrates a large hiatal hernia with a predominantly intrathoracic stomach and associated pneumoperitoneum. (B) Sagittal CT reconstruction demonstrates the intrathoracic stomach within the hernia with associated free intraperitoneal fluid extending into the hernia sac.

A decision was made to perform an emergent laparoscopic reduction of gastric volvulus and gastropexy, as well as repair of gastric perforation. In the operating room, the patient was found to have gastric content contamination in the entire abdomen and pelvis with extensive inflammation and fibrinous exudates. Small bowel loops were very dilated and tethered to the anterior lateral abdominal wall. Further exploration revealed a large incarcerated paraesophageal hiatal hernia with mixed organo-axial and mesenterico-axial gastric volvulus and significant upper retention to gastric outlet and duodenal due to hernia incarceration. In order to reduce the hiatal hernia, the right crura of the diaphragm was incised. The patient was found to have a 1.5-cm gastric perforation with bowel content spillage along the lesser curvature. The area was at the level of the diaphragmatic hiatus, consistent with Cameron ulcer perforation. Perforation was repaired primarily with Graham patch reinforcement. Bile was aspirated from the mediastinum and all quadrants of the abdomen and pelvis were washed out. Gastropexy was then performed and a JP drain was left in the subhepatic space next to the gastric perforation repair. Placement of a gastrojejunostomy tube was considered during the case. However, given that the patient was in septic shock and the gastric tissue was already tenuous after reduction of the hernia, a decision was made to minimize operative time and bring the patient to intensive care unit (ICU) for continuous resuscitation as soon as possible. Should a patient survive the initial catastrophe, a gastrojejunostomy tube would be placed at a later time with a gastropexy already in place.

Postoperatively, the patient required dual pressor support and CVVH. She was noted to have bilious output in her JP drain starting on postoperative day (POD) 2. Leakage of repair was not noted on imaging, however, the patient was given blue Gatorade on POD 10, which was quickly identified in the JP drain, concerning for leakage of the repair. Endoscopic suturing was considered okay but ultimately not pursued. Her postoperative course was also complicated by multiple mediastinal and pelvic collections (Figure 2), thrombus in the inferior vena cava, portal venous system and splenic vein, iliopsoas hematoma after initiation of anticoagulation. Given her multiple complications and failure to progress, and after ongoing multidisciplinary goals of care discussion, the patient transitioned to comfort measures only on POD 14 and passed the following day.

CT on POD 8 revealed mediastinal abscess (A) and deep pelvic abscess (B).

DISCUSSION

This case illustrates a rare and severe manifestation of a Cameron ulcer in the setting of a giant paraesophageal hernia with gastric volvulus. Cameron lesions are most commonly associated with chronic gastrointestinal bleeding and anemia and are found in approximately 5–10% of patients with large hiatal hernias.2,3 Acute presentations such as overt hemorrhage are less common but have been reported.4 In contrast, the progression of a Cameron ulcer to free perforation is extraordinarily uncommon but has been documented in a handful of cases.5,6 These reports, along with the present case, suggest that although Cameron ulcers generally present as a chronic bleeding source, they can very rarely lead to an acute surgical emergency with high mortality.

Several pathophysiological factors in the present case likely contributed to ulcer formation and eventual perforation. The patient’s large paraesophageal hernia may have caused persistent mechanical stress on the gastric mucosa at the diaphragmatic hiatus, predisposing to Cameron ulcer development. In addition, the incarceration of the stomach in the chest with gastric volvulus could have created a closed-loop obstruction and compromise of the gastric blood circulation. Gastric volvulus is a known complication of paraesophageal hernias and can precipitate strangulation with resultant ischemic necrosis of the stomach wall.7 The classic clinical features of acute gastric volvulus include the Borchardt triad (severe epigastric pain and distension, unproductive retching, and inability to pass a nasogastric tube), reflecting acute obstruction and strangulation. In our patient, however, these specific signs were overtaken by the presentation of diffuse peritonitis and shock from the perforation. It is likely that the combination of mucosal ischemia from strangulation and direct frictional trauma at the hernia orifice led to a full-thickness ulcer perforating at the site of the Cameron lesion.

Emergent surgical intervention is indicated for patients with perforated viscus or gastric strangulation in the context of a hiatal hernia. In the present case, the patient underwent an urgent diagnostic laparoscopy, which confirmed a perforated Cameron ulcer on the stomach within the hernia sac. A laparoscopic approach was undertaken to reduce the hernia and volvulus of the stomach, followed by primary repair of the ulcer perforation and an abdominal washout. Gastropexy was also performed after reduction to help prevent recurrence of the herniation and volvulus. Notably, definitive antireflux surgery (e.g., fundoplication) or a large gastric resection was not performed in this setting, due to the patient’s unstable condition and the need to minimize operative time and complexity. There is limited literature guiding the optimal surgical management for complicated paraesophageal hernias with gastric necrosis or perforation, given the rarity of this scenario. Traditionally, an open laparotomy with reduction of the hernia, resection of nonviable gastric tissue, and repair of the hiatus has been the standard approach in emergent cases.6,7 However, there are increasing reports of successful laparoscopic management of acute gastric volvulus and incarcerated hiatal hernias in hemodynamically stable patients, offering the benefits of reduced postoperative pain and shorter hospital stay.7,8 In our case, given the patient’s advanced age and frailty, an open approach would perhaps have been more appropriate and would have allowed for more thorough washout. On the contrary, utilizing a laparoscopic approach is overall less invasive and technically challenging to perform when considering the typical need for complete hernia sac reduction to visualize the stomach and the damage control nature of this surgery.

There have been no reported precedents of thoracic surgery involvement in managing perforated Cameron’s ulcer. There is a report of video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) for acute mediastinitis after a traumatic gastric rupture in a much younger patient with evidence of loculated fluid collections in paraesophageal and pleural cavity.9 In a another report of mediastinal emphysema secondary to perforated gastric ulcer in an elderly patient, thoracic exploration was not pursued and mediastinal emphysema resolved with nonoperative management.10 In our case, given the intraoperative finding of mediastinal contamination, thoracic exploration for mediastinal washout and drainage could also be considered. However, given patient frailty and comorbidity, it is unlikely that she would tolerate a thoracic surgery.

Despite prompt surgical management, our patient’s outcome was poor, reflecting the high morbidity and mortality associated with acute complications of paraesophageal hernias. In addition, this patient’s complicated postoperative course likely contributed under the context of poor tissue healing leading to a persistent leak despite surgical repair. Large population studies and case series have shown that patients who require emergency surgery for giant hiatal hernias have significantly worse outcomes than those who undergo elective hernia repair.8 Moreover, emergent repair of giant paraesophageal hernias is associated with higher perioperative morbidity and poorer functional recovery, suggesting that watchful waiting in asymptomatic patients may carry risks that outweigh its benefits in some cases.8 This case therefore underscores the importance of considering elective surgical correction for large paraesophageal hernias, especially in patients with anemia or other signs of mucosal injury, to prevent progression to acute perforation. Early identification of Cameron lesions in patients with hiatal hernia should prompt medical management with acid suppression and iron repletion, and referral for surgical consultation. This report adds to the growing evidence that large paraesophageal hernias, even if initially stable, can lead to life-threatening emergencies, and it reinforces the need for vigilance and appropriate management to improve outcomes in this high-risk population.
