Introduction

The global food system faces increasing challenges, needing to reduce the impacts of current systems while feeding a growing population. Livestock production is integral to feeding the planet, and animal health, welfare and breeding contribute ­significantly to its sustainability. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO, 2023) gives guidance on improvements to the livestock supply chain, while increasing food accessibility. Key takeaways include productivity increases (per animal) and breeding (reducing calving interval and adopting optimal genetics) which are major contributors to reducing the overall footprint. Adoption of vaccinations against common diseases can also reduce the emissions and the size of the supporting herd and hence the land take.

A sustainable food system is a food system that ensures food security and nutrition for all so that the economic, social and environmental bases to generate food security and nutrition of future generations are not compromised (High Level Panel of Experts, 2014). The Global Roundtable for Sustainable Beef (GRSB)’s definition of sustainable beef encompasses a socially responsible, environmentally sound, and economically viable system that prioritizes planet, people, animals, and progress.

Animal Health and Animal Welfare are part of the definition of sustainable Beef; for any system to be considered sustainable, it must encompass three pillars: environmental health, economic viability and ethical/social acceptance, which are the “Triple Bottom Line” of sustainability (Elkington, 1997). We consider these three aspects and the ways in which health and welfare contribute to sustainable beef production, and how actions aimed at sustainability reinforce health and welfare.

Ethical Sustainability

We have a duty of care to the animals we raise for food, and must ensure a “life worth living” by minimizing negative experiences and providing the opportunity for positive experiences (Matheny and Chan, 2005; Mellor, 2016).The same ethical concern should be shown to those working in the industry, often in very low-income areas and relying on livestock for finance, food, draft power, and security. Farming shapes landscapes and local economies and therefore has the potential to benefit whole societies.

The “One Health” concept (Gibbs, 2014) is defined as a worldwide strategy for expanding interdisciplinary collaborations and communications in all aspects of health care for people, animals, and the environment. This builds on the ethical imperative; livestock production impacts human health through controlling zoonoses and improving nutritional quality. This ethical responsibility extends to prudent pharmaceutical stewardship exemplified by minimizing use of critically important antibiotics, e.g., reduction of macrolide use in pre-weaning calves though improved vaccination and management of cattle to maximize resilience both in pastured cattle and in feedyards.

Health–Welfare–Husbandry Interactions

It is clear that good animal health contributes to welfare and vice versa. Apart from ethical reasons for good welfare, it makes good financial sense for all those in the chain to ensure that cattle are well treated from birth onward. Reducing the burden of endemic disease on production systems has huge implications for human and animal health, profitability, and environmental impact (Oxford Analytica, 2023).

Management procedures such as disbudding early, with anesthesia and analgesia (Herskin and Nielsen, 2018), leads to calves that eat better and are less likely to succumb to infections. Similarly, strategies such as fenceline weaning are less stressful (Mac et al., 2024) leading to less movement and increased appetence. This produces calves better conditioned and resistant to disease for later transport and finishing. Genetic selection adapts animals to climate, available forage, or topography and endemic diseases and is a foundation for a healthy herd (Simm et al., 1996). Practical Husbandry interventions illustrate synergies for health and welfare: mineral supplementation (Palomares, 2022), wintering practices in pasture based systems (Schütz et al., 2025), reducing manure, and stocking density in housed or penned cattle (Macitelli et al., 2020) all contribute to welfare and directly to health.

Environmental Sustainability

While the global food system faces the challenges of increasing demand while reducing impact, food production itself is affected by climate change and biodiversity loss. Uniquely, agriculture has the ability to contribute to both adaptation and mitigation of climate change as well as the restoration of nature (Rivera-Ferre et al., 2016). Livestock are part of the solution as the majority of agricultural land (70%) cannot grow human-edible crops; ruminants upcycle grass and other roughage into nutrient dense meat and milk with highly bioavailable minerals and vitamins that are deficient in many human diets (Mottet et al., 2017).

The beef industry has been on a decreasing emissions trajectory per kilogram of product (intensity) for decades. There are numerous ways we can reduce emissions associated with beef production, in intensity as outlined above in health, welfare, and husbandry interactions, and if adopted widely enough a reduction in the total emissions of the sector. Productivity increases per animal (see Figure 1), e.g., reduced calving interval, and breeding are major contributors to reducing emissions, as are animal health, feed quality, and reducing food waste. On the other side of the equation, soil carbon sequestration acts as a sink. In addition, rumen manipulation via feed additives, genetics, or vaccines reduces enteric methane at source. In intensive systems, manure management and biogas capture can turn emissions into an energy source. Based on an understanding of the potential the beef industry has to reduce climate impact, GRSB set a goal to reduce emissions by 30% by 2030, on a pathway to net zero. As methane is degraded in the atmosphere over around 12 yr, it is termed a short-lived GHG, which means that any reduction in emissions from a given herd can contribute to reduced temperature forcing (Cain et al. 2019).

With respect to intensity of emissions, a major challenge comes from increasing demand in developing countries, where herd increases are the primary means of increasing supply (Thornton, 2010); investment in those regions is essential to meet demand sustainably by increasing efficiency rather than through herd expansion.

Contributions Toward Lowering Impacts of Livestock

A sustainable future for all means investing to ensure that developing countries progress on a more sustainable path than High Income Countries have over the last 2 centuries. Investment in veterinary medicine and husbandry extension in lower income countries is critical to the sustainability of animal production and the availability of nutritious animal source foods for growing populations. Providing transparency and insights on good husbandry, health, and welfare along the chain also contributes to greater value for both primary producers and buyers (Maia de Souza et al., 2017).

The connection between the expansion of the Brazilian cattle herd and deforestation is often cited, along with the resulting biodiversity loss and associated GHG emissions. There are many factors that contribute to deforestation, including timber extraction, land value speculation, and the desire to bring more land into crop production; in short economic development. The total footprint of the cattle herd in Brazil has not grown since the 1990s, but it has moved with the frontier and cattle numbers have increased (Telles et al., 2024). With improved productivity, the footprint and size of the national herd could shrink, as has been the case in the US. While deforestation rightly receives a large amount of attention, grassland ecosystems are the most threatened on the planet; the pressures on many grasslands include conversion to producing human food, feed, or bioenergy as well subdivision, urban sprawl, and infrastructure development.

Regenerative approaches to land management contribute to ecosystem services via the water cycle, improving biodiversity and soil carbon sequestration. Such management has potential to reverse land degradation (Angerer, 2023). Up to half of global rangelands face some form of degradation, and there are further large areas of degraded croplands, but pastoralism can mitigate biodiversity losses (Briske et al., 2026). Restoration could relieve pressure on conversion of forests and other ecosystems, but requires investment.

Well-managed grazing and silvopastoral systems not only sequester carbon but also improve animal comfort and health through shade and diverse forage. Well-managed grazing preserves and enhances the biodiversity and ecosystem functions of native grasslands, regardless of the species involved, just as a lack of management can result in degradation even where wild species are involved. The fact that regenerative systems also contribute to adaptation (Brown et al., 2022) and mitigation of climate change (Gosnell et al., 2020) enhances economic and ethical sustainability, even if they do not maximize yield per unit area.

Within such approaches, silvopastoral systems (SPSs) offer a triple win (Poudel et al., 2024). SPSs can enhance biodiversity, carbon capture, and water cycle, whilst increasing carrying capacity, income and resilience. They improve animal health and welfare through reducing surface temperature and increasing shade and provide a wider diversity and higher quality of feed and micronutrients. Despite establishment of SPSs being generally more expensive than grass systems to establish, the return on investment is only a few years and benefits to people, planet, and animals are clear.

Economic Sustainability

The productivity gains enabled by better health and welfare also drive economic viability; the system must be economically viable to survive and improvements such as greater efficiency often enhance ethical and environmental sustainability at the same time. Without adequate inputs, healthcare and good husbandry, an expanding herd has significant environmental impacts, whereas a herd that increases production per head over time while decreasing in size reduces impact while feeding more people. Health for Animals’ report found that “Every 1% reduction in beef cattle disease rates would: a) increase production enough to meet the average beef consumption needs of 317 million people b) increase producer revenue by US$3.2 billion.” (Oxford Analytica, 2023).

Vaccinations, and veterinary pharmaceuticals help keep livestock healthy, productive, and profitable. These routine treatments make the difference between viability and economic loss and directly impact the livelihoods of the people involved. Yet there are large areas of the world where availability of vaccines and treatments is limited, the distances and lack of infrastructure to access veterinarians make it impossible to keep up regular vaccinations or treatments. Animals located more than one hour from veterinarians are overwhelmingly (93.8%) in low- and middle-income countries (Criscuolo et al., 2025). As a result, simple problems remain unrecognized or untreated due to lack of extension advice. Consequently, Africa and Asia, where two thirds of the large ruminants in the world are found, only produce one third of bovine products;

Good health and welfare results in more product per animal or per day of life and lower impact. This is the trajectory followed in higher-income countries, which in turn means a smaller footprint and at least the opportunity for more nature positive production. U.S. total cattle inventory peaked around 1975 at 132 million head, and is today 93 million, a reduction of 29% (Fancher et al., 2025), but beef production is 17% higher at around 12.2 million tonnes in 2022. If the United States still produced in the same way as in 1975, the country would need a herd of 60 million more head than it actually has, using more water, land and feed.

Genetic improvements help animals thrive, in turn contributing to ethical acceptance, profitability and reduced carbon intensity. Biomarkers associated with residual feed intake (Kenny et al., 2018) are now included in some estimated breeding values and estimated progeny differences. Since feed represents around 70% of costs, this can represent a significant saving to producers in terms of feed required to achieve a given output. While use of high potential genetics is an important tool, we should not lose sight of the need to preserve genetic diversity in cattle, as it may help us meet challenges in the future.

Conclusion

To conclude, transitioning livestock production to more sustainable systems is possible, but not automatic. Current system dynamics lack resilience in the face of multiple challenges, including biodiversity, climate crises, and lack of financial incentives. Trade arrangements that favor a race to the bottom in terms of price and political decision making that does not invest in or support transformation are all obstructions to sustainability. We need investment in all aspects of animal health and welfare to help drive the combined benefits to human lives, health, and livelihoods that increased animal production with lower resource use and impact can deliver.
