Exploring the clinical characteristics and etiological diagnosis of Chlamydia psittaci pneumonia confirmed by targeted next-generation sequencing: A retrospective observational study
Authors: Zhenzhen Yang, Cheng Chang, Ruiting Feng, Lu Zhang, Xiaolin Zhang, Shuman Du, Yinsen Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chlamydia psittaci (C psittaci) is an important causative agent of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP). However, conventional pathogen detection methods are often ineffective in the early diagnosis of CAP, making C psittaci pneumonia challenging to detect. C psittaci ranges from mild, self-limited to acute respiratory failure, and the early identification of this disease can be challenging. Targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid has the potential to improve the pathogen identification in C psittaci pneumonia. This retrospective study aims to systematically evaluate the clinical characteristics and etiological diagnostic value of tNGS in patients with C psittaci pneumonia, providing further insights into the diagnosis and management of this disease. We performed a retrospective analysis of samples obtained from 40 patients diagnosed with C psittaci pneumonia using tNGS at Zhengzhou People’s Hospital between January 2024 and March 2025. The clinical characteristics, laboratory findings, radiological features, and treatment strategies for each patient were systematically evaluated. The analysis revealed that C psittaci pneumonia was frequently associated with avian or poultry exposure. Predominant clinical manifestations included fever, cough, sputum production, headache, and fatigue. Laboratory tests commonly indicated elevated neutrophil percentage, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, and interleukin-6 levels, alongside reduced median lymphocyte percentage, eosinophil percentage, and albumin levels. Chest computed tomography scans revealed the presence of unilateral or bilateral pulmonary infiltrates. Upon tNGS-based pathogen identification, antimicrobial therapy was promptly adjusted to doxycycline and moxifloxacin, which resulted in favorable clinical outcomes. Although C psittaci-induced CAP lacks pathognomonic symptoms, characteristic laboratory markers may aid its early identification. The tNGS was found to be a rapid and accurate diagnostic tool and demonstrated substantial clinical utility in the timely detection of C psittaci pneumonia. Future studies with larger sample sizes and prospective designs are warranted to validate these findings.

1. Introduction

Psittacosis, caused by the obligate intracellular gram-negative (G−) bacterium Chlamydia psittaci, is a zoonotic disease that can progress to community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) and cause potentially life-threatening complications, including severe pneumonia and acute respiratory distress syndrome.[1,2] Avian exposure is the primary risk factor for this condition, with human transmission occurring via direct contact or the inhalation of aerosolized pathogens from the excreta of infected birds or respiratory/ocular secretions.[3] The rapid progression of the disease highlights the critical importance of early diagnosis and prompt clinical intervention. However, conventional diagnostic methods, such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and serological testing, are limited by their suboptimal sensitivity and specificity.[4,5] Furthermore, nonspecific clinical manifestations often result in incorrect or delayed diagnosis.[6]

The advent of metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has revolutionized pathogen diagnosis in infectious diseases, particularly in respiratory infections.[7,8] Multiple studies have demonstrated the robust diagnostic performance of mNGS in the detection of C psittaci infections.[9,10] Currently, targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) is being adopted increasingly in clinical practice. Compared with mNGS, tNGS offers superior advantages as a more rapid and accurate nucleic acid-based detection method. However, the application of tNGS in C psittaci pneumonia diagnosis has been systematically evaluated in a limited number of studies.

In this retrospective study, we used tNGS as a promising novel diagnostic method for C psittaci pneumonia. We attempted to summarize the clinical manifestations of psittacosis while evaluating the diagnostic value of tNGS, providing clinical references for early diagnosis and treatment decision-making.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This retrospective study included 40 patients with C psittaci pneumonia who were admitted to Zhengzhou People’s Hospital from January 2024 to March 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients diagnosed with pulmonary infection, identification of C psittaci-specific DNA fragments by tNGS, and availability of complete clinical data. The exclusion criteria were the following: failure to pass tNGS quality control during sample collection or testing procedures, and incomplete clinical and laboratory data.

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Zhengzhou People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2025-KY-004902) and strictly adhered to the ethical principles for retrospective studies outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, as well as local regulatory requirements. As this study solely involved analysis of anonymized retrospective data and met all criteria for ethical exemption, the Ethics Committee formally waived the requirement for obtaining written informed consent.

2.2. Data collection

Patient data were retrieved from the electronic medical record system, including baseline clinical characteristics (demographic information, comorbidities, epidemiological history, and clinical symptoms), laboratory test results (complete blood count, inflammatory markers, bacterial infection biomarkers, blood biochemistry, myocardial injury markers, and clinical microbiology tests), radiological imaging findings (location and characteristics of pulmonary lesions, including consolidation, interstitial changes, and pleural effusion), and treatment regimens. All collected data underwent systematic evaluation and analysis.

2.3. Targeted next-generation sequencing

2.3.1. Nucleic acid extraction

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid or sputum samples were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 minutes in microcentrifuge tubes. After discarding the supernatant, 600 μL of the remaining sample was retained for nucleic acid extraction. Nucleic acids (DNA/RNA) were extracted using a co-extraction kit (DP307; Tiangen, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted nucleic acids were stored at −20°C for subsequent library preparation.

2.3.2. Library construction and sequencing

Species-specific primers were designed based on conserved genomic sequences from representative pathogenic microorganisms. Clinical samples underwent optimized multiplex PCR amplification using these primer sets, followed by purification of the amplicons. A second-round PCR was then performed to incorporate sequencing adapters (including sequencing primers and index sequences). After additional magnetic bead purification, the final sequencing library was obtained.

The constructed multiplex pathogen libraries were pooled in equal amounts. DNA nanoballs were generated using the DNA nanoballs Preparation Kit (cat. no. 1000026466; MGI Tech, Wuhan, China) and sequenced on the MGISEQ-200 platform with single-end 75 bp read length. A minimum output of 100,000 reads per sample was required for subsequent bioinformatics analysis.

2.3.3. Data analysis

Following sequencing, the obtained amplicons were processed and analyzed to identify high-quality pathogen-specific amplicons. Subsequently, the amplicons were mapped back to the designed target regions to determine the species of pathogenic microorganisms present in the samples.

2.3.4. Interpretation of tNGS results

The identification of potential pathogens was based on the amplicon coverage and normalized read count of microbial sequences amplified with target-specific primers. To categorize a microorganism as a potential pathogen, the following criteria were established: bacteria (excluding Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex), fungi, and atypical pathogen: amplicon coverage ≥ 50% and normalized read count ≥ 10; viruses: amplicon coverage ≥ 50% and normalized read count ≥ 3, or normalized read count ≥ 10; and M tuberculosis complex: normalized read count ≥ 1.

Subsequently, 2 experienced clinicians independently assessed the patient’s clinical data, blinded to each other’s assessments, to determine the presence of pulmonary infection and the clinical relevance of potential pathogens. The comprehensive assessment incorporated medical history, symptoms, imaging findings, tNGS results, and conventional microbiological test outcomes. Any discrepant interpretations were resolved by consulting a senior physician to reach a consensus.

2.4. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk). Continuous variables following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent Student t tests. For non-normally distributed continuous variables, data were presented as median with interquartile range (median [Q1, Q3]) and analyzed using Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages (n [%]) and compared using χ2 tests, with Yates correction or Fisher exact tests applied when appropriate based on expected cell frequencies. A two-tailed P value < .05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

3. Results

3.1. Patient characteristics

The study cohort comprised 40 eligible patients with a median age of 57 years (mean age of 54 ± 16 years). The study had 19 male patients (47.5%) and 21 female patients (52.5%). Among the enrolled participants, 26 patients (65.0%) had underlying comorbidities, with hypertension (32.5%), diabetes mellitus (23.0%), and coronary heart disease (12.5%) being the most prevalent ones. Epidemiological investigation revealed a history of avian exposure in 22 cases (55.0%). Fever (38/40, 95.0%) was the most common clinical manifestation, followed by cough (29/40, 72.5%), sputum production (27/40, 67.5%), headache (25/40, 62.5%), and fatigue (25/40, 62.5%). The tNGS analysis showed the presence of C psittaci, with sequence reads ranging from 6 to 223,676 across samples (Table 1).

3.2. Distribution of pathogens in patient samples

The tNGS analysis of the 40 enrolled patients with C psittaci infection revealed definitive or probable pathogenic microorganisms. The tNGS-detected pathogens were further analyzed. Among the pathogenic infection types, bacterial and viral coinfections showed the highest detection rate (35.0%, 14/40), followed by fungal infections (25.0%, 10/40).

In this study, G− bacteria were detected at a higher rate (10/40, 25.0%) than gram-positive (G+) bacteria (8/40, 20.0%). Among G− isolates, Klebsiella pneumoniae showed the highest detection rate (G−, 5/40, 12.5%), followed by Acinetobacter baumannii (G−, 2/40, 5.0%) and Escherichia coli (G−, 2/40, 5.0%). Among G+ bacteria, Staphylococcus aureus (G+, 3/40, 7.5%) and Tropheryma whipplei (G+, 2/40, 5.0%) were predominant (Fig. 1A).

Among DNA viruses, Human alphaherpesvirus 1 was most frequently detected (7/40, 17.5%), followed by Epstein–Barr virus (2/40, 5.0%). Among RNA viruses, Influenza A virus, Human rhinovirus C, Human rhinovirus B, Human rhinovirus A, Human respiratory syncytial virus, and Human coronavirus NL63 were each detected once (1/40, 2.5%) (Fig. 1B).

3.3. Comparison of infection types

Etiological classification helped distinguish between C psittaci monoinfection and mixed infections, with mixed infections defined by the concurrent detection of additional pathogenic microorganisms along with C psittaci in a single specimen. Our analysis revealed monoinfection in 10 cases (25.0%) and mixed infections in 30 cases (75.0%). Among mixed infections, coinfection of C psittaci with bacteria (26.7%, 8/30) and triple infection with bacteria and viruses (20.0%, 6/30) were predominant, followed by fungal–viral coinfection (20.0%, 6/30) (Fig. 2).

3.4. Laboratory findings

Here, we analyzed laboratory results from 40 patients, including their complete blood counts, inflammatory marker levels, bacterial infection indicators, blood biochemistry markers, myocardial injury markers, clinical microbiology tests, and infection-related immune cytokine levels.

Upon admission, all 40 C psittaci-infected patients exhibited a significantly elevated median neutrophil percentage (76.6%), median C-reactive protein (CRP) levels (80.8 mg/L), and median procalcitonin (PCT) level (0.2 ng/mL). In contrast, the median lymphocyte percentage (13.2%), median eosinophil percentage (0.1%), and mean albumin levels (37.3 g/L) were lower. Other laboratory parameters – including the white blood cell count and the alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, lactate dehydrogenase, and creatine kinase levels – remained within normal ranges (mean or median values). In clinical microbiological testing, traditional culture methods yielded a positivity rate of 30.0% (12/40), whereas molecular biological methods showed pathogen presence in 27.5% (11/40) of the cases (Table 2). Infection-related immune cytokine profiling involved testing for interleukin (IL)-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-17, IL-1β, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha. Among the 40 patients, 18 patients (45.0%) showed elevated cytokine levels, with uniformly elevated IL-6 levels. The remaining 55.0% of the patients (22/40) tested negative.

Upon the tNGS-based identification of C psittaci infection, antimicrobial therapy was optimized to either doxycycline (85.0%, 34/40) or moxifloxacin (22.5%, 9/40). All patients demonstrated significant clinical improvement, leading to successful discharge. Comparative therapeutic analysis revealed statistically significant intergroup differences. The monoinfection cohort exhibited a significantly higher moxifloxacin utilization during empirical treatment (60.0% vs 40.0%, P = .041), whereas the mixed-infection group demonstrated the predominant utilization of imipenem-cilastatin sodium (0.0% vs 46.7%, P = .007). Post-tNGS therapeutic adjustment did not alter this differential pattern, with the monoinfection group continuing to show elevated moxifloxacin administration (50.0% vs 13.3%, P = .041).

4. Discussion

According to the Global Burden of Disease Collaborative Network, pneumonia and other lower respiratory tract infections affected nearly 600 million people worldwide in 2019 and resulted in 2.5 million deaths.[11] CAP is often associated with delayed or inconclusive etiological diagnosis, which leads to the administration of empirical antibiotic therapy; this may contribute to the irrational use or overuse of antimicrobial agents and subsequent resistance.[12] In recent years, the pathogen spectrum of CAP has shifted, with an increasing prevalence of atypical pathogens. Owing to limitations in diagnostic methods, C psittaci remains one of the most frequently missed pathogens that causes severe CAP.[13,14] Early diagnosis is critical for managing psittacosis, as timely therapeutic adjustment can significantly improve prognosis.

The classic clinical manifestations of C psittaci pneumonia include fever, cough, headache, fatigue, and myalgia.[15–17] In this study, the 40 participants exhibited varying clinical presentations but had common features. Fever (95.0%) was the most prevalent symptom, followed by cough (72.5%), sputum production (67.5%), headache (62.5%), and fatigue (62.5%). Some patients also exhibited nasal congestion/rhinorrhea, vomiting, or diarrhea. The nonspecific and heterogeneous symptomatology of psittacosis – lacking pathognomonic characteristics that can help distinguish it from other CAP pathogenic infections – has led to substantial underdiagnosis.[18] Although C psittaci infection is associated with higher rates of severe pneumonia, even critically ill patients can achieve full recovery with accurate and prompt treatment. Transmission occurs through direct contact with birds, avian nasal discharges, or contaminated feces, or through the inhalation of feather dust,[19] with rare reports of human-to-human spread.[18] Notably, 55.0% (22/40) of our patients had a history of avian exposure. This underscores the diagnostic relevance of avian exposure, given the high zoonotic risk associated with recurrent bird contact.

Conventional diagnostic approaches for C psittaci infection have substantial clinical limitations. Standard bacterial cultures exhibit low sensitivity and prolonged processing time, and serological testing offers limited diagnostic value in the early stages. Although real-time PCR was previously considered the gold standard for psittacosis detection, its clinical utility is restricted to acute-phase infections owing to its variable sensitivity.[5] The recent increase in confirmed psittacosis cases likely reflects the increasing clinical application of mNGS.[20] However, the high cost and extended turnaround time of mNGS have relegated its application primarily to diagnostically challenging cases.[21,22] The tNGS represents a significant technological advancement, combining ultra-multiplex PCR with high-throughput sequencing. This innovative approach uses multiple specific primers to selectively amplify pathogen-specific nucleic acid sequences while depleting human-derived DNA, enabling the comprehensive analysis of target pathogens in clinical samples.[23,24] Compared with mNGS, tNGS offers distinct advantages, such as a lower processing time, lower costs, and the simultaneous detection of both DNA and RNA pathogens.[25]

In our study cohort of 40 C psittaci-infected patients, analysis of samples using tNGS revealed that bacterial and viral coinfections were most prevalent (35.0%), followed by fungal infections (25.0%). Among bacterial pathogens, G− organisms (25.0%) were detected more frequently than G+ species (20.0%). H alphaherpesvirus 1 (17.5%) and C albicans (17.5%) emerged as the most commonly identified viral and fungal pathogens, respectively. Etiological classification revealed mixed infections in 60.0% of cases, with C psittaci co-occurrence with bacterial infections (26.7%, 8/30) or bacterial–viral coinfections (20.0%, 6/30) being predominant, followed by fungal–viral coinfections (20.0%, 6/30). These findings underscore the diagnostic superiority of tNGS in pathogen detection while highlighting the need for careful interpretation of its results based on its technical constraints. The high frequency of polymicrobial infections in our study emphasizes the importance of comprehensive diagnostic approaches in psittacosis management.

Current evidence indicates that C psittaci pneumonia typically presents with elevated CRP and PCT levels, normal or mildly higher white blood cell counts, an accelerated erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and frequent abnormalities in hepatic enzymes, cardiac markers, and coagulation parameters.[26,27] Our findings corroborated these observations. We observed a significantly elevated median neutrophil percentage (76.6%) and CRP (80.8 mg/L) and PCT (0.2 ng/mL) levels upon admission, along with a lower median lymphocyte percentage (13.2%), eosinophil percentage (0.1%), and mean albumin level (37.3 g/L). Notably, comparative analysis revealed statistically significant differences in the platelet counts between monoinfected and mixed-infected groups (232.2 ± 119.5 vs 223.9 ± 85.1 × 109/L, P = .002). This hematological distinction may reflect differing pathophysiological mechanisms in pure versus polymicrobial C psittaci infections, suggesting the potential utility of platelet parameters as distinguishing markers for infection complexity. Platelets serve as a central hub connecting inflammatory responses and coagulation dysfunction. Thrombocytopenia has previously been established as an independent predictor of poor prognosis in patients with severe infections and pneumonia. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that thrombin-stimulated platelets play a critical role in the activation and recruitment of neutrophils, promote the release of neutrophil extracellular traps, and directly contribute to viral pathogenesis in the lung.[28,29] Dynamic monitoring of platelets and related parameters may therefore serve as a valuable approach for predicting infection severity, alerting to complications, and assessing prognosis.

Cytokines, a class of biologically active small polypeptides or glycoproteins primarily secreted by innate and adaptive immune cells, play pivotal roles in immune regulation. Their quantitative assessment is considered an established method for evaluating immune status, facilitating disease diagnosis, and monitoring therapeutic responses in clinical practice.[30,31] Emerging evidence highlights the crucial involvement of specific cytokine networks in the progression of respiratory infections.[32] In our prospective analysis of 40 patients with psittacosis, 45.0% (18/40) of the patients exhibited elevated serum cytokine levels, with IL-6 levels exhibiting global elevation in these cases. The consistent upregulation of IL-6 suggests its potential role as a key mediator in C psittaci infection, possibly through the activation of the infection-associated cytokine cascade. IL-6 is a multifunctional cytokine that plays a regulatory role in inflammatory and immune responses.[33] Previous studies have demonstrated that IL-6 and IL-8 levels were closely associated with pulmonary infection severity, and the cytokine profile showing increased levels of IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha is significantly associated with pulmonary infections.[34,35] Detection of these cytokines may help differentiate the types of pathogenic bacteria causing lung infections. The observed peripheral blood cytokine elevation patterns may serve as valuable diagnostic indicators, reflecting the complex immunopathological mechanisms triggered in psittacosis.

Chest CT findings in C psittaci pneumonia primarily indicate pulmonary consolidation, pleural effusion, ground-glass opacities, and air bronchograms. The lesions typically originate as unilateral lobar inflammation, progressing to bilateral multilobar involvement with disease progression, occasionally accompanied by small pleural effusions. Patients with multilobar lesions face a significantly higher risk of respiratory failure.[5,36] Here, we analyzed the imaging results of the 40 participants. Initial chest CT scans revealed left lung involvement (80.0%), right lung involvement (85.0%), bilateral involvement (65.0%), consolidation (37.5%), pleural effusion (35.0%), and interstitial changes (7.5%). No significant differences were observed between the monoinfected and mixed-infected groups (Table 3). The high frequency of bilateral involvement (65.0%) and consolidation (37.5%) indicated the aggressive inflammatory nature of C psittaci, consistent with the known clinical progression patterns of C psittaci infection. The comparable radiographic presentations among infection types suggest that imaging characteristics alone may not be reliable for differentiating between pure and mixed C psittaci infections. This underscores the need for comprehensive microbiological testing in clinical evaluation.

Pharmacological studies incorporating intracellular activity, pharmacokinetic profiles, and clinical trial evidence have demonstrated that C psittaci remains susceptible to tetracyclines, macrolides, and fluoroquinolones, which exert antimicrobial effects by disrupting DNA replication and protein synthesis.[19,37,38] However, emerging reports indicate increasing tetracycline and macrolide resistance among Chlamydia species, prompting a clinical shift toward fluoroquinolone use despite their marginally inferior efficacy compared with tetracyclines and macrolides.[39,40] To ensure therapeutic effectiveness and prevent relapse, a minimum treatment duration of 10 to 14 days is mandatory.[41] In our cohort, all patients underwent either tetracycline or fluoroquinolone therapy. Following the confirmation of C psittaci infection using tNGS, antibiotic regimens were promptly adjusted to doxycycline (85.0%, 34/40) or moxifloxacin (22.5%, 9/40), with subsequent clinical improvement observed among patients and, eventually, their discharge from the institution. For patients with concurrent pathogen infections, targeted antimicrobial therapy was administered based on the pathogens identified and the clinical manifestations observed. Post-tNGS treatment analysis revealed significantly higher moxifloxacin utilization in the monoinfected versus mixed-infection groups (50.0% vs 13.3%, P = .041). Mixed-infection cases frequently required additional antibiotics, including imipenem-cilastatin (30.0%, 9/30), piperacillin-tazobactam (23.3%, 7/30), and cefoperazone-sulbactam (10.0%, 3/30). These findings position fluoroquinolones, particularly moxifloxacin, as potential first-line agents for C psittaci pneumonia. However, further clinical studies are warranted to validate their therapeutic efficacy and optimize treatment guidelines.

5. Limitations

This study had several limitations owing to its single-center retrospective design. First, the relatively small sample size may have introduced bias in the analysis, and the lack of a priori sample size power analysis means the findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Therefore, further validation in larger, well-powered studies is warranted. Second, as there are no molecular biological or serological diagnostic kits for C psittaci pneumonia available commercially at present, all patients in our study were diagnosed using tNGS, which may have led to the underestimation of the actual incidence of C psittaci pneumonia. These limitations highlight the need for prospective multicenter studies with standardized diagnostic criteria and larger sample sizes to better characterize the clinical features and optimize the management of C psittaci pneumonia.

6. Conclusion

In summary, C psittaci pneumonia is predominantly associated with avian exposure history and is typically characterized by a high fever, often accompanied by respiratory symptoms (cough, sputum production, and dyspnea) and systemic symptoms (headache and fatigue). Laboratory findings include elevated neutrophil percentages and increased CRP, PCT, and IL-6 levels, whereas chest CT results commonly show the presence of unilateral or bilateral pulmonary infiltrates. The tNGS has emerged as a rapid and accurate diagnostic tool for the early detection of C psittaci. Our findings support the selection of fluoroquinolones (particularly moxifloxacin) as first-line therapeutic agents and emphasize the importance of prompt diagnosis and standardized treatment for optimal outcomes.
