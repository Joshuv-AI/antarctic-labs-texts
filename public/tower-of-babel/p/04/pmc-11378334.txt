Electronically Reconfigurable Memristive Neuron Capable of Operating in Both Excitation and Inhibition Modes
Authors: Lingxiang Hu, Zongxiao Li, Jiale Shao, Peihong Cheng, Jingrui Wang, Athanasios V Vasilakos, Li Zhang, Yang Chai, Zhizhen Ye, Fei Zhuge
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThreshold switching (TS) memristors are promising candidates for artificial neurons in neuromorphic systems. However, they often lack biological plausibility, typically functioning solely in an excitation mode. The absence of an inhibitory mode limits neurons’ ability to synergistically process both excitatory and inhibitory synaptic signals. To address this limitation, we propose a novel memristive neuron capable of operating in both excitation and inhibition modes. The memristor’s threshold voltage can be reversibly tuned using voltages of different polarities because of its bipolar TS behavior, enabling the device to function as an electronically reconfigurable bi-mode neuron. A variety of neuronal activities such as all-or-nothing behavior and tunable firing probability are mimicked under both excitatory and inhibitory stimuli. Furthermore, we develop a self-adaptive neuromorphic vision sensor based on bi-mode neurons, demonstrating effective object recognition in varied lighting conditions. Thus, our bi-mode neuron offers a versatile platform for constructing neuromorphic systems with rich functionality.Keywords: Memristor, Threshold switching, Filamentary mechanism, Artificial neuron, Neuromorphic computing

Conventional von Neumann computing architectures are approaching their physical limits, prompting a surge of interest in brain-inspired neuromorphic computing. This approach offers a highly parallel and event-driven scheme of computation that is highly energy-efficient.1 Central to the development of neuromorphic computing architectures are artificial synapses and neurons.2,3 While the emulation of basic biological synaptic functions, such as short-term and long-term plasticity, has been successfully realized using different techniques,4−13 including complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor (CMOS) circuits4,5 and emerging memory devices,6−13 the dynamic behaviors of biological neurons present a greater challenge. Artificial neurons are generally constructed using CMOS circuits containing many active components,14−16 aiming to replicate the intricate behaviors of their biological counterparts. Recent advancements have introduced several emerging electronic devices, such as threshold switching (TS) memristors,17−22 phase-change devices,23,24 spintronic devices,25,26 and transistors,27,28 proposed to function as artificial neurons. These devices have shown promise in mimicking a few neuronal functions, including leaky integrate-and-firing (LIF),18,19,23 threshold-driven firing,18,22,27 and all-or-nothing behavior.17,22,28 Their development holds potential for neuromorphic chips with ultrahigh integration density. Nevertheless, a notable limitation of these devices is their relatively low biological plausibility, as they are often restricted to operating in a single mode, i.e., excitation.

In the intricate networks of the cerebral cortex, neurons exhibit remarkable capabilities in spatially and temporally integrating input signals from numerous synapses, comprising both glutamatergic excitatory signals and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) inhibitory signals.29 Specifically, glutamate, a primary neurotransmitter, elicits an increase in neuronal membrane potential, amplifying excitatory responses, whereas GABA induces a membrane potential decrease, promoting inhibitory responses.30 This intricate balance between excitatory and inhibitory signals is crucial for regulating neuronal firing patterns, enabling the human brain to execute complex cognitive functions, including behavioral and emotional regulation.29 Similarly, for artificial neurons in neuromorphic computing systems, the ability to synergistically integrate excitatory and inhibitory signals is pivotal for ensuring computing accuracy and stability.31,32 However, achieving this synergy poses a challenge. Traditional CMOS neurons necessitate highly complex digital circuits, hindering their ultrahigh-density integration.33−35 Adjusting gate voltage (VG) allows emerging single transistor-based neuron devices to implement inhibition.31,36 Nevertheless, two-terminal TS memristors face limitations in their inability to operate in inhibition mode due to insufficient state-changing dynamics, severely restricting their potential applications in neuromorphic computing.

In this work, a novel artificial neuron capable of operating in both excitation and inhibition modes is realized based on a unique bipolar TS (BTS) memristor featuring a simple structure of Cu/ZnS/Pt wherein the ZnS thin film was deposited via thermal evaporation. The device exhibits self-compliance semivolatile TS characteristics during positive voltage sweeping, with relatively stable threshold voltages (Vths); that is, its resistance state does not return to the initial state after the first TS cycle (electroforming or FORM process) owing to a residual Cu filament segment. Upon negative voltage sweeping, further dissolution of the residual Cu leads to an increase in the Vth. Importantly, the Vth value can be reversibly modulated by tuning the length of residual Cu using both positive and negative voltages. This property enables the device to function as an electronically reconfigurable bi-mode neuron, capable of responding to both excitation and inhibition stimuli without requiring peripheral circuits. The inherent characteristics of the BTS memristive neuron facilitate its integration into neuromorphic systems, offering versatility in computational tasks. For instance, given the fact that the synergistic effect of excitatory and inhibitory inputs on neuronal firing dynamics plays a key role in dark and light adaptation of the human eye,37 a BTS neuron-based neuromorphic vision sensor enables autonomous adaptation to external brightness changes. This work provides an ingenious approach to implementing highly biologically plausible artificial neurons.

Figure 1a provides a schematic representation of a basic biological neuron network, illustrating the interaction between pre-neurons and a post-neuron through synapses. In this setup, the post-neuron integrates both the excitatory and inhibitory signals from pre-neurons. Upon receiving excitatory inputs, the neuron’s cation channels, predominantly Na+ and K+, open, allowing an influx of these ions to the inner side of the neuronal membrane, which in turn leads to membrane depolarization or an increase in membrane potential (Figure 1b). Conversely, when the neuron receives inhibitory inputs, the activation of Cl– channels of the post-neuron causes Cl– ions to flow into the neuron cell, resulting in membrane hyperpolarization or a decrease in membrane potential (Figure 1b). The firing of an action potential is triggered when the membrane potential exceeds a certain threshold influenced by the synergistic effects of excitatory and inhibitory inputs.

With their volatile resistive switching behavior and high energy efficiency, TS memristors have emerged as one of the most promising candidates for artificial neurons. Typically, TS behavior can be classified into nonpolar TS (NTS)22,38−42 and unipolar TS (UTS).17−21,43−46 The representative current–voltage (I–V) characteristics of NTS and UTS are schematically illustrated in Figure 1c and d, respectively. During voltage sweeping, the device undergoes a transition from a high resistance state (HRS) to a low resistance state (LRS) at Vth, known as the SET operation. Upon reverse voltage sweeping, the device returns to the HRS at a holding voltage (Vh). However, artificial neurons based on NTS and UTS memristors can only operate in excitatory mode, lacking the ability to replicate the anion dynamics observed in biological neurons necessary for processing inhibitory inputs.

To enable the integration of both excitatory and inhibitory inputs, it is crucial for the Vth of the TS device to be tunable, e.g., by applying a voltage signal with a polarity opposite to Vth. This type of TS device, termed the BTS memristor, is schematically depicted in Figure 1e. Under positive voltage, the device exhibits a TS behavior, while negative voltage sweeping resets it from the HRS to a higher resistance state (RESET operation). Importantly, this RESET operation increases the Vth value for subsequent TS cycles. As a result, an increased Vth decreases the neuronal firing probability upon the same synaptic stimulation, allowing the device to operate in the inhibition mode. Figure 1f illustrates how an artificial neuron based on a BTS memristor can effectively process both excitatory and inhibitory inputs, thereby enhancing its biological plausibility.

To fabricate the BTS memristor, a sandwich structure of Cu/ZnS/Pt was employed. The left inset of Figure 2a presents the cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of the device. Initially, the ZnS thin film was deposited on a Pt/Ti/SiO2/Si substrate by vacuum thermal evaporation at 200 °C. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the as-deposited film on quartz reveals a diffraction peak at 2θ = 28.7° corresponding to the (111) reflection of cubic ZnS (Figure S1a). Figure S1b shows the atomic force microscope (AFM) image of the film over a 5 × 5 μm2 area with a low average roughness of approximately 1 nm. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern of ZnS in the right inset of Figure 2a displays multiple discontinuous electron diffraction spots, indicative of a relatively poor polycrystalline structure, which is favorable for rapid metal atom or ion migration and diffusion within the film, crucial for realizing BTS as well as self-compliance. Given the strong connection between the TS behavior of metal-filamentary memristors and the diffusion of metal atoms,39 a high metal atom diffusion coefficient in the thermally evaporated ZnS thin film enables the realization of TS instead of the nonvolatile resistive switching observed in memristors using the magnetron-sputtered ZnS film.47,48

Figure 2a illustrates the self-compliance TS behavior of the Cu/ZnS/Pt device under positive voltage following a FORM process, i.e., the first TS cycle (Figure S2a). A distinct transition from HRS to LRS, i.e., the SET process, is observed at the Vth. This self-compliance feature facilitates high-density device integration and low energy consumption. Upon reversing the voltage sweeping, the device spontaneously reverts to the HRS at Vh. Throughout 100 TS cycles, Vth and Vh exhibit minimal fluctuations around +0.8 V and +0.2 V, respectively (Figure 2b). In addition, 10 randomly selected devices from 5 batches demonstrate relatively consistent Vth and Vh values (Figure 2c). It is worth noting that when the 10 devices are from the same batch, significantly lower device-to-device variation is observed, as shown in Figure S3. The memristive device exhibits a good endurance performance in pulse mode (Figure S4).

Notably, the Vth of the ZnS TS memristor can be modulated by a negative bias. As illustrated in Figure 2d, the memristor initially exhibits a Vth of +0.8 V (black dashed line). Upon application of a negative voltage sweep, a RESET process is observed (blue solid line), wherein the device transitions to a higher resistance state. This indicates that the ZnS memristor is not fully volatile but semivolatile. Post-RESET, Vth is found to significantly increase to approximately +1.5 V (red solid line). Subsequent TS cycles witness a gradual decrease in Vth toward its initial value. Furthermore, the effect of the negative voltage sweep range on the Vth variation is investigated (Figure S5). The results are summarized in Figure 2e. Generally, the Vth of the first TS cycle post-RESET increases with a larger negative voltage sweep range, highlighting the tunable nature of the device as a typical BTS memristor.

The mechanism underlying the tunable Vth in our BTS memristor can be elucidated by considering the change in the Cu filament length under positive and negative voltages. The Cu/ZnS/Pt device operates as an electrochemical metallization memristor, where the formation/rupture of the Cu filament plays a pivotal role.47,48 To check the filament state of our device, the I–V curve of the LRS shown in Figure S2a is replotted on a double-logarithmic scale (Figure 2f). The replotted curve exhibits good linearity with a slope of about 2, suggesting space charge limited conduction rather than an Ohmic behavior.49 This indicates incomplete Cu filament formation within the ZnS thin film; that is, it does not directly connect the top and bottom electrodes, explaining the observed self-compliance TS behavior. The potential reason is that a complete Cu filament is initially formed during the resistive switching only to be immediately fractured by a significant amount of Joule heat generated by a high current passing through the filament. Herein, we hypothesize the presence of a gap between the Cu filament and the Pt electrode,47 as schematically illustrated in Figures 2g and S2c. Upon voltage reversal to Vh, Joule heat-driven diffusion and high interfacial energy lead to spontaneous Cu filament rupture,18,39 widening the gap (Gap-1, Figures 2h and S2d). Application of a negative voltage (VRESET) causes further filament rupture due to the synergy of the electric field and Joule heat,47,48 widening the gap even more (Gap-2, Figure 2i). Consequently, the increased gap width results in a higher Vth for the first TS cycle post-RESET. During the subsequent TS cycles, the gap width gradually approaches Gap-1, restoring Vth to its initial value. The tunable length of the Cu filament under different polarity voltages imparts semivolatile TS behavior to our device.

In the Cu/ZnS/Pt memristor, a sufficient electric field is crucial for the dissolution of the Cu electrode and the subsequent nucleation of Cu clusters.50,51 At various gap widths, the required electric field should remain constant, establishing a linear connection between Vth and the gap width. In the FORM process, the gap width equals the ZnS film thickness (200 nm). By measuring the Vth of the FORM process, the Vth at gap widths <200 nm can be determined, as illustrated in Figure S6. The Vth of the FORM process ranges from 1.7 to 2.2 V, obtained from 5 devices in 5 batches, resulting in an electric field ranging from 0.085 to 0.11 MV/cm, slightly lower than that for oxide devices.50,51

Generally, metal-filamentary memristors demonstrate TS behavior at a low current compliance.40,41,44 Additionally, metal filament residues are consistently present during resistive switching.21,48,51 Thus, we can infer that this BTS phenomenon may be widespread in metal-filamentary memristors with an asymmetrical device structure, where one electrode consists of active metal (e.g., Cu or Ag) while the other electrode is composed of an inert metal (e.g., Pt or W). It is worth noting that besides electricity, light can also be utilized to control the Vth in optoelectronic devices demonstrating TS behavior.52,53

Our BTS memristor can function as a highly biologically plausible artificial neuron. Figure 3a shows its LIF dynamics in response to a sequence of input voltage pulses (Vin) with an amplitude of 1 V, a width of 50 ms, and an interval of 100 ms. The firing threshold is set at Iout = 10 μA. Initially, the device exhibits no current response during the early pulse applications. However, after approximately 8 s, obvious current responses emerge and Iout reaches the firing threshold at 11 s. Subsequently, the firing probability steadily increases, reaching near 100% after 28 s. Herein, the return of the current to its initial level following the removal of voltage pulses indicates neuronal leaky behavior. We also measured the memristor’s LIF behavior using voltage pulses with an amplitude of 1.0 V, a width of 5 ms, and an interval of 10 ms (Figure S7). The device demonstrates LIF behavior similar to that in Figure 3a, except that it requires less cumulative time (or fewer pulses) to trigger firing events, likely due to the higher pulse frequency. Notably, at a fixed pulse number (e.g., 20), the firing probability of our device exhibits a strong dependence on pulse amplitude, as shown in Figure 3b. The firing probability profiles against pulse amplitude align closely with the sigmoid activation function commonly employed in artificial neural networks. Specifically, the firing probability approaches 0 at a pulse amplitude below 0.6 V, while reaching near 100% at pulse amplitude above 1 V.

Further experimentation reveals that for a device exhibiting 100% firing probability upon a sequence of +1 V pulses (Figure 3c), alternating between −1 and +1 V pulses effectively suppresses its firing, as illustrated in Figure 3d. This inhibition stems from the negative voltage pulses, elevating the Vth of our BTS memristor, thereby impeding its neuronal firing. We further investigate the dependence of negative voltage pulse amplitude on neuronal firing behavior. As demonstrated in Figure 3e, increasing the negative pulse amplitude significantly delays firing activity, necessitating a greater cumulative time (or more positive pulses) to trigger firing events. The positive pulse numbers (Nf) required to elicit a firing event at different negative pulse amplitudes are plotted in Figure 3f. Correspondingly, the firing probability significantly decreases as the negative pulse amplitude increases from 0.5 to 3 V, indicating effective neuronal inhibition in response to negative pulses (Figure 3g). These findings underscore the synergistic modulation of firing dynamics in our BTS memristor neuron by both excitatory (e.g., positive voltage pulses) and inhibitory (e.g., negative voltage pulses) inputs, crucial for enhancing the computational accuracy of neuromorphic architectures. It is worth mentioning that our BTS neuron operates without the need for auxiliary circuits, facilitating ultrahigh-density device integration and reducing power consumption.

The synergistic effect of excitatory and inhibitory synaptic inputs on neuronal firing dynamics is crucial for dark and light adaptation of the human eye.37 Visual adaptation relies on the coordinated activity of various neuronal cells in the retina, including photoreceptors, bipolar cells, and ganglion cells (Figure 4a), enabling the eye to perceive and adapt to changes in external brightness.54 Among them, photoreceptors, categorized into rod and cone cells, convert light into electrical signals, with rods being highly photosensitive and excelling in low-light conditions and cones functioning optimally in bright environments. Bipolar cells are responsible for transmitting sensory signals from photoreceptors to ganglion cells, which can be classified as ON and OFF cells.55 In bright environments, ON cells are activated, while OFF cells are inhibited, whereas in darkness, ON cells are inhibited and OFF cells are activated.

While optoelectronic devices56−63 with light-controlled paired-pulse synaptic potentiation and depression behaviors have been employed to mimic eye adaptation in recent years, research on emulating eye adaptation using emerging neuronal devices remains limited. Our BTS neuron offers a pathway to develop a self-adaptive neuromorphic vision sensor (Figure 4b). Traditional CMOS vision sensors struggle to obtain clear images under very bright (overexposure) or dark (underexposure) conditions. In our neuromorphic vision sensor, inhibitory (negative voltage pulses) or excitatory (positive voltage pulses) inputs are employed to decrease or increase the firing probability of BTS neurons, facilitating light or dark adaptation. Specifically, for overexposed images, applying appropriate negative pulses (e.g., −2.5 V) partially inhibits neuronal firing, corresponding to a light adaptation process (Figure 4c). On the contrary, in the case of underexposed images, neurons become relatively more excitatory with positive pulses (e.g., +0.9 V), corresponding to a dark adaptation process (Figure 4d).

To validate the efficacy of light and dark adaptation in our neuromorphic vision sensor, a convolutional neural network (CNN) is constructed based on the conductance increase/decrease cycles of an all-optically controlled (AOC) memristor with demonstrated robustness in our previous work64 (Figure S8). The conductance of the AOC memristor can be reversibly tuned by changing only the wavelength of the controlling light. This prevents microstructural changes and Joule heat during operation, leading to a significantly improved stability in conductance modulation. Utilizing the standard CIFAR-10 data set (Canadian Institute for Advanced Research, 10 classes) for testing, the images are artificially overexposed and underexposed using Python to simulate the imaging results obtained by CMOS photodetectors in very bright and dark environments, respectively (Figure S9). Subsequently, the over- and underexposed images are subjected to light and dark adaptation processes based on our BTS neurons. These processed images are then fed into the CNN for recognition. As shown in Figure 4e,f, the recognition accuracies of adapted images reach about 90% after 100 epochs of training, whereas unadapted images exhibit significantly lower recognition accuracies even after 200 epochs.

Apart from our BTS neuron, other emerging electronic device-based neurons can also operate in inhibition mode.18,24,31,36,65−67 Depending on the device type, these neurons are categorized into two groups: transistor type31,36,65−67 and memristor type.18,24 For the former, neuronal inhibition is achieved by tuning the gate voltage. For the latter, two different methods are employed to realize inhibition, depending on the memristor type. The nonvolatile phase change memory (PCM)-based neuron replicates inhibitory functions by combing two PCM memristors,24 while nine n-type MOS transistors are used to mimic lateral inhibition for the volatile UTS memristor-based neuron.18 Compared with these neurons, the primary advantage of our BTS neuron is its capability to implement inhibition in a single two-terminal memristor without any peripheral circuits, which is beneficial for its ultrahigh-density integration. In summary, we demonstrated a novel Cu/ZnS/Pt BTS memristor featuring electrically tunable Vth. The self-compliance BTS behavior originates from the semivolatile TS characteristics attributed to a relatively poor polycrystalline structure of the thermally evaporated ZnS thin film, which facilitates rapid Cu2+ migration and diffusion. Leveraging this tunable Vth, the memristor exhibits the capability of functioning as an artificial neuron operating in both excitation and inhibition modes. Furthermore, we have demonstrated the practical application of this BTS memristor in constructing a self-adaptive neuromorphic vision sensor capable of emulating light and dark adaptation observed in the human eye. In essence, our BTS memristor represents a significant advancement in the realm of artificial neurons, offering a highly biologically plausible solution for the construction of more robust and efficient neuromorphic architectures. From the viewpoint of their applications in neural networks, designing suitable peripheral circuits to enable BTS neurons to pass signals to the next stage is necessary, which is one of the future research directions. By using TS memristive neurons composed of a TS memristor and simple digital circuits, two-layer spiking neural networks have been experimentally demonstrated.68 It is also worth noting that CMOS neuron circuits, consisting of only 10 MOSFETs, have the ability to pass signals to the next stage.69 These studies are useful for designing peripheral circuits to advance the practical application of our BTS neuron.
