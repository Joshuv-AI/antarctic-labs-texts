Multimodal assessment of the prevention of surgical site infections in breast surgery in a French university hospital
Authors: Christelle Elias, Juliette Dessemon, Elisabetta Kuczewski, Laura Gabet, Fanny Bazin, Arnaud Friggeri, Sophie Gardes, Françoise Rumeau, Pierre-Adrien Bolze, François Golfier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMonitoring surgical site infections (SSIs) incidence through surveillance systems enables tailored infection prevention interventions. In our facility, annual surveillance of SSIs in breast surgeries revealed a significant increase of SSI incidence rates six times higher than the national average in France in 2024. This study aimed to investigate the factors contributing to the increase in SSIs following breast surgery at the South Hospital Group of the Hospices Civils de Lyon (HCL), Pierre-Bénite, France.MethodsThe study was divided into three parts, all including breast surgeries: (1) an observational audit of professional practices assessing the compliance of operator attire in the operating room, conducted from May 30 to July 5, 2024), (2) an observational audit assessing the compliance of patient preoperative skin preparation, (May 30–July 5, 2024), (3) a retrospective case-control study nested within a prospective cohort of adult women who underwent breast surgery between January 1, 2019, and June 1, 2024.ResultsOverall, out of 220 observations, 207 (94%) professionals wore a mask upon entering the operating room, and 192 (87%) wore a mask that covered both the chin and nose. Of the 25 skin preparation observations, six (24%) antiseptic applications did not dry naturally, and 10 (40%) did not dry for at least 30 s. The case-control study revealed that coagulase-negative staphylococci and Staphylococcus aureus were the most prevalent pathogens. A BMI > 30 kg/m2 was a risk factor for SSIs in breast surgery (OR = 3.21, 95% CI [1.57–6.56), as well as diabetes (OR = 3.09, 95% CI [1.16–8.31]) and the administration of antibiotic prophylaxis other than cefazolin (OR = 6.49, 95% CI [1.12–37.44]). Traceability of disinfection in the patient record with an alcohol-based antiseptic agent was a protective factor against the occurrence of SSIs (OR = 0.48, 95% CI [0.24–0.97]).ConclusionThis multimodal study identified significant non-compliance regarding the wearing of caps, masks, and jewellery, as well as patient skin preparation. Obese and diabetic patients, as well as those receiving prophylactic antibiotics other than cefazolin, are particularly at risk for SSIs. Delving deeply into the reasons why professionals disregard recommendations related to operating room attire and antisepsis could be highly beneficial for the prevention of SSIs.Keywords: Breast surgery, Infection prevention and control, Skin antisepsis, Surgical site infection, Surveillance

Introduction

Surgical site infections (SSIs) are healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) that occur within 30 days after surgery and up to one year for patients receiving implants or prosthetics [1]. According to the 2022 national prevalence survey of nosocomial infections in France [2], SSIs represent the third leading cause of HAIs. SSIs are among the most common complications following surgery, including breast surgery. Despite advances in surgical techniques and infection control practices, SSIs continue to pose significant risks to patient outcomes, with substantial clinical, psychological, economic, and institutional consequences. In breast surgery, SSIs can result in prolonged hospital stays, delayed recovery, poor aesthetic outcomes, and, in severe cases, life-threatening complications [1, 3]. SSIs can also lead to reoperations, extended antibiotic treatments, and additional care, negatively impacting patients’ quality of life [4]. To illustrate, Edwards et al. found that SSIs extended hospital stays by an average of 9.7 days and increased the risk of mortality by 2 to 11 times [5]. Furthermore, these infections also affect hospital quality indicators and reputation, potentially leading to financial penalties and lowering institutional rankings [6].

Breast surgery, whether oncologic or reconstructive, presents specific challenges for infection control. Factors such as the surgical site, the use of prosthetic implants, and the patient’s overall health condition all contribute to infection risk. Patients undergoing breast surgery are also at increased risk due to radiation therapy, chemotherapy, or compromised immune function. SSIs in breast surgery are primarily caused by skin flora, particularly Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis, highlighting the need for targeted prevention strategies [7–9]. As the number of breast surgeries increases, particularly for cancer treatment and reconstruction, implementing effective SSI prevention strategies is essential [10].

Preventing SSIs in breast surgery requires a comprehensive approach involving preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative measures [9]. This includes strict adherence to sterile techniques, appropriate operating room attire, optimal patient preparation, judicious use of prophylactic antibiotics, and addressing modifiable patient risk factors [11–14]. Risk factors for SSIs, including smoking, high body mass index (BMI), diabetes, and neoadjuvant chemotherapy, are well-documented in general, though not always specific to breast surgery [3, 11, 15]. Infections can also delay adjuvant treatments, increasing the risk of breast cancer recurrence [7]. Conversely, proper skin preparation, adherence to aseptic protocols, and the administration of antibiotic prophylaxis are recognized protective measures against SSIs [13–15].

Effective SSI prevention and control are crucial for managing infection risks and improving the quality of care and patient safety. This aligns with the objectives set forth in the 2022–2025 national action plan for infection prevention and antimicrobial resistance [16].

Monitoring SSI incidence through surveillance systems enables tailored infection prevention interventions [17–19]. In our facility, annual surveillance of SSIs in breast surgeries revealed a significant increase in incidence rates. In the first half of 2024, the SSI incidence rate was three times higher than the estimated rate for the same period in 2023 and six times higher than the national average in France [20], with coagulase-negative Staphylococci and Staphylococcus aureus the most commonly isolated pathogens.

Given this unexpected rise in SSI rates, our study aimed to investigate the factors contributing to the increase in SSIs following breast surgery in our facility. The objective was to identify the determinants, such as non-compliant practices in infection prevention, particularly regarding operator attire and patient skin preparation, contributing to SSIs in breast surgery at a French university hospital.

Methods

Study type and population

The study was divided into three distinct parts:

An observational audit of professional practices assessing the compliance of operator attire in the operating room during breast surgeries, conducted from May 30 to July 5, 2024, at the South Hospital Group (GH Sud) of the Hospices Civils de Lyon (HCL), Pierre-Bénite, France.

An observational audit of professional practices assessing the compliance of operator attire in the operating room during breast surgeries, conducted from May 30 to July 5, 2024, at the South Hospital Group (GH Sud) of the Hospices Civils de Lyon (HCL), Pierre-Bénite, France.

An observational audit assessing the compliance of patient preoperative skin preparation for all breast surgeries during the same period, from May 30 to July 5, 2024, at the GH Sud, Pierre-Bénite, France.

An observational audit assessing the compliance of patient preoperative skin preparation for all breast surgeries during the same period, from May 30 to July 5, 2024, at the GH Sud, Pierre-Bénite, France.

A retrospective case-control study nested within a prospective cohort of patients who underwent breast surgery at the GH Sud of the HCL, Pierre-Bénite, France, between January 1, 2019, and June 1, 2024.

A retrospective case-control study nested within a prospective cohort of patients who underwent breast surgery at the GH Sud of the HCL, Pierre-Bénite, France, between January 1, 2019, and June 1, 2024.

In 2024, our facility had 1062 hospital beds, accommodating 43,788 admissions for full hospitalization and outpatient care. The GH Sud has three gyneco-obstetric operating rooms and a gyneco-oncology surgical hospitalization unit with 22 full hospital beds equivalent to 2161 patient’s admissions.

Eligibility criteria

For all three parts of the study, we included all adult women undergoing breast surgical procedures at GH Sud of the Hospices Civils de Lyon in Pierre Bénite, France. These procedures encompassed total or partial tumorectomy, total or partial mastectomy (with or without lymph node dissection), regardless of hospitalization type (conventional or ambulatory) and with or without antibiotic prophylaxis, as specified by the national protocol for the prevention of surgical site infections [20].

For the audit on operating room attire, were included all professionals present in the operating room from the time the patient entered the operating room until the incision.

For the case-control study, cases were defined as patients who developed a SSI within 30 days following surgery. A SSI was defined by any positive microbiological sample, superficial or deep, taken for diagnostic purposes at the surgical site, with one or more isolated microorganism(s), and performed within 30 days following the target surgical procedure during the index surgical stay or upon readmission, further validated by a surgeon. The surgical site is defined as the site of the surgical incision or any anatomical site in contact with and/or manipulated during the target surgical procedure according to the French protocol for the surveillance of SSIs [21]. Cases without samples taken or with sterile cultures were excluded from this study. Controls were patients who did not develop an SSI within 30 days of the surgery. SSIs were detected by the system implemented by the GH Sud infection control unit, which involved cross-referencing data from the Medical Information Systems Program of patients who underwent surgery with microbiology laboratory data filtered for clinical samples such as wound swabs, suppuration, abscess, lymphocele, or wounds during post-operative follow-up consultations. The results were then compared and analyzed to identify risk factors associated with SSIs.

Data collection

For the audit on operating room attire, information regarding attire, cap wearing and type, surgical mask use, and hand hygiene requirements (notably the absence of jewellery) was collected using the standardized evaluation grid adapted from the national recommendation for the operating room attire [13]. Healthcare workers could be observed more than once.

For the skin preparation audit, information was collected using the standardized evaluation grid from the 2024 Pre-OP audit of the national surveillance mission on surgical and interventional procedures (SPICMI) [22]. Care practices were classified into four themes, in accordance with the latest recommendations: preoperative shower, hair removal, skin cleaning, and antisepsis/skin disinfection.

For the case-control study, data on age, gender, Body Mass Index (BMI), operating room, surgery duration, type of procedure, antibiotic prophylaxis, American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) score [23], Altemeier classification [24], and National Nosocomial Infections Surveillance System (NNISS) score [19] were prospectively collected for both cases and controls following the national methodology [20, 25]. Comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, immunosuppression, respiratory diseases, malignancies, and renal or liver conditions were recorded. Lifestyle data included active smoking at the time of surgery, chronic alcoholism, and drug addiction.

Preoperative and clinical data included length of preoperative hospital stay, the oncological nature of the surgery, isolated fever, preoperative infection, emergency surgery context (not scheduled 24 h before surgery), and the placement of a harpoon for marking the excision zone.

Data on the traceability of SSI prevention measures included antiseptics used for patient skin preparation and antibiotic prophylaxis. Surgery-related data included the order of the procedure on the surgery schedule. Postoperative data included removal of dressing, surgery duration, total length of hospital stay and in-hospital death.

Data specific to SSIs included the date of the sample, the bacterial species and its resistance profile, the type of SSI (superficial, deep or organ/space infection), the delay to SSI onset, and reoperation for SSI.

Data were collected by two surveyors specifically trained for the purpose of this study. Data from the audits on operating room attire compliance and patient skin preparation were obtained via direct visual observation of professionals and practices, respectively. Some fields (preoperative shower) required direct questioning of the patient or, if not possible, the State-Certified Operating Room Nurse, or, as a last resort, consulting the traceability in the patient’s medical record to complete the questionnaire.

For the case-control study, data were collected from the electronic medical records. Data were entered via a secure RedCAP® electronic form (https://redcap.chu-lyon.fr/redcap/).

Statistical data analysis

For the case-control study, each case was randomly matched with two controls over the years (2019, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, and from January 1 to June 1, 2024) and by age (age groups of 10 years). The required number of subjects was calculated for a risk difference of 3 between the two groups, with a 20% exposed control population, with obesity as the exposure of interest [11]. Descriptive analyses were performed for the three parts of the study. For quantitative variables, medians (interquartile ranges) or means (standard deviation) were reported depending on normal distribution. Qualitative variables were expressed as numbers (n) and percentages (%). For the case-control study, in univariate analysis, contributing factors were compared between cases and controls using non-parametric Wilcoxon tests, and Pearson’s Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test if the Chi-squared test assumptions were not met. Variables with a p-value below the 0.1 threshold in univariate analysis were included in a multivariate model. Multivariate analysis was performed using logistic regression [26].

Comparative analysis results were expressed as Odds Ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The statistical significance threshold was set at 0.05. SSI rates were expressed as cumulative incidence rates per year (number of SSIs per 100 surgeries, %) with 95% CI. The trend in SSI incidence rates over time was analyzed by testing the slope of the linear regression. These statistical analyses were performed using Stata® version 18.0.

This included 52 (24%) surgeons or surgical residents, 44 (20%) nurse anaesthesiologist, 40 (18%) circulating operating room nurses, 34 (15%) anaesthesiologists or anaesthesiology residents, 27 (12%) scrub nurses, and 23 (11%) nursing assistants and other personnel.

Case-control study

From January 1, 2019, to June 1, 2024, 198 patients were included in the study, among whom 66 were cases and 132 were controls. Among the infected patients, 44 (66.7%) had deep SSIs, 22 (33.3%) had superficial SSIs, and 20 (30.3%) of the infected patients required surgical reintervention. Deep breast abscess samples (35, 53%) and lymphoceles (20, 30.3%) were the most prevalent specimens taken in cases. Of the 100 pathogens identified in the SSIs, 21 (21%) were Staphylococcus aureus and 48 (48%) were coagulase-negative staphylococci (including 26 (26%) Staphylococcus epidermidis, 1 (1%) Staphylococcus haemolyticus, and 21 (21%) other coagulase-negative Staphylococcus species). Discussion

This multimodal study, composed of three parts, shed light on the contributory factors leading to surgical site infections in breast surgery, which have been steadily increasing since 2021 in our facility. The audit regarding the attire of professionals in the operating room revealed that nearly one third of professionals did not wear compliant caps, and an eighth wore jewellery or a mask that did not cover both the chin and nose. One quarter of observations of the surgical preoperative site preparation showed that the last application of the antiseptic agent did not undergo spontaneous, unassisted drying, and 40% of antisepsis procedures did not adhere to the full 30-second drying time. Additionally, the case-control study indicated that obese patients with a BMI over 30 kg/m² and diabetic patients had three times the risk of developing an SSI after breast surgery. Administration of antibiotic prophylaxis other than cefazolin was associated with a six-fold increased risk of developing an SSI in breast surgery. However, traceability of disinfection in the patient’s medical record was associated with half the risk of developing an SSI. In more than two thirds of the cases, the most prevalent pathogens found in SSIs were coagulase-negative Staphylococci and Staphylococcus aureus.

The case-control study results align with those from the two audits conducted. Indeed, the non-compliance related to surgical attire showed that over two thirds of professionals wore reusable caps, contrary to local recommendations, and one third of caps allowed hair to escape, potentially explaining the presence of skin flora pathogens like coagulase-negative staphylococci. Additionally, observations indicated that 13% of professionals wore masks that did not cover the chin and/or nose, significantly contributing to the transmission of oro-pharyngeal germs such as Staphylococcus aureus from healthcare workers to patients. It is well known that proper surgical attire and personal protective equipment play a crucial role in preventing postoperative infections by limiting the transfer of microorganisms and organic fluids between healthcare providers and patients [13]. These discrepancies may be related to staff turnover, seasonal variations in observations, and/or a lack of training for professionals. Therefore, it is crucial to explore further these findings and to raise awareness among operating room professionals, accompanied by rigorous monitoring of practices, to reverse this trend and improve patient safety. However, reassuring and satisfactory practices were noted, particularly that the order of surgical interventions in the program did not appear to be a determining factor, suggesting effective air treatment in the operating rooms and efficient biocleaning between procedures, along with consistent performance from operators regardless of the order in the schedule.

The study also highlighted that traceability of disinfection using an alcohol-based antiseptic agent (here, alcoholic povidone-iodine) was a protective factor that halves the risk of SSI. This traceability, recorded for 140 (71%) patients, can be interpreted as a proxy for the quality of the application of the antiseptic agent during surgical site preparation and proper skin disinfection before incision. This result significantly aligns with observations where more than one third of antiseptic applications were non-compliant. Indeed, non-spontaneous, unnatural, and/or incomplete drying can lead to insufficient contact time with the antiseptic agent, compromising its antimicrobial effectiveness. This contributes to the persistence of skin flora germs like coagulase-negative staphylococci and exposes patients to the risk of surgical site infections. These findings are similar to national trends from the PREOP 2023 audit by the SPICMI 2023 mission, which reported 25% non-compliance regarding this parameter [22].

Additionally, the study indicates that the use of prophylactic antibiotics other than cefazolin was associated with a six-fold increase in the risk of SSI in breast surgery. Other antibiotics used included clindamycin combined with gentamicin for patients reported as allergic to beta-lactams and were employed for surgeries classified as Altemeier II (clean-contaminated surgeries). However, coverage by antibiotics other than first or second-generation cephalosporins like cefazolin is clearly linked to an increased incidence of SSIs described in other observational studies [27–29]. In reality, the majority of patients claiming to be allergic to penicillin are not [10, 29]. These results suggested reserving alternatives to beta-lactams only for confirmed allergy cases and conducting thorough documentation in instances of reported beta-lactam allergies.

The NNISS score greater than 1, calculated based on the ASA score, duration of surgery, and Altemeier classification of the procedure, allows for stratification of patients based on their risk of nosocomial infections, facilitating the implementation of targeted and appropriate prevention measures, although this score does not appear to be a determining factor for SSI in breast surgery in the multivariate analysis of this study (p > 0.05). Results suggested that diabetes is a risk factor, aligning with other literature data [12, 30]. Additionally, obese patients with a BMI over 30 kg/m2 have a three-fold increased risk of developing an SSI. These data support results from a previous study conducted in 2016–2017 [11] and suggested adjusting the dose of prophylactic antibiotics based on weight for these specific populations to significantly reduce the risk of SSIs [14]. Moreover, active smoking is a known risk factor for postoperative infectious complications [3, 31], largely due to reduced tissue oxygenation, compromising healing and leading to bacterial proliferation. However, our results did not identify smoking as a risk factor for SSIs in breast surgery. Patient-reported data may question their reliability, and there was a lack of data collected regarding smoking exposure and cessation. Furthermore, our facility had previously experienced high SSI rates in 2016 (13%) due to poor antiseptic practices during the placement of harpoons in breast surgery, particularly related to non-compliant use of sterile gloves. Results from this study are reassuring regarding professional practices during harpoon placement (p = 0.253) and align with literature data that did not associate harpoon placement with an SSI risk factor [32, 33].

This study presents several strengths, notably that it was conducted over a short period addressing a practical issue and utilizing a multimodal approach with three components combining observations of practices and epidemiological research. It also enriches knowledge about the determinants of SSIs in breast surgery, an area where literature is relatively sparse. However, this study has limitations. First, although case-control studies are typically subject to selection bias, this was partially mitigated by matching cases and controls by age category and year of surgery and using random selection for controls. Second, only microbiologically documented SSIs were included, specifically those detected at our facility. Patients may have received antibiotics prior to sampling, and some may have been infected and sought care in the community or another facility rather than GH Sud of HCL for their SSI. This data was not captured by our methodology, which may lead to an underestimation of the number of SSIs. Third, although the results were based on a small sample size, they may only be representative of the population included in this study. Prospective data with a larger sample size would be needed. Fourth, there was a temporal bias in surgical practices; infection prevention protocols and follow-up techniques may have changed over time, influencing results if practices differ between professionals and recommendations. Some medical teams may be more proactive in preventing and treating SSIs, complicating comparison of infection rates across different study groups. Fifth, observed professionals may have been subject to the Hawthorne effect, wherein they alter their behaviours or practices knowing they are being observed, aiming to conform to expected standards [34]. Consequently, the data collected may not accurately reflect actual practices. Sixth, an investigator bias, known as the halo effect, may have influenced certain results from observational audits [35]. Seventh, our results were subject to information bias since some collected data were self-reported, such as the number of preoperative showers using antiseptic soap, and habits regarding alcohol, tobacco, and other drug use. Eighth, the audit on surgical site preparation did not include data on surgical hand disinfection, which is an indicator of quality in SSI prevention [36]. Ninth, other factors such as surgeons’ experience, activity in theatre, time of removal of dressing, hypothermia, hand hygiene compliance, glove use, and door openings were not captured in our study. Finally, this study did not explore postoperative care, particularly at home, through aseptic rules during dressing changes, which could contribute to the occurrence of SSIs.

Conclusion

This multimodal study identified several factors contributing to the increase in surgical site infections in breast surgery, where the most prevalent germs were coagulase-negative staphylococci and Staphylococcus aureus. Audits revealed significant non-compliance regarding the wearing of caps, masks, and jewellery, as well as patient skin preparation. A substantial proportion of antisepsis procedures did not adhere to the required drying time, compromising the effectiveness of the antiseptics. Obese and diabetic patients, as well as those receiving prophylactic antibiotics other than cefazolin, are particularly at risk for SSIs. The study highlights the necessity of strictly adhering to recommendations for antibiotic prophylaxis, as well as thoroughly documenting beta-lactam drug allergies to administer the most effective antibiotic for preventing SSIs and programming training to HCW on the recommend practices. It would be beneficial to explore the motivations and constraints that lead professionals to disregard recommendations related to operating room attire and antisepsis. Delving deeply into the reasons why professionals neglect these guidelines could help understand this dynamic. Such data would be valuable for developing tailored strategies aimed at improving compliance, quality, and safety of care in the operating room.
