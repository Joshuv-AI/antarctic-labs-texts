Mechanistic Insights into Nrf2 Activation and Oxidative Stress Responses of Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances (PFAS) in Human Keratinocytes
Authors: Samantha Serafin Sevilleno, Hye Jin Park, Min Sik Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPer- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are widely used in consumer products for their hydrophobic and chemical stability, yet their dermatotoxicological effects remain underexplored. This study investigated the ability of representative PFAS (PFBS, PFBA, PFHpA, PFOA, PFNA, PFDA, 4:2 FTOH, and 6:2 FTOH) to activate the Nrf2–Keap1–ARE signaling pathway and induce oxidative stress in human keratinocytes. Results revealed that PFOA and PFHpA tested positive for the KeratinoSens™ assay, while PFNA showed borderline activity. PFNA and 6:2 FTOH produced the highest ROS increases at sub-cytotoxic levels, decreasing at higher doses, suggesting cytotoxic stress. PFHpA, PFOA, PFBS, PFBA, 4:2 FTOH and PFDA induced relatively minor fluctuations in ROS across all concentrations. Although previously reported as negative in the murine Local Lymph Node Assay (LLNA), PFOA tested positive in both KeratinoSens™ and ROS assays, indicating activation of oxidative stress-driven cellular pathways distinct from classical immune sensitization mechanisms. In silico prediction failed to capture these effects, underscoring current limitations of computational toxicology for PFAS. These findings suggest that PFAS-induced oxidative stress may compromise skin barrier function and inflammatory signaling, with implications for dermatological disorders and consumer safety. These results highlight Nrf2-mediated redox signaling as a mechanistically relevant target for evaluating PFAS-induced dermatotoxicity and support the integration of pathway-based in vitro assays into PFAS risk assessment.Keywords: Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), Dermatotoxicity, Keratinocytes, Oxidative stress, Nrf2 activation, In vitro assay

INTRODUCTION

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are a diverse class of synthetic chemicals characterized by strong carbon-fluorine bonds and are widely applied in consumer products and industrial processes due to their exceptional thermal stability, surfactant properties, and resistance to degradation (Al-Maqtari et al., 2025). Due to these properties, PFAS are extensively used in everyday items such as textiles, food packaging, firefighting foams, and personal care products, resulting in their widespread environmental distribution and frequent human exposure (Brendel et al., 2018; Glüge et al., 2020). Increasing concern has been directed regarding the toxicological profiles of PFAS, particularly their persistence and bioaccumulation.

Human skin is a key route of exposure to PFAS through occupational handling, consumer product use, and environmental contact. Daily exposure of the skin to persistent PFAS may lead to health effects that remain uncertain, as significant gaps in scientific understanding still exist (Haarmann-Stemmann and Esser, 2025). Keratinocytes, the predominant cell type in the epidermis, play a crucial role in both barrier function and the initiation of adaptive stress responses. One key cytoprotective mechanism involves activation of the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway, a central regulator of cellular stress adaptation that controls antioxidant and detoxification gene expression in response to electrophilic and oxidative challenges (Ma, 2013). Dysregulation of this pathway is linked to inflammation, oxidative damage, and cytotoxicity, highlighting its relevance in dermatotoxicology. The KeratinoSens™ assay provides a mechanistically anchored reporter system that captures Nrf2–ARE pathway activation in keratinocytes, enabling the evaluation of stress-response signaling relevant to skin toxicity (Emter et al., 2010). Applying such assays to PFAS may help bridge current knowledge gaps in their dermatological risk.

Oxidative stress represents another key mechanism underlying chemical-induced skin toxicity. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), when excessively generated, can damage cellular macromolecules, alter redox homeostasis, and trigger cytotoxic pathways (Sies et al., 2017). Some PFAS have been reported to influence mitochondrial function and oxidative stress responses in other cellular systems (Wielsøe et al., 2015), but knowledge on whether PFAS elicit ROS-mediated cytotoxicity in skin cells remains limited. Considering that keratinocytes play a central role in maintaining cutaneous barrier function and orchestrating inflammatory responses, investigating ROS induction by PFAS in these cells provides crucial insights into potential adverse dermatological outcomes (Choi et al., 2020). Oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory mediators are established disruptors of keratinocyte differentiation and epidermal barrier homeostasis. Consequently, these perturbations may constitute a mechanistic axis through which PFAS exposure facilitates the pathogenesis of cutaneous inflammatory disorders (Haarmann-Stemmann and Esser, 2025).

The present study investigates how selected PFAS compounds influence activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway and trigger oxidative stress in keratinocytes using in vitro cellular models. By integrating an established skin sensitization assay with measurements of reactive oxygen species, the research offers cellular-level insights into the toxic potential of these PFAS alternatives. The findings enhance current understanding of the dermatotoxicological significance of emerging PFAS alternatives and may inform risk assessment strategies and safer chemical substitution.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Test chemicals

As part of the initial experiments to demonstrate the technical proficiency of the conducting laboratory, the OECD KeratinoSens™ guideline proficiency substances were assessed for their skin sensitization potential, and the obtained in-house results were compared with existing in vivo data and published KeratinoSens™ findings. Data on the chemicals is summarized in Table 1 and their chemical structures are presented in Fig. 1. All proficiency test compounds, as well as the following test chemicals: PFBS, PFBA, PFHpA, PFOA, PFNA, PFDA, and 6:2 FTOH, were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). 4:2 FTOH was purchased from TCI Chemicals (Tokyo, Japan). All compounds were first dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, CAS No. 67-68-5, purity >99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich) to prepare a working solution, then serially diluted to prepare test concentrations. The final concentration of DMSO was 0.1% in both vehicle control and treatment groups. Cinnamic aldehyde (CAS No. 14371-10-9; purity >98%), the reference chemical for the KeratinoSens™ assay, was procured from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA).

Cell culture

The KeratinoSens™ cell line, a human HaCaT keratinocyte cell line that contains a luciferase reporter under the control of the antioxidant response element (ARE), was obtained from Givaudan, Switzerland. KeratinoSens™ cells were cultured in complete Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 mg/mL Penicillin-Streptomycin (GenDepot, TX, USA) and allowed to adhere overnight at 37°C with 5% CO2. Sub-culturing was performed every 3 to 4 days when cells reached 85 to 90% confluency, with a maximum of 25 passages.

Nrf2/ARE luciferase gene reporter assay

The Nrf2/ARE luciferase gene reporter assay (KeratinoSens™ assay) measures the activation of the Nrf2-ARE pathway, which is a key event in the skin sensitization adverse outcome pathway (AOP). The KeratinoSens™ test method was performed as described by Settivari et al. (2015) and adopted by OECD Test Guideline 442D (OECD, 2024) with minor modifications.

For chemical exposure, the KeratinoSens™ cells were seeded in 96-well plates (clear plates for cytotoxicity testing, white plates for activation assay) at a density of 10,000 cells per well. Following 24-h of culture (on the day of treatment), cell culture medium in each well was replaced with fresh antibiotic-free medium containing the test substance and a final level of 1% of the solvent, DMSO. PFAS chemicals were tested at 12 two-fold dilutions ranging from 0.98 to 2000 μM. Cinnamic aldehyde, the positive control, was diluted two-fold in DMSO and tested at five concentrations, ranging from 4 to 64 µM. Each 96-well testing plate contained up to 2 test chemicals, positive control, negative control (media with 1% DMSO; 6 wells), and one well as a no-cell blank. Test substance treatments were conducted under standard cell culture conditions for a duration of 48 h and then washed with 100 μL of phosphate-buffered saline. Luciferase activity was measured according to the manufacturer’s protocol (One-Glo™ luciferase assay system kit; Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

Cell viability was determined using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Abcam, CB, UK) which measures the reduction of the water-soluble tetrazolium salt WST-8 into a formazan product (Chamchoy et al., 2019; Freeberg et al., 2018). After exposure of cells to varying concentrations of PFAS for 48 h, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added per well and cells were incubated for an additional 2 h. The absorbance was detected at 460 nm on a microplate spectrophotometer (Spark®, TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland).

In each repetition, three replicates were used for the luciferase activity measurements, and at least one parallel replicate plate was used for the cell viability assay. Each replicate was considered acceptable if all of the following criteria were fulfilled: (1) the positive control induced a dose-dependent increase in luciferase activity, with an EC1.5 value between 7 and 30 μM; (2) maximum luciferase induction at 64 µM was between 2- and 8-fold; and (3) the average coefficient of variation for the luminescence readings of the solvent-control wells was less than 20%. Chemicals were classified as positive in the assay if they met the following three criteria: (1) the EC1.5 value was below 1000 μM in all three repetitions or at least two repetitions; (2) at the lowest concentration with gene induction above 1.5-fold (i.e., at the EC1.5 determining concentration), cellular viability was above 70%; and (3) there was a clear overall dose-response for luciferase induction, consistent across repetitions.

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) were quantified using the DCFDA/H2DCFDA Cellular ROS Assay Kit (ab113851, Abcam Limited, Cambridge, UK) following the manufacturer’s protocol with minor modifications. KeratinoSens™ cells were seeded into black, clear-bottom 96-well plates at a density of 10,000 cells/well and cultured overnight. Cells were exposed to PFAS test compounds in phenol red-free medium for 24 h at various concentrations. Following chemical exposure, cells were washed twice with pre-warmed PBS to remove residual test substance. A 20 µM DCFDA working solution, prepared in serum- and phenol red-free medium, was then added (100 µL per well), and plates were incubated for 45 min at 37°C in the dark. Fluorescence was measured immediately at 485 nm/535 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (Spark®, TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland). ROS production was calculated as fold increase in fluorescence intensity relative to vehicle-treated controls. All treatments were performed in triplicate in at least two independent experiments. A known ROS inducer, tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP; 50 µM), was used as the positive control in each assay run. Cell viability in this assay was evaluated using a method similar with that applied in the KeratinoSens™ assay.

Data analysis

Luciferase induction and cytotoxicity were analyzed according to the protocols of OECD Test Guideline 442D (OECD, 2024) for KeratinoSens™ assay. All data analyses were performed using GraphPad InStat version 5.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of at least three independent experiments.

In silico prediction of toxicological properties

To complement the in vitro assays, toxicity predictions of the tested PFAS compounds were obtained using the web-based platform ProTox 3.0 (Charité-University Medicine Berlin, Germany; accessible at https://tox.charite.de/protox3/), an in silico prediction server based on machine learning models trained on public toxicological databases (Banerjee et al. 2024). The Nrf2/ARE model within this in silico approach predicts the likelihood of a compound to activate the Nrf2 pathway, which is associated with antioxidant and electrophilic stress responses. Each compound was individually analyzed by entering its canonical SMILES structure into the ProTox 3.0 prediction interface. The algorithm compares the molecular features of the query compound against curated datasets of chemicals with experimentally validated Nrf2 activation potential. The output provides a binary activity classification: “1” for active (positive) or “0” for inactive (negative), alongside a probability score (0-1) indicating prediction confidence.

RESULTS

Laboratory proficiency of the KeratinoSens™ assay

Proficiency chemicals were tested to confirm assay reliability and reproducibility in accordance with validation guidelines. The resulting data were generated specifically for this study and were not previously reported or used in any other publication. In the proficiency test conducted to evaluate the technical performance and reproducibility of the KeratinoSens™ assay, all 10 reference substances were accurately classified, with ethylene glycol dimethacrylate, cinnamyl alcohol, 2-mercaptobenzothiazole, 4-methylaminophenol sulfate, methyldibromoglutaronitrile, and 2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene identified as sensitizers, and salicylic acid, lactic acid, glycerol, and isopropanol identified as non-sensitizers (Table 2). All tested sensitizing compounds showed dose-dependent elevations in luciferase activity exceeding the 1.5-fold threshold at concentrations below 1000 μM, with IC30 values (indicating 30% cytotoxicity) higher than their respective EC1.5 responses. In contrast, the non-sensitizers failed to trigger luciferase induction or did so only at cytotoxic levels (cell viability below 70%). These results confirmed the technical proficiency of the laboratory to proficiently conduct the KeratinoSens™ assay.

Evaluation of PFAS chemicals using the KeratinoSens™ assay

Cell viability was examined across a range of PFAS concentrations to assess compound-specific cytotoxicity profiles (Fig. 2). Cytotoxicity data are reported as IC30 values. At concentrations up to 125 µM, all tested PFAS maintained cell viability above 70%, indicating low cytotoxicity within this range. However, at concentrations of 250 µM and higher, certain carboxylates (PFOA, PFNA, PFDA) and 6:2 FTOH induced a pronounced, dose-dependent decline in cell viability, with these compounds reducing viability to below 40% and approaching complete cellular loss at the highest tested concentrations. In contrast, short-chain PFAS such as PFBS and PFBA, as well as 4:2 FTOH, displayed minimal cytotoxic effects, retaining high cell viability even at the maximum concentration tested.

(A) Short perfluoroalkyl substances (PFBS, PFBA), (B) Long perfluoroalkyl substances (PFOA, PFHpA, PFNA, PFDA), and (C) fluorotelomer alcohols (4:2 FTOH, 6:2 FTOH). The dashed line indicates the IC30, concentration at which 30% reduction of cell viability occurs.

In the KeratinoSens™ assay, the evaluated PFAS compounds exhibited variable induction of the ARE-dependent luciferase reporter (Fig. 3). The EC1.5, maximal fold induction (Imax) and cell viability values for each chemical are presented in Table 3. The marked induction of the luciferase reporter by PFDA and PFNA at 250 µM coincided with a steep decline in cell viability at this and higher concentrations, as observed in the cell viability assay. Notably, for both compounds, cellular viability dropped below the cytotoxicity threshold near or above 250 µM, suggesting that the apparent Nrf2 reporter activation reflects secondary stress responses associated with cytotoxicity rather than regulated, adaptive Nrf2 pathway engagement. Based on the OECD TG 442D classification criteria, two chemicals, PFOA and PFHpA, were classified as positive, fulfilling all three requirements for activation of the Nrf2-Keap1 pathway (EC1.5 <1000 µM, Imax ≥1.5-fold, cell viability >70% at EC1.5, and clear dose-response). In contrast, several shorter- and longer-chain PFAS, including PFBS, PFBA, PFNA, PFDA, 4:2 FTOH, and 6:2 FTOH, showed no significant induction in luciferase activity and were therefore categorized as negative. Although PFNA fulfilled the first three OECD positive criteria, it failed to demonstrate a clear dose-response in luciferase induction. Accordingly, this outcome was classified as borderline and cannot be interpreted as a definitive positive response.

Induction of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) following the treatment of PFAS chemicals

The intracellular generation of ROS in KeratinoSens™ cells was quantified following 24-h exposure to increasing concentrations (1-2000 μM) of the tested PFAS compounds (Fig. 4). The baseline fluorescence value (1.0) represented the negative control (vehicle-treated cells), and fold increases above this level indicated ROS induction. As shown in Fig. 4, PFAS exposure produced diverse effects on cellular ROS levels. Although none of the tested compounds induced a robust, dose-dependent increase in ROS generation over the baseline level, most PFAS show variable folds of ROS induction.

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation in KeratinoSens™ cells following PFAS exposure. Intracellular ROS levels were quantified using the DCFDA assay and expressed as fold increase relative to the vehicle control (0.1% DMSO, baseline=1). (A) Short perfluoroalkyl substances (PFBS, PFBA), (B) Long perfluoroalkyl substances (PFOA, PFHpA, PFNA, PFDA), and (C) fluorotelomer alcohols (4:2 FTOH, 6:2 FTOH).

PFNA and 6:2 FTOH produced the highest increases in cellular ROS at lower concentrations (up to 31 µM), though the effect declines at higher concentrations. At concentrations above 62 µM, PFNA and fluorotelomer alcohols show a marked reduction in ROS, falling below baseline, indicating that excessive exposure may suppress detectable oxidative signaling due to cytotoxicity or loss of cellular metabolic capacity. Whereas PFHpA, PFOA, PFBS, PFBA, and PFDA produced relatively minor ROS fluctuations across the concentration range.

In silico prediction

In silico analysis using the ProTox 3.0 predictive toxicity platform was conducted to estimate the potential of selected PFAS to activate the Nrf2/ARE oxidative stress response pathway. The predictive model classified all tested compounds as inactive for Nrf2 activation, with probabilities ranging from 0.96 to 1.0. The high probability scores for inactivity (close to 1.0) suggest that these PFAS are unlikely to directly engage the Nrf2 signaling pathway under typical model assumptions.

DISCUSSION

Recent studies have increasingly associated human exposure to PFAS, both from consumer products and environmental sources with various skin conditions such as atopic dermatitis, psoriasis and allergic diseases, through mechanisms that may involve immune dysregulation and oxidative stress pathways (Glüge et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2024). In response to these emerging health concerns, this study investigated the effects of several PFAS on the activation of the Nrf2-Keap1-ARE signaling pathway, intracellular ROS generation, and in silico-predicted Nrf2 activity using the ProTox 3.0 platform (Fig. 5).

Summary of experimental workflow and key findings for PFAS-induced responses in keratinocytes. PFAS compounds were tested for their ability to induce cytotoxicity, oxidative stress, and Nrf2 activation using KeratinoSens™ cells. The workflow integrates luciferase reporter activity, ROS generation, and cell viability assays. PFAS induced variable ROS responses, with selective activation of Nrf2 signaling observed for PFOA and PFHpA. ROS generation did not trigger Nrf2 activation. All PFAS were predicted as inactive in silico, highlighting limitations of computational models.

Results revealed that PFOA and PFHpA triggered a positive response in Nrf2 pathway activation, while PFNA showed a borderline response and other PFAS tested negative. Notably, PFNA and PFDA exhibited Nrf2 activity at 250 μM but not at higher concentrations. This pattern is consistent with cytotoxicity-driven signal attenuation. At concentrations above 250 μM, PFNA and PFDA induced marked cytotoxicity, which likely reduced viable cell numbers and overall transcriptional activity, thereby diminishing luciferase reporter signals. Thus, the apparent loss of Nrf2 activity at higher concentrations does not necessarily indicate absence of pathway activation, but rather suppression of measurable reporter activity due to severe cytotoxicity (Natsch, 2017). In contrast, PFOA exhibited comparatively lower cytotoxicity at equivalent concentrations, allowing sufficient cell viability to sustain measurable Nrf2-dependent luciferase activity. Therefore, the positive response observed with PFOA more likely reflects genuine oxidative stress–mediated Nrf2 activation rather than an artifact of cytotoxicity. This distinction underscores the importance of interpreting reporter assay results in the context of concurrent viability measurements.

The ROS assay further highlighted that certain PFAS, particularly PFNA, and 6:2 FTOH, induced increased intracellular ROS levels at moderate concentrations. At higher concentrations, a decline in ROS was observed. As the DCFDA assay measures total intracellular ROS production, diminished signals at highly cytotoxic concentrations likely reflect loss of viable cells, impaired probe retention, or metabolic dysfunction, rather than an actual decrease in oxidative stress (Jakubowski and Bartosz, 2000; Kato, 2025). On the other hand, the remaining PFAS exhibited relatively minor ROS induction. Together, these results imply that, although some PFAS may induce modest oxidative stress within a restricted concentration range, this does not consistently translate into Nrf2 pathway activation as measured by the KeratinoSens™ assay (Natsch, 2017; Ojo et al., 2022). Oxidative stress may in some cases induce the Nrf2 pathway, but this often only occurs at cytotoxic concentrations or is transient and below the thresholds for classification as a sensitizer in the KeratinoSens™ system (Natsch, 2017).

PFAS are widely recognized to trigger generation of free radicals, particularly ROS, leading to a marked reduction in total antioxidant capacity and consequently causing oxidative stress in target tissues (Wielsøe et al., 2015). ROS serve as early mediators of oxidative imbalance in epithelial models and may trigger Nrf2 activation when redox stress surpasses a cellular threshold (Frias et al., 2020). Peropadre et al. (2018) demonstrated that moderate exposure to PFOA causes persistent DNA damage and induces the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) in human HaCaT keratinocytes. A subcellular study of PFAS-treated HepG2 cells found that some PFAS exposures produced moderate, transient ROS increases that were not sufficient to induce Nrf2 activation or significant antioxidant gene expression, suggesting that endogenous defenses buffer sub-threshold oxidative insults and prevent induction of the cellular stress response (Amstutz et al., 2025). Comparable transient ROS elevations have also been noted in epithelial cell models exposed to PFAS (Sant et al., 2018). Collectively, these findings reinforce that PFAS may induce oxidative and inflammatory responses in epithelial tissues. Since keratinocytes represent the first line of defense in human skin, these results are particularly relevant to dermatotoxicology, where disruption of redox-sensitive pathways may influence inflammation, aging, and barrier function (Schäfer and Werner, 2015).

Notably, PFOA tested negative in the murine Local Lymph Node Assay (LLNA) yet positive in both the KeratinoSens™ and ROS assays. These chemical and biological distinctions become particularly evident when interpreting divergent assay results. LLNA identifies adaptive immune sensitization via T-cell proliferation in lymph nodes (Gerberick et al., 2007). In contrast, KeratinoSens™ detects molecular initiating events related to Nrf2-mediated cytoprotective gene activation in keratinocytes, an early oxidative stress response (Natsch 2017). Studies confirm that chemicals may test negative in LLNA but positive in KeratinoSens™ if they trigger redox signaling without classical immunogenic effects, underscoring that Nrf2-mediated redox perturbation represents a toxicological pathway distinct from adaptive immune sensitization detected by LLNA (Casati et al., 2014; Strickland et al., 2016). Hence, compounds like PFOA may disrupt redox equilibrium and induce Nrf2 activation in keratinocytes while not necessarily provoking T-cell-mediated immune responses detectable in LLNA. While LLNA-negative results confirm the absence of classical sensitization, oxidative stress-driven responses observed in keratinocytes may still indicate sub-toxic perturbations relevant to chronic or barrier-compromised skin.

Complementing these findings, the in silico predictions using the ProTox 3.0 model classified all tested PFAS as inactive for Nrf2/ARE pathway activation, revealing a difference between computational outputs and in vitro observations. This discrepancy does not necessarily indicate model inaccuracy but highlights current limitations in modeling PFAS-specific mechanisms not based on conventional electrophilic structures. The ProTox 3.0 model bases predictions on similarity to known electrophilic or oxidative stress-inducing compounds (Banerjee et al., 2024) thus, the inherently inert and perfluorinated nature of PFAS likely contributed to their negative predictions. These findings highlight the complexity of PFAS-cell interactions and underscore the importance of employing integrated mechanistic strategies within the Adverse Outcome Pathway (AOP) paradigm for skin sensitization. Combining in silico, in chemico, and in vitro assays provides a weight-of-evidence approach that strengthens mechanistic understanding of PFAS-related effects.

From a dermatological perspective, this study provides valuable findings that PFAS may disrupt oxidative balance, barrier function, and inflammatory signaling in skin cells. Nrf2 serves as a key adaptive defense regulator in keratinocytes, controlling the cellular antioxidant response to oxidative stress. However, chronic Nrf2 activation may have detrimental effects, including pro-inflammatory cytokine upregulation, altered epidermal differentiation, and delayed wound healing (Kahremany et al., 2022; Salman et al., 2025). Persistent oxidative stress disrupts keratinocyte-fibroblast crosstalk that is crucial for epidermal homeostasis and barrier maintenance, weakening the lipid barrier and increasing vulnerability to external irritants (Nicolaou and Kendall, 2024). Depending on the specific PFAS compounds as well as the duration and level of exposure, these substances can inhibit skin immune function while promoting oxidative stress and inflammatory responses within the tissue. While current evidence suggests that PFAS exposure may have only a modest impact on childhood atopic dermatitis, emerging reports linking PFAS to psoriasis and melanoma highlight the urgent need for population-based investigations complemented by mechanistic experimental research to elucidate the pathways through which PFAS disrupt skin homeostasis, thereby informing preventive strategies and clarifying the dermatological risks associated with PFAS exposure (Haarmann-Stemmann and Esser, 2025). Given the widespread use of PFAS in cosmetics, waterproof fabrics, and household cleaners (Mousavi et al., 2021; Weatherly et al., 2024), these findings highlight the importance of re-evaluating dermal exposure risks, particularly in occupational or high-contact settings. By characterizing early redox and stress-response pathways, this study broadens the toxicological understanding of PFAS beyond immunological endpoints and offers a mechanistic basis for the assessment of non-sensitizing but biologically active compounds. Further studies are warranted to clarify long-term outcomes and to guide preventive strategies in dermatotoxicology.
