Exploring the lived experiences of depression among youths and their guardians in sub-Saharan Africa: a systematic review protocol
Authors: Precious Makiyi, Michael Udedi, Moses Kumwenda, James January
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe intersection between mental health and culture is more prominent when we study how different mental health conditions present in different cultures. There is enough evidence to suggest that mental health conditions like depression, for instance, present differently in Western and non-Western societies. Most phenomenological studies on youth depression have been done in high-income countries. This systematic review, therefore, seeks to understand how depression is experienced by youths (10–24 years) and their carers in SSA.MethodsTwo research assistants (with the help of P. M.) shall independently search articles on the following databases: PubMed, PsycINFO, Embase, HealthSTAR, CINAHL, Google Scholar, Web of Science, and Scopus. Two reviewers will then independently evaluate the selected articles based on a systematic search strategy, extract relevant data, and synthesize findings using meta-aggregation techniques. We will use the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines in reporting results. The protocol is registered with the PROSPERO database (CRD42024556661).DiscussionThe results from this systematic review will help researchers especially in SSA to have a better understanding of how depression is experienced by adolescents in the region and its impact on their guardians. This has a potential of informing a diagnostic or screening tool that is sensitive to the context of SSA.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO CRD42024556661.Date of registration: 10th June 2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-025-02873-5.

Background

About 14% of the global burden of diseases is due to mental disorders like depression [1]. Unfortunately, this 14% is not equally distributed across the globe, as most (over 80%) of these cases are in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [1]. Furthermore, adolescents and young people (10 to 24 years old), who are in the majority in SSA [2], are largely affected by mental health problems [2]. A recent systematic review on the prevalence of mental health problems among adolescents in SSA found a median point prevalence of depression of 26.9% in the general adolescent population [2].

Despite the high burden of youth mental health challenges in LMICs and the subsequent demand for mental health services by this age group, there seems to be inadequate services to cater for their mental health needs. Studies indicate that young people with depression in SSA experience a double burden of depression and increased risky behaviours like substance abuse and suicide leading to more mental and physical health problems [3]. Different psychological interventions for depression have been tested and proven to work among youths in low-income countries like those in SSA. A meta-analysis of the effects of interventions to reduce youth/adolescent depression in LMIC indicated that interventions like cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT), microfinance/economic interventions, and interpersonal therapy help to reduce depression [4]. A scoping review on the mental health interventions for adolescents and young people in Africa indicated that different mental health interventions for this age group exist in the region, and that they can be implemented in various settings including schools, communities, health facilities, and in camps for internally displaced persons (IDP) [5].

There is a bidirectional relationship between the sociological aspects and biological-psychological aspects of depression in that one influences and gets influenced by the other [6]. Historically, depression was perceived as a predominantly Western disease, and that people from non-Western cultures like SSA region were less likely to experience it [6]. This was so because of how other cultures viewed and perceived depressive symptoms. Culture has an influence on how a phenomenon like depression is experienced and communicated [7]. The manifestation or interpretation of depressive symptoms also varies among different cultures. Other cultures put much emphasis on somatic/physical symptoms, whereas in other cultures the emphasis is on cognitive or emotional symptoms [6]. As a result of this difference in perceptions of depressive symptoms, diagnosing depression in some cultures is difficult since the symptoms may not be perceived as a disorder and also because people of that particular culture may not be willing to talk about their emotions or seek help [6]. Furthermore, language also plays a role in mental health in that some languages, especially in SSA, may not have the appropriate words through which one can communicate their feelings and emotions [8]. This has implications on help-seeking behaviours as well as prevalence rates because in a culture where depression is less understood and expressed, prevalence rates may be reported as low [6]. An example is given of Japan and India whereby in India depression is well understood and reported unlike in Japan where depression and all mental disorders are like a taboo; thus, few people will report their emotions [6]. Thus, in India, the prevalence of depression may be seen as high compared to Japan because of cultural influence on mental disorders.

The challenge in diagnosing depression across cultures is also influenced by the method or process through which a diagnosis is made. For example, the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), which is considered the gold standard of diagnosing mental disorders like depression, originated from the Western world, where the case definition of depression may be different from non-western cultures like in SSA [9]. Without cultural adaptation, tools used for diagnosing depression may have varying performance across cultures.

Culture also plays a role in the management of depression and, subsequently, the outcome of the depression. For an intervention to be relevant in a particular setting different from where it originated, it must undergo cultural validation. Subsequently, the cultural context of the users should also be taken into consideration when defining and evaluating treatment outcomes [9].

The benefit of understanding depression from the perspective of the adolescents themselves is that cases are less likely to be underdiagnosed, misdiagnosed, or mistreated [10] because case definition may be sensitive to how the SSA adolescents perceive and experience the condition. Exploring the lived experience of clients helps researchers to determine clients’ understanding of the illness, their insight, their health-seeking behaviours, and the pathway to care [11]. As for the guardians, through their experience of taking care of an adolescent with depression, we can determine the burden of care associated with adolescent depression and its impact on the mental health of their guardians.

Lived experience is a broad term which compasses a lot of processes including but not limited to getting a diagnosis, accessing mental health support, receiving treatment, experiencing peer and family support, and achieving recovery or remission of the symptoms [12]. In the context of youth depression, it may encompass concepts like the symptoms of depression experienced by the adolescents, the social-cultural context of depression, the pathway to care taken, and the burden of care for the parents or guardians and the whole family system [13, 14].

Most studies on lived experiences of depression by youth or adolescents have been done in high-income countries [10, 15, 16], making it difficult for researchers to infer the findings in the context of SSA due to differences in cultures and sociodemographic characteristics. In our preliminary literature search, we identified four systematic and meta-analysis/synthesis on the lived experience of depression by youths across the globe [10, 14–16]. Despite this evidence, we identified significant gaps such as underrepresentation of SSA studies [14] and restrictive age range [10, 15].

Almost all the reviews we came across concentrated mainly on features of depression experienced by the adolescents as their primary outcome. The lived experience of mental health support and care during the depressive episode was not fully explored. Unfortunately, the experiences of the guardians or the family members were also not explored. However, we believe that this is an important gap worthy of exploring. Our systematic review will therefore look at the lived experience of depression by the adolescents and their guardians in SSA. We shall further consider studies that look at other areas of experience as mentioned above other than just the symptomatology. This systematic review, therefore, aims to understand how depression is experienced by adolescents in the SSA region. Furthermore, as a secondary objective, we shall review the experience of guardians of adolescents with depression. To be more precise, we wish, through this review, to determine if depression is experienced by SSA adolescents differently from their Western-world counterparts [17].

Guardians of children with depression are likely to undergo a significant psychological distress because of being overly burdened by taking care of an adolescent with depression. A study on the prevalence of depression and associated factors among primary caregivers of children and adolescents with mental disorders in Ethiopia found an overall prevalence of 58% [18]. Among other factors, the duration of care, the absence of other caregivers, and being a mother were associated with the depression [18]. Even though this study did not offer a comparison of mothers of children with mental illness and those without, it is apparent that the prevalence is higher than the 25% found in the general population. Depression among caregivers of adolescents with mental disorders like depression is likely to increase the adolescent’s condition. A study done in South Africa found that adolescents with depression are likely to have increased symptoms of depression and conduct problems if their guardians or primary caregivers are not warm or supportive due to reasons like depression or distress [19]. A study on the perception and experience of caregivers of adolescents with depression done in Brazil, among other things, found that adolescent depression is a condition that requires treatment [20]. However, most of the caregivers thought that youth or adolescent depression is just inappropriate behaviour or attention-seeking by the adolescents [20]. Furthermore, the main causes of adolescent depression, according to the carers, are parental rejection, loss of significant others, sexual abuse, and parental divorce [20]. With regard to the symptomatology, the experience of the caregivers was that adolescent depression is characterized by the following symptoms: social isolation, irritability and aggressiveness, poor school performance, sadness, crying, change in eating behaviour, self-harm and suicidal behaviours, and hallucinations, among others [20]. Thus, it is imperative to consider not only the experience of depression among the adolescents but also the experience of their guardians because chances are high that for a better outcome, the environment in which the adolescents are operating must be conducive and supportive.

It is worth noting that cultural influence on depression is not only negative. In some instances, culture acts as a protective factor against developing depression. For example, in some cultures, community cohesion and social inclusion are promoted, thus preventing individuals from developing depression [21].

Methods

Information sources

The following electronic databases shall be used to search for related papers to answer our systematic review question: PubMed, PsycINFO, Embase, HealthSTAR, CINAHL, AnthroSource, Web of Science, Embase, and Scopus. The databases shall be searched from inception until August 30, 2024, and only reports written in English shall be considered. Where applicable, we shall contact authors of included reviews to seek clarification on their reported published information or to get additional unpublished data.

Grey literature shall also be utilized to obtain rich data. This will include but not limited to unpublished manuscripts, dissertations/theses, (auto)biographies, news articles, conference abstracts, and relevant citations in selected articles. Furthermore, we will examine reference lists of related articles to identify relevant journal papers to include in the review. Depending on the availability of the articles we are looking for, if need be, we shall do forward citation searches, and this will be reported appropriately. Furthermore, we shall provide an appendix of a list of sources screened and primary studies or reports that we shall review.

Search strategy

The following search concepts form the search strategy for this review: (1) Youth or adolescents or caregivers, (2) depression, (3) phenomenology, (4) perception or experience, and (5) sub-Saharan Africa. Each of the above-mentioned databases shall have tailored controlled vocabulary words. The electronic search strategy below is the one that was used in one of the databases (PubMed). It is sufficient to mention that the search strategy for this systematic review has been adapted/modified from Paolo Fusar-Poli et al. (2023) and Anna Viduani et al. (2024) [13, 14]. Assessment of methodological quality and risk-of-bias assessment

After running a search strategy, two independent reviewers shall screen the identified papers (title and abstract). We shall use Microsoft Excel to remove duplicates. The identified papers shall undergo an independent quality assessment using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme checklist (CASP) [10] and the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) by the two independent researchers with supervision by the supervisors (M. U., M. K., J. J.). The researchers shall set a predetermined criteria to be met by a paper before it is included. Through this quality assessment, we shall also assess for any biases. Thereafter, a full-text review for all identified reports shall be done before proceeding with data extraction and meta-aggregation. At this stage, some reports may be removed if they do not meet the inclusion criteria.

Data extraction, management, and synthesis

Data extraction shall be done independently by two researchers. The extraction for this review shall be done in multiple phases whereby a codebook for each paper shall be developed. This codebook shall contain details like the population, phenomenon of interest, the context, relevant methodological information, and the study findings/results in one column and codes in another column. In developing the codes, a line-by-line coding of the codebooks shall be done independently by two researchers. When each article has been reviewed independently, the two researchers shall have debriefing meetings where the codes shall be discussed and synchronized to come up with a single codebook for each article/report. This will ensure that there is reflexivity. A coding matrix for all the codebooks will then be developed to allow for cross-comparison during data synthesis. This coding matrix shall include a code, its description, and illustrations of the codes. During the extraction and analysis, our goal shall be to identify common themes, coping strategies, and the impact of sociocultural factors on the lived experience of depression among adolescents/youths and their guardians in SSA.

Data synthesis shall be done using meta-aggregation method. Our meta-aggregation shall be based on the following steps as proposed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [22] as follows:We shall gather all findings (codes and themes) from all the reports included, including accompanying illustrations.We shall categorize findings based on similarity in wording or concept with a minimum of two findings per category.Finally, we shall develop one or more synthesized findings of a minimum of two categories.

Notably, our working definition of findings shall be all themes, metaphors, and other analytic or observational data from the papers by the authors. Furthermore, the category descriptions and the synthesized findings and their subsequent descriptions shall be created by a consensus process between the two reviewers mentioned above.

The strength of the evidence of this review shall be assessed using the GRADE-CERQual (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation-Confidence in the Evidence from Reviews of Qualitative Research) (23). Through the GRADE-CERQual, the following four components or domains shall be explored: (1) Methodological limitations, (2) coherence, (3) adequacy, and (4) relevance [23]. The GRADE-CERQual will also be instrumental in the assessment of meta-bias.

Expected results

We hypothesize that common features of youth depression in SSA will be different from those experienced by adults and fellow youths from the Western world. Furthermore, we expect that the sociocultural context in SSA will shape the lived experience of depression among youths and their guardians in SSA. We will use the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines in reporting our results (Appendix/Additional File 1).

Discussion

The findings from this systematic review shall be reported based on the PRISMA-P guidelines, and the subsequent discussion of this review shall be based on results of our meta-aggregation. Our discussion shall centre around how adolescents in SSA experience depression and how this is different from the other regions.

Conclusion and implications

This systematic review comes at a time when the world is recovering from the COVID-19, and it is our hope that its findings will provide an overview of how adolescents in SSA experience depression. Furthermore, we hope that this review shall inform policy and guide practice on how adolescent depression is experienced in the context of SSA and, most importantly, how adolescents with depression can easily be identified.
