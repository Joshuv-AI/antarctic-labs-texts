Sexual knowledge as a predictor of intention in sexual activity in university women
Authors: Janett V. Chávez-Sosa, Luis M. Ticahuanca Tacora, Criss M. Flores Burga, Jerusalen A. Garnique Fernandez, Salomon Huancahuire-Vega
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDSexual health knowledge is a key determinant of adolescents’ and young adults’ sexual and reproductive behaviors. However, limited evidence exists on how sexual knowledge influences behavioral intentions among university women who have not yet initiated sexual activity. Understanding these relationships is essential for designing evidence-based educational and public health strategies that promote safer sexual decision-making and reproductive autonomy.OBJECTIVETo evaluate whether the level of sexual health knowledge predicts intentions regarding sexual activity, sexual protection, and sexual risk among university women with no history of sexual intercourse.STUDY DESIGNA cross-sectional, analytical study was conducted among female university students aged 18–35 years enrolled in a private university in East Lima during the 2025-I academic semester. A total of 493 participants met eligibility criteria, which included absence of prior sexual intercourse and capacity to complete questionnaires independently. Sexual health knowledge was measured using the validated knowledge dimension of the CAPSEX questionnaire, and sexual intentions were assessed with the female version of the Intention Scale in Sexual Activity. Descriptive statistics, Spearman correlation analyses, and multiple linear regression models were used to examine associations between knowledge scores and intentions. Multivariate models were adjusted for age, relationship status, place of origin, school of enrollment, year of study, family income, and employment status. Significance was set at P<.05.RESULTSOverall, 51.3% of participants demonstrated adequate sexual knowledge. Knowledge was not significantly correlated with intention of sexual activity (ρ=–0.063; P=.165). In multivariate analyses, higher sexual knowledge predicted lower intention of sexual risk (B=−0.302; 95% CI: −0.462 to −0.141; P<.001) and higher intention of sexual protection (B=0.127; 95% CI: 0.019 to 0.235; P=.021). Age was positively associated with sexual risk intention (B=0.181; 95% CI: 0.018 to 0.344; P=.029). Low family income was associated with higher intention of sexual protection (B=0.572; 95% CI: 0.145 to 0.999; P=.009) and reduced intention of sexual risk (B=−0.744; 95% CI: −1.382 to −0.105; P=.023). Not working showed lower scores with sexual protection intention (B=−0.513; 95% CI: −0.988 to −0.039; P=.034).CONCLUSIONSexual health knowledge independently predicts both decreased sexual risk intention and increased protective intention among university women without prior sexual activity. Socioeconomic disadvantage remains an important modifying factor. Strengthening comprehensive sexuality education within higher education institutions may enhance reproductive autonomy and reduce future sexual health risks.

Introduction

The right to access modern and informed contraceptive methods is a fundamental human right.1 However, of the 1.1 billion women of reproductive age who require contraception in the world, around 270 million still have unmet needs.2,3 This deficit is more common among adolescents than in adult women, with an estimated 23 million adolescents in low- and middle-income countries not having access to modern contraceptives.4,5 This situation is aggravated by the physical, biological, and hormonal changes characteristic of adolescence, which often lead them to initiate risky sexual practices. Factors such as alcohol and drug use also increase the likelihood of unprotected sex, which increases exposure to unplanned pregnancies and sexually transmitted infections (STIs).6, 7, 8

STIS represent a global public health problem, with more than one million cases transmitted every day. It is estimated that 499 million corresponds to gonorrhea, syphilis, chlamydia, and trichomonas. These infections, if not treated promptly, can lead to severe complications such as infertility, pelvic inflammatory disease, ectopic pregnancy, and cervical cancer. Likewise, the presence of non-HIV STIs increases the risk of acquiring this infection.9,10

Sex education has been implemented in different modalities in school and university environments. On the one hand, abstinence-based programs have shown limitations by focusing solely on the prevention of pregnancy through the avoidance of the sexual act. In contrast, comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) holistically addresses aspects such as human development, interpersonal relationships, sexual and reproductive health, sexual behavior, and sociocultural dimensions, intending to promote skills, knowledge, and attitudes for a more responsible and healthy sexual life.11,12

In Latin America, the situation of sexual and reproductive rights continues to be a challenge. Despite regulatory advances, gaps in access to reliable information and adequate services persist, particularly in rural areas and among vulnerable young populations.13 In Peru, there are limitations in the coverage of comprehensive sexual education programs, in addition to sociocultural and gender barriers that restrict women's access to information and contraceptive methods. Research carried out in the Peruvian Amazon has documented that rural youth face insufficient access to reproductive health services, which limits their ability to prevent unplanned pregnancies and protect themselves from STIS.14 However, studies that explore in depth the level of sexual knowledge in university women and how these influence their decisions regarding sexual activity are still scarce.

This scenario is especially critical in the case of university women, who, despite having greater educational opportunities, are not exempt from risky sexual practices. Several studies have reported that only about 56% of university women claim to have used contraceptives in their last sexual intercourse, with moderate efficacy methods such as contraceptive pills and male condoms predominating, compared to a low use of long-lasting and high efficacy methods (LARC), such as intrauterine devices and hormonal implants.15,16 These deficiencies have a direct impact on their academic trajectory and their life project, since unplanned pregnancies in this population group are associated with university dropout, decreased future income, lower physical and emotional well-being, and limitations in the exercise of their sexual and reproductive rights.17 The available literature focuses mainly on method use prevalence and access barriers. However, studies that explicitly link sexual knowledge with the intention of initiating or regulating sexual activity in this age group are limited.

Likewise, it has been shown that the intention in sexual activity and the willingness to adopt protective behaviors depend on multiple factors, including the level of sexual knowledge and the perception of risk. Empirical evidence shows that adolescents and young people with greater knowledge about contraception and STI prevention have a greater intention to use protective measures in their sexual relationships.18 In this sense, it is crucial to analyze how sexual knowledge acts as a predictor of intention in sexual activity, especially in university women, who are at an important stage of the consolidation of their reproductive autonomy. This gap justifies the need to explore in greater detail the role that sexual knowledge plays in their intentions of sexual activity.

Therefore, the study aimed to determine the extent to which sexual knowledge constitutes a predictor of intention in sexual activity in university women.

Materials and methods

Design, population, and sample

Study with a cross-sectional and analytical design. The eligible population consisted of 1908 female students enrolled during the 2025-I academic semester at a private university in the Lima East region. The sample was selected through intentional nonprobabilistic sampling, including only the participants who met the established selection criteria. The sample size calculation indicated a minimum of 320 university women, with 95% confidence and a 5% margin of error. However, the final sample comprised 493 university women, exceeding the minimum required and strengthening the accuracy of the results. The final sample represents approximately 25.8% of this population; however, given the nonprobabilistic sampling approach, this proportion should not be interpreted as population representativeness. University women between 18 and 35 years of age, currently enrolled in the 2025-I academic semester, who had not engaged in sexual relations, had sufficient cognitive capacity to answer the questionnaires autonomously, and agreed to participate in the study by signing the informed consent form, were included in the study. On the other hand, pregnant or breastfeeding women were excluded, who presented cognitive, psychological, or health difficulties that prevented adequate understanding and response of the instruments, who had previously participated in formal intensive sex education programs at the same university, and those who did not complete all the questionnaires applied.

Measurement instruments

The Questionnaire of Sexual Knowledge, Attitudes and Practices in University Youth (CAPSEX) was developed and validated in Peru by Herrera and collaborators to comprehensively assess knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to sexuality among university youth. The instrument consists of 22 items distributed in three dimensions: knowledge about sexuality, attitudes, and sexual practices.19 For this research, only the knowledge dimension was used. This section of the questionnaire consists of 8 polytomous items (false, true, I do not know) designed to assess knowledge of contraception and sexual health. The final score is obtained by the summation of correct answers, so that a higher score reflects a better level of expertise. In its original validation, this dimension demonstrated adequate reliability, with a Cronbach's alpha coefficient exceeding 0.70, supporting its relevance to studies of the Peruvian university population.

The Intention Scale in Sexual Activity, developed by Espada and collaborators in Spain and replicated in the Colombian population.20 In this study, only the 6 items corresponding to the version for women were used, which explore the probability of initiating or maintaining sexual behaviors with and without penetration. Responses are organized on a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from "not at all likely" to "very likely." The total score is the sum of the 6 items, with higher values reflecting a greater intention to maintain sexual behaviors. In validation studies, the instrument has demonstrated high reliability, with Cronbach's alpha values exceeding 0.85, indicating its psychometric strength for evaluating sexual intention among young females and university students.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28. Prior to analysis, the database was reviewed for completeness, consistency, and potential data entry errors. Only questionnaires with complete responses were included in the final analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and syudy variables. Categorical variables, including sex, place of origin, relationship status (with vs without partner), family economic income, employment status, school of enrollment, and year of study, were presented as absolute frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables including age and questionnaires scores, were summarized using means and standard deviation (SD), as well as medians and interquartile range (IQR) when appropriate. The distributions of scores for the variables "Knowledge about sexual health," "Intention of sexual activity," "Intention of sexual protection," and "Intention of sexual risk" were examined through descriptive measures and graphical inspection.

Because the study variables were derived from questionnaire scores and did not meet assumptions of normal distribution, bivariate associations between "Knowledge about sexual health" and the outcome variables "Intention of sexual activity," "Intention of sexual protection," and "Intention of sexual risk" were evaluated using the Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ). Correlation coefficient and corresponding P-values were reported, considering P<.05 as statistically significant.

Since the dependent variables "Intention of sexual protection" and "Intention of sexual risk" correspond to continuous scores derived from the sum of Likert-type scale items, multiple linear regression models were applied to evaluate their association with knowledge about sexual health. Covariates included in the multivariable models were selected a priori based on theoretical relevance and evidence from previous studies examining determinants of sexual health knowledge, sexual decision-making, and behavioral intentions among adolescents and young adults. Variables such as age, relationship status, place of origin, field of study (Health Sciences vs Nonhealth Sciences), year of study, family income, and employment status were considered potential sociodemographic and educational factors that could influence both sexual health knowledge and sexual behavioral intentions.21 Two independent models were built: one for the dependent variable "Intention of sexual risk" and another for "Intention of sexual protection." In both models, the primary independent variable was the total score of "Knowledge about sexual health." Potential confounding variables were selected based on theoretical relevance and previous literature and included age, relationship status, place of origin, school of enrollment, year of study, family economic income, and employment status. Categorical variables were entered into the models using dummy coding, with one category designated as the reference group.

Model results were expressed as unstandardized coefficients (B) with their respective 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) and P-values. Before fitting the models, the assumptions of the linear regression were evaluated, including linearity, independence of observations, homoscedasticity of residuals, absence of multicollinearity among predictors, and normality of residual distributions. Statistical significance was established at a two-sided P-value <.05.

Ethical aspects

The research received ethical approval from the Universidad Peruana Unión Ethics Committee (No. 2024-CEUPeU-0032) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, with electronic informed consent obtained after providing detailed study information. Data collection was anonymous via a secure online platform, and all records were stored in password-protected files accessible only to the research team. No personal identifiers or images were included in the study materials.

Discussion

Principal findings

The findings of this study reveal that, after adjusting for sociodemographic covariates, greater sexual knowledge significantly predicted a lower intention to participate in risky sexual behaviors and a higher intention to use protective measures. Although these associations were statistically significant, the magnitude of the effects was modest, indicating that sexual health knowledge contributes incrementally to behavioral intentions rather than producing large individual-level changes. However, given that sexual behavior is inherently multifactorial, even modest effects may be meaningful, particularly when considering cumulative changes across realistic variations in knowledge levels.

Results in the context of what is known

These results confirm the protective role of informed knowledge, in line with previous literature that suggests that adequate understanding about contraception and STI prevention promotes more responsible sexual decisions among adolescents and young adults.22,23 In addition, the findings indicate that sexual knowledge is a relevant but complex determinant of sexual intentions, both protective and risky, with implications for the design and implementation of comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) strategies in higher education.24

Although slightly more than half of the respondents demonstrated adequate knowledge in sexual health, a considerable proportion still had deficiencies, despite being in higher education. This knowledge gap underscores the persistent limitations in school and preuniversity sex education and the absence of systematic curricular inclusion of reproductive health issues at the university level. Similar deficits have been documented in other low- and middle-income countries, including among health science students, highlighting a broader regional and structural challenge in sexual health literacy.11,14

In the bivariate analysis, a higher level of sexual knowledge was paradoxically associated with lower intentions of protection and higher intentions of risk. However, this apparent contradiction was clarified in the multivariate models. After adjusting for sociodemographic covariates, greater sexual knowledge significantly predicted a lower intention to participate in risky sexual behaviors and a higher intention to use protective measures. These findings suggest the presence of confounding variables, such as socioeconomic status, education, or employment status, that can mask the protective effects of knowledge if not statistically controlled for.

Our results align with previous research suggesting that knowledge alone may not be sufficient to modify behavior unless accompanied by enabling environments and personal autonomy.12 In addition, a higher level of knowledge can sometimes coexist with overconfidence or underestimation of risk, a phenomenon described in theories of risk compensation in sexual behavior.8,25 Our findings can be interpreted within established behavioral frameworks such as the Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) model and the Theory of Planned Behavior, which posit that knowledge alone is insufficient to drive behavioral change. According to these models, behavioral intentions are also shaped by attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control, as well as environmental and contextual factors.21 Similarly, the COM-B model highlights that behavior results from the interaction between capability, opportunity, and motivation, further emphasizing that knowledge represents only one component of behavioral change.26 In this context, the modest effect sizes observed in our study are consistent with the understanding that sexual health knowledge represents only one element within a broader set of determinants influencing sexual behavior.

On the other hand, the effect of knowledge on the intention to adopt protective behaviors during sexual activity remained significant even after adjusting for sociodemographic variables such as age, relationship status, school of enrollment of studies, family economic level, and employment status. This reinforces the hypothesis that sexual knowledge constitutes an independent predictor of intentional behavior in the sexual field, being a central component of the sexual and reproductive autonomy of university women.27,28

The positive association observed between age and sexual risk intention suggests that, even within a population of women without prior sexual experience, increasing age may be accompanied by greater openness or willingness to engage in risk-related sexual behaviors. This finding may reflect developmental and psychosocial transitions, including increased autonomy, exposure to diverse social environments, and changing attitudes toward sexuality.29 However, the absence of a corresponding association with protective intention indicates that greater age does not necessarily translate into a higher inclination toward protective behaviors. Previous research has shown that risk-taking and protective behaviors are influenced by distinct cognitive and social mechanisms, and may not evolve in parallel.30 Sexual risk among university women includes behaviors such as unprotected intercourse, inconsistent contraceptive use, and exposure to sexually transmitted infections. These behaviors are influenced not only by individual knowledge but also by psychosocial, cultural, and structural factors, including peer influence, gender dynamics, and access to reliable information.29 Greater sexual health knowledge may help reduce risk-related intentions by promoting informed decision-making and awareness of the consequences of unsafe sexual behaviors.22,23,30

Multivariate models also revealed significant associations between socioeconomic disadvantage and sexual behavioral intentions. Students from households with incomes below the minimum wage demonstrated higher protective intention and lower sexual risk intention. This pattern may reflect heightened risk awareness among economically disadvantaged students, for whom the potential consequences of unintended pregnancy or sexually transmitted infections may carry greater perceived impact. At the same time, employment status was also associated with protective intention, with students who were employed showing higher levels of protective intention compared to those who were not working. This may be related to greater autonomy, financial independence, or exposure to broader social environments that reinforce responsibility and health awareness. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as socioeconomic vulnerability is typically associated with structural barriers to accessing sexual health resources.31 These findings may also reflect unmeasured contextual influences, such as cultural norms, perceived future opportunities, or differences in access to information. Overall, these results highlight the complex and context-dependent role of socioeconomic factors in shaping sexual health intentions.

Although no significant association was observed between School of enrollment and sexual behavioral intentions in the adjusted models, this variable was included to capture potential differences in educational environments and exposure to health-related information. Students enrolled in Health Sciences programs may have greater opportunities to acquire knowledge related to human health, disease prevention, and reproductive health through their academic training, which could indirectly influence sexual health attitudes and intentions.28 Therefore, field of study was considered a contextual educational variable rather than a direct determinant of behavior. The absence of significant associations in the present study suggests that differences in sexual behavioral intentions may be influenced more strongly by individual and socioeconomic factors than by academic discipline alone, although further research is warranted to explore these relationships in greater depth.

These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) across the life course. While universities can reinforce knowledge, effective education should begin earlier in adolescence. Evidence shows that early, school-based sexuality education improves knowledge and promotes safer behaviors.32 Persistent knowledge gaps suggest limitations in preuniversity education, underscoring the need for higher education institutions to strengthen and complement prior learning through comprehensive, evidence-based programs.33,34 On the other hand, these findings should also be interpreted within a broader gender and structural context. In many settings, including Latin America, women’s sexual decision-making is shaped not only by individual knowledge but also by gender norms, socioeconomic inequalities, and power dynamics within relationships. These factors may limit women’s ability to access accurate information, negotiate contraceptive use, or adopt protective behaviors, even when adequate knowledge is present.35 The association observed between socioeconomic disadvantage and sexual risk intention in this study may reflect these structural constraints. Evidence from global health literature further highlights that gender inequality and social determinants of health significantly influence reproductive autonomy and access to sexual health services.36 Interventions should extend beyond knowledge provision to address gender inequalities and broader social determinants influencing women’s health behaviors.

Taking these results together, this study contributes novel evidence by focusing on university women with no prior sexual experience, an underrepresented group in the literature. The findings show that sexual health knowledge influences intentions before sexual debut, reducing risk and increasing protective behaviors. Additionally, the use of multivariate analysis demonstrates that knowledge is an independent and context-dependent predictor, highlighting the importance of early, comprehensive sexual education in higher education settings.

Clinical and research implications

An important contribution of this study is its focus on university women who had not yet initiated sexual activity, allowing the evaluation of behavioral intentions before the establishment of sexual practices. This perspective provides an opportunity to identify preventive factors at an earlier stage of sexual development, when educational interventions may have the greatest impact. The findings suggest that sexual health education programs in higher education should move beyond the provision of factual information and incorporate components aimed at strengthening risk perception, decision-making skills, self-efficacy, and reproductive autonomy. Universities could implement targeted interventions during the first years of study, including workshops on contraception, sexually transmitted infection prevention, and communication skills for negotiating protective behaviors.

From a public health perspective, these findings support the urgent need to strengthen and institutionalize comprehensive sex education in higher education setting. Effective programs must integrate not only biological and contraceptive information but also psychosocial, behavioral, and gender-related components that promote critical thinking, emotional intelligence and healthy decision-making. In addition, the association observed with socioeconomic factors indicate that interventions should be adapted to the specific needs of vulnerable student groups and complemented by equitable access to sexual and reproductive health services. Policies that ensure free, confidential, and youth-friendly reproductive health services on university campuses could help mitigate the impact of financial barriers, particularly among economically disadvantaged students. Such approaches may contribute to increasing protective intentions and reducing risk-related intentions before sexual debut, thereby promoting healthier sexual trajectories and greater reproductive autonomy throughout adulthood.

Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of targeted educational and behavioral interventions designed for university populations with different socioeconomic and academic backgrounds. Longitudinal studies are also needed to determine whether improvements in sexual health knowledge translate into sustained changes in behavioral intentions and subsequent sexual behaviors over time.

Strengths and limitations

This study adds to the limited literature on sexual health knowledge and behavioral intentions among university women in Latin America. The use of validated instruments and multivariate models strengthens their conclusions. However, several limitations must be recognized. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inference. The nonprobabilistic sample limits generalization beyond the study context. Self-reported data may introduce a social desirability bias, particularly on sensitive topics. Future research should consider longitudinal and mixed-method designs and evaluate the possible mediating role of perceived behavioral control, sexual self-efficacy, and access to reproductive health services. Additionally, given the voluntary nature of participation and the sensitive topic of sexual behavior, the study may be subject to self-selection bias. Cultural norms surrounding sexual abstinence may have influenced willingness to participate, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to other university populations or sociocultural contexts. Finally, access to digital information sources, including social media and the internet, was not assessed in this study. Given their growing role in shaping sexual health knowledge and attitudes, future research should incorporate these variables to better understand their influence on sexual behavior and decision-making among university students.

Conclusion

In conclusion, knowledge about sexual health plays a significant, albeit context-dependent, role in shaping sexual behavioral intentions among university women. While greater knowledge is associated with higher intentions to protect and lower intentions to take risks, this effect is moderated by socioeconomic and academic variables. Therefore, public health initiatives must address informational and structural determinants to effectively promote sexual and reproductive autonomy among young women in higher education.
