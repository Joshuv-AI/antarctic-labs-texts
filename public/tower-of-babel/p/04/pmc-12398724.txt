A reconsidered diagnosis in an unresolving myocarditis—a case report
Authors: Bradley Woolfenden, Christian Cawley, Deirdre Ward
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyocarditis typically presents with chest pain, a raised troponin and is associated with late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR). However, non-resolution of symptoms despite treatment should raise cause for concern that an alternative diagnosis may be present.Case summaryA 17-year-old gentleman with a background history of developmental delay, gastrointestinal motility issues, retractile testes, and patellar dislocation presented to our emergency department with chest pain. Investigations revealed static mildly elevated troponin T. An electrocardiogram and transthoracic echocardiogram were both unremarkable. CMR revealed sub-endocardial and mid-wall LGE. He was treated as myocarditis with steroids and colchicine, however symptoms persisted and repeat CMR reported progressive LGE. The diagnosis of myocarditis was reconsidered given an atypical presentation and taking into account his past medical history, and so a whole gene exome was performed. This revealed a dual diagnosis of Klinefelter’s Syndrome and a mutation in the dystrophin gene located on the X chromosome.DiscussionIn this case, an interplay between Klinefelter’s Syndrome and Muscular Dystrophy is demonstrated, potentially protecting our patient against a more aggressive Duchenne phenotype of muscular dystrophy. Upon non-resolution of symptoms, re-evaluating his presentation, investigations and taking into account his background multi-system medical history lead us to performing the genetic testing which revealed the correct diagnosis.

Introduction

Duchenne and Becker Muscular Dystrophies are X-linked conditions which occur due to mutations in the Dystrophin gene, and typically present with musculoskeletal, respiratory, and cardiac symptoms. Klinefelter’s syndrome is characterized by a chromosomal anomaly in which a male presents with two copies of an X chromosome, which can clinically manifest in reduced fertility, altered secondary sexual characteristics and neurocognitive impairment. This case report demonstrates an unusual interplay of these two uncommon genetic conditions.

Case presentation

A 17-year-old male patient presented to our local hospital’s Emergency Department in late 2023 originally with left upper chest pain and a syncopal event. The pain was positional and non-exertional, and was reproducible on palpation. It was intermittent with variable duration, usually lasting 10 min to 2 h, and occurring a few times a day. The syncopal event had a prodrome of generalized weakness and dizziness before ultimately losing consciousness. His past medical history included developmental delay with investigation under paediatric services unable to identify a cause, although elevated creatine kinase (CK) had been noted, and it was ultimately attributed to mild cerebral palsy. He also had recurrent issues with constipation and vomiting, bilateral retractile testes with orchidopexy, and patellar dislocation. His only medication was laxatives as needed. Physical examination was unremarkable aside from reproducible chest wall pain on palpation.

Laboratory investigations identified a troponin T of 32 ng/L (Ref <14 ng/L), with a flat trajectory. A full blood count, biochemical profile, CK, and C-reactive protein were all within normal limits. His electrocardiogram was unremarkable, in sinus rhythm with no ischaemic changes. A chest X-ray was unremarkable. Transthoracic echocardiogram showed normal left ventricular size and function, and no structural abnormality aside from a prominent coronary sinus. He was discharged home at this point for outpatient follow-up and completion of investigations.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) in early 2024 showed sub-endocardial and mid-wall late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) in the basal to mid-anterolateral, basal to mid-inferolateral, and apical lateral segments, felt to be in keeping with myocarditis (Figure 1). His left ventricular ejection fraction was measured at 50%. His prominent coronary sinus was confirmed as a persistent left sided superior vena cava.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging early 2024 showing sub-endocardial and mid-wall fibrosis (arrows).

The CMR prompted clinical review and updated blood investigations. These revealed a CK of 730 IU/L (ref <190 IU/L), and a stable troponin of 32 ng/L. Additional investigations included renal profile and full blood count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, complement profile, serum protein electrophoresis, an auto-antibody screen, and an extended metabolic panel, all of which were within normal range. Because the original chest pain continued intermittently, he was started on colchicine and a tapering dose of prednisolone, which his symptoms appeared to respond to. However, his taper had to be restarted on multiple occasions due to symptom relapse. His CK continued to fluctuate up to a maximum of 1984 IU/L. His clinical course was complicated by the evolution of sequelae of chronic steroid use, with physical and psychological changes. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was repeated later that year which was reported as showing progression of his LGE and basal and mid-inferolateral wall oedema (Figure 2).

(A) Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging late 2024 showing sub-endocardial and mid-wall fibrosis (arrows) and (B) cardiac magnetic resonance imaging late 2024 showing inferolateral wall oedema (arrows).

At this point, he was reviewed in our inherited cardiac conditions centre. A detailed family history was not strongly suggestive of a familial pattern. Considering his background medical history of developmental delay, as well as gastrointestinal motility issues and patellar dislocation, as well as the atypical presentation and lack of response to treatment, the diagnosis of recurrent myocarditis was revised, and a genetic multi-system condition was considered. A blood sample was sent off for whole-exome sequencing.

This revealed two significant findings. The first was a chromosomal anomaly of 47 XXY (Klinefelter syndrome), which could explain the developmental delay, patellar dislocation, and retractile testes. The other finding was of a heterozygous pathogenic variant in the dystrophin-encoding gene DMD, which would be associated with an X-linked inherited muscular dystrophy. The latter would explain his cardiac findings, including the blood markers, as well as some of his extra-cardiac symptoms such as gastrointestinal motility issues and could also be associated with patellar dislocation. We felt these genetic variants would explain his above presentation and past medical history. He clinically presents as someone with a diagnosis of Becker Muscular Dystrophy given his symptom onset in his late teens.

The initial management has centred on explaining this complex diagnosis to the patient and his family, and allowing them to come to terms with a likely explanation finally for most if not all of the medical issues experienced since birth. We are also arranging for clinical assessment in the patient’s mother, and genetic counselling to consider testing her for the dystrophin variant. Currently, we have not commenced him on medication, but introducing cardioprotective medications as per European Society of Cardiology cardiomyopathy guidelines will be a consideration in the future. Echo surveillance is ongoing on 6 monthly basis, with left ventricular ejection fraction currently maintained >50%. Rhythm monitoring via implanted loop recorder is ongoing and to date reassuring. Referral has been made to local endocrine services, and we have notified our Orthopaedic and Neurology Colleagues who have pending appointments with our patient. We have also updated Clinical Genetics on the results and await their expert opinion and assistance in disseminating information and arranging cascade screening.

Discussion

The co-occurrence of Klinefelter and a dystrophin variant has been described rarely in the literature. Female dystrophin variant carriers are found to manifest signs of the condition in cardiac and/or skeletal muscle in 2.5–7.8% of cases.1 This disease expression in female carriers has been shown to occur when there is skewed X chromosome inactivation (XCI—the unaffected X is inactivated in a disproportionate number of cells). In individuals with Klinefelter, XCI also occurs.2 In our patient perhaps random XCI, or even ‘preferential’ XCI has occurred, favouring the unaffected X chromosome and protecting against a more aggressive Duchenne phenotype.

The above case we would consider to be an example of Occam’s razor—a principle stated by the Scholastic philosopher William of Ockham (1285–1347) that pluralitas non est ponenda sine necessitate, ‘plurality should not be posited without necessity.’ The principle gives precedence to simplicity: of two competing theories, the simpler explanation of an entity is to be preferred. In this case, we considered that instead of an unrelated diagnosis of myocarditis being accepted (particularly made in the absence of histological support) in addition to previous complex multi-system presentations, that a unifying multi-system genetic condition could explain most if not all of his developmental and medical issues. In this case, the access to cost-effective whole-exome sequencing genetic testing, which was not as readily available when he was first investigated in the Paediatric sector, allowed rapid provision of a plausible if rare diagnosis. The implications of having a correct diagnosis with extensive work up and linking in with services can improve a patient’s quality of life with focused therapies or even with reassurance.

This case is an important example of complex genetic interaction which has seldom been described in the literature, first described in 1989.3 As described above, and in the previous reports, the second X chromosome, due to XCI, explains the milder or delayed onset presentation, as Duchenne’s and Becker muscular dystrophies are X-linked recessive conditions. The pattern of XCI, whether random or skewed, is likely to determine the severity of the X-linked condition.
