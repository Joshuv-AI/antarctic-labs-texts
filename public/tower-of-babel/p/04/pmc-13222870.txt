Formulation of mucoadhesive dissolving film containing cinnamaldehyde and chitosan incorporating papain enzyme for inhibit oral candidiasis: in vitro study
Authors: Andi Asti Akhriani, St. Fadila Mutmainnah, Nurul Musyrifah, Arfansyah Arfansyah, Sarah Fajriani, Nursyamsi Djamaluddin, Erni Marlina, Maretaningtias Dwi Ariani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective: To find a mucoadhesive dissolving film formula based on cinnamaldehyde (C) and shrimp skin chitosan (K) incorporating papain enzyme (P), in order to inhibit oral candidiasis caused by Candida albicans, in diabetes mellitus patients. Study design: Oral candidiasis in people with diabetes mellitus is an infection that often occurs due to Candida albicans. The proposed innovative treatment is a mucoadhesive dissolving film. The research stages consisted of extraction, formulation, and testing for antifungal capability, toxicity, the determination of characteristics, FTIR and SEM. The data was analyzed using probit regression, One Way Anova. Results: Cinnamaldehyde and shrimp skin chitosan incorporating papain enzyme was characterized as suitable for functional group testing. Mucoadhesive film F8 showed antifungal properties for Candida albicans, with an average inhibition zone of 25.3mm, organoleptic properties of good flexibility, yellow color, distinctive odor and smooth texture. Its dissolving time was 5 minutes, thickness 0.24mm, pH 7.39, tensile strength 7.482 kg/cm2, durability of 310 folds and absorption of 2.2203 its FTIR wave number, as well as SEM, show the functional groups of cinnamaldehyde and shrimp skin chitosan appear smooth, dense and flattened in texture without porosity. Conclusions: Mucoadhesive film F8 obtained the most optimum film characteristics to inhibit Candida albicans.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45154-4.

Introduction

Candida albicans (C. albicans) is a microorganism found in the oral cavity. It can cause oral candidiasis if there are risk factors for infection such as a weakened immune system due to metabolic disorders1.

Current oral candidiasis tablets and capsules with a hard shell and large size, cause difficulty in swallowing for as many as 26% of patients. Gel preparations become unstable in heat and light so that the level of the active compound is affected5,32. Oral suspensions can cause some patients to experience nausea, diarrhoea, and impaired liver metabolism. The solution to these problems is a mucoadhesive dissolving film which are water-soluble, elastic, and does not irritate the oral cavity. Mucoadhesive dissolving filmincludes active substances, polymers and plasticizers 9,28.

The active substances currently used are the antifungal groups of polyene (nystatin) and azole (fluconazole) which do not have a fungicidal effect and are toxic, causing side effects such as indigestion and rashes and are susceptible to drug resistance24,6. An alternative active substance is cinnamaldehyde, which is the largest component in essential cinnamon oil (Cinnamomum burmannii), and works as an antifungal in oral candidiasis. It has the potential to inhibit the enzyme secreted aspartic proteases (SAP) 4–6 in C. albicans which can prevent invasion of the oral epithelium. It is also a strong antifungal opposing Candida by affecting the synthesis of (1,3)-β-D-glucan which damages cell walls, destroys cell organelles and interferes with the inhibition of fungus6,17. However, cinnamaldehyde is difficult to dissolve in water, affecting its effectiveness as an active substance7,12. Cinnamaldehyde can bind to the side chain of the chitosan polymer (poly-β-1,4-glucosamine) which is obtained from chitin in shrimp skin waste (Litopenaeus vannamei) to reduce environmental pollution. Chitosan shrimp skin, with 93.47% deacetylation, has a primary amine function that can react to α unsaturated aldehyde group, β cinnamaldehyde to control release of cinnamaldehyde with bonds responsive to stimuli such as imine, hydrazine, acetal (pH responsive) and thioacetal (ROS-responsive)4,39.

However, chitosan can only dissolve in acidic conditions so it cannot be used in biomedical applications in the oral cavity that has a neutral pH. Therefore enzymatic depolymerization is required using papain enzymes to increase chitosan solubility. The papain enzyme is the most abundant compound in papaya latex (Carica papaya L.), which can break the glycosidic β (1,4) bond of chitosan thereby increasing the solubility of chitosan at a neutral, harmless and environmentally friendly pH23. This innovative research aimed to find a mucoadhesive dissolving film formulation that inhibits oral candidiasis in patients suffering from diabetes mellitus. It consists of a combination of cinnamaldehyde as the active substance and chitosan incorporated with papain enzyme as a film-forming polymer. This is expected to inhibit Candida albicans which causes in vitro oral candidiasis.

Statement of clinical relevance

Mucoadhesive dissolving film can be integrated quickly, has good bioavailability by passing through first pass metabolism and is easy to apply so it is an excellent choice to place in the oral cavity compared to other preparations (Balwant et al.,42).

Methods

Time and place

This laboratory experimental research, using the complete random design method (RAL) with three replications, was undertaken from May 2 – June 30, 2024 at seven laboratories at Hasanuddin University (UH), Indonesian Muslim University (UMI) and Ujung Pandang State Polytechnic (PNUP). It received an ethical permit from the Health Research Ethics Committee Number 0100/PL.09/KEPK FKG-RSGM UNHAS/2024. The research material has been identified using a determination test at the Pharmacognosy Laboratory, Faculty of Pharmacy and Fishes Biology, Faculty of Marine and Fisheries Sciences (FIKP), Hasanuddin University. The data for this study was analysed using the One Way ANOVA test.

Materials and tools

The tools used were Aldrich® Buchner funnels, Aldrich® chemistry glassware, Aldrich® high-efficiency Liebig reflux condenser, Chopper Miyako CH-208, Faithful WHL-25AB, ICO105 Memmert, IKA® C-MAG HS hotplate stirrers, LAQUA-PH2000, Rotavapor®, (Shimadzu, IRAffinity-1 Kyoto, Japan), (Shimadzu, UV-1280, Japan), Taffware Digital Vernier Caliper, Ultra-Turrax, WANT FA2204G.

The ingredients used were 70% alcohol (OneMed), aluminum foil (Godrej Indonesia), aquadest (OneMed), acetone (Supelco), cinnamon sticks, papaya fruit, C. albicans, CH3COOH (Sigma Aldrich®), 96% ethanol (OneMed), ether (Supelco), iodine salt (Calibre Chemicals PVT.LTD), HCl (Supelco), shrimp shell, formaldehyde (Cv Cakra Bismaka Maheswara), 0.9% NaCl solution (PT Suryamakmur Agunglestari), methylene blue (CV. Extraction and Isolation of cinnamaldehyde from essential cinnamon oil

Using the steam method19, distillates were washed with DCM solvent until separated into 2 layers: water and organic phases. The latter is rinsed with NaCl and dried with anhydrous Na2SO4. The solvent was then evaporated using a rotary evaporator (Rotavapor®) until cinnamaldehyde was obtained. This extraction and isolation of cinnamaldehyde was undertaken at the UH Pharmaceutical Phytochemistry Laboratory11.

Chitosan extraction from shrimp skin

Through four stages, namely deproteinization, demineralization, depigmentation and deacetylation of chitin to produce chitosan. Deproteinization: shrimp skin powder is mixed with a 3.5% NaOH solution, ratio of 1:10 (g/mL), refluxed at 65° for 2 h, then the residue was washed until neutral and dried. This was followed by demineralization using HCl 1 N in 1:10 (g/mL) ratio for 1 h at room temperature. The residue was neutralized and dried, producing chitin, which was then depigmented in a solution of acetone for 1 h and 0.5% NaClO for 1 h at room temperature, then neutralized and dried. It was then deacetylated using a 1:20 solution of 50% NaOH refluxed at 100\ °C for 1 h, filtered, neutralized and dried until chitosan was obtained35. The process was undertaken at the Pharmaceutical Phytochemistry Laboratory at UH.

Cinnamaldehyde and Chitosan functional group test

This test was carried out by looking at the functional groups of Cinnamaldehyde and Chitosan using Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) (Shimadzu, IRAffinity-1 Kyoto, Japan) in the Integrated Laboratory of Chemistry of FMIPA, UH18.

Isolation of papain enzymes

Tapped papaya sap was mixed in ratio of 1:4 with 0.7% sodium bisulfate solution and stirred till thick, resulting in papain isolate. This process was undertaken at the Chemical Analysis and Food Quality Control Laboratory, Faculty of Agriculture, UH36.

Papain enzyme activity test

Papain enzyme activity was tested using the Walter method is based on papain’s ability to hydrolyze the casein substrate into peptides and the amino acid tyrosine. This tyrosine formation is the basis for calculating papain activity36. This test used UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, UV-1280, Japan) at the Chemical Laboratory, Food Quality Analysis and Monitoring, Faculty of Agriculture, UH33,36.

Chitosan depolymerization

Depolymerization of chitosan began with dissolving 2 g of chitosan in 100mL of 1% CH3COOH, which was then stirred with a magnetic stirrer (IKA® C-MAG HS hotplate stirrers) at 50 °C for 1 h. The solution was then enzymatically hydrolysed using 15 g/L papain enzyme in an incubator CO2 (ICO105 Memmert) for 16 h, at 40 °C and pH 5. The reaction was stopped in boiling water for 10 min. The sample was then stabilized for 10 min, neutralized using isopropyl alcohol (IPA 99%) with a ratio of 1:2, filtered, dried at 18 °C for 4–5 h and its yield calculated23. This procedure was carried out at the Pharmaceutical Phytochemistry Laboratory, UH.

Making mucoadhesive dissolving film formula

The formulation was conducted using the solvent casting method by mixing of chitosan with 20 mL of 5% acetic acid which was then homogenized using Turrax (Ultra-Turrax) until it thickened. Then PVP, PEG 400, cinnamaldehyde were added with a formula made in eight variations:

These formula were then printed on petri dishes or molds (Aldrich® chemistry glassware) and dried at 37 °C in an incubator at the Pharmaceutical Pharmacy Laboratory, UH.

In vitro antifungal activity test

The direct sensitivity disc diffusion test was used. The medium used were three petri dishes of Sabourand Dextrose Agar (SDA), each dish contained three films of cinnamaldehyde concentration, namely 1%, 2% or 3%. The sterilized cotton was dipped in pepton water containing Candida albicans suspension and then scratched evenly over the surface of SDA and left to rest for 5 min. The film consisting of several concentrations was then placed on the surface of the petri dish and pressed lightly to better adhere. After that the SDA medium that already contained the film were incubated at a temperature of 37 °C for 24 h. Then the diameter of the barrier zone, i.e. the clear zone around the film, was measured using calipers at the Pharmaceutical Laboratory of the Faculty of Pharmacy, UH2.

Toxicity test

This was performed using the Brine Shrimp Lethality Test (BSLT), namely Artemia salina Leach. Ten shrimp eggs were placed in each vial bottle, then formula solved with 5 different concentrations (1000, 500, 100, 50, 10 ppm) were added and left for 24 h under lights. After that time, the number of dead shrimp was noted15. This activity was undertaken at the Pharmaceutical Laboratory, Faculty of Pharmacy, UH.

Organoleptic test

Each formulation i.e. F1 – F8, were observed for color, odor, texture and malleability of the mucoadhesive film preparations at the Pharmaceutical Laboratory, Faculty of Pharmacy, UH5.

Dissolve time test

A total of 3 samples of F4-F8 formula film measuring 1 × 1 cm were placed in petri dishes containing up to 10 mL of artificial saliva with a pH of 6.8. The process and time for the films to dissolve was noted5. This test was conducted at the Pharmaceutical Laboratory, Faculty of Pharmacy, UH.

Film thickness test

This was carried out using a digital micrometer (Taffware Digital Vernier Caliper) to measure and record the F4-F8 film thicknesses22. This test was conducted at the Pharmacy Laboratory, Faculty of Pharmacy, UH.

pH preparation test

1 × 1 cm F4-F8 films were soaked in petri dishes containing 5 mL of distilled water and allowed to swell for 30 min. The pH’s were then tested using a pH meter (LAQUA-PH2000)22. This test was conducted at the Pharmacy Laboratory, Faculty of Pharmacy, UH.

Film absorption test

This was carried out using methylene blue solution. The films were shaken for 180 min at a speed of 100 rpm. The absorption of each part at max λ was measured using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, UV-1280, Japan) at a maximum wavelength of 665 nm at the Laboratory of Chemistry Analysis and Food Quality Control, Faculty of Agriculture, UH8.

Tensile strength test

The film samples with dimensions of 22 were placed between two clamps positioned 3 cm apart. Tension was applied until the sample tore apart and the maximal tension noted5. This test was undertaken at the FT PNUP Mechanical Laboratory.

Folding durability test

This test was done by folding the film through 180°, consistently at the same point, until it parted. The number of folds was recorded in the Pharmaceutical Laboratory, Faculty of Pharmacy, UH5.

FTIR preparation test

This test was carried out by measuring the functional groups of cinnamaldehyde and chitosan contained in the films using Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) analysis (Shimadzu, IRAffinity-1, Kyoto, Japan) at the Analytical Chemistry Laboratory, Faculty of Agriculture, UH22.

SEM test

This was done by studying the morphology of the film surface using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Nova NanoSEM 450, USA) at the FT Microstructure Laboratory, UMI22.

Data analysis

The data from this study was analysed using the One Way ANOVA test.

Discussion

Chitin compounds have wave numbers 0f 3487.30 (-OH), 2881.65 (-CH), 1662.64 (C = 0), 894.97 (CH deformation). Chitosan FTIR has wave numbers of 1660.71 (C = 0), 1024.20 (C-0), 887.26 (CH deformation). The (-OH), (-NH), (C = 0) and (C-O) groups are the main functional groups that make up chitin and chitosan4. From the FTIR data, differences can also be found in chitin and chitosan such as the degree of distillation. The degree of acetylation in chitin and chitosan is one of the important physiochemical characteristics that greatly influences the physical, chemical and biological properties of chitin and chitosan21. Cinnamon bark contains many chemical compounds such as eugenol essential oil and safrole, as well as cinnamaldehyde, tannins and calcium oxalate30. The results of the cinnamaldehyde extract test in Fig. 1b. Show wave numbers of 3059.10 (-CH), 2951.09 deformation (-CH2), 1678.07 (C = O), 1448.54 stretch (aromatic C-C) and 746.45 (aromatic mono-substitution). The results showed the main functional group of cinnamaldehyde26.

In papaya fruit there is sap that contains protein/proteolytic breakdown enzymes, one of which is the papain enzyme. Besides that, the papain enzyme can be a depolymerization agent of a polymer10. In the enzyme activity test, it was found that the activity of the papain enzyme isolate was better than the activity of manufactured papain enzyme. The higher the activity of papain, the better it is for protein hydrolysis36. In chitosan depolymerization, this enzyme breaks down chitosan molecules to release monomer or dimers gradually until they become chitosan that can be dissolved in neutral pH23.

Antifungal testing using the fungus Candida albicans, which is seen from the inhibition data, determines the magnitude of the release of active substances by measuring the diameter of the fungal growth inhibition zone31. The results of the anti-growth resistance test of C albicans fungus were characterized by the formation of clear zones ≥ 20 mm (very strong), 11–20 mm (strong), 5–10 mm (medium) and ≤ 4 mm (weak)2. Cinnamaldehyde 3% has the largest inhibition zone. This proves that the higher the concentration of active substances, the wider the inhibition zone38.

The Brine Shrimp Lethality test (BSLT) is a simple preliminary/pre-screening test of biological activity to precisely determine the toxicity of a compound, or extract, using shrimp larvae (Artemia salina nauplii). The parameter showing the presence of biological activity in a compound with Artemia salina Leach is the number of shrimp larval deaths due to the effect of administering compounds with predetermined doses16 and can be seen in Fig. 5. Which is based on LC50 values. Values above 1000 ppm are categorized as non-toxic15.

Organoleptic tests use the five human senses and the person who performs the test is called ‘the panelist’. Organoleptic examination aims to determine the preparations’ physical qualities including shape, color, smell and flexibility27. Based on Fig. 6, the characteristics of F4-F8 are superior to those of F1-F3, having good flexibility, yellow color, distinctive smell and fine texture. Thus compounds F4-F8 were chosen for follow-up tests.

The dissolution time test determines the time it takes for the preparations to dissolve in saliva.5 Based on the data presented in Fig. 7, preparations F4-F8 meet the dissolution requirement range of 4.25–5 minutes3. This is due to the hydrophilic polymers used, which very quickly hydrate and have a fast dissolution time40. In addition, orally dissolving film mucoadhesive based optimally has a relative humidity (RH) at 25 °C with 50% concentration of cinnamaldehyde for storage, it would be affect to physical stability and integrity of the film41.

The results of the thickness test seen in Fig. 8. Show that F4-F8 were within the desired range of 0.2–0.24 mm, obtaining a film thickness of < 0.25 mm.25 Thickness is a physical property that functions as a water vapor barrier from the product to the environment and vice versa20. The thickness of the edible film affects the rate of water vapor, percent elongation, permeability of gases and other volatile compounds. The thicker the edible film, the greater reduction of gas permeability which means that the ability to resist gas migration as stronger because of its tighter structure14.

The pH test results in Fig. 9. Show that F4 – F8 were within the required range of 7.2–7.3 and had had approached the normal pH of the oral cavity which is from 6.2 to 7.45.

Figure 10. Shows the results of the film adsorption test which used methylene blue to note the adsorption capacity of the film which shows its hydrophilic properties with water. The greater this capacity the greater the chance that particles from the adsorbent come into contact with the adsorbate8. Based on the results of the adsorption test, F6, F7 and F8 are the most optimum formulations with the highest adsorption capacity.

The tensile strength test results in Fig. 11. Show that the F8 formula has the greatest tensile strength of 7.482 Mpa compared with F6 and F7 which achieved 3.433 Mpa. All these meet the minimum requirement of 0.39226 Mpa. This result is influenced by the plasticizer used, which can reduce the internal hydrogen bond by increasing the free space between molecules, thus lowering the rigidity and improving the flexibility of the film13.

The fold durability test aims to determine the level of flexibility of the film5. The folding resistance can be said to be good if the preparation is folded many times without tearing, so that it is not easily damaged during storage or when it is used37. Fig. 12. Shows that F8 has the highest folding durability of 310 times and has met the requirement for excellent flexibility of 300 folds per film5.

FTIR testing on film preparations is carried out to observe the presence of its constituent composition after it is in film form and can also be used to observe the interaction between active substances and other additives. The test results can be seen in Fig. 13. Wave numbers achieved were: 3402.71 (-OH); 2926.56 (= C-H); 2871.26 (stretch C-H); 1633.85 (C = O); 1552.63 (N-H); 1451.43 (aromatic C-H); 1376.02 (C-H); 1303.49 (C-N); 1067.21 (C-O-C); 687.29 (C-H deformation). These results show the existence of the main functional groups of the film components, i.e. chitosan and cinnamaldehyde. The greater concentration of chitosan added to the edible film, the stronger the constituents of that film34. The percentage of purity of cinnamaldehyde obtained was 99% and chitosan obtained was 90%.

Figure 14. Shows the results of the SEM (Scanning Electron Microscope) test. F8 is the most optimum preparation which appears smooth, dense, of even texture and without pores or cracks. The morphology and topography of F8 met the requirements to optimally control hydration and drug molecule release22. Good films have little or no cavities and a tight structure so that the air transfer process does not damage the film29.

Conclusions

This research succeeded in discovering mucoadhesive dissolving film formulations of cinnamaldehyde and chitosan shrimp skin incorporating papain enzyme. The analysis of formulation characteristics, plus the mechanical and antifungal properties of each formulation showed that F8 (C 3% + K 4% + PEG 0.4%) was the most suitable. Its characteristics are: antifungal inhibition zone of 25.3 mm, good flexibility, yellow color, a distinctive odor, smooth texture, 5 min dissolution time, 0.24 mm thickness, pH 7.39, 7.482 Mpa tensile strength, folding resistance of 310 folds, absorption of 2.2203 and the FTIR wavenumber indicating functional groups of cinnamaldehyde and shrimp skin chitosan, with SEM that appears smooth, dense and even texture without pores or cracks. For further research, it is recommended to conduct further tests in the form of clinical trials of mucoadhesive film preparations that can be applied to the human oral mucosa.
