Efficacy of a Commercial Mycotoxin Adsorbent Against Low‐Level Aflatoxin B 1 and Fumonisin B 1 Exposure in Broiler Chickens: In Vitro and In Vivo Assays
Authors: M. J. Luna, V. Poloni, M. V. Coniglio, A. Vallejos, M. C. Isgro, F. Escobar, M. E. Ortiz, L. R. Cavaglieri, J. Parada, A. P. Magnoli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mycotoxins cause significant economic losses in poultry. This study evaluates the ability of a commercial mycotoxin adsorbent to bind toxins in vitro and its effects at different concentrations on broiler chickens exposed to low levels of aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) and fumonisin B1 (FB1). In vitro study: standard solutions of AFB1 and FB1 were prepared in buffer solutions at pH 2 and 5. Two concentrations were used: AFB1 (20 and 50 μg/kg) and FB1 (5 and 20 mg/kg). An artificial intestinal fluid was used; pH was adjusted to 5 to simulate the intestinal environment. The adsorbent (1%) was incubated with 1 mL of the artificial intestinal fluid containing each toxin. After being centrifuged, they were analyzed by HPLC. In vivo study with the following four treatments (35 broiler chickens each) was included: G1 negative group: basal diet (BD) without mycotoxins or any adsorbent; G2 positive group: BD + AFB1 (37.88 μg/kg) + FB1 (7.3 mg/kg); G3 adsorbent group A1: BD + AFB1 (54.85 μg/kg) + FB1 (8.1 mg/kg) + 1 kg/t adsorbent; and G4 adsorbent group A2: BD + AFB1 (40.9 μg/kg) + FB1 (8.1 mg/kg) + 2 kg/t adsorbent. Over 42 days, productive parameters, carcass yield, intestinal histomorphometry, liver histopathology, liver fat content, volatile fatty acid concentration (VFA), and genotoxicity were evaluated. In vitro, the mycotoxin adsorbent shows a higher percentage of adsorption for AFB1, followed by FB1. In vivo, there were no significant differences in productive parameters overall (p ≥ 0.001), but G3 showed improved growth and feed conversion compared to G2. G2 exhibited liver damage, increased liver fat, and reduced intestinal villus length and crypt depth, which was moderated in G3/G4. G3 showed elevated VFA levels. Mycotoxins induced DNA damage, whereas A1/A2 provided protective effects. According to our results, we observed that the commercial adsorbent effectively adsorbs mycotoxins in vitro and improves the parameters evaluated in the chronic co‐occurrence of AFB1 and FB1, depending on the dose used.

1. Introduction

Broiler chicken feed is made mainly with cereal grains, which are often contaminated with multiple mycotoxins [1]. Numerous fungal species, including Fusarium spp., Aspergillus spp., Alternaria ssp., Penicillium spp., and Claviceps spp., are well‐known producers of mycotoxins in various grain crops [2]. Around 400 mycotoxins have been discovered, but only 30 are known to be harmful to humans and animals [1]. The most important mycotoxins in naturally contaminated food and animal feed are the aflatoxins (AFs), ochratoxin A (OTA), zearalenone (ZEA), T‐2 toxin (T2), deoxynivalenol (DON), and fumonisins (FBs) [1]. AFs are a type of toxin produced by several species of Aspergillus fungi that affect crops such as corn. Aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) is a major concern to human health because of its significant toxicity, therefore considered a Group 1 human carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) [3], and poultry products were suggested as a source of contamination for humans [4]. FBs are naturally produced by several species of Fusarium fungi, where fumonisin B1 (FB1), considered as the most prevalent, is classified by IARC as a potential carcinogen (Class 2B) [5]. According to the Codex Alimentarius Commission and the European Union regulations, the maximum permitted level of AFs in chicken products is 20 μg/kg [4, 6]. According to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the maximum permissible levels for FB1 in the finished feed for broiler chickens are 50 mg/kg [7]. Additionally, according to the European Union, the maximum permissible levels for FBs in poultry feed are 20 mg/kg [8]. In a previous study from Latin America, the prevalence of mycotoxins in finished feed for poultry in 2024 was 46% for AFs and 81% for FBs, reporting levels as high as 1,160 μg AFs/kg and 11,919 μg FB/kg [9]. Chronic toxicity is the most common form of mycotoxicosis, and it is caused by the consumption of relatively small amounts of these toxic compounds over an extended period [10]. The co‐occurrence of multiple mycotoxins in feed can exacerbate their harmful effects due to synergistic interactions, causing more severe damage than a single mycotoxin alone [11].

One promising strategy to counteract mycotoxins’ effects in poultry is using adsorbents in the diets to decrease their absorption and bioavailability [12]. The most commonly used binders include zeolite, hydrated sodium calcium aluminosilicate (HSCAS), bentonite, montmorillonite, and activated carbon. Another control approach is the use of antimycotoxin additives (AMAs), defined as products that, when added to animal feed, are capable of adsorbing, inactivating, or neutralizing mycotoxins in the gastrointestinal tract, for example, yeast cell wall (YCW) preparations [12]. Xu et al. [13] proposed that the YCW is the active component that interacts with the mycotoxin molecule. Weaver et al. [14] suggested that the addition of dietary glucomannans is effective in broiler chickens to counteract the in vivo toxic effects of feed naturally contaminated with mycotoxins.

These types of additives, which are mainly derivatives of the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae, have esterified glucomannans that can efficiently adsorb several mycotoxins [6, 10]. The glucan portion of the YCW additives can also stimulate the immune system and contribute to the integrity of the intestinal mucosa as prebiotics, which improve animal health [15]. A prebiotic is a selectively fermented ingredient that results in specific changes in the composition and/or activity of the gastrointestinal microbiota, thus conferring benefit(s) upon host health [16].

Therefore, this study evaluates the ability of a commercial mycotoxin adsorbent to bind toxins in vitro and its effects at different concentrations on broiler chickens exposed to low levels of AFB1 and FB1.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Mycotoxin Adsorbent

The commercial mycotoxin adsorbent used in this study was formulated with 80% sodium calcium aluminosilicate, 15% YCW hydrolysate (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), 0.6% artichoke, and 2.5% choline chloride. It was administered in powder form, with dosages of 1 and 2 kg/t (A1 and A2, respectively), at the manufacturer’s recommended dose (Bedson S.A., Argentina).

2.2. In Vitro Study

Standard solutions of each toxin were used, resuspended in buffer solutions at pH 2 and 5, simulating gastrointestinal conditions, at two concentrations: AFB1 (20 and 50 μg/kg) and FB1 (5 and 20 mg/kg). Artificial intestinal fluid (trypsin and chymotrypsin 1 mg·mL−1 and ox bile at 0.3% w/v) was used to perform the adsorption assay, adjusting the pH to 5 to simulate the gastrointestinal tract. The adsorbent (1%) was incubated for 1 h at 39°C in a shaking bath with 1 mL of artificial intestinal fluid containing each of the mycotoxins. The solution was centrifuged for 10 min at 5000 rpm, and the supernatant containing each of the unbound mycotoxins was collected and stored at −20°C for high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis according to Kumar et al. [17] and Smaoui et al. [18]. Positive (artificial intestinal fluid plus mycotoxin) and negative (artificial intestinal fluid plus adsorbent) controls were included for all experiments. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

2.3. In Vivo Study

The working protocol and the techniques used comply with the regulations of the Animal Bioethics Subcommittee under the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of the National University of Río Cuarto, Córdoba, Argentina (383/22).

2.3.1. AFB1 and FB1 Production, Extraction, and Determination

AFB1 production and extraction were realized according to the methodology proposed by Poloni et al. [19]. The AFB1 content of the resulting powder was quantified according to Kumar et al. [17] by an HPLC with a fluorescence detector. The AFB1 concentrate was used to contaminate the feed. The milled contaminated substrate was added to the basal diet (BD) premix to provide a concentration of 20–30 μg AFB1/kg feed. FB1 was produced by the Fusarium verticillioides (MRC 826) strain on the corn substrate according to the method of Kövesi et al. [20]. The FB1 extraction was performed according to the protocol of Motta et al. [21], and the FB1 content of the resulting powder was quantified according to Smaoui et al. [18]. The FB1 concentrate was used to contaminate the feed. The milled contaminated substrate was added to the BD premix to provide a concentration of about 2–4 mg FB1/kg feed.

2.3.2. Formulation of Feed Contaminated With AFs and FBs

Contaminated feed of broiler chickens was prepared by mixing the BD with the AF–FB concentrate (described above). Samples (1 kg) of the starter and finisher feeds were collected before and after the incorporation of the milled contaminated substrate and homogenized and quartered to obtain a laboratory sample, according to the European Commission recommendations [22] and its modification by Regulation 519/2014 [23]. The levels of AFB1 and FB1 in the starter and finisher feeds are shown in Table 1. The feed samples were stored at ambient room temperature in a dark and cool storage space until they were required for use. The concentrations of AFB1 and FB1 within the feeds were quantified using HPLC [17, 18].

2.3.3. Study Period and Location

The study was conducted from August to October 2023. This experiment was carried out in the experimental unit of Animal Nutrition of the Faculty of Agronomy and Veterinary Medicine of the National University of Río Cuarto, Argentina. The rainy season lasts approximately 10 months, with a sliding 31‐day rainfall of at least 13 mm and an average total accumulation of 131 mm. The period of the year without rain lasts approximately 2 months, with an average total accumulation of 8 mm. The temperate season has an average daily maximum temperature of 27°C and an average minimum temperature of 19°C. The cool season has an average daily maximum temperature of < 18°C and an average minimum temperature of 5°C.

2.3.4. Animals and Housing

One hundred and forty‐one‐day‐old male Cobb 500 broiler chickens, with an initial body weight of 0.0472 ± 0.020 kg and vaccinated against Marek’s disease, were purchased from a commercial hatchery located in the province of Córdoba, Argentina (INDACOR SA, Córdoba, Argentina). Feed and water were available ad libitum throughout the experiment. Animal management followed the methodology described by Magnoli et al. [24]. The birds were acclimated for 1 week before the experiment. After this period, at 8 days of age, the broiler chickens were individually weighed and then redistributed into seven replicates per group, with five chickens housed per cage (90 × 100 × 39 cm). Throughout the 42‐day experimental period, from 7 to 49 days of age, the chickens were fed a diet corresponding to their respective group in crushed form. A standard maize–soybean meal starter commercial diet (BD) was fed to meet the requirements of the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM) guidelines [25], and these requirements formed the basis for the formulation of experimental diets (Table 2). Diets were prepared by mixing AFB1 and FB1 and/or absorbent 1 kg/t (kg per metric ton of feed) (A1) and 2 kg/t (A2) using a 130‐L food mixing machine equipped with a WEG electric motor (WEG, Argentina). The dietary treatments involved the following groups (G): G1 negative group: received the BD without mycotoxins or adsorbent; G2 positive group: was given the BD contaminated with AFB1 (37.88 μg/kg) + FB1 (7.3 mg/kg) without adsorbent; G3 adsorbent group A1: received the AFB1 (54.85 μg/kg) + FB1 (8.1 mg/kg) contaminated diet supplemented with 1 kg/t adsorbent; and G4 adsorbent group A2: received the AFB1 (40.9 μg/kg) + FB1 (8.1 mg/kg) contaminated diet supplemented with 2 kg/t adsorbent.

2.3.5. Evaluated Parameters

2.3.5.1. Productive Parameters

The broiler chickens were weighed individually every week until the end of the study. Daily monitoring for clinical signs and the mortality record was also conducted. Total weight gain (TWG‐kg) was measured per pen as the difference between the final and initial weights. Total feed intake (TFI‐kg) was recorded for each animal; the feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated as the ratio between TFI and TWG at 42 days of assay, 49 days of age.

2.3.5.2. Pathological Examination, Histopathology, and Histomorphology

At 49 days of age, 15 broiler chickens per group were randomly selected and euthanized by white bloodletting. A necropsy was performed to assess macroscopic changes in the liver. In addition, liver and small intestine (duodenum) samples were collected to evaluate microscopic alterations. For histological analysis, fifteen liver and duodenal samples (approximately 6 mm2, taken from the midpoint of the duodenum between the gizzard and the entry of the pancreatic and bile ducts) were collected per group and immediately fixed in buffered formalin (10%), following the methodology described by Poloni et al. [26]. Liver sections were examined for characteristic signs of intoxication and hepatocellular degeneration according to Fochesato et al. [27]. Morphometric measurements of the duodenum (villus length and width, and crypt depth) were estimated as described by Luna et al. [28]. The apparent absorptive surface area of the duodenal villi was calculated following the methodology proposed by Magnoli et al. [29]. Additionally, the weights of key carcass cuts (leg–thigh and breast), as well as the relative liver weight ((liver weight/body weight) × 100), were recorded to assess potential effects on carcass traits.

2.3.5.3. Analysis of Cecal Volatile Fatty Acids and Hepatic Fat Content

Samples of the cecum content were collected in tubes (15 ceca/group) and frozen at −20°C until the concentration of volatile fatty acids (VFAs) was determined following the methodology of Park et al. [30] with some modifications [28]. The supernatant was analyzed by gas chromatography to determine the content of acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid according to Parada et al. [31]. The quantification of the fat content in the liver samples (15 livers/group) was determined using the Soxhlet extraction technique [32].

2.3.5.4. Genotoxicity Assay

The genotoxic effect of adsorbent, AFB1, and FB1 on broiler chicken erythrocytes was evaluated by the bone marrow micronucleus assay and the alkaline comet assay [29]. For the micronucleus assay, femurs were removed (seven broiler chickens/group), and the bone marrow was flushed with fetal bovine serum according to the methodology described by Magnoli et al. [24]. Slides (in duplicate) were prepared from each animal, and a coded nomenclature was applied. To determine the genotoxic capacity, the number of micronuclei (MN) in 1,000 erythrocytes per broiler was assessed. The alkaline comet assay was performed as described by Ballatore et al. [33], with some modifications. Broiler blood (seven broiler chickens/group), 15 μL, was mixed with 85 μL of 0.6% low‐melting agarose, and the suspension was pipetted onto precoated slides containing 1.0% normal‐melting agarose. Observations were conducted using an epifluorescent microscope (Olympus BX50F4, Japan). The results of the comet assay, expressed as the comet area data of the cells, were determined using Comet Score software.

2.4. Statistical Analysis

The collected data were analyzed using a general linear mixed model (GLMM) with version 2.03 of the statistical software InfoStat, 2020 (Córdoba, Argentina). The ANOVA was performed to determine the significance of differences between the treatment groups. Productive parameters, including TWG, TFI, and FCR, as well as histomorphological measurements, VFAs, liver fat content, and genotoxicity, were treated as dependent variables, while groups (G2 vs. G3 and G4) were considered fixed factors. Different significance thresholds were applied according to the biological characteristics and variability of the evaluated parameters. A more stringent significance level (p < 0.001) was adopted for productive parameters, breast and leg–thigh weight, and relative liver weight, given their lower intrinsic variability and their relevance as key performance indicators in broiler production. For cecal VFA concentrations, a significance level of p < 0.05 was used, in accordance with previous studies and considering the moderate biological variability associated with intestinal fermentation processes. For the liver fat content, a threshold of p < 0.10 was applied with an exploratory purpose, acknowledging the higher interindividual variability typically observed in chronic low‐level mycotoxin exposure.

Mean comparisons were made using Fisher’s protected least significant difference (LSD) test. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was verified using Levene’s test. Non‐normally distributed data were log‐transformed prior to analysis. Results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Where applicable, statistically significant differences among groups were indicated using different superscript letters (e.g., a, b).

3.2.2. Macroscopic and Microscopic Analysis and Fat Content in the Liver

No significant differences were found in the relative weights of the livers in the different groups (p ≥ 0.001) (Table 5). However, G2 showed higher relative weight values of the livers compared to the other groups. Macroscopically, the livers of G1, G3, and G4 presented a reddish‐brown color considered normal, while G2 showed a yellowish‐brown color indicative of possible damage caused by mycotoxins (Figure 1). Microscopic changes are shown in Figure 2. The livers of G1 did not show significant histological changes (Figure 2(a)). In the livers of broiler chickens in Group 2, moderate multifocal and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates, diffuse microvacuolar fatty degeneration, and mild bile duct hyperplasia were observed (Figure 2(b)). On the other hand, the livers of broiler chickens of G3 and G4 did not show significant histopathological changes between them, exhibiting a similar appearance to G1 (Figures 2(c) and 2(d)). Significant differences in the liver fat content were observed among the groups (p ≤ 0.05). Broilers exposed only to mycotoxins (G2) showed the highest hepatic fat accumulation. In contrast, the inclusion of the adsorbent in the diet, at both tested doses (G3 and G4), was associated with a reduction in the liver fat content. These results are detailed in Table 5.

3.2.4. VFAs of Cecal Content

Statistically significant differences were observed in the concentrations of acetic, propionic, and butyric acids among the dietary groups (p ≤ 0.05). Broilers receiving the combination of mycotoxins and the adsorbent in both doses (G3 and G4) exhibited the highest cecal VFA concentrations, significantly exceeding those of G1 and G2 (p ≤ 0.05). 3.2.5. Genotoxicity Assay

The exposure to mycotoxins resulted in a significant increase in the frequency of micronucleated erythrocytes in G2 and G3 compared to the control. This could be because the amount of adsorbent was insufficient to mitigate the genotoxic effect of the toxins (Figure 3). In contrast, the addition of the adsorbent in G4 (2 kg/t) mitigated this effect, showing values comparable to the control group. A similar pattern was observed in the comet assay, where DNA damage (evidenced by an increased comet area) was significantly greater in G2 and G3, while G4 did not differ significantly from the control (Figure 4).

4. Discussion

The presence of mycotoxins in feed for broiler chickens poses a significant concern for the poultry industry as these toxins have been shown to adversely affect the health and productivity of broiler chickens and lead to the toxin carryover in the food chain [34, 35]. Due to the above, in this study, the authors proposed to assess the effectiveness and the adsorption capacity of one commercial mycotoxin adsorbent formulated with a sodium calcium aluminosilicate and YCW hydrolysate, in broiler chickens fed with low levels of AFB1 and FB1, which commonly co‐occur in complete feeds and feed ingredients such as maize. In vitro mycotoxin adsorption assays serve as an efficient method for rapidly screening and identifying agents with potential for mycotoxin sequestration [10]. The present in vitro study demonstrated that the commercial mycotoxin adsorbent, formulated with a combination of aluminosilicate and YCW hydrolysate, exhibited a good adsorption capacity for AFB1 and FB1. Our results agree with different authors, who demonstrated a high adsorption capacity of AFB1 by the HSCAS, associating this effect with the YCW, the mannans, and β‐glucan [13, 16, 36].

In the in vivo study, both doses of the commercial adsorbent of mycotoxins managed to maintain the productive variables, showing improved performance with the addition of the adsorbent at the lower dose of 1 kg/t (G3). These findings are consistent with a previous study reporting an improvement in FCR with the addition of 0.5% HSCA and 4% discarded date stones (DDPs) as AF adsorbents, compared to the broiler group fed 1000 ppb AFB1 [37]. Similarly, Azghadi et al. [38] demonstrated that the addition of a toxin binder formulated with yeast wall 25%, activated carbon, calcium propionate, aluminosilicate, vitamin E, organic selenium, antioxidant, and Silybum marianum seed extract (silymarin) to an AFB1 0.75 mg/kg infected diet did not affect the average daily weight, but the conversion rate improved.

The weights of the breast and the leg–thigh did not show differences with the addition of the adsorbent. However, Yue et al. [39] demonstrated a marked decrease in the breast weight in broiler chickens fed with 50 μg/kg body weight of AFB1, which was reversed by the addition of 125 mg/kg of total flavonoids of Rhizoma Drynaria (TFRD). In this study, the relative weights of the livers showed no differences between the experimental groups. These results disagree with those of Srinual et al. [40], who demonstrated a significant increase in the relative weight of the livers of broiler chickens fed with levels of 49.86 μg/kg and 99.08 μg/kg of AFB1, which was reversed with the addition of 0.5–1.0 g/kg of red yeast. Therefore, the disparities in outcomes can be attributed to the fact that perhaps these variables are not affected by low doses of AFB1 and FB1.

In the present study, a beneficial effect of commercial mycotoxin adsorbent could be occurring, as deduced by the macroscopic examination of livers. A slightly darker color was observed in the livers of the broilers fed with the additive, whereas livers from the group with AFB1 and FB1 showed the typical pale yellow color pattern of subclinical mycotoxicosis.

The macroscopic aspect of the livers of the present study reflects slightly the effect of AFB1 in broilers, confirmed by other previous in vivo studies [38]. Prolonged exposure to mycotoxins can result in changes in the color and size of the liver and kidneys, serving as a visible indicator of feed contamination at the time of slaughter [41].

Histopathological analysis of liver sections revealed that broilers fed diets containing AFB1 and FB1 (G2) showed notable tissue damage, whereas the inclusion of the adsorbent in the diet (G3 and G4) demonstrated the presence of less severe lesions, which could indicate a protective effect, with liver morphology closer to that of the control group (G1). The histopathological changes of the liver induced by AF have been extensively described in broilers, suggesting that the effects of AFB1 on histopathology are directly related to the concentration of AFB1 and the duration of the exposure [42]. These findings are in partial agreement with Rashidi et al. [43], who demonstrated significant damage to the liver tissue of broiler chickens fed the AFB1 (0.5 mg/kg) diet, which was reduced by the addition of a probiotic containing nine bacterial strains. On the other hand, in disagreement with our results, Saminathan et al. [44] found no apparent histological alterations in the tissues of the liver samples from the broiler chickens fed with 20 ppb of AFs.

The stability and health of the gastrointestinal tract, particularly the gut, are crucial for the growth of useful bacteria in the intestine and, eventually, the efficiency of broilers [45]. Parameters such as villus height, crypt depth, and the ratio of villus height to crypt depth, as well as absorptive area in different sections of the intestine, may be indicative of epithelial cell turnover at the intestinal barrier [46]. As previous studies have demonstrated, mycotoxins alter the histomorphology of intestinal villi, including atrophy, inflammatory infiltrate, and goblet cell hyperplasia [12, 19, 45]. It has been proven that these changes are mitigated with the addition of HSCA and prebiotics. The mode of action of the HSCA is to bind toxins at the intestinal level and excrete them together with undigested waste, preventing their absorption in the gastrointestinal tract. This process reduces their toxicity, improves intestinal health, and enhances growth performance in animals. In addition, prebiotics increase the number of beneficial bacteria in the intestine (LAB), which helps in the competitive exclusion of pathogens [46]. In the current study, exposure to low levels of AFB1 and FB1 compromises gut morphology, resulting in a shorter villus length and lower crypt depth, while the addition of the adsorbent demonstrated higher crypt depth values. While the addition of the adsorbent at a dose of 1 kg/t demonstrated higher crypt depth values. Our results partially agree with Huang et al. [47], who demonstrated a decrease in duodenal villus height and villus height/crypt depth ratio in broiler chickens fed diets contaminated with 100 g of AFB1 per kg body weight, reversed with the addition of 125 mg/kg of TFRD. Similarly, Srinual et al. [40] demonstrated an increase in the height and width of the villi in broiler chickens with the addition of 0.5–1.0 g/kg of a probiotic (Sporidiobolus pararoseus), in diets contaminated with 50–100 μg/kg of AFB1, ZEA, OTA, T‐2, and DON. Abudabos et al. [37] show an increase in ileal villus height and surface area in broiler chickens fed with the addition of 0.5% HSCA and 4% DDPs as AF adsorbents compared to the broiler group fed 1000 ppb AFB1.

In the present study, higher concentrations of cecal VFAs were observed in broiler chickens supplemented with 1 kg/t of the commercial mycotoxin adsorbent. The adsorbent is formulated with yeast wall hydrolysate, artichoke, and choline chloride, which could be responsible for the increase in cecal VFAs. These results agree with Saminathan et al. [48], who show an increase in the concentration of cecal VFAs with the addition of 5 and 10 g/kg of a prebiotic (isomalto‐oligosaccharides) and a multistrain probiotic (consisting of 11 Lactobacillus strains). It is known that prebiotics and phytobiotics have direct and indirect effects on the microbiota of broiler chickens. VFAs are the main final products from the fermentation process produced by anaerobic bacteria in the large intestine. These are efficiently absorbed by the colonic mucosa, providing energy to the host, and their health‐promoting effects are well known [46]. In addition, they are responsible for reducing pathogenic bacteria in the ceca of broiler chickens and creating a lower pH gut environment, which inhibits viability and growth of redundant bacteria, generating greater accessibility of nutrients in the gastrointestinal tract, which can improve growth and feed efficiency of broiler chickens by increasing uptake of nutrients [46].

Our findings suggest that dietary mycotoxins significantly increase genotoxicity in broiler chickens, as shown by the higher number of micronucleated erythrocytes in G2. The elevated levels of MN suggest that the mycotoxins (AFB1 and FB1) induce DNA damage in erythrocytes, consistent with prior research indicating that exposure to AFs can lead to genotoxic effects [1, 24, 49]. Interestingly, G4, which included a higher dosage of absorbent, displayed results similar to the control group (G1). This observation implies that the absorbent used at the highest dose may have a protective effect against the genotoxicity induced by mycotoxins. The ability of adsorbents to mitigate the effects of mycotoxins is supported by previous studies, which report that certain adsorbents can bind mycotoxins in the gastrointestinal tract, thereby reducing their systemic absorption and subsequent toxicity [6]. Additionally, the results from the alkaline comet assay corroborate the findings from the micronucleus test, demonstrating significantly elevated comet area values in G2 and G3, with G4 similar to the control group (G1). This consistency between both assays strengthens the validity of the genotoxicity assessment, indicating that the absorption of mycotoxins leads to increased DNA strand breaks or damage.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, the commercial adsorbent formulated with sodium calcium aluminosilicate, yeast wall hydrolysate, artichoke, and choline chloride effectively adsorbs mycotoxins in vitro and mitigates their detrimental effects in vivo. This adsorbent improved the productive performance, protection of hepatic and intestinal integrity, modulation of gut fermentation, and prevention of genotoxicity in the chronic co‐occurrence of AFB1 and FB1, depending on the dose used. These results suggest that the commercial adsorbent may serve as a useful intervention for managing low‐level mycotoxin contamination in animal feed.

However, further in vivo studies are needed to assess the appropriate dosage of the commercial adsorbent, its efficacy under conditions of higher concentration of mycotoxin, and larger sample sizes to ensure statistically robust conclusions.
