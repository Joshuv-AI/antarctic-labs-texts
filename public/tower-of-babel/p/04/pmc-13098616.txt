MMP-9-responsive contact lens facilitating disease-adaptive and safer dexamethasone delivery for corneal neovascularization therapy
Authors: Chenxiao Chu, Xi Chen, Yaxin Deng, Jie Zhang, Xiaoshuang Bi, Yunfeng Ma, Mingli Wei, Jiansong Zhao, Haibing He, Jingxin Gou, Tian Yin, Xing Tang, Li Yang, Yu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCorneal neovascularization (CNV), an inflammation-driven pathological process responsible for irreversible vision loss, remains an urgent unmet challenge in ophthalmic medicine. Current interventions—ranging from surgery to topical steroids—fail to prevent recurrence or avoid severe side effects like intraocular hypertension and even corneal ulceration. Here, we introduce CL-Peptide-Dex, a transformative contact lens (CL) engineered for matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9)-responsive dexamethasone (Dex) release, to resolve this decades-long challenge. By grafting dexamethasone onto the lens surface via an MMP-9-cleavable peptide linker (GPLGLAGC), CL-Peptide-Dex achieves disease-adaptive drug delivery: rapid release during acute inflammation and sustained release throughout CNV progression. Unlike conventional drug-eluting systems, this surface-grafting strategy eliminates burst release while preserving lens transparency and oxygen permeability. In the rabbit corneal neovascularization model, a single administration of CL-Peptide-Dex could maintained a stable Dex level for 7 d, suppressed VEGF by 77.5%, and reduced neovascular area by 79.5%. Critically, CL-Peptide-Dex avoided intraocular pressure spikes and exhibited good corneal biocompatibility and compliance, enabling safe extended wear. By integrating MMP-9-responsive precision delivery with a patient-friendly design, CL-Peptide-Dex represents a significant advance in precision ophthalmology.Keywords: Corneal neovascularization, Dexamethasone, MMP-9 responsiveness, Surface grafting, Corneal contact lens

Introduction

Vision is one of the most important senses for humans, but it is often affected by corneal diseases. Among them, corneal neovascularization (CNV) is one of the main causes of blindness worldwide, ranking second only to cataract in severity [1,2]. CNV arises from a dysregulated inflammatory cascade triggered by insults such as infection, chemical injury, or transplant rejection [3,4]. This process recruits neutrophils and macrophages, which secrete pro-angiogenic mediators such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and matrix metalloproteinases [5]. Matrix metalloproteinases degrade the extracellular matrix (ECM) to promote endothelial cell migration, while VEGF directly promotes angiogenesis, leading to corneal opacity, edema, and irreversible vision loss [6]. Although there have been advancements in ocular treatment, current management strategies for CNV have failed to address its dynamic pathophysiology [7,8].

Current therapeutic interventions for corneal neovascularization, such as laser ablation and surgical procedures, can only provide temporary relief of symptoms. However, they fail to address the underlying pathological drivers, leaving residual vascular networks susceptible to inflammatory reactivation and disease recurrence [9]. These invasive treatment methods themselves pose risks of mechanical damage to ocular tissues, postoperative infection, and continuous disruption of the corneal epithelial barrier [[10], [11], [12]]. Conventional topical corticosteroid drugs aim to inhibit pathological angiogenesis and inflammatory cascades, but their clinical application is limited by rapid clearance in the cornea, requiring frequent administration, which reduces patient compliance. Moreover, prolonged corticosteroid use is associated with serious ocular side effects, including elevated intraocular pressure, glaucoma, cataract formation, delayed wound healing, and stromal thinning, further complicating treatment outcomes [[13], [14], [15], [16]].

The drug release pattern of traditional drug-eluting contact lenses is unstable, which affects the accuracy of drug delivery and thus limits their clinical application [[17], [18], [19]]. Contact lenses that rely on drug immersion, matrix embedding or the addition of barrier agents such as vitamin E usually show obvious initial burst release and non-specific leakage. These shortcomings result in drug levels exceeding the safe range, premature payload depletion, and reduced therapeutic durability and safety [[20], [21], [22]]. Acuvue® Theravision™ is the only drug-eluting contact lens approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) specifically for allergic conjunctivitis, but provides no viable option for CNV patients [23,24]. Recently, reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive contact lenses have been designed to release dexamethasone (Dex) in accordance with elevated ROS levels during neovascularization [25]. However, their clinical applicability is limited by the constitutive ROS concentrations found in healthy ocular tissues (∼20 µM), which can provoke unintended drug leakage and potential systemic exposure. In contrast, matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) is scarcely detectable in normal corneas but becomes highly upregulated during CNV, where it directly governs ECM remodeling and angiogenic activation [[26], [27], [28]]. An MMP-9-responsive contact lens can therefore achieve spatiotemporally regulated drug release, eliminating the nonspecific leakage seen in conventional and ROS-responsive systems while enabling precise on-demand therapy.

In this work, we present a novel MMP-9-responsive contact lens, named CL-Peptide-Dex, designed for pathology-adaptive dexamethasone delivery (Scheme 1). The lens surface is functionalized with MMP-9-cleavable peptide sequences (GPLGLAGC), permitting controlled drug release exclusively during active CNV states while maintaining stability under physiological conditions. This design circumvents key limitations of traditional matrix embedding systems, preserving lens transparency and oxygen permeability without an initial burst effect. In rabbit CNV models, CL-Peptide-Dex demonstrated prolonged anti-inflammatory and anti-angiogenic activity, decreasing VEGF levels by 77.5% and reducing neovascular area by 79.5% relative to control groups. It also resolved the problems of low ocular bioavailability and steroid-induced intraocular pressure elevation, overcoming a long-standing safety concern in corticosteroid therapy. By synchronizing drug release with pathological activity, this technology introduces a new framework for personalized CNV management, offering concurrent enhancements in both efficacy and safety.

Materials and methods

Materials

N-vinylpyrrolidone (NVP), 3-(trimethoxysilyl) propyl methacrylate (KH570), Photoinitiator 1173 and acryloyl chloride (Acr Cl) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemicals (Shanghai, China). GPLGLAGC was purchased from Shanghai Gil Biochemical Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). MMP-9 was purchased from Wuhan Cloud-Clone Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). 0.4% Proparacaine hydrochloride eye drops were purchased from Santen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. Commercially available contact lenses (CL@C) (HYDRON® EASY DAY Contact Lens, composed of HEMA, EGDMA, PEG200DAB, AIBN, GMAA and UV416, 14.2 mm in diameter) were purchased from HYDRON Co., Ltd.

Preparation of blank contact lenses (CL-B)

The preparation of CL-B involved ultraviolet-induced crosslinking polymerization, transforming monomeric components into a three-dimensional cross-linked network structure. A homogeneous monomer solution was formulated by mixing hydrogel monomer (HEMA), hydrophilic monomer (NVP), and silicone-containing monomer (KH570) at a volumetric ratio of 70:20:10. The crosslinker ethylene glycol dimethacrylate (EGDMA, 0.5%, v/v) and photoinitiator (0.5%, v/v) were then added to the mixture to obtain a precursor solution. The precursor solution (50 µl) was injected into a master mold, covered with a counterpart mold under bubble-free conditions, and photopolymerized under 365 nm UV irradiation for 40 min to obtain CL-B. CL-B was placed in boiling water for 20 min to remove unreacted monomers and surface microbial load, and then washed three times with water for injection. CL-B was stored in simulated tear fluid (STF, 6.78 g/l NaCl, 2.18 g/l NaHCO3, 1.38 g/l KCl, 0.084 g/l CaCl2⋅2H2O, adjust pH to 7.4 with 0.1 M hydrochloric acid, filtered through a 0.22 µm filter membrane).

Synthesis of non-responsive Dex-loaded contact lenses (CL@Dex)

To highlight the advantages of disease-adaptive drug release, a non-responsive corneal contact lens CL@Dex was prepared using the traditional matrix embedding method for comparison. Specifically, Dex was added to the monomer solution at concentrations of 15 or 30 mg/ml prior to crosslinking and curing to obtain CL@Dex. Subsequent preparation steps were identical to those described for CL-B.

To extract Dex from the silicone hydrogel network for drug loading analysis, CL@Dex lenses were mechanically ground into powder and exhaustively extracted with methanol. Specifically, after thorough hydration, CL@Dex with different drug doses were immediately freeze-dried and crushed into fine powder. Each sample was mixed with 20 ml methanol, stirred for 4 h, sonicated for 10 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. The dexamethasone concentration in the supernatant was quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), and the drug loading capacity of CL@Dex was calculated. Detailed HPLC methods are provided in Supplementary materials.

Synthesis of MMP-9-responsive Dex-loaded contact lenses (CL-Peptide-Dex)

Acrylation of blank contact lenses for the synthesis of AcrCL-B

α, β-Unsaturated carbonyl groups were introduced onto the surface of CL-Bs via nucleophilic substitution between the hydroxyl groups of HEMA and Acr Cl. The modified lenses were designated as AcrCL-B. Specifically, six CL-Bs were immersed in 5 ml anhydrous dichloromethane (DCM), followed by dropwise addition of acryloyl chloride (5 mmol) using pyridine (Pyr, 0.1 equiv relative to AcrCl) as a catalyst. The reaction proceeded under dark conditions at room temperature for 3 h. The lenses were then rinsed three times with dimethylformamide (DMF) (5 min) and deionized water (24 h) to yield AcrCL-B.

Preparation of CL-Peptide via grafting sensitive peptide onto AcrCL-B

The thiol group of cysteine in the sensitive peptide (GPLGLAGC) was conjugated to the double bonds of AcrCL-B via a Michael addition click reaction, resulting in peptide-grafted contact lenses (CL-Peptide). Six fully hydrated AcrCL-B were transferred into a PBS solution (0.1 M, pH 8.0, 10 ml) containing GPLGLAGC (60 mg). The pH was adjusted to 8.5–8.7 using 0.1 M NaOH. The reaction was carried out under nitrogen protection at room temperature for 36 h. Subsequently, the lenses were sequentially washed with PBS (1 M, pH 7.4) and purified water for 24 h to obtain CL-Peptide.

Synthesis of 4-nitrophenyl chloroformate-modified Dex (Dex-COPNP)

Dex (311 mg) and anhydrous DCM (8 ml) were added to a sealed reaction vessel. Pyr (191 µl, 2.37 mmol) was introduced, followed by gradual addition of 4-nitrophenyl chloroformate (208 mg, 1.03 mmol), yielding a clear solution. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 4 h. The organic layer was extracted with ice-cold water (30 ml) and sequentially washed with 0.1 M HCl (aq), saturated sodium bicarbonate, and saturated sodium chloride. After drying over anhydrous sodium sulfate, the product was purified via rotary evaporation and column chromatography. Dex-COPNP was obtained in 52.4% yield and characterized by NMR and MS.

Synthesis of CL-Peptide-Dex via grafting of Dex-COPNP onto CL-Peptide

CL-Peptide were functionalized via coupling of terminal amino groups (from glycine residues) with Dex-COPNP (10 mg per lens) in a Pyr/DCM mixture for 16 h, yielding drug-loaded contact lenses (CL-Peptide-Dex). To remove unreacted Dex-COPNP, the lenses were rinsed sequentially with DCM for 1 h and STF for 24 h. The resulting MMP-9-responsive drug-eluting contact lenses (CL-Peptide-Dex) were stored in STF until further use.

To quantify the drug loading of CL-Peptide-Dex, CL-Peptide-Dex lenses (n = 6) were immersed in 20 ml of STF containing 200 ng/ml MMP-9. After a 10-d release period, the total amount of Dex released into the medium was analyzed using HPLC. CL-Peptide-Dex underwent a terminal sterilization step using ultraviolet (UV) at 254 nm for 30 min. In addition, to directly quantify any potential residual organic solvents, we performed gas chromatography (GC) analysis on the CL-Peptide-Dex lens, as detailed in the Supplementary materials.

Characterization of contact lens

Synthesis validation of CL-Peptide-Dex

The surface chemistry of CL-B, AcrCL-B, CL-Peptide and CL-Peptide-Dex was analyzed using FTIR-ATR (Bruker tensor 27, Bruker Instruments, Billerica, MA, USA) equipped with a diamond ATR cell. The elemental chemical state composition of CL-B, AcrCL-B, CL-Peptide and CL-peptide-Dex surfaces was determined using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha XPS scanning spectrometer. The relative content of fluorine in CL-Peptide-Dex before and after MMP-9 stimulation was determined. The integrated intensity of the XPS peaks was used to analyze the surface elemental composition, and the high-resolution spectra were fitted with split peaks.

Morphology and roughness

The surface morphology and roughness of CL-C, CL-B, CL-Dex, CL-Peptide, CL-Peptide-Dex and rCL-Peptide-Dex were analyzed by AFM. In vitro release study

The MMP-9-responsive release profile of CL-Peptide-Dex was investigated using a Franz diffusion cell. CL@Dex (525/1050 µg) and CL-Peptide-Dex (1050 µg) were placed on the dialysis membrane in the donor cell. 5 ml of STF, or STF containing 100 ng/ml and 200 ng/ml MMP-9 were added into the receptor chamber as the release medium at 35 °C and 50 rpm [29]. Dex levels were measured by HPLC after replacing the release medium with fresh medium at each time point. In addition, the release medium (STF containing 200 ng/ml MMP-9 or STF) was changed every 12 h to explore the ability of CL-Peptide-Dex to modulate Dex release rate at different MMP-9 concentrations. In addition, to assess whether these sterilization processes affect the functional performance of the drug-eluting lenses, we evaluated the Dex grafting efficiency and the MMP-9-responsive release profile before and after sterilization (n = 6).

The drug release mechanism of CL-Peptide-Dex was investigated. To ensure rapid and complete cleavage of the sensitive peptide of CL-Peptide-Dex, the tailored CL-Peptide-Dex, which retained one-tenth of its size, was exposed to 5 ml STF containing MMP-9. Release medium were extracted at 1 and 5 min and immediately lyophilized for mass spectrometry, respectively.

In vivo pharmacokinetic study

A rabbit CNV model (2.1–2.5 kg, 3–5 months old) with high MMP-9 expression was created using chemical alkali cauterization. The ocular surface was anesthetized by topical instillation of 0.4% proparacaine hydrochloride. A circular filter paper (7 mm in diameter) soaked in 1 M NaOH solution was attached to the center of the cornea for 40 s, and then continuously washed with normal saline for 1 min.

Twelve CNV rabbits were divided into three groups (n = 4). The left eye received a single dose: dexamethasone sodium phosphate (DSP) solution (0.5 mg/ml, 263 µl, equivalent to 50 µg Dex) instilled into conjunctival sac, CL@Dex (1050 µg Dex) or CL-Peptide-Dex (1050 µg Dex) applied topically. Here, DSP was intentionally selected as a prodrug of Dex due to its significantly enhanced aqueous solubility compared to native Dex. Upon ocular administration, DSP is rapidly hydrolyzed by endogenous phosphatases present in the tear film and corneal epithelium, thereby ensuring prompt release of active Dex. At different time points, 5 µl tear fluid was collected and subsequently determined for Dex concentration according to the methods described in the Supplementary materials. Pharmacokinetic parameters were derived using DAS 2.0 pharmacokinetic analysis software. Relative bioavailability (F) was obtained using Eq. 5 in the Supplementary materials.

Level A in vitro-in vivo correlation (IVIVC) was established to correlate the in vitro release profile of CL-Peptide-Dex with its in vivo pharmacokinetic behavior. Specifically, the AUC was compared to the percentage of drug released under different in vitro conditions. The in vitro and in vivo drug release percentages were calculated using Eqs. 6 and 7 in the Supplementary materials, respectively.

In vivo pharmacodynamic study

Pharmacodynamic studies were performed using rabbits with both eyes successfully modeled. The left eye of rabbits was worn CL-Peptide-Dex (1050 µg Dex) or CL@Dex (1050 µg Dex) for 7 d. DSP solution (0.25 mg/ml, equivalent to 50 µg Dex) and NaCl were administered by eye instillation three time a day for 7 d consecutivly. The administration was stopped on Day 7, and the observation was continued until 14 d.

Evaluation of ocular CNV development

On postoperative days (POD) 2, 4, 7, 10 and 14, the CNV development was assessed using hand-held slit lamp and Image J to measure CNV length and area.

Analysis of gene expression by qRT-PCR

The mRNA expression levels of VEGF and MMP-9 in corneal tissues were analyzed by qRT-PCR. On POD 4, 7 and 14, rabbits in each different treatment group were euthanized with carbon dioxide. Corneal tissues were promptly dissected and snap-frozen in RNAiso Plus reagent for RNA stabilization. Total RNA was extracted, and the mRNA expression levels of VEGF and MMP-9 were quantified by qRT-PCR. The detailed protocol is provided in the Supplementary material [25].

MMP-9 expression detection

Corneal MMP-9 expression was evaluated using immunofluorescence staining. Briefly, fresh corneal tissue was fixed (4% paraformaldehyde, 2 h, room temperature), permeabilized (0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS, 15 min), and blocked (5% BSA in PBS, 1 h). Anti-MMP-9 primary antibody (1:200 dilution) incubation was performed overnight at 4 °C. Following PBS washes, Cy3-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:500 dilution) was applied (1 h, room temperature). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. Images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Zeiss, Germany).

The MMP-9 expression of was quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Briefly, the corneal samples were minced and homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (100 µl) on ice for 1.5 h. The homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatants. The total protein concentration in the supernatant was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Solarbio, China). MMP-9 concentrations were then measured using a commercial ELISA kit (Abcam, UK) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with absorbance read at 450 nm on a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, USA). Final MMP-9 levels were normalized to the total protein content and are expressed as nanograms per milligram of total protein.

Corneal ROS detection

Corneal reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were assessed on 4, 7 and 14 d. Corneal tissues were incubated with dihydroethidium (DHE) stock solution at 37 °C in the dark, and nuclei were labeled with DAPI. Fluorescence pictures were acquired and semi-quantified to assess corneal tissue reactive oxygen species levels.

Inflammatory factors examination

Corneal tissues were collected from euthanized rabbits and processed for inflammatory cytokine analysis [30]. Each tissue sample was weighed and immediately homogenized on ice using nine volumes of pre-cooled PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4). Following homogenization, samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The concentrations of key inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-1β, were quantified in the supernatants using specific commercial ELISA kits (Elabscience, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance at 450 nm were recorded using a microplate reader.

Biosafety evaluation

Human corneal epithelial cytotoxicity

HCE-T cells were plated in 96-well plates at 1 × 104 cells per well using 100 µl DMEM/F12 containing 15% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin‑streptomycin. Cells were allowed to adhere and proliferate over 24 h under a 5% CO₂ atmosphere at 37 °C (n = 6). After removing the medium, fragments (about 2 mm × 2 mm) of CL‑C, CL‑B and CL‑Peptide‑Dex lenses were prepared under aseptic conditions, sterilized by UV exposure for 30 min, and placed directly onto the cell layer. Co‑incubation was performed in fresh complete medium at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 24 h or 72 h [31]. Following incubation, lens fragments were carefully taken out and cells were washed with PBS. Each well then received 100 µl of 10% CCK‑8 solution and was incubated for another 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was recorded at 450 nm with a Thermo Fisher microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to untreated controls, which were set as 100%.

Ocular irritation

A total of 16 healthy rabbits were divided at random into four groups (n = 4): NaCl solution, DSP solution, CL‑C and CL‑Peptide‑Dex. The study lasted 7 d, NaCl and DSP solutions were applied three times per day. A modified Eye Irritation Scoring System was employed to evaluate corneal reactions, with scores interpreted as follows: 0–3 (none), 4–8 (mild), 9–12 (moderate), and 13–16 (severe). To examine corneal integrity, 2% sodium fluorescein was applied and the cornea was inspected under cobalt blue light. After 7 d, corneal tissue samples were collected and histopathological evaluation was conducted via H&E staining.

Protein adsorption

After wearing of the CL-C, CL-B, CL-Dex and CL-Peptise-Dex for 7 d, the protein absorbed on the lenses was collected with 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate solution and then determined using the BCA kit (Meilunbio, China).

Intraocular pressure (IOP)

IOP was measured using a rebound tonometer in healthy rabbits following 7-d treatment with NaCl solution, DSP solution, CL-Dex or CL-Peptide-Dex, and observation for another 7 d (n = 4).

Statistical analysis

All results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation from at least 3 measurements. Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA was used for statistical analysis. Statistical significance is defined as a P value below 0.05 (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).

Results and discussion

Synthesis and validation of MMP-9-responsive contact lens

CL-B were synthesized via UV-induced crosslinking of hydrogel monomers (HEMA), hydrophilic monomers (NVP), and silicon-containing monomers (KH570) (Fig. 1A). α, β-unsaturated carbonyl groups were introduced onto the surface of the CL-B through a substitution reaction between the hydroxyl groups of HEMA and Acr Cl, yielding AcrCL-B. Thiol groups from cysteine residues in the MMP-9-sensitive peptide (GPLGLAGC) were conjugated to the double bonds of AcrCL-B via Michael addition click chemistry, resulting in peptide-grafted contact lenses (CL-Peptide). Dex modified with 4-nitrophenyl chloroformate (Dex-COPNP) was synthesized to enable covalent conjugation with free amino groups on CL-Peptide [32], ultimately producing MMP-9-responsive drug-eluting contact lenses (CL-Peptide-Dex).

Synthesis and validation of CL-Peptide-Dex. (A) Preparation process of CL-B; (B) 1H NMR spectra and (C) MS spectra of Dex-COPNP; (D) ATR-FTIR adsorption spectra of CL-B, AcrCL-B, CL-Peptide, and CL-Peptide-Dex; (E) XPS analysis of C1s, N1s, O1s, and F1s spectra for (i) CL-B, (ii) AcrCL-B, (iii) CL-Peptide, (iv) CL-Peptide-Dex and CL-Peptide-Dex released for (r-iv-1) 1 d and (r-iv-2) 2 d.

The structure of Dex-COPNP was confirmed by proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) and mass spectrometry (MS) spectra (Fig. 1B and 1C). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses provided robust evidence of stepwise synthesis. The FTIR spectra revealed the occurrence of the stretching vibration absorption peak of the double bond at 1650 cm−1, confirming the successful modification of AcrCl onto CL-B (Fig. 1D). Peaks at 1615 cm−1 (νC = O) and 1550 cm−1 (δN–H) in the amide II band validated CL-Peptide synthesis, whereas carbonyl stretching vibrations at 1668 cm−1 and 1627 cm−1 confirmed Dex grafting. Deconvolution of the C1s spectra showed an increase in –C=O–C=O content (288.80 eV) post-acylation, followed by elevated –CO–NH–/–C–NH2 signals (286.75 eV) after peptide attachment. Subsequent Dex conjugation reduced amine group signals, confirming covalent linkage (Fig. 1E). Notably, a significant decrease in the F1s XPS signal under MMP-9 stimulation highlighted the enzyme-triggered drug release mechanism, a critical feature for minimizing off-target effects.

The solid-phase synthesis strategy of CL-Peptide-Dex circumvented drug property-dependent loading limitations, enabling precise and tunable therapeutic conjugation. The drug-loading capacity of CL-Peptide-Dex was determined to be 1023.2 ± 3.21 µg, comparable to the cumulative dosage of conventional eye drops over 7 d. For comparison, CL@Dex with drug loadings of 519.0 ± 7.66 µg and 1025.2 ± 5.55 µg were prepared via traditional drug embedding. For the sake of consistency, the drug loadings are uniformly referred to as 525 µg and 1050 µg, respectively, in subsequent sections. Unless otherwise specified, CL@Dex used the 1050 µg formulation, whereas the 525 µg variant was employed exclusively for comparative in vitro release studies. The results of residual solvent analysis showed no detectable levels of DCM, pyridine or DMF in the CL-Peptide-Dex samples (Fig. S1A–S1C), which indicated that organic solvents had been fully removed during the preparation process, indicating the rationality and safety of the preparation process.

Characterization of contact lenses

Transmittance

As shown in Fig. 2A, CL-Peptide-Dex appeared to have a rounded and transparent morphology with a diameter of 13.9 mm, base curve radius of 8.6 mm, and average central thickness of 126 ± 6 µm. Transmittance, a critical parameter for visual clarity, must exceed 90% for daily wear lenses. The transmittance of CL-B was not significantly different from that of commercial contact lenses (CL-C) (Fig. 2B). The poor transmittance of CL@Dex may be related to the fact that Dex loading destroys the continuity of the silicone hydrogel network. By contrast, both CL-Peptide-Dex and CL-Peptide-Dex after release (rCL-Peptide-Dex) maintained transmittance values exceeding 94% across the visible spectrum (400–800 nm), attributable to their surface-grafted architecture that preserves the native matrix integrity. This underscores the superiority of surface-grafting contact lens systems over drug-embedded counterparts in maintaining optical performance.

Water content

As illustrated in Fig. 2C, the water content of CL-C, CL-B, CL@Dex (1050 µg), CL-Peptide-Dex and rCL-Peptide-Dex was evaluated. The conventional HEMA hydrogel lens (CL-C) displayed a high moisture level of 51.7% ± 0.81%. Excessive water content can disrupt the natural hydration balance between the lens and ocular surface. To compensate for evaporative losses, such lenses tend to extract water from the anterior corneal tear film, accelerate tear film breakdown, and cause ocular surface irritation [30,33]. Compared with CL-B (36.7% ± 0.79%), the water content of CL-Peptide-Dex remained almost unchanged (37.5% ± 1.14%), while that of CL@Dex decreased by 4%. These results indicate that surface grafting has minimal effect on lens hydration compared to matrix-embedded drug loading. The suitable water content of CL-Peptide-Dex avoids the problem of reverse tear absorption common to traditional hydrogel lenses. This is attributed to the properties of silicone hydrogel-based materials (KH570), which usually have a low intrinsic water content due to the presence of hydrophobic siloxane bonds. In addition, the addition of hydrophilic monomer NVP enhances the water retention through hydrogen bonding, thereby improving the softness and extended wearing comfort of the lens.

Surface roughness

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was employed to analyze the morphology and surface roughness of the contact lenses [34]. No significant difference in average roughness (Ra) was observed between CL-B (7.928 nm) and CL@Dex (8.996 nm) (Fig. 2D). The reason why the hydrophobic drug Dex hardly affects the surface morphology of contact lenses is that Dex successfully integrates into the hydrophobic region of the amphiphilic copolymer matrix and is restricted from moving by the in-situ cross-linked polymer network, thus avoiding surface crystallization. Grafting the peptide sequence onto CL-B increased the Ra to 12.454 nm (CL-Peptide), and subsequent conjugation with Dex further elevated it to 13.950 nm (CL-Peptide-Dex). This sequential increase provides indirect evidence for the successful grafting of both the peptide and the drug. Following MMP-9 stimulation, the shortening of the grafted sequence on the contact lens surface led to a slight reduction in the surface roughness of rCL-Peptide-Dex. Whether the increased Ra of CL-Peptide-Dex causes safety concerns will be investigated later.

Oxygen permeability

The cornea is an avascular and lymphatic-free transparent refractive tissue that relies on oxygen diffusion from the external environment to maintain metabolic activity and physiological integrity. This oxygen needs to cross the contact lens to achieve ocular surface oxygen supply. Inadequate oxygen supply leads to corneal edema, neovascularization, and an increased risk of infection. Oxygen permeability was measured as a Dk value. As shown in Fig. 2E, CL-C has a Dk of 24.9 ± 0.96, which just meets the oxygen transmittance requirements for daily wear. Traditional hydrogel contact lenses mainly rely on water channels to achieve oxygen diffusion, but their limited water absorption ability is not conducive to improving oxygen permeability. Although increasing lens thickness helps to ensure mechanical durability and wear comfort, it also hinders oxygen transport due to the inverse relationship between thickness and permeability.

In contrast, the Dk values of CL-B, CL@Dex, CL-Peptide-Dex and rCL-Peptide-Dex all met the criteria for continuous wearing. This is attributed to the fact that the silicone hydrogel material KH570 promotes oxygen transport through two pathways, including an aqueous channel and a continuous siloxane derived channel. Oxygen diffusion is further facilitated by the high mobility and efficient arrangement of sioxane groups in the KH570 side chain. However, when the KH570 concentration exceeds a critical value, the mutual entanglement and closer arrangement of high-density side chains will limit the oxygen channel in the gap, thus causing the relationship between KH570 content and oxygen permeability to increase first and then decrease instead of monotonically increasing [21].

Mechanical strength

Tensile strength and breaking elongation are key mechanical properties of contact lenses. Tensile strength reflects the structural integrity of the matrix, whereas elongation at break measures material toughness. As shown in Fig. 2F and 2G, CL-B demonstrated higher tensile strength (0.80 ± 0.05 N/mm2) and elongation at break (212% ± 2.6%) compared to those of CL-C (0.11 ± 0.014 N/mm2, 146% ± 1.7%). These improvements are attributed to the lower water content of CL-B, which enhanced polymerization and intermolecular binding, resulting in a more compact gel structure. No significant differences in tensile strength or elongation at break were observed between CL-B and CL-Peptide-Dex, indicating that surface synthesis did not disrupt the crosslinking network of contact lens matrix. Due to the intact matrix physical properties and partially retained hydrophilic peptides, the mechanical strength of rCL-Peptide-Dex (0.82 ± 0.035 N/mm2, 210% ± 5.5%) was almost unchanged after release. In summary, CL-Peptide-Dex achieves an optimal balance of water content, light transmittance, oxygen permeability, and softness by copolymerizing high-water-content NVP with high-oxygen-permeability siloxane monomers, meeting the stringent requirements for long-term contact lens wear.

In vitro MMP-9 responsive release of CL-Peptide-Dex

Under physiological conditions, MMP-9 levels in tear fluid are minimal (<5 ng/ml), but rise markedly (100–200 ng/ml) in pathological states such as corneal neovascularization and inflammation [28,35]. To simulate these distinct ocular microenvironments, we incubated CL-Peptide-Dex in STF containing 100 or 200 ng/ml MMP-9. As illustrated in Fig. 3A, negligible Dex release (<5%) was observed over 168 h in the absence of MMP-9, confirming the stability of the construct under normal ocular conditions. In contrast, Dex release from CL-Peptide-Dex was significantly accelerated in an MMP-9 concentration-dependent manner, reaching cumulative release rates of 61.7% and 90.2% at 100 and 200 ng/ml MMP-9, respectively, after 168 h.

By contrast, CL@Dex, which was not equipped with capabilities for MMP-9-mediated regulation, exhibited an uncontrolled and initial burst release of Dex, particularly within the first 12 h (Fig. 3B–3D). Such uncontrolled release can lead to supraphysiological drug levels, raising the risk of adverse effects. Furthermore, CL@Dex exhibited incomplete drug release, with cumulative values of only 55.1% and 69.9% at doses of 525 µg and 1050 µg, respectively. These limitations are characteristic of conventional matrix-embedded systems, where surface-adsorbed drug contributes to burst release, while strong drug–polymer interactions and diffusional barriers limit complete drug elution. Notably, CL-Peptide-Dex exhibited adaptive “on–off” release behavior. Specifically, Dex release initiated promptly upon MMP-9 exposure and ceased upon its removal (Fig. 3E), mirroring the fluctuating MMP-9 expression typical of CNV progression.

Sterilization is essential for therapeutic contact lenses to prevent corneal infection and maintain therapeutic performance. The sterilization process included boiling treatment for blank lenses (CL-B) and UV irradiation for surface-modified lenses (CL-Peptide-Dex). To evaluate potential impacts of sterilization on drug loading and release performance, the Dex grafting amount and the MMP-9-responsive release profile were compared before and after sterilization. Post-sterilization, the Dex loading remained unchanged (Fig. S2A), and the MMP-9-responsive release kinetics were preserved (Fig. S2B), confirming that the sterilization did not impair the stimulus-responsive release behavior.

To elucidate the release mechanism, CL-Peptide-Dex was incubated with MMP-9-containing medium for 1 or 5 min, followed by lyophilization and MS analysis. At 1 min, the release medium formed a decarboxylated peak at m/z 659.2 corresponding to Dex-COGPL, which proved that the cleavage site was GPL↓GLAGC (Fig. 3F). At 5 min, the Dex-COGPL peak disappeared, and a new peak at m/z 415.2 ([M + Na]+) corresponding to free Dex emerged (Fig. 3G), indicating MMP-9-induced secondary cleavage of the unstable intermediate Dex-COGPL into Dex. This cascade reaction mediated by MMP-9 facilitated rapid peptide cleavage and subsequent liberation of free Dex (Fig. 3H), while MMP-9 deficiency does not trigger Dex release (Fig. 3A). Consistent with a biocatalytic mechanism, MMP-9 remained active throughout the process without undergoing self-degradation. In summary, CL-Peptide-Dex enables on-demand dexamethasone release in response to MMP-9, supporting precise dosing control while averting uncontrolled release under physiological conditions.

In vivo pharmacokinetic study

The pharmacokinetic profile of Dex in tear fluid was assessed in the rabbit CNV model induced by alkali burn, following single-dose administrations of DSP solution, CL@Dex and CL-Peptide-Dex [36]. As shown in Fig. 4, the DSP solution was rapidly cleared, with tear concentrations falling below 100 ng/ml within 4 h post-administration, underscoring the need for frequent dosing that may increase systemic risks. CL@Dex demonstrated burst release behavior, yielding drug overaccumulation and a limited mean residence time (MRT0-t) of 15.9 h (Table 1). By contrast, CL-Peptide-Dex strikingly extended MRT0–t to 56.6 h through its disease-adaptive release mechanism, maintaining stable therapeutic concentrations for over 7 d. The relative bioavailability of CL-Peptide-Dex was 3.99-fold and 3.07-fold higher than DSP solution and CL@Dex, respectively, effectively addressing bioavailability limitations and concentration fluctuations inherent to conventional delivery systems.

An in vitro–in vivo correlation (IVIVC) was constructed by comparing the percentage of drug absorbed in vivo with the cumulative release in vitro. In vivo pharmacodynamic study

CNV suppression

Pharmacodynamic assessments were performed in both healthy and CNV-model rabbit corneas (Fig. 5A). The progression of CNV is characterized by early inflammatory infiltration (Day 1–4), neovascular sprouting (Day 4–7), and vessel maturation (Day 7–14), which were monitored in the experiment. While the CL@Dex group partially suppressed CNV during the initial 4 d, a subsequent inflammatory burst and vascular proliferation undermined its efficacy (Fig. 5B and 5C). Compared with the CL-Peptide-Dex group, the neovascular area in the CL-Peptide-Dex group decreased by 79.5%. CL-Peptide-Dex exhibited a biphasic release characteristic, that is, rapid administration during the acute inflammation on POD 4, followed by continuous release to prevent the progression of CNV in the later stage. Although triple-daily topical DSP solution was equivalent to a single dose of CL-Peptide-Dex in inhibiting CNV, frequent administration would reduce patient compliance and lead to cumulative side effects of dexamethasone.

To further evaluate the anti-angiogenic effect, we conducted immunofluorescence analysis of CD31, which is related to leukocyte adhesion and vascular remodeling [37]. High CD31 expression and enlarged vascular pores were observed in the NaCl and CL@Dex groups by Day 14, indicating active neovascularization (Fig. 5D). Similar to healthy corneas, the CL-Peptide-Dex group showed almost no CD31 staining, confirming its advantage in immediate and long-term CNV control.

Suppression of the VEGF–MMP-9 feedforward loop

In CNV procession, VEGF and MMP-9 act synergistically to promote endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and tube formation [38]. Specifically, VEGF upregulates MMP-9, which leads to extracellular matrix degradation and endothelial cell migration. Additionally, MMP-9 enhances VEGF diffusion and receptor activation, forming a self-amplifying loop that aggravates CNV [39].

The expression levels of VEGF and MMP-9 in rabbit corneas were quantified using quantitative real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) (Fig. 6A and 6B). The NaCl group showed sustained high expression of both factors. For CL@Dex, VEGF and MMP-9 expression was reduced at POD 4 but significantly increased at POD 7 because of the difficulty in maintaining effective dexamethasone therapeutic concentrations with the burst release of CL@Dex. Significantly, the expression of VEGF and MMP-9 in the CL-Peptide-Dex group was significantly lower than that in the DSP group on POD 4 and 7, showing a significant advantage in CNV inhibition.

(C) Fluorescence imaging of corneal MMP-9 for healthy rabbits and CNV rabbits treated with CL-Peptide-Dex, DSP solution, CL@Dex and NaCl. Scale bar: 1000 µm. (D) MMP-9 levels in the cornea tissue determined by ELISA for healthy rabbits and CNV rabbits on POD 4, 7 and 14 (mean ± SD, n = 4, ns: P > 0.05, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).

Under physiological conditions, MMP-9 is hardly detectable in the ocular surface. After alkali injury, the expression of MMP-9 increases sharply. Therefore, the expression of MMP-9 can be used as a key indicator reflecting the progression of neovascularization. We further evaluated MMP-9 at the protein level using immunofluorescence and ELISA. The expression of MMP-9 was almost undetectable in the healthy corneas (Fig. 6C). In contrast, the NaCl group showed strong MMP-9 immunofluorescence and a higher protein concentration (923.3 ng/mg) (Fig. 6D). Meanwhile, owing to the inability of CL@Dex to sustain effective drug concentrations over time, the MMP-9 levels gradually increased by POD 7 and 14, accompanied by the emergence of a limited number of neovascular lumens. Both the DSP and CL-Peptide-Dex groups effectively suppressed neovascularization, reinforcing the association between MMP-9 expression and CNV progression. On POD 7 and 14, MMP-9 levels in the CL-Peptide-Dex and DSP groups were significantly lower than in the NaCl group (P < 0.001), indicating that sustained drug exposure effectively downregulates this pro-angiogenic factor, thereby inhibiting extracellular matrix degradation during vessel formation and CNV formation. The disease-adaptive drug release of CL-Peptide-Dex can be explained as the rapid release of dexamethasone in the early stage of CNV with high expression of MMP-9, and then the release slows down as the secretion of MMP-9 decreases in the later stage of treatment.

ROS scavenging and anti-inflammatory effects

ROS contribute to CNV by activating the transcription factor NF-κB, which drives inflammatory responses and angiogenesis [40,41]. Significantly increased ROS levels were observed in corneal tissues of the NaCl control group, particularly in the stromal layer (Fig. 7Aand 7B). On POD 14, the CL-Peptide-Dex group showed significantly lower ROS production than the DSP, CL@Dex and NaCl groups. In contrast, the DSP solution group, although relying on frequent high-dose administration to reduce oxidative stress, failed to maintain long-term stable therapeutic concentrations as CNV progressed. Similarly, the initial burst release from CL@Dex resulted in loss of ROS control by Day 7.

During the acute and chronic phases, the upregulation of inflammatory cytokines directly stimulates the migration and proliferation of vascular endothelial cells and promotes the production of angiogenic factor [42,43]. Dexa inhibits the binding of NF-κB to DNA, thereby inhibiting inflammatory signal transduction and oxidative stress. We quantified inflammatory cytokine levels using ELISA on POD 4, 7 and 14. Notably, the inflammatory factors levels (TNF-α and IL-1β) of the CL-Peptide-Dex group were well controlled through POD 14 (Fig. 7C and 7D). A single dose of CL-Peptide-Dex provided a more persistent anti-inflammatory effect compared to repeated use of DSP eye drops. The increase in inflammatory factor expression in the DSP group may be due to rapid tear clearance and unstable drug levels. Additionally, the CL@Dex group failed to continuously inhibit inflammation due to its burst release characteristic and incomplete drug utilization. These results indicate that CL-Peptide-Dex can intelligently regulate the release of dexamethasone in response to changes in the pathological microenvironment, effectively alleviating oxidative stress and inflammatory responses during the formation of CNV.

Structural preservation validating ocular safety

Histopathological analysis of corneal H&E staining was shown in Fig. 8. NaCl group exhibited significant inflammatory cell infiltration and neovascularization on POD 7 and extensive disruption of the epithelial, Bowman’s and stromal layers, and numerous neovascularization cavities on POD 14. The CL@Dex-treated corneas showed transient improvements but ultimately mirrored saline-group pathology, with edema and capillary lumen formation. DSP solution group still showed mild epithelial and stromal layer damage, potentially attributable to intraocular pressure elevation from high-dose Dex. In contrast, the CL-Peptide-Dex group maintained corneal morphology comparable to healthy controls, demonstrating its superior ability to continuously suppress inflammation and CNV formation.

Biocompatibility evaluation

Irritation mitigation and corneal integrity preservation

The CCK-8 assay showed that the lens extract had no significant cytotoxic effect on HCE-T cells (Fig. S4). Specifically, after 24 h of incubation, the cell viability values of CL-C, CL-B and CL-Peptide-Dex reached 98.7%, 100.2% and 99.2%, respectively. Even after prolonging the incubation period to 72 h, cellular viability consistently exceeded 96%. These results confirm the absence of significant cytotoxicity induced by CL‑Peptide‑Dex, supporting the biosafety of its fabrication procedure.

Ocular irritation scores indicated progressive alleviation in the CL-Peptide-Dex group 24 h post-application, which may reflect either physiological adaptation of the ocular surface or improved lens properties following drug elution (Fig. 9A). In contrast, the NaCl and DSP solution groups exhibited negligible irritation in the healthy rabbit eyes over 7 d. Corneal damage was assessed using fluorescein sodium staining and HE staining. Fluorescein sodium imaging revealed that CL-C causes corneal edema and structural damage, which may be related to its low oxygen permeability. By contrast, CL-Peptide-Dex maintained corneal integrity with no observable pathological changes (Fig. 9B). Histological examination after 7-d continuous wear confirmed intact corneal morphology in the CL-Peptide-Dex group, characterized by well-organized squamous epithelial stratification, intact basal membrane and Bowman’s layer continuity (Fig. 9C). These findings validate the biocompatibility of CL-Peptide-Dex, supporting its potential for long-term ocular therapeutics.

Surface hydration engineering mitigating protein adsorption

Protein adsorption on contact lenses remains a significant concern, often leading to diminished optical clarity, discomfort, and corneal pathologies such as papillary conjunctivitis. The protein adsorption levels of contact lenses worn in rabbit eyes for 7 d are illustrated in Fig. 9D. CL‑C showed the highest protein deposition (33.9 ± 2.5 µg/lens), which may be associated with its high water content (51.7% ± 0.81%). Whereas conventional understanding suggests that enhanced surface roughness (Ra = 13.950 nm for CL-Peptide-Dex vs 7.928 nm for CL-B) typically promotes protein adsorption, CL-Peptide-Dex exhibited comparable adsorption levels to that of CL-B (12.7 ± 1.7 µg/lens vs 8.2 ± 2.0, P > 0.05) and significantly lower than those of CL@Dex (25.6 ± 2.3 µg/lens, P < 0.001) and CL-C (P < 0.001). This apparent contradiction may be explained by the peptide-functionalized surface, where hydrogen bonding between peptide bonds and water facilitates a dense hydration layer. This hydrophilic interface serves as a dynamic kinetic barrier, sterically repelling protein adsorption and reducing nonspecific deposition.

IOP homeostasis via disease-adaptive design

Topical Dex is widely used to manage corneal neovascularization, typically administered over 7-d cycles. However, repeated high-dose application raises risks of adverse effects including cataract formation and glaucoma. In addition, it can also cause increased intraocular pressure by altering trabecular meshwork cell morphology and promoting extracellular matrix deposition [44]. IOP monitoring for 14 d in rabbit models demonstrated that CL-Peptide-Dex maintained physiological IOP levels (12.6 ± 0.9 mmHg) indistinguishable from healthy controls (11.2 ± 1.6 mmHg, P > 0.05) through its disease-responsive release kinetics (Fig. 9E). Pharmacokinetic analyses revealed frequent instillation of DSP solution induced drug accumulation with sustained concentration fluctuations, which triggered progressive ocular hypertension during the observation period (peak IOP = 18.5 ± 1.3 mmHg, P < 0.001 vs NaCl group). Similarly, the CL@Dex group exhibited continuous IOP elevation due to burst release and overdosing accumulation within 24 h of wear, reaching a peak IOP of 17.1 ± 1.0 mmHg on Day 8, followed by a gradual decline during the subsequent observation period. In conclusion, CL-Peptide-Dex can release sufficient drug in the early stage to suppress CNV outbreak, and then intelligently adjust the drug release rate with CNV severity to ensure efficacy while reducing the occurrence of adverse reactions. This disease-responsive release not only circumvents IOP-related optic neuropathy but also surpasses traditional regimens in terms of safety, aligning with emerging paradigms in intelligent drug delivery for ocular conditions.

Conclusion

The CL-Peptide-Dex platform establishes a new paradigm in ophthalmic therapeutics by merging precision drug delivery with enhanced clinical translatability. Unlike traditional platforms, CL-Peptide-Dex utilizes MMP-9 as a disease-specific trigger, enabling on-demand drug release that aligns with pathological progression. This system overcomes key limitations of conventional drug-eluting contact lenses, such as uncontrolled release profiles, material-drug incompatibility, and long-term safety issues. The surface-tethered design preserves fundamental lens characteristics such as oxygen permeability and mechanical strength, while enabling pathology-responsive drug exposure that minimizes corticosteroid overdosing and maximizes anti-angiogenic efficacy. More importantly, CL-Peptide-Dex aligns with emerging trends in personalized medicine, offering a viable platform for early-stage management of inflammatory corneal diseases. Future studies will focus on evaluating its potential in combination therapies for multifactorial ocular pathologies.
