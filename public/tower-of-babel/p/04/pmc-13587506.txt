Health literacy and vaccination attitudes among parents from different ethnic groups: a health belief model approach
Authors: Vasfiye Bayram Deger, Ahmet Butun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHealth literacy is a key determinant of health behaviours, including vaccination. Despite the proven benefits of immunization, vaccine hesitancy remains a global challenge, shaped by cultural, social, and individual factors. The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between parents’ health literacy and vaccine attitudes according to the health belief model among different ethnic groups. This study is expected to highlight the role of cultural differences in achieving public health objectives, while also contributing to improved health literacy and the development of more inclusive policies in child health and immunization.MethodsThis descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted with 908 parents (Turkish: 249; Kurdish: 217; Arab: 245; Assyrian: 197) living in Mardin, Turkey, between January and July 2025. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a sociodemographic form, the Public Attitude Towards Vaccination Scale-Health Belief Model, and the Turkey Health Literacy Scale-32. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, one-way ANOVA, and generalized linear model analyses.ResultsHealth literacy and vaccination attitudes differed significantly according to ethnic group (p< .001). Arab parents had the lowest general health literacy scores and the most negative vaccination attitudes, including the highest perceived barriers to vaccination (24.04 ± 3.98). In contrast, Assyrian parents had the highest scores for sensitivity and severity, awareness of importance, belief in vaccine benefits, and health responsibility. Higher health literacy was positively associated with more favourable vaccination attitudes among Turkish, Kurdish, and Assyrian parents. However, among Arab parents, higher health literacy was associated with lower awareness of importance, lower belief in vaccine benefits, lower health responsibility, and greater perceived barriers. Significant interaction effects between ethnicity and health literacy were found for awareness of importance, belief in vaccine benefits, and health responsibility.ConclusionHealth literacy is an important factor influencing vaccination attitudes, but its effect varies according to ethnic background and cultural context. In some groups, higher health literacy supports more positive vaccination attitudes, whereas in others it may not lead to greater vaccine acceptance. Public health interventions should therefore use culturally sensitive and trust-based approaches to improve vaccination attitudes among diverse populations.Keywords: Parent, Child, Childhood, Vaccine, Vaccination, Vaccine hesitancy, Ethnic group, Health belief model, Health literacy, Attitude

Introduction

Health literacy (HL) refers to individuals’ ability to access, understand, appraise, and apply health information to make informed health decisions [1]. It is considered an important determinant of health behaviours and outcomes, influencing individuals’ capacity to engage with healthcare services, adopt preventive practices, and manage health-related decisions effectively [2, 3].

The elimination and eradication of vaccine-preventable infectious diseases has been targeted to improve global health. Under the global vaccination action plan, in addition to ensuring vaccine accessibility, an increase in vaccine hesitancy has been observed. The reasons for vaccine hesitancy vary individually and socially. Vaccine hesitancy and refusal to vaccinate are the main reasons for the increase in the proportion of unvaccinated children. There are challenges in achieving the universal vaccination goal and the vaccination coverage targets for children under five years of age. Vaccine hesitancy and the refusal to use vaccines and child health services among individuals responsible for caring for children are influenced by socio-cultural, religious, economic, political, and institutional factors. This situation affects children’s health-seeking behaviours and vaccine acquisition [4]. Low vaccination rates lead to a decrease in community immunity and jeopardize the eradication and elimination of vaccine-preventable infectious diseases [5]. This situation is creating a global health issue, leading to a resurgence of infectious diseases in childhood [6]. Studies based on the Health Belief Model (HBM) play an important role in explaining the perceived threats and behavioural assessments that lead to vaccine hesitancy in parents, as well as in helping to improve health behaviours and in the development of preventive services in public health nursing [7].

Culture is defined as the entirety of the tools used to transmit all material and spiritual values existing within the historical social development process to subsequent generations. Culture shapes the way of life of society by influencing individuals’ behaviours. The importance of the concept of culture has increasingly risen in recent times due to rising rates of migration. Societies that strive to maintain their own cultures and pass them on to future generations have reflected this in health behaviours and sought cultural solutions to their health issues. The cultural structure that guides individuals’ behaviours and lifestyles directs perceptions of health and illness, access to care services in cases of illness, acceptance of treatment, and adaptation processes and health practices. The concept of health is also an entity that varies from culture to culture [8]. Cultural norms and social values significantly influence individuals’ health behaviours, decisions made, and the level of knowledge upon which these decisions are based [8, 9]. The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between parents’ health literacy and vaccine attitudes according to the health belief model among different ethnic groups. This study is expected to highlight the role of cultural differences in achieving public health objectives, while also contributing to improved health literacy and the development of more inclusive policies in child health and immunization.

In Turkey, routine childhood immunisations are administered free of charge within the national immunisation programme, which is integrated into the primary healthcare system and delivered through family health centres. The national schedule covers vaccines against tuberculosis, hepatitis B, diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis, polio, Haemophilus influenzae type b, pneumococcal disease, measles-mumps-rubella, varicella, and hepatitis A. Parents are not required to pay for any vaccine included in the national schedule, and uptake is actively monitored by public health authorities. In addition to these routine vaccinations, certain optional (private) vaccines, such as rotavirus and meningococcal vaccines, are available for purchase at private healthcare providers. The present study focuses primarily on attitudes toward routine childhood vaccinations delivered through the national schedule, although private vaccination status was also assessed as a secondary variable.

Moreover, a conceptual distinction must be drawn between vaccine hesitancy and vaccine refusal, as these terms are frequently conflated in the literature yet represent fundamentally different phenomena requiring different public health approaches. Vaccine hesitancy refers to a delay in acceptance or refusal of vaccination despite the availability of vaccination services and is complex and context-specific, varying across time, place, and vaccine type [10]. In contrast, vaccine refusal involves an active decision not to vaccinate, often grounded in firm anti-vaccine beliefs, religious or ideological convictions, or distrust of health authorities [11]. Hesitant parents may ultimately vaccinate with appropriate communication and support, whereas refusers may require fundamentally different, more intensive interventions targeting deeply held belief systems. In the present study, the Public Attitude Towards Vaccination Scale–Health Belief Model assesses vaccination-related beliefs and attitudes (e.g., perceived barriers, perceived benefits, awareness of vaccine importance, health responsibility) that are theoretically associated with vaccine hesitancy, but does not directly measure hesitancy as a behavioural or intentional construct, nor does it assess active vaccine refusal. The instrument is designed for attitudinal profiling, not for prediction of future vaccination behaviour. Accordingly, throughout this study the term vaccine hesitancy is used descriptively to characterise attitudinal profiles associated with suboptimal vaccination beliefs, while acknowledging that the scale measures belief and attitude dimensions rather than behavioural intention or past vaccination behaviour.

The Health Belief Model (HBM) was originally developed in the 1950s by Rosenstock [12] and subsequently refined by Janz and Becker [13] as a framework for understanding why individuals engage, or fail to engage, in health-protective behaviours. The model posits that health behaviour is determined by four core constructs: (1) perceived susceptibility, the individual’s subjective assessment of their risk of developing a disease or health problem; (2) perceived severity, the belief regarding the seriousness of the condition and its potential consequences; (3) perceived benefits, the belief that a specific action (e.g., vaccination) will effectively reduce the health threat; and (4) perceived barriers, the anticipated costs or obstacles associated with taking that action, whether physical, financial, psychological, or social. Later extensions of the model incorporated two additional constructs: cues to action (internal or external stimuli that trigger health behaviour) and self-efficacy (the individual’s confidence in their ability to perform the health behaviour). The HBM has been widely applied in vaccination research and has demonstrated utility in predicting both vaccine acceptance and hesitancy across diverse populations. It is important to note, however, that the Public Attitude Towards Vaccination Scale-Health Belief Model used in this study is an operationalisation developed by Kocoglu-Tanyer et al. [7] that draws on HBM constructs but does not fully represent the complete original theoretical model. Specifically, cues to action and self-efficacy are not separately operationalised in the scale, and the instrument is designed for attitudinal assessment rather than prediction of individual vaccination behaviour.

In light of the theoretical framework and empirical context outlined above, this study was guided by the following research questions:

Research question 1: Do health literacy levels and HBM-based vaccination attitudes differ significantly across ethnic groups (Turkish, Kurdish, Arab, and Assyrian) residing in Mardin, Turkey?

Research question 1: Do health literacy levels and HBM-based vaccination attitudes differ significantly across ethnic groups (Turkish, Kurdish, Arab, and Assyrian) residing in Mardin, Turkey?

Research question 2: Does ethnic group membership moderate the relationship between health literacy and HBM-based vaccination attitudes, such that the direction or magnitude of this association differs across groups?

Research question 2: Does ethnic group membership moderate the relationship between health literacy and HBM-based vaccination attitudes, such that the direction or magnitude of this association differs across groups?

These questions were addressed using a moderation framework within a General Linear Model (GLM) approach, with ethnicity operationalised as the moderator variable and overall health literacy as the predictor. It was hypothesised that (H1) significant between-group differences in health literacy and vaccination attitudes would be observed, consistent with documented disparities in health outcomes among ethnic minority populations; and (H2) the positive association between health literacy and favourable vaccination attitudes would be attenuated or reversed in groups characterised by structural disadvantage or cultural barriers to health information utilisation.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a descriptive cross-sectional design.

Setting

The study was conducted in Mardin, a multicultural city located in southeastern Turkey. Mardin is a metropolitan city with a population of 840,000. Mardin is a multicultural city with diverse ethnic and religious communities. Mardin has a much more heterogeneous population in terms of culture and ethnicity than any other city in Turkey.

Participants, sample, and sampling

The study population consisted of parents from four different ethnic backgrounds (Turkish, Kurdish, Arab, and Assyrian) living in Mardin, a multicultural city located in southeastern Turkey. Both mothers and fathers were eligible to participate. Because one of the study aims was to compare ethnic groups, efforts were made to reach a sufficient number of participants from each group. However, probability sampling was not feasible because there was no accessible sampling frame listing parents according to ethnic background. In particular, the Assyrian community represents a relatively smaller and harder-to-reach population in the study setting. Therefore, Assyrian participants were initially recruited using snowball sampling. After initial participants from the Assyrian community were contacted, they were asked to inform other eligible parents within their social networks about the study. Subsequently, Turkish, Kurdish, and Arab participants were recruited using convenience sampling, while considering broadly similar sociodemographic characteristics where possible.

Using the G-power 3.1.9.7 program, the total sample size was calculated as 876 with a total difference of 10%, a margin of error of 5%, and a power of 90%. To reach a similar number of individuals from each ethnic group, individuals from the limited population of Assyrian individuals were initially accessed using the snowball sampling method. Subsequently, by considering similar sociodemographic variables in the environment, Turkish, Kurdish, and Arab participants were reached through a convenient sampling method. This study was completed with 908 participants including a sufficient number of participants from each ethnic group (Turkish: 249, Kurdish: 217, Arab: 245, Assyrian: 197). Although Mardin is a multicultural and multilingual province, all participants were able to communicate in Turkish, which was the language used in the data collection process. Participants who could not communicate effectively in Turkish were not included in the study.

Data collection

The data were collected between January 13, 2025 and July 11, 2025. The data were collected through face-to-face interviewer-administered questionnaires, in which trained researcher read each item aloud to participants and recorded their responses directly. This approach was chosen to facilitate participation among individuals with lower literacy levels and to ensure data completeness across all ethnic groups. However, it should be noted that interviewer presence may have introduced social desirability bias, as participants may have provided responses perceived as more socially acceptable in the interviewer’s presence.

Data collection tools

The data collection tool consisted of 3 sections: The 1st section contains questions related to sociodemographic data. The 2nd section includes the Public Attitude Towards Vaccination Scale-Health Belief Model, and the 3rd section contains the Turkey Health Literacy Scale-32 (THLS-32) Scale.

The sociodemographic form

The sociodemographic form included questions on parents’ demographic characteristics, health insurance status, access to healthcare institutions, vaccination-related education, and childhood vaccination practices. Vaccination-related questions assessed whether the child’s vaccines had been administered completely and on time, whether the child had received routine childhood vaccines included in the vaccination schedule, and whether the child had received private or optional vaccines outside the routine programme. For private vaccines, rotavirus, meningococcal, and influenza vaccines were specifically listed.

Public attitude towards vaccination scale-health belief model

This scale was originally developed by Koçoglu-Tanyer et al. [7]. Although the scale was developed based on the HBM, it evaluates selected vaccination-related belief dimensions and does not measure every construct of the original model, such as cues to action and self-efficacy. This scale measures vaccination-related beliefs and attitudes within selected Health Belief Model dimensions, including sensitivity and severity, awareness of importance, belief in vaccine benefits, perceived barriers, and health responsibility. It does not measure vaccination intention or objectively verified vaccination behaviour. In contrast, past vaccination behaviour was assessed separately through the sociodemographic form.

The scale does not yield a total score, and each subdimension is interpreted separately. All sub-dimensions of the scale, which has five sub-dimensions and responses in a five-point Likert format, are evaluated separately. The sensitivity and seriousness sub-dimension consist of four items (minimum 4, maximum 20 points); the benefit and health responsibility sub-dimension consists of five items (minimum 5, maximum 25 points), while the barrier sub-dimension comprises eight items (minimum 8, maximum 40 points). A decrease in score in the barrier sub-dimension indicates a positive attitude, while an increase in score in the other sub-dimensions signifies a positive attitude. In the present study, the subdimensions showed acceptable to good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.792 to 0.867.

Turkey Health Literacy Scale-32 (THLS-32)

The scale was adapted into Turkish by Okyay et al. [14] and assesses parents’ health literacy levels. The scale consists of 32 questions, and participants rate the difficulty level of each topic using a 5-point Likert scale (very easy = 1, easy = 2, difficult = 3, very difficult = 4, I have no opinion = 5), where 0 indicates the lowest health literacy and 50 indicates the highest health literacy. A score of 0–25 indicates insufficient health literacy; >25–33 score indicate problematic-limited health literacy; >33–42 score indicate sufficient health literacy; >42–50 score indicate excellent health literacy [14]. The THLS-32 was developed and validated for use in Turkey and assesses individuals’ perceived difficulty in accessing, understanding, evaluating, and using health-related information. Previous validation work reported that the scale had acceptable psychometric properties for assessing general health literacy in Turkish-speaking adult populations [14]. In the present study, the scale also demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.862 to 0.927 across the total scale and subdimensions.

Data analysis

The data were analysed using the SPSS 22.0 statistical program. In the descriptive statistics, categorical variables were summarized with frequency (n) and percentage (%); continuous variables were summarized with mean (Mean) and standard deviation (SD). The normality assumption of continuous variables was evaluated by examining skewness and kurtosis coefficients; it was determined that all variables were within the ± 1.5 range, and it was accepted that the application of parametric tests was appropriate (Table 1).

Normal distribution

The distribution of socio-demographic variables according to ethnic groups was analysed using the Chi-square test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to determine whether scale scores differed by ethnic origin, and Bonferroni-corrected post hoc comparisons were conducted for results found to be significant. The effect size of group differences was evaluated using the eta squared (η²) coefficient, interpreted as small (≥ 0.01), medium (≥ 0.06), and large (≥ 0.14) according to Cohen’s conventions.

General Linear Model (GLM–Univariate) analyses were used to determine whether the effect of health literacy on vaccination attitudes varied by ethnic background. Dependent variables (sensitivity and severity, awareness of importance, belief in vaccine benefits, perceived barriers, and health responsibility) were entered separately into each model. Ethnic background was specified as a fixed factor, while overall health literacy was included as a continuous covariate. To test the moderation effect, an ethnicity × health literacy interaction term was added to each model. The Type III sum of squares method was employed throughout. To address the potential confounding introduced by the significant sociodemographic differences observed across ethnic groups, a set of adjusted GLM models was also estimated. These adjusted models additionally included age, sex, parental education, perceived income, social security status, healthcare access, and vaccination education as covariates. Turkish parents served as the reference group in all interaction contrasts. Both unadjusted and adjusted results are reported to allow direct comparison and to evaluate the robustness of interaction effects. Levene’s test for homogeneity of variance indicated violations in several models (Table 4). However, GLM procedures with large samples (N = 908) and moderately unbalanced group sizes are considered robust to such violations, as the Central Limit Theorem and the relative stability of Type I error rates under heteroscedasticity support their continued use [15]. Sensitivity analyses using Welch-corrected ANOVA for group comparisons yielded substantively identical conclusions. Parental education variables referred to maternal and paternal educational attainment within the household, irrespective of the sex of the respondent completing the questionnaire.

Model results were reported with the F value, degrees of freedom (df), significance level (p), proportion of explained variance (R²), and partial eta squared (partial η²) values. In models where the interaction was found to be statistically significant, simple slopes analyses were conducted to determine slopes specific to ethnic groups. The slope of health literacy on the relevant vaccine attitude dimension was reported separately for each group. Additionally, interaction plots were created using low (− 1 SD) and high (+ 1 SD) levels of the health literacy variable for visual presentation of the interaction. In all analyses, the level of statistical significance was accepted as p < .05.

In this study, the internal consistency of the Turkey Health Literacy Scale-32 and the Public Attitude Towards Vaccination Scale–Health Belief Model was evaluated with Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. The alpha values calculated for the sub-dimensions of Health Literacy are as follows: general health literacy (α = 0.927), accessing information (α = 0.901), understanding information (α = 0.889), evaluating information (α = 0.862), using information (α = 0.878), treatment and services (α = 0.893), and health protection (α = 0.885).

According to the Public Attitude Towards Vaccination Scale-Health Belief Model, the Cronbach’s alpha values for the subdimensions were as follows: sensitivity and severity (α = 0.814), awareness of importance (α = 0.842), belief in the benefits of the vaccine (α = 0.867), perceived barriers (α = 0.792), and health responsibility (α = 0.856). These values indicate that the scale assesses both positive beliefs and perceived barriers and responsibility dimensions with sufficient reliability.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

The ethnic composition of the study sample broadly reflects the multicultural character of Mardin province, where Turkish, Kurdish, Arab, and Assyrian communities have historically coexisted. Although official ethnic composition statistics for Mardin are not publicly available, regional demographic descriptions indicate the presence of these communities in the province. The predominance of female participants is consistent with maternal attendance patterns at childhood vaccination appointments in family health centres.

The age group of 18–30 constitutes the highest proportion in all groups, with 57.6% of Kurdish parents belonging to this age range. A significant difference was also found in gender distribution (p < .001); for example, 96.3% of the participants in the Arab group were women, while the male ratio in the Kurdish group rises to 21.2%. Chi-Square Analysis

A statistically significant difference was found between the ethnic groups in the education level of parents (p < .001). While 53.4% of Kurdish parents were primary school graduates, the highest rate of bachelor’s degree graduates was among Turkish parents with 34.6%. The father’s education status also showed a similar difference (p < .001); the high school graduation rate among Turkish fathers was the highest at 34.1%. There was also a significant difference between ethnic groups in the distribution of perceived income situation (p = .001); while 28.2% of Arab parents answered “income is more than expenses” this rate decreases in other groups.

In terms of health insurance, 85.9% of Turkish parents indicate that they have social security coverage, while the rate of those who did not have health insurance was more prevalent in the Assyrian group with 22.8%, in Arab group with 22.0%, and in Kurdish group with 21.2% (p < .001). The presence of a healthcare staff in the family was highest in the Turkish group (65.5%; p = .001). Significant differences were found in the number of children aged 0–5 (p < .001); the rate of having 1 child in the Kurdish group was 41.5%, while in the Arab group, the response ‘all my children are over 5 years old’ was high at 21.2%.

In the variable regarding the timely and complete vaccination status, the Assyrian group gave a “yes” response at a rate of 93.4%, and this rate created a significant difference between groups (p < .001). A difference was also observed in the education status regarding vaccines; 63.7% of the Arab group indicated that they received training (p < .001). In terms of accessing to healthcare institutions, the responses “good” and “medium” were the most frequently selected options; however, there was also a significant difference between groups in this variable (p < .001).

The rate of childhood vaccinations in the Assyrian and Turkish groups was found to be quite high (94.4% and 91.2%); this difference was statistically significant (p < .001). There was also a difference between the groups regarding private vaccinations (p = .015); 34.1% of Turkish parents reported having private vaccinations. Overall, it appears that ethnicity was significantly related to many socio-demographic and health-related variables.

The ethnic group proportions in this study should not be interpreted as prevalence estimates for Mardin; rather, they were structured to ensure adequate numbers of participants in each ethnic group for comparative analysis.

The differentiation of vaccination attitude scores based on health literacy and health belief model of individuals according to ethnic group was presented in Table 3. According to the results of the one-way variance analysis, Health Literacy and Health Belief Model Vaccination Attitude scores significantly differ by ethnicity. The overall health literacy score was found to be lowest in the Arab group (Mean = 21.75 ± 8.14), and this difference was statistically significant (F = 73.851, p < .001, η²=0.197, representing a large effect). Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons confirmed that the Kurdish, Assyrian, and Turkish groups all scored significantly higher than the Arab group (Arab < Kurdish, Assyrian, Turkish; all p < .001), while these three groups did not differ significantly from one another. This pattern was similarly observed in the sub-dimensions of accessing information, understanding information, evaluating information, using information, accessing healthcare services, and health protection (all p < .001; η²=0.131–0.197, all large effects). The Arab group had significantly lower scores in all dimensions.

In the perceived barriers sub-dimension, the Arab group reported the highest scores (Mean = 24.04 ± 3.98), indicating greater perceived obstacles to vaccination (F = 11.228, p < .001, η²=0.036, small effect). Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons confirmed that the Arab group scored significantly higher than all other groups (Arab > Kurdish, Assyrian, Turkish; all p < .05), consistent with the pattern of systematically less favourable vaccination attitudes in this group.

In terms of health responsibility scores, the Arab group had the lowest mean (Mean = 15.73 ± 3.21), while the Assyrian group had the highest mean (Mean = 18.80 ± 3.14). The between-group difference was statistically significant (F = 33.652, p < .001, η²=0.100, medium-to-large effect). Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons demonstrated that the Arab group scored significantly lower than all other groups, and the Assyrian and Turkish groups scored significantly higher than the Kurdish group. As a result, it was observed that the Arab group consistently demonstrates lower scores in both health literacy and beliefs about vaccination, while the Assyrian group exhibits more positive attitudes. These results indicate that the Arab group exhibits systematically lower scores in both health literacy and vaccination beliefs, while the Assyrian group demonstrates more positive attitudes across all dimensions.

The interaction between health literacy and ethnicity was statistically significant (F(3, 900) = 13.093, p < .001, η² = 0.042), indicating that the effect of health literacy on this dimension varied across ethnic groups. While a positive association was observed between health literacy and belief in vaccine benefits in the Turkish reference group (B = 0.140), the negative and significant interaction coefficient for the Arab group (B = − 0.210, p < .001) suggests that the slope reversed in direction for Arab participants. The GLM analysis of the interaction between health literacy and ethnicity on perceived barriers indicated a significant overall model (F(7, 900) = 9.547, p < .001, R² = 0.069). General health literacy was a negative and significant predictor of perceived barriers (B = − 0.122, p = .007, η² = 0.024), consistent with the general expectation that higher health literacy is associated with fewer perceived barriers. The main effect of ethnicity was marginally significant (F(3, 900) = 2.629, p = .049, η² = 0.009). Critically, the health literacy × ethnicity interaction was not statistically significant (F(3, 900) = 2.005, p = .112, η² = 0.007), indicating that the effect of health literacy on perceived barriers did not differ significantly across ethnic groups. Parameter estimates supported this interpretation; interaction coefficients for the Arab (B = − 0.022, p = .722) and Kurdish (B = − 0.051, p = .432) groups were non-significant, and although the Assyrian group showed a positive trend (B = 0.117, p = .098), it did not reach statistical significance. In conclusion, while between-group differences in barrier levels may be observed, the direction and magnitude of health literacy’s effect on this subscale do not appear to be moderated by ethnic background.

The GLM analysis of the interaction between health literacy and ethnicity on health responsibility revealed a significant overall model (F(7, 900) = 28.593, p < .001, R² = 0.182). The main effect of ethnicity was significant (F(3, 900) = 8.387, p < .001, η² = 0.027). Most critically, the interaction between health literacy and ethnicity was statistically significant (F(3, 900) = 16.549, p < .001, η² = 0.052), indicating that health literacy’s effect on health responsibility varied across ethnic groups. Inspection of the parameter estimates revealed a positive association between health literacy and health responsibility in the Turkish reference group (B = 0.161), while the negative and significant interaction coefficient for the Arab group (B = − 0.222, p < .001) was consistent with a reversal of this relationship for Arab participants. Slope differences for the Kurdish (B = 0.014, p = .725) and Assyrian (B = − 0.033, p = .445) groups relative to the Turkish reference were not statistically significant. These results suggest that the expected increase in health responsibility with rising health literacy does not operate in the same manner in the Arab group, pointing to the potential influence of cultural context, trust, and the nature of individuals’ relationship with the healthcare system.

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between health literacy and vaccination attitudes among parents from different ethnic groups using the Public Attitude Towards Vaccination Scale–Health Belief Model. Although this instrument is grounded in the Health Belief Model, it should not be interpreted as a full measurement of the original theoretical model. The original HBM includes constructs such as perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, and later extensions such as cues to action and self-efficacy. In contrast, the scale used in this study measures selected vaccination-related belief dimensions, including sensitivity and severity, awareness of importance, belief in vaccine benefits, perceived barriers, and health responsibility. Therefore, the findings are discussed in relation to these measured scale dimensions rather than as evidence for the full original HBM.

This study revealed significant differences in vaccine attitudes among ethnic groups in terms of both health literacy levels and HBM dimensions. The Arab group scored the lowest in all sub-dimensions of health literacy and in the HBM sub-dimensions related to vaccination, which might indicate that this group faces disadvantages in both accessing health information and in the processes of interpreting and utilizing this information. This result may reflect the potential influence of language barriers, possible differences in trust toward the healthcare system, and broader cultural factors shaping health behaviours, especially among minority groups [16, 17]. Cultural diversity, language barriers, trust, and potential barriers to the use of health care services could contribute to health care inequalities among ethnic groups [18]. Conversely, the Assyrian group having the highest scores in health literacy and HBM dimensions can be associated with protective factors such as a culturally high sense of health responsibility, fewer barriers to accessing information, or positive health norms within the community. Barriers such as language and literacy can impede access to health information. Community engagement and social networks also facilitate information sharing and health-seeking behaviours [19, 20]. Communities with strong social support, shared cultural practices, and collective health norms foster environments where healthy behaviours are encouraged and sustained [20].

The lower health literacy scores among Arab parents are consistent with previous studies showing that health literacy is closely related to education, socioeconomic status, access to healthcare, and language-related barriers. Sørensen et al. explained that health literacy is shaped not only by individual skills but also by social and cultural conditions, including education and access to health services [21]. Similarly, Paasche-Orlow and Wolf emphasized that people with lower education levels and poorer access to healthcare are more likely to have limited health literacy and difficulty using health information effectively [22]. In the present study, Arab parents generally had lower education levels, lower health insurance coverage, and lower rates of complete and timely childhood vaccination. These factors may help explain why they also had lower health literacy scores.

In contrast, Assyrian parents had the highest health literacy scores and the most positive attitudes toward vaccination. They also reported the highest rates of complete and timely vaccination and one of the highest levels of access to healthcare institutions. It is possible that more frequent contact with health services and stronger engagement with preventive care contributed to these positive attitudes. Previous studies have shown that individuals who are more involved in preventive health services are also more likely to have higher health literacy. Wagner et al. found that higher health literacy is associated with better understanding of preventive health recommendations and greater use of preventive services [23].

Another important finding was that Assyrian parents scored highest in the Health Belief Model dimensions of susceptibility, severity, vaccine benefits, and health responsibility, whereas Arab parents scored lowest. According to the Health Belief Model, people are more likely to engage in preventive health behaviours when they believe they are at risk of illness, believe the illness could be serious, understand the benefits of prevention, and perceive fewer barriers. The higher scores among Assyrian parents suggest that they may see childhood vaccination as both necessary and beneficial. Similar findings have been reported in other studies. Shmueli found that parents who perceived higher susceptibility and greater benefits were more willing to vaccinate [24]. Wong et al. also showed that stronger beliefs in vaccine benefits and greater personal responsibility for health are linked to more positive vaccination attitudes [25].

In contrast, Arab parents reported the highest perceived barriers to vaccination. These barriers may potentially include concerns about vaccine safety, fear of side effects, reduced trust in healthcare institutions, exposure to misinformation, or communication difficulties with healthcare professionals, although these constructs were not directly measured in the present study and should be interpreted as hypotheses rather than conclusions. Previous research has consistently shown that perceived barriers are among the strongest attitudinal correlates of vaccine hesitancy and suboptimal vaccination behaviour. Larson et al. highlighted that vaccine confidence is influenced by trust in healthcare providers and health systems [26]. Dubé et al. similarly noted that misinformation, mistrust, and cultural beliefs can increase concerns about vaccines and reduce vaccine uptake [27].

Another important result of this study was that health literacy did not improve vaccination attitudes in the Arab group in the same way it did in the other ethnic groups. Among Turkish parents, higher health literacy was associated with greater awareness of vaccine importance, stronger belief in vaccine benefits, and greater health responsibility. However, among Arab parents, these relationships were negative. This finding suggests that health literacy may not always function as a protective factor. One possible explanation may involve differences in trust toward healthcare institutions, information sources, or culturally embedded interpretations of health information; however, these constructs were not directly assessed in the present study and therefore warrant further investigation in future research. Some previous studies support this explanation. Biasio argued that health literacy alone is not enough to increase vaccine acceptance because people also need the skills to critically evaluate the quality and reliability of the information they receive [28].

The results of this study suggest that interventions focused only on increasing parents’ knowledge may not be sufficient to improve vaccination attitudes. These data emphasize the necessity of health education programs that are culturally sensitive and that these programs should be structured considering the health belief systems of ethnic groups. The literature suggests that interventions aimed at increasing health literacy should not only provide information but also shape the dimensions of the HBM [29]. In this context, community-based, culturally sensitive educational approaches that overcome linguistic barriers may be particularly effective in increasing vaccine acceptance among ethnic groups with low health literacy. Ekezie et al. [30] highlighted that communication barriers can impede the acceptance of vaccines among disadvantaged populations. They emphasize the importance of cultural competence in healthcare interventions, which can lead to better communication strategies and, consequently, higher vaccine uptake [30]. Public health interventions should also address trust, cultural beliefs, communication styles, and the social context in which parents make decisions about vaccination. Community leaders and respected individuals within communities may play an important role in improving trust and vaccine confidence. In addition, educational materials should be culturally sensitive, easy to understand, and adapted to the language and beliefs of different communities. From a nursing and public health perspective, these findings are particularly important because nurses are often the healthcare professionals who have the most direct communication with parents about childhood vaccination. Nurses can help improve vaccine confidence by providing clear information, correcting misinformation, and building trusting relationships with families. Previous research has shown that nurses play a central role in parents’ vaccination decisions and that trust-based communication is essential for reducing vaccine hesitancy [31].

Strengths and limitations

One of the most important strengths of this study is that it encompasses parents from different ethnic groups (Turkish, Kurdish, Arab, and Assyrian) in the province of Mardin, which reflects Turkey’s multicultural structure, and allows for the examination of cultural differences within the framework of health literacy and health belief models. Especially the access to the hard-to-reach Assyrian participants through snowball sampling method is a significant contribution to filling the gap in the literature regarding minority groups’ health attitudes. Moreover, the determination of the sample size based on power analysis and achieving a participant number significantly above the targeted figure (n = 908) enhances the statistical power of the study and the reliability of the results. The use of the THLS-32 and Public Attitude Towards Vaccination Scale–Health Belief Model, which are widely used in the literature and have high validity and reliability, supports the quality of measurement.

However, there are several limitations to the study. Firstly, the cross-sectional design does not permit causal inferences; associations observed between health literacy and vaccination attitudes should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. Additionally, the combination of snowball and convenience sampling may limit the representativeness of the sample to the broader population of each ethnic group in Mardin, potentially restricting generalisability. Researchers should exercise caution when extrapolating these findings to other regions or ethnic compositions. In addition, because data were collected using interviewer-administered face-to-face questionnaires, participants may have provided socially desirable responses, particularly for questions concerning childhood vaccination practices and attitudes toward vaccination. Therefore, social desirability bias should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Moreover, variables that may importantly confound or mediate the observed relationships, including religious beliefs, trust in the healthcare system, social media use, and prior vaccination experiences, were not included in the research model and represent avenues for future investigation. In addition, although adjusted GLM analyses controlling for key sociodemographic covariates were conducted and yielded substantively consistent findings, residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. Also, the relatively smaller Assyrian sample (n = 197), obtained through snowball sampling, may be subject to within-group homogeneity bias, as snowball sampling tends to recruit socially connected individuals who may share similar attitudes. Finally, the scale used (Public Attitude Towards Vaccination Scale–Health Belief Model) operationalises selected HBM constructs and does not fully represent the complete theoretical model, including cues to action and self-efficacy.

Implications and future research

The implications of the results emphasize the necessity of culturally tailored health literacy interventions when addressing vaccination behaviours across different ethnic groups. Strategies that merely focus on enhancing parents’ cognitive health literacy could be inadequate if they do not account for the emotional and social dimensions that affect health beliefs. Interventions designed collaboratively with community members are essential for ensuring that culturally specific concerns are addressed, thereby enhancing both trust and participation in vaccination programs. In practical terms, culturally sensitive interventions might include: (1) community-based vaccination education sessions delivered by trusted community members or bilingual healthcare staff; (2) engagement of religious and community leaders as vaccine advocates, as these figures may carry greater credibility than formal healthcare institutions; (3) training for nurses and primary care providers in culturally competent communication to address vaccine concerns; (4) development of accessible educational materials in different languages that address common vaccine misconceptions; and (5) proactive outreach through family health centres to families who have delayed vaccination appointments. These recommendations are grounded in the quantitative patterns observed and should be further refined through qualitative research conducted with each community.

The results of this study showed that health literacy is an important factor affecting vaccination attitudes, but its effect depends on the cultural and social context. The positive relationship between health literacy and favourable vaccination beliefs among Turkish, Kurdish, and Assyrian parents is consistent with the existing literature. However, the reverse pattern observed among Arab parents suggests that vaccination attitudes are shaped not only by knowledge but also by trust, culture, social norms, and the credibility of information sources. Therefore, future vaccination programs should go beyond one-size-fits-all approaches and use culturally sensitive strategies that address the specific needs of different ethnic groups.

Based on the results of this study, some recommendations could be made for future research. First, since the cross-sectional nature of this study does not allow for the identification of causal relationships, the cause-and-effect relationships between health literacy and vaccination attitudes should be examined more clearly using longitudinal or quasi-experimental designs. Second, it is recommended to conduct qualitive studies to explore experience of individuals regarding health literacy and vaccine attitudes. Third, comparative studies could be conducted among individuals living in different geographical regions, rural and urban settlements, to evaluate the effects of structural inequalities on health literacy and vaccine attitudes.

Conclusion

In this study, the relationship between the levels of health literacy of parents and their vaccination attitudes according to the Health Belief Model has been examined based on different ethnic groups (Turkish, Kurdish, Arab, Assyrian). The results revealed that health literacy and beliefs about vaccination significantly differ among ethnic groups. It was found that the Arab group had the lowest average health literacy scores, while the Assyrian group had the highest scores. Similarly, positive beliefs about vaccination (susceptibility, awareness of importance, belief in benefits, health responsibility) were significantly lower in the Arab group, and higher in the Assyrian and Turkish groups.

Moderation analyses (GLM with ethnicity x health literacy interaction) revealed positive and significant associations between health literacy and favourable vaccination beliefs in Kurdish, Assyrian, and Turkish groups. In contrast, the Arab group exhibited a reversed pattern: higher health literacy was associated with lower awareness of vaccine importance, reduced belief in vaccine benefits, and diminished health responsibility. Both unadjusted and adjusted GLM models (the latter controlling for age, sex, parental education, income, social security, healthcare access, and vaccination education) yielded consistent results, confirming that these interaction effects were robust to potential confounding by sociodemographic variables.

In conclusion, this study showed that health literacy is a decisive factor influencing individuals’ attitudes toward vaccination, and this effect cannot be evaluated independently of the cultural context. The inverse relationships observed particularly in the Arab group indicate that, in addition to access to information, trust in the healthcare system and cultural values also determine vaccination attitudes. Therefore, public health interventions aimed at increasing health literacy should be designed with inclusive strategies that consider not only information-based approaches but also ethnic, cultural, and social factors.
