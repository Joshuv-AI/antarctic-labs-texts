Adverse Effects of Low Serum HDL‐C Levels on Prognosis and Tumor Immune Microenvironment in Patients With Newly Diagnosed Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia
Authors: Weiran Lv, Shutong Liu, Jiangtao Song, Zhijian Liang, Huaze Liao, Qianqian Huang, Ziang Zhu, Zhoupei Mo, Yun Wang, Yang Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDisorders of lipid metabolism contribute to the occurrence and progression of hematological malignancies, including chronic lymp hocytic leukemia (CLL). The objective of this study was to investigate the association between serum lipid levels and prognosis in patients with CLL. We retrospectively analyzed data from 142 patients with newly diagnosed CLL (ND‐CLL). Our survival analysis showed that low levels of high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol/Apolipoprotein‐A1 (HDL‐C/ApoA1) were associated with poor overall survival (OS) and progression‐free survival in patients with ND‐CLL. Multivariate analysis identified HDL‐C as an independent hazard element for OS. A nomogram combining HDL‐C levels and classical prognostic factors increased the accuracy of predicting 5‐year OS compared to prediction based solely on classical prognostic factors. The adverse effect of low levels of HDL‐C on prognosis in CLL patients was further validated through external validation, wherein the functionality of HDL‐C was simulated by assessing cholesterol efflux in a cohort comprising 448 CLL patients. Moreover, flow cytometry analysis of 194 peripheral blood lymphocyte subpopulations demonstrated that the levels of HDL‐C/ApoA1 was significantly positively correlated with natnatural killer (NK) cells. Subsequent in vitro cell experiments confirmed the ability of HDL to augment NK cell cytotoxicity, as evidenced by elevated expression levels of CD107a and tumor necrosis factor‐α. In conclusion, low serum HDL‐C levels are related to a poor prognosis in patients with ND‐CLL, potentially due to impaired NK cell cytotoxicity, providing insight into the prognostic role of HDL‐C and proposing a new potential therapeutic target in patients with ND‐CLL.

Introduction

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is a type of blood cancer characterized by an abnormal increase in the number of small CD5+ B cells in the bone marrow, peripheral blood, and lymphatic system [1]. The primary clinical manifestation of CLL is occasional increased lymphocyte counts detected during routine peripheral blood examinations [2]. Lymphadenopathy is a secondary clinical manifestation [2]. Notably, the clinical symptoms of CLL due to bone marrow infiltration are relatively uncommon [2].

CLL is mostly prevalent among people in Western countries, especially Europeans, but is rare in Asian populations, including China [3, 4]. Recently, the successful development of anti‐CD20 antibodies rituximab or obinutuzumab, combined with Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor (BTKi) [5], has significantly improved CLL outcomes. However, despite these advances, there is no cure for CLL. Progression or treatment‐refractory CLL is observed in most patients, resulting in treatment failure [2].

Chinese individuals diagnosed with CLL tend to be younger at diagnosis, have atypical morphological characteristics, display different immune phenotypes, exhibit higher rates of mutational immunoglobulin heavy chain variable region (IGHV) and rearranged IGHV, and experience a poorer prognosis when compared to Europeans. These differences are likely attributed to background genetic differences between Chinese and European populations [4]. The international prognostic index for CLL (CLL‐IPI) has proven to be more efficient than the traditionally used Rai and Binet staging systems in predicting prognosis in patients diagnosed with CLL [6, 7, 8]. However, CLL‐IPI's clinical use is limited in some centers due to their inability to perform necessary laboratory experiments. Therefore, a more applicable and accurate prognostic model for CLL is urgently needed in clinical practice.

Lipids are involved in many vital physiologic processes, including inflammation, cell proliferation, and signal transduction. Notably, previous research has demonstrated dyslipidemia as a factor in the neoplastic processes of various cancers, including hematological malignancies [9, 10, 11, 12]. This includes a strong association between decreased levels of high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol/Apolipoprotein‐A1 (HDL‐C/ApoA1) and several types of blood cancers, such as acute myelogenous leukemia (AML), acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), and others [11, 12]. Conversely, abnormalities in triglyceride (TG), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), or total cholesterol (TC) are not significantly linked to cancer risks [11, 13]. Consequently, lipid abnormalities in the blood may serve as a critical prognostic indicator for certain hematological malignancies.

To date, studies examining the prognostic effect of dyslipidemia in patients with CLL are lacking, and the mechanism of dyslipidemia in these patients is not well understood. Our current study aimed to evaluate the latent prognostic value of dyslipidemia in patients with newly diagnosed CLL (ND‐CLL) and explore its possible underlying mechanism.

Methods

Patient Selection in the Real World

Our study retrospectively analyzed data from 406 patients diagnosed with CLL between March 2005 and April 2022 at the Sun Yat‐sen University Cancer Center's (SYSUCC) Department of Hematologic Oncology. CLL diagnosis was based on the National Cancer Institute criteria.

Inclusion criteria included newly diagnosed CLL with complete lipid examination data. Exclusion criteria encompassed (a) any acute or chronic underlying diseases affecting lipid metabolism, such as chronic renal failure, nephrotic syndrome, thyroid hypofunction, gastrointestinal malabsorption, and hereditary hyperlipidemia, (b) use of lipid‐lowering medications, (c) insufficient data for analysis, and (d) presence of other malignant diseases.

Patient data were collected using an electronic hospital medical records system prior to the initiation of treatment. Baseline collected data for the enrolled patients with ND‐CLL included age, sex, clinical staging system (including Rai and Binet stagings), body mass index (BMI), routine blood tests, lipid data, dates of diagnosis, death, last follow‐up, and treatment therapies. Laboratory examinations were conducted on fresh peripheral blood samples before diagnosis or treatment. Peripheral blood parameters included lymphocyte count (LN), platelet count (PLT), white blood cell count (WBC), hemoglobin (Hb), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), beta‐2‐microglobulin (β2M), albumin (ALB), and C‐reactive protein (CRP). Lipid data included ApoA1, HDL‐C, apolipoprotein B (ApoB), LDL‐C, TC, and TG. The detection of the lipid fractions was performed using the lipid kit (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN), and the data were analyzed using Cobas 8000 (Roche). Lymphocyte subset analysis was also conducted for some patients using flow cytometry (BD FACSDiva Software; Biosciences), including CD3+ T cells, CD3+/CD4+ T cells (helper T cells), CD3+/CD8+ T cells (cytotoxic T cells), CD4+/CD8+ T cells, CD19+ B cells, CD3‐CD16+&CD56+ cells (NK cells), CD4+/CD25+ T cells (regulatory T cells), and CD8+/CD25+ T cells (regulatory T cells). The information regarding the antibodies for flow cytometry is presented in Table S1. The latest follow‐up data were available until May 1, 2023. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee at Sun Yat‐sen University Cancer Center.

Definitions

The concept of overall survival (OS) was defined in the following manner: the time interval starting from the initial CLL diagnosis in patients until their demise or the most recent follow‐up date for those who are still living. The concept of progression‐free survival (PFS) was defined in the following manner: the duration from the commencement of treatment until the occurrence of disease advancement or mortality due to any reason. Imaging techniques, medical records, and other parameters were applied to assess the CLL progression.

Database Cohorts' Acquisition and Preprocessing

The CLLE‐ES dataset, including clinical information, survival data, and gene expression matrix (RNA‐seq), for patients with CLL (N = 297) was retrieved from the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC) database accessible at https://dcc.icgc.org/. Additionally, the GSE22762 dataset [14] encompassing survival data and gene expression data matrix (microarray) for patients with CLL (N = 151), was acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, which can be accessed via https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/.

To ensure uniformity, the gene symbols were harmonized with the corresponding gene IDs. In instances where multiple gene IDs were mapped to a single gene symbol, we opted for the median value as an indicative measure of gene expression level. Normalization and log2 conversion were performed for both gene expression matrices.

The GOBP_NEGATIVE_REGULATION_OF_CHOLESTEROL_EFFLUX gene set was acquired from the Molecular Signature Database v2023.2 (MSigDB). The primary function of HDL‐C is to elicit cholesterol efflux from cells, mediating the elimination of cellular cholesterol. Therefore, the GOBP_NEGATIVE_REGULATION_OF_CHOLESTEROL_EFFLUX gene set could be used to evaluate HDL‐C function.

Analysis of Single‐Sample Gene Set Enrichment

The ssGSEA2 was introduced to calculate the GOBP_NEGATIVE_REGULATION_OF_CHOLESTEROL_EFFLUX gene set enrichment (NRCE) score for CLLE‐ES and GSE22762, using the R package “GSVA.”

Cytotoxic Activity of Natural Killer (NK) Cells

The Stem cell kit was employed to isolate NK cells from the peripheral blood of healthy individuals. Myco‐Blue Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Vazyme) was used to test the absence of mycoplasma contamination of the above NK cell. Two experimental groups were established, namely the NK group and the NK+HDL group, which were cultured in 24‐well plates. The cell density for each group was set at 1 × 106 cells. The NK group was treated with 1 mL of complete medium per well. In the NK+HDL group, HDL (MkBio, YB‐003) was added to the complete medium at a concentration of 100 μg/mL per well. The CD56 marker was utilized for the phenotypic identification of NK cells. The cytotoxic activity of NK cells was assessed by employing CD107a, interferon‐γ (IFN‐γ), and tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α) as markers. To perform intracellular staining with CD107a, NK cells were cultured in medium supplemented with phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA) (50 ng/mL; Sigma–Aldrich), ionomycin (1 mg/mL; Sigma–Aldrich), monensin (10 ng/mL; Sigma–Aldrich), and FITC‐CD107a for a duration of 4 h at a temperature of 37°C in a CO2 incubator. Subsequently, surface marker staining was conducted. After membrane permeabilization, intracellular staining of IFN‐γ‐Y610‐mCHERRY and TNF‐α‐V610 was performed. The proportion of CD107a+NK cells (FSV440‐CD56+ CD107a+), IFN‐γ+NK cells (FSV440‐CD56+ IFN‐γ+) and TNF‐α+NK cells (FSV440‐ CD56+ IFN‐γ + TNF‐α+) was detected and analyzed using flow cytometry.

Statistical Analysis

We utilized R software version 4.1.0 for data analysis in our research. Fisher's exact test was conducted to analyze categorical variables. Furthermore, the “suffer minor” package was used to determine optimum cutoff values. The OS and PFS were assessed utilizing the Kaplan–Meier methodology. The prognostic impact of lipid factors was evaluated using both univariable and multivariable Cox regression analysis, in comparison with other commonly observed prognostic factors. Due to the collinearity between ApoA1 and HDL‐C, ApoA1 was excluded from the multivariate analysis. The lipid profile was assessed using Pearson's correlation analysis to examine its relationship with various immune cells, resulting in the computation of Pearson correlation coefficients. The receiver operating characteristic analysis was used to develop and validate a prognostic risk nomogram model. In the database cohort validation, the samples were classified into distinct groups based on their scores, with one group comprising high‐scoring samples (high‐score group) and the other group consisting of low‐scoring samples (low‐score groups) using the optimal cutoff NRCE score determined using the R package “survminer”. Comparisons of continuous variables among the groups were conducted by employing 1‐way ANOVA analysis. The statistical significance was determined for P values below 0.05.

Survival Analysis

Association of OS With Lipids Regarding the in Patients With ND‐CLL

In patients with ND‐CLL, the 3‐year OS rate was 80.8% ± 3.4%, and the median survival time was 56.4 months, ranging from 5.2 to 209.6 months. OS analysis was conducted based on lipid data, including ApoA1, HDL‐C, ApoB, LDL‐C, TG, and TC levels (Figure 1). Patients were categorized as high‐risk or low‐risk based on the optimum cutoff thresholds for lipids. Patients in the low ApoA1 group exhibited a significantly poorer 3‐year OS compared to those in the high ApoA1 group (74.0% ± 4.6% vs. 95.2% ± 3.4%, p < 0.001) (Figure 1A). Additionally, the low HDL‐C level group had a comparatively lower 3‐year overall survival rate compared to the group characterized by high HDL‐C levels (73.9% ± 4.6% vs. 95.3% ± 3.3%, p < 0.001) (Figure 1C). Furthermore, a poorer 3‐year OS was observed in the low LDL‐C and low TC groups compared to the high LDL‐C (p = 0.037, Figure 1D) and high TC groups (p = 0.010, Figure 1E). But the OS between the ApoB (Figure 1B) and TG groups (Figure 1F) was not significantly different.

Prognostic Value of Lipids Based on PFS in Patients With ND‐CLL

The 3‐year PFS in patients with ND‐CLL was 65.5% ± 4.1%, with a median survival time of 41.2 months (range, 0.3–188.3 months). The influence of ApoA1, HDL‐C, ApoB, LDL‐C, TG, and TC levels on PFS is illustrated in Figure 1. Patients with lower levels of ApoA1 had a lower 3‐year PFS rate compared to those with higher levels of ApoA1 (58.9% ± 5.1% vs. 79.4% ± 6.2%, p = 0.013) (Figure 1G). Similarly, patients in the low HDL‐C group had a poorer 3‐year PFS compared with those in the high HDL‐C group (58.9% ± 5.2% vs. 79.5% ± 6.1%, p = 0.002; Figure 1I). However, ApoB, LDL‐C, TC, and TG levels did not significantly affect PFS (Figure 1H,J–L).

Considering the impact of the therapeutic regimen on PFS, ApoA1 or HDL‐C risk groups were further divided into six subgroups based on three therapy types (including BTKi‐based therapy, chemotherapy combined with R or not, and the “watch and wait”). In six classes of ApoA1, the high levels of ApoA1 or HDL‐C groups might have a survival benefit in PFS despite no statistical significance being found in the Kaplan–Meier curves. The HDL‐C subgroups had the same result, especially in patients who received BTKi‐based therapy (Figure S1).

Univariate and Multivariate Analysis of OS

In the univariate analysis, HDL‐C (hazard ratio [HR] 0.156, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.056–0.439; p < 0.001) and ApoA1 (HR 0.131 [95% CI 0.032–0.541]; p = 0.005) demonstrated significant independent values in predicting OS (Figure 2A). Notably, Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that high levels of LDL‐C and TC were not significantly linked to ND‐CLL progression; however, they had independent prognostic capabilities (TC, HR 0.454 [95% CI 0.245–0.839], p = 0.012; LDL‐C, HR 0.499 [95% CI 0.256–0.972], p = 0.041) in the univariate analysis. In addition, other identified prognostic factors included age (HR 2.574 [95% CI 1.411–4.695]; p = 0.002), LDH (HR 3.135 [95% CI 1.667–6.896]; p < 0.001), and β2M (HR 3.685 [95% CI 1.691–8.031]; p = 0.001). However, in the multivariate analysis, among the identified risk factors for OS, only HDL‐C (HR 0.113 [95% CI 0.022–0.574]; p = 0.008) and LDH (HR 2.945 [95% CI 1.143–7.591]; p = 0.025) exhibited significant independent associations (Figure 2B). Due to collinearity between ApoA1 and HDL‐C, the multivariate analysis solely focused on HDL‐C.

Nomogram Model

We developed a nomogram‐based survival prediction model that integrated clinically significant risk factors identified through multivariate analysis, with the aim of accurately forecasting the OS for patients with ND‐CLL (Figure 3A). Three factors were selected and formulated to predict OS: HDL‐C level, LDH level, and age. The accuracy of predicting OS rates at 1, 3, and 5 years was assessed by using the calibration nomogram curve (Figure 3B). The area under the curve (AUC) of HDL‐C level in 5‐year OS (0.67 [95% CI, 0.59–0.75]) demonstrated a significantly higher value than that of the Rai stage 0.55 [95% CI, 0.46–0.64] (Figure 3C). In addition, the AUC of the merged score in 5‐year OS was 0.79 [95% CI, 0.70–0.88], which demonstrated a significantly higher value than that of the Rai stage (0.55 [95% CI, 0.46–0.64]), suggesting that addition of the HDL‐C parameter can improve the ability of the model to predict OS compared with that with classical prognostic elements. This study's prognostic model constructed with HDL‐C could effectively predict the OS of patients with ND‐CLL; however, future studies need to validate this.

Flow Cytometry Analysis of Lymphocyte Subpopulations in Peripheral Blood

We performed a correlation analysis between ApoA1 or HDL‐C and different immune cells at 194 time points in 35 patients. The findings of our study unveiled a significant correlation between the levels of HDL‐C/ApoA1 and the abundance of B cells, CD4+ T cells, and NK cells. Results indicated that the level of ApoA1 (Pearson, −0.40, p < 0.0001; Figure 4A) and HDL‐C (Pearson, −0.32, p < 0.0001; Figure 4D) exhibited an inverse correlation with the quantity of B cells, whereas the level of ApoA1 (Pearson, 0.31, p < 0.0001; Figure 4B) and HDL‐C levels (Pearson, 0.21, p = 0.005; Figure 4E) exhibited a positive correlation with the quantity of CD4+ T cells. The NK cell count was also positively correlated with ApoA1 (Pearson, 0.39; p < 0.0001; Figure 4C) and HDL‐C levels (Pearson, 0.28; p < 0.0001; Figure 4F), with the relationship between NK cells being the most pronounced. These results indicate that HDL‐C and ApoA1 may participate in the immune regulation of the tumor microenvironment. Subsequently, we constructed a Pearson correlation heatmap, revealing a negative correlation between the quantity of CD19‐positive B lymphocytes and the quantities of CD3‐positive T lymphocytes, as well as those expressing both CD4 and CD8 markers (Figure 4G). Notably, the levels of lipid parameters, such as ApoA1, HDL‐C, ApoB, LDL‐C, and CHO, showed a negative correlation with the quantities of CD19‐positive B lymphocytes. This observation aligns with the trend observed in the survival analysis. We also found a significant and positive correlation between the levels of ApoA1 and HDL‐C, indicating that ApoA1 and HDL‐C may have a mutually reinforcing effect.

The results of the in vitro cell experiment demonstrated a significantly higher expression of CD107a (30.9% ± 5.5% vs. 20.7% ± 7.3%, p = 0.036) and TNF‐α (65.2% ± 1.0% vs. 40.8% ± 4.6%, p < 0.0001) protein in NK cells within NK + HDL group compared to the NK group (Figure 5), indicating an enhanced activation of NK cells. External Validation

Survival data from 448 patients across two CLL cohorts including CLLE‐ES (N = 297) and GSE22762 (N = 151) were utilized for validation. Twenty‐two candidate negative regulators of cholesterol efflux genes were acquired from the Molecular Signature Database v2023.2 (MSigDB). The essential clinical characteristics of individuals in the two registered cohorts are delineated in Table S2.

The samples were classified into groups based on high and low scores, utilizing the optimal NRCE cutoff scores. In both CLL cohorts (CLLE‐ES and GSE22762), patients in the low‐risk NRCE score group exhibited significantly inferior survival outcomes than those in the high‐risk NRCE score group (Figure 6A,B).

Following multivariate adjustments according to other common clinical elements, the NRCE score remained significant in predicting OS, with an HR of 3.572 [95% CI, 1.976–6.456] (Figure 6D).

Discussion

This study retrospectively investigated the correlation between HDL‐C levels and the prognosis of patients with ND‐CLL, while also conducting a preliminary mechanistic investigation. We demonstrated that low HDL‐C/ApoA1 levels were related to an unfavorable prognosis in patients with ND‐CLL. Further flow cytometry analysis of 194 peripheral blood lymphocyte subpopulations, encompassing T cells, NK cells, and B cells, revealed a positive correlation between HDL‐C/ApoA1 levels and CD4+ T cells as well as NK cells; conversely, a negative correlation was observed with CD19+ B cells. This suggests that HDL‐C levels can serve as a complementary factor in existing prognostic ND‐CLL models.

As the major component of the cell membrane, cholesterol is crucial in stabilizing cell structure and regulating cell proliferation [15]. Previous studies have shown that alterations in lipid metabolism were involved in certain carcinogenic processes [16]. Similarly, previous epidemiological research has indicated a more robust association between HDL‐C levels and the vulnerability to hematologic malignancies compared to the associations observed with TG, TC, or LDL‐C levels and blood tumors [11]. Low levels of HDL‐C have been linked to the risk of NHL, AML [17], and ALL [18].

The microenvironments found in lymph nodes and bone marrow contribute to the survival and stimulate the growth of CLL cells. Lipids and lipoproteins serve as a key energy supply for the proliferation of CLL cells and are involved in facilitating the communication between CLL cells and supporting cells in the tumor microenvironment. Cholesterol might be crucial in meeting the elevated energy requirements linked to the accelerated proliferation of CLL cells within these specific niches, such as by increasing cholesterol uptake [19]. Previous studies have shown that obesity increases the incidence and mortality of CLL in adults [20], and it has been linked to reduced PFS and OS in CLL patients undergoing hemoimmunotherapy with additional rituximab therapy [21]. Notably, several studies have investigated cholesterol levels in patients with CLL [22, 23]. The analysis of lipid profiles in patients with CLL by Mulas et al. (18 participants) [22] and Lorenc et al. (128 participants) [20] showed decreased levels of cholesterol (including HDL‐C and LDL‐C) in patients with CLL, consistent with our results. In particular, Mulas et al. [19] studied the activation of phytohemagglutinin in the mitosis of CLL cells' growth due to increased cholesterol levels. Reduced levels of TC, HDL‐C, and LDL‐C have been observed in patients with newly diagnosed CLL, and these lipid profile alterations are associated with an unfavorable prognosis [24]. Overexpression of the LDL receptor has been observed in multiple types of cancer, where it facilitates the uptake of LDL‐C and supports the synthesis of new cell membranes to fulfill the metabolic needs of tumor cells [24]. Holger et al. showed that high levels of lipoprotein lipase (LPL) expression were associated with poor outcomes of CLL [25]. Our results revealed that patients diagnosed with ND‐CLL who had reduced levels of HDL‐C/ApoA1 had worse OS and PFS. Statins, widely used for their ability to lower lipid levels, have attracted significant interest due to their possible benefits beyond cholesterol reduction. Reduced cholesterol availability could potentially inhibit the proliferation and migration of cancer cells [26]. The use of statins has been recognized as an independent favorable prognostic indicator in individuals with CLL, regardless of the specific treatment regimen utilized [26]. Statins may inhibit ibrutinib‐insensitive signaling pathways through an increase in plasma cortisol levels [27]. In addition, preclinical studies have demonstrated that statins improve the efficacy of venetoclax and navitoclax in eliminating CLL cells by suppressing glutamine uptake or metabolic activity, along with related downstream signaling pathways [28]. Moreover, clinical trials have reported enhanced responses to venetoclax in patients who were also using statins [29].

However, relevant investigations regarding the potential mechanisms underlying the impact of HDL on tumor prognosis are currently insufficient. As the main apolipoprotein of HDL‐C, ApoA1 plays a tumor‐suppressive role in chronic myeloid leukemia by activating the cholesterol‐exporting protein ABCA1 [30]. Yang et al. [31] found that the mimetics of ApoA‐1 and synthetic HDL‐C nanoparticles could inhibit the growth of B‐cell lymphoma in vivo. Our results also showed that an elevated proportion of CD19+ cells, which indicates the burden of CLL, was associated with decreased HDL‐C levels. Therefore, we speculate that HDL‐C could have antitumor effects in patients with CLL, and a low level of HDL‐C may lead to the progression of ND‐CLL, resulting in a poor prognosis. Low proportions of naïve CD4+ and CD8+ T cells have recently been linked to a decreased duration to first treatment in patients with CLL with indolent disease and those with shorter OS [25]. Previous studies have demonstrated that NK cells in peripheral blood protect and predict a good prognosis in patients with CLL [32]. Natural killer (NK) cells possess the ability to elicit robust antitumor activity. After being activated, NK cells induce cytotoxicity by releasing granules that contain perforins and granzymes, as well as generating proapoptotic cytokines like IFN‐γ and TNF‐α [33]. Our results align with these studies, showing that a decreased level of HDL‐C was found to be linked with a decreased proportion of the aforementioned three immune cell types in patients with ND‐CLL. Reinforced by additional cell experiments, it was demonstrated that HDL augmented the cytotoxicity of NK cells. These observations may partially explain the negative impact of HDL‐C on the prognosis of patients with ND‐CLL in our study.

HDL‐C is a well‐established anti‐atherosclerotic factor, primarily known for its role in reverse cholesterol transport (RCT). Based on this model, HDL‐C elicits cholesterol efflux from cells [34]. The cellular cholesterol content is determined by cholesterol uptake, whereas the elimination of cellular cholesterol is mediated by cholesterol efflux. Prior research has demonstrated that dysregulation of cholesterol metabolism is involved in various cancer cells, including leukemia cells, such as acute and chronic myeloid leukemia, resulting in intracellular cholesterol accumulation [35, 36]. Ceroi et al. [37] identified that the stimulation of cholesterol efflux, mediated by ApoA1, exerted an anti‐leukemic effect in blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasms, which limited proliferation and induced leukemia cell apoptosis. In our study, we demonstrated that patients with CLL with a high score for negative regulation of cholesterol efflux‐related genes had significantly poorer OS compared to those with a low score. These results align with the anti‐leukemic effect of cholesterol efflux mediated by HDL‐C/ApoA. Thus, our findings further support the notion that decreased levels of HDL‐C/ApoA1 are related to an unfavorable prognosis in patients with ND‐CLL.

Notably, the majority of patients in our study received chlorambucil‐ or rituximab‐based chemotherapy, due to the change and progress in CLL treatment. However, only a small proportion of patients received BTKi‐based therapy. In our cohort, risk factors for CLL, such as the IGHV mutational status, del(17p), TP53 mutation, and serum β2M, were not uniformly available in every case. This was primarily due to various reasons, including the high cost of examinations; their absence represents a critical limitation. We aim to incorporate these markers in future research to enhance the reliability and clinical applicability of our findings. In spite of these constraints, our research offers a new outlook on the prognostic role of HDL‐C and proposes a novel method to predict the outcome of newly diagnosed CLL using readily available clinical data. Our retrospective study confirmed that HDL‐C is independently associated with OS and PFS in patients with CLL, suggesting the role of metabolic factors in the biology and prognosis of CLL. The association between HDL‐C and CLL can provide a new variable for subsequent prospective analyses in the future, such as the CLL‐IPI model. Further investigations are required to delve into the metabolism of HDL‐C in patients with CLL and elucidate the effects of lower HDL‐C levels on CLL management. In addition, the prognostic role of HDL‐C should be validated in diverse cohorts and populations.

In conclusion, our retrospective study demonstrated that low HDL‐C/ApoA1 ratios were associated with poor prognosis, including shorter OS and PFS, in patients with ND‐CLL. Furthermore, patients classified by HDL‐C levels had distinguishable immune cell infiltration and tumor burden. Cell experiments in vitro further verified that HDL could enhance the cytotoxicity of NK cells. These results provide a new perspective on the prognostic role of HDL‐C and propose a new method for predicting the outcome of ND‐CLL. However, the underlying biological mechanisms necessitate further exploration.
