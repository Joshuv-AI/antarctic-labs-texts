Design of Nanocarriers for Kidney Targeted Delivery of Nucleic Acid Therapeutics
Authors: Jun Hyuk Lee, Junku Han, Sehwan Park, Hyejung Mok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDriven by recent successful clinical translation, nucleic acid therapeutics are being actively investigated to expand their applications to various diseases, including renal genetic disorders. As life expectancy increases and the burden of renal diseases continues to rise, nucleic acid therapeutics are considered promising alternatives to conventional treatments. This review summarizes key considerations in the design and fabrication of nanocarriers for the systemic delivery of nucleic acid therapeutics to the kidneys. Key aspects of the renal microenvironment are discussed to guide the design of nanocarriers, including conjugates and nanoparticles. This review also covers renal genetic disorders and nucleic acid‐based treatment approaches, including small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) systems, and messenger RNA (mRNA) therapeutics. In particular, recent strategies for kidney‐targeted nanocarriers are highlighted, with an emphasis on their efficacy, challenges, and limitations. Perspectives on the design of nanocarriers for systemic nucleic acid delivery to the kidneys are also provided, offering insights into potential therapeutic approaches for renal genetic disorders.Keywords: genetic disorder, kidney targeting ligand, kidney, nanocarrier, nucleic acid

Introduction

The kidneys are essential organs for maintaining body fluid homeostasis through daily ultrafiltration [1]. When the nephron and tubular structures of the kidneys are disrupted by renal cell damage and cyst formation, caused by genetic mutations or non‐genetic factors, impaired homeostasis can lead to the development of a broad spectrum of renal diseases [2]. In Fabry disease, genetic mutations that cause a deficiency of the enzyme alpha‐galactosidase A result in the accumulation of glycolipids and podocyte injury, eventually progressing to renal fibrosis [3, 4]. Endothelial cells in the glomerular and peritubular capillaries can also be damaged by inflammation, which may contribute to the development of kidney diseases [5, 6]. In addition, chronic diseases, such as sustained hypertension and diabetes, are closely associated with endothelial cell injury and renal failure, resulting in renal fibrosis and albuminuria [7, 8]. Given the high prevalence of chronic diseases and the growth of the aging population, the incidence of these kidney diseases is expected to increase [9]. Currently, small‐molecule drugs and enzyme therapies are used to delay disease progression or alleviate the symptoms of renal diseases. For example, renin‐angiotensin system inhibitors and sodium‐glucose co‐transporter 2 inhibitors can attenuate renal fibrosis and inflammation in chronic kidney disease [10, 11]. However, many of these therapeutics have limitations, including the high cost of continuous treatment and variable efficacy among individuals. In addition, enzyme therapies often require lifelong, frequent, and repeated administration via intravenous injection or infusion, which can elicit infusion reactions and the production of neutralizing antibodies [12, 13]. Renal replacement therapies, including dialysis and kidney transplantation for the treatment of end‐stage chronic kidney disease, also face several challenges, such as donor organ shortages, immune rejection, and physical discomfort associated with frequent dialysis [9]. Therefore, diverse therapeutic strategies need to be developed to treat a wide range of kidney diseases.

Driven by recent successful clinical translation, nucleic acid therapeutics are being actively investigated to expand their applications to various diseases, including renal genetic disorders. Accordingly, nucleic acid therapeutics have been considered promising alternatives to address the limitations of current treatments for renal genetic disorders. Advances in the chemical modification of nucleic acids have improved their serum stability in vivo for durable therapeutic effects and reduced safety concerns, such as inflammation and immunogenicity [14, 15, 16]. In addition, correction of disease‐causing mutations using a gene‐editing system could potentially provide lifelong therapeutic effects after a single administration [17]. By modulating disease‐causing genes, nucleic acid therapeutics may reduce variability in the efficacy of current treatment approaches. However, the delivery of therapeutic nucleic acids to the kidneys remains challenging because of the intrinsic physicochemical properties of nucleic acids, as well as renal barriers encountered after systemic administration. Permeability across the glomerular filtration barrier (GFB) and tubular epithelial barrier is tightly regulated. The architecture of the GFB enables highly selective, size‐dependent (approximately 4–12 nm), and charge‐dependent filtration of molecules [18]. In particular, negatively charged nucleic acids with high molecular weights, such as siRNA (∼14 kDa), ASOs (4–10 kDa), and mRNA (600–10 000 kDa), may have limited permeability through the anionic glycosaminoglycan‐rich glycocalyx of the glomerular filtration barrier [19]. Furthermore, after cellular entry, nucleic acids must be directed to specific intracellular compartments to exert their biological functions. Accordingly, the development of efficient and safe carriers that deliver nucleic acid therapeutics to the appropriate intracellular locations in specific renal cells is crucial for therapeutic efficacy. To date, only a few studies have reported the delivery of nucleic‐acid‐based drugs to address the underlying causes of renal diseases, such as genetic mutations and loss of functional genes.

In this review, we introduce key features of renal cells and intrinsic renal barriers that hinder nanocarrier‐mediated renal delivery after systemic administration. In addition, we discuss renal genetic disorders and their treatment through gene inhibition, gene editing, and gene expression approaches using small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) systems, messenger RNA (mRNA), and plasmid DNA (pDNA). Furthermore, we focus on nanocarriers for kidney‐targeted delivery of nucleic acids, discussing the strengths and limitations of each nanocarrier, types of targeting ligands, and their therapeutic implications. Finally, we provide perspectives on the design of nanocarriers for the systemic delivery of nucleic acid therapeutics to treat renal genetic disorders.

Key Aspects of Renal Cells and Microenvironment

Renal Cells

Glomerular endothelial cells form the inner lining of the glomerular capillaries, as shown in Figure 1a. Although the glomerular endothelium contains fenestrations, the permeability of the plasma components is strictly regulated. The luminal surface is covered with a hydrated carbohydrate‐rich glycocalyx layer, which serves as a dynamic filtration interface. The glycocalyx is primarily composed of negatively charged glycosaminoglycans that modulate the permeability of plasma components. Mesangial cells located in the glomerular capillary walls are closely related to the development of glomerular structures and structural support for capillaries, which provide structural integrity to the glomerular capillary loops [20]. In addition, the contractile properties of mesangial cells enable the organization of the matrix structure and capillary surface area. Lots of extracellular matrix components, such as collagen type IV, fibronectin, and laminin, are produced by mesangial cells. Accordingly, mesangial cell injury leads to the loss of capillary loops in glomerular structures [20]. Because of their phagocytic capabilities, mesangial cells are a strategic location where relatively large nanoparticles (approximately 100 nm in size) in the blood are susceptible to delivery [21]. Although glomerular endothelial cells and mesangial cells can proliferate to repair mild injury, chronic injury can impair these repair mechanisms. Glomerular podocytes are highly differentiated epithelial cells that cover the glomerular capillaries [22]. Both foot processes and slit diaphragms in the glomerulus are formed by podocytes, which feature pores of approximately 12–32 nm in size [23, 24]. In particular, podocyte injury can lead to severe kidney diseases because podocytes have negligible regenerative capacity [25]. Tubular epithelial cells possess greater regenerative capacity than glomerular cells. Both proximal tubular epithelial cells and distal tubular epithelial cells are responsible for the reabsorption of the glomerular filtrate and secretion of toxins [26]. The apical membrane of tubular epithelial cells is characterized by extensive microvilli, which provide a large surface area, and high expression of transporters and receptors, enabling efficient transport and reabsorption [27].

GFB

The glomerular filtration barrier exhibits selective permeability based on the size and charge of various substances (Figure 1b,c). Low‐molecular‐weight metabolites and excess electrolytes pass through the GFB into Bowman's space, whereas high‐molecular‐weight biomacromolecules, such as proteins and nucleic acids, are largely retained because of the size‐ and charge‐selective properties of the GFB [28, 29]. GFBs are composed of four distinct layers: the fenestrated endothelium, glycocalyx, glomerular basement membrane, and podocyte slit diaphragm (Figure 1b). Glomerular endothelial cells possess fenestrae measuring 60–80 nm in diameter [30]. However, the luminal surface of endothelial cells is densely coated with the glycocalyx (0.5–3 nm space), which is a dense, hydrated gel‐like matrix composed of proteoglycans (heparan sulfate and chondroitin), glycoproteins (selectin, integrin), and glycolipids [31]. In addition, the strong negative charge of the glycocalyx limits the passage of biomacromolecules. The glomerular basement membrane, which is approximately 340–420 nm thick, is an acellular meshwork composed of type IV collagen, laminin, and proteoglycans produced by both glomerular endothelial cells and podocytes [32, 33]. They possess a pore size of approximately 2–10 nm and maintain a negative charge, thereby providing primary mechanical support to the endothelial layer. In addition, podocytes cover the outer surface of the glomerular basement membrane with their foot processes, which could result in specific size‐dependent (approximately 4–12 nm) and charge‐selective filtration of molecules [18]. In addition, the neonatal Fc receptors and integrin receptors are expressed on the basolateral membrane of podocytes and can be harnessed for the selective delivery of nanocarriers to podocytes [34, 35].

Tubular Epithelium Barrier

As shown in Figure 1c, molecules in post‐glomerular blood can cross the tubular epithelium through endothelial fenestrae (approximately 5 nm) via transcellular and paracellular pathways. Peritubular endothelial cells form a continuous endothelial layer with tight junctions, serving as a selective route that restricts the movement of low‐molecular‐weight substances and ions. However, these cells also enable vesicular trafficking through diverse membrane proteins and vesicle transport systems [36]. In a previous study, particles larger than 100 nm were delivered to the proximal tubule via a tubular secretion mechanism [37]. Megalin and cubilin are highly expressed on the apical membranes of renal tubular epithelial cells, particularly proximal tubular epithelial cells (PTECs) [38]. Folate is reabsorbed through folate receptors on the apical membrane of PTECs and can be released via reduced folate carriers located on the basolateral membrane [39]. In addition, the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and G protein‐coupled receptors (GPCRs) are located on the basolateral membrane of PTECs to circulate hormones [40]. Accordingly, these receptors have been used for the selective delivery of nanocarriers to tubular epithelial cells.

Permeability in Renal Disease Models

Permeability through kidney barriers can vary in diseased states, compared with healthy states (Table 1). Most particles larger than 100 nm generally do not accumulate in the kidneys of healthy mice, whereas marked nanoparticle accumulation and leakage of macromolecules, such as albumin, have been observed in kidney injury models. While the particle diameter for glomerular penetration was approximately 10 nm in intact glomerular filtration barriers, nanoparticles with a size of 80–150 nm could infiltrate via glomerular structures in the diabetic nephropathy models and the podocyte injury models [41, 42]. In addition, cisplatin‐injected acute kidney injury mouse models showed a 2‐ to 5.8‐fold higher accumulation of nanoparticles with a size of 120–150 nm via increased glomerular permeability compared with those of healthy mice [37, 43, 44]. In lipopolysaccharide (LPS)‐induced acute kidney injury mouse models, nanoparticles with a size of 140–150 nm showed higher accumulation in the kidney by 1.3–2‐fold than in healthy mouse models via elevated permeability of GFB [45, 46, 47]. An adenine diet also elicits injuries to proximal tubular cells, which cause elevated levels of urinary albumin and accumulation of polymeric nanoparticles (approximately 200 nm) in an adenine‐induced chronic kidney disease mouse model [48, 49]. In addition, nanoparticles (120–200 nm) have been successfully delivered to the proximal tubular epithelial cells and peritubular capillaries in acute and chronic kidney injury models [44, 50, 51, 52]. Surgical methods, including unilateral ureteral obstruction and cecal ligation and puncture (CLP), have also been used to induce kidney injury in mouse models [51, 53]. These surgical kidney injury models also exhibited increased kidney permeability for nanoparticles with a size of 120–200 nm by 4‐fold, compared with those of healthy mice.

Renal Genetic Disorders

As shown in Table 2, mutations in several genes expressed in renal cells lead to various inherited kidney disorders. Congenital nephrotic syndrome is a rare glomerular disease caused by various pathogenic mutations in genes such as NPHS1 (19q13.1), NPHS2 (1q25–q31), and LAMB2 (3p21) [54]. The main clinical manifestations of congenital nephrotic syndrome include proteinuria and hyperlipidemia, which result from impairment of the glomerular filtration barrier [54]. Fabry disease, with a prevalence of approximately 1/40 000–117 000 is caused by a mutation in the GLA gene on the X‐chromosome, which encodes alpha‐galactosidase A [3]. Deficiency of alpha‐galactosidase A in Fabry disease leads to the abnormal accumulation of specific glycosphingolipids (Gb3) within lysosomes in podocytes, endothelial cells, and mesangial cells, as shown in Figure 2a [4, 55]. This lysosomal storage disorder affects multiple organs and causes significant renal cellular damage, leading to progressive deterioration of renal function. Renal manifestations include proteinuria and a reduced glomerular filtration rate, along with podocyte foot process effacement and disruption of the intricate slit diaphragm structure [4]. Currently, therapeutic enzymes, e.g., agalsidase alfa and pegunigalsidase alfa, have been administered every two weeks via intravenous injection and infusion for the treatment of Fabry disease, which might be limited by high costs, accessibility, and a significant time burden [56, 57]. In addition, these enzyme replacement therapies could also elicit infusion reactions and production of neutralizing antibody, which could alleviate therapeutic efficacy [12]. Alport syndrome is one of the hereditary renal diseases caused by mutations in the genes (COL4A3, COL4A4, and COL4A5) encoding collagen type IV α3, α4, and α5 chains [58]. Because of their essential roles in the formation and functions of the basement membrane, mutant collagen type IV α3, α4, and α5 chain proteins cause splitting of the glomerular basement membrane, podocyte effacement, and kidney fibrosis. Accordingly, mutations in COL4A5 (Xq22) correspond to ∼85% of patients with Alport syndrome who can develop end‐stage renal disease. Alport syndrome has been treated with angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (e.g., ramipril and lisinopril) for symptomatic relief, e.g., lowering proteinuria levels and retarded renal functional decline by lowering the intraglomerular pressure [59]. However, these approaches cannot fully restore advanced fibrosis and the loss of podocytes [60].

There are three representative genetic disorders of tubular epithelial cells: Dent disease, nephronophthisis (NPHP), and autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD). Dent disease, caused by mutations in CLCN5 and OCRL1 genes on chromosome X, is a hereditary kidney disease that manifests in males and has a milder phenotype in female carriers [61, 62]. The main symptoms of Dent's disease are proximal tubule dysfunctions, such as proteinuria and hypercalciuria. Because the CLCN5 gene encodes an electrogenic Cl−/H+ exchanger on endosomal membranes, impaired expression of CLCN5 diminishes the capacity to reabsorb proteins via reduced endocytic activity in proximal tubular cells. NPHP is caused by mutations in the genes from NPHP1 to NPHP16 [63]. Its main symptoms are loss of tubular function and chronic renal failure via disruption of the tubular basement membrane and the formation of cysts, usually in patients under 30 years of age [64]. As the most prevalent hereditary renal disorder, ADPKD (> 10 million patients worldwide) is characterized by enlarged kidneys and loss of kidney function, such as decreased glomerular filtration rate [65, 66]. The causes of ADPKD are mainly the mutations in the genes PKD1 (approximately 75%) and PKD2 (approximately 15%), which encode polycystin1 and polycystin2 [66]. Deficiency of polycystin1 and polycystin2 results in cystogenesis in the kidneys and reduced renal function. There are diverse and complex mutations in PKD1 and PKD2 that complicate diagnostic processes that are time‐consuming and costly [67]. For the treatment of ADPKD, ACE inhibitors and vasopressin V2 receptor antagonists have been used to delay cyst formation [68, 69]. However, use of these treatments could be restricted to specific patient groups, including pregnant women and those suffering from advanced liver disease [70].

Nucleic Acid Therapeutics

Gene Inhibition by siRNA and ASO

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) is a short, synthetic double‐stranded RNA that can be loaded into the RNA‐induced silencing complex in the cytoplasm, resulting in the cleavage of complementary mRNA in a sequence‐specific manner [71]. siRNAs are considered promising therapeutic candidates as well as genetic tools. Upregulation of troublesome genes in diseased tissues can be suppressed by systemic siRNA treatment. Since the first clinical translation of siRNA therapeutics in 2018, diverse siRNA‐based therapeutics have been tested in clinical trials to treat genetic disorders and incurable diseases [72]. To date, eight siRNA therapeutics (patisiran, givosiran, lumasiran, inclisiran, vutrisiran, nedosiran, fitusiran, and plozasiran) have been approved for clinical use, and more than 35 candidates are currently in clinical development [73, 74, 75]. In our previous study, the inhibition of globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) synthase by small interfering RNA (siRNA) was examined as a feasibility study for substrate‐reduction therapy in Fabry disease [76, 77]. Six siRNAs were designed against Gb3 synthase exon3 to inhibit Gb3 synthase expression. The most potent symmetric 19‐mer siRNA (sense strand 5′‐GGAGAAUAAUCAAUAGAAUTT‐3′, antisense strand 5′‐AUUCUAUUGAUUAUUCUCCTT‐3′) with 2 deoxythymidine overhangs at both 3`‐ends was selected to be incorporated into lipid nanoparticles [76]. To improve the endosomal escape efficiency, histidine polypeptides of different length (5 mer, 10 mer, and polymer) were encapsulated together with siRNAs (Figure 2b). Treatment with lipid nanoparticles results in higher transfection efficiency in endothelial cells compared to that of podocytes [72]. In addition, Gb3 accumulation was markedly reduced in both cell lines. Notably, delivery of Gb3 synthase‐targeting siRNA restored the gene expression profiles disrupted by GLA deficiency to near‐wild‐type levels. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that siRNA‐mediated inhibition of Gb3 synthase represents a promising therapeutic approach for Fabry disease.

In an ADPKD model, inhibition of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)‐dependent protein deacetylase sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) using nicotinamide and SIRT1‐targeting siRNA was shown to prevent renal cyst formation [78]. However, in this study, SIRT1‐targeting siRNA was used not to develop a nucleic acid therapeutic for ADPKD, but rather to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying renal cyst growth driven by epithelial cell proliferation. Smad4 siRNA has also been examined as a therapeutic agent for the suppression of renal fibrosis. After the intraperitoneal injection of folate to prepare a renal fibrosis model, Smad4 siRNA (5 nmol) was intravenously injected nine times over 3 weeks [79]. After 21 days, Smad4 expression in renal tissue was significantly reduced following treatment with Smad4‐targeting siRNA, leading to an approximately 50% reduction in the renal fibrotic area. This study demonstrated the in vivo therapeutic potential of Smad4‐targeting siRNA for renal fibrosis, even without chemical modification of the siRNA or the use of additional delivery carriers.

ASOs are also a promising strategy for inhibiting the expression of disease‐associated genes and thereby alleviating disease symptoms. ASOs bind to complementary sequences in target mRNAs and subsequently suppress gene expression through RNase H‐mediated degradation or splice modulation [80]. Currently, nine ASO drugs (i.e., fomivirsen, mipomersen, nusinersen, inotersen, tofersen, eplontersen, imetelstat, olezarsen, and donidalorsen) have been translated clinically [81, 82, 83, 84, 85, 86, 87, 88, 89]. For renal diseases, recent studies have reported the feasibility of ASO‐based approaches for the treatment of ADPKD [90, 91]. For in vivo stability, ASOs chemically modified against angiotensinogen or mammalian target of rapamycin complex1 (mTORC1) were injected without carriers [90, 92]. Intraperitoneal administration of ASO against angiotensinogen or mTORC1 at a dose of 50–100 mg/kg per week for 12 weeks resulted in decreased cyst size and improved renal function in a murine model of polycystic kidney disease [90, 92]. Another study demonstrated the therapeutic effect of ASO against asparagine synthetase in the treatment of ADPKD [91]. In a murine model of polycystic kidney disease, intraperitoneal administration of an ASO targeting asparagine synthetase at 50–100 mg/kg/week for 2 months resulted in reduced kidney size and improved renal function. Together, these studies suggest that ASO‐mediated inhibition of disease‐related genes may represent a promising strategy for improving renal function in ADPKD. To treat Alport syndrome, a chemically modified ASO against the COL4A5 gene was injected subcutaneously at a dose of 50 mg/kg once or twice a week for 17 weeks in a COL4A5 mutant murine model [93]. To date, most therapeutic ASOs have been administered without delivery carriers via intraperitoneal or subcutaneous routes, resulting in target gene suppression and improved renal function in murine models of renal disease. It is conceivable that the therapeutic efficacy of ASOs could be further enhanced through the development of appropriate nanocarriers.

Gene Editing by CRISPR System

CRISPR is a promising gene‐editing system for permanently correcting mutated genes [94]. To achieve gene correction, both CRISPR‐associated protein 9 (Cas9) and guide RNAs must be delivered into the nucleus. Accordingly, efficient delivery of the CRISPR system is crucial for its biological activity. Representative delivery strategies include electroporation, viral carriers, and nonviral carriers, which can deliver plasmid DNA encoding Cas9 and guide RNAs, Cas9 mRNA together with guide RNAs, or ribonucleoprotein complexes composed of purified Cas9 protein and guide RNAs into cells. Since the clinical approval of the first CRISPR‐based therapeutic (Casgevy) for the treatment of sickle cell disease in 2023, this gene‐editing technology has been widely investigated as a potential therapeutic approach for kidney disorders such as ADPKD and Alport syndrome [66, 95, 96]. Considering approximately 75% of ADPKD cases are due to mutations in PKD1 gene, correction of this gene may be promising and permanent therapeutic strategy for the treatment of ADPKD [95]. For the treatment of Alport syndrome caused by variants of collagen IV genes, COL4A5 in Xq22 and COL4A3 in 2q36‐37 were corrected by transfection of plasmid DNA with guide RNA and Cas9 via electroporation [97]. Base editing was performed in COL4A5 (1871G>A) and COL4A3 (2567G>A) to substitute glycine at position 624 in COL4A5 and glycine at position 856 in COL4A5 with aspartic acid and glutamic acid, respectively, in podocytes derived from urine of patients with Alport syndrome [97]. These studies demonstrate the therapeutic potential of delivering CRISPR systems into cells via electroporation or microinjection for the treatment of genetic kidney disorders in vitro. However, to date, few studies have reported therapeutic recovery in genetic kidney disorders following in vivo systemic injection of such CRISPR systems.

Gene Expression by mRNA and pDNA

Since the development of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) vaccine, mRNA therapeutics have shown remarkable progress in cancer immunotherapy, influenza vaccines, and COVID‐19 vaccines. To date, four mRNA vaccines targeting either severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) or respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) have been approved for clinical use [98, 99, 100, 101]. In addition, more than 30 mRNA‐lipid nanoparticle‐based therapeutics are currently undergoing clinical trials for infectious diseases and cancer [102, 103]. The production of functional proteins by the administration of mRNAs in lipid nanoparticles is a promising strategy for recovering missing genes in renal cells and improving their functions. To restore the impaired enzyme function caused by mutations in the GLA gene for the treatment of Fabry disease, enzyme replacement therapy still has unmet medical needs. Therefore, mRNA of GLA gene could be considered as an alternative to enzyme replacement therapy [3]. Alpha‐galactosidase A mRNA was encapsulated within lipid nanoparticles and administered intravenously to Fabry disease mice. This study clearly demonstrated that mRNA administration successfully produced functional alpha‐galactosidase proteins and reduced Gb3 accumulation without notable immune responses. Cystinosis, a genetic disorder caused by the mutation of the cystinosin gene, can be treated with cystinosin mRNA to produce functional proteins [104]. After cystinosin mRNA was injected into zebrafish embryos, renal function was restored, as evidenced by increased proximal tubular reabsorption and reduced proteinuria. Although this approach remains at an early stage of research, restoring deficient or dysfunctional proteins by administering wild‐type functional mRNA may represent a promising therapeutic alternative for patients with mutated or missing genes, provided that reliable and tissue‐specific delivery carriers are developed.

Loss of gene function due to gene deficiency can also be reversed by pDNA delivery. A pDNA vaccine targeting SARS‐CoV‐2, ZyCoV‐D, was authorized for clinical use in India [105]. Previously, naked pDNA α‐galactosidase A was injected into the left kidney of Fabry mice, which showed increased α‐galactosidase A activity and attenuated Gb3 accumulation [106]. These findings highlight the feasibility of plasmid‐based gene delivery to achieve therapeutic effects in genetic kidney disorders. Recently, the α‐galactosidase A pDNA was incorporated into lipid nanoparticles, which were coated with an anti‐E‐selectin antibody, considering Gb3 accumulation induces high expression of E‐selectin [107]. In this study, α‐galactosidase A pDNA was successfully delivered into immortalized Fabry endothelial cells, resulting in an approximately eightfold increase in α‐galactosidase A activity compared with the untreated control. Using these delivery systems, pDNA can be delivered systemically, for example via intravenous or subcutaneous administration, rather than by direct injection into the kidney. However, few studies have reported therapeutic recovery in genetic kidney disorders following in vivo systemic administration of pDNA.

Nanocarriers

There is a growing interest in nucleic acid therapeutics as a promising alternative that could overcome the drawbacks of existing treatments. Nevertheless, the kidney remains a challenging target organ for the efficient and selective delivery of nucleic acids, highlighting the need for the development of appropriate nanocarriers [108]. As shown in Figure 3, several parameters, including carrier type, therapeutic nucleic acids, spacers, conjugation chemistry, and targeting ligands, should be considered in the design of nanocarriers for kidney‐targeted delivery. The selection of a nucleic acid cargo depends on the mechanisms of the target kidney disorders. To inhibit the expression of the mutant COL4A5 gene, siRNA and ASO were selected for the treatment of Alport syndrome. CRISPR‐Cas systems can be used to correct mutated genes, e.g., the PKD1 gene for ADPKD therapy. To recover activity of nonfunctional proteins, mRNA and pDNA can be chosen as therapeutics, e.g., mRNA of the GLA gene for Fabry disease. The carrier type could be selected considering the target renal cell types, renal permeability, and kinds of nucleic acid payload. Conventionally, conjugates, nanoparticles, and viral carriers have been investigated for the delivery of nucleic acid‐based therapeutics.

Design of diverse nanocarrier platforms for kidney‐targeted delivery.

Conjugates

Nucleic acid‐based conjugates can be designed to be less than 10 nm in diameter, enabling preferential renal accumulation after systemic administration. As shown in Figure 1b,c, nucleic acid‐based conjugates < 10 nm in size might bypass the glomerular filtration barriers and be delivered to podocytes. Kidney targeting ligand‐nucleic acid conjugates can be a promising strategy for kidney‐targeted delivery. To improve serum stability and prolong the systemic circulation of conjugates, chemical modification of nucleic acids and conjugation to stealth polymers such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) can be considered. In addition, functional groups for covalent attachment should be introduced into nucleic acid therapeutics. Conventionally, functional groups such as primary amines, thiols, and azides have been incorporated at the termini of siRNAs and ASOs. Recently, 22 types of peptide‐ASO conjugates via thiol‐maleimide conjugation were reported for the comparative evaluation of delivery efficiency [109]. Despite successful targeting and renal accumulation, a marked decrease in hypoxia‐inducible factor (HIF)‐1α mRNA by ASO was observed not in the kidney but in the liver. Insufficient target gene suppression in the kidney may be attributed to poor endosomal escape following cellular uptake. Therefore, endosomal escape strategies should be incorporated into the design of nucleic acid‐based conjugates to achieve efficient biological activity. In addition, after intracellular uptake, release of the active nucleic acid payload may be critical for its interaction with intracellular machinery required for biological processing. Cleavable linkers such as hydrazone linkers and disulfide bonds in endosomes and the cytoplasm can be considered for the conjugation [110]. In addition, targeting ligands (RNA and DNA aptamers) or nanostructures (DNA origami structures) can be directly linked to nucleic acid therapeutics via phosphodiester linkers such as aptamer‐siRNA conjugates [111]. Small mirror DNA with tetrahedral structures was linked to p53 siRNA (∼52 kDa), which had a hydrodynamic size of approximately 6 nm. Owing to its small size, the DNA‐RNA hybrid structure showed an approximately 4‐fold increase in kidney accumulation in an acute kidney injury mouse model [112]. Conjugate‐based delivery of plasmid DNA and mRNA has rarely been explored, probably due to their large hydrodynamic size.

Nanoparticles

Nanoparticles, including lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, micelles, and biologically derived nanovesicles, have been intensively investigated as kidney‐targeting carriers [41, 43, 44, 46]. Because nanoparticle‐based delivery is not limited by the size of the nucleic acid cargo, various types of nucleic acids, including siRNAs, ASOs, pDNA, and mRNA, can be encapsulated and delivered to protect them from degradation and clearance during circulation. In addition, functional molecules for efficient endosome escape, such as ionizable lipids, can be easily incorporated together with nucleic acids within the nanoparticles. Recently, LNPs have been extensively investigated for the delivery of nucleic acids because of their biocompatibility and efficient intracellular localization in vivo [102]. LNPs with superoxide dismutase2 mRNA resulted in a reduction in serum creatinine levels and recovery of kidney integrity in an ischemia‐reperfusion‐induced mouse model after in vivo injection [113]. Polyelectrolyte complexes of p53 siRNA and cationic polymers (approximately 100–130 nm in size) demonstrate high intracellular uptake in human proximal tubule cells (HK‐2) in vitro, and these complexes effectively suppress target genes by reducing p53 mRNA expression levels [43]. However, the particles are conventionally 30 nm in size, which could hinder their efficient localization in the kidney after systemic administration. As shown in Figure 1b,c, to deliver nucleic acids to podocytes, drug carriers < 10 nm in size have been designed to bypass the glomerular filtration barriers. Particles with a molecular weight of 40 000–50 000 Da (approximately < 5.5 nm) typically freely penetrate the glomerular filtration barriers and are excreted in the urine. Nanoparticles exceeding 10 nm cannot bypass GFB under normal physiological conditions. Because of the overall negative charge of the barrier, positively charged particles tend to be captured by the filtration barrier significantly faster than negatively charged particles. However, because mesangial cells are located near the renal endothelium, particles 60–70 nm in size can penetrate endothelial fenestrae and accumulate within the mesangium. In a previous study, ∼75 nm of nanoparticles were optimal for targeted delivery to kidney mesangium, while larger particles (∼130 nm) showed low kidney localization [114]. In addition, particles below 200 nm can be delivered to tubular epithelial cells via endocytosis. It is also considered that the loading amount of nucleic acids in typical siRNA LNPs is less than 10% of total weight, while more than 90% of the particles consist of lipids [115]. Except for CRISPR‐based gene editing therapy, other nucleic acid‐based therapeutics, e.g., siRNAs and ASO should be administered repeatedly. Accordingly, degradability and clearance of particles should be considered in the design of nanocarriers. In addition, strategies to elevate loading amounts of nucleic acids in nanocarriers should be investigated.

Viral Carriers

Since the first clinical translation of viral gene therapy drug, Glybera, in 2012, a wide range of adeno‐associated virus (AAV) carriers have been popularly harnessed for the delivery of therapeutic nucleic acids [116]. In particular, AAVs have been developed for kidney‐targeted delivery via optimization of viral capsid proteins not only for inflammatory kidney disease models but also for healthy mice [117, 118, 119]. Previously, the feasibility of AAV9 with the Cas9 gene and guide RNA was investigated to correct mutations in the PKD1 gene in ADPKD [95]. This therapeutic AAV9 was delivered to the liver and kidney, and led to reduced cystic growth and improved liver function after 3 and 6 months with a single dose of AAV9 (1 × 1011–1 × 1012 vector genomes (vg) per intravenous injection) in vivo. In addition, another group selected pathogenic mutations of the PKD1 gene from patients with ADPKD and utilized AAV8 to deliver Cas9 and guide RNA for the pathogenic missense mutation in PKD1 [66]. This missense variant was successfully corrected using the AAV8‐CRISPR Cas9 system in primary renal epithelial cells. After the injection of tamoxifen into the cystic kidney model, AAV8 (8 × 1011 vg per mouse) was administered via intraperitoneal injection. However, cyst reduction was observed only after approximately 2.5 months, likely because hepatic transduction efficiency was higher than renal transduction efficiency owing to the strong liver tropism of AAV8. Accordingly, achieving kidney‐selective localization of AAVs with minimal liver tropism remains challenging. Several issues also remain to be addressed, including high‐dose toxicity and the risk of reduced efficacy due to neutralizing antibodies upon re‐administration [120, 121].

Kidney Targeting Ligands

Table 3 shows several targeting ligands to deliver drugs to kidney tissues. Because there are only a limited number of kidney‐targeted delivery systems loaded with nucleic acids, studies involving delivery systems loaded with non‐nucleic acid drugs but targeting kidney tissues were also included. To deliver drugs to renal tubular epithelial cells, megalin receptor‐interacting peptides have been widely used as conjugates and nanoparticle coatings for megalin receptor‐mediated endocytosis. A cysteine‐containing peptide (COOH‐C(KKEEE)3K‐NH2) was conjugated to DSPE‐PEG‐maleimide via a thioether bond to form micelles (approximately 15 nm) for kidney‐targeted delivery [122]. Kidney‐targeted micelles showed approximately 1.4‐fold higher accumulation in the kidney than non‐targeted micelles. For kidney‐targeted delivery of ASO, the carboxyl groups of the peptide sequences (COOH‐C(KKEEE)3K‐NH2) were functionalized with maleimide to conjugate thiol‐modified ASOs [109]. The HIF‐1α ASO‐peptide conjugates showed 10‐fold higher kidney accumulation than unmodified ASO after intravenous injection into a healthy mouse model. A cysteine‐containing peptide (COOH‐C(KKEEE)3K‐NH2) was also conjugated to thiol‐modified siRNAs via cleavable disulfide bonds [123]. After intravenous injection, the peptide‐siRNA conjugates showed 13‐fold higher accumulation than naked siRNA. Another megalin receptor‐interacting peptide (CLPVASC), which was observed in the glomeruli and tubules via phage display in a previous study, has been widely used in the design of kidney‐targeted carriers [41, 51, 53, 124]. Previously, kidney targeting peptide (NH2‐KCSAVPLC‐COOH)‐coated zeolite nanoparticles (approximately 120 nm; −17 mV) showed higher internalization by approximately 4‐fold than non‐targeted nanoparticles after 1–3 h of incubation in murine renal tubular epithelial cells (TCMK‐1) [51]. In the acute kidney injury model, the expression level of kidney injury molecule‐1 (KIM‐1; a phosphatidylserine receptor) is notably elevated in the apical membranes of proximal tubular epithelial cells compared to normal kidneys [125, 126]. Accordingly, KIM‐1 receptors can be facilitated for targeted delivery to tubular epithelial cells in acute kidney injury models. KIM1 interacting peptides were selected via alpha‐fold‐based virtual screening, and their delivery efficiency was evaluated in human kidney tubular cell lines (HK‐2 cells) in vitro and in a cisplatin‐induced acute injury mouse model in vivo [44, 126]. After surface immobilization of KIM‐1 interacting peptides onto liposomes (approximately 125 nm), the kidney accumulation of KIM‐1‐liposomes was elevated by approximately 2.5‐fold compared with that of naked liposomes in vivo [44].

To deliver microRNA33 for the treatment of kidney fibrosis, microRNA33 peptide nucleic acids (PNA) were conjugated with carbohydrates interacting with renal tubular cells [127]. The carbohydrate‐microRNA33 PNA conjugates were examined in healthy and fibrotic mouse models in terms of kidney accumulation and target gene regulation. After subcutaneous injection into wild‐type mice, carbohydrate‐microRNA33 PNA conjugates showed approximately 3.2‐fold higher kidney accumulation than naked microRNA33 PNA. In addition, the accumulation of carbohydrate‐microRNA33 PNA conjugates in folate‐induced kidney injury models was 8‐fold higher than that in wild‐type mice after subcutaneous injection. This study indicates that delivery efficiency via megalin receptor‐ or cubilin receptor‐mediated endocytosis could be elevated in kidney injury models compared to healthy models. To deliver the kinase inhibitor dactolisib to tubular epithelial cells via folate receptor‐targeted delivery, folate‐PEG‐dactolisib conjugates were prepared [128]. After incubation of the conjugates with cells, HK‐2 cells showed a much higher uptake than A549 cells in vitro. In addition, higher accumulation of folate‐PEG‐dactolisib conjugates was observed in the kidney using confocal microscopy than in the liver and spleen after intraperitoneal injection in Pkd1 deletion mice, an autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease model, following 1.5 h of incubation. Previously, PLGA‐based nanoparticles mimicking the glycan surface of viral particles, ranging from 150 to 200 nm, showed proximal tubular targeting [53, 129].

For podocyte targeting, several targeting ligands, e.g., cyclic RGD peptides to target αvβ3 integrin receptors and albumin‐based nanoparticles to target neonatal Fc Receptors (FcRn), have been reported [130]. Due to the constraints of the glomerular filtration barrier, delivery systems targeting podocytes are frequently developed in the form of nucleic acid‐ligand conjugates. To deliver the immunosuppressant tacrolimus to the kidney, the RGD peptide was coated onto albumin‐tacrolimus nanoparticles [35]. In this study, RGD‐coated albumin‐tacrolimus nanoparticles with a size of approximately 120 nm and RGD‐albumin‐tacrolimus conjugates were prepared using EDC/NHS chemistry and were comparatively examined for podocyte delivery. After 24 and 48 h of intravenous injection, RGD‐albumin‐tacrolimus conjugates exhibited higher kidney accumulation than RGD‐coated albumin‐tacrolimus nanoparticles. Considering the expression level of vascular cell adhesion molecule‐1 (VCAM‐1) on the surface of endothelial cells is elevated by inflammation, VCAM‐1 interacting peptide (VHPKQHRGGSKGC) has been harnessed to deliver drugs to glomerular endothelial cells and podocytes [131]. Lipid nanoparticles (diameter: 114.0 ± 1.85 nm; surface charge: 11.86 ± 0.95 mV) coated with VCAM‐1 interacting peptides showed ∼2–3‐fold higher intracellular uptake for mouse glomerular endothelial cells and podocytes [131]. Albumin‐drug conjugates for targeted delivery to podocytes via neonatal Fc receptor‐mediated endocytosis and methylprednisolone–albumin conjugates have been prepared [34]. The mRNA expression level of the Fc receptor in human podocytes was approximately 8‐fold higher than that in HK‐2 cells and 400‐fold higher than that in vascular smooth muscle cells. Accordingly, methylprednisolone‐albumin conjugates showed higher intracellular uptake in human podocytes and kidney accumulation after intravenous injection in vivo. Not only the direct attachment of targeting ligands but also surface engineering for in situ binding of targeting ligands during circulation in vivo can be harnessed for kidney targeting. Recently, the surface of LNPs was modified with cationic lipid (1,2‐dioleoyl‐3‐trimethylammonium‐propane; DOTAP) to adsorb vitronectin in plasma for targeted delivery to renal cell carcinoma via integrin/vitronectin receptor‐mediated endocytosis [132].

After the selection of the targeting ligands, they are attached to nanocarriers and conjugates via covalent and non‐covalent interactions. After the formulation of nanocarriers using maleimide‐functionalized polymers such as maleimide‐poly(ethylene glycol)‐block‐poly(lactic‐co‐glycolic acid) (PEG‐PLGA), cysteine‐containing peptides can be readily conjugated onto the nanocarriers in an aqueous solution [133]. Conjugation of targeting peptides to nanocarriers via click reactions has also been widely examined [134]. Carboxyl groups or primary amine groups on the surface of nanocarriers can be modified with azide groups via carbodiimide chemistry using azido‐amino or azido‐carboxylic acid‐functionalized molecules such as azido‐aminopropane and tert‐butyloxycarbonyl‐4‐azido‐phenylalanine [134, 135]. The alkyne‐modified peptides and azide‐modified nanocarriers were mixed under click reaction conditions in the presence of CuSO4 [135]. Several conjugation strategies, such as carbodiimide‐mediated coupling reaction with amine groups and maleimide‐thiol conjugation, have been widely utilized to immobilize targeting ligands on the surfaces of nanoparticles or conjugates.

Current Clinical and Preclinical Studies of Kidney Targeted Nucleic Acid Therapeutics

Although no renal‐targeted nucleic acid therapeutics have yet been commercialized, several candidates are currently undergoing clinical or preclinical evaluation. As shown in Table 4, four clinical trials evaluating nucleic acid therapeutics for renal diseases have been reported to date, including two siRNA therapeutics and two ASO therapeutics [136, 137, 138, 139]. Three of the trials are designed to target proximal tubular epithelial cells, whereas one targets podocytes. To suppress complement component 3b (C3b)‐mediated complement activation in immunoglobulin A nephropathy, a human complement factor I gene was introduced to promote the cleavage and inactivation of C3b. For podocyte‐targeted delivery, the complement factor I gene was delivered using an AAV vector bearing a podocyte‐tropic capsid. This approach is being evaluated in a phase 1/2 study designed to assess reductions in proteinuria [136]. Among the clinical trials identified, two have been discontinued: a trial evaluating an ASO targeting miR‐21 and a trial evaluating an siRNA targeting the p53 gene. According to a previous study, the clinical trial of Lademirsen was terminated due to a lack of sufficient clinical efficacy [138].

Lessons From Previous Studies and Perspectives

To date, the management of genetic kidney disorders has largely relied on symptomatic treatment or kidney transplantation. Accordingly, curative approaches that directly correct the underlying genetic causes are needed to broaden the therapeutic options for renal diseases. The recent success of nucleic acid therapeutics in treating various genetic diseases has sparked growing interest in developing similar approaches for genetic kidney disorders. Nucleic acid therapeutics offer the advantage of addressing the root causes of diseases by modulating the expression of disease‐associated genes, either through inhibition or restoration of gene expression. Furthermore, these drugs can be combined with conventional therapies to elicit synergistic therapeutic effects. However, owing to their high molecular weight and polar nature, nucleic acid therapeutics have limited ability to cross cellular membranes independently, necessitating delivery vehicles that can transport them to specific intracellular sites of action. To enable the delivery of nucleic acid therapeutics, various delivery platforms, including conjugates and nanoparticles, have been developed. However, each delivery system has its own strengths and limitations. Although conjugate‐based delivery systems offer the major advantage of efficient renal targeting with low hepatic uptake, the delivered nucleic acids may exhibit limited biological activity. In many cases, this limitation is attributable to poor endosomal escape, which prevents nucleic acids from reaching their intracellular sites of action. The advantage of nanoparticle‐based systems lies in their high endosomal escape efficiency, which can be achieved by incorporating functional components such as ionizable lipids and can thereby support robust biological activity of the delivered nucleic acids. Conversely, in healthy mice, these nanoparticles often show high hepatic uptake and limited renal distribution, although nanoparticle accumulation in diseased kidneys may be enhanced owing to increased tissue permeability. Overcoming the limitations of each carrier type will be key to maximizing the therapeutic potential of nucleic acid therapeutics for renal disorders.

The development of kidney‐targeted delivery systems can improve therapeutic efficacy, minimize nonspecific adverse effects, and reduce the required dose of nucleic acid drugs. However, current kidney‐specific delivery systems have several limitations. Notably, ongoing clinical and preclinical studies aimed at treating genetic kidney diseases have mainly focused on proximal tubular epithelial cells, often through passive accumulation. In addition, many of these approaches rely on receptor‐mediated pathways, such as those involving the megalin receptor, indicating that delivery is largely restricted to tubular epithelial cells. Consequently, there remains a clear lack of research on targeted delivery to podocytes, despite their importance in kidney pathology. These gaps could be addressed by leveraging recent computational tools for protein structure and interaction prediction to identify novel ligands. Therefore, there is an urgent need to broaden and accelerate basic research aimed at discovering new targeting moieties. In addition, extensive research is required to develop diverse kidney‐targeted delivery strategies, including stimuli‐responsive delivery systems using light, magnetic fields, or ultrasound. Furthermore, previous studies have indicated that renal permeability varies significantly depending on the kidney disease model used. This highlights the need for more detailed comparative studies to determine how these variations differ across animal species. In particular, comparative research is necessary to establish the degree of correlation between results obtained in animal models, such as mice and nonhuman primates, and those observed in humans. Taken together, kidney‐specific drug delivery research remains at an early stage, and the development of efficient carriers is expected to accelerate the advancement of therapeutics for hereditary renal diseases.
