“Advancing cardiac patch viability and functionality: innovations in scaffold design and cellular optimization”
Authors: Ahmed Eliwa, M. K. G. Abbas, Maryam Al-Ejji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cardiac patches represent a groundbreaking approach in the treatment of heart disease, offering a new hope for patients with damaged heart tissue following a myocardial infarction (MI). These engineered patches not only provide essential structural support to weakened heart tissue but also actively promote regeneration by recreating a functional, contractile environment. However, achieving long-term success with cardiac patches requires innovative strategies to address the complexities of the cardiac environment. This review addresses the significant challenge of maintaining cell viability and functionality in the large-scale production of cardiac patches. It aims to advance the effectiveness of cardiac patches for clinical applications in treating heart diseases through various methods, including the incorporation of conductive materials and the use of biocompatible scaffold materials to mimic native cardiac tissue. Strategies to promote vascularization, optimize cell sources, and refine cell culture conditions are also discussed. Additionally, controlled release systems for growth factors, surface modification techniques, and mechanical conditioning during in vitro culture are highlighted as crucial aspects of patch fabrication.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Despite substantial advancements over the last fifty years in the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular diseases, heart disease continues to pose a significant global health challenge, especially in developed countries [1]. A significant contributor is myocardial infarction (MI), caused by partial or complete obstruction of coronary arteries, which cuts off blood supply to the myocardium, resulting in irreversible loss of cardiomyocytes (CMs) [2]. Given the adult heart’s limited regenerative capacity, lost myocardium is typically replaced with fibrotic scar tissue, lacking the contractile and conductive properties necessary for synchronized heart function [3, 4]. As a result, patients with extensive damage often face progressive cardiac dysfunction, arrhythmias, and, ultimately, heart failure [5]. Existing treatments for advanced heart disease, such as revascularization, medications, mechanical devices, and even heart transplantation, have notable limitations, including immune rejection, donor shortages, and constrained long-term efficacy [6–9]. These limitations underscore the urgent need for regenerative strategies capable of repairing the infarcted myocardium and restoring cardiac function.

Among emerging therapies, tissue engineering offers a promising approach by integrating biomaterials, cells, and bioactive molecules to create functional cardiac tissues [10]. The cardiac patch, a TE-based intervention, is specifically designed to deliver therapeutic agents directly to the infarcted area [11]. These bioengineered constructs, created by seeding functional cells into or onto 3D biomaterial scaffolds, aim to support cell organization and foster tissue repair when applied to damaged heart tissue. The ideal cardiac patch mimics the natural extracellular matrix (ECM) of the heart, providing mechanical and biochemical cues that encourage cells to form functional, anisotropic tissues with properties akin to native myocardium [12]. Advances in materials science and engineering have enabled the creation of sophisticated 3D scaffolds that support cell alignment, synchronous contraction, and anisotropic electrical conductivity, crucial features for coordinated heart function [13]. Upon implantation, these scaffolds degrade, leaving behind an integrated, functional tissue patch on the damaged heart [12]. Despite these advances, ensuring the effectiveness of engineered cardiac patches in clinical applications remains a significant challenge [14]. For instance, while patches can be engineered to exhibit the necessary structural and electrical properties in vitro, accurately assessing their functional quality without impairing their performance can be difficult [14]. Engineered patches that are electrophysiologically inactive or unable to synchronize with native cardiac tissue may be implanted with limited therapeutic potential, compromising their regenerative capacity [15].

This review highlights key strategies for maintaining cell viability and functionality, a vital area for advancing effective therapies for heart disease. It underscores the use of biocompatible scaffolds like collagen, fibrin, and dECM, which offer structural support and an optimal biochemical environment for cell attachment and growth. Additionally, it examines incorporating conductive materials like carbon nanotubes (CNTs), graphene, or conductive polymers within cardiac patches, which can improve the integration of patches with host tissue by promoting synchronized electrical impulses across the damaged area, restoring proper heart rhythm and function. Furthermore, it explores novel technologies to encourage vascularization using angiogenic growth factors that stimulate blood vessel formation around the patch. The review also discusses surface modification techniques like, peptide coating, and chemical functionalization with cell-adhesive molecules which can enhance cell retention and reduce immune response, increasing the likelihood of successful integration with host tissue. It also compares the advantages and limitations of various cell sources, assessing factors like differentiation potential, immune compatibility, and ability to survive and integrate within the cardiac environment. Additionally, it delves into methods of strengthening the scaffold’s mechanical integrity, such as cyclic stretching or electrical stimulation during cell culture.

Scaffold material preparation

Cardiac patch engineering employs transient scaffolds in conjunction with cells and growth-promoting signals to achieve effective tissue repair and regeneration when placed on an area of MI [16]. These scaffolds must emulate the ECM specific to cardiac cells [17]. For cardiac tissue constructs, they should closely mimic native heart muscle, maintain viability after implantation, and enhance both systolic and diastolic functions of the damaged myocardium [18]. Scaffolds endowed with appropriate elastic and electrical properties are particularly suited for achieving the heart’s contractile and impulse-conducting capabilities [19]. Consequently, an ideal cardiac-specific construct must exhibit robust contractility, mechanical strength and flexibility, rapid or extensive vascularization post-implantation, and be electrophysiologically stable and non-immunogenic [16]. Various cardiac grafts have been developed through different methodologies, such as cell-seeded scaffolds, biomaterial gels, cell films derived from cardiac cells, and porous or fibrous sheets [20]. The choice of biocompatible scaffold material and the incorporation of conductive materials are critical determinants of achieving the durability and success of cardiac patch fabrication.

Biocompatible scaffold materials

Decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM)

The ECM fulfills numerous essential functions, including providing structural support to cells and contributing to the mechanical properties of tissues [21]. It also regulates cellular behaviors by impacting cell proliferation, homeostasis, survival, morphology, migration, and differentiation [19]. Furthermore, the ECM acts as a reservoir for GFs, augmenting their effects, and enables tissue remodeling during development, aiding in differentiation and the wound healing process [22]. ECM is defined as a non-cellular, three-dimensional macromolecular network that is a crucial component of natural tissue [23]. It consists of three primary elements: glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), adhesive glycoproteins, and fibrous proteins like collagen and elastin, which provide tensile strength [24]. The decellularization process involves the removal of cells from tissues or organs, while preserving the ECM’s composition, structural integrity, mechanical properties, and biological activity [24]. Various strategies have been developed for tissue decellularization, including enzymatic, chemical, and mechanical methods [25]. Common reagents used in cardiac tissue decellularization studies include sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), ethanol with supercritical carbon dioxide, and nucleases [26]. Decellularized tissues are typically characterized by their residual cellular and nuclear content, ECM composition and organization, the presence of macromolecules and matrix-bound GFs, mechanical strength, and preservation of the vascular tree [27].

Decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM) stands out as a promising biomaterial for cardiovascular tissue repair due to its ability to retain essential components of natural tissues, such as proteins, GAGs, and proteoglycans [28]. This retention provides an ideal environment for the regeneration and repair of damaged myocardium. dECM can be applied in various forms, from solid scaffolds that preserve the natural vascular architecture to gelatinous scaffolds that require an additional step to break down the ECM structure [29]. In addition, dECM patches improve cardiac function by either acting alone or in conjunction with cells and/or other biomaterials, including GFs, stem cells, or polymers such as chitosan, silk, and gels. In a study conducted by Sarig et al. [30], the regenerative potential of decellularized porcine cardiac ECM patches was examined in both acute and chronic MI models. Even without supplementary biological agents, the implantation of dECM patches has been shown to stimulate the regeneration of CMs post-scar tissue formation. Furthermore, their results demonstrated that these patches could mitigate further cardiac deterioration, enhance myocardial contractility, and improve cardiac remodeling in rat hearts post-MI. [30].

Research indicates that acellular cardiac ECM scaffolds can activate the body’s inherent tissue repair mechanisms [31]. The interaction between the acellular ECM and host cells is crucial for tissue development and wound healing [32]. Svystonyuk et al. [33] demonstrated that acellular bioscaffolds in a rodent model of myocardial injury redirected cardiac fibroblasts, reducing fibrosis and promoting new blood vessel formation. These preclinical findings were also observed in humans with ischemic heart injuries, where human cardiac fibroblasts on intact bioscaffolds downregulated fibrotic genes and upregulated vasculogenic genes [33]. In another study, Shah et al. [34] explored the therapeutic efficacy of decellularized porcine myocardium slices (dPMS) as acellular cardiac patches in a rat model of acute MI. Evaluations conducted four weeks post-surgery revealed significant improvements in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and myocardial wall contraction. Furthermore, dPMS promoted neovascularization by facilitating the infiltration of host cells within one week of transplantation [34]. Consequently, there was a significant improvement in wound healing and repair at the infarct area.

Hydrogel-based scaffolds

Hydrogels have emerged as one of the most promising biocompatible scaffold materials in cardiac patch engineering due to their unique properties and versatility [35]. These 3D cross-linked hydrophilic polymers can absorb large amounts of water, creating a gel-like substance that closely mimics the natural ECM of cardiac tissue [36]. This similarity to the ECM makes hydrogels ideal for supporting cell growth, proliferation, and differentiation, essential processes in tissue regeneration and repair [36]. The properties of hydrogels are fundamentally determined by the biomaterials employed in their fabrication [37]. These biomaterials influence key factors such as conductivity, cross-linking, density, degradation rates, and interactions with therapeutic agents, including drug release and protection [38]. Furthermore, the manufacturing process is pivotal in shaping the hydrogel’s final characteristics, enabling precise control over parameters like surface topography, size, porosity, mechanical strength, and chemical environment [39]. The polymer selection for hydrogel development is contingent on its ultimate application, taking into account its intrinsic physical and chemical properties [37]. The decision to use naturally derived or synthetic polymers should be informed by the specific cellular interactions required.

Utilizing hydrogels as a scaffold for cardiac patch engineering can significantly enhance the viability and prolong the functionality of cardiac patches post-implantation [40]. This improvement is attributable to the numerous advantages offered by hydrogels [40]. Electrical conductivity is a crucial attribute for hydrogels, essential for facilitating the transmission of electrical signals and ensuring synchronous beating between the implanted cardiac patch and the native myocardium [41]. Introducing conductive inorganic particles into the hydrogel can enhance the electrical conductivity of the hydrogel-based scaffold [42]. For example, gold nanoparticles (GNPs) were integrated into chitosan to create a conductive hydrogel, with its conductivity adjustable by varying the GNP concentration [43]. This GNP-infused chitosan scaffold supported the survival, proliferation, and migration of bone marrow-derived human mesenchymal stem cells (BM-hMSCs), making it a promising platform for engineering electrically active cardiac tissues in vitro [43]. Similarly, incorporating reduced graphene oxide (rGO) into a gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel resulted in enhanced electrical conductivity, significantly improving the contraction speed, viability, and proliferation of cultured cardiac cells compared to pure GelMA hydrogel [44]. By mimicking the electrical properties and biochemical cues of native cardiac tissue, hydrogels can support the integration and function of the cardiac patch, thereby improving its durability.

Biocompatibility of hydrogel scaffolds plays a crucial role in enhancing the long-term viability and durability of cardiac patches within the human body [38]. This compatibility is essential for cardiac patches, as it reduces the risk of inflammation and rejection, ensuring a stable and supportive environment for tissue repair [45]. Due to the remarkable biocompatibility of hyaluronic acid (HA), there is substantial interest in utilizing HA-based hydrogels as carriers for the controlled release of GFs, drugs, proteins, and other therapeutic agents to infarcted myocardium [46]. For instance, positive results were observed when Neuregulin (NRG) was encapsulated in an HA hydrogel scaffold implanted on infarcted mice [47]. Six days post-treatment, NRG remained detectable in the myocardium of mice treated with the NRG/hydrogel combination, whereas it was undetectable when administered in saline [47]. This led to significantly enhanced mitotic activity of CMs and cardioprotection. Furthermore, echocardiographic evaluations conducted two weeks after treatment revealed reduced left ventricular dilation and improved LVEF in the NRG/hydrogel group compared to controls [47]. This controlled delivery helps in modulating the local microenvironment, promoting tissue repair and regeneration while reducing adverse effects [48]. The sustained release of therapeutic agents supports the long-term efficacy of the cardiac patch.

In addition to biocompatibility, hydrogels support the formation of intrinsic self-assembled microvascular networks, which enhance the survival and engraftment of cardiac patch post-transplantation [49]. It has been shown that incorporating angiogenic factors or creating microchannels within the hydrogel can facilitate the formation of a vascular network, ensuring adequate blood supply to the cardiac patch [50]. Therefore, enhanced vascularization within the patch will improve nutrient and oxygen delivery, crucial for the long-term survival and functionality of the cardiac patch [51]. As an example, Schaefer et al. [52] engineered a mechanically constrained bilayer fibrin patch containing co-cultured human blood outgrowth endothelial cells and pericytes to achieve aligned microvessel formation within the hydrogel. When implanted into a nude rat model, this cardiac patch exhibited rapid engraftment and successful inosculation of microvessels within the host vasculature [52]. Furthermore, Garcia et al. [53] developed a device for the minimally invasive delivery of polyethylene glycol (PEG) hydrogel into the pericardial space. This device creates a temporary separation between the pericardium and epicardium, allowing for the controlled release and gelation of PEG, thereby mitigating the risks of embolization, thrombotic occlusion, and arrhythmia [53]. This approach facilitates the minimally invasive delivery of hydrogels that incorporate various cells, pharmaceuticals, therapeutic agents, and nucleic acids to the epicardial surface.

Incorporation of conductive materials

In native myocardial tissue, Purkinje fibers form a network that ensures uniform electrical conduction, leading to coordinated excitation and contraction of myocardial cells [54]. The ECM supports mechanical coupling among these cells, providing the heart with its distinctive electrical and mechanical properties [55]. After MI happens, myocardial cell death, along with biochemical and mechanical alterations, causes electrical conduction blockages in the affected areas [56]. Consequently, impaired cardiac conductivity causes accelerated decline in cardiac function and could potentially causes fatal cardiac arrythmias [57]. Due to the distinctive electrophysiological properties of myocardial tissue, conductive biomaterials have garnered significant interest for mimicking myocardial architecture and treating MI [58]. In addition, the integration of conductive nanomaterials or conductive polymers into cardiac scaffolds has been the subject of numerous cardiac tissue engineering studies [59]. Such modification has shown promising potential to enhance cardiac patch viability and durability through improving electrical conduction, and revascularization in the infarcted area, ultimately promoting better myocardium regeneration [60]. Electroconductive materials are divided into various categories, including carbon-based materials, such as CNTs, graphene derivatives, and carbon dots; conductive polymers like polypyrrole (PPy); metallic nanomaterials such as gold; and newly emerging biomaterials like melanins and silicon nanowires [61]. These materials are predominantly utilized in engineering constructs for both soft and hard tissues, providing the necessary mechanical and electrical characteristics [62].

There are several mechanisms by which the integration of conductive biomaterials can improve cardiac patch viability and functionality. The first mechanism is enhancing the electrical conductivity of the scaffold [63]. This enhancement facilitates the propagation of electrical signals through the infarct area, ensuring better synchronization of the cardiac patch with the heart’s natural electrical activity [64]. In a study conducted by Cui et al. [65], Researchers have shown that PPy/CS hydrogels can autonomously synchronize the contractions of physically separated cardiomyocyte clusters in vitro and enhance electrical signal propagation in scarred cardiac tissue in vivo. In a subsequent study, it was confirmed that action potentials from beating Langendorff-perfused hearts could be converted into electrical currents using PPy/CS hydrogels, suggesting their potential for resynchronizing cardiac contractions post-MI [66]. In a similar study, the injection of a PAMB-grafted (Poly-3-amino-4-methoxybenzoic acid) gelatin hydrogel into scarred MI areas demonstrated effectiveness in enhancing electrical impulse propagation and synchronizing heart contractions, leading to preserved ventricular function and reduced incidence of cardiac arrhythmia [67]. Consequently, this synchronization is crucial for maintaining the regular contraction and relaxation cycles of the heart, leading to improved functionality of the cardiac patch [67].

One significant benefit of incorporating conductive biomaterials into cardiac patch scaffolds is the enhancement of intercellular communication between CMs [68]. This improvement can be attributed to conductive nanomaterials’ ability to activate specific intracellular signaling pathways, which subsequently upregulate and assemble essential cardiac proteins [69]. These cardiac proteins allow for faster signal transduction between cardiac cells through the movement of calcium ions. Consequently, this enhanced intercellular communication facilitates the proper functioning of CMs, essential for effective heart contraction and relaxation [14]. In a study conducted by Li et al. [70], they developed and characterized a hybrid material composed of a natural collagen scaffold integrated with gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) for cardiac tissue engineering. Their results demonstrated that AuNP–collagen scaffolds with appropriate stiffness distribution could efficiently regulate the assembly and formation of intercalated discs-related proteins, such as connexin 43 (Cx43) in cultured cardiac myocytes. Another mechanism by which conductive biomaterials enhance the viability and functionality of cardiac patches is through the promotion of cardiac cell growth and proliferation [71]. This effect is attributed to the ability of conductive materials to establish a bioelectrically favorable microenvironment that supports cellular survival and tissue regeneration [72]. Studies have shown that cells seeded on conductive scaffolds such as gold nanoparticles exhibit higher viability and proliferation rates [71]. As an example, Sridhar et al. [73] integrated various materials, including gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), vitamin B12, and silk fibroin, into a series of polycaprolactone (PCL) scaffolds. These scaffolds were used to co-culture CMs and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs). The scaffolds blended with AuNPs promoted the proliferation and cardiomyogenic differentiation of MSCs [73]. It was also observed that the AuNP-functionalized biomaterials demonstrated superior mechanical strength and resulted in improved contractile properties of the cardiac cells [73]. [75], GelMA hydrogels were combined with gold nanorods to create a novel class of scaffolds. The conductive hybrid hydrogels that were created were able to effectively accommodate cardiac cells, leading to favorable outcomes such as powerful tissue-level beating activity, great cell retention, spreading, and homogenous distribution of markers specific to the heart [75]. A potential advantage of using conductive biomaterials in cardiac patch scaffolds is that they possess a high degree of mechanical stability and flexibility [76]. In fact, this mechanical robustness is fundamental for withstanding the dynamic environment of the heart during both relaxation and contraction [77]. For instance, Olvera and colleagues utilized PCL covered with in situ polymerized polypyrrole to create an electroconductive cardiac patch [78]. They employed a melt electrospinning approach to construct an auxetic patch that could endure the strains and pressures that the human heart experiences during systole and diastole. This auxetic patch additionally showed anisotropic mechanical characteristics, which mimicked the original myocardium’s directional mechanics [78]. Therefore, a mechanically stable and flexible scaffold ensures that the cardiac patch can endure these forces without degrading or losing functionality [79]. In summary, the incorporation of conductive materials into the cardiac patch scaffold enhances electrical conductivity, cell communication, and tissue proliferation and regeneration, while also providing mechanical stability. These improvements collectively contribute to the longer viability and durability of the cardiac patch inside the human body.

Enhancing cell viability and functionality

Optimization of cell sources

The ability of CMs to restore normal heart function following MI is limited since they are terminally differentiated cells with no capacity for regeneration [80]. Therefore, one of the essential functions of a cardiac patch is to deliver stem cells to the infarcted area of the heart. As progenitor-based and stem cell-based tissue engineering technologies evolve, it has become possible to deposit and retain a substantial number of therapeutic cells near the damaged myocardial tissue [81]. This capability enables the cardiac patch to replace scar tissue, prevent scar bulging and LV dilation, reduce wall stress at the border zone of the infarct, improve myocardial bioenergetics, and ultimately facilitate the remuscularization of the heart [82]. Research has explored the use of cells derived from various sources to replace missing CMs in the native heart and initiate cardioprotective actions through paracrine signaling after implantation, with the goal of repairing or restoring myocardial tissues [83]. The transplanted cells have the ability to promote numerous regeneration processes such as angiogenesis, endogenous progenitor cell activation, reduced cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and decreased fibrotic tissue [84].

Optimizing cell sources involves selecting and preparing cells that are ideal for cardiac tissue regeneration [85]. Using high-quality cells such as induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), cardiac progenitor cells, or MSCs enhances the overall functionality of the cardiac patch [86]. These cells can be programmed to exhibit characteristics similar to native cardiac cells, ensuring better integration and performance. One of the fundamental types of cells used in cardiac tissue regeneration is induced iPSCs. iPSCs are pluripotent stem cells capable of self-renewal, created by reprogramming fibroblasts or other somatic cells using specific transcription factors or chemical combinations [87]. Under certain situations, these cells could be induced to differentiate into cardiac cells with the use of different growth factors (GFs) and small molecules, including activin, retinoic acid, Bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), Fibroblast growth factor (FGF), and TGF-β [88]. Heart microstructures, such as sarcomeres, myosin heavy chains, myosin light chains, and mitochondria, are present in CMs that have been produced using iPSCs [89]. According to Ishida et al. [90], the iPSC-CM sheet improved regional function in pig models of MI more successfully than SM and MSC sheets. It has been shown that iPSC-CM sheets release angiogenic cytokines like vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and Hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), which cause angiogenesis in injured tissues when implanted into animal models [90].

In another study led by Yoshiki Sawa, approval was granted to use hiPSC-CM sheets for treating patients with heart failure. Although this approach is promising, it necessitates using only thin cell layers, ~100 μm, due to the absence of an integrated microvascular system, which may impact graft survival [91]. Therefore, various strategies have been devised to enhance the survival of iPSCs-CM, including the implantation of cell sheets on the omentum [92], co-culturing with endothelial cells and smooth muscle cells, and incorporating ECM components like laminin-211 into the scaffold [93]. These techniques have all successfully improved the survival of transplanted hiPSC-CMs and demonstrated positive outcomes in animal models of MI. By refining cell culture conditions and differentiation protocols, researchers can increase the viability and functionality of these cells. This leads to improved tissue regeneration, better repair of damaged myocardium, and sustained cell performance over time. Enhanced cell viability and functionality result in more effective and durable cardiac patches, which can maintain their therapeutic benefits for longer periods and reduce the risk of complications post-implantation.

Furthermore, another type of cell used in cellular cardiac patches is MSCs. MSCs are multipotent self-renewal stromal cells that can differentiate into a variety of cell types, including osteoblasts (bone cells), myocytes (muscle cells), and adipocytes (fat cells) [94]. These cells are primarily found in the bone marrow but can also be isolated from other tissues such as adipose tissue and umbilical cord blood [95]. MSCs are characterized by their ability to adhere to plastic surfaces when cultured in vitro, and their potential to differentiate along multiple lineages under appropriate conditions [96]. They are also unique for having paracrine effects and immunomodulatory properties [95]. MSCs can differentiate into cardiac myocytes through various mechanisms involving specific culture conditions, GFs, and signaling molecules [97]. Exposure to GFs such as transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) and BMPs, particularly BMP-2 and BMP-4, has been shown to promote cardiac differentiation by enhancing the expression of cardiac-specific genes [98].

MSCs have been implanted into the damaged epicardium after being embedded in dECM hydrogel [99], fibrin gels, poly (e-caprolactone) scaffolds [100], or PCL/gelatin nanofibers. When implanted properly, the MSC-based cardiac patch can aid in cardiac repair at the site of injury in animal models of MI and can significantly enhance the overall functionality and durability of cardiac patches. One studied mechanism is the ability of MSCs to enhance vascularization to the patch and the infarcted area [101]. By promoting angiogenesis, MSCs help in the formation of new blood vessels within the cardiac patch. This improves blood supply, oxygen delivery, and nutrient transport to the cells within the patch, enhancing their survival and function over time [101]. As an example, MSCs were seeded into a hydrogel patch and implanted into the infarcted myocardium of a rat model [102]. The hydrogel served as a scaffold to support the MSCs and promote their survival and integration into the heart tissue. The results demonstrated that the MSC-seeded hydrogel patch significantly improved cardiac function and reduced scar formation compared to control groups. It was also shown that the patch facilitated angiogenesis and myocardial regeneration [102].

Another mechanism by which MSCs prolong cardiac patch durability is through improving patch integration via matrix remodeling [103]. MSCs were shown to produce enzymes, such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), to remodel the ECM, which helps in integrating the cardiac patch with the native heart tissue [104]. This remodeling is crucial for the mechanical stability and functionality of the patch. Consequently, this integration is essential for the patch to function as part of the heart, rather than a foreign object, thereby prolonging its durability [105]. In a recent study, researchers created a three-dimensional bioprinted cardiac patch containing MSCs and tested its efficacy in a rat model of MI [106]. The bioprinted patch was designed to mimic the structural and functional properties of native myocardium. The findings showed that MSC-loaded bioprinted patch improved myocardial repair, reduced scar tissue, and enhanced cardiac function. In addition, the 3D structure facilitated the alignment and integration of MSCs with the host tissue [106].

Surface modification techniques

The surface of a material exhibits a unique reactivity and possesses properties that differ from its bulk characteristics. Surface modification seeks to enhance these surface attributes to improve the biological performance of the material, without altering its bulk properties [107]. Liu et al. have suggested that surface properties, including surface energy, charge, wettability, chemistry, and topography, significantly impact the biological performance of solid biomaterials [108]. For cardiac patches, it involves altering the surface properties of biomaterials used in the patches to improve their interaction with biological tissues. This process can enhance cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation, thus improving the functionality and durability of the cardiac patch [109]. Surface modifications can be achieved through various techniques such as chemical coatings, physical treatments, or the incorporation of bioactive molecules.

Chemical modification involves altering the surface chemistry of the cardiac patch to improve its interaction with biological tissues [110]. Functional groups present on the patch’s surface can engage in reactions with specific target groups, leading to the covalent attachment of grafted brushes to the material [111]. In contrast to physical methods, chemical techniques utilize chemical reactions that create covalent bonds between the polymer brush and the substrate surface, ensuring a stable and durable modification [111]. This can be achieved through the chemical grafting of functional groups, such as acetylation, fluorination, silanization, and the incorporation of sulfonate groups, or by altering existing functional groups through oxidation and reduction processes [102]. It can also involve grafting bioactive molecules like peptides, proteins, or GFs [112]. The avidin–biotin binding system represents a sophisticated method of chemical modification for functionalizing cardiac patches [113]. Avidin, a tetrameric glycoprotein derived from egg white, has a molecular weight of 68 kDa and a size of 5 nm [114]. It forms an exceptionally specific and strong interaction with biotin (vitamin H), which has a molecular weight of 244.3 kDa [115]. Avidin features four biotin binding sites, allowing the formation of a highly specific and stable complex with an affinity constant of 1015 M−1. This binding system has been extensively employed in various biotechnological applications, including affinity chromatography, histochemistry, diagnostics, immunoassays, and drug delivery systems [116].

In the context of cardiac patches, the avidin-biotin binding system is a highly effective method for functionalizing the patch due to its exceptional binding affinity and specificity [113]. Initially, the surface of the cardiac patch is chemically modified to introduce reactive groups that can covalently bond with avidin [117]. Once avidin is securely attached to the patch surface, biotinylated molecules, such as GFs, antibodies, or peptides, are introduced [117]. These biotinylated molecules rapidly and specifically bind to the avidin due to the strong avidin-biotin interaction [118]. This binding enhances the biological functionality of the cardiac patch by promoting cell adhesion, proliferation, and targeted delivery of therapeutic agents, ultimately improving myocardial repair and regeneration [119]. As an example, Davis et al. [120] employed a sophisticated technique to conjugate biotinylated Insulin-like Growth Factor 1 (IGF-1) with streptavidin, subsequently attaching it to biotinylated self-assembling peptides (Fig. 3). This biotin–streptavidin–biotin system enabled the secure attachment of IGF-1 to the peptides while preserving their self-assembly properties [120]. Upon injection into rat myocardium, these biotinylated IGF-1 nanofibers facilitated sustained IGF-1 release for 28 days, enhancing protein kinase B activation within the myocardium. Moreover, CMs incorporated into the biotinylated IGF-1 nanofibers demonstrated improved systolic function, a 28% reduction in caspase-3 cleavage, and a 25% increase in myocyte cross-sectional area, outperforming cells embedded within nanofibers alone or with untethered IGF-1 [120].Fig. 3Illustration of the biotin sandwich delivery system. 1 Tethering of Biotinylated IGF-1 to unbiotinylated tetravalent streptavidin. 2 The compound was then conjugated with biotinylated self-assembling peptides to form Biotin-IGF-1-streptavidin complex

The chemical coating of cardiac patch scaffolds with laminin represents a sophisticated surface modification strategy aimed at enhancing the biocompatibility and functional performance of cardiac patches. Laminin, a key component of the ECM, plays a crucial role in cell adhesion, differentiation, migration, and survival [121]. Given that cardiac muscle consists of highly organized fibers, laminin lanes have been meticulously microcontact-printed onto non-adhesive surfaces to orchestrate the alignment of cultured CMs, thereby replicating their natural in vivo arrangement [122]. This spatial guidance compels adherent CMs to form elongated, rod-shaped cells with myofibrils aligned parallel to the laminin pathways [122]. To further refine this technique and better emulate the mechanical properties of native cardiac tissue, researchers have explored different substrate materials. For example, similar cardiomyocyte alignment has been achieved on biodegradable polyurethane (PU) substrates, distinguished for their elastomeric characteristics, which are ideal for myocardial tissue regeneration [123]. Thin films crafted from PU, synthesized using poly (ε-caprolactone) diol, 1,4-butandiisocyanate, and L-lysine ethyl ester dihydrochloride chain extender, were patterned with laminin via microcontact printing and subsequently seeded with neonatal rat CMs. This process yielded a multi-layered, densely packed, and highly aligned tissue that exhibited the ability to contract within the thin films [124]. This approach to surface modification exploits the intrinsic properties of laminin to promote precise cell alignment, organization, and functionality, thereby significantly enhancing the effectiveness and longevity of cardiac patches.

Moreover, surface modification of cardiac patches plays a crucial role in enhancing their functionality and durability, particularly through the use of advanced nanotopography to reduce inflammatory responses [125]. For example, strategies that tailor-engineer nanoscale surface modifications have been shown to mitigate the foreign body response, leading to reduced inflammation and better integration of the biomaterial with the host tissue [126]. A prime example of this is the CorTMPATCH epicardial patch by Cormatrix, which has received FDA approval for supporting and repairing weakened areas of the heart [127]. The CorTMPATCH is derived from porcine small intestinal submucosa that has been decellularized to remove cellular components while preserving the ECM. This process results in a tissue scaffold that retains its inherent micro and nanotopographical features. The nanotopography of the CorTMPATCH is particularly significant because it mimics the natural ECM, which is composed of nanoscale structures that provide a conducive environment for cellular ingrowth and tissue regeneration [128]. These nanostructures play a vital role in regulating cell behavior, including adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation [129]. By providing a biomimetic surface, the nanotopographical features of the CorTMPATCH facilitate the integration of host cells and promote angiogenesis, which is the formation of new blood vessels [129]. This is critical for supplying nutrients and oxygen to the regenerating tissue, thereby enhancing the overall healing process. Moreover, the nanotopography of the CorTMPATCH helps to minimize the inflammatory response [127]. Inflammation is a common issue with implanted biomaterials, as the body’s immune system often recognizes them as foreign objects and mounts an inflammatory response [130]. However, the nanostructured surface of the CorTMPATCH is designed to reduce this immune reaction [131]. The preserved ECM structures are recognized by the body as more natural, leading to reduced immune cell activation and inflammation [131]. This reduction in inflammatory response is essential for the long-term viability of the cardiac patch, as it prevents the formation of scar tissue and other complications that could impair the function of the implanted patch. This approach underscores the importance of surface modification in the development of effective and durable cardiac biomaterials.

Promoting vascularization

The generation of new blood vessels, or angiogenesis, is essential for numerous physiological and pathological processes, such as tissue healing, tumor progression, and embryogenesis. The migration and proliferation of endothelial cells are involved in this complex, multi-stage process [132]. In the context of cardiac patches, promoting vascularization is crucial for ensuring that the cells within the patch receive sufficient oxygen and nutrients after implantation. Without proper vascularization, the implanted cells may suffer from hypoxia, leading to cell death and compromised functionality of the patch. The primary goal of a cardiac patch is to repair or regenerate damaged heart tissue, such as that caused by MI. For the patch to integrate successfully with the host tissue and remain functional over time, it must establish a blood supply that can support the metabolic demands of the cells within the patch. Proper vascularization ensures long-term cell viability, promotes tissue regeneration, and enhances the mechanical stability of the patch. The integration of angiogenic growth factors is one of the main methods of promoting vascularization within cardiac patches.

Incorporation of angiogenic growth factors

GFs are small, soluble polypeptides that function as natural signaling molecules, secreted by cells to regulate vital biological activities such as cellular growth, proliferation, and differentiation. They interact with specific cell surface receptors, which in turn relay growth signals to intracellular pathways, ultimately leading to changes in gene expression [133]. This overall mechanism, where an external molecular signal triggers a cellular response, is referred to as signal transduction. Due to their limited diffusion capacity and short half-life, GFs primarily act in localized environments. Consequently, these molecules are integral in facilitating myocardial repair and regeneration, particularly at the site of infarction, by promoting healing processes within damaged tissue [134]. Examples of GFs encompass both widely acting mitogens, such as FGF and newly discovered factors, like platelet-derived endothelial cell growth factor (PDGF), that target vascular endothelial cells exclusively. A few other substances, such TGF-β, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), and VEGF also encourage angiogenesis through various cellular mechanisms [135].

One of the main angiogenic growth factors used in cardiac patch applications is VEGF. It is a dimeric glycoprotein, which is produced and secreted by numerous cell types. Its binding to the ECM occurs indirectly via linker molecules like heparan sulfate proteoglycans, which modulate VEGF’s bioavailability [136]. In response to hypoxic or inflammatory triggers, CMs release VEGF to orchestrate blood vessel formation and maturation, with a specific effect on vascular endothelial cells [137]. VEGF drives angiogenesis and the formation of coronary collaterals in infarcted myocardium by facilitating vessel breakdown, promoting endothelial cell migration and proliferation, and inducing the formation of endothelial cell-derived vascular structures [138]. A study conducted by Miyagi et al. [139], showed the incorporation of VEGF into cardiac patch was successfully achieved through covalent immobilization to a porous collagen scaffold. The results of this experiment demonstrated enhanced tissue regeneration by promoting cell proliferation both in vitro and in vivo, leading to increased vascular density and reduced scaffold thinning in a rat model of right ventricular free wall repair. Notably, a dose-dependent response was observed, with high VEGF scaffolds displaying a greater abundance of vascular structures compared to low VEGF scaffolds by the fourth week post-implantation [139]. This rapid vascularization addressed the oxygen and nutrient transport limitations, suggesting that VEGF-functionalized collagen scaffolds possess the requisite mechanical and biological attributes for effective heart tissue repair.

One important mitogen for smooth muscle cells, cardiac cells, and fibroblasts is PDGF [140]. PDGF activates a variety of cell types that are necessary for wound healing. It promotes smooth muscle cells’ and fibroblasts’ mitogenicity as well as the chemotaxis of neutrophils and macrophages [141]. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that PDGF increases the synthesis of a number of matrix molecules, including HA [142], proteoglycans, collagen, and fibronectin [143]. Since PDGF promotes collagen matrices to contract in vitro, it may also have a role in wound contraction in vivo during later phases of wound healing [144]. Due to its effective role in wound healing, PDGF is incorporated in cardiac patch to accelerate wound healing and tissue remodeling after MI.

In a recent study, an electrospun gelatin patch was developed using poly(lactide-co-glycolide) acid (PLGA) and porous silica nanoparticles (pSi) loaded with both PDGF-BB and VEGF-A, and the angiogenic potential of this material was evaluated [145]. This structure combines materials with sizes varying from nano to millimeters, each component having a distinct function. A primary delivery vehicle with high loading and growth factor stability is offered by the pSi platform. To encourage angiogenesis, the PLGA coating enables tailoring and prolonged release of GFs. Lastly, localized growth factor delivery and cell adhesion are made possible by the ES gelatin core. VEGF and PDGF-BB were both loaded into pSi and coated with various PLGA formulations to achieve a sequential and sustained release [145]. Ultimately, combined ES patches were placed subcutaneously into a BALB/c mouse model, and the effectiveness of the explanted patches was evaluated by quantitative reverse transcription–polymerase chain reaction and immunohistology. After 21 days in vivo, the results show a synergistic effect with the release of VEGF-A164 and PDGF-BB, supporting enhanced angiogenesis by the overexpression of various cellular receptors (Fig. 4). Carriers and controlled release systems

Controlled release systems are pivotal in enhancing the functionality and durability of cardiac patches. These systems function by incorporating bioactive agents into the cardiac patch matrix, which are then gradually released over a defined period [146]. This sustained release ensures a constant supply of therapeutic agents to the damaged myocardial tissue, thereby enhancing the regenerative potential of the patch [146]. By delivering GFs, cytokines, or small molecules in a controlled manner, these systems can modulate the local microenvironment within the patch, promoting tissue regeneration and repair [147]. The release kinetics can be tailored through various methods, including encapsulation in biodegradable polymers, incorporation into nanoparticles, or embedding in hydrogel matrices [148]. This approach addresses several challenges associated with cardiac patches, such as limited cell viability, inadequate integration with host tissue, and insufficient long-term functionality.

To preserve the bioactive molecules for extended use, carriers can be included in the scaffold when a prolonged immobilization of proteins is necessary [149]. This can be accomplished by embedding microscopic carriers or drug-releasing implants into the scaffolds [150]. For various biological applications, these carriers can be shaped like micro- or nanoparticles or matrices to produce zero- or first-order release kinetics, respectively [151]. For example, hydrogels can be designed with dual phases, each containing different GFs. The outer phase may contain a rapidly degrading polymer that releases growth factor rapidly, while the inner phase contains a more stable polymer that releases another growth factor over a longer period to support long-term tissue regeneration [152]. In an important study, human embryonic stem cells were encapsulated in a dextran-based hydrogel that contained VEGF-loaded PLGA microparticles [153]. PLGA microparticles were created using a double emulsion solvent evaporation process, and the impact of GF release on vascular development was investigated. The release kinetics of microparticles loaded with VEGF demonstrated a burst effect in the first phase, which was followed by a decreased steady-state release. The assessment of the VEGF release from the loaded microparticles was conducted by analyzing the impact of VEGF on the in vitro survival of an endothelial cell line [153].

Furthermore, using controlled release systems to deliver multiple GFs can significantly enhance the functionality and durability of cardiac patches by mimicking the complex signaling environment of the myocardium. This approach involves the design of delivery systems that can release two or more GFs in a spatially and temporally controlled manner. Layer-by-layer (LbL) assembly is one of the effective methods to deliver multiple GFs [154]. This method involves the sequential deposition of alternating layers of materials, such as polyelectrolytes, proteins, or nanoparticles, onto a substrate, allowing for the controlled release of GFs [155]. For instance, by incorporating different GFs into specific layers, the LbL technique enables sequential and spatially controlled release, enhancing tissue regeneration [155]. A study demonstrated a multi-layered vascular scaffold that was created using dual-source and dual-power electrospinning. The inner layer consisted of poly(ethylene glycol)-b-poly(L-lactide-co-ε-caprolactone) (PELCL)/Gelatin fibers, the middle layer was made of PLGA/Gelatin fibers, and the outer layer contained PCL/Gelatin fibers. To incorporate VEGF, PELCL fibers with embedded chitosan hydrogel were produced by suspension electrospinning, while PLGA fibers with PDGF were fabricated by emulsion electrospinning (Fig. 5). [156]. Mechanical and electrical conditioning

Mechanical conditioning refers to the process of applying controlled mechanical forces (such as stretching, compressing, or bending) to biomaterials or tissues during in vitro culture [161]. This technique is used to mimic the natural physical environment of tissues in the body, particularly those that experience repetitive mechanical stress, like the heart. When applied to cardiac patches, mechanical conditioning aims to simulate the forces that the patch will experience once implanted in the heart, such as the rhythmic stretching and contraction from the heartbeat [162]. One advantage of mechanical conditioning is improving the patch strength through enhancing ECM deposition. Mechanical forces can enhance the production of essential ECM components, such as collagen and elastin, which are vital for providing structural support to cardiac patches [163]. Therefore, altering the ECM’s composition can influence the material and structural properties of tissues. This is due to the fact that mechanical stress plays a key role in regulating the expression of ECM and protein-related genes. For example, Lee and von Recum adapted a cell culture platform using N‐isopropylacrylamide (NIPAAm) copolymers for mechanical conditioning, allowing cell sheets to be detached while retaining their conditioned cell-derived matrix [164]. Subsequently, this approach significantly increased contractile protein expression, such as α- and β-myosin heavy chain (αMHC and βMHC) and sarcomeric α-actinin in mouse CMs, demonstrating the benefits of mechanical conditioning in enhancing cellular function [165].

Another advantage of exposing the patch construct to mechanical stretching is improving the patch capability to handle physiological stress [166]. By mimicking the physiological stresses the patch will encounter in vivo, this process adjusts key attributes such as stiffness and elasticity, ensuring the patch is better equipped to withstand the heart’s dynamic forces [167]. In a study by Zimmermann et al. [168], cardiomyocyte-seeded collagen matrices were subjected to cyclic mechanical stretching to mimic the mechanical forces that native heart tissue encounter. The results of the study demonstrated that cyclic stretching improved the alignment of CMs and enhanced tissue-like properties in the patch. In addition, the CMs showed better contractile function and improved organization within the matrix. The mechanically conditioned patches also had increased tissue thickness, better electrical coupling, and enhanced mechanical strength compared to unconditioned patches [168]. Consequently, mechanical stretching enhances the patch’s ability to handle continuous contractions and stress, promoting seamless integration with native heart tissue and increasing its long-term durability and functionality after implantation.

Alternatively, electrical conditioning is a process in tissue engineering where controlled electrical stimulation is applied to engineered tissues or biomaterials to mimic the natural electrical signals that cells would experience in the body [169]. This approach is particularly relevant in cardiac and neural tissue engineering, where tissues like the heart and nerves rely on electrical signals for proper function [170]. In the context of cardiac patches, exposing the construct to electrical stimulation can improve the integration of the patch with native heart tissue. This is explained by the fact that electrical stimulation encourages CMs to align and synchronize their contractions, mimicking the coordinated beating of the heart muscle [171]. In addition, electrical stimulation plays a significant role in potentiating the contractility of cardiac cells embedded within the patch. By simulating the electrical impulses that normally stimulate heartbeats, electrical conditioning improves the contractile force of cardiac patches, making them more capable of handling the mechanical demands of the heart after implantation [172]. In a work by Radisic et al. [173], engineered heart tissues made of neonatal rat CMs and a collagen-based scaffold were subjected to electrical stimulation. The aim of this study was to achieve synchronous contractions in cultured cardiac constructs. To simulate the pulsatile environment of the heart, electrical signals mimicking those of the heart were applied. Within just 8 days of in vitro electrical stimulation, cell alignment and coupling were enhanced, the amplitude of synchronous contractions increased sevenfold, and ultrastructural organization significantly improved. The development of conductive and contractile properties occurred simultaneously and was closely tied to the timing and duration of electrical stimulation [173].

Furthermore, both mechanical and electrical conditioning have potential effects on promoting stem cell differentiation and maturation into functional cardiac cells [174, 175]. While hiPSC-CMs offer a valuable benefit in the construction of cardiac patches, their immaturity limits their effectiveness in regenerating and repairing damaged heart tissue. Therefore, various types of stimulation are required to help these stem cells mature into functioning heart cells. Accordingly, synchronous application of electrical and mechanical stimuli has the potential to promote cellular differentiation and maturation of CMs [176]. This is attributed to the ability of electromechanical conditioning to modulate gene expression and upregulate several cardiac proteins essential for the process of maturation. For instance, to promote better integration of therapeutic cells into heart tissue, a recent study investigated the effects of electromechanical stimulation on cardiac adipose tissue-derived progenitor cells (cardiac ATDPCs) [177]. The cells were stimulated with electrical pulses and mechanical stretching for 7 days, then embedded in a fibrin hydrogel and implanted into the damaged heart area of mice with MI. After 21 days, stimulated cells showed higher expression of key cardiac genes and improved heart function, with a 12% increase in left ventricle ejection fraction compared to untreated groups. Additionally, these cells promoted vascularization and integrated with the host tissue, demonstrating the potential of electromechanical conditioning as a strategy for heart repair after MI [177].

In another study, researchers investigated how mechanical conditioning and electrical pacing could enhance the maturation of hiPSC-derived cardiac tissues [178]. CMs were cultured on collagen-based constructs and subjected to different mechanical stress and electrical stimulation protocols. Results showed that after 2 weeks of static stress conditioning, the engineered tissues exhibited increased contractility, stiffness, cell size, and alignment, along with elevated SERCA2 expression, improving heart muscle function. When combined with electrical pacing, force production further increased, without changes in alignment or cell size, indicating enhanced maturation of the tissues’ electrical and mechanical properties. The study concludes that combining mechanical and electrical stimulation is effective in promoting the maturation of hiPSC-derived cardiac tissues [178].

Summary and future perspectives

This comprehensive review presents innovative techniques in cardiac patch fabrication and scaffold designing. An optimal cardiac construct should demonstrate robust contractility, mechanical durability, flexibility, and efficient vascularization after implantation, while maintaining electrophysiological stability and avoiding immune responses. Different types of cardiac grafts have been developed through various techniques, including cell-seeded scaffolds, biomaterial gels, cardiac cell-derived films, and porous or fibrous sheets. The choice of biocompatible scaffold material and the incorporation of conductive materials or nanoparticles, such as CNTs, graphene, and conductive polymers, into the patch scaffold can enhance electrical conductivity and synchronization with the heart’s electrical activity. It also enhances cell communication and tissue growth, promoting regeneration and mechanical stability. Furthermore, by optimizing cell culture environments, researchers can significantly enhance cell viability and functionality, leading to improved tissue regeneration and repair of damaged myocardium. This ultimately results in more effective, durable cardiac patches that retain their therapeutic efficacy for longer periods and reduce the risk of complications following implantation. Surface modifications of the biomaterials used in cardiac patches can also enhance their interaction with biological tissues, improving cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation, thus boosting both the functionality and longevity of the patches. Controlled release systems also play an essential role in enhancing the functionality of cardiac patches. These systems incorporate bioactive agents into the patch matrix, which are then gradually released over time, ensuring a sustained supply of therapeutic agents to the damaged myocardial tissue. By delivering GFs, cytokines, or small molecules in a controlled manner, these systems modulate the local microenvironment, encouraging tissue repair and regeneration.

In addition to these strategies, promoting vascularization within the patch is crucial for ensuring that the cells receive enough oxygen and nutrients post-implantation. Incorporating angiogenic growth factors such as VEGF and PDGF is a key method of promoting vascularization and supporting long-term functionality. Mechanical conditioning significantly improves the patch structural integrity, mechanical properties, cell differentiation, and vascularization, making it more suitable for implantation in the dynamic environment of the heart. Electrical conditioning, on the other hand, plays an equally crucial role in cardiac tissue engineering. By mimicking the heart’s natural electrical signals, it enhances cardiomyocyte maturation, alignment, and synchronization, resulting in patches that can better integrate with native heart tissue and maintain synchronized, durable contractions post-implantation. These techniques collectively contribute to the increased viability and durability of the patch within the body.

Conclusion

In conclusion, cardiac patches hold the potential to bridge the gap between traditional treatments and regenerative medicine, offering hope for improved heart function and quality of life in patients suffering from MI and heart failure. However, their clinical success depends on sophisticated strategies to maintain cellular viability, promote vascularization, and ensure long-term functionality. Incorporating conductive and biocompatible materials, optimizing cell culture conditions, and leveraging controlled release systems for GFs are essential for creating durable, effective cardiac patches. As research continues to refine these techniques, cardiac patches may soon emerge as a practical and life-saving therapy for patients with limited options in traditional cardiac care.
