MRI features of nipple leiomyoma in a male patient: A case report
Authors: Saya Ando, Kazuna Kawabata, Rio Kou, Kango Kawase, Naotoshi Hatsuda, Mako Ikeda, Mitsunori Kanagaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nipple leiomyoma is a benign cutaneous tumor that arises from the smooth muscle fibers of the musculus areolae and represents the rarest subtype of cutaneous leiomyoma. Since its first description by Virchow in 1854, approximately 50 cases have been reported, with only 20 involving male patients. Imaging findings, particularly the MRI characteristics, have been insufficiently documented. We report a man in his 70s with a 10-year history of left nipple pain. Ultrasonography revealed a mildly irregular hypoechoic mass with increased vascularity. MRI demonstrated a well-defined, smoothly marginated mass with intermediate signal intensity on STIR and T1WI, a hypointense rim, and a slow, persistent contrast-enhancement pattern without diffusion restriction. Core needle biopsy confirmed a leiomyoma. The lesion was managed conservatively at the patient’s request. Recognition of the MRI characteristics of nipple leiomyoma may help the differential diagnosis of nipple lesions.

Introduction

Nipple leiomyomas are extremely rare benign tumors and are one of the least common subtypes of cutaneous leiomyomas [1,2]. Cutaneous leiomyomas are benign tumors and originate from smooth muscle cells, and are classified into 3 subtypes based on their site of origin: pilar leiomyoma, arising from the arrector pili muscle; angioleiomyoma, arising from the tunica media of blood vessels; and genital leiomyoma, arising from smooth muscle in the genital region, including the scrotum, the nipple/areola, and the vulva [1]. Among these, genital leiomyomas are uncommon and nipple leiomyomas are considered the rarest [1,2]. Since the first report by Virchow in 1854, approximately 50 cases have been reported. However, cases in male patients are particularly rare, with only 22 cases identified in a 2023 review [2].

Imaging findings, particularly MRI features, have rarely been described. To date, the literature reports only 1 case involving a female patient [3]. Therefore, the imaging characteristics of nipple leiomyoma remain poorly understood. Here, we present the imaging findings of a nipple leiomyoma in a male patient and discuss them in the context of the existing literature.

Case report

A man in his 70s had experienced pain in his left nipple for approximately 10 years. Eight years earlier, owing to worsening pain, he presented to the Department of Plastic Surgery at our hospital. A skin infection was initially suspected and oral antibiotics were prescribed; however, his symptoms persisted. He was then referred to the Department of Breast Surgery, where a skin biopsy was performed and a diagnosis of nipple leiomyoma was made. Since then, symptomatic treatment with topical corticosteroids has been administered intermittently for several years during episodes of pain. Because of the recent exacerbation of symptoms, MRI was performed to further evaluate the lesion. He had no significant medical history, was not taking any medications, and had no family history of breast cancer.

Physical examination revealed bilateral gynecomastia and a firm, elastic mass in the left nipple (Fig. 1). Mammography confirmed bilateral gynecomastia; however, the nipple lesion was not clearly visualized (Fig. 2A). Ultrasonography revealed a mildly irregular, hypoechoic mass with posterior acoustic enhancement, measuring approximately 0.95 cm, just beneath the left nipple (Fig. 2B). At the patient's request, complete excision was not performed and the lesion is currently being managed conservatively with observation.

Discussion

Nipple leiomyomas are extremely rare benign tumors and are one of the least common subtypes of cutaneous leiomyomas [1,2]. Although leiomyomas typically arise from the arrector pili, vascular smooth, or genital smooth muscles, those originating from the nipple are rare [1]. Nipple leiomyomas typically present as unilateral, slowly enlarging nipple masses, and are generally small (with a mean diameter of approximately 2 cm) [1,2]. In the present case, no significant changes in size were observed over a 10-year period.

These tumors are often associated with pain or pruritus triggered by external stimuli such as cold, friction, or stress. Pain is also a common feature of cutaneous leiomyomas at other sites and is thought to result from the calcium-dependent contraction of smooth muscle cells [1,2,4]. Previous studies have shown a female predominance of approximately 3:1, with the tumor most commonly occurring in middle-aged women [[1], [2], [3]]. Associations with female hormones, trauma, and oral contraceptive use have been suggested as possible contributing factors [2,5].

A PubMed search identified 23 reported cases of nipple leiomyoma in males, including the present case. Cases have been reported in association with both gynecomastia and idiopathic settings, and bilateral nipple leiomyomas have also been reported [2]. Some cases have demonstrated the expression of estrogen and progesterone receptors on histopathologic evaluation [2,5]; however, no correlation with the underlying gynecomastia has been observed [6]. Because immunohistochemical evaluation of hormone receptors has not been performed in all previously reported cases, including the present one, the significance of hormone receptor expression in tumor proliferation remains unclear.

Reports describing the MRI findings of nipple leiomyomas are extremely limited, with only 1 female patient identified in our literature search [3]. Similar to ours, the lesion was well-defined with intermediate signal intensity on both T1WI and T2-weighted images (T2WI) and demonstrated a slow and persistent enhancement pattern on dynamic contrast-enhanced imaging that may be characteristic of nipple leiomyoma. In contrast to the previous report, our case differed in that the lesion was not clearly visualized on DWI, and rim enhancement was not observed on post-contrast images.

Among genital leiomyomas, including nipple leiomyomas, vaginal leiomyomas have been more frequently evaluated using MRI. These typically present as well-circumscribed lesions with low signal intensity on both T1WI and T2WI, and show strong early enhancement with persistent contrast enhancement [7]. However, it has been reported that the signal intensity on T2WI and enhancement patterns may vary depending on the proportion of capillaries and cellular components within the lesion [7]. This variability may account for the differences observed in our case. Although the lesion in our case demonstrated a slow and persistent enhancement pattern similar to a previously reported case of nipple leiomyoma, it differed in that rim enhancement was absent and the lesion was poorly visualized on DWI. In addition, the small size of the lesion may have contributed to these findings. Further studies are needed to investigate potential sex-related differences and to evaluate the relationship between imaging findings and hormone receptor expression.

Differential diagnoses of nipple leiomyoma include adenoma, intraductal papilloma, syringomatous tumor, and Paget’s disease of the breast. Nipple adenoma is a relatively rare, benign glandular tumor that arises within the ducts of the nipple or just beneath the areola. It is often associated with nipple induration, bleeding, and erosion. On MRI, it typically appears as a well-circumscribed solid mass with smooth margins and shows heterogeneous enhancement with a fast-persistent or washout pattern on dynamic contrast-enhanced imaging [8]. Intraductal papilloma is a papillary tumor arising within the ducts and contains vascular and fibrous stromal components arranged in a branching pattern. In the absence of an associated cystic component, it may appear as a well-defined mass with intermediate signal intensity on T2WI. Contrast-enhanced MRI often reveals rim enhancement, and dynamic imaging typically shows a fast washout or plateau pattern [8]. Syringomatous tumors are rare benign neoplasms arising from eccrine sweat glands, which are skin adnexal structures. Ultrasonography reveals an irregular mass in the subareolar region of the nipple, and MRI findings are generally nonspecific, except for the presence of contrast enhancement within the lesion [9]. Paget’s disease of the breast typically presents with clinical symptoms such as nipple erythema, erosion, or bleeding. MRI may show linear or segmental enhancement in cases with associated ductal extension [8]. A summary of the distinguishing MRI features is provided in Table 1. Because there is considerable overlap in imaging features among these entities, a definitive diagnosis based solely on imaging findings is often difficult. Treatment of nipple leiomyoma is primarily symptomatic, and calcium channel blockers and alpha-adrenergic receptor antagonists are used to manage pain [10]. The definitive treatment is surgical excision of the lesion. Although recurrence data are limited, Hammer et al. [11] reported 6 cases with no recurrence over a follow-up period of up to 12 years. To date, malignant transformation has not been reported in the literature.

To our knowledge, this is the first report describing the MRI findings of nipple leiomyoma in a male patient. Previous MRI documentation has been limited to a single case report in a female patient. Although both cases shared imaging features, including a well-circumscribed mass with intermediate signal intensity on T1- and T2-weighted images and a slow, persistent enhancement pattern, our case differed in the absence of a clearly identifiable abnormality on diffusion-weighted imaging and the lack of rim enhancement. By presenting detailed multiparametric MRI findings in a male patient, this report further expands the limited imaging features on this rare entity.
