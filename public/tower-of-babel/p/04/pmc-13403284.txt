A novel triple-device recanalization strategy combining a small-diameter balloon, guide extension catheter, and distal protection device for large thrombus-induced coronary artery occlusion: a case report
Authors: Yudai Tanaka, Riku Arai, Tetsuro Nagao, Keisuke Kojima, Yasuo Okumura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) carries high mortality, and prompt revascularization is essential. However, the presence of a large intracoronary thrombus burden remains a major procedural challenge.1–3 Extensive thrombus during percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) increases the risk of distal embolization and impairs microvascular perfusion, often resulting in suboptimal outcomes.1 Standard methods to reduce thrombus burden—including pharmacological therapy, distal protection devices, and both mechanical and aspiration thrombectomy techniques2–4—may fail in cases of refractory thrombus. Although several adjunctive techniques have been introduced to overcome these limitations,1,5–9 their applicability and efficacy remain inconsistent. Although aspiration devices are widely used, their effectiveness is limited in organized or massive thrombi. Kusumoto et al. (2021) reported a novel technique combining a guide extension catheter and distal filter device to mechanically capture large thrombi refractory to aspiration.1 Building on this concept, we report a case where a modified balloon-assisted locking configuration achieved successful en bloc thrombus extraction in a right coronary artery (RCA) occlusion with a large thrombus burden, after the failure of both conventional aspiration and the previously described guide extension catheter—a distal filter device technique.

Case presentation

An 80-year-old man with a history of old myocardial infarction, hypertension, and dyslipidaemia presented with chest pain lasting over 3 h. On arrival, his blood pressure was 116/91 mmHg, heart rate was 90 bpm. Twelve-lead electrocardiography showed ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, aVF, and V6 (Figure 1). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a severely reduced left ventricular ejection fraction of approximately 20% with diffuse hypokinesis. Chest X-ray revealed bilateral pulmonary oedema and left-sided pleural effusion, consistent with acute heart failure. The patient was classified as Killip class III and SCAI stage B. Intravenous dobutamine was initiated at 2.25 μg/kg/min for haemodynamic support. Emergency coronary angiography revealed total occlusion of the proximal RCA (segment #1) (Figure 2A). Primary PCI was performed to recanalize the occluded RCA via a 7 Fr left radial approach using a 7 Fr AL1 guide catheter. A 0.014-in. SION guidewire (Asahi Intecc, Seto, Japan) was advanced across the occlusion site with the support of a Caravel MC microcatheter (Asahi Intecc, Seto, Japan). Intravenous unfractionated heparin was administered during the procedure, with the activated clotting time (ACT) maintained at ≥250 s to ensure adequate anticoagulation. Initial attempts with aspiration catheters failed to restore flow (Figure 2B). Balloon dilation of the occlusion site allowed intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) interrogation, which revealed a calcified proximal lesion and large distal thrombus burden persisted despite repeated aspiration attempts (Figure 3A). Because conventional aspiration was ineffective, we attempted a thrombus removal technique using a 7 Fr Guidezilla II, (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, Massachusetts, USA) guide extension catheter, which has an inner diameter of 1.6 mm, and the filter body of the Filtrap device (Nipro Corporation, Nagoya, Japan), as described in previous studies. However, during device withdrawal, the Filtrap was inadvertently drawn into the guide extension catheter (Figure 2C). As a result, thrombus retrieval using the initial (unmodified) technique was unsuccessful (Figure 2D). To overcome this mechanical limitation and prevent filter migration, we constructed a modified retrieval system by placing Ryurei 2.0/10 mm balloon catheter (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) between the Guidezilla II and the Filtrap device (Figure 4). The balloon was first positioned such that its shaft was aligned along the proximal portion of the Filtrap filter body. Subsequently, the Guidezilla II was advanced and positioned at the proximal edge of the thrombotic lesion. The balloon was then inflated to 6 atmospheres to secure the connection, resulting in an inflated balloon diameter of approximately 2.0 mm (Figure 2E, Figure 4). The entire assembly was gently withdrawn as a single unit, successfully removing a large red thrombus (Figure 5 and Videos 1 and 2). Final angiography demonstrated complete revascularization with TIMI 3 flow (Figure 2F), and IVUS confirmed minimal residual thrombus (Figure 3B). The proximal calcified lesion was subsequently treated with a Synergy Megatron 3.5/24 mm stent (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, Massachusetts, USA). The procedure was technically successful. The patient received antibiotic therapy from admission for concomitant pneumonia. However, the subsequent clinical course was characterized by worsening pulmonary oedema and progressive heart failure, and he died of heart failure-associated multiorgan failure on hospital day 16.

Discussion

This balloon-assisted modification addresses a key mechanical limitation of earlier hybrid techniques. Importantly, the interposed balloon functioned as a mechanical stabilizer, preventing device slippage and enabling firm thrombus capture between the guide extension catheter and the filter device, thereby allowing controlled retrieval. This approach may reduce distal embolization risk and may help avoid stent implantation in thrombus-laden segments in selected patients.

Primary PCI in STEMI with a large, refractory thrombus burden is associated with increased risks of no-reflow and distal embolization.1,10–12 Although the Guidezilla II–Filtrap hybrid technique has been previously described as a mechanical bailout strategy, its clinical utility is limited by the risk of the Filtrap system migrating proximally into the guide extension catheter while leaving a lot of the thrombus within the coronary artery.

The Filtrap is designed as a distally deployed filter with an expanded basket structure that captures thrombus while maintaining distal coronary flow. When the filter is drawn into the guide extension catheter, it collapses within the confined lumen and loses contact with the vessel wall, resulting in ineffective thrombus capture.

Our balloon-assisted configuration should be recognized as a mechanically integrated thrombus control system, in which each component performs a distinct and non-redundant mechanical role: (1) Guidezilla II (7 Fr)—acts as a proximal thrombus container and lid, capable of receiving the bulk thrombus en bloc due to its larger inner diameter within the Guidezilla Ⅱ lumen (1.6 mm) compared with the inner diameter of the retrieval kit of Filtrap (1.1 mm). The device also reduces the risk of proximal thrombus escape into the aorta by providing a controlled intermediate receptacle before withdrawal into the guiding catheter lumen. (2) Small balloon (2.0 mm)—provides two critical mechanical functions: (a) a spacer effect that prevents suction-induced migration of the Filtrap body into the Guidezilla lumen. The retrieval balloon must be small enough to collapse into a 7 Fr guiding catheter lumen (2.3 mm) during withdrawal. Therefore, the 2.0-mm balloon represents the maximal realistic size that preserves retrievability without thrombus escape; and (b) stabilization of the thrombus between the balloon and the distal tip of the Guidezilla, helping maintain thrombus capture during device withdrawal. (3) Filtrap (5 mm)—remains fully deployed distally and serves as a safety reservoir for fragment salvage, capturing micro-thrombus debris that may escape from the proximal side during device withdrawal, functioning as the available coronary device capable of distal umbrella-deployment fragment capture.

Although this case used the Filtrap system, the underlying concept may apply to other distal protection devices when there is appropriate compatibility among the filter device, balloon catheter, and guide extension catheter. In such settings, adjustment of balloon size or guide extension catheter size may be necessary to achieve stable thrombus capture and safe retrieval. However, because this report describes a single case using one specific device combination, the applicability of this approach to other filters remains theoretical.

In a thrombus analogue retrieval simulation, real-time imaging (Video 1) showed that Residual thrombus remained attached to the coronary wall after withdrawing the three-device system, confirming that the system does not provide complete circumferential wall sweeping. Supported by this experimental observation, the current indication for this technique should be restricted to the RCA and not extrapolated to the left coronary artery, where bifurcations may redirect emboli into non-target coronary territories. The RCA typically lacks a competing large bifurcation before the crux, supporting safer en bloc device retrieval.

While this system provides enhanced mechanical stability, several risks must be acknowledged: (1) Vessel injury or endothelial injury due to device interaction during system withdrawal, particularly if filter collapse or balloon oversizing generates excessive resistance. (2) Device entrapment or balloon rupture, which may compromise retrieval or create iatrogenic injury. (3) Incomplete thrombus capture, as micro-fragment escape cannot be fully prevented.

Therefore, the technique must be performed under strict procedural safeguards, including: (1) Pre-procedural vessel sizing by IVUS confirmation before filter deployment, ensuring adequate lumen and reducing oversizing-related injury risk. (2) Controlled balloon inflation pressure (e.g. 6 atm for 2.0-mm balloon) to maintain retrievability and avoid excessive radial force. (3) Gentle en bloc withdrawal under fluoroscopic guidance, with careful attention to resistance during retrieval, stopping if marked resistance is encountered (Video 2).

In this case, nearly all distal thrombus was removed, allowing us to avoid stenting in the thrombus-filled segment and limit stent placement to the calcified proximal lesion, thereby minimizing the risk of procedure-related adverse events. Although revascularization and thrombus extraction were technically successful, the patient ultimately died of heart failure-associated multiorgan failure. The patient received antibiotic therapy from admission for concomitant pneumonia; however, the subsequent clinical course was characterized by worsening pulmonary oedema and progressive heart failure. The patient presented late (>3 h of symptoms) with haemodynamic instability (Killip Ⅲ), suggesting a large infarct size at baseline (Peak CK 2312 U/L, peak CK-MB 369 ng/mL). This highlights that mechanical thrombus removal, even when successful, may not necessarily alter the clinical trajectory in patients with extensive myocardial injury or significant comorbidities. Therefore, this technique should be regarded as a procedural option rather than a prognostic intervention.

Conclusion

A balloon-assisted modification of the Guidezilla II–Filtrap retrieval system enabled the successful en bloc extraction of a large thrombus when conventional aspiration and the standard Filtrap-based technique failed. While the technique offers a potential option for managing refractory thrombus-rich lesions, its safety and general applicability warrant further study, particularly given that procedural success may not necessarily translate into improved clinical outcomes.
