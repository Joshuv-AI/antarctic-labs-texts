Genomic epidemiology of ceftriaxone-resistant non-typhoidal Salmonella enterica strain in China
Authors: Danni Bao, Lei Chen, Weiwei Shen, Xiaohong Xu, Lifei Zhu, Yizhang Wang, Yanhong Wu, Xianhong He, Fengjiao Zhu, Hongzhang Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) is one of the top causes of diarrhea worldwide. Ceftriaxone is commonly recommended as the initial treatment option for Salmonella infections due to its antibacterial effectiveness. The objective of this study was to investigate the molecular epidemiological characteristics of NTS and to compare the phenotypic and genotypic profiles of antimicrobial resistance in multidrug-resistant Salmonella strains by sequencing 329 NTS strains collected from a county-level hospital between 2018 and 2021. Multi-locus sequence typing (MLST), antimicrobial resistance genes and plasmid types were identified by BacWGSTdb 2.0 webserver. Phylogenetic analysis of all NTS strains was carried out using Snippy and Gubbins software. The transferability of ceftriaxone resistant plasmids was confirmed through plasmid conjugation assays, and verified by S1-PFGE-Southern blot assays. The predominant serotypes among all NTS strains were Typhimurium (161/329), Enteritidis (49/329) and London (45/329). The most common sequence type observed was ST34 (86/329), followed by ST19 (72/329) and ST11 (47/329). The antimicrobial resistance of Salmonella to a wide range of antimicrobials showed an overall increase. Out of these 37 (11.24%) ceftriaxone-resistant strains, with the majority of them (33/37) being blaCTX−M. The predominant plasmid types identified were IncHI2 (14/21) and IncI1 (6/21), ranging in size from 70 kb to 360 kb. The conjugation efficiency was calculated with the high conjugation efficiency of 1.1 × 10− 5 to 9.3 × 10− 2. The strains varied widely, ranging from 3 to 45,024 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). There are close linkages observed among the predominant lineage, with an average of 78 SNPs between each pair of ST34 strains. The findings contribute to our understanding of the transmission and resistance mechanisms of multidrug-resistant Salmonella, thereby facilitating the development of effective control strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10890-2.

Introduction

Salmonella, a genus comprising two species, includes six subspecies: enterica, arizonae, diarizonae, houtenae, indica, and salamae. [1]. According to the invasiveness, S. enterica can be divided into typhoidal Salmonella and Non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS). NTS is characterized by a broader host range and typically causes self-limiting gastroenteritis through consumption of contaminated food and water [2]. According to a recent World Health Organization (WHO) report, a multi-country epidemic of Salmonella Typhimurium ST34 was linked to consume of chocolate items, leading to sickness in 143 (89%) children under the age of ten across at least 11 countries. This incident have raised concerns within the global public health community [3]. However, specific consideration should be given to antimicrobial treatment for individuals at higher risk for invasive infections, such as infants and the elderly [4]. A study from Zhejiang Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention (ZJCDC) highlighted that the majority of reported Salmonella cases affected children (< 4 years) and older adults (> 60 years), with hospitalization rates of 48.14% and 27.70%, respectively [5]. The rise of Salmonella strains displaying resistance to single, or multiple first-line medications has posed challenges in treating invasive salmonellosis. Consequently, the third generation cephalosporins, such as ceftriaxone, have emerged as preferred treatment options for managing severe Salmonella infections [6, 7].

Due to the limited treatment options for Salmonella infections in children, β-lactams are the most commonly utilized antimicrobials in clinical practice [8]. Ceftriaxone, a potent and broad-spectrum cephalosporin with unique pharmacokinetic properties [9], is the empirical choice for treating Salmonella infections. However, a growing concern in recent years is the increasing incidence of cephalosporins-resistant Salmonella strains. The carriage of extended-spectrum β-lactamase genes (ESBLs) are the main cause of ceftriaxone resistance.

Since their discovery in the early 1980s, ESBLs have become prevalent in Enterobacterales isolated from both hospital-associated and community-acquired infections, posing a global public health concern. ESBL types such as TEM, SHV, CTX-M are particularly noteworthy. Among them, CTX-M have become the most prevalent ESBLs worldwide, surpassing TEM and SHV [10]. Based on sequence homologies, the majority of CTX-M enzymes could be further divided into five main groups: CTX-M-1, CTX-M-2, CTX-M-8, CTX-M-9 and CTX-M-25. CTX-M-15, CTX-M-14 and CTX-M-27 are the most commonly encountered variants worldwide [11]. In addition to ESBLs, AmpC β-lactamases, including CMY and DHA, also play a significant role in ceftriaxone resistance. CMY-type enzymes can be broadly categorized into two groups: CMY-1-like enzymes and CMY-2-like enzymes, with CMY-2-like being the most widely distributed plasmid-mediated AmpC variant globally [12].

In this study, we sought to investigate the molecular epidemiology of Salmonella enterica, as well as to elucidate the mechanisms of resistance and modes of transmission for ceftriaxone-resistant strains. The genomic characteristics and phylogenetic relationships of ceftriaxone-resistant Salmonella were also performed to unrevealing the genetic basis responsible for this resistance.

Methods

Strains collection

The study included all patients whose clinical samples were collected and found to contain Salmonella at Sanmen Peopleʼs Hospital, Zhejiang, China. The study collected a total of 329 Salmonella strains from January 2018 to December 2021. The majority of strains (n = 317, 96.4%) were obtained from feces samples, while 7 strains (2.1%) were from blood, 2 strains (0.6%) were from urine, 2 strains (0.6%) were from pus samples, and 1 isolate (0.3%) were from sputum. Among the 7 blood originated strains, they were obtained from patients in different departments, including pediatrics, gastroenterology, internal medicine-cardiovascular and intensive care unit (ICU). Patient identities were anonymized and other information including age, sex, and infection site was taken from the digital record system. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sanmen Peopleʼs Hospital (Approval No. 20230069).

Bacterial isolate identification and Salmonella Serotyping

Fresh fecal samples were obtained and incubated in selective bacterial growth augmenting solution at 36℃ for 18 to 24 h. The cultured bacterial solution was then plated on xylose lysine deoxycholate agar (XLD) and incubated at 36℃ for 18 to 24 h. Suspicious bacterial colonies (colorless and transparent, translucent or exhibiting a black “cat’s eye sign” in the middle) were isolated and subjected to further tests. Whole blood (5 mL) was drawn and injected into a pediatric bottle (BACT/ALERT PF, BioMérieux, France) for culture. The culture was maintained for 5 days or until a positive result was obtained in an automatic blood culture system (BACT/ALERT 3D). Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry systems (bioMérieux, Lyon, France) were used for species identification. Serogrouping was conducted using a seroagglutination test (Tianrun, Ningbo, China) according to the White-Kauffmann-Le Minor scheme. Normal saline served as the control.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility determined by broth microdilution for following antimicrobials: ampicillin, ampicillin/sulbactam, gentamicin, amikacin, ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, cefepime, aztreonam, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, imipenem and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. The Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was interpreted using the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2021 guidelines. E. coli ATCC25922 was used for microbiological quality control.

Whole-genome sequencing

Short-read Illumina HiSeq X-Ten (Illumina, San Diego, CA, United States) platform was employed for whole-genome sequencing of all Salmonella strains, utilizing a 150 bp paired-end strategy. Then, Shovill (https://github.com/tseemann/shovill) was used to assemble the reads, and any unqualified contigs (coverage < 10 or length < 250 bp) were excluded from further analysis.

BacWGSTdb 2.0 online server [13], was used to identify sequence types and antimicrobial-resistance genes (ARGs) of NTS strains. Their serotypes were further analyzed using the SeqSero2 [14]. PlasmidFinder 2.1 to identify plasmid incompatibility (Inc) groups [15]. Phylogenetic analysis of all NTS strains was carried out using Snippy v4.6.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy) and Gubbins v3.3.1 (Croucher et al., 2015). The phylogenetic tree was visualized through the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) V5 web server [16]. The WGS data of all Salmonella strains involved in this study were deposited in the NCBI database under the BioProject accession number PRJNA976072.

The genetic surrounding of ESBLs genes

The contigs containing ESBL genes were extracted and subjected to annotation using NCBI Prokaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline (PGAP), with a particular focus on utilizing ISfinder [17] for the identification of mobile genetic elements. This approach allowed us to identify the presence of transposable elements, such as transposons, insertion sequences, or other mobile genetic elements, within the contigs carrying ESBL genes. Easyfig was used to visualize the genetic structure of ESBL genes.

Conjugation experiments, S1-PFGE and Southern Blot Assay

To evaluate the transferability of ESBLs-carrying plasmids from the strains, filter mating experiments were conducted using E. coli J53 as the recipient strain. The transconjugants were selected on BHI agar plates supplemented with 4 mg/L ceftriaxone, and the conjugation efficiency was measured and calculated. Sanger sequencing was performed to detect the presence of ESBL genes in transconjugants. The conjugation frequency was calculated as the ratio of transconjugants to recipient cells. A Salmonella serotype Braenderup strain H9812 was used as the reference size marker [18]. The size of the ESBLs-carrying plasmids was estimated by using S1-PFGE, and the genetic location of the ESBLs was determined by performing southern blot analysis with ESBLs gene-specific probes.

Definitions

We defined “non-invasive” cases as those in which Salmonella had been isolated from stool specimens and “invasive” cases as those in which Salmonella had been isolated from specimens such as blood, cerebrospinal fluid, other normally sterile fluids, pus and urine, which from other extraintestinal sites.

Multidrug resistance (MDR) [19] was defined as resistance to three or more antibacterial classes, such as aminopenicillins (ampicillin), β-lactam combination agents (ampicillin-sulbactam), cephalosporins (ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, cefepime), monobactams (aztreonam), carbapenems (imipenem), dihydrofolate reductase inhibitors (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole), aminoglycosides (amikacin, gentamicin) and fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin).

Results

Demographics and strains

A total of 329 non-duplicated Salmonella strains were collected during the study period. Salmonella isolation rates for males and females were approximately equal (males, 51.06%; females, 48.94%). However, for invasive non-typhoidal Salmonella (iNTS), the isolation rates were significantly higher in males compared to females (6:1). In contrast to other studies, only 15.5% (51/329) of Salmonella strains were obtained from children (< 4 years) in this study, and isolation rates for male children and female children were 1.7:1. Notably, children under 2 years of age accounted for 84.3% (43/51) of the strains. The majority of Salmonella strains were detected from May to September, with highest number in July, followed by August, May, June and September, accounting for 78.4% (258/329), which shown a seasonal variation in Salmonella isolation rates (Fig. 1).

Antimicrobial susceptibility of Salmonella strains

Among the collected Salmonella strains, there were 37 ceftriaxone-resistant, resulting in a resistance rate of 11.25%. The other antimicrobial resistance rates were ampicillin (81.76%, 269/329), followed by ampicillin-sulbactam (39.33%, 129/328), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (30.04%, 112/329), gentamicin (16.11%, 53/329), aztreonam (11.25%, 37/329), ciprofloxacin (9.73%, 32/329), levofloxacin (3.34%, 11/329), amikacin (0.3%, 1/329) and no imipenem-resistant strains were identified. The overall rate of multidrug resistance in this study was 38.60% (127/329).

During the period of antimicrobial resistance monitoring, the resistance rate of Salmonella to most antimicrobials increased in varying degrees. The resistance rate of ampicillin rose from 59.62% in 2018 to 72.11% in 2021, ceftriaxone from 5.77 to 13.61%, aztreonam from 5.77 to 12.93%, ampicillin/sulbactam from 11.54 to 40.14%, gentamycin from 5.77 to 21.09%, and cotrimoxazole from 21.15 to 34.01%. However, the resistance rate to ceftazidime, cefepime, imipenem and amikacin remained constant. Notably, the resistance rates of ciprofloxacin (from 13.46 to 4.76%) and levofloxacin (from 7.69 to 1.36%) showed a significant downward trend (Fig. 2). The discrepancy in resistance rates between ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin, despite both being fluoroquinolones, can be attributed to differences in structural affinity, bacterial target preference, mechanisms of resistance, and clinical use patterns.

This study involved 251 Salmonella strains carrying PMQR (plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance) genes or QRDR (quinolone resistance-determining region) mutations (Supplementary Table 2). There were seven subtypes of mutations at four different positions in the QRDR of the gyrA and parC genes. The amino acid substitutions in GyrA and ParC were detected in 196 strains, with the highest mutation rate observed in ParC (33.43%, 110/329), including ParC: T57S (110/329) and S80I (2/329). This was followed by GyrA mutations (31.00%, 102/329), which included D87Y (38/329), S83Y (36/329), D87N (14/329), D87G (10/329), and S83F (4/329). Among them, the T57S (31.91%, 105/329) mutation in ParC was the most prevalent one. However, Salmonella strains harboring only this mutation exhibited a low level of resistance to quinolones, with only 14.52% of isolates being non-susceptible to CIP and LEV. The role of this mutation in quinolone resistance mechanisms requires further investigation and validation. The point mutations at positions 83 and 87 of GyrA result in strains with low-level quinolone resistance, which are commonly found in highly resistant strains with double or multiple mutations. Among all strains, 196 strains (59.57%, 196/329) carried at least one PMQR gene, just two strains exhibited dual mutations at multiple sites, such as ParC: T57S-GyrA: S83F and ParC: T57S-GyrA: S83F. Another two strains showed the combination of ParC: T57S/S80I and GyrA: S83F/D87G mutations, which demonstrated high-level resistance to both levofloxacin (≥ 8 mg/L) and ciprofloxacin (≥ 16 mg/L). Among the PMQR genes, qnrS had the highest carriage rate (18.54%, 61/329), including two subtypes: qnrS1 (58/329) and qnrS2 (3/329). This was followed by qnrB (9.12%, 30/329), which included qnrB6 (26/329), qnrB4 (3/329), and qnrB19 (1/329). Other genes detected were oqxAB (2.13%, 7/329), qnrD1 (0.61%, 2/329), aac(6’)-Ib-cr5 (0.61%, 2/329) and qnrA1 (0.30%, 1/329). The presence of PMQR genes alone has a minimal impact on quinolone resistance mechanisms. Synergistic effects are observed when the presence of these genes in combination with QRDR mutations, enhancing resistance through various mutation combinations. It is worth noting that two strains developed resistance to ciprofloxacin despite the absence of any PMQR or QRDR mutations. The specific reasons for this phenomenon remain unclear and require further investigation.

The acquired antimicrobial resistance genes identified from whole-genome sequencing data were then compared with the results of the antimicrobial susceptibility testing. The data revealed the highest concordance rate between resistance genes and resistance phenotypes for carbapenems, with predicted resistance genes perfectly matching the resistance phenotypes. The next highest concordance rate was observed for ceftriaxone and amikacin, with a coincidence rate of 99.70% (Table 1). There is a strong agreement between the presence of resistance genes and the resistance phenotypes for β-lactams, with the concordance rate surpassing 95%. Combined with the analysis of point mutations, the concordance rates of the acquired resistance genes and point mutations of ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin with resistance phenotypes are 91.49% and 95.44%, respectively. The concordance between the resistance genes and phenotypes of aminoglycoside class gentamicin is relatively low, with a concordance rate of 40.12%. This is mainly characterized by a high detection rate of aminoglycoside-related resistance genes, which differs significantly from the susceptible strains.

Serotype distribution and MLST analysis

All Salmonella strains were serotyped using the White-Kauffmann-Le Minor scheme, and their serotypes were further analyzed using the SeqSero tool based on whole-genome sequencing data. A comparison between the White-Kauffmann-Le Minor serotyping and the in silico prediction results revealed an overall coincidence rate of 86.93% in predicting serotype. Out of the all strains, 287 were highly consistent between the two methods, while 27 strains showed difference in serotype assignment and 11 strains were failed serotyped using the White-Kauffmann-Le Minor scheme.

The White-Kauffmann-Le Minor scheme can yield false-positive reactions due to weak, nonspecific agglutination. Additionally, autoagglutination and loss of antigen expression, particularly in rough, nonmotile, and mucoid strains, may occasionally lead to strain untypeability [20]. It is commonly used as an initial screening, followed by molecular subtyping to identify [20]. According to the whole-genome serotyping, 28 serotypes were identified among 329 Salmonella strains, of which Typhimurium (1, 4, [5], 12: i: 2 and 1, 4, [5], 12: i: -) accounted for the largest proportion (49%, 161/329), followed by Enteritidis (15%, 49/329), London (14%, 45/329), Rissen (5.2%, 17/329) and other serotypes that were mostly scattered (Fig. 3). This distribution aligns with both national and global trends, where Typhimurium and Enteritidis consistently rank among the most commonly isolated serotypes. For example, from 2013 to 2022, the nationwide incidence of Salmonella infections in Denmark was predominantly characterized by three serotypes: S. Enteritidis (26.4%), monophasic S. Typhimurium (16.5%), and S. Typhimurium (13.5%) [21]. The MLST results showed that ST34 was the predominant sequence type (26%, 85/329), followed by ST19 (22%, 72/329), ST11 (14%, 47/329), ST155 (14%, 46/329) and others (24%, 78/329) (Fig. 4). This is also consistent with global findings, where ST34 [22], a common variant of Typhimurium, has been identified as a dominant strain in multiple regions, particularly in association with multidrug resistance. ST19 [23], which is also a subtype of Typhimurium, is widely distributed globally and often linked to both human and animal infections.

Phylogenetic analysis

The phylogenetic tree revealed that different serovars occupied distinct positions and were unrelated on the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 5and Fig. 6). The strains varied widely, with an average of 15,326 SNPs, ranging from 3 SNPs to 45,024 SNPs. The strains are predominantly sporadic, but there are also a few instances of clonal outbreaks.

Close linkages were observed among the predominant strains, with an average of 78 SNPs between each pair of ST34 strains. Notably, there are 8 pairs with less than 20 SNPs, indicating possible clonal spread between them. Additionally, strains 2021-65 and 2021-70, detected in July 2021, showed only 3 SNPs difference, which corresponds to a time difference of less than 4 days. Similarly, strains 2021 − 142 and 2021 − 145, detected in September 2021, exhibited 7 SNPs difference with only 2-day time gap, indicating the short-term clonal transmission of these strains. Strains 171, 178, 124, 2021 − 1102, 2021 − 1078 and 85 displayed less than 20 SNPs difference despite being isolated from 2018 to 2021, suggesting a long-term local epidemic trend for this clone.

Ceftriaxone-resistant Salmonella strains

The molecular epidemiology and phylogenetic relationship of 37 ceftriaxone-resistant Salmonella strains were further clarified using whole-genome sequencing data. With the exception of one isolate from blood and two from pus samples, all ceftriaxone-resistant Salmonella strains were from feces. According to the in silico MLST analysis, ST34 was the most prevalent lineage (65%, 24/37), followed by ST19 (10.8%, 4/37), ST155 (10.8%, 4/37), ST198 (2.7%, 1/37), ST11 (2.7%, 1/37), ST40 (2.7%, 1/37), ST321 (2.7%, 1/37) and one novel ST were identified. In terms of serotypes, Typhimurium accounted for a significant proportion (75.6%, 28/37), followed by London (10.8%, 4/37), Kentucky (5.4%, 2/37), Derby (2.7%, 1/37), Enteritidis (2.7%, 1/37), Muenster (2.7%, 1/37).

Antimicrobial resistance genes were examined in order to comprehend the mechanisms underlying ceftriaxone in these resistance Salmonella strains. Almost all ceftriaxone-resistant strains were found to produce CTX-M type ESBLs, except for 4 strains that produced the AmpC cephalosporinase CMY-2-type β-lactamase (10.8%, 4/37). Among the CTX-M-producing strains, blaCTX−M−55 (39.4%, 13/33) was the most prevalent, followed by blaCTX−M−65 (36.4%, 12/33), blaCTX−M−14 (15.2%, 5/33), blaCTX−M−125 (3.0%, 1/33), blaCTX−M−130 (3.0%, 1/33), and blaCTX−M−27 (3.0%, 1/33). In addition, these strains carried genes responsible for resistance to β-lactams [blaLAP−2, blaOXA−10 and blaTEM−1], aminoglycosides [aac(2’)-IIa, aac(3)-IId, aac(3)-Iva, aadA1, aadA2, aph(3’’)-Ib, aph(3’)-Ia, aph(4)-Ia, aph(6)-Id and arr-2], chloramphenicol [cmlA1, cmlA5 and cmlA6], tetracyclines[tet(A) and tet(B)], fluoroquinolones [oqxA, oqxB and qnrS1], macrolides [mef(B) and mph(A)], sulfonamide[sul1, sul2, sul3, dfrA12 and dfrA14]. The antimicrobial resistance rates of the 37 ESBLs-producing Salmonella strains were highest for ampicillin (100%, 37/37), ceftriaxone (100%, 37/37) and aztreonam (100%, 37/37), followed by ceftazidime (54.1%, 20/37), cefepime (48.6%, 18/37), gentamicin (32.4%, 12/37), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (29.7%, 11/37), ampicillin-sulbactam (21.6%, 8/37), ciprofloxacin (8.1%, 3/37), and levofloxacin (2.7%, 1/37). No strains resistant to imipenem and amikacin were identified. The hydrolytic abilities of different CTX-M subtypes differed to ceftazidime and cefepime. For example, the ceftazidime and cefepime antimicrobial resistance rates for 13 blaCTX−M−55-carrying strains was 100% (13/13) and 92.3 (12/13), respectively. In contrast, the rates for 12 blaCTX−M−65-carrying strains were 8.3 (1/13) and 0% (with an intermediate rate of 58.3%), respectively.

The results of S1-PFGE-Southern blot assay indicated that the blaCTX−M gene was predominantly located on the plasmid (67%, 22/33). The most common plasmid types were IncHI2 and IncI1. Specifically, IncHI2 plasmid ranged from 240 kb to 360 kb, while the IncI1 and IncFII plasmids were approximately 70 kb in size. All plasmids in the strains ranged from 70 kb to 360 kb in size and had the ability to carry antimicrobial resistance genes for horizontal transfer (Supplementary Fig. 1). In addition, confirming the transferability of ceftriaxone resistant plasmids, a conjugation assay was performed, which demonstrated a success rate of 90% (20/22) for transferability. The conjugation efficiency was calculated with the high conjugation efficiency of 1.1 × 10− 5 to 9.3 × 10− 2.

Genetic environment of blaCTX−M genes

The genetic environment analyses revealed a significant presence of various transposable elements, such as IS26, IS903B, ISEcp1, IS5/IS1182, IS4 and IS5, which participated in facilitating the horizontal transfer of ESBLs genes. The blaCTX−M−55 gene was found to have two different genetic environments: ISEcp1-blaCTX−M−55-Tn3 and ΔISEcp1-blaCTX−M−55. The blaCTX−M−65 gene was also exhibited two different environments: IS1380-blaCTX−M−65-IS903B-IS26 and IS1380-blaCTX−M−65-IS903B. The blaCTX−M−14 gene demonstrated three different environments: IS4-blaCTX−M−14-IS903B, ΔISEcp1-blaCTX−M−14 and ΔblaCTX−M−14-IS5/IS1182. The blaCTX−M−125 gene exhibited the ISEcp1- blaCTX−M−125-IS903B structure. The blaCTX−M−27 gene displayed the IS1B-Tn3-IS26-blaCTX−M−27-IS5-IS26-IS1 structure. In the case of CMY-2, the gene upstream was ISEcp1 (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Non-invasive Salmonella infection was frequently linked to gastroenteritis, while a minority of iNTS infections, encompassing bloodstream infections, urinary tract infections, and wound infections was also identified. Although only 15.5% (51/329) of Salmonella strains were obtained from children (< 4 years) in this study, however, as a vulnerable population, children still cannot be ignored. According to previous studies, various factors contribute to the vulnerability of children, including underdeveloped immune function, absence of breastfeeding, crawling and sucking behaviors, as well as the introduction of complementary foods [24]. Among children below the age of 2 (43/51, 84.3%) with males exhibiting higher infection rates compared to females. The proportion of Salmonella-infected male children compared to female children (1.7:1) aligns with previous research findings, showing a higher ratio among males, which may be attributed to their increased levels of physical activity [25]. Our analysis revealed that in cases of bloodstream infection, there were higher rates of iNTS infections among male infant and the elderly. However, due to the limited number of cases, we were unable to conduct a comprehensive examination of gender-specific risk factors.

Salmonella infection exhibits a seasonal pattern, with a majority of cases occurring between May and September, as this aligns with the optimal growth temperature for Salmonella growth ranging of 35–43 °C [26]. High temperature and humidity are conducive to bacterial proliferation, and increase the risk of infection from consuming raw or cold food. The distribution of Salmonella serotype shows regional variations. According to data from the Foodborne Diseases Active Surveillance Network (FoodNet) sites of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the most commonly reported serotypes in the US between 1996 and 2014 were S. Typhimurium, S. Enteritidis, and S. Newport [27]. In Nigeria, S. Typhimurium (3.8%) remains the most prevalent serotype, followed by S. Enteritidis (2.8%) [28]. The ZJCDC reported S. Typhimurium as the most common serotype in China, followed by S. enteritidis, with S. London ranking third between 2010 and 2021 [5]. In our study, the distribution of serotypes closely matched the statistical data provided by the ZJCDC. Of particular significance is the prevalence of monophasic S. Typhimurium ST34 (1, 4, [5], 12: i: -) strains which accounted for 50% (80/161) of all S. Typhimurium strains and 54.1% of ceftriaxone-resistant Salmonella strains. Recent studies have highlighted the robust biofilm-forming capability and multidrug resistance displayed by these strains [29–31]. Alarmingly, resistance to polymyxin B and imipenem has also been observed in these strains [31, 32]. These findings underscore the public health threat posed by monophasic S. Typhimurium ST34 and emphasize the necessity for comprehensive surveillance of these strains.

NTS typically causes self-limiting gastroenteritis, but invasive infections require antimicrobial therapy for life saving treatment [4]. The recommended treatment options also vary, with ceftriaxone or ciprofloxacin commonly chosen for empirical treatment. However, the use of fluoroquinolones in children is restricted due to the potential cartilage damage. Ceftriaxone has become an empirical drug for treating of Salmonella infection in children. Nevertheless, the ceftriaxone resistance rate continues to rise in recent years. In China, the resistance of ceftriaxone varies across different cities, with rates of 5.9% in Taiwan, 3% in Hong Kong, 20.9% in Guangdong, 28% in Hubei and 25.9% in Hangzhou [33–37]. Ceftriaxone resistance was present in our study with a rate of 11.2%, the resistance rate has increased from 5.77% in 2018 to 13.61% in 2021. Notably, 20 of the 37 strains of Salmonella collected in 2021 exhibited resistance to ceftriaxone, indicating a concerning trend. Similar to our data, a retrospective survey conducted in Canada over a ten-year period revealed that ceftriaxone-resistant strains accounted for 1.6% of the total strains overall, however, they represented 10.1% of the total strains tested in 2019, also indicating a significant increase over time [38].

In contrast to a study from China Hangzhou [38], the resistance rates of ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin did not show a consistent trend, but demonstrated a clear downward trend. The ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin resistance rates from 13.46% in 2018 to 4.76% in 2021 and from 7.69% in 2018 to 1.36% in 2021, respectively. It is worth noting that while plasmids could carry multiple antimicrobial resistance genes, most clinical strains do not possess ciprofloxacin and ceftriaxone resistance genes. In addition, with the use of quinolones were restricted in Salmonella in children, their resistance trend has decreased significantly, which is in line with clinical practice [35, 39].

In this study, the whole-genome sequencing data of Salmonella were analyzed and compared with the antimicrobial resistance phenotype results. The results show that the coincidence rate of resistance phenotype predicted by WGS is high, which is consistent with the findings of other investigations. Neuert et al. studied the resistance of 3415 Salmonella strains to 15 antimicrobial agents and compared the carriage with the genotypes and showed 97.8% agreement [40]. In addition, for β-lactams and sulfonamides, the coincidence rate between resistance phenotype and genotype was high, which is consistent with the results of other studies. In the 189 Salmonella strains studied by Zhu et al., the prevalence of WGS resistance to sulfamoxazole, ampicillin, and tetracycline with their resistance phenotype were 97.8%, 94.6%, and 85.7%, respectively [41]. According to the major resistance mechanisms of Salmonella to quinolones, besides the plasmid-mediated resistance genes, point mutations in the chromosome-borne gyrA and parC genes in the quinolone resistance determining region (QRDR) can also confer quinolone resistance [42].

Serotyping is the basis of public health surveillance of Salmonella. Serotyping deciphers the antigenic makeup of the organism by identifying the somatic (O) and flagellar (H) antigens through reactions with specific antisera. The White-Kauffmann-Le Minor scheme is a subtyping technique traditionally conducted by inducing agglutination of Salmonella cells using specific antisera targeting the detection of lipopolysaccharide O antigens and flagellar H antigens [20, 43]. Within the White-Kauffmann-Le Minor scheme, over 2,600 distinct Salmonella serotypes have been identified [44]. The process of phenotypically determining serotypes is labor-intensive and time-consuming, typically requiring at least 2 days to complete. SeqSero, introduced in 2015, is a bioinformatics tool developed for Salmonella serotype determination utilizing whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data [14]. Building upon extensive testing and real-world application of SeqSero in the United States and other nations, SeqSero2 integrates crucial enhancements and updates to enhance its utility for routine and large-scale surveillance of Salmonella through whole-genome sequencing [14]. In this study, a comparison between the White-Kauffmann-Le Minor serotyping and the in silico prediction results revealed an overall coincidence rate of 86.93% in predicting serotype. Inconsistent results appeared between the two typing methods, mainly due to the difference in the results of H antigen identification. In addition, the quality of serum and the subjective judgment of staff are also the main reasons. Zhang et al. [45] conducted molecular analysis of 308 known Salmonella serovars by WGS, with 304 strains showing complete consistency in serotyping, yielding a coincidence rate of 98.7%. Similarly, Diep et al. [46] found that among 100 Salmonella strains from the Dutch region, resulting in a coincidence rate of 98.0%. Therefore, WGS-based method opens a new door for the accurate identification of Salmonella serotypes, which is expected to become a new standard for Salmonella serotyping [47].

Previous studies have revealed blaCMY−2 and blaCTX−M−3 as the two most prominent ceftriaxone resistance genes in Salmonella [48]. However, blaCTX−M−55 and blaCTX−M−65 was the most prevalent ESBLs in this study. Typically, plasmids are closely associated with cephalosporin resistance genes in clinical strains. Interestingly, in our study, most blaCTX−M−55 was located on the chromosome (84.6%, 11/13), similar to a previous study in Guangdong [35]. Except for strains 2021 − 155 and 2021 − 1001, the remaining strains failed to transfer blaCTX−M−55 to E. coli J53. This finding raises questions about the prevalence of blaCTX−M−55. In contrast, the blaCTX−M−65 gene was consistently found on plasmids (100%, 12/12), facilitating its transfer to E. coli through natural conjugation in our study. In addition, 7 strains were able to transfer plasmids carrying blaCTX−M−125, blaCTX−M−130, blaCTX−M−14, blaCTX−M−27 and blaCMY−2 to E. coli. The common feature of these strains is the ESBL genes being located on the IncHI2, IncI1 and IncFII-type plasmids. We hypothesize that the plasmids containing ESBL genes may have originated from different bacteria present in the human gastrointestinal tract. Further research is needed to investigate the source of plasmid and the prevalence of blaCTX−M−55 among Enterobacterales.

By analyzing the genetic environment surrounding ESBL genes, the research findings demonstrate the transposable elements are actively involved in facilitating the transfer of antimicrobial resistance genes. Different ESBL genes exhibit distinct genetic environmental structures, wherein transposable elements and other genetic elements play crucial roles in regulation and mediation. The most prevalent transfer observed for blaCTX−M−65 was IS1380-blaCTX−M−65-IS903B-IS26. By combining the results of the plasmid conjugation experiment conducted in this study, it was observed that blaCTX−M−65-carrying plasmid exhibited the highest success rate of conjugation. Shi et al. has previously reported blaCTX−M−55 as an epidemic gene with a high transferability rate [30]. However, despite our repeated attempts, only the strains 2021 − 155 and 2021 − 1001 were successful in transferring the blaCTX−M−55 gene to E. coli J53. The most prevalent transfer observed for blaCTX−M−55 was ISEcp1-blaCTX−M−55. By analyzing the surrounding structures of blaCTX−M−14, blaCTX−M−27, blaCTX−M−125, and blaCMY−2, it was observed that effective transfer occurs when both upstream and downstream regions contain IS elements. These results enhance our understanding of the transmission mechanisms of ESBL genes and the genetic context of antimicrobial resistance genes within bacteria. Further investigations can shed light on the interaction and evolutionary processes between these transposable elements and ESBL genes, leading to deeper insights and strategies for combating antimicrobial resistance.

Another critical aspect of Salmonella control is predicting the antimicrobial susceptibility of strains, which helps guide appropriate treatment strategies. WGS allows the accurate detection of genetic markers associated with antimicrobial resistance, enabling rapid and accurate prediction of resistance profiles [49]. By analyzing the presence of known resistance genes, mutations, and mobile genetic elements, WGS can provide valuable information on the potential resistance patterns of Salmonella strains, which can assist in guiding treatment decisions, optimizing therapy, and minimizing the spread of antimicrobial-resistant Salmonella strains [50].

Our study has some limitations that need to be acknowledged. Firstly, it is important to note that our study is a retrospective analysis conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Secondly, the number of ceftriaxone-resistant strains included in our study is relatively small due to the low rate of resistance in China. To enhance our understanding, we will continue to expand the surveillance network to recover additional ESBLs carrying Salmonella strains for further investigation.

Conclusion

The incidence of cephalosporin-resistant Salmonella strains has been increasing in recent years due to the horizontal transfer of conjugative plasmids carrying blaCTX−M−55 and blaCTX−M−65 genes. Whole-genome sequencing offers notable advantages over conventional serotyping and antimicrobial susceptibility testing. This study provides valuable insights into the genomic epidemiological characteristics of ceftriaxone-resistant Salmonella strains and emphasizes the significance of monitoring antimicrobial resistance in Salmonella infections. The findings contribute to our understanding of the transmission and resistance mechanisms of Salmonella, thereby facilitating the development of effective control strategies.
