Quadratic response assessment of gypsum and elemental sulphur on the yield and S use efficiency for soybean
Authors: Tauseef A. Bhat, Aijaz Nazir, Bilkees Jamsheed, Bisma Jan, Zahoor Ahmad Shah, Eajaz Ahmad Dar, Himansha Tanwar, Khursheed A. Dar, Amal Saxena, Ashu Sharma, Saika Ashraf, Ibrahim Al-Ashkar, Skalický Milan, Marian Brestic, El-Sabagh Ayman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As sulphur is the most vital nutrient for the growth and yield of soybean, determining its optimum source and dose becomes very critical. Experiment was conducted at the Agricultural Research Farm, Faculty of Agriculture (Wadura), SKUAST-Kashmir, India to assess the effects of different sources of sulphur at varying levels on the performance of soybean during the Kharif seasons of 2021 and 2022. Two sulphur sources (gypsum and elemental sulphur) were evaluated at five levels (0, 30, 45, 60, and 75 kg sulphur per hectare) using factorial randomized block design with three replicates. The quadratic response function was estimated for each source’s optimum sulphur level. The findings showed that gypsum application consistently led to superior growth performance compared to elemental sulphur and higher seed yield of 18.53 and 18.71 q ha−1 and stover yield of 23.60 and 26.82 q ha−1 in the years 2021 and 2022 respectively. However, a significant difference was not observed between the two sources regarding AE, PE, and ANR. In the sulphur levels studied, employing 60 kg sulphur ha−1 demonstrated the most favourable outcomes for growth parameters followed by 75 kg sulphur ha−1. However, with 60 kg sulphur ha−1 an increase of 4.17 and 2.75% for seed yield was shown, 3.97 and 3.08% for stover yield, and 2.62 and 2.19% for biological yield over 75 kg sulphur ha−1 in 2021 and 2022, respectively. Gypsum application resulted in higher S uptake than elemental sulphur in grain and stover highlighting its effectiveness as a sulphur source. S uptake in grain and stover increases with increasing sulphur levels, with the highest uptake observed at 60 kg S ha−1 being coequal to 75 kg S ha−1. The quadratic response, therefore, concluded that for achieving the best performance of Soybean, the sulphur application should be optimized at 60 kg ha−1. The need to assess and analyze the use of S @ 60 kg ha−1 using gypsum is critical in future as sulphur deficiency is becoming very common in Indian soils.

Introduction

Soybean [Glycine max(L.) Merrill.] a golden bean is one of the major legume with extensive global production of 266.2 million metric tons and an area of 127.8 million hectares1. In India, an area of 12.70 million hectares remains under soybean cultivation, producing 10.45 metric tons per hectare1. Soybeans are an excellent source of nourishment because of their high protein and oil content. They are utilized in a variety of human foods as well as animal feeds. Furthermore, oil collected from soybean seeds can be utilized to make biofuels2. Due to its high isoflavone content, it aids in the prevention of cancer and heart disease. The most popular vegetable oil in the world is soybean oil, which is also used to make biodiesel and other industrial products. It has high seed protein (36–40%) and high oil content (18–20%) and can fix atmospheric nitrogen through symbiosis, making it one of the most commonly cultivated crops worldwide3.

Since sulphur is essential for synthesizing proteins and amino acids, it is a crucial element required for the growth and development of crops. Sulphur contributes to the production of chlorophyll, glucosides, glucosinolates, and enzyme activation. It also forms sulphydryl (-SH) bonds, flavoring the oil. Thus, since oil seed crops need more sulphur, they extract more of it from the soil. It has been found that approximately 3 kg of sulphur is removed from every grains tonne of cereals, compared to 12 kg for oilseeds4. The percentage of Indian state soils with a sulphur deficit ranges from 5 to 83%5. In different Indian states, research findings indicate that 46% of soils are sulphur deficient, while 30% exhibit medium levels of bioavailable S, which can be regarded as potentially sulphur deficient. Sufficient sulphur is necessary for optimal plant growth throughout plant ontogeny since plants use roughly one-tenth the quantity of sulphur (S) compared to nitrogen (N). The decline in the efficiency of sulphur and nitrogen utilization poses a challenge for sustainable agricultural production. When oilseed crops do not receive sufficient sulphur supply, seed yield and oil content decrease6, biological nitrogen fixation (BNF), being heavily dependent on sulphur throughout the R1 to R3 stages of growth of soybean grain, starts to absorb S at these stages. If soybeans don’t receive enough nutrients, they won’t be able to achieve their maximum potential and produce the required biomass7. The growth and yield of soybean increased with the split application of sulphur as basal and at the R2 stage with 50 kg S ha[−1 8,9. The findings indicated that applying S separately, with 25 kg ha−1 basally and at the R2 stage resulted in the highest nutrient uptake, and seed and stover yields. The nutrient use efficiencies, and Zn and Fe uptake by seeds exhibited noticeable improvements with this split application approach compared. As sulphur is immobile mainly in plant tissues, it must be obtained from the soil, which accounts for the majority of its needs10,11. The lack of sulphur in the soil can negatively impact crop yield and quality, as S is essential for the synthesis of proteins and enzymes. Additionally, in the absence of sulphur, the efficiency of added nitrogen in the soil decreases. Therefore, adding S to optimize the impact of nitrogen-based fertilizers is important. The recommended nitrogen to sulphur ratio is 7:1. A ratio lower than 7 results in lower seed output5. Soybeans show a strong reaction to sulphur compared to other vital nutrients needed for their best growth. If soybeans don’t receive enough nutrients, they won’t be able to achieve their maximum potential and produce the required biomass7. The response of soybeans to applied sulphur is determined by soil type, source, management strategy, and crop. If the soil is deficient in S, the crop might need more than what the required P doses through SSP can supply, which might not be economically feasible12,13.

The two major types of sulphur fertilizers that are most frequently found are soluble sulfate and elemental forms. Plants have no access to sulphur in its elemental form. Before crops can use it, soil bacteria must first convert it to sulfate (SO4-S). As a result, S takes far longer to become available than fertilizers in the form of soluble sulfate14. This conversion process is affected by various elements in the soil environment15.The key elements that influence the oxidation of S0 include the soil’s nutritional and organic matter content, temperature, pH, humidity, aeration, and the complete aura of particular microbes16. Compared to elemental sulphur, single superphosphate is preferred because it has higher solubility in H2O and a sulphur content S-SO42− content ranging from 8 to 12%17,18. Due to the high sulphur content (between 85 and 99%), elemental sulphur may be a reasonable alternative. With inadequate nitrogen in the soil and a lack of S for crops, the higher dose of gypsum lowers the yield. Agricultural gypsum contains the macronutrient sulphur (S-SO4 −2), which has been used in fertilization programs. However, its effectiveness in displacing other nutrients underground encourages the availability of nutrients throughout the soil profile, favoring the movement of some nutrients to deeper layers and raising the soil Ca and sulphur levels19. The primary sources of sulphur used actively in agriculture are single superphosphate, ammonium sulfate, and gypsum (calcium sulfate), at 12, 24, and 15%, respectively, even though nearly 70% of the world’s sulphur production comes from elemental sulphur20. In certain modern fertilizer technologies, elemental sulphur (ES) is added to N-P fertilizers with or without ammonium sulfate (AMS)21. As ES is virtually entirely composed of S, its integration won’t substantially impact the N and P contents of N-P fertilizers. Nutrient uptake and nutrient use efficiency are the two aspects of nutritional efficiency that are most frequently mentioned in the literature. Efficiency in nutrient usage is more significant than efficiency in nutrient intake for low-fertility soils for annual crops like soybeans. Yet, to maximize nutritional effectiveness, both attributes should be included22. It is crucial to emphasize that sulphur has a direct impact on essential amino acids. Its absence can impede plant growth and the production of proteins found in seeds17. The leading causes of sulphur deficiency are the surge in the use of low-element fertilizers, the decline in the use of fungicides and pesticides that include S, and the increased use of high-yielding cultivars that quickly exhaust the soil’s available S23. Based on the above findings and the research gaps, it was hypothesized that different sources and sulphur doses will significantly influence soybean growth and yield. This experiment aimed to find the optimum source and dose of sulpur with higher S use efficiency for soybeans under temperate climates.

Materials and methods

Details of experimental site

The experimental field is located at 34° 34’ N latitude and 74° 40’ E longitude, with an altitude of 1580 m above sea level. The area has a temperate climate and receives an average annual precipitation of 812 mm. The soil at the site is silty clay loam in texture, with a neutral pH, medium organic carbon content, normal electrical conductivity, and medium in available of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and sulphur (Table 1).

Weather parameters

The entire crop-growing period experienced variable weather conditions during both years. The maximum temperature ranged from 22.71 to 33.29 °C during the 2021 crop-growing period and 22.43 to 32.13 °C during the 2022 crop-growing period. The minimum temperature ranged from 10.21 to 19.59 °C during the 2021 crop-growing period and 9.57 to 18.91 °C during the 2022 crop-growing period. Likewise, the maximum relative humidity ranged from 61.14 to 89.71% and 61.14 to 86.86% during the 2021 and 2022 crop growing periods. In contrast, minimum relative humidity ranged from 39.43 to 72.00% and 35.14 to 65.14% in the 2021 and 2022 crop-growing periods respectively. The total precipitation received was 450.70 mm and 420.80 mm during the crop-growing seasons of 2021 and 2022 (Fig. 1).

Design of experiment and treatments employed

The experimental design used was factorial Randomized Complete Block Design. The treatments consisted of two sulphur nutrient sources: Elemental Sulphur (ES) with 90–99% S and Gypsum with 18.6% S, along with five levels of sulphur (control, 30, 45, 60, and 75 kg ha−1). The recommended doses of primary nutrients, NPK applied through urea, DAP, and MOP were 20:40:20 kg ha−1. The soybean variety WSB-101 was used for the study.

Crop management practices

A well-pulverized soil up to a depth of 15–20 cm is required for the optimum growth of soybeans. The experimental plot was established through the treatments, and the manual preparation of replication borders and bunds of the experimental plots was done. It was assured that the soil had enough moisture for the seed to germinate before the sowing operation. Pre-soaked and inoculated seeds with Rhizobium spp. @ 5–10 g/kg seed for 10 min were sown in lines @ 75 kg ha−1 after shade drying at a distance of 45 cm x 10 cm. Following the recommended practice package, the necessary operations were performed as required. At 3 days after sowing (DAS), a pre-emergence spray of pendimethalin @ 1 kg a.i ha−1 was done, followed by manual hand weeding at 25–30 DAS for weed control. To prevent insect/pest infestation of pod borer (Helicoverpa armigera) and flea beetle (A. hemensis), Mancozeb (0.05%) solution was sprayed once on the standing crops. The net plot area was computed after removing two border rows from the experimental plots. Crop samples were gathered from the specified plot area for yield assessment. Manual harvesting occurred after the pods turned yellow, followed by threshing to separate the seeds from the pods.

Biometric crop monitoring

The plant height and number of branches per plant were measured at maturity. After harvesting, the biomass and seeds from each plot were sun-dried and weighed to determine the biological yield and seed yield. After threshing, the remaining part of the biomass constituted stover yield (q ha−1). The characteristics such as pod length (from the base to the tip of the pod), number of pods per plant, and the number of seeds per pod were measured after the harvest from 10 randomly selected plants in each treatment group of the experiment. The average values were then recorded. To determine the seed index (g), the weight of 100 sundried seeds drawn randomly from the collection in each treatment was recorded. The harvest index (%) is calculated as the ratio of the economic yield to the biological yield multiplied by 100. The Protein content of soybean seeds was determined using Near infra- spectroscopy29. The oil content in soybean was estimated by using the Soxhlet extraction method30.

Sulphur content in the seed and stover is determined by wet acid digestion of the sample with a 9:4 ratio (nitric acid and perchloric acid)31.The ground plant samples are digested with concentrated sulphuric acid to determine the nitrogen content26 (Subbiah and Asija, 1954) of the seeds and stover by using the micro Kjeldahl method whereas the sulphur and nitrogen uptake was estimated by the product of the sulphur and N content of seed and stover with their respective yields.

Quadratic response function

The quadratic response function suggests that with the increase in input, the response/yield increases to a maximum potential, beyond which further increase results in decreased output. This highlights the influence of the independent variable on the dependent variable to determine the optimum level of a factor that will maximize the response. Statistical analysis

To statistically explore the impact of different sulphur sources and levels on soybean performance, we analyzed the experimental data using statistical techniques applied to a factorial randomized complete block design. We used the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test at a 5% level of significance to assess the statistical differences between treatment main effects as the interaction between sulphur sources and levels was found non-significant in this study. R software version 4.2.033 (https://www.r-project.org/) with R agricolae package was used to perform the statistical analysis operations.

Results

Growth and yield attributes

Sources and levels of sulphur demonstrated significant differences (of p ≤ 0.05) among the treatments for all growth parameters studied for 2 years. Gypsum application consistently led to superior growth performance compared to elemental sulphur, as evidenced by higher values in plant height (111.68 and 107.01 cm), branches per plant (8.33 and 8.50), pods per plant (25.21 and 27.01), pod length (4.48 and 4.72 cm), and seeds per pod (3.41 and 3.25), respectively. In 2021, the application of gypsum resulted in a 5.27% increase in plant height, a 15.36% increase in branches per plant, a 12.21% increase in pods per plant, a 13.16% increase in pod length, and a 23.75% increase in seeds per pod compared to elemental sulphur. In 2022, the percentages were 6.46%, 15.41%, 10.95%, 14.83%, and 23.69% in contrast to elemental sulphur for the same parameters. Furthermore, the impact of sulphur levels on growth parameters was notable, with the application of 60 kg sulphur ha−1 demonstrating the most favorable outcomes for growth parameters (Table 2), followed by the application of 75 kg sulphur ha−1. Treatment receiving the application of 60 kg sulphur ha−1 recorded a plant height of 122.29 and 112.71 cm, 9.15 and 9.01 branches per plant, 28.70 and 30.17 pods per plant, pod length of 4.73 and 4.97 cm, and 3.71 and 3.31 seeds per pod, during 2021 and 2022, respectively with an increase of 3.77, 9.50, 9.51, 4.65, 9.97% increase in plant height, branches per plant, pods per plant, pod length, and seeds per pod as compared to 75 kg sulphur ha−1 in 2021 and the corresponding figures for 2022 were 2.76, 3.88, 8.41, 5.03 and 5.96% 75 kg sulphur ha−1, respectively.

Yield

Significant differences (p ≤ 0.05) were observed among treatments for yield parameters corresponding to different sources and levels of sulphur, demonstrating the substantial impact of sulphur management on crop productivity. Gypsum consistently outperformed elemental S, leading to higher seed yield of 18.53 and 18.71 q ha−1, stover yield of 23.60 and 26.82 q ha−1, biological yield of 43.04 and 46.24 q ha−1, seed index of 18.74 and 19.15 g and a harvest index of 43.10 and 41.64 %, respectively (Table 3). The percentage increase in seed yield, stover yield, and biological yield in the gypsum treatment compared to elemental sulphur was 12.84% and 12.13%, 6.39% and 8.83%, 8.73% and 9.90% in 2021 and 2022, respectively. The use of 60 kg sulphur ha−1 resulted in increased values for seed yield (20.37 and 20.34 q ha−1), stover yield (26.89 and 29.19 q ha−1), seed index (19.53 and 19.86 g), biological yield (46.91 and 48.25 q ha−1) and harvest index (43.47 and 42.89 %), during 2021 and 2022, respectively. These findings underscore the significance of gypsum application at higher sulphur levels, particularly 60 kg S ha−1, in increasing the growth and yield of soybeans. Applying 60 kg of S ha−1 resulted in a 4.17% increase in seed yield and a 3.97% increase in stover yield in 2021 compared to 75 kg of S ha−1. In 2022, the increase was 2.75% for seed yield and 3.08% for stover yield. Additionally, the biological yield increased by 2.62% in 2021 and 2.19% in 2022, compared to using 75 kg of S ha−1.

Sulphur content and uptake

There were significant differences (of p ≤ 0.05) observed among various sources and levels of sulphur for S content (%) and uptake (in kg ha−1) in the grain and stover of soybean. This indicates that crop sulphur management influences the crop’s uptake of S. Generally, sulphur content in the grain and stover increased with higher sulphur levels. (Table 4), peaking at 75 kg S ha−1 (0.43 and 0.45%, 0.23 and 0.24%), suggesting a direct relationship between sulphur application rate and content. Gypsum application resulted in higher S content compared to elemental sulphur in grain and stover (0.39 and 0.41%, 0.19 and 0.20%), respectively, highlighting its effectiveness as a S source. Sulphur uptake in grain and stover followed a similar trend, increasing with higher S levels, with the highest uptake observed at 60 kg S ha−1 being at par with 75 kg S ha−1. These results emphasize the importance of sulphur management practices, particularly the choice of sulphur source and application rate, in optimizing S uptake and improving crop productivity.

Nitrogen content and uptake

The effect of different sources of sulpur on N content in seed and stover was found to be non-significant, however as far as nitrogen content and uptake in grain and stover is concerned, the results revealed significant difference (p ≤ 0.05) among different levels of sulphur, indicating impact of sulphur management on nitrogen uptake by crop. Nitrogen content in grain and stover generally increases with higher sulphur levels, peaking at 60 kg sulphur ha−1 (3.46 and 3.49%, 0.55 and 0.50%) being coequal with 75 kg sulphur ha−1 (Table 5). This suggests a direct relationship between sulphur application rate and nitrogen content. Gypsum application led to higher nitrogen content in grain and stover (3.28 and 3.29%, 0.50 and 0.48%) compared to elemental sulphur, demonstrating the effect of gypsum as a sulphur source in enhancing nitrogen uptake. Nitrogen uptake in grain and stover followed a similar trend, increasing with higher sulphur levels and reaching maximum values at 60 kg sulphur ha−1 (61.13 and 58.70 kg ha−1, 14.74 and 14.08 kg ha−1) being equal to that of 75 kg sulphur ha−1. These findings highlight the importance of sulphur management practices, particularly the choice of sulphur source and application rate, in improving nitrogen uptake and potentially enhancing the overall nutrient status and productivity of soybeans.

Sulphur use efficiencies

Sulphur use efficiencies were not influenced significantly by different sources of S except partial factor productivity (PFP) which showed a significant difference between gypsum and elemental S. It was found that gypsum recorded higher values of PFP as compared to elemental S during both the years (Table 6). Significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed among S levels for agronomic efficiency (AE), apparent nutrient recovery (ANR), physiological efficiency (PE), and partial factor productivity (PFP). Both elemental sulphur and gypsum demonstrated similar AE values, indicating no adequate difference in the recovery of applied nutrients between the two sources. ANR and PE values were higher (Table 6; Figs. 2 and 3) at lower sulphur levels (30 and 45 kg S ha−1), suggesting a more efficient use of sulphur for grain production compared to higher levels (60 and 75 kg S ha−1). Similarly, PFP values are high at low sulphur levels, indicating a more efficient conversion of applied nitrogen into grain yield. These results underline the significance of ideal sulphur management for crop sustainability by indicating that reduced sulphur levels may improve soybean sulphur use efficiency. The letters in the columns indicate significant differences between treatment means within each parameter. In AE case, elemental sulphur and gypsum indicate no significant difference between them in sulphur recovery. However, in case of ANR, treatments with different sulphur levels demonstrate significant differences in sulphur recovery efficiency among these treatments. Similarly, different sulphur levels exhibit significant differences in sulphur use efficiency for grain production in the PE.

Oil and protein content and yield

Based on the data, it is clear that there are noteworthy differences (p < 0.05) between the treatments regarding the oil and protein yield and content metrics. Quadratic relationship between levels of sulphur vs. yield

Initially, as sulphur levels increase from control (0 kg sulphur ha−1) to 30 and 45 kg sulphur ha−1, there is a steady increase in seed yield. However, at higher sulphur levels i.e., 60 and 75 kg sulphur ha−1, the rate of increase in seed yield slows down (Fig. 4). This quadratic relationship suggests that there is an optimal sulphur level (around 60 kg sulphur ha−1) for maximizing seed yield in soybean. Beyond this optimal level, further increases in sulphur may not result in significant improvement in seed yield and could potentially lead to reduced yields. The quadratic relationship between levels of sulphur and stover yield indicates a similar trend to that observed with seed yield. Initially, as sulphur levels increase from the control (0 kg sulphur ha−1) to moderate levels (30 and 45 kg sulphur ha−1), there is a gradual increase in stover yield (Fig. 5). At higher sulphur levels (60 and 75 kg sulphur per hectare), the increase in stover yield diminishes, and there may even be a slight decrease in stover yield at the highest sulphur level (Fig. 4). This pattern suggests that there optimal sulphur level (60 kg ha−1 ) for maximizing stover yield in soybean. Beyond this range, further increases in sulphur may not result in significant improvements and could potentially lead to diminishing returns.

Quadratic relationship between levels of sulphur vs. sulphur use efficiency

The quadratic relationship between levels of sulphur and sulphur use efficiency parameters (AE, ANR, PE, and PFP) reveal insights into the optimal sulphur level for maximizing these parameters in soybean. As sulphur levels increased from the control (0 kg sulphur ha−1) to moderate levels (30 and 45 kg sulphur ha−1), there is gradual decrease in AE, ANR, PE, and PFP (Figs. 5 and 6). However, at higher sulphur levels (60 and 75 kg sulphur ha−1), the rate of decrease in AE, ANR, PE, and PFP diminishes, and there is a plateau (Figs. 5 and 6). This pattern suggests that there is an optimal sulphur level (around 30–45 kg sulphur ha−1) for maximizing AE, ANR, PE, and PFP in soybean, beyond which further increases in sulphur may not result in significant improvements or could lead to reduced efficiency. Understanding this relationship is crucial for determining the appropriate sulphur application rate that optimizes sulphur use efficiency while minimizing environmental impacts.

Discussion

The results of this experiment show that the growth, production, and quality of soybean crops are substantially affected by varying the sources and levels of sulphur. The results found that the use of gypsum proved to be more effective sulphur source in enhancing the growth and yield of soybean than elemental sulphur (Table 1). Compared to Gypsum, the elemental sulphur has to be oxidized to sulphate (SO42−) through microbial process, thus making the availability of sulphur to plants slower than gypsum32. Gypsum on the other hand, contains calcium which is supposed to have an additive effect on crop performance20. The findings are also supported by Singh et al.7, who concluded that applying gypsum resulted in maximum plant height and dry matter accumulation compared to other sources of S such as SSP, elemental sulphur, and Epsom salt. Other studies have also shown that applying gypsum increases the accumulation of various nutrients7,34 which may have affected the crop’s performance over elemental sulphur. The crop’s performance improved with increasing levels of S from both sources. It reached maximum plant height and branches per plant at 60 kg ha−1 of sulphur, similar to the results at 75 kg ha−1, but significantly different from the other sulphur levels. Our findings align with those of Singh et al.7who reported that soybean exhibited the highest response to sulphur levels at 60 kg ha[−135–37. Sulphur assumes a pivotal role in metabolism of nitrogen within plants. Studies have underscored the significant interaction of sulphur with nitrogen for biosynthesis of amino acids, the fundamental components of proteins13,38,39. Sulphur application has positively affected cell division, enlargement, and elongation, resulting in faster and more consistent vegetative growth in crops40. Compared to the control, Dhaker et al.41 and Noman et al.42 reported that the application of 40 kg sulphur ha−1 increased the number of pods per plant, the length of the pods, and the weight of 1000 seeds.

The grain and stover yield varied significantly with varying sources possibly due to improved growth parameters and nutrient uptake under the application of gypsum (Table 2). A similar report20 suggested that sources of sulphur had a significant impact on soybean yield. However, the findings of Dhaker et al.41 bear no coherence with our results. Varun et al.43 found that the omission of sulphur resulted in a significant decline in crop productivity. Similar results have been consistently observed in multiple studies, including those conducted by Bairwa et al.44, Sawarkar et al.45. Gupta et al.46 and Glowacka et al.47 reported significant decline in biological yield accounting for about 20% yield reduction in soybean due to the exclusion of sulphur over balanced fertilizer application. Gypsum application resulted in higher sulphur content compared to elemental sulphur in grain and stover. These observations contradict the results of Akhter et al.37 who claimed that the sources imposed an equivalent effect on sulphur content in grain as well as stover. The amount and uptake of sulphur in soybean stover and grain increased to 60 kg ha−1 sulphur with each subsequent treatment. Moreover, it was observed that sulphur concentration was more in grain than in stover. The implications agree with the results obtained by Glowacka et al.47. This was attributed to the integration of sulphur into amino acids which are transformed to organic compounds that tends to accumulate in seed37. Consequently, the sulphur content tends to be higher in grains than in stover. Jadhav et al.48, Gupta et al.46 and Raghuwanshi et al.49 claimed similar findings.

Gypsum application consistently yielded maximum oil and protein content and yield compared to elemental sulphur (Table 6). The results were supported by Raghuwanshi et al.49. The oil content was not significantly affected by increasing levels of sulphur but increased progressively upto 75 kg S ha−1, which was attributed to the direct involvement of sulphur in lipid and fatty acid synthesis. At the same time, the acetyl-CoA enzyme itself contains sulphur as highlighted by Rathore et al.50. These results agree with Ahmed et al.51 and Venugopal et al.52. It has been found that sulphur is required to synthesize fatty acids and S-containing amino acids, essential components of oil and protein53. Approximately 50–60% of the nitrogen needed by soybeans is obtained through nitrogen fixation10,11. The process of biological nitrogen fixation slows down after reaching its peak at the flowering stage, and the plant starts redistributing internal nitrogen during pre-flowering if its needs are unmet. However, application of sulphur helps to maintain the activity of root nodules and favours fixation of nitrogen for a prolonged period which in turn serves high yield and good protein content15,49. This quadratic relationship suggests that there is an optimal sulphur level (60 kg S ha−1) for maximizing seed yield in soybean. Beyond this optimal level, further increases in sulphur may not result in significant improvement in seed yield and could potentially lead to reduced yields. Also, the optimal sulphur level (around 30–45 kg S ha−1) was found for maximizing AE, ANR, PE, and PFP in soybean, beyond which further increases in sulphur may not result in significant improvements or could lead to reduced efficiency. These findings are also supported by the results of Moreira et al.54.

Conclusions

The two-year experiment has yielded valuable insights into the effects of different sources and levels of sulphur on the growth, yield, and sulphur use efficiencies of soybeans. Gypsum demonstrated a superior influence on growth and yield compared to elemental S, though no significant difference was observed between the two sources for sulphur use efficiencies except PFP. The crop’s response improved as S levels increased, peaking at 60 kg ha−1 and then declining beyond that level, suggesting that 60 kg S ha−1 was optimum and beneficial for enhancing the growth and yield of soybean. The yield response to sulphur levels for both sources followed a quadratic pattern, indicating that 60 kg ha−1 is the ideal S application rate. Additionally, sulphur use efficiencies were analyzed across different sulphur levels and sources, emphasizing the importance of optimizing S application for maximum efficiency and precise management. From the present study, it was therefore concluded that the application of sulphur @ 60 kg ha−1 was found suitable for enhancing the soybean yield under temperate climatic conditions.
