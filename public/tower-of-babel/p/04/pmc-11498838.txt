Characterization of Health and Safety Resources for Athletes With Sickle Cell Trait at NCAA Institutions
Authors: Susan W. Yeargin, Courtney M. Meyer, Rebecca M. Hirschhorn, Abbi D. Lane, Shawn M. Arent, Clint R. Haggard
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.

Introduction

Sickle cell trait (SCT) is a hereditary hematologic condition in which a person inherits one β-A gene from one parent and one β-S gene from the other parent (25). Although SCT is usually benign, exercise can change the RBC’s normal round shape to a crescent “sickling” shape, thereby decreasing their ability to transport oxygen and log jamming vessels (11,20). The primary clinical concern for athletes with SCT is sudden death. Additional clinical concerns include compartment syndromes, gross hematuria, splenic infarction, and hyposthenuria (7,12,13,22,26,35). Seventeen or more deaths have been attributed to SCT in collegiate student-athletes since 1982. In all cases, deaths occurred following vigorous exercise (14,29). High-intensity exercise, without proportional rest, is considered a primary reason red blood cells change from round to crescent shape, in athletes with SCT. The crescent shaped cells are stiff and cause blood vessels to occlude by “log jamming.” If exercise continues, more vessels occlude and distal muscle tissue becomes ischemic. Athletes will experience muscle weakness and pain, particularly in the low back and muscles that were being used to complete the exercise. Massive rhabdomyolysis from the ischemic tissue overwhelms the kidneys and eventually precipitate a cardiac event (12,26) Therefore, exertional sickling can be prevented by instituting appropriate work-to-rest ratios, usually managed by strength and conditioning coaches (SCCs) knowledgeable about the condition (7,28). Morbidity and mortality can be prevented by onsite athletic trainers (ATs) trained to intervene early when signs and symptoms of exertional sickling arise (7,28).

Sickle cell trait disproportionately affects Black persons, as the estimated annual incidence of SCT in newborns is 73 cases per 1,000 Black births compared with only 3 cases per 1,000 White births (27). The prevalence of SCT in Division I universities (non-historically Black colleges or universities [HBCU] is approximately 1% of all athletes (17) but may affect up to 6% of athletes at HBCU institutions (18). However, data regarding SCT prevalence in athletes were either collected in a small sample or obtained 15 years ago (17,18). Updated numbers are needed to support screening strategies and help healthcare administrators with resource requests.

Despite early screening, very few adults are aware of their SCT status (21,34,38). Between 2010 and 2013, the National Collegiate Athletic Association (NCAA) mandated SCT status verification at all division levels (23). Student-athletes were required to (a) complete a testing screen upon arrival at the university, (b) show previous test results indicating their SCT status, or (c) sign a waiver declining to be screened (23). As of 2023, options 1 and 2 are still required. Student-athletes with SCT, who declined testing, when that option was available, may be at an increased risk for unrecognized exertional sickling events. Although NCAA institutions must offer SCT status screening, they are not required to cover the cost. Research has indicated that HBCUs have lower institutional budgets and resources then non-HBCUs (5,9), which could impact the level of care available for monitoring an athlete with SCT or intervening promptly. However, it is not known if a disparity of best-practice screening procedures and athlete care services exists between HBCU and non-HBCU athletic departments.

Determining the current culture of health and human performance services provided to athletes with SCT could provide insight into areas of maintenance or improvement for institutions. Therefore, the aims of our study were 4-fold: (a) report prevalence of student-athletes with SCT, (b) determine which screening practices NCAA institutions most commonly use, (c) describe the health and human performance services provided to athletes with SCT, and (d) ascertain if differences between NCAA designations and divisions exist.

Methods

Experimental Approach to Problem

A web-based, cross-sectional, survey design was used to query healthcare administrators at NCAA athletics departments regarding SCT characteristics. The independent variables were designation (HBCU or non-HBCU) and Division (I, II, and III). The electronic survey recorded dependent variables of SCT prevalence, and the health and human performance services provided to athletes with SCT. University of South Carolina's Institutional Review Board approval was obtained before school recruitment.

Subjects

Division and designation (https://web3.ncaa.org/directory/) stratified the publicly available list of NCAA institutions. A computer-generated random sample from each stratified list was used to contact the healthcare administrator on record. If the email was not accurate, a similar representative within the athletic department (i.e., director of sports medicine, head AT of a sport, compliance officer, associate athletic director) was contacted using the institution's directory. The email included an invitation to participate (informed consent), explaining the study's purpose and time commitment. The only exclusion criteria were NCAA institutions in the process of changing their division status at the time of data collection and institutions in US territories (i.e., PR). Using previous survey research in HBCU and non-HBCU institutions (18,30), an a priori power analysis (G*Power software, power set = 0.95; α = 0.05, parametric test family, 2 group comparison independent samples), indicated that a minimum sample size of 52 institutions was necessary to obtain adequate power for comparisons between the designations. Because designation type is unbalanced within NCAA member institutions (95% non-HBCU, 5% HBCU), a minimum of 10 HBCU was used as a goal for the sample.

Procedures

A 22-item survey with radio button selectors and fillable fields was used to gather specific variables of interest: institutional demographics (student-athlete numbers, etc), number of athletes with SCT, SCT screening options used by student-athletes at the institution, financial support of screening, number of full-time ATs, number of SCCs, education of staff in SCT, and the existence of SCT safety policies and procedures (all survey items are provided in article tables or see Table, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/JSCR/A525). Once initiated, the survey could be accessed by the institutional representative as many times as needed to complete it. Study data were collected and managed using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) tools hosted at the University of South Carolina (15).

Institutional representatives were given 2 weeks to begin the survey, with reminders at 2 time points (4 and 8 days). After 2 weeks, additional institutions were contacted from the randomized list, until the minimum target sample size was met. Each institution was given a unique identifier number to protect confidentiality. At the end of data collection, data were exported from REDCap, cleaned, coded, and analyzed. Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics were calculated for each questionnaire response. Prevalence of student-athletes with SCT was calculated only for institutions reporting zero waivers to ensure that the prevalence calculated was based on known SCT status; the SCT status for student-athletes who elected to sign the waiver is unknown. This was determined by using the number of student-athletes with SCT, divided by the total number of student-athletes, multiplied by 100. The prevalence was calculated for each individual institution, then averaged, and reported as a mean percentage overall or by designation or division. A ratio of AT or SCC to student-athlete was calculated to account for different institution sizes. The number of staff (AT or SCC) was divided by the total number of student-athletes multiplied by 100. These ratios were calculated for each individual institution, then averaged, and reported as the ratio per 100 student-athletes. Interactions were examined with a 2 × 3 Analaysis of Variance for the prevalence of SCT and methods of screening used by institutions. Differences in staff ratios between divisions was determined by a Kruskal-Wallis H test followed by a Dunn's post hoc test. Differences in staff ratios between designations were determined with a Mann-Whitney U test. A χ2 test of association followed by cross tabulations was used to compare health and human service question responses between divisions or designations. An alpha level of 0.05 was used for all tests. When checking equal variances, if Levene's test was nonsignificant, simple main effects were used for post hoc testing. If significant, a conservative approach (p < 0.001) was taken to interpret the interaction and post hoc testing using the harmonic means (i.e., type III approach), and least significant difference was used instead (32). If comparison tests were significant, the corresponding value for effect size was included (i.e., φ, ε2, ηp2). Statistical analysis was conducted with Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) (Version 28.0; IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Armonk, NY).

Results

School Demographics

Sixty colleges or universities completed the survey, with 73.3% (n = 44) designated as non-HBCU and 26.7% (n = 16) as HBCU. This resulted in a response rate of 10.3% (44 responses/429 non-HBCU emailed) and 29.6% (16 responses/54 emailed), respectively. Because recruitment was stratified, divisions were almost evenly represented, with most responses (38.3%, n = 23) from Division II institutions. Total student-athletes at each institution ranged from 50 to 943. All school demographics are provided in Table 1.

Resources and Policies Related to Exertional Sickling Prevention or Care

The number of ATs (1–21) and SCCs (0–10) employed varied across institutions. The mean AT to student-athlete ratio was 1.3 per 100 student-athletes (range: 0.4–3.6 per 100 student-athletes). The mean SCC to student-athlete ratio was 0.5 per 100 student-athletes (range: 0.0–2.4 per 100 student-athletes). There was a significant main effect for division (H(2) = 18.264, p < 0.001, ε2 = 0.31), with Division II (p = 0.007) and Division III (p < 0.001) significantly different for AT to student-athlete ratios as compared with Division I (see Table, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/JSCR/A525). The same results were found for SCCs to student-athlete ratios (H(2) = 22.062, p < 0.001, ε2 = 0.37), with Division II (p < 0.001) and Division III (p < 0.001) reporting significantly different from Division I. There was no difference in the AT to student-athlete ratio (U = 30.75, p = 0.854) or the SCC to student-athlete ratio (U = 29.55, p = 0.481) between the designations.

Respondents indicated that all ATs were educated to recognize exertional sickling (n = 60, 100.0%) and most (n = 48, 80.0%) were trained to develop exercise protocols for athletes with SCT. Institutions reported that SCC coaches were educated to identify exertional sickling (n = 36, 60.0%) and develop exercise protocols for athletes with SCT (n = 26, 43.3%). Most schools report (n = 36, 60.0%) that they do not have an institutional-level policy that an AT must be present for conditioning sessions and do not have supplemental oxygen present at training facilities (n = 31, 51.7%). Yet most (n = 42, 70.0%) reported having a policy and procedures section regarding the prevention, recognition, and treatment of exertional sickling. Regarding this section of the policies and procedures manual, all of the institutions (n = 60, 100.0%) indicated that SCC coaches did not help develop the policies and procedures on exertional sickling. However, most institutions (n = 35, 58.3%) educated their SCCs on the policies and some (n = 6, 10.0%) included a signature providing affirmation of the education. All survey responses regarding resources are provided in the Supplemental Digital Content (see Table, http://links.lww.com/JSCR/A525). There was no association with designation for all questions related to resources (p > 0.05). As for division, there was no association for questions related to resources (p > 0.05), except for one. A greater proportion Division II and Division III institutions do not have a policy for ATs to be present at conditioning session as compared with Division I (χ2 = 15.603, p < 0.001, φ = 0.51).

Discussion

Exertional sickling has been one of the top 4 causes of all-sport athlete deaths in the NCAA and accounted for upward of 42% of exertional football deaths (4,13). Because instituting the screening mandate in 2010, there has been a significant drop in deaths related to SCT (6). Previous research has explored opinions of athletes regarding the screening mandate and prevalence of SCT as identified by screening (17,19). This study was the first to expand the sample to provide a more complete and accurate estimate of SCT in student-athletes and determine current culture surrounding the implementation of the screening mandate.

Institutions that responded to the survey served a large range of student-athlete numbers. Institutions from each US census region were also represented (40); however, most were from the Southern portion. Southern schools manage SCT frequently and might have been more willing to answer the survey (1). Both institutional designations (HBCU and non-HBCU) are accounted for in our sample. However, the HBCU email list was exhausted 3 times over before an adequately powered sample was obtained. This recruitment challenge may be an indication of possible administrative barriers that their healthcare providers must contend with in terms of the screening mandate. Nonetheless, our results have a broad representation and can be generalized to other NCAA member institutions.

Only those schools that reported having a record of SCT status for all individuals were used to calculate the prevalence of SCT for the student-athlete body. Average prevalence for non-HBCU was 1%, which matches our previous work in a smaller sample (17). The average prevalence for SCT at HBCU institutions was 5%, once again similar to previous research that was conducted before the screening mandate (18). Yet some institutions reported that upward of 10% of their student-athlete body was SCT positive, a compelling statistic regarding resources needed and supportive buy-in from all stakeholders at the institution. The divisions did not statistically differ from each other in terms of prevalence. Not surprisingly, however, the prevalence of SCT-positive athletes at HBCUs was significantly higher than at non-HBCUs. Yet non-HBCUs report that 14% of all student-athletes are Black, and football teams report that 36% of student-athletes are Black (24). Therefore, it was thought that the prevalence of SCT would have been higher at non-HBCU institutions than what we report for that designation. Our previous research in this area found a clinical, but not statistical, difference in observed versus expected prevalence of SCT in student-athletes (17). Further research is needed to examine why actual prevalence seems lower than expected prevalence. Understanding the prevalence of student-athletes with SCT is important for preparation purposes. Institutions can estimate the number of student-athlete with SCT for incoming cohorts to budget for testing and purchasing resources (supplemental oxygen tanks). High schools, in which there is no screening mandate, could use our prevalence data as a starting point to estimate how many of their own student-athlete may have SCT to support prevention strategies and resources as well.

A main purpose of the study was to determine whether institutions were using the waiver, not as an option, but as the primary way to adhere to the NCAA mandate. This practice was confirmed in our sample because 10% of institutions exclusively had their athletes complete the waiver. These schools reported 3,320 student-athletes who signed waivers, representing a large number who likely do not know their personal status/diagnosis. Our results indicate that Division III universities were using the waiver method most. We did not ask institutions why this method was used. It could be assumed that this was a way to minimize budget ramifications associated with on-site testing, and faster for student-athlete to begin participation. Requiring definitive SCT testing is positive change, as knowing SCT status is associated with reductions in sudden death (6). However, that means institutions, particularly Division III schools, will need to budget accordingly or may choose to place the cost of SCT testing on the student-athlete instead. Our results indicate that more HBCU's than non-HBCU's have student-athletes bring a SCT laboratory test (not a newborn screen) obtained before arrival on campus. This practice places the time, logistical, and expense burden of SCT testing on the athlete.

About one-third of institutions use solubility testing to identify hemoglobin S, which is a simple, accurate, and affordable method to provide SCT status (16,33). However, it does not provide a breakdown of the individual's hemoglobin profile (39). Understanding the hemoglobin profile is beneficial as other hemoglobin abnormalities can be identified that cause or protect against anemic-like clinical symptoms (33,39). A third of institutions follow a positive solubility test with electrophoresis testing to obtain a full breakdown of hemoglobin subtypes, which requires additional expense. Most of these respondents were at Division I institutions. One-third of respondents did not know what kind of testing was used at their institution. Of these, 5 schools used waivers in 100% of their student-athletes, leaving 14 schools with no knowledge of their testing procedure.

Few schools knew the costs associated with labortory testing. Some reported only $5, assumingly for solubility testing only, which has been reported in the previous literature (39). The other end of the reported range ($200), seems inflated, even with solubility and electrophoresis testing together possible due to school location or laboratory contracts. Institutions should work with third party laboratories to obtain bulk pricing for testing. Previous articles have cited only $30 for electrophoresis testing (39). Of those reporting costs, about one-third indicated the student-athlete absorbed the expense themselves. Although the financial burden is real, institutions should work toward covering the costs for new cohorts, as known status is associated with effective prevention strategies and reductions in sudden death (6,7,26), Another way to improve affordability is to assist student-athletes in obtaining newborn screens. Since 2006, the United States has instituted newborn screening programs for hemoglobinopathies, including SCT (2,37). Most health department websites have links with information of how to obtain these records, until there is a culture to keep record of the newborn screen with families (36).

Exertional sickling prevention strategies, such as heat acclimatization, hydration access, and activity modifications, based on wet-bulb globe temperature are implemented by both ATs and SCCs (7,26). Therefore, we calculated staff ratios based on the total student-athlete population at the institution not just those with SCT. We reported an average of 1.3 per 100 student-athletes but with a large range of ratios. This can provide baseline information, in addition to the previous work (3), for future research to build upon from the perspective of health and human performance optimization. The lower the ratio, the better attention to prevention, recognition, and management of exertional sickling (7,26).

Recent policies by the NCAA to ensure consistent credentials of SCCs provide an obvious connection between their presence and role in injury prevention (23). Athletic trainers are taught information regarding exertional sickling per educational program standards and are assessed on this knowledge on the national Board of Certification test (10). The same is not true for SCC educational programs, meaning that it is the responsibility of ATs at the school to equip them with the knowledge of prevention and recognition of exertional sickling (28). Encouragingly, more than half of institutions did indicate that their SCCs were educated on SCT. However, the largest impact SCCs can make in the prevention of exertional sickling is the development of appropriate work-to-rest ratios (8). Only 40% of institutions indicated that the SCCs were educated on using this prevention strategy. It could be those who answered the survey did not fully understand the SCCs' knowledge in this area unless they had educated them on this matter. ATs and SCCs should hold an interprofessional meeting to discuss this prevention strategy in detail before off-season conditioning.

The first line management of exertional sickling is triage of the situation and use of supplemental oxygen with a rebreather mask (7,28). Only half of institutions reported having both an on-site AT and supplemental oxygen at all facilities. One-quarter of institutions reported an emergency related to exertional sickling, supporting the need for on-site resources and staffing to intervene early (26). Prevention and management strategies are most likely to be adopted and implemented if a policy exists (31). Budgets, location of tanks, and refilling need to be discussed and formalized into policy by the sports medicine team to optimize access for all teams conditioning or practices. One-quarter of the institutions in our sample reported not having a policy beyond the screening itself. It is best practice for institutions to have a section in their policy and procedures manual on exertional sickling beyond just testing to encompass prevention, recognition, and management. More importantly because SCCs are vital to preventing and possibly recognizing exertional sickling if an AT is not on-site, they should be involved in creating, acknowledging, and implementing these policies. None of the institutions in our sample indicated that this practice being operationalized in the current culture. For institutions without a policy on exertional sickling, involving knowledgeable SCCs from the beginning drafting stage could be beneficial to create thorough policies.

Our study expanded upon previous research describing the prevalence of athletes with SCT and provided an update on current screening practices by NCAA institutions. Even so, our study did have limitations. Our sample size is not large, but similar to previous survey research in the collegiate setting (18,30). The designation group samples were unequal, which represents the actual population of NCAA institutions. We also excluded those institutions who were changing divisions, which limits fully describing the current culture. The cross-sectional research design only provides a snapshot of prevalence and screening at the time the survey was completed. We are unable to infer any cause-effect relationships between the variables. Although surveys were anonymous (names of respondents and schools were not requested), responses might have been biased in wanting to provide socially desirable answers. The study also had nonresponse bias because we had a large number of institutions, particularly HBCUs, never complete the survey. Finally, only calculating the prevalence of SCT for institutions reporting zero waivers may overlook the true prevalence of SCT among student-athletes because it excludes those who opted for waivers.Practical ApplicationsHistorically Black colleges or university institutions have a higher prevalence of student-athletes with SCT than non-HBCU institutions, which should be considered when ordering and preparing resources for incoming cohorts. The NCAA's recent policy to remove waivers as an option was supported because some institutions were using it with 100% of student-athletes. Respondents reported a variety of testing methods and other screening options. Institutions should attempt to obtain bulk laboratory pricing to cover the testing costs and help the student-athlete obtain their newborn screen. Athletic trainers and SCCs should review SCT laboratory results together before the season begins, so yearly prevalence can be determined and prevention strategies be developed. Smaller divisions have lower staffing and supplemental oxygen access; therefore, creative solutions need to be considered to ensure that best-practice health care and performance optimization is still provided to student athletes with SCT. Some NCAA institutions need to create policy and procedure sections beyond just screening. The AT and SCCs need to have a strong collaborative relationship to develop policy and procedure sections together, host education sessions to review signs and symptoms, discuss training plans, and facilitate the regular implementation of prevention strategies.

Practical Applications

Historically Black colleges or university institutions have a higher prevalence of student-athletes with SCT than non-HBCU institutions, which should be considered when ordering and preparing resources for incoming cohorts. The NCAA's recent policy to remove waivers as an option was supported because some institutions were using it with 100% of student-athletes. Respondents reported a variety of testing methods and other screening options. Institutions should attempt to obtain bulk laboratory pricing to cover the testing costs and help the student-athlete obtain their newborn screen. Athletic trainers and SCCs should review SCT laboratory results together before the season begins, so yearly prevalence can be determined and prevention strategies be developed. Smaller divisions have lower staffing and supplemental oxygen access; therefore, creative solutions need to be considered to ensure that best-practice health care and performance optimization is still provided to student athletes with SCT. Some NCAA institutions need to create policy and procedure sections beyond just screening. The AT and SCCs need to have a strong collaborative relationship to develop policy and procedure sections together, host education sessions to review signs and symptoms, discuss training plans, and facilitate the regular implementation of prevention strategies.
