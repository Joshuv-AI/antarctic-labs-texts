A retrospective study on the predictive value of D-dimer for mortality in patients with acute arterial occlusive mesenteric ischemia
Authors: Yaliu Yang, Peng Liu, Ruilong Gao, Zhengqin Zhai, Yifeng Zhou, Jiahui Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute arterial occlusive mesenteric ischemia (AOMI) is a catastrophic vascular emergency associated with exceedingly high mortality rates. The lack of reliable early prognostic tools creates an urgent need for accessible biomarkers to guide management. This study aimed to evaluate mortality outcomes and assess the prognostic utility of baseline serum D-dimer levels in patients with AOMI.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of all AOMI patients admitted to China-Japan Friendship Hospital between January 2010 and August 2024. Patients were stratified by 30-day survival status. The predictive value of serum D-dimer levels, measured within 24 h of diagnosis, for mortality was assessed using multivariate Cox regression analyses. Prognostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis.ResultsAmong the 61 patients included, the all-cause mortality rate was 47.5% at 30 days and 54.1% at one year. Elevated baseline D-dimer levels were independently associated with both 30-day mortality [hazard ratio (HR) 1.158, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.080–1.243; p < 0.001] and 1-year mortality (HR 1.277, 95% CI 1.134–1.439; p < 0.001). ROC analysis indicated strong predictive accuracy, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.850 (95% CI 0.755–0.946) for 30-day mortality and 0.837 (95% CI 0.738–0.936) for 1-year mortality. The optimal prognostic cut-off values were 0.578 mg/L (sensitivity 82.8%, specificity 75.0%) for 30-day mortality and 0.516 mg/L (sensitivity 90.9%, specificity 60.7%) for 1-year mortality.ConclusionsAOMI continues to portend a grave prognosis. Background

Acute mesenteric ischemia (AMI) is a life-threatening vascular emergency caused by a sudden reduction in intestinal blood flow, with an estimated annual incidence of 7–12 cases per 100,000 people [1–4]. Despite advances in treatment, mortality rates remain high, showing only a modest decline from 68.7% in cohorts before 2000 to approximately 55% in recent reports [5]. The prognosis is especially poor in arterial occlusive mesenteric ischemia (AOMI), which accounts for 65–75% of AMI cases and arises through two main mechanisms: mesenteric arterial embolism (EAMI, 50%), often of cardioembolic origin (particularly in atrial fibrillation), and arterial thrombosis (TAMI, 15–25%), which usually develops on pre-existing atherosclerotic lesions [1, 6].

Although the widespread use of computed tomography angiography (CTA) has improved diagnostic accuracy, the absence of validated prognostic biomarkers continues to hinder risk stratification, treatment decisions, and timely intervention. Thus, the development of reliable predictive biomarkers remains a critical unmet need in the management of this time-sensitive condition.

D-dimer, a fibrin degradation product, has been established as a valuable biomarker in the diagnosis, evaluation of treatment efficacy, and prognostic assessment of thrombotic disorders [7, 8]. Previous studies on D-dimer in AMI have primarily focused on its diagnostic utility, demonstrating high sensitivity (96%) but limited specificity (40%) [9]. In terms of prognostic value, an observational study of 50 patients with acute superior mesenteric venous thrombosis found that serum D-dimer levels served as an early indicator of poor outcomes [10]. However, the relationship between serum D-dimer levels and disease severity in AOMI remains unclear.

This retrospective study was designed with two principal aims: first, to characterize patient demographics and treatment patterns and to determine short-term (30-day) and long-term (1-year) mortality in AOMI patients at our institution; second, to investigate the prognostic utility of serum D-dimer levels at initial diagnosis, both as an independent predictor and in integration with other clinical variables.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective analysis was conducted on patients discharged from China-Japan Friendship Hospital in Beijing, China between January 2010 and August 2024 with a diagnosis of AMI, including its subtypes EAMI and TAMI. Eligibility required confirmation of AOMI via at least one of the following modalities: enhanced computed tomography, CTA, mesenteric angiography, or pathological examination.

Exclusion criteria comprised: age below 18 years at diagnosis, absence of D-dimer test results in the medical record, loss to follow-up, chronic mesenteric ischemia unless acute embolism or thrombosis was radiologically or pathologically confirmed, and non-occlusive arterial or venous mesenteric ischemia.

Prospective written informed consent was obtained from all participants at the time of hospital admission. The standard admission consent form explicitly authorized the use of clinical and operative data for future research. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of China-Japan Friendship Hospital and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision).

Data collection and clinical definition

A comprehensive retrospective review of medical records was performed to collect the following data: [1] demographic characteristics; [2] medical history and comorbidities; [3] recent surgical history; [4] smoking status; and [5] current medications. Comorbid conditions were rigorously defined according to standardized criteria: hypertension, congestive heart failure, stroke, and peripheral artery disease (PAD) were identified based on physician documentation in medical records; coronary artery disease (CAD) was defined as a history of angina and/or myocardial infarction, irrespective of revascularization; chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined as a sustained reduction in glomerular filtration rate (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² for ≥ 3 months, calculated using the CKD-EPI equation) [11, 12]; atrial fibrillation (AF) required confirmation via medical record documentation or electrocardiographic evidence.

Patients on maintenance dialysis were excluded due to their distinct pathophysiology. Additional clinical data collected included: [1] etiology of AOMI (embolic vs. thrombotic); [2] time interval from symptom onset to diagnosis; [3] treatment modalities (antimicrobial therapy, anticoagulation regimen, and revascularization procedures); [4] procedure-related complications.

Laboratory parameters measured within 24 h of diagnosis included: D-dimer (measured by latex-enhanced immunoturbidimetry; STA®-Liatest D-Di, Stago); complete blood count [white blood cells (WBC), monocytes]; C-reactive protein (CRP, immunoturbidimetric assay); serum lactate (Lac); renal and liver function markers [creatinine (Cr), alanine aminotransferase (ALT)]; lipid profile [high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C)]. The monocyte-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (MHR) was calculated as an emerging inflammatory biomarker associated with endothelial dysfunction and cardiovascular risk [13]. All data collection adhered to standardized protocols and definitions consistent with current clinical guidelines.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were all-cause mortality at 30 days (short-term) and 1 year (long-term) after the diagnosis of AOMI. The secondary endpoint aimed to evaluate the association between early-stage D-dimer levels, along with other clinical and laboratory variables, and both short- and long-term prognosis in AOMI patients.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac, Version 29.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.), with two-tailed tests and a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Data normality was evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables are expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normal distributions or mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data, while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages.

The study population was stratified into two groups based on clinical outcomes: [1] 30-day mortality (survivors vs. nonsurvivors) and [2] 1-year mortality (detailed in the Appendix). Inter-group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, Mann-Whitney U test for non-normal distributions, Pearson’s χ² test for categorical variables, and Fisher’s exact test when appropriate.

Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify mortality predictors. Variables with p < 0.1 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate models. Results are presented as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) across three models: unadjusted (Model 1), adjusted for age and sex (Model 2), and a fully adjusted model (Model 3) incorporating other significant univariate predictors (p < 0.05) and clinically relevant confounders.

The prognostic performance of baseline D-dimer was assessed through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with optimal cutoff values determined by the Youden index (maximizing sensitivity + specificity-1). Survival probabilities were estimated using Kaplan-Meier analysis and compared via log-rank test based on the established D-dimer threshold.

Results

Study population

A total of 87 patients with a final diagnosis of AMI, EAMI, or TAMI were identified between January 2010 and August 2024. After applying predefined exclusion criteria, 17 patients were excluded due to diagnostic ineligibility: 8 for lack of confirmatory imaging or pathological evidence, 5 for chronic mesenteric arterial ischemia, 2 for non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia, and 2 for mesenteric venous thrombosis. An additional 9 patients were excluded due to missing data (6 lost to follow-up and 3 lacking baseline D-dimer levels). Thus, the final study population included 61 eligible patients (Fig. 1).

Clinical characteristics and outcomes

Among the 61 included patients, all-cause mortality was 47.5% (n = 29) at 30 days and 54.1% (n = 33) at one year. The cohort had a median age of 70.0 years (IQR 64.5–78.0) and was predominantly male (67.2%, n = 41). Cardiovascular comorbidities were highly prevalent, including AF (63.9%), hypertension (62.9%), and atherosclerotic disease (CAD 31.1%, stroke 27.9%, PAD 3.3%). No significant differences in baseline demographics, comorbidities, or pre-admission antithrombotic use (including anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents) were observed between 30-day survivors and non-survivors (Table 1.).As shown in Table 2, nonsurvivors had a significantly higher prevalence of EAMI compared to survivors (65.5% vs. 34.5%, p = 0.029). Early diagnosis (within 24 h of symptom onset) was achieved in 45.9% (n = 28) of patients. Clinical presentations included abdominal pain (most common), bloating, nausea, vomiting, hematochezia, diarrhea, fever, intestinal obstruction, and peritonitis, with bowel obstruction being significantly more frequent in nonsurvivors than survivors (44.8% vs. 15.6%, p = 0.013). No significant differences were observed between groups in time to diagnosis or other presenting symptoms (Table 2).

Baseline laboratory parameters revealed significant elevations in non-survivors, including WBC (16.9 vs. 9.5 × 10⁹/L, p = 0.003), MHR (28.3 vs. 15.3, p = 0.011), CRP (67.4 vs. 17.2 mg/L, p = 0.033), ALT (24.0 vs. 17.0 U/L, p = 0.008) and D-dimer (4.8 vs. 1.5 mg/L, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Treatment was determined by a multidisciplinary team. Overall, 57.4% (n = 35) received conservative management (gut rest, analgesia, antimicrobial/antithrombotic therapy, parenteral nutrition), while 42.6% (n = 26) underwent emergency intervention. Interventions included endovascular revascularization [n = 9; aspiration embolectomy, superior mesenteric artery (SMA) stenting, or catheter-directed thrombolysis], surgery (n = 15; bowel resection with or without SMA embolectomy), or hybrid therapy (n = 2). The treatment strategy itself was not associated with a significant difference in survival. Broad-spectrum antibiotic use was more frequent in non-survivors (96.6% vs. 78.1%, p = 0.055), while a non-significant trend toward survival was observed with anticoagulant therapy (84.4% vs. 72.4%, p = 0.255) (Table 3).

The most common complications were systemic embolic events (26.2%), septic shock (23.0%), acute kidney injury (21.3%), and heart failure (21.3%). Systemic embolism distribution included: renal (n = 5, 8.2%), splenic (n = 3, 4.9%), cerebral (n = 5, 8.2%), coronary (n = 1, 1.6%), and peripheral arterial (n = 2, 3.3%) events. Non-survivors had a significantly higher incidence of septic shock, acute kidney injury, respiratory failure, and heart failure (p < 0.05 for all) (Table 3).

Predictors of mortality and prognostic performance of D-dimer

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to evaluate associations with mortality. We systematically compared fourteen key variables between groups: age, gender, underlying etiologies, smoking status, presence of abdominal bloating and bowel obstruction, baseline serum levels of WBC, ALT, CRP, D-dimer and MHR at diagnosis, administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics and anticoagulants, and therapeutic strategy selection. After adjustment for confounders, multivariate analysis identified elevated baseline D-dimer (HR = 1.158, 95% CI 1.080–1.243; p < 0.001) and bowel obstruction (HR = 3.436, 95% CI 1.454–8.122; p = 0.005) as independent predictors of 30-day mortality (Tables 4 and 5). Anticoagulant therapy was associated with a significant protective effect (HR = 0.277, 95% CI 0.101–0.762; p = 0.013) (Table 4). Similarly, baseline D-dimer was an independent predictor of 1-year mortality (HR = 1.277, 95% CI 1.134–1.439; p < 0.001) (Table 6 and S4).

Discussion

Analyzing 61 consecutive AOMI patients, this retrospective study documented alarmingly high mortality rates (30-day: 47.5%; 1-year: 54.1%), underscoring the critical need for improved risk stratification. Our analysis reveals that elevated baseline D-dimer levels are not only a diagnostic marker but also a powerful, independent prognostic indicator for both short- and long-term survival. This was confirmed by multivariable Cox regression (30-day: HR = 1.158, p < 0.001; 1-year: HR = 1.277, p < 0.001) and ROC analysis, which showed excellent predictive accuracy (AUCs of 0.850 and 0.837). We further identified bowel obstruction as a key risk factor (HR = 3.436, p = 0.005) and anticoagulation therapy as a significant protective intervention (HR = 0.277, p = 0.013). These results significantly advance the clinical relevance of D-dimer from diagnosis to prognosis, suggesting that its early measurement could be pivotal in guiding timely and aggressive management for high-risk patients.

The mortality rates observed in our AOMI cohort are consistent with previous reports, underscoring the condition’s persistently severe prognosis and providing critical context for our findings. A systematic review of 45 observational studies from 1966 to 2002 documented a declining mortality trend over four decades, from 92.2% (Ottinger LW et al., 1967) to 48.2% (Endean ED et al., 2001), which included patients managed conservatively [14–16]. More recent data reflect this enduring high risk. Bednarsch et al. reported a postoperative in-hospital mortality of 58.7% within an AOMI subgroup treated between 2009 and 2021 [17]. Similarly, a retrospective study of 173 AMI patients from 2015 to 2020, which included both invasive and non-invasive treatments, documented a 30-day mortality of 48% and a 1-year mortality of 61%; the 30-day mortality specifically for AOMI patients was 41.8% [18]. Our observed rates of 47.5% at 30 days and 54.1% at one year align closely with these contemporary benchmarks, further emphasizing the critical need for improved prognostic and therapeutic strategies.

Currently, clinical evidence supporting the use of D-dimer for predicting AMI severity remains limited. However, emerging studies suggest its potential value. Experimental work in a rat strangulated hernia model by Tufan et al. indicated that D-dimer could be a valuable parameter for the early detection of intestinal necrosis [19]. This finding is supported by clinical research; a retrospective case-control study of 151 AMI patients identified elevated admission D-dimer levels as an independent risk factor for in-hospital mortality, with a sensitivity of 77.7% and a specificity of 73.9% [20]. Furthermore, an observational study has proposed D-dimer as an early marker of disease severity in veno-occlusive mesenteric ischemia (VAMI) [10]. Adding to this body of evidence, a Turkish retrospective study demonstrated that D-dimer levels vary significantly among AMI etiologies, with the highest levels observed in embolic AMI (2.90 µg/mL FEU), followed by thrombotic (2.10 µg/mL FEU), non-occlusive (1.30 µg/mL FEU), and veno-occlusive (0.80 µg/mL FEU) subtypes (p = 0.04) [21].

Our findings extend existing literature by demonstrating that baseline D-dimer levels, obtained within 24 h of diagnosis, serve as an independent predictor of both short- and long-term mortality in AOMI. The prognostic accuracy for 30-day (AUC 0.850; optimal cutoff 0.578 mg/L) and 1-year mortality (AUC 0.837; optimal cutoff 0.516 mg/L), with high sensitivity at each timepoint, positions D-dimer as a clinically useful prognostic marker that could aid in risk stratification across multiple clinical timeframes.

While D-dimer elevation is a nonspecific marker reflective of multiple pathological processes—including thrombosis, inflammation, and tissue injury—its prognostic value in early AOMI is underscored by three key observations. First, baseline measurements were obtained prior to the onset of secondary complications such as infection or acute kidney injury, confirming its role as an early indicator. Second, D-dimer remained an independent predictor of mortality after rigorous multivariate adjustment for CRP and other potential confounders. Third, and most critical from a clinical standpoint, D-dimer demonstrated high sensitivity (82.8%) for early mortality, a characteristic that parallels its established role in other acute vascular syndromes like pulmonary embolism and deep vein thrombosis. This consistency across vascular emergencies suggests that, when interpreted within the appropriate clinical context, D-dimer elevation provides meaningful prognostic information despite its inherent lack of specificity. Collectively, these findings advocate for the incorporation of D-dimer assessment into the early risk stratification of AOMI patients.

Our study found that an early elevation in D-dimer levels is a significant predictor of mortality in AOMI patients. The identified cutoffs—0.578 mg/L for 30-day mortality and 0.516 mg/L for 1-year mortality—provide clinically actionable thresholds for risk stratification. These values enable clinicians to [1] identify high-risk patients for intensive monitoring [2], expedite multidisciplinary consultation with vascular surgery, gastroenterology, and critical care teams, and [3] initiate timely, targeted interventions to improve outcomes.

We identified anticoagulation as a protective factor for survival in AOMI patients, a finding supported by existing evidence and guidelines. An international retrospective study (n = 370) found early full-dose anticoagulation improved 30-day survival (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.44–0.84, p = 0.02) [22], a result bolstered by a French single-center study showing an even greater benefit (OR 0.09, 95% CI 0.02–0.5, p = 0.004) [18]. Although the prospective SURVI study found no significant prevention of intestinal necrosis [23], current guidelines strongly recommend early anticoagulation—particularly for occlusive AOMI—to prevent subsequent thrombotic complications [2].

Bowel obstruction at admission was associated with a significantly increased risk of 30-day mortality (HR 3.44, 95% CI 1.45–8.12, p = 0.005), though not with 1-year survival. Pathophysiologically, AMI-induced intestinal hypoxia causes smooth muscle dysfunction and paralytic ileus. Clinically, obstruction is a critical indicator of advanced disease, signifying progression to intestinal paralysis or necrosis, and mandates immediate intervention through revascularization or surgical resection.

Although nonsurvivors exhibited a tendency toward higher levels of WBC, CRP, MHR, and ALT, univariate Cox regression revealed no significant associations with mortality in our cohort. This finding contrasts with established literature. Elevated MHR has been correlated with adverse outcomes in ischemic stroke and acute coronary syndrome [24, 25] and demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy for in-hospital mortality in pulmonary embolism (sensitivity 89.3%, specificity 82.0%) [26]. Similarly, a prior study linked elevated preoperative ALT levels to mortality in AMI patients, suggesting a potential prognostic role for hepatic injury [27]. The lack of significance in our analysis may be due to sample size or population-specific factors.

Our findings regarding age and lactate levels contrast with some previous studies. While Leone et al. identified advanced age and lactate > 2.7 mmol/L as independent risk factors for ICU mortality [28], and Otto et al. confirmed lactate’s significance (though not age) in a surgical cohort [17], these associations were not statistically significant in our cohort. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in our study population, which included non-surgical patients and those managed in general wards, as well as our limited sample size.

The non-specific clinical presentation of AMI necessitates a diagnosis driven primarily by a high index of clinical suspicion. This is especially critical given that cardiovascular diseases—including atrial fibrillation, hypertension, and atherosclerotic disease—are the most prevalent comorbidities in this patient population. The diverse pathways of our cohort illustrate this diagnostic challenge. While most patients were admitted through the emergency department and transferred to specialized units, a significant subset (n = 7) were directly admitted to the cardiovascular department for management of their primary cardiac conditions. In one illustrative case, a patient was admitted to the cardiac ICU for cardiogenic shock and placed on veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V-A ECMO) but ultimately succumbed to AMI. This case underscores a vital point: as the use of V-A ECMO for cardiogenic shock expands, cardiovascular intensivists must be vigilant for AMI as a lethal complication. This is supported by a study reporting a 9% incidence of AMI in patients on V-A ECMO, with a consequent mortality of 100% [29]. Therefore, since AMI often presents atypically or develops during hospitalization for other conditions, there is a crucial need to enhance the capabilities of non-specialist physicians in the general ward. Improving competencies in early recognition, rapid diagnosis, accurate risk stratification, and timely intervention is essential to optimizing survival outcomes.

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. Its retrospective design is inherently susceptible to inaccuracies in historical medical records, potentially affecting data quality and completeness. The absence of randomized treatment allocation, particularly for anticoagulation, introduces the potential for selection bias and unmeasured confounding, meaning observed associations should not be interpreted as causal. Finally, the relatively small sample size, while reflective of the disease’s rarity, limits the statistical power and may affect the generalizability of our findings. These constraints underscore the necessity for future prospective, randomized studies with larger cohorts to validate and extend our conclusions.

Conclusion

In summary, AOMI continues to carry a high risk of early mortality. This study identifies elevated baseline D-dimer as an independent predictor of both 30-day and 1-year mortality, highlighting its utility as a practical prognostic biomarker for early risk stratification. Incorporating D-dimer into the initial clinical assessment may facilitate timely multidisciplinary intervention and improve patient outcomes. Validation of these findings in larger, prospective, multi-center cohorts is essential to refine risk stratification models for AOMI.
