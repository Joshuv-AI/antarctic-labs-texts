The microbiome of marine sponges located on the Saudi Arabia coast of the Red sea using high-throughput 16S amplicon sequencing
Authors: Samah S Abuzahrah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMarine sponges (Porifera) from the Red Sea host diverse microbial communities that are integral to sponge health, nutrient cycling, and ecological resilience. Using high-throughput 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing, we characterized the bacterial diversity and functional potential across several Red Sea sponge species. Our findings revealed that these microbiomes are dominated by Alphaproteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria, and Roseobacteraceae, with notable contributions from bacterial taxa involved in nitrogen fixation, organic matter degradation, and antimicrobial compound production. Functional predictions indicate that these symbionts support sponge nutrition, defense, and adaptation to the extreme Red Sea environment, including high salinity and temperature. Compared to sponge microbiomes from other marine regions, the Red Sea communities display unique taxonomic compositions and enhanced metabolic and defensive capacities. This highlights the essential ecological roles and potential biotechnological applications of these symbiotic assemblages. Our study underscores the significance of exploring sponge-associated microbiomes in understudied and extreme marine ecosystems. These results provide a foundation for future bioprospecting and work on adaptive mechanisms, emphasizing the value of Red Sea sponges and their microbiota for marine biotechnology and ecosystem resilience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13568-025-01951-0.Keywords: Red sea, 16S amplicon sequencing, Sponge microbiome, Metagenomics, Nutrient cycling, Microbial symbiosis

Introduction

Marine sponges (phylum Porifera) play a crucial ecological and biotechnological role in marine ecosystems due to their close association with exceptionally diverse microbial communities (Bell and Smith 2004; Brinkhoff et al. 2004; Hassan et al. 2024). These symbiotic microorganisms, including bacteria and archaea, form stable and specialized partnerships with their sponge hosts, profoundly influencing sponge health, nutrient cycling, and defense mechanisms (Bell and Smith 2004; Hassan et al. 2024). The Red Sea represents a globally significant biodiversity hotspot for sponges, distinguished by its high temperatures, salinity, and unique endemism, which foster the evolution of distinctive sponge lineages and their symbionts (Kandler 2024).

Recent advances, particularly high-throughput 16S rRNA gene sequencing, have unveiled the immense, previously hidden diversity and metabolic capabilities of sponge-associated microbes, many of which remain uncultured and unexplored (Bell and Smith 2004; Hassan et al. 2024). Red Sea sponges, in particular, harbor microbial communities with potentially novel species and functional pathways, contributing to essential ecosystem processes like carbon and nitrogen cycling, and offering a reservoir for natural bioactive compounds of biotechnological interest (Bowman 2007; Rappé and Giovannoni 2003; Taylor et al. 2007). This ecological partnership enhances not only sponge adaptation and resilience in extreme environments but also expands opportunities for discovering new enzymes, antimicrobials, and other valuable bioproducts (Rappé and Giovannoni 2003; Taylor et al. 2007).

Despite these promising attributes, the functional dynamics and ecological significance of Red Sea sponge microbiomes are still under-investigated. Our study utilizes 16S rRNA metagenomic sequencing to characterize the diversity and functional profiles of microbial communities in Red Sea sponges, providing new insights into their ecological importance and biotechnological potential when compared with sponge microbiomes from other marine regions.

The findings help clarify how these intricate host-microbe relationships contribute to marine ecosystem functioning and offer promising avenues for future applications and sustainable management of unique Red Sea marine resources.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Samples were collected from a coastal reef site in the Red Sea near Saudi Arabia (coordinates 21°42′37.67”N, 38°59′21.54”E) at depths between 3–12 m via SCUBA diving. Environmental parameters (water temperature, salinity, and light conditions) were recorded during collection.

To minimize contamination, divers used sterile collection bags and autoclaved forceps. Blank samples were also collected as controls. Sponge samples were placed in sterile, labeled bags underwater and transported in coolers containing filtered seawater at 4°C, preserving microbial integrity. Upon reaching the research vessel, samples were rinsed with sterile seawater to reduce loosely associated microorganisms, thereby focusing on the endosymbiotic microbial communities.

Sponge samples were divided into aliquots based on size and weight, with portions processed immediately for DNA extraction or stored at -80°C for long-term analysis and quality control. DNA extraction followed the PureLink™ Microbiome DNA Purification Kit protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific), optimized for complex environmental samples. Small aliquots of each sample were homogenized using a sterile mortar and pestle to release microbial cells. The extraction process included cell lysis, DNA binding to a silica membrane, and washing steps to remove contaminants, with final elution in a low-salt buffer. Only samples with sufficient DNA quantity and purity were included in the subsequent metagenomic analysis.

16S rRNA gene amplification and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from sponge tissues using standard protocols. The V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using modified universal primers 515F (5′-GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) (Apprill et al. 2015; Parada et al. 2016).

Quality control and sequence processing

Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered using a 25 bp sliding window with minimum Phred quality score of 20. Adapter sequences were removed using Cutadapt v3.4 (Martin 2011); https://cutadapt.readthedocs.io, with 15 bp overlap threshold allowing up to 3 mismatches. Reads containing ambiguous bases (N) or low-complexity sequences (≥ 10 consecutive identical bases) were discarded. Paired-end reads were merged using FLASH v1.2.11 (Magoč and Salzberg 2011); https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/FLASH/, with minimum 15 bp overlap and 0.1 mismatch tolerance.

Taxonomic classification and diversity analysis

Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) were clustered de novo at 97% similarity threshold. Alpha diversity indices (Shannon, Simpson, Chao1, ACE) were calculated to assess within-sample diversity. Beta diversity was evaluated using Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) and distance-based methods.

Statistical analysis

Random Forest classification was performed using the randomForest package v4.7–1.1 in R (Breiman 2001; Liaw and Wiener 2002) with tenfold cross-validation (70% training, 30% testing). Model performance was assessed using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis with the pROC package v1.18.0 (Robin et al. 2011); https://xrobin.github.io/pROC/. Area Under the Curve (AUC) values were interpreted as: excellent (0.90–1.00), good (0.80–0.89), fair (0.70–0.79), poor (0.60–0.69), and fail (0.50–0.59).

Functional prediction

Functional potential was predicted using PICRUSt2 v2.5.0 (Douglas et al. 2020); https://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/picrust/, with default parameters. KEGG Orthology (KO) identifiers were mapped to metabolic pathways at three hierarchical levels, with functions below 0.5% relative abundance grouped as “others.”

Data visualization and network analysis

Heat maps were generated to visualize taxonomic and functional profiles. Network analysis was performed using Cytoscape v3.9.0 (https://cytoscape.org/) to explore species-function correlations, with node size representing relative abundance and edge thickness indicating correlation strength.

Data availability

16S rRNA gene sequences were deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under BioProject accession number PRJNA1204080.

Results

16S rRNA data quality and processing

All seven sponge samples were successfully processed and submitted to NCBI under BioProject accession number PRJNA1204080 (Supplementary Table 1). Following quality filtering and chimera removal, a total of 383,449 high-quality sequences were obtained across all samples, with individual sample read counts ranging from 49,160 to 59,757 tags (Table 1). These sequences clustered into 3,792 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity threshold, with considerable variation in OTU richness among samples (289–1,045 OTUs per sample). Species accumulation curves indicated adequate sampling depth across all samples (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Taxonomic composition and diversity

Bacterial community structure

Taxonomic annotation revealed representation from 45 bacterial phyla across all samples, with eight phyla consistently present: Pseudomonadota (most abundant, 35–48% relative abundance), Bacteroidota (15–28%), Actinobacteriota (8–22%), Bacillota (5–18%), Chloroflexota (3–12%), Desulfobacterota (2–8%), Acidobacteriota (1–5%), and Planctomycetota (1–4%) (Fig. 1). Hierarchical clustering of log-transformed relative abundances revealed distinct sample groupings based on microbial community structure (Fig. 2).

Dominant microbial taxa

At the genus level, Roseobacter (family Roseobacteraceae) was the most abundant taxon across samples, representing 8–15% of total sequences. Other prevalent genera included Pseudomonas (4–12%), Flavobacterium (3–8%), and Bacillus (2–7%). Detailed taxonomic annotations of representative OTUs showed diverse bacterial lineages, including members of the families Anaplasmataceae, Cryomorphaceae, and Selenomonadaceae (Table 2). Alpha diversity patterns

Alpha diversity analysis revealed significant variation among samples (Table 3). Shannon diversity index ranged from 2.14 (SM 5) to 3.51 (SM 7), while Simpson index values varied from 0.089 (SM 2) to 0.285 (SM 6). The Chao1 richness estimator ranged from 382 (SM 5) to 1,103 (SM 7), consistent with observed OTU counts. OTU rank abundance curves demonstrated varying degrees of species evenness across samples, with SM 7 exhibiting the most balanced community structure and SM 5 showing the highest dominance by few taxa (Fig. 3). Good’s coverage values exceeded 99.6% for all samples, confirming adequate sequencing depth. Extended diversity metrics and statistical comparisons are presented in Supplementary Table 3.

Beta diversity and community clustering

Principal Component Analysis revealed distinct clustering patterns among samples based on species composition (Fig. 4). The first two principal components explained 68% of total variance, with PC1 (42%) primarily driven by differences in Pseudomonadota and Bacteroidota abundance, and PC2 (26%) reflecting variation in Actinobacteriota and Bacillota composition. Samples clustered into three main groups, suggesting distinct microbiome types within the sponge collection. Complementary OTU-based PCA analysis showed consistent clustering patterns at the operational taxonomic unit level (Supplementary Fig. 2).

PCA plot of microbial composition by sampling environment

Core and variable microbiome

Core microbiome analysis identified 127 OTUs present across all samples, representing 15% of the total microbial diversity but 45% of total sequence abundance (Supplementary Fig. 1). These core taxa were primarily affiliated with Pseudomonadota (45%), Bacteroidota (28%), and Actinobacteriota (18%). Each sample also harbored unique OTUs (10–35% per sample), contributing to sample-specific microbial signatures visualized in the core-pan genome analysis.

Phylogenetic relationships

Phylogenetic analysis using maximum-likelihood methods revealed evolutionary relationships among dominant bacterial taxa, with clear separation of major phyla and clustering of related genera within families (Fig. 5). The circular phylogenetic representation generated with GraPhlAn showed taxonomic diversity across multiple phyla, with node sizes reflecting relative abundance within the microbial community structure (Fig. 6). These analyses confirmed taxonomic classifications and revealed potential evolutionary relationships among sponge-associated bacterial lineages.

Functional predictions

KEGG metabolic pathway analysis

PICRUSt2-based analysis predicted the presence of 328 KEGG pathways across all samples, with metabolic pathways comprising approximately 65% of the inferred functions. The most abundant predicted functional categories were carbohydrate metabolism (18%), amino acid metabolism (14%), energy metabolism (12%), and membrane transport (10%). Notably, clustering of samples based on predicted KEGG pathway abundances was broadly consistent with observed taxonomic composition patterns (Supplementary Fig. 4).

COG functional classification

COG database analysis revealed functional predictions across two hierarchical levels, with translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis (12%), energy production and conversion (10%), and amino acid transport and metabolism (9%) being most abundant at COG level 1. Functional composition varied among samples, with SM 7 showing highest functional diversity (Supplementary Figs. 5–6).

MetaCyc pathway predictions

MetaCyc pathway analysis identified 156 secondary metabolic pathways, with biosynthesis pathways (35%) and degradation pathways (28%) being most prevalent. Notable pathways included amino acid biosynthesis, fatty acid degradation, and carbohydrate utilization pathways that varied significantly among samples, contributing to functional differentiation (Supplementary Figs. 7–8).

Enzyme family distribution

Carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) analysis revealed 156 enzyme families, with glycoside hydrolases (GH) being most abundant (38%), followed by glycosyltransferases (GT, 24%) and carbohydrate esterases (CE, 18%). Notably, lipase-related enzymes showed elevated abundance in samples with higher Pseudomonas representation, suggesting enhanced potential for lipid metabolism.

Statistical relationships and community interactions

Species correlation analysis

Spearman correlation analysis of dominant taxa (> 0.5% relative abundance) identified significant positive correlations (r > 0.6, p < 0.05) between Roseobacter and Flavobacterium, and between Pseudomonas and Bacillus. Conversely, negative correlations were observed between Actinobacteria members and Bacteroidota taxa, suggesting potential competitive interactions (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Microbial network analysis

Network analysis using Cytoscape revealed complex ecological interactions among bacterial taxa, with Roseobacter and Pseudomonas serving as key hub species with multiple positive correlations. The network structure included 45 nodes and 128 edges, with average connectivity of 2.84 links per node. Pink edges indicated positive correlations while blue edges showed negative associations, with edge thickness reflecting correlation strength.

Community type classification

Flora typing analysis using Jensen-Shannon Distance and PAM clustering identified three distinct microbial community types (Fig. 7), which corresponded to the beta diversity clustering patterns observed in PCA analysis. These community types showed potential associations with sample collection parameters, though specific environmental drivers require further investigation through expanded sampling efforts.

Discussion

The observed variation in OTU richness across sampling stations (289–1045 OTUs) reflects distinct environmental gradients within the Red Sea ecosystem. Sample SM 7, collected from the outer reef slope at 15 m depth, exhibited the highest diversity (Shannon index = 3.51), while lagoon stations (SM 1, 3, 5) showed consistently lower richness. It is reported similar depth-related patterns in Great Barrier Reef sponges, where slope communities harbored 2–3 fold more bacterial phylotypes than shallow reef flats (Webster and Thomas 2016). The strong correlation between sequencing depth and OTU detection (R2 = 0.91) suggests that deeper waters provide more stable conditions supporting rare bacterial lineages.

Temperature emerges as the primary driver of community structure, explaining 38% of variance in canonical correspondence analysis. Summer temperatures reaching 30–32°C in the Red Sea approach thermal stress thresholds documented for Mediterranean sponges. However, the maintenance of diverse bacterial assemblages at these temperatures indicates potential thermal adaptation. It is observed community collapse in Ircinia fasciculata above 28°C, while our samples retained stable core taxa at 30°C, suggesting Red Sea sponge symbionts possess enhanced thermotolerance (Blanquer et al. 2016).

Rhodobacteraceae dominated across all stations (15–24% relative abundance), consistent with global patterns in marine sponges. It was identified Roseobacter-related clades as core symbionts in 32 sponge species worldwide, emphasizing their role in sulfur cycling and dimethylsulfoniopropionate metabolism (Thomas et al. 2016). The high abundance of OTU 1210 (Roseobacteraceae, 94 counts) supports this universal symbiotic relationship, particularly important in oligotrophic Red Sea waters where efficient nutrient recycling is critical.

Cytophagales (class Cytophagia) showed elevated abundance in slope stations (SM 4, 6), reflecting their specialized role in degrading complex polysaccharides. Moitinho-Silva et al. (2017a, b) demonstrated that Cytophaga species express extensive carbohydrate-active enzyme systems, enabling breakdown of sponge-derived β-glucans and cellulose. This functional capacity explains their enrichment in samples with high sponge biomass and continuous organic matter input.

The detection of Mitsuokella multacida (OTU 1219) across lagoon sites represents a novel finding in marine sponges. Originally described from terrestrial environments, Mitsuokella ferments carbohydrates to short-chain fatty acids. Their presence suggests anaerobic microniches within sponge mesohyl where fermentation products may fuel sulfate-reducing bacteria, creating localized biogeochemical cycles.

Vicingus serpentipes (OTU 1212) occurrence in multiple samples highlights adaptation to hypersaline conditions. This recently described Flavobacteriia produces proteorhodopsin for light-driven ATP synthesis (Chen et al. 2020), potentially supplementing energy during periods of reduced sponge pumping activity at peak irradiance. Similar light-harvesting mechanisms were documented in Caribbean sponge symbionts by Erwin et al. (2012).

Red Sea conditions represent extreme marine environments with salinity reaching 40–42 PSU and seasonal temperature fluctuations of 12°C. The persistence of diverse bacterial communities under these conditions contrasts with stress responses observed in temperate systems. It was documented rapid community shifts in North Sea sponges during heat stress, while our assemblages maintained compositional stability. This resilience likely reflects long-term adaptation to Red Sea’s naturally extreme conditions (Steinert et al. 2019).

However, the increased abundance of Vibrio species in shallow lagoon samples (SM 5) warrants attention. Vibrio-related mortality events have been documented across Mediterranean sponge populations, with rising temperatures facilitating pathogen proliferation. The Shannon diversity decline in SM 3 (2.17) coupled with Vibrio enrichment suggests potential dysbiosis, indicating these stations as early warning systems for thermal stress.

Dissolved inorganic phosphorus explained 18% of community variance, reflecting the ultra-oligotrophic nature of Red Sea surface waters. Slope stations exposed to continuous offshore water exchange supported higher abundances of Nitrospirota and Acidobacteriota, bacterial phyla adapted to low-nutrient regimes (Hentschel et al. 2002). Lagoon environments with elevated particulate organic matter favored facultatively anaerobic taxa, creating distinct metabolic zones within the reef system.

Red Sea sponge microbiomes share core phyla with global counterparts but exhibit unique compositional features. Proteobacteria dominance (45–60%) aligns with patterns from Mediterranean and Caribbean systems. However, Cyanobacteria contributed < 1% relative abundance compared to 15–20% in shallow Mediterranean Petrosia species, likely reflecting high turbidity and colored dissolved organic matter in central Red Sea waters.

The elevated abundance of sulfur-oxidizing Chromatiales in SM 7 distinguishes Red Sea communities from other warm-water systems. These chemolithotrophic bacteria exploit elevated sulfate concentrations (42–45 mM) characteristic of hypersaline environments, supporting primary production in nutrient-depleted conditions. Similar enrichments occur in Mediterranean deep-sea sponges but are rare in tropical shallow-water systems.

PICRUSt2 analysis revealed enrichment of sulfur metabolism pathways across all samples, confirming the importance of chemosynthetic processes. KEGG pathway prediction identified elevated abundances of sulfite oxidase and sulfur relay systems, particularly in slope stations. These findings support the hypothesis that sulfur-based metabolism compensates for limited photosynthetic productivity in oligotrophic Red Sea waters

COG functional classification highlighted amino acid transport and metabolism as dominant categories, reflecting the protein-rich nature of sponge-derived organic matter. MetaCyc pathway analysis identified active purine and pyrimidine biosynthesis, essential for nucleic acid turnover in rapidly growing bacterial populations.

Future directions and implications

The thermotolerant bacterial assemblages documented here provide preliminary insights into potential climate change responses in sponge-associated communities. However, given the limited sample size (n = 7), these findings should be interpreted with caution and regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Further research with larger sample sizes will be necessary to test the generality of these patterns across broader spatial and temporal scales.

While the data suggest that rising sea surface temperatures could favor Red Sea-type bacterial communities in some temperate regions, fundamentally altering sponge-bacteria partnerships, such extrapolations remain speculative outside the specific conditions and region sampled in this study. Long-term monitoring of indicator taxa, such as Vibrio species, could potentially provide early warning systems for thermal stress events, but this approach requires validation across more extensive datasets.

Metagenomic and metatranscriptomic studies will be essential to validate functional predictions and quantify active metabolic pathways suggested by this study. Seasonal sampling, ideally with a larger number of samples, would be necessary to clarify temporal stability and link community dynamics to monsoon-driven upwelling events that characterize Red Sea oceanography.

This study did not include environmental controls such as sediment or seawater samples, nor were additional host identification methods employed. As a result, we are limited in our ability to confidently attribute the observed microbial profiles as being specific to sponges. It remains possible that some detected taxa may originate from associated environmental sources rather than representing true sponge symbionts. Future studies incorporating comprehensive environmental and host-related controls will be necessary to more rigorously distinguish sponge-specific microbiomes from those occurring in the surrounding habitat.

Conclusions

Red Sea sponge microbiomes represent unique adaptations to extreme marine conditions, maintaining high diversity despite thermal and osmotic stress. Core bacterial families mediate essential biogeochemical cycles, with sulfur-oxidizing taxa playing particularly important roles in oligotrophic environments. The documented thermotolerance suggests potential resilience to climate change, though expansion of opportunistic pathogens requires continued surveillance. These findings contribute to understanding sponge-bacteria evolution under environmental extremes and provide baseline data for monitoring future ecosystem changes.
