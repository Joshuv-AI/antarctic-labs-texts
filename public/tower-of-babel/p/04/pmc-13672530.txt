IL-17–associated Pro-inflammatory Programs in Aortic Valve Interstitial Cell Osteogenic Differentiation: JUN as a Candidate Regulator
Authors: Yining Dai, Siyu Kong, Yupeng Yang, Yeshen Zhang, Yu He, Pengda He, Haobin Liu, Ning Tan, Pengcheng He, Ling Xue, Zhenyang Fu, Yuanhui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD) involves fibro-calcific remodeling, in which osteogenic differentiation of valvular interstitial cells (VICs) is a key process. Although inflammation is implicated, uncertainties persist regarding how defined pro-inflammatory pathways are transcriptionally coupled to the VIC osteogenic program at the cell-intrinsic level. We implemented an interleukin-17 (IL-17) pathway-guided transcriptomic strategy in a pure human VIC osteogenic differentiation model. RNA sequencing identified osteogenesis-associated differentially expressed genes and IL-17-related inflammatory signatures. Functional enrichment and multilayer network analyses were applied to prioritize candidate regulatory nodes, followed by in vitro validation. An IL-17-associated transcriptional program was activated during VIC osteogenic differentiation and was enriched for stress-activated and MAPK-related inflammatory pathways. Network-based analyses consistently identified the AP-1 transcription factor JUN as a central regulatory node within this IL-17-linked signature. Experimentally, JUN expression increased during VIC osteogenic differentiation, and JUN silencing attenuated calcification. IL-17 A rapidly increased the p-c-JUN/JUN ratio, and prolonged treatment upregulated JUN and IL-17RA in a concentration-dependent manner. Exogenous IL-17 A stimulation enhanced calcification-related phenotypes in VICs, which were partially reversed by JUN knockdown. In a chronic kidney disease-induced mouse CAVD model, JNK inhibition with SP600125 reduced valve calcification and leaflet thickness. IL-17-associated inflammatory signaling is engaged during VIC osteogenic differentiation and may promote calcification, partially through a JUN-dependent mechanism. These findings provide insight into how inflammatory programs interface with osteogenic transcription and offer a framework for dissecting inflammation-driven calcification in CAVD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10753-026-02565-9.

Background

Calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD) is a progressive disorder marked by fibrosis and mineral deposition, and effective pharmacological options to slow disease progression remain limited [1, 2]. A key cellular event in this process is the osteogenic differentiation of valvular interstitial cells (VICs), which activates osteogenic programs and drives mineralized nodule formation [1, 3]. Although inflammatory signaling is frequently implicated in CAVD and is thought to amplify tissue remodeling, it remains unclear, in a VIC-centered context, how defined pro-inflammatory pathways are coupled to the osteogenic transcriptional program [4]. In particular, the intracellular regulatory node(s) that transduce interleukin-17 (IL-17)-associated inflammatory activation are known to play important roles in chronic inflammation-driven calcification-related diseases (e.g., vascular calcification) [5, 6]; however, their role in VIC osteogenic remodeling has not been well defined, limiting mechanistic understanding and target prioritization.

To address this gap, we implemented an IL-17 pathway-guided workflow using a pure VIC model. Using GSE252266, which profiles human aortic valve interstitial cells cultured in growth medium or osteogenic medium for 6 days, we first identified osteogenesis-associated differentially expressed genes [7]. We then focused on IL-17 pro-inflammatory pathway-related genes to capture the inflammation-linked transcriptional program activated during osteogenic induction. Enrichment and network-based analyses were integrated to prioritize candidate regulators within this IL-17-centered signature, followed by functional validation in VIC osteogenic differentiation assays.

The innovation of this study is threefold. First, by centering the analysis on an in vitro VIC osteogenic induction system, we focus on cell-intrinsic inflammation-to-osteogenesis transcriptional reprogramming and reduce confounding effects present in tissue-level datasets. Second, we used an IL-17 pathway-guided, convergent screening strategy that integrates differential expression with network analyses to identify key hub regulators efficiently. Third, we closed the evidence gap through mechanistic validation. Exogenous IL-17 A, the major effector of the IL-17 family, is used to demonstrate that the pro-calcific effects of IL-17 are at least partly JUN-dependent, thereby defining an actionable IL-17-JUN linkage axis.

Together, our findings support a model in which IL-17-associated inflammatory signaling is engaged during VIC osteogenic differentiation and promotes calcific phenotypes through a JUN-dependent mechanism. By combining pathway-guided transcriptomic prioritization with functional validation, this study clarifies how a defined inflammatory pathway interfaces with the VIC osteogenic program and provides a framework for future efforts to disrupt inflammation-driven VIC calcification.

Methods

Study Design

This study integrated transcriptomic analyses of an in vitro human aortic valve interstitial cell model (aortic valve interstitial cells, AVICs; also referred to as valvular interstitial cells, VICs) undergoing osteogenic differentiation with mechanistic validation experiments. RNA-seq data (GSE252266) were used to identify osteogenic differentiation-associated differentially expressed genes (DEGs), characterize IL-17-linked pro-inflammatory pathway activation in VICs, and prioritize candidate regulators. An independent human aortic valve tissue RNA-seq cohort (GSE148219) was additionally used for external validation of key transcriptomic findings; only adult control and calcified tricuspid aortic valve (TAV) samples were included, whereas bicuspid aortic valve and fetal valve samples were excluded. The transcription factor c-JUN (JUN) was then functionally tested in VIC osteogenic differentiation assays, and its role in IL-17-driven pro-calcific effects was evaluated using JUN knockdown combined with IL-17 stimulation.

Bioinformatics Analysis

Data Acquisition (GEO)

RNA-seq data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession GSE252266 (“Exploring molecular profiles of osteogenically differentiated aortic valvular interstitial cells”) [7]. The dataset includes six samples generated on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (GPL24676): NC1-NC3 (VICs cultured in growth medium) and OST1-OST3 (AVICs cultured in osteogenic differentiation medium) for 6 days. The raw read count matrix provided by GEO was downloaded for downstream analyses. For external validation, RNA-seq data from GSE148219 were also obtained from GEO. To minimize biological heterogeneity, only adult control and calcified TAV samples were retained for validation analyses, while calcified bicuspid aortic valve and fetal valve samples were excluded.

Data Preprocessing and Normalization

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.0). Raw counts were processed using DESeq2, including library-size normalization (size-factor estimation) and dispersion modeling. For visualization and unsupervised analyses, normalized expression values were obtained using the variance-stabilizing transformation implemented in DESeq2. Lowly expressed genes were filtered using DESeq2 default independent filtering during differential expression analysis. GSE148219 was processed separately from GSE252266 and used only for external validation; no merged analysis across datasets was performed.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

PCA was performed on the variance-stabilized expression matrix to evaluate global transcriptional differences between the NC and OST groups. PCA was computed using the prcomp function in R and visualized using ggplot2 (version 3.4.2) and ggpubr (version 0.6.0).

Differential Expression Analysis and Visualization

Differential expressions between the OST and NC groups was assessed using DESeq2. P values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Genes meeting |log2 fold change| > 1 and adjusted P < 0.05 were defined as DEGs. Volcano plots were generated using ggplot2 (version 3.4.2). Heatmaps of representative DEGs were generated using pheatmap (version 1.0.12) after Z-score scaling across samples.

IL-17 Pathway Gene Set and Overlap Analysis

To evaluate IL-17-linked pro-inflammatory activation during VIC osteogenic differentiation, DEGs were intersected with the MSigDB Reactome gene set REACTOME_INTERLEUKIN_17_SIGNALING [8]. Overlapping genes were visualized using heatmaps (pheatmap, version 1.0.12) and groupwise expression comparisons (ggpubr/ggplot2). Because GSE252266 is a pure AVIC culture model, the results reflect cell-intrinsic transcriptional programs rather than immune-cell infiltration effects.

Independent Validation in Human Valve Tissue Cohort

To assess whether the key findings from the discovery AVIC model were also detectable in human CAVD tissue, selected results were examined in GSE148219. Validation analyses were restricted to adult control versus calcified TAV samples. The expression patterns of key candidate genes and/or the directionality of the IL-17-related overlap signature identified in GSE252266 were evaluated in this independent cohort.

Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) Analysis and Module Detection

A PPI network was constructed for the IL-17-signature overlapping genes using the STRING database with a minimum interaction score of 0.400 [9]. The network was visualized in Cytoscape (version 3.9.1) [10]. Dense modules were identified using the MCODE plugin with default parameters. Hub genes were prioritized using the cytoHubba plugin with the maximal clique centrality method, and top-ranked candidates were selected for downstream regulator prioritization.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) and pathway enrichment analyses, including Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Reactome where applicable, were performed using WebGestalt [11]. Terms with P < 0.05 were considered significant. Enrichment results were visualized using ggpubr (version 0.6.0) and ggplot2 (version 3.4.2).

Regulatory Network Analyses (TF–gene and miRNA–mRNA)

Upstream transcription factors (TFs) were predicted using the NetworkAnalyst platform with TF-binding profiles from the JASPAR database [12, 13]. Predicted TFs for candidate hub genes and/or module genes were intersected to identify common upstream TFs. Candidate miRNAs targeting module or hub genes were retrieved from publicly available miRNA-target resources (miRDB [14, 15], DIANA-microT [16], and miRWalk [17]). TF–gene and miRNA-mRNA regulatory networks were assembled and visualized in Cytoscape (version 3.9.1) to prioritize central regulators, highlighting JUN for experimental validation.

Human Aortic Valve Tissue

Control aortic valves were obtained from heart transplant recipients and from patients with Stanford type A acute aortic dissection or aortic valve regurgitation without calcification. Calcified valves (CAVD group) were obtained from patients with severe aortic stenosis who underwent aortic valve replacement surgery. All patients underwent preoperative cardiac ultrasound or aortic computed tomography (CT) scan to assess the aortic valves. Patients with congenital valvular disease and those with a medical history of endocarditis, rheumatic heart disease, or diabetes were excluded. Resected aortic valves were washed with PBS and then prepared for pathological sectioning and primary cell isolation or stored at -80℃ for later study.

Cell Culture

Human AVICs were seeded and cultured in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells at passages 2–5 were used. VICs were cultured in a specialized medium for human AVICs. For nagetive control, VICs were culture in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Osteogenic differentiation of VICs was induced using osteogenic medium (OM) containing high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 10 mmol/L β-glycerophosphate, 0.1 µmol/L dexamethasone, and 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid. When VICs reached > 80% confluence, calcification was induced by culture in OM.

Experimental Validation In Vitro

VIC Osteogenic Differentiation, IL-17 A Stimulation, and JUN Knockdown

Human VICs were cultured in growth medium and induced to undergo osteogenic differentiation by incubation in osteogenic medium. Recombinant human IL-17 A (GB300036, Servicebio, China) was added to the osteogenic medium to activate IL-17 A signaling. JUN knockdown was performed using JUN-targeting siRNA, with a nontargeting control. Knockdown efficiency was validated by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and Western blotting (Supplementary Material Fig S1. A-C). Because siJUN-2 knocked down JUN protein more effectively, it was selected for subsequent experiments.

Experimental Validation In Vivo

Animals and Ethics

6–8 weeks old male ApoE⁻/⁻ mice (purchased from the Guangdong experimental animal center in China) were housed under specific pathogen‑free conditions with a 12‑h light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. The animal procedures were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval No. KY2025-732-02).

CAVD Model Induction

The CAVD model was established by 5/6 nephrectomy combined with a high‑phosphate diet [18]. The surgery was performed in two stages: first, the right kidney was removed (the adrenal gland was preserved); after a 7‑day interval, two‑thirds of the left kidney was removed. Immediately after the second surgery, mice were fed a high‑phosphate diet for 4 weeks to induce aortic valve calcification.

Experimental Groups and Treatment

Mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6 per group): Sham + Vehicle: Sham operation + normal diet + vehicle; CAVD + Vehicle: 5/6 nephrectomy + high‑phosphate diet + vehicle; CAVD + SP600125: 5/6 nephrectomy + high‑phosphate diet + SP600125 (20 mg/kg/day, intraperitoneal injection). SP600125 (WGC308090, Servicebio, China) is a widely used selective JNK inhibitor, and was formulated in a vehicle containing 5% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween 80 and 50% saline (v/v) [19]. Treatment began on day 0 (the start of the high‑phosphate diet) and was administered daily for 4 weeks.

Echocardiography and Sample Collection

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed at the end of the 4‑week feeding period using a high‑frequency ultrasound system equipped with a 30‑MHz probe. Mice were anesthetized, and placed in a supine position on a heated platform (37°C). Aortic valve peak velocity was measured by pulsed‑wave Doppler in the parasternal long‑axis view with the Doppler beam aligned parallel to the aortic outflow. The peak velocity (m/s) was recorded as an indicator of aortic valve stenosis. For each mouse, at least three consecutive cardiac cycles were averaged. Left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) was calculated from M‑mode tracings of the left ventricle in the parasternal short‑axis view at the level of the papillary muscles, or by tracing end‑diastolic and end‑systolic volumes using the biplane Simpson’ s method. Measurements were performed by an operator blinded to the experimental groups. After echocardiography, mice were euthanized under deep anesthesia. Blood was collected via cardiac puncture, and serum was separated by centrifugation and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent biochemical analyses. Under a dissecting microscope, the aortic valve and ascending aorta were carefully dissected. The tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for histopathological staining.

Calcification Analysis

To assess calcification, VICs were stained with Alizarin Red S (Cyagen Biosciences) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, after 21 days of osteogenic differentiation, VICs were washed twice with PBS and fixed for 30 min in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by staining with Alizarin Red S for 10 min and washing to remove excess dye. Images were captured using an optical microscope (Leica), and ImageJ software was used to evaluate calcium deposits. The Alizarin Red-positive area was measured with ImageJ, averaging three sections per sample, and the experimenter was blinded to the experimental conditions during quantification. To quantify calcium deposition, stained cells were treated with 10% hexadecylpyridinium (Sigma-Aldrich) at room temperature, and the supernatant was analyzed spectrophotometrically at 562 nm.

Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) Activity

ALP staining was performed using the BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Color Development Kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, VICs were washed twice with PBS and fixed for 30 min in 4% paraformaldehyde. The cells were then incubated with ALP color solution at room temperature in the dark for 60 min. After the substrate solution was washed off, images were captured using a light microscope (Leica), and ALP-positive cells appeared dark blue-violet. Positive ALP areas were measured using ImageJ. The average of three sections per sample was calculated, and the experimenter was blinded to the experimental conditions during ImageJ quantification. ALP activity was quantified using an ALP detection kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, VICs were lysed with lysis buffer, and the supernatants were incubated with chromogenic substrate at 37 °C for 30 min. ALP activity was measured by absorbance at 405 nm.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Tissue samples were embedded in paraffin and sectioned longitudinally at 4 μm. Tissue sections were dewaxed and rehydrated, and antigens were retrieved with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) in a pressure cooker. Sections were incubated in 3% H2O2 for 30 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. After two washes in PBS, the tissue sections were blocked in 5% BSA for 30 min and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the tissue sections were incubated with fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. After two PBS washes, DAPI staining was performed at room temperature for 15 min in the dark. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope, and semiquantitative data were presented as integral optical density.

Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of IL-17 A in the supernatants of cultured VICs were measured using a human IL-17 A ELISA kit (GEH0016, Servicebio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantitative PCR

For qPCR, total RNA was extracted and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA, followed by quantitative amplification using SYBR Green chemistry. RNA was isolated with the Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme). The isolated RNA was then reverse-transcribed using HiScript IV RT SuperMix (Vazyme). PCR analysis was subsequently performed with Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) and manufactured primers (AG) (Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Material Table. S1). Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with a housekeeping gene as the internal control.

Western Blotting

Following lysis in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors, protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay. After heat denaturation, total protein lysates were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. To reduce nonspecific binding, membranes were blocked according to the manufacturer’s protocol and then incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The following primary antibodies were used: RUNX2, ALPL, c-JUN, and GAPDH. After washing, the membranes were incubated with the appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were developed using the Western blotting detection kit (Advansta) and analyzed using ImageJ. For quantification, the protein of interest was normalized to the corresponding loading control. Fold changes were calculated by dividing each ratio by the ratio of the control line.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0) and SPSS software (version 26.0). For transcriptomic analyses, multiple testing correction was conducted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. For experimental data, comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-sided Student’s t-test or ANOVA or Wilcoxon rank-sum test as appropriate, and two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise stated.

Results

Osteogenic Induction Induces Distinct Transcriptional Signatures in VICs

To characterize transcriptomic remodeling during osteogenic differentiation, we analyzed GSE252266 (NC1-3 and OST1-3). PCA showed clear separation between OST and NC samples, indicating robust transcriptional reprogramming after osteogenic stimulation (Fig. 1A). DESeq2 analysis (|log2FC| > 1, adjusted P < 0.05) identified 3,958 upregulated and 3,818 downregulated genes in OST versus NC (Fig. 1B). The heatmap of the top 30 DEGs showed consistent clustering by condition (Fig. 1C). Among the most prominent changes, STC1, PDK4, SAXO1, and SAA2 were strongly induced, whereas immune-associated genes such as PTPRC and PEAK3 were markedly reduced (Fig. 1B). To assess IL-17-linked inflammatory programs during osteogenic differentiation, DEGs were intersected with REACTOME_INTERLEUKIN_17_SIGNALING, yielding 26 overlapping genes (Fig. 1D). These overlapping genes were predominantly upregulated in OST (Fig. 1E) and displayed a clear OST-high pattern on the heatmap (Fig. 1F), supporting engagement of an IL-17-associated inflammatory signature during AVIC osteogenic differentiation.

GO enrichment highlighted stress-activated protein kinase/MAPK cascades, I-kappaB kinase/NF-kappaB signaling, cytokine-mediated signaling, and responses to bacterial molecules and lipopolysaccharide (Fig. 2C-F). Enriched molecular functions included protein serine/threonine kinase activity and transcription factor binding, whereas cellular components featured transcription regulator complexes and related regulatory assemblies (Fig. 2C-F). KEGG analysis indicated enrichment in MAPK signaling, TNF signaling, Toll-like receptor signaling, IL-17 signaling, and osteoclast differentiation (Fig. 2G-H). Together, these results indicate that osteogenic differentiation in AVICs is accompanied by activation of stress/MAPK and cytokine-centered inflammatory pathways consistent with an IL-17-linked axis.

Multilayer Network Analyses Identify JUN as a Central Regulatory Node

MCODE analysis identified a core module within the IL-17-overlap genes (Fig. 3A). To infer upstream regulation, miRNA-mRNA (Fig. 3B) and TF-gene (Fig. 3C) networks were constructed. Across these multilayer networks, JUN consistently appeared as a central hub, along with AP-1/MAPK-related components (e.g., FOS, ATF2, MAPKs), suggesting an AP-1-related regulators may contribute to the IL-17-associated inflammatory response during AVIC osteogenic differentiation.

Selected IL-17-related Transcriptomic Features Were Externally Supported in an Independent Human TAV Cohort

To externally assess whether the IL-17-related transcriptomic features identified in the discovery AVIC osteogenic model were also detectable in human valve tissue, we analyzed GSE148219 after restricting the cohort to adult control and calcified tricuspid aortic valve samples. Differential expression analysis identified 1,085 DEGs between the two groups, and heatmap clustering showed clear transcriptomic separation between control and calcified TAV tissues (Supplementary Material Fig. S3A-B). Intersection of the GSE148219 DEGs with the Reactome IL-17 signaling gene set yielded four overlapping genes, namely RPS6KA1, JUN, FOS, and DUSP6 (Supplementary Material Fig. S3C). Although the overlap was limited, these data provide external support that selected IL-17/MAPK/AP-1-related transcriptomic components identified in the discovery model are also altered in human calcified TAV tissue.

JUN is Upregulated During VIC Osteogenic Differentiation

Based on the analyses above, we validated the top 5 core module genes of the PPI network by using OM treatment to induce osteogenic differentiation of VICs. We found that the expression of JUN, MAPK1, MAPK14, NFKB1, and TRAF6 was significantly elevated from day 3, reached its peak mainly on day 7, and then delined to levels similar to baseline by day 14 (Fig. 4A). We confirmed that culture in OM for 3 or 7 days increased RUNX2 mRNA and protein expression, indicating initiation of the calcification process (Fig. 4B-C). Because JUN was most prominently elevated in the present model and served as a central regulatory node, we next examined its protein expression and found that it was increased on days 3, 7, and 14 (Fig. 4D).

JUN Promotes Calcification During VIC Osteogenic Differentiation

In the absence of OM, the expression of JUN and the osteogenic markers ALP and RUNX2 was low. After 7 days of culture in OM, the protein levels of JUN, ALP, and RUNX2 increased significantly, and this effect was inhibited by siJUN knockdown (Fig. 5A). Consistently, ALP staining confirmed increased ALP activity in OM on day 7, and Alizarin Red confirmed mineralized nodule formation in OM on day 21; addition of siJUN reduced these effects (Fig. 5B-C). Together, these findings support that JUN knockdown reduced osteogenic marker expression and markedly suppressed mineralized nodule formation, indicating that JUN is required for full activation of VIC osteogenic differentiation.

IL-17 Promotes VIC Calcification in a JUN-dependent Manner

Our analyses showed the importance of IL-17 signaling in VIC osteogenic differentiation. We then validated the function of exogenous human IL-17 A in VIC osteogenic differentiation. IL-17 A levels were low when VICs were cultured in noncalcifying medium, and IL-17 A expression increased significantly after 3 days and more pronounced after 7 days in OM (Fig. 6A). We next found that adding IL-17 A to OM increased the expression of IL-17 signaling downstream pro-inflammatory response genes in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 6B).

We then investigated the long-term effects. After 7 days of culture in OM with various concentrations of IL-17 A, JUN and IL-17RA protein levels were significantly increased in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 7B). We further investigated whether IL-17 A induces calcification through JUN. Our results showed that adding exogenous IL-17 A to OM increased RUNX2 and JUN protein levels on day 7, and siJUN knockdown partially reduced this effect, whereas siJUN knockdown did not affect the expression of IL-17RA (Fig. 7C). Together, exogenous IL-17 A induces osteogenic marker upregulation and calcification during VIC osteogenic differentiation, at least in part through a JUN-dependent mechanism. Moreover, our data showed that under OM condition (Fig. 7C and E), siJUN reduced RUNX2 expression and calcium deposition by 42.1% and 29.8%, respectively. In the OM + IL-17 A condition (Fig. 7C and E), siJUN reduced these parameters by 50.1% and 43.1%, respectively. These data provide additional evidence that siJUN not only attenuated the general osteogenic effect but also suppressed the IL-17 A‑specific pro‑calcific effect.

Inhibited JNK/JUN Signaling Attenuates CAVD

We verified the expression and function of JUN in vivo. Detailed parameters of the mice are provided in the (Supplementary Material Table. S2). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that JUN was predominantly expressed in the nuclei of VICs, and the JUN‑positive area was significantly larger in valves from patients with CAVD than in those from patients without CAVD (Fig. 8A). Subsequently, using a chronic kidney disease‑induced mouse model of CAVD, we found that CAVD significantly increased peak velocity, leaflet thickness, and aortic valve calcification, whereas treatment with SP600125 (a widely used selective JNK inhibitor) attenuated these effects (Fig. 8B–D). These findings indicate that inhibition of JNK/JUN signaling reduces calcification in CAVD mice.

Discussion

This study examined whether an IL-17-linked pro-inflammatory transcriptional program is engaged during VIC osteogenic differentiation and sought to identify an intracellular regulatory node suitable for experimental evaluation [20, 21]. Using the human AVIC osteogenic induction dataset GSE252266, we confirmed a marked transcriptomic separation between osteogenic and control conditions and identified extensive differential expression under stringent criteria. Within this broader response, restriction to the Reactome IL-17 signaling gene set identified a discrete overlap signature that was predominantly upregulated during osteogenic induction [22]. Enrichment and network analyses of this IL-17-overlap set consistently highlighted stress and inflammatory signaling modules, including MAPK-related processes and NF-kB-associated pathways, and prioritized JUN as a central network node [23, 24]. Selected transcriptomic findings were further externally supported in an independent human calcified TAV cohort (GSE148219), in which four genes overlapping the Reactome IL-17 signaling set were also identified, including RPS6KA1, JUN, FOS, and DUSP6. In functional VIC assays, JUN increased during osteogenic differentiation, and JUN knockdown was associated with reduced osteogenic marker expression and mineralization [25]. Mechanistically, IL-17 A rapidly increased the p-c-JUN/JUN ratio within 30 min in a time- and concentration-dependent manner, without immediate changes in total JUN or IL-17RA. Prolonged IL-17 A treatment (7 days) upregulated both JUN and IL-17RA protein levels in a concentration-dependent fashion. Functionally, IL-17 stimulation enhanced calcification-related readouts, and this effect was attenuated by JUN silencing [25]. These temporal and dose-response data support that IL-17 A directly activates the JUN pathway via rapid phosphorylation, followed by longer-term upregulation of JUN and its receptor, consistent with a sustained autoregulatory loop. Our ELISA data (Fig. 6A) further showed that VICs secrete IL-17 A under osteogenic conditions, raising the possibility of an autocrine IL-17 signaling loop that could reinforce JUN activation and contribute to the osteogenic phenotype. Together, these findings support the interpretation that IL-17-associated inflammatory signaling accompanies VIC osteogenic remodeling and that JUN may help couple inflammatory cues to osteogenic phenotypes under the conditions tested [26]. However, the available evidence supports a contributory role for this axis rather than establishing it as the sole or dominant driver of VIC calcification.

These findings are consistent with the broader view that inflammation is intertwined with CAVD progression and that VIC osteogenic differentiation is a central cellular event in valve mineralization [27, 28]. Previous studies have implicated cytokine signaling, including IL-17-related pathways, in fibro-calcific remodeling and inflammatory amplification in cardiovascular settings. However, much of that evidence derives from tissue-level observations, in which immune-cell infiltration and multicellular interactions complicate inference about VIC-intrinsic mechanisms [29].

Unlike earlier studies that primarily evaluated IL-17 A abundance or systemic blockade at the tissue or organismal level, this study focuses on cell-intrinsic transcriptional reprogramming in VICs [30]. A practical strength of this design is that it reduces tissue-level confounding and allows the IL-17-associated signature to be interpreted as a transcriptional program activated during osteogenesis. The pathway-guided network strategy further clarifies how IL-17-associated inflammatory signaling may intersect with the VIC osteogenic program. However, this design also limits direct extrapolation to in vivo CAVD, where IL-17 sources, immune-stromal crosstalk, and mechanical forces are likely to influence pathway engagement. Accordingly, our data do not establish IL-17 as a dominant upstream driver in vivo; rather, they indicate that VICs can engage IL-17-associated programs during osteogenic differentiation and respond to IL-17 in ways that modulate calcification-related phenotypes.

Prioritization of JUN is biologically plausible given the established role of AP-1 family transcription factors in integrating stress and inflammatory signals and in regulating cell-state transitions [31, 32]. The enrichment patterns observed here, including MAPK- and NF-kB-linked processes, are consistent with signaling contexts in which JUN/AP-1 activity is commonly engaged [31, 32]. However, the current data primarily support the functional relevance of JUN at the level of marker expression and mineralization; they do not yet identify direct JUN target genes in VICs or exclude parallel pathways that may independently promote osteogenesis [23, 33]. VIC osteogenic differentiation is known to involve multiple convergent modules, including osteogenic regulators such as RUNX2 and ALPL, as well as signaling pathways related to mechanical stress, metabolism, and extracellular matrix remodeling [33, 34]. Within this broader framework, the IL-17-JUN relationship supported by our results is best interpreted as one component of a larger regulatory network rather than as a single explanatory mechanism. We also obtained initial in vivo evidence using a CKD-induced mouse CAVD model. Systemic administration of SP600125, a selective JNK inhibitor, significantly reduced aortic valve peak velocity, leaflet thickness, and calcification (Fig. 8B–D). Given that JNK is the major upstream kinase that phosphorylates and activates JUN, these findings support the functional relevance of the JNK/JUN axis in CAVD progression in vivo.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the discovery transcriptomic inference is based on a single dataset with a small sample size and one time point, although selected findings were externally assessed in an independent human TAV tissue cohort, broader validation across additional datasets, time points, and model systems is still needed. Second, the IL-17-centered strategy improves interpretability but may bias discovery toward a predefined pathway and overlook other relevant inflammatory or noninflammatory drivers. Third, regarding the IL-17–JUN axis, our time-course experiments showed that IL-17 A rapidly increased p-c-JUN levels within 30 min (without immediate changes in total JUN or IL-17RA), while prolonged treatment (7 days) elevated both JUN and IL-17RA in a concentration-dependent manner. Knockdown of JUN significantly attenuated calcification but did not affect IL-17RA expression. Although these data suggest that JUN acts downstream of IL-17 signaling, we did not perform a rescue experiment by re-expressing a siRNA-resistant JUN construct in JUN-knockdown cells. Consequently, we cannot completely rule out off-target effects of the siRNA, nor can we definitively conclude that IL-17 promotes calcification exclusively through JUN. Other pathways downstream of IL-17 may also contribute. Fourth, our in vivo evidence relies on pharmacological inhibition of JNK rather than direct JUN knockdown. While JNK is the major kinase activating JUN, this does not replace a genetic loss-of-function study for Jun itself. Future studies using conditional JUN knockout or local delivery of AAV9-shJUN, combined with rescue experiments, are required to firmly establish the IL-17–JUN axis in CAVD progression.

Despite these limitations, this work provides a VIC-focused framework for examining how a defined inflammatory pathway may intersect with osteogenic remodeling. The data together with external transcriptomic support from an independent human TAV cohort, support a testable model in which IL-17-associated signaling accompanies VIC osteogenic differentiation and may promote calcific phenotypes partly through JUN. Future studies should establish the temporal and mechanistic ordering of this relationship, identify direct JUN targets, and determine whether pharmacologic or genetic interruption of IL-17 signaling or JUN/AP-1 activity modifies calcification outcomes in more complex models. Overall, our findings provide an interpretable, pathway-guided link between IL-17-associated inflammatory activation and VIC osteogenic phenotypes while underscoring the need for broader validation and deeper mechanistic resolution.

Conclusions

Using a human VIC osteogenic induction transcriptome dataset, external transcriptomic support from an independent human TAV cohort, complementary in vitro validation, this study supports the view that an IL-17-associated pro-inflammatory transcriptional program is engaged during VIC osteogenic differentiation. Pathway-guided network analyses identified JUN as a candidate regulatory node within this IL-17-linked signature, with selected IL-17/MAPK/AP-1-related components also represented in an independent human calcified TAV cohort. Functional experiments further showed that JUN increased during VIC osteogenic differentiation and that JUN knockdown was associated with reduced osteogenic marker expression and mineralization, whereas IL-17 stimulation enhanced pro-calcific readouts that were attenuated by JUN silencing. Collectively, these findings support a working model in which IL-17-related inflammatory signaling may interface with the VIC osteogenic program partly through JUN (Fig. 9). Further validation in time-resolved and in vivo or ex vivo models will be needed to define the upstream sources of IL-17, confirm direct JUN targets, and assess the translational relevance of the IL-17-JUN axis in CAVD progression.
