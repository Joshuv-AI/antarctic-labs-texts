Development and Evaluation of Multiple-Unit Tablets for the Controlled Release of Lornoxicam
Authors: Yang Liu, Qiong-zhi Shi, Yanchen Wang, Ze-chun Long, Man Han, Yuan Zeng, Xiang-yang Xie, Hui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveLornoxicam (LOX) is a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug used for pain management but requires frequent dosing due to its short half-life. This study aimed to develop a biphasic-release multiple-unit tablet (MUT) to achieve rapid and sustained LOX release with dose flexibility.MethodsLOX-loaded extended-release (ER) pellets were prepared by fluidized-bed coating of microcrystalline cellulose cores and optimized using a Box–Behnken design. The pellets were coated with Eudragit® RL/RS and combined with an immediate-release (IR) component to form MUTs by conventional tableting. Pellets and tablets were evaluated for physicochemical properties and in vitro drug release.ResultsOptimized ER pellets showed high drug-loading efficiency (92.2 ± 3%) and good mechanical properties. Drug release from ER pellets was best described by the Korsmeyer–Peppas model, indicating Fickian diffusion. The MUTs exhibited an initial LOX release of approximately 38%, followed by sustained release for 24 h, with > 90% cumulative release. Split tablets demonstrated release profiles comparable to intact tablets (f2 > 50).ConclusionThe developed biphasic-release MUTs provide rapid onset, sustained LOX delivery, and reliable dose flexibility, representing a promising oral formulation for LOX therapy.

Key Points

Biphasic-release lornoxicam multiple-unit tablets were successfully developed using extended-release pellets and conventional tableting.The formulation achieved rapid initial release followed by sustained delivery over 24 h.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease primarily affecting the joints, causing joint inflammation, pain, and swelling. It can also lead to joint deformity and disability [1]. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are one of the vital components of RA treatment, serving as adjunctive therapy to anti-inflammatory therapies [2]. NSAIDs are often prescribed when the disease activity of RA is high or during flare-ups to provide rapid relief. However, long-term use of NSAIDs may be associated with side effects such as gastrointestinal ulcers, bleeding, and kidney problems. Therefore, it is recommended to use these medications at the lowest effective dose for the shortest possible treatment duration [3].

Lornoxicam (LOX), an oxicam class NSAID, is recognized for its potent anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties, which are a result of its strong inhibition of prostaglandin biosynthesis. It is commonly prescribed for the symptomatic management of pain and inflammation associated with conditions such as RA, osteoarthritis, low back pain, as well as for oncological and post-operation pain relief [4]. LOX is classified as a class II drug within the biopharmaceutical classification system (BCS) with poor aqueous solubility and a relatively short biological half-life (3–4 h) [5]. It is commercially available in both conventional immediate-release tablets (4 and 8 mg) and parenteral formulations (8 mg for intravenous and intramuscular administration) [6]. Therefore, to maintain therapeutic plasma concentrations and ensure consistent pain relief, LOX requires repeated daily dosing. However, the current dosage forms may limit its clinical utility due to the frequent dosing and fluctuations in blood drug concentrations.

To reduce the frequency of oral dosing, various extended-release (ER) or sustained-release dosage forms of LOX have been developed. These included sustained-release matrix tablets [7], sustained-release double-layer tablets [8], compression-coated tablets [9, 10], gastro-retentive floating matrix tablets [11], and sustained-release fast-disintegrating compressed tablets [12]. Nonetheless, a substantial limitation of these formulations is their inability to be broken for dose fractionation to achieve the lower and safe effective doses, or they require a relatively complex preparation method.

The advent of multiple-unit dosage forms (MUDFs) may offer a potential solution to the challenges faced by LOX oral ER formulations. MUDFs are characterized by their composition of multiple small discrete particulates, such as pellets, microparticles, or nanoparticles, which are combined in a single dosage unit, typically in the form of a tablet or capsule [13]. These dosage forms provide several advantages, such as the flexibility to adjust the dose and a high degree of dispersion within the gastrointestinal tract (GI), which helps to mitigate local irritation resulting from elevated drug concentrations [14]. MUDFs can be designed to release drugs at two different rates by integrating immediate-release (IR) components for rapid plasma concentration profiles with ER units that prolong the drug release phase. This biphasic release profile is particularly advantageous for therapeutic interventions necessitating prompt achievement of maximum efficacy, coupled with sustained release to diminish the frequency of dosing and improve patient compliance. NSAIDs, including LOX, are considered suitable candidates for this type of release strategy [12, 15]. Tablets are preferred over capsules as oral solid dosage forms due to their good patient compliance, lower production costs, and higher production efficiency [16]. Pellets, which offer a larger surface area for drug release and absorption, are less affected by gastric emptying. This property may help reduce the risks of systemic toxicity and local irritation [17]. Consequently, MUTs that integrate IR components with ER pellets could provide an innovative and effective strategy for delivering LOX.

This study aimed to develop biphasic-release LOX MUTs by compressing ER pellets together with an IR component using standard tablet-compression equipment (Fig. 1). This formulation strategy allows tablet segmentation and facilitates flexible dose adjustment. The LOX-loaded MUTs were designed to provide both rapid and sustained drug release in the gastrointestinal tract, enabling prompt onset and prolonged maintenance of the therapeutic effect.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the preparation process of the biphasic-release lornoxicam multiple-unit tablet (MUT). In this study, LOX-loaded microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) pellets were prepared using a fluidized-bed coating process, with Eudragit® RL and RS employed as film-forming materials. The resulting ER pellets were characterized in terms of bulk density, friability, morphology, and in vitro release behavior. Subsequently, the ER pellets were compressed with an IR component (LOX 6 mg) to produce MUTs. Finally, the physical properties and in vitro release characteristics of the formulated MUTs were evaluated.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The following materials were utilized in this study: lornoxicam (LOX, Huadong Medicine Bohua Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China); compressible microcrystalline cellulose (MCC; Ceolus PH-200, Asahi Chemicals, Japan); microcrystalline cellulose pellets (CP-203, Asahi Chemicals, Japan); Kollicoat RS-30D aqueous dispersion (30% w/v, containing 27% polyvinyl acetate, 2.7% povidone, and 0.3% sodium lauryl sulfate; BASF, Ludwigshafen, Germany); PVP K30 (Chongqing Stec Ruiden Material Technology Co., Ltd., China); Eudragit RS-30D (Evonik Operations GmbH, Germany); Eudragit RL-30D (Evonik Operations GmbH, Germany); triethyl citrate (TEC; Morimura Bros. Inc, Japan); lactose monohydrate (Armor Proteines S.A.S., France); talcum powder (Talc, Aladdin, China); magnesium stearate (MgSt, Aladdin, China); cross-linked carboxymethyl cellulose sodium (CCMC-Na, Aladdin, China); dimethyl silicone oil (Dow Corning); acetonitrile (HPLC grade, Supelco®); triethylamine (Sigma-Aldrich) and phosphoric acid (Sigma-Aldrich). Other chemicals and solvents used were of analytical grade and were used as received.

Drug-Excipient Compatibility

In this study, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) were utilized to investigate the potential interactions between LOX and the excipients used in MUT formulations. Spectra were obtained for LOX and physical mixtures composed of the drug and various excipients (1:1, W/W).

Physical mixtures of drug and excipient powders (passed through an 80 mesh sieve) were prepared by mixing in a mortar and pestle for 3 minutes and then filled into glass dishes. Spectra analysis was conducted both before and after a 15-day storage period under controlled conditions of 75 ± 5% relative humidity and 40 ± 2 °C [18]. During this period, regular visual inspections were performed to monitor for any signs of clumping, liquefaction, color changes, unusual odors, or gas emissions.

FTIR spectra were acquired using a Bruker TENSOR II FTIR spectrometer (Billerica, MA, USA) with potassium bromide as the background. The samples were scanned from 4000 to 400 cm−1 at a scan speed of 1 cm/s. Spectra were recorded for the drug, excipients, and their physical mixtures.

DSC thermograms of LOX and its physical mixtures were recorded using a HITACHI DSC200 differential scanning calorimeter (Tokyo, Japan) in an inert nitrogen environment (nitrogen pressure: 2 bar, flow rate: 50 mL min−1). The samples were heated at a rate of 10 °C/min within the temperature range of 20–250 °C. Approximately 4 mg of sample was carefully loaded into an aluminum pan, and an empty pan served as the control.

The change in the drug crystalline state was assessed using an EMPYREAN X-ray powder diffractometer (XRPD, PANalytical Co., Lelyweg, Netherlands). Monochromatic Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) was passed to the powder at room temperature. The current and voltage were set to 30 mA and 40 kV, respectively. Then, the diffraction pattern over 2θ range of 10°–50° was scanned at a scanning rate of 1 s/step.

Preparation of Drug-Loaded Pellets

Blank MCC pellets with a particle size of 200–300 μm were coated with drug layers using a water-ethanol solution (50:50, v/v) containing 1.25% (w/w) LOX, supplemented with 2% (w/w) PVP K30 as the adhesive agent. The coating process was performed in a FLZB-1.5 fluidized bed coater (Chanse Technology [Jiangsu] Inc., China). A total of 900 g of the coating suspension was gradually sprayed onto 100 g of blank MCC pellets using a bottom-spray configuration with a 20-mm gap size and a nozzle diameter of 0.8 mm. The critical process parameters for coating were as follows: inlet airflow rate of 0.3 m3/min, inlet air temperature of 65–85 °C, product temperature of 42 °C, fluidizing air flow rate of 130 m3/h, spraying pressure of 0.8–1.2 bar, and liquid pump speed of 5–15 rpm/min. After coating, the drug-loaded pellets were dried in the fluidized bed at 70 °C for 10 min.

Characterization of Extended-Release Pellets

To evaluate the flowability of the ER pellets, a comprehensive analysis was performed on several critical parameters, including bulk density, tapped density, compressibility (Carr's index), Hausner ratio, and angle of repose. These parameters were measured by a BT-1001 Intelligent Powder Characteristic Tester (Dandong Baite Instrument Co., Ltd., China), in accordance with the guidelines set forth in the Chinese Pharmacopeia (2025 Edition). Surface morphology was examined using a HITACHI SU8600 scanning electron microscope (SEM; Tokyo, Japan). Prior to analysis, the pellets were embedded in epoxy resin on a sample holder and coated with gold. SEM micrographs were captured at magnifications ranging from 50× to 300×, with an acceleration voltage set at 3.0 kV under extended vacuum pressure conditions. The mean diameters of the coated pellets were measured using an optical microscope equipped with a stage micrometer [19].

Friability was assessed using a FT2000-AF friabilator (Tianda Tianfa Technology Co., Ltd., China). Initially, approximately 10 g of pellets were weighed to establish the initial mass. The pellets were then placed in a cylindrical container that rotated at 25 revolutions per minute (rpm) for 4 min. After the test, the pellets were retrieved from the container, free of any dust, and reweighed to obtain the final mass [17]. An in vitro drug release study was conducted on coated drug-loaded pellets using a USP dissolution apparatus (Type II, paddle type, 50 rpm). The dissolution medium consisted of 400 mL of 0.1 N HCl (37 ± 0.5 °C) for the first 2 h, after which 200 mL of 0.2-M tri-sodium orthophosphate solution was added (37 ± 0.5 °C) [9]. Accurately weighed pellets, each containing 16 mg of LOX, were placed in the baskets of the apparatus. At predetermined time intervals (0.25, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 h), 5-mL aliquots of the dissolution medium were withdrawn, filtered through a 0.45-μm membrane filter, and analyzed for drug concentration using a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) method. After each sample collection, 5 mL of fresh dissolution medium was added to replace the volume removed.

The drug content of the ER pellets was determined using an HPLC method. A Shimadzu LC-20AT HPLC system (Shimadzu Corporation, Japan) equipped with a Welch XB C18 column (150 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) was employed for this analysis. The mobile phase consisted of a 0.275% phosphoric acid solution (pH 3.6, adjusted with triethylamine) mixed with acetonitrile in a volumetric ratio of 57:43 (V/V). The flow rate was set to 1.0 mL/min, and the detection wavelength was 383 nm, with the column maintained at a temperature of 40 °C. A sample volume of 20 μL was injected for analysis, and the retention time for LOX was observed to be 5 min. Calibration curves were established to quantify LOX concentrations ranging from 1 to 40 μg/mL. The regression coefficient (R2) of the calibration curve was 0.9999, and the linear regression equation was determined to be y = 57.939x − 7.564. Validation of the HPLC method for specificity, accuracy, and both intra-day and inter-day precision was conducted, demonstrating compliance with the requirements outlined in the Chinese Pharmacopeia (2025 Edition). All evaluations were conducted in triplicate, and the standard deviation was calculated.

Preparation of Controlled-Release Multiple-Unit Tablets

To produce MUTs, IR granules were prepared using a formulation comprising 100 g of MCC, 100 g of lactose monohydrate, and 10 g of CCMC-Na, along with distilled water via the wet granulation technique. The designated amounts of MCC, lactose, and CCMC-Na were initially passed through a sieve with a 0.80-mm mesh and mixed for 15 minutes using a Turbula T2F blender (Willy A. Bachofen AG, Switzerland). Distilled water, utilized as the granulating agent, was then added to the mixture, and the wet mass was mixed again before being passed through a sieve with the same 0.80-mm mesh size. The granules were subsequently dried in an oven (Jinghong Instrument Ltd., China) at 60 °C and manually sifted through a set of sieves with aperture sizes of 0.16 mm, 0.20 mm, and 0.30 mm (Shangyu Sunlong Experimental Instrument Ltd., China). Only the granules sized between 0.20 mm and 0.30 mm were selected for tablet compression (This size similarity was to ensure good flowability, uniform mixing with the ER pellets (200–300 μm), and minimal segregation during tablet compression). Prior to compression, the ER pellets and IR granules were uniformly blended with magnesium stearate in a turbula mixer for 10 minutes to ensure homogeneous distribution. Tablet compression was performed using a single-punch tableting machine (Hengyang Chuangkai Pharmaceutical Equipment Co., China), equipped with round, flat-faced punches and dies with a diameter of 12 mm. A total of 100 tablets were produced and subsequently evaluated for uniformity of drug content, in vitro drug release, friability, hardness, and disintegration time.

Tablet Assay and Physical Evaluation

The drug content of MUT across all batches was assessed. To achieve this, 20 tablets from each batch were accurately weighed and reduced to fine powders using a ceramic mortar and pestle. The resulting powders, equivalent to 16 mg of LOX, were then transferred into a 100-mL conical flask containing 900 mL of the HPLC mobile phase and sonicated for 10 minutes. Following sonication, the dispersion was filtered through a 0.45-μm membrane, and the filtrates were analyzed using the previously described HPLC system. Tablet thickness was measured using a SL01-3 vernier caliper (Biaokang Instrument Co., Ltd., China). Five tablets from each batch were randomly selected, and their thicknesses were precisely measured. Tablet hardness was evaluated using a YPD 200C tablet hardness tester (Shanghai Huanghai Pharmaceutical Instrument Co., China). Three tablets were randomly selected from each formulation batch, and one tablet was placed in the tester. The maximum force required to fracture the tablet was recorded in Newtons (N).

To assess the consistency of tablet weight, a sample of 20 tablets was randomly selected from each formulation batch. Each tablet was weighed individually using a BT25S electronic digital balance (Sartorius Lab Instruments GmbH & Co.KG, Germany). The mean mass of the tablets was calculated and compared with their individual masses to determine the extent of weight variation within the batches.

Friability was evaluated using a FT2000-AF friability tester (Tianda Tianfa Technology Co., Ltd., China), following the guidelines established by the Chinese Pharmacopeia (2025 Edition). Approximately 6.5 g of tested tablets were measured as the initial mass and placed in a drum-like container. The drum was rotated for 4 minutes at a speed of 25 rpm. After the test, the tablets were retrieved from the container, dust was removed from their surfaces, and the final mass was recorded. The process followed the same procedure as described above.

In Vitro Drug Release Studies of Multiple-Unit Tablets

The in vitro drug release study of MUTs was conducted using a USP dissolution apparatus (Type II, paddle type, 50 rpm). All other conditions and treatments were consistent with those described in the in vitro release study of ER pellets. The concentration of the drug in the release medium was quantified using HPLC. Additionally, the drug release profiles for the half and quarter tablets were evaluated under the same conditions and procedures described in this study.

Kinetic Modeling of Drug Release

To investigate the drug release kinetics of LOX from MUTs, we employed several mathematical models: zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer–Peppas for data fitting [20]. The selection of the most appropriate model for describing drug release kinetics was based on a comprehensive analysis of statistical criteria, including the smallest residual sum of squares (RSS), the lowest reduced chi-squared value, and the highest coefficient of determination (R2) [21].

Results

Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy

The FTIR spectrum of LOX (Fig. 2) displays several characteristic peaks that provide insights into its molecular structure and functional groups. A prominent peak at 3058 cm−1 is attributable to the stretching vibrations of the amino (–NH) group or the aromatic C-H bonds present in heteroaromatic compounds. Additionally, peaks observed at 1592 cm−1 and 1530 cm−1 likely correspond to the bending vibrations of nitrogen-hydrogen (N–H) groups. The sulfonamide group is characterized by stretching vibrations at 1141 cm−1, 1319 cm−1, and 1378 cm−1, which are associated with the O=S=O functional group. Furthermore, the peak at 829 cm−1 can be linked to the bending vibrations of the aromatic =CH bonds in heteroaromatic structures. Lastly, a peak at 780 cm−1 may correspond to bending vibrations associated with the C–Cl bond [23]. Differential Scanning Calorimetry

Figure 5 presents the DSC thermograms for LOX, the excipients, and their physical mixtures. The DSC thermogram of LOX exhibits a distinct and sharp exothermic peak at 217.4 °C, which is likely associated with the melting and decomposition of the drug [9]. At this temperature, the crystal structure of LOX does not experience melting; rather, molecular cleavage occurs, resulting in an exothermic reaction. A similar phenomenon was observed in MCC and CCMC-Na, although their exothermic peaks were broader and less pronounced.Fig. 5Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) thermogram patterns of lornoxicam (LOX), microcrystalline cellulose (MCC), lactose, cross-linked carboxymethyl cellulose sodium (CCMC-Na), magnesium stearate (MgSt), and their physical mixtures

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) thermogram patterns of lornoxicam (LOX), microcrystalline cellulose (MCC), lactose, cross-linked carboxymethyl cellulose sodium (CCMC-Na), magnesium stearate (MgSt), and their physical mixtures

In contrast, the DSC thermograms for both lactose and magnesium stearate (MgSt) revealed two endothermic peaks. For lactose, the first endothermic event at 149.4 °C is likely related to a dehydration process, while the second endothermic peak at 211.4 °C corresponds to its melting process [26]. Likewise, the endothermic peak at 99.4 °C for MgSt suggests dehydration of residual moisture, and the subsequent peak at 115.8 °C indicates anhydrate melting, followed by an exothermic phenomenon occurring at temperatures above 200 °C [27].

Discussion

Compatibility between LOX and formulation excipients was evaluated using FTIR, DSC, and XRPD to ensure stability in controlled-release tablets. FTIR spectra of LOX showed characteristic peaks for amino (–NH), sulfonamide (O=S=O), aromatic =CH, and C–Cl bonds. In physical mixtures with MCC, lactose, CCMC-Na, and MgSt, these peaks were retained without significant shifts, indicating no chemical interactions. The spectra of the mixtures appeared as superpositions of the individual components, confirming LOX’s chemical stability. DSC thermograms showed LOX’s exothermic peak at 217.4 °C, while excipients displayed their typical thermal transitions. Physical mixtures exhibited superimposed thermal events with no new or shifted peaks, and the slight reduction in LOX peak intensity was attributed to dilution and overlapping excipient transitions rather than interaction. XRPD analysis confirmed that LOX maintained its crystalline structure in the mixtures, with characteristic peaks preserved. Lactose and MgSt were crystalline, whereas MCC and CCMC-Na were amorphous. No peak broadening or disappearance was observed, indicating the absence of solid-state interactions or transformation to an amorphous form. Overall, the combined FTIR, DSC, and XRPD results demonstrate that LOX is chemically and physically compatible with the selected excipients. These findings support the formulation of stable and reproducible controlled-release LOX tablets, ensuring the integrity of the drug during manufacturing and storage.

The drug-loading process for LOX pellets was optimized using a three-factor, three-level design assessing inlet air temperature, spraying pressure, and coating flow rate. Quadratic regression modeling showed excellent fit (R2 = 0.983, p < 0.0001). All factors positively affected drug loading at moderate levels, while negative quadratic terms indicated optimal intermediate conditions. A strong synergistic interaction between temperature and pressure was observed. Optimal conditions (76 °C, 1 bar, 10 rpm) yielded a mean drug-loading efficiency of 92.2 ± 3% and drug content of 10.2 ± 0.6%, consistent with model predictions.

Small molecules such as drugs, solvents, or plasticizers can lower the glass transition temperature, which is a critical factor for pharmaceutical applications. Triethyl citrate serves as the primary plasticizer in Eudragit formulations [31]. In the context of tablet formulations using Eudragit as a coating material, TEC plays an essential role as a plasticizer, enhancing the coating’s properties. Eudragit RL and RS are frequently employed in controlled-release formulations. The incorporation of plasticizers is vital for improving processing characteristics, flexibility, and drug-release profiles of the coating.

Eudragit® RL and RS are copolymers made from ethyl acrylate, methyl methacrylate, and a small proportion of methacrylic acid ester containing quaternary ammonium groups. These polymers are extensively employed in the pharmaceutical industry due to their distinctive physicochemical properties. Eudragit RL and RS are widely used in a range of dosage forms, including tablets, capsules, granules, and implants, making them crucial materials for the development of novel sustained-release and controlled-release formulations. Eudragit RL is characterized by high permeability, whereas Eudragit RS exhibits lower permeability. The combination of these two polymers allows for precise modulation of coating permeability, facilitating the controlled release of drugs in various formulations. By adjusting the ratio of Eudragit RL to RS, different drug release rates can be achieved [32].

The observed extension of lag time correlates positively with increasing coating levels. This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact that the lag time for membrane diffusion is proportional to the thickness of the coating film [33]. The three batches of optimal ER pellets exhibited a drug release onset after a lag time of approximately 0.5 h, indicating a consistent release pattern across the batches. This time lag suggests that the pellets and their coating membranes experienced a swelling and/or hydration process prior to the drug release, which is essential for achieving a prolonged release profile. Furthermore, the pellets attained a cumulative drug release of 85% within 24 h, confirming that this formulation possesses good sustained-release capabilities.

The Korsmeyer–Peppas model is widely applied for drug release from polymeric matrices, correlating the logarithm of the fraction of drug released (Log Mt/M0) with the logarithm of time (Log t). The diffusion exponent (n) derived from this model provides insight into the underlying transport mechanism. An n ≤ 0.45 indicates Fickian diffusion (Class I), 0.45 < n < 0.95 indicates non-Fickian (anomalous) transport, and n ≥ 0.95 suggests erosion-controlled release (Class II or super case II transport) [33]. In this study, n was calculated as 0.424, indicating that the drug-release process of LOX-loaded ER pellets is predominantly governed by Fickian diffusion.

Eudragit RL and RS are water‑insoluble acrylic copolymers with differing quaternary ammonium content, resulting in distinct permeabilities: RL is more permeable, while RS slows diffusion [33, 34]. In RL/RS matrices, drug release is primarily driven by concentration‑gradient–dependent diffusion through the polymer network, consistent with the n value observed. The release behavior aligns with Higuchi-type kinetics reported for similar polymeric systems [33], confirming that polymer relaxation or erosion contributes minimally under the conditions studied.

Although the Korsmeyer–Peppas model effectively describes overall release, it is semi‑empirical and does not resolve microscale transport phenomena such as polymer-free volume, microstructure, or polymer–drug interactions. Further mechanistic investigations—such as in vitro diffusion studies across RL/RS films or computational modeling of drug transport through polymer networks—are warranted to elucidate the physical basis of release and to guide optimization of ER formulations and RL/RS ratios for targeted therapeutic profiles.

The dose selection for the IR (6 mg) and ER (10 mg) components was guided by the conventional dosing regimen of lornoxicam (4–8 mg every 8–12 h) and its pharmacokinetic characteristics, particularly its short elimination half-life of approximately 3–4 h. The 6-mg IR fraction is intended to provide rapid onset of analgesia, whereas the 10-mg ER component is designed to maintain therapeutic plasma concentrations over a 24-h period, thereby reducing dosing frequency and potentially improving patient adherence in chronic conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis. In addition, the split-tablet design allows dose flexibility during titration. It should be noted, however, that this specific dose ratio (6 mg/10 mg) is preliminary and serves primarily as a proof of concept; optimization of the dose ratio for clinical application would require confirmation through in vivo pharmacokinetic studies.

Although incorporating release-related parameters could provide a more comprehensive perspective on formulation development, our experimental strategy was guided by preliminary studies indicating that drug loading efficiency was not a critical determinant of the final coated pellet performance. Specifically, we found that the desired in vitro release profile could be consistently achieved through adjustment of the coating formulation, irrespective of the drug loading level in the core pellets. Accordingly, the primary objective of the Box-Behnken design was to optimize the core pellets for maximum drug loading capacity, thereby establishing an efficient starting point for the subsequent coating process, which independently governs drug release.

In the MUT formulation, MCC served as a filler to facilitate disintegration [35]. CCMC-Na served as the disintegrant, with its action influenced by swelling, erosion, and pore formation [36]. Although CCMC-Na is typically used at 0.5–5.0% (w/w), higher concentrations can lead to the formation of a viscous gel layer that may impede disintegration.

One key advantage of MUTs is their dosage flexibility for clinical applications. Ensuring consistent drug release across split tablets is critical for quality control. The similarity factor (f2) values between release curves of different dosages were all above 50 (f2 = 71–90), indicating that the split MUTs maintained consistent in vitro release behavior. This finding provided a reliable basis for dose adjustment of LOX-loaded MUTs in clinical use.

Despite these successes, several limitations exist. First, the study was confined to in vitro evaluations; the influence of physiological conditions on in vivo release remains unknown. Second, the dosage (6 mg IR + 10 mg ER LOX) and the split-tablet design are preliminary; their therapeutic efficacy and safety require validation through pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic studies, and the establishment of in vitro–in vivo correlation (IVIVC) remains necessary. Third, long-term stability under variable storage conditions was not assessed. Finally, in vivo performance of split tablets was not examined, raising questions about dose uniformity under gastrointestinal transit.

In conclusion, this study successfully developed a stable LOX MUT with a biphasic release profile and demonstrated process optimization strategies to maximize drug-loading efficiency. Future work should focus on in vivo validation, long-term stability, and scale-up to ensure clinical applicability and reproducible therapeutic outcomes.

Conclusions

This study reports the development of a novel MUT formulation of LOX that combines IR and ER components to achieve a dual-release profile from a single dosage form. The MUTs demonstrated rapid initial drug release from the IR fraction, followed by sustained release from the ER pellets, providing both prompt onset of action and prolonged therapeutic effect. Split MUTs exhibited in vitro release profiles comparable to whole tablets, confirming the consistency and reliability of the release mechanism.

The formulation also met key physical and chemical quality criteria, including hardness, friability, disintegration, and drug content, supporting its suitability for pharmaceutical use. Overall, this innovative LOX MUT has the potential to improve clinical management of RA by enhancing drug-release efficiency and patient convenience. Future studies will focus on in vivo evaluation of efficacy and safety to further validate its therapeutic potential.
