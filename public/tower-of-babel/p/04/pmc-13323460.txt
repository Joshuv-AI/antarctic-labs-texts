Optimization of extraction conditions of Micromeria fruticosa and use of optimized extract in beef patties production
Authors: Pınar Anlar Daldal, Kübra Cinar Topcu, Özlem Çakır
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to optimize the conditions for obtaining an ethanol extract from Micromeria fruticosa (M. fruticosa) and to determine the antioxidant activity and phenolic compound content of the resulting extract. Subsequently, the use of the optimized extract in beef patties was evaluated, and the effects of the cooking process on the physicochemical properties of the product were examined. In this context, the highest efficiency was obtained in terms of total phenolic contents (TPC), as well as DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) and ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) values, during the ethanol extraction of the plant. The optimum extraction conditions were determined to be 12 g of sample, 20 min, and 75% ultrasonic power. Under these conditions, the TPC of the extract was found to be 37.128 mg GAE/g dry weight, the DPPH value was 42.865, and the FRAP value was 254.776 mg TE/g dry weight. In the second phase of the study, the optimized extract was used at different concentrations (0%, 0.5%, 1.0%, and 1.5%) to produce beef patties, which were then analyzed for pH, moisture content, TBARS (thiobarbituric acid-reactive substances), and cooking yield. The use of the extract did not cause a statistically significant difference in the moisture content and TBARS values of the samples (p > 0.05). However, it was determined that the pH values of the samples containing 0% and 0.5% extract were lower than those containing 1.0% and 1.5% extract (p < 0.01). In addition, it was observed that the use of the extract had a positive effect on cooking yield, and that cooking yield increased with increasing extract concentration. As a result of the study, it was concluded that M. fruticosa holds promising potential as a natural additive in meat and meat products, offering a functional ingredient that can contribute to the development of value-added, health-oriented food products.Keywords: Micromeria fruticosa, Response surface methodology (RSM), Patties, Antioxidant activity, Cooking yield

Introduction

Patties and similar processed meat products are among the most widely consumed meat products globally, primarily because they have an easy production procedure and affordable prices, making them popular in both fast-food restaurants and home cooking (Bagheri et al., 2021). However, their shelf life remains limited, as the production process can lead to the deterioration of cell membrane structures and promote oxidative reactions, ultimately resulting in nutritional losses (Hajrawati et al., 2021). Furthermore, the high temperatures reached during cooking can further accelerate oxidation, contributing to a decline in the overall quality of the product (Rosario Ramirez et al., 2004).

In recent years, increasing consumer demand for natural and functional foods has led to increased interest in the use of natural additives in the food industry, particularly in meat products. This shift is largely driven by concerns over the potential toxicity and carcinogenic effects associated with synthetic antioxidants, which have limited their widespread acceptance (Wang et al., 2022). As a result, plant-based extracts are being actively explored as promising natural alternatives. Owing to their high levels of phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and other bioactive constituents, these extracts are recognized for their ability to enhance food quality and extend shelf life by effectively inhibiting oxidative reactions (Al-Juhaimi et al., 2020; Ahmed et al., 2022).

M. fruticosa L., an aromatic plant of the Lamiaceae family, is widely cultivated across the Mediterranean region (Abu-Reidah et al., 2019). Traditionally used in herbal teas, soups, and various dishes, it is recognized for its therapeutic effects, including antiseptic, antirheumatic, and central nervous system-stimulating properties (Al-Hamwi et al., 2021). Recent research has examined its effects on both humans and animals, particularly highlighting its anti-inflammatory and gastroprotective activities in mice (Abu-Gharbieh et al., 2013). Moreover, in vitro studies have shown that ethanol extracts of M. fruticosa possess antiproliferative and pro-apoptotic effects against several human cancer cell lines, including breast (MCF-7), colorectal (HCT-116), and glioblastoma (U-87 MG) cells (El-Huneidi et al., 2020).

Although the antioxidant potential of M. fruticosa has previously been investigated in meat systems (Awad et al., 2021; Kılıç Altun et al., 2024), studies that combine Response Surface Methodology (RSM) to optimize ethanol-based ultrasound-assisted extraction with the subsequent incorporation of the optimized extract into cooked beef patties are still limited. Ultrasonic treatment is known to enhance the extraction efficiency of bioactive compounds, particularly polyphenols, by disrupting plant cell walls and improving solvent penetration (Tahir et al., 2024; Rueangsri et al., 2025). Therefore, this study aimed to optimize the ultrasound-assisted ethanol extraction of M. fruticosa to maximize its antioxidant activity and phenolic compound content using RSM. Following optimization, the obtained extract was incorporated into beef patties to evaluate its functional potential in meat products. In addition, the study investigated the effect of cooking on key physicochemical parameters, including pH, moisture content, lipid oxidation, and cooking yield. By assessing the performance of the plant extract as a natural additive, this study aims to contribute to the development of clean-label meat products and to promote the broader use of functional ingredients in the food industry.

Materials and methods

Material

The leaves of M. fruticosa used in the study were obtained from Erzurum, Türkiye, in 2024. The plant material was brought to the Bayburt University Food Engineering Laboratory, then it was dried at room temperature for approximately 3 days and ground into powder using a laboratory blender.

Extraction, experimental design, and optimization

Extraction was carried out by using 90 ml of 80% ethanol (ethanol–water, v/v) with the ultrasonic bath method (DAIHAN, Ultrasonic cleaner set, WUC-D06H, 50 Hz, 510 W) by modifying the method given by Meng et al. (2011). The obtained extracts were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 20 min and stored at -20 °C until analysis.

Experimental conditions were determined using the Box-Behnken response surface methodology. The design was created with Minitab 18.1 software. The extracts were analyzed for TPC, DPPH radical scavenging activity, and FRAP. These parameters were considered to optimize the extraction efficiency.

The levels of the extraction variables were determined based on both preliminary experiments and reference to previous studies employing ultrasound-assisted extraction from herbal materials (Meng et al., 2011; Rueangsri et al., 2025). These ranges (4–12 g sample, 20–50 min sonication time, 25–75% ultrasonic power) were selected to provide a balanced and practical approach for phenolic compound recovery and to ensure compatibility with food-grade processing systems (Table 1). A total of 15 test runs were conducted under these conditions (Table 2). Statistical analysis was performed with significance testing at a p-value less than 0.05. The suitability of the extraction conditions model was evaluated using ANOVA.

Antioxidant activity

Antioxidant activity was evaluated using DPPH and FRAP assays, with minor modifications applied to the methods described in the cited references. For DPPH, 100 µL of extract was mixed with 2000 µL of 0.2 mM DPPH solution, incubated for 30 min, and absorbance was measured at 517 nm. Results were expressed as Trolox equivalent (mg TE/g dry weight) (Kumaran & Karunakaran, 2007). For FRAP, 150 µL of the extract was reacted with FRAP solution for 30 min, and absorbance at 593 nm was recorded, with results expressed as mg TE/g dry weight (Upadhyay et al., 2010).

TPC

5 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent: pure water (1:10 v/v), 4 mL of 7.5% (Na2CO3: pure water w/v) sodium carbonate, and 100 µL of diluted extract were mixed. After 60 min incubation in the dark at room temperature, absorbance values ​​were measured with a spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800) at a wavelength of 765 nm. The results were given as gallic acid equivalent (GAE) (mg GAE/g dry weight) (McDonald et al., 2001).

Production of beef patties

Beef patties were prepared using the optimized M. fruticosa extract at varying concentrations of 0.0% (control), 0.5%, 1.0%, and 1.5%. The extract has been added to the meat samples in liquid form as an optimized extract. Each formulation included 20% fat and 1.5% salt. The mixtures were homogenized and shaped into patties with a diameter of 9.0 ± 0.2 cm, a thickness of 1.0 ± 0.3 cm, and a standardized weight of approximately 50 g. The patties were cooked on a preheated hot plate at 180 °C for a total of 12 min (6 min on each side). This cooking protocol was established based on preliminary trials to ensure proper doneness and to achieve an internal temperature of at least 72 °C, in accordance with food safety standards.

pH and moisture contents

pH and moisture contents of the samples were determined according to Gökalp et al. (2010). The pH value of the samples was measured using a pH meter (Mettler Toledo S220–K) calibrated with appropriate buffer solutions.

TBARS

TBARS values were determined by mixing 2 g of sample with TCA (trichloroacetic acid) solution, then filtering and reacting with TBA (thiobarbituric acid) solution. After boiling and centrifugation, absorbance was measured at 532 nm. The results were expressed as µmol MDA (malondialdehyde)/kg (Kılıç and Richards, 2003).

Cooking yield

Cooking yield was calculated by taking into account the weight of the beef patties before and after cooking.

Statistical analysis

The obtained data were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27). The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation of the replicates. Statistically significant differences between the means were determined by Duncan’s multiple comparison test at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Phenolic substances are synthesized during plant development and are found in almost every organ of the plant (Kulbat, 2016). In addition to their roles in plant growth, reproduction, and defense against pathogenic microorganisms, these compounds also exhibit numerous biochemical and pharmacological activities relevant to human health, including effects on cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative disorders, diabetes, immune system regulation, and the elimination of enzymes and free radicals that trigger cancer formation (Razavi et al., 2020). In this study, the effects of sample amount, time, and ultrasonic power factors on TPC content were evaluated by the RSM method (Fig. 1). According to ANOVA, none of the factors (sample amount, time, and ultrasonic power) had a statistically significant effect on TPC, and most of the two-factor interactions were also found to be insignificant (p > 0.05). The lack-of-fit test indicated that the model was compatible with the experimental data (p > 0.05), and the model’s explanatory power, as indicated by the coefficient of determination (R²), was 75.26%. This value suggests that 75.26% of the variation in the response variable could be explained by the model (Table 3). Since R² values above 70% are generally considered acceptable for modeling physicochemical responses (Granato et al., 2010), and the lack-of-fit was not significant, the TPC model was considered statistically valid for optimization purposes. Furthermore, the TPC values of the extracts in this study ranged between 7.86 and 39.59 mg GAE/g dry weight.

Since antioxidant activity determination methods depend on various factors, such as the substrate, reaction conditions, concentration, and structure of the compound being analyzed, there is no standardized method for assessing the antioxidant activity of a compound (Şen, 2011). Therefore, two different antioxidant activity determination assays (DPPH and FRAP) were performed in the study to evaluate the antioxidant properties of the extract more accurately. The effects of sample amount, extraction time, and ultrasonic power on DPPH capacity were analyzed using RSM (Fig. 2). According to ANOVA, both sample amount and time had a significant effect on DPPH (p < 0.05), while the linear effect of ultrasonic power was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Among the two-factor interactions, the interaction between sample amount and ultrasonic power showed a tendency toward significance, while the other interactions were not significant (p > 0.05). The model showed high explanatory power, with a coefficient of determination (R²) of 88.44% (Table 3). The DPPH values of the samples ranged from 4.83 to 37.98 mg TE/g dry weight.

The change in FRAP value, another method used to determine antioxidant activity, was analysed using RSM depending on sample amount, extraction time, and ultrasonic power (Fig. 3). According to ANOVA, the effects of sample amount, time, ultrasonic power, and their two-factor interactions on FRAP values were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). The coefficient of determination (R²), which indicates the explanatory power of the model, was found to be 71.03%. R² values above 70% are generally considered acceptable for modeling physicochemical parameters in food studies (Granato et al., 2010) (Table 3). Furthermore, the lack-of-fit test was non-significant (p > 0.05), indicating that the FRAP model was statistically compatible with the experimental data. In this study, the FRAP values of the M. fruticosa extract ranged from 25.53 to 219.25 mg TE/g dry weight.

In the present study, the aim was to optimize the process to achieve the maximum levels of FRAP, DPPH, and TPC. In this context, suitable combinations of sample amount, extraction time, and ultrasonic power were determined for all three parameters. As a result of the optimization, the most suitable combination was identified as 12 g of sample, 20 min of extraction, and 75% ultrasonic power. The results predicted by the model were found to be highly accurate, as confirmed by the confidence intervals. Under these optimal conditions, TPC was determined to be 37.13 mg GAE/g dry weight, while FRAP and DPPH values were 254.78 mg TE/g dry weight and 42.87 mg TE/g dry weight, respectively. These findings indicate that the optimization process was completed and that the target parameters could be achieved with high precision.

The effect of M. fruticosa extract, obtained under optimized conditions, on the pH values of beef patties is presented in Table 4. The pH values of the control group and the samples containing 0.5% extract were found to be lower than those of the samples containing 1.0% and 1.5% extract, and the differences were statistically significant (p < 0.05).

The moisture content of meat is a critical parameter in terms of meat texture, appearance, taste and aroma, crispiness, processability, storage, freezing, packaging, microbial quality, and storage period (Cheng and Sun, 2008; Hughes et al., 2014). For this reason, the moisture content of the samples was also examined in the study, and it was determined that the use of M. fruticosa extract obtained under optimized conditions in beef patties did not have a significant effect on moisture content (p > 0.05) (Table 4).

Oxidation leads to a reduction in the nutritional value of the product, degradation of pigments, and a decrease in quality in terms of color, odor, taste, and texture (Hajrawati et al., 2021). Therefore, it is important to delay and/or prevent oxidation by incorporating antioxidant substances into products (Dorman et al., 2003). In this context, the TBARS value in the control group was observed to be 13.18 µmol MDA/kg, whereas it decreased to an average of 10.57 µmol MDA/kg with the use of the extract. However, this reduction was not statistically significant compared to the control group (p > 0.05) (Table 4).

It was determined that the use of optimized M. fruticosa extract in beef patties had a positive effect on cooking yield values, and cooking yield increased with the use of the extract (Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, the phenolic content and antioxidant properties of M. fruticosa were investigated, and it was determined that the plant contains high levels of phenolic compounds and exhibits strong antioxidant activity. Although the TPC values obtained were generally consistent with those reported in the literature, some differences were also observed. For example, Güllüce et al. (2004) reported the TPC value of a methanol extract of M. fruticosa as 55.2 ± 2.00 µg GAE/mg dry weight, while Sadeq et al. (2021) reported the TPC value of an ethanol extract obtained from plant pollen as 56.78 ± 0.49 mg GAE/g. On the other hand, Abu-Gharbieh et al. (2016) reported a TPC value of 3.92 ± 0.06 mg GAE/g for an ethanol extract obtained from M. fruticosa samples collected in Palestine. The differences among these values may be attributed to various factors, such as the specific plant part used (leaf, pollen, whole plant), geographical origin and climatic conditions, the type and polarity of the extraction solvent, sample preparation techniques, and analytical methods (Rodríguez-Carrasco et al., 2018). In this context, it is thought that the relatively low TPC values obtained in our study may be related to the specific extraction conditions employed and the targeted plant material.

The DPPH radical scavenging activity of the plant extract obtained under optimized conditions was evaluated by comparing it with data from the literature. Duysak and Kılıç Baygutalp (2023) examined methanol extracts of M. fruticosa leaves at concentrations of 125, 250, and 500 µg/mL and reported DPPH results as percentage inhibition (%). The highest radical scavenging activity was observed at a concentration of 500 µg/mL. Similarly, Güllüce et al. (2004), Al-Nuri et al. (2022), and Kılıç Altun et al. (2024) also reported that M. fruticosa exhibits high DPPH activity. However, since DPPH data in the literature are commonly reported in different units, such as percentage inhibition (%) or EC₅₀, it is difficult to make a direct comparison with the results expressed in mg TE/g in this study. Nevertheless, based on the overall trend, it can be stated that the plant demonstrates strong antioxidant activity. Furthermore, Duysak and Kılıç Baygutalp (2023) determined the FRAP activity of methanol extracts of M. fruticosa leaves spectrophotometrically at 595 nm and reported Trolox equivalent values of 3.038, 11.264, and 17.519 µg/mL at concentrations of 125, 250, and 500 µg/mL, respectively. These findings support the high antioxidant potential of the plant, and the results of the present study are consistent with this trend.

In conclusion, the results of phenolic content and antioxidant activity obtained in this study are generally consistent with findings reported in the literature. However, some of the observed differences can be attributed to various factors such as the type of solvent used, extraction time, source of plant material, and analytical methods. In this regard, the present study supports the diversity observed in the literature and reinforces the relevance of the findings.

The pH value of patties and similar products is a crucial factor in terms of both taste and safety. In this study, the increase in the pH value of patty samples with higher extract ratios is thought to be related to the pH of the M. fruticosa extract itself (average 6.15 ± 0.01). It was also observed that cooking affected the increase in pH. In this context, while the pH value of the patties in the control group was 5.79 ± 0.02 on average before cooking, it increased to 5.91 ± 0.03 on average with the cooking process (Table 4). Elbir et al. (2023) explained the increase in pH in meat following cooking as a result of the release of bonds containing imidazole, hydroxyl, and sulfhydryl groups.

In the current study, it was determined that the use of M. fruticosa extract obtained under optimized conditions in beef patties did not have a significant effect on moisture content. This observation may be attributed to the extract’s ability to interact with meat proteins and enhance the structural integrity of the meat matrix. Additionally, phenolic compounds present in the extract may contribute to the stabilization of the meat matrix, thereby reducing moisture loss and improving the retention of juiciness during cooking (Al-Juhaimi et al., 2020).

In the present study, a decrease in TBARS values was observed in samples treated with M. fruticosa extract, but this difference was not statistically significant. Although there are a limited number of studies on the use of M. fruticosa in meat products in the literature, these studies report similar results. For example, Awad et al. (2021) added 1.5% and 3.0% dried M. fruticosa leaves to beef meatballs and stored the products at 4 °C for 7 days and at -18 °C for 40 days. The results showed that the plant extract improved storage stability by increasing antioxidant capacity. In another study, different meat types were marinated with M. fruticosa extract, and various cooking methods were applied. It was reported that increasing the extract concentration slowed lipid oxidation by significantly decreasing TBARS values (Kılıç Altun et al., 2024). These effects are attributed to the strong antioxidant properties of phenolic compounds present in the plant (Zwolan et al., 2020). Based on these findings, the lack of a significant decrease in TBARS values observed in the present study may be related to the potential degradation or reduced activity of phenolic compounds during the cooking process, as well as differences in the form of the plant material and the extraction method (Shah et al., 2014).

Many quality parameters, such as crispiness, colour, nutrient composition, and flavour of meat, change with the cooking process (Devi et al., 2019). In addition, depending on the cooking conditions and formulation, stiffness occurs in the myofibrillar structure due to denaturation of proteins during cooking, and this is associated with increased water loss. However, it can be said that an increase in the pH values of the samples may contribute to increasing the cooking yield by reducing water loss (Cinar Topcu et al., 2024). Losses occurring during cooking determine the technological efficiency of the cooking process and are a critical factor for the industry (Hughes et al., 2014). In this context, the fact that the extract used in this study increased the cooking yield is a very important finding for the study. This result is in line with studies reporting that different plants and plant extracts positively affect cooking yield (Cabi Çarşı et al., 2019; Al-Juhaimi et al., 2020; Ahmed et al., 2022). In addition to the relationship of cooking yield with pH, it is thought that phenolic compounds in the extract may have positively affected the cooking yield by affecting protein-water interactions and increasing matrix stabilisation. It is known that phenolic compounds can form hydrogen bonds with protein molecules, which may contribute to the formation of a more compact protein network that retains water more effectively during heat treatment (Wang et al., 2023). In addition, these interactions can limit protein denaturation and aggregation, reducing fluid loss and thus cooking loss (Tannin et al., 2020). The antioxidant components contained in the extract may also contribute indirectly to water retention by maintaining the structural integrity of muscle proteins under thermal stress (Wang et al., 2024). Although pH data before and after cooking are informative, the interpretation of cooking yield would be more meaningful if supported by water retention capacity and tissue properties such as shear force or hardness. In order to better understand these mechanisms, it may be useful to investigate the relationships between these parameters in future studies.

Conclusion

In this study, the ethanol extract of M. fruticosa was optimized using response surface methodology, and its incorporation into beef patties was evaluated. The extract showed moderate levels of total phenolic content and antioxidant activity under optimized conditions. While M. fruticosa extract applications had no significant effect on TBARS values, they were found to increase cooking yield. Therefore, while the extract demonstrates potential as a natural additive, its effects on lipid oxidation and moisture retention require further investigation. The absence of sensory evaluation and storage stability assessments in this study limits the ability to generalize its commercial applicability. Future research should include sensory testing, long-term storage trials, and broader comparisons with other natural additives to better determine the practical viability of M. fruticosa in meat products. Nevertheless, this study contributes a useful methodology and offers a starting point for more comprehensive evaluations of this aromatic plant in functional food systems.
