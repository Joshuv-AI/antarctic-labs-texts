Characteristics and outcomes of younger adults with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest resuscitated with extracorporeal membrane oxygenation: a nationwide study
Authors: Yu Amemiya, Ayuka Kuwabara, Ryo Hisamune, Ryosuke Zushi, Hitoshi Kobata, Ryo Yamamoto, Akihiko Inoue, Toru Hifumi, Tetsuya Sakamoto, Yasuhiro Kuroda, Akira Takasu, Kazuma Yamakawa, Hirotaka Sawano, Hirotaka Sawano, Yuko Egawa, Shunichi Kato, Naofumi Bunya, Takehiko Kasai, Shinichi Ijuin, Shinichi Nakayama, Jun Kanda, Seiya Kanou, Seiya Kanou, Toru Takiguchi, Shoji Yokobori, Hiroaki Takada, Kazushige Inoue, Ichiro Takeuchi, Hiroshi Honzawa, Makoto Kobayashi, Tomohiro Hamagami, Wataru Takayama, Yasuhiro Otomo, Kunihiko Maekawa, Takafumi Shimizu, Satoshi Nara, Michitaka Nasu, Kuniko Takahashi, Yoshihiro Hagiwara, Shigeki Kushimoto

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExtracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation has shown promise in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, but evidence in younger adults remains limited. Younger adults represent a unique subgroup with distinct etiologies and potentially greater physiological reserve. This study aimed to identify clinical characteristics and assess factors associated with outcomes in younger adults receiving extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation.MethodsThis retrospective observational study analyzed data from the Study of Advanced Life Support for Ventricular Fibrillation with Extracorporeal Circulation in Japan registry, which prospectively collected data on adult out-of-hospital cardiac arrest patients receiving extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation across 36 Japanese institutions (2013–2018). Patients were stratified by age: younger adults (≤ 40 years) versus older adults (> 40 years). The primary outcome was favorable neurological status at discharge (cerebral performance category 1–2). Multivariable logistic regression and restricted cubic spline analyses were performed to identify factors associated with outcomes.ResultsOf 2,129 patients, 212 (10.0%) were younger adults (mean age, 32.2 ± 6.8 years) and 1,917 (90.0%) were older adults (mean age, 62.2 ± 11.1 years). Younger adults showed distinct etiological patterns with fewer acute coronary syndromes (13.7% vs. 53.4%) and more arrhythmias (30.2% vs. 9.8%) and myocarditis/cardiomyopathy (13.7% vs. 4.9%). Notably, the proportion of cases with unknown causes was higher among younger adults (40.6%) compared to older adults (18.3%). Multivariable analysis revealed that shockable rhythm was not associated with neurological outcomes in younger adults, in contrast to older adults where shockable rhythm retained its prognostic significance. Nonlinear spline analysis showed that the odds of favorable neurological outcomes remained relatively stable in younger adults despite prolonged low-flow durations, in contrast to older adults who showed deteriorating outcomes with longer ischemic periods.ConclusionsYounger adults undergoing extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation demonstrated distinct etiological patterns and enhanced tolerance to prolonged low-flow time relative to older adults. These findings generate the hypothesis that age-related differences in tolerance to prolonged low-flow time may exist between younger and older adults. However, given the observational and post-hoc nature of this study, prospective validation is needed before age-stratified extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation protocols can be recommended.

Introduction

Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR), defined by extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) during refractory cardiac arrest, has become an increasingly utilized intervention in patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) [1, 2]. ECPR has demonstrated promising outcomes in patients with cardiac arrest of cardiac origin (especially ventricular fibrillation/ventricular tachycardia) [2, 3], as well as in those with a short interval from cardiac arrest to ECPR initiation [4], and witnessed arrest with immediate initiation of basic life support. Several recent studies have shown that shorter low-flow time (i.e., the duration between the initiation of conventional cardiopulmonary resuscitation [CPR] and the start of ECMO) is associated with improved survival and favorable neurological outcomes, whereas a prolonged low-flow time is correlated with a worse prognosis [5–7]. However, most existing evidence was from heterogeneous adult populations and a significant proportion of cases were elderly [8, 9].

Younger adults with OHCA represent a unique subgroup with distinct etiologies, including congenital, structural, and primary arrhythmic causes [10, 11]. They typically have fewer comorbidities and potentially greater physiological reserve, suggesting they might derive greater benefit from advanced resuscitative measures such as ECPR [12]. In particular, individuals younger than 40 years of age are often defined as younger adults in the context of OHCA; this threshold aligns with the age classification used in the American Heart Association’s Heart Disease and Stroke Statistics report, which categorizes adults aged 20–39 years as “younger adults” and those aged 40–59 years as “middle-aged adults” [13], and with a recent JAMA review that adopted an upper age limit of 40 years for this population [14]. The estimated global incidence of OHCA in this age group ranges from 4.4 to 14.4 per 100,000 person-years [14]. Another study also reported more favorable outcomes (including higher survival rates and better neurological status as assessed by Cerebral Performance Category [CPC] scores) in patients under 40 years of age relative to older cohorts [15]. However, there are few large-scale studies on the use of ECPR in younger adults.

Given the ethical and resource implications of ECPR, especially in time-sensitive and resource-limited settings, a better understanding of the epidemiology, clinical characteristics, and outcomes of ECPR in younger adults is essential. Clarifying these factors may support more targeted clinical decision-making and improve patient selection criteria.

In this study, we performed a post-hoc analysis of a nationwide Japanese registry including over 2,000 adult ECPR cases to describe and evaluate patients of ≤ 40 years of age who underwent ECPR. The aim of this study was to identify clinical characteristics, cardiac arrest etiologies, and in-hospital outcomes, and to assess factors associated with mortality and poor neurological outcomes in this age group. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale analysis focusing specifically on younger adult patients treated with ECPR. It provides novel insights into the role and effectiveness of ECPR in this population.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective observational study utilized data from the SAVE-J II (Study of Advanced Life Support for Ventricular Fibrillation with Extracorporeal Circulation in Japan) registry. The SAVE-J II study is a multicenter observational registry that prospectively collected data on adult patients with OHCA who received ECPR across 36 institutions in Japan between January 2013 and December 2018 [15].

Study population

All adult patients (≥ 18 years) with OHCA who received ECPR and were registered in the SAVE-J II database were initially considered for inclusion. The exclusion criteria were as follows: initiation of ECMO after intensive care unit admission, missing data on primary outcome, age, or cardiac arrest etiology, transfer from or to another hospital on the day of ECMO initiation, and achievement of ROSC after successful cannulation, as continued ECPR would no longer be indicated in such cases. Patients were stratified into two groups based on age: the younger adult group (age ≤ 40 years) and the older adult group (age > 40 years).

Data collection and variables

The following clinical data were obtained from the SAVE-J II registry: age, sex, whether the cardiac arrest was witnessed, presence of bystander CPR, initial cardiac rhythm at the scene and on hospital arrival, location of cardiac arrest, administration of adrenaline and defibrillation before hospital arrival, airway management prior to hospital arrival, cardiac rhythm at the initiation of ECMO, treatment-related factors, time course variables, cause of cardiac arrest, return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) after hospital arrival, and ECMO-related information. Additional data collected included the length of hospital and intensive care unit stay, in-hospital mortality, and neurological outcomes. Shockable rhythm was defined as ventricular fibrillation, pulseless ventricular tachycardia, or any rhythm identified as shockable by an automated external defibrillator used by emergency medical services (EMS). The location of cardiac arrest was recorded using the following predefined categories in the SAVE-J II registry: private house, apartment, public space, road, workplace, ambulance, and other. For the purposes of this analysis, private house and apartment were combined into a single “home” category. The “other” category was assigned at the discretion of the treating physician at each institution and comprised cases that could not be classified into any of the predefined location groups, such as rivers, inside vehicles, or agricultural settings. Cardiac arrest occurring in the ambulance was defined as arrest that developed after EMS arrival in patients who had spontaneous circulation upon initial EMS evaluation. ROSC was defined as the presence of a palpable pulse sustained for at least one minute. The time from emergency call to hospital arrival was defined as the interval between the EMS call and hospital arrival. The time from hospital arrival to the initiation of ECMO was defined as the interval between hospital arrival and the establishment of ECMO support. The time from the emergency call to the initiation of ECMO was also recorded. Estimated low-flow time was defined as the time from cardiac arrest to the initiation of ECMO in cases where the arrest occurred in the ambulance, or from the EMS call to the initiation of ECMO in cases where the arrest occurred elsewhere. The causes of cardiac arrest were classified as acute coronary syndrome, aortic dissection or rupture of an aortic aneurysm, arrhythmia, myocarditis or cardiomyopathy, and others/unknown.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome was favorable neurological status at hospital discharge, defined as a CPC score of 1 (good recovery) or 2 (moderate disability). Secondary outcomes included survival to hospital discharge.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median with interquartile range and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. To identify factors associated with favorable neurological outcomes in the younger adult group, we performed univariate analysis followed by multivariable logistic regression. Variables with P values of < 0.10 in a univariate analysis or those considered clinically relevant were included in the multivariable model. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Additionally, we examined the nonlinear relationship between estimated low-flow time and favorable neurological outcome using restricted cubic spline regression with three knots positioned at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the low-flow time distribution. All statistical analyses were performed using R (ver. 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Two-sided P values of < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The SAVE-J II registry was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Kagawa University (Approval Number: 2018–110) as well as by the ethics committees of all participating institutions. The requirement for individual informed consent was waived due to the retrospective observational nature of the study and the use of anonymized data.

Results

Study population

During the study period, a total of 2,129 adult patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest who received ECPR were enrolled from the SAVE-J II registry (Fig. 1). Of these, 212 (10.0%) were classified into the younger adult group and 1,917 (90.0%) were classified into the older adult group. Neurological outcomes and associated factors

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, factors associated with favorable neurological outcomes varied between younger and older adults (Table 2, Fig. 2). Among younger adults, increasing age within the group was inversely associated with favorable neurological outcomes (OR, 0.948; 95% CI, 0.899–0.998; p = 0.044). Cardiac arrest occurring at other locations was significantly associated with better outcomes (OR, 6.287; 95% CI, 1.585–26.094; p = 0.009), though this finding should be interpreted with caution given the small number of cases in this subgroup (n = 14). Witnessed arrest (OR, 2.203; 95% CI, 0.769–7.496; p = 0.167) and shockable rhythm compared to asystole (OR, 2.305; 95% CI, 0.708–9.431; p = 0.197) were not significantly associated with neurological outcomes. Other factors, including sex, bystander CPR, prehospital ROSC, and estimated low-flow time, were not significantly associated with the outcomes in this group. In contrast, among older adults, increasing age was also inversely associated with favorable neurological outcomes (OR, 0.973; 95% CI, 0.960–0.986; p < 0.001), and male sex was associated with worse neurological outcomes in comparison to female sex (OR, 0.603; 95% CI, 0.417–0.883; p = 0.008). Arrest in public places (OR, 2.096; 95% CI, 1.404–3.123; p < 0.001) and arrest witnessed by EMS (OR, 2.324; 95% CI, 1.447–3.721; p < 0.001) were significantly associated with favorable neurological outcomes. In addition, bystander CPR (OR, 1.499; 95% CI, 1.084–2.087; p = 0.015), prehospital ROSC (OR, 2.981; 95% CI, 2.054–4.296; p < 0.001), and shockable rhythm (OR, 3.374; 95% CI, 1.749–7.355; p < 0.001) were significant predictors of favorable outcomes. Secondary outcomes

In the multivariable analysis for mortality in the younger adult group, estimated low-flow time was not significantly associated with mortality (OR, 0.985; 95% CI, 0.967–1.001; p = 0.090). Most other factors did not demonstrate significant associations with mortality outcomes in the younger adult group. In the older adult group, increasing age (OR 0.980; 95% CI, 0.970–0.990; p < 0.001) and male sex (OR, 0.630; 95% CI, 0.469–0.850; p = 0.002) were associated with higher mortality rates. Multiple factors related to the location of cardiac arrest influenced mortality. Absence of prehospital ROSC (OR, 0.351; 95% CI, 0.157–0.789; p = 0.010) and prolonged estimated low-flow time (OR, 0.988; 95% CI, 0.983–0.993; p < 0.001) were also independent predictors of mortality.

Discussion

This study investigated the characteristics and outcomes of younger adults undergoing ECPR compared to older patients, based on the largest ECPR registry dataset. Our findings revealed distinct age-dependent patterns in response to prolonged ischemic insults during cardiac arrest. Most notably, estimated low-flow time was not a significant predictor of poor neurological outcomes or mortality in younger adults (≤ 40 years), contrasting with older adults where prolonged low-flow time was an independent predictor of mortality. Although this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size of the younger adult group, it raises the hypothesis that current time-based ECPR selection criteria, primarily derived from mixed-age populations, may warrant evaluation in larger-scale studies specifically focused on younger adults.

Distinct etiological patterns

In our cohort, younger adults had markedly different cardiac arrest etiologies from older adults. While acute coronary syndrome was the predominant cause in older adults (53.4%), it accounted for only 13.7% among younger adults. Instead, arrhythmia (30.2%), myocarditis or cardiomyopathy (13.7%), and a large proportion of other or unknown causes (40.6%) were more prevalent in the younger group. These findings are consistent with previous epidemiological studies reporting markedly different etiological patterns across age groups. In a multicenter registry from South Asia, acute coronary syndrome accounted for over 60% of cardiac events in older adults (> 65 years), whereas it was observed in less than 12% of younger adults (≤ 45 years) [16]. Similarly, large-scale registry studies have shown that arrhythmias—particularly primary ventricular tachyarrhythmias—are more common causes of arrest in younger individuals [14], while ischemic events predominate in older populations.

This etiological diversity highlights the complex pathophysiology underlying cardiac arrest in younger adults, often involving inherited conditions, substance abuse, or rare cardiomyopathies [17–20]. Non-cardiac etiologies—including intracranial hemorrhage, opioid toxicity, electrolyte disturbances, and substance-induced QT prolongation—also contribute to this diagnostic complexity [21–23].

The notably high proportion of unknown etiologies in younger adults (40.6%) may reflect both the limitations of retrospective registry-based data collection and a genuine diagnostic challenge in this population. Unlike older adults in whom ischemic heart disease is often readily identified, younger adults frequently present with structurally occult or arrhythmia-based conditions that may require advanced post-arrest diagnostics such as genetic testing, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, or toxicological screening to establish a definitive etiology. This finding underscores the need for systematic post-arrest evaluation protocols specifically tailored to younger patients.

Our findings also identified male sex as associated with worse neurological outcomes in older adults, which is consistent with prior evidence from ECPR-specific and general OHCA populations. A secondary analysis of the SAVE-J II registry by Kawauchi et al. demonstrated that female sex was independently associated with favorable neurological outcomes in ECPR patients (adjusted OR, 1.60; 95% CI, 1.05–2.43), attributing this advantage to both potential selection bias favoring women with favorable prognosis and biological neuroprotective effects of estrogen [24]. However, a systematic review by Lakbar et al. encompassing over 1.9 million OHCA patients found no significant sex difference in survival after adjustment for confounders, suggesting that observed disparities may largely reflect differences in baseline characteristics and post-arrest care rather than intrinsic biological differences [25]. Similarly, a registry-based study by Park et al. in TTM-treated OHCA patients found no independent association between sex and neurological outcomes after full covariate adjustment [26]. These findings collectively suggest that the sex-based outcome difference observed in our older adult cohort warrants further investigation, particularly with respect to post-arrest management practices.

Low-flow time tolerance differences

The most clinically significant finding is the differential response to prolonged low-flow time between age groups, as demonstrated by our nonlinear spline analysis. These findings challenge conventional wisdom regarding low-flow time limits for ECPR candidacy and suggest that current time-based exclusion criteria may be overly restrictive for younger adults. The physiological mechanisms underlying this age-related difference likely involve superior cellular energy metabolism [27], enhanced antioxidant capacity [28, 29], better collateral circulation [30], and absence of significant comorbidities that compromise ischemic tolerance in younger adults.

Study limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a post-hoc analysis of a prospectively registered observational registry, and the age cutoff of ≤ 40 years was not pre-specified prior to analysis. Although this threshold aligns with the age classification used by the American Heart Association [13] and prior literature on OHCA in young adults [14], we cannot exclude the possibility that the cutoff may have influenced between-group differences. Second, this observational study cannot establish causation, and unmeasured confounders may have influenced the results. The ECPR selection criteria varied among participating centers, which may have introduced selection bias and affected the generalizability of our findings. Third, the location of cannulation (pre-hospital vs. in-hospital) was not systematically recorded in the registry and varied according to institutional protocol. The lack of data on pre-hospital ECMO initiation limits the characterization of ECPR implementation across centers. Fourth, the “other” arrest location category, which was associated with favorable neurological outcomes in younger adults, encompasses heterogeneous settings outside predefined registry categories. Given the small number of cases in this subgroup (n = 14), the possibility of a chance finding due to limited statistical power cannot be excluded. Fifth, the estimated low-flow time used in this study was defined as the interval from the EMS call to ECMO initiation for most patients, rather than the actual time from collapse to ECMO initiation. This definition may introduce misclassification of true ischemic duration, particularly in unwitnessed arrests, and may have influenced the observed relationship between low-flow time and outcomes. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the spline analysis results. Future research should focus on prospective validation of age-stratified ECPR criteria and investigation of the underlying mechanisms of enhanced ischemic tolerance in younger adults.

Conclusions

Younger adults undergoing ECPR demonstrated distinct etiological patterns and relatively stable neurological outcomes across a range of low-flow durations compared to older adults. These findings raise the possibility that age-related differences in ischemic tolerance may exist. However, given the observational and post-hoc nature of this study, as well as the limited sample size of the younger adult group, these results should be considered exploratory. Prospective studies with pre-specified age stratification are needed to determine whether age-stratified ECPR selection criteria, including potentially extended time windows for younger adults, are warranted.
