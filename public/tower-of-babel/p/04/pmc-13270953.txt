Introduction

The management of arrhythmias in pediatric patients with corrected congenital heart disease (CHD) presents a challenge owing to the interplay between scar-related anatomic substrates and autonomic dysfunction.1,2,3 Although iatrogenic substrate modification can lead to macroreentrant tachycardias, such as atrial flutter (AFl), the development of sinus node dysfunction (SND) creates a difficult therapeutic dilemma regarding the need for permanent pacing.1 Postsurgical AFl typically involves circuits dependent on the cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI) or atypical circuits related to surgical incision lines. However, previous catheter ablations in the right atrium may also contribute to further substrate modification, facilitating new arrhythmias.4 Concurrently, SND in these patients often involves a mixed etiology: structural damage to the node and extrinsic parasympathetic suppression. Recently, cardioneuroablation (CNA), which modulates parasympathetic innervation by ablating ganglionic plexi, has emerged as a physiological alternative to pacemakers for functional bradyarrhythmias.5, 6, 7, 8 We present the case of a 6-year-old girl with a complex arrhythmic profile consisting of SND with a functional component, atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia (AVNRT), and a macroreentrant atrial tachycardia. This case illustrates the management of a complex post-CHD substrate where autonomic modulation and sequential ablation were valuable in addressing reversible contributors to arrhythmias, thereby avoiding the present need for permanent pacing.

Case report

This is the case of a 6-year-old female patient (weight 20 kg; height 1.2 m; body surface area 0.79 m2) with a history of corrective surgery for complex CHD performed at 7 months of age. The repair included correction of the total anomalous pulmonary venous connection, which typically requires opening the pulmonary venous confluence and closing the associated ostium secundum atrial septal defect using a bovine pericardial patch, in addition to the ligation of a patent ductus arteriosus. At 5 years of age, she began experiencing episodes of dizziness and syncope, leading to a diagnosis of SND. An epicardial dual-chamber permanent pacemaker system was implanted. Owing to generator extrusion, the entire system was completely explanted at our hospital. During her hospitalization, a 24-hour Holter monitor documented sinus bradycardia with pauses up to 4.4 seconds (Figure 1A). Crucially, this pause was documented during a period of wakefulness. An atropine challenge test (0.04 mg/kg) demonstrated a heart rate (HR) increase of >25%, reaching 98 beats per minute (bpm) (specifically, a 25.6% increase from a baseline HR of 78 bpm). Although primary sinus node impairment was still considered present, atropine challenge provided evidence of additional functional impairment on account of autonomic suppression. This was considered a treatable and reversible condition amenable to catheter ablation.

First procedure: Slow pathway ablation and CNA

A combined procedure was performed under general anesthesia, guided by the CARTO 3 3-dimensional electroanatomic mapping system (Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA). The baseline electrophysiology study revealed normal conduction intervals (PA 45 ms; AH 80 ms; HV 42 ms) and an antegrade Wenckebach point at 390 ms (153 bpm). During programmed atrial stimulation, typical AVNRT was induced, with a ventriculoatrial interval of 60 ms and concentric, early retrograde atrial activation (Figure 1B), and ventriculoatrial dissociation upon ventricular stimulation from the right ventricular apex at 520 ms, a finding that has a high positive predictive value for typical AVNRT.8

Successful slow pathway ablation was performed using radiofrequency (RF) energy, guided by the CARTO 3 system and using an ablation index (AI)–guided approach (mean AI 400). This technique allows for effective lesion delivery while minimizing the risk of atrioventricular block, addressing the safety concerns inherent in pediatric AVNRT ablation.8 Success was confirmed by the induction of a junctional rhythm and subsequent noninducibility of the tachycardia.

Next, CNA was performed. Using a voltage and activation map of the right atrium, the sinus node was identified adjacent to a low-voltage border zone (Figure 1C). The superior and inferior right-sided ganglionic plexi regions were mapped and ablated, identified by the presence of fractionated atrial potentials (Figure 1D and Supplemental Video 1). 21 RF applications were delivered (mean power 30 W; mean AI 400) until elimination of these potentials.

Procedural success was confirmed by observing an attenuated response to a new dose of atropine, with an HR increase of <10% (from 96 bpm to 102 bpm, an increase of 6.25%), indicative of effective vagal denervation and meeting the physiological endpoint of vagal abolition.4 The patient was discharged in a stable sinus rhythm at 88 bpm and remained asymptomatic at follow-up, with a mean HR of 98 bpm on Holter monitoring and no significant pauses.

Second procedure: AFl ablation

6 months later, the patient reported palpitations and a decline in functional class. An electrocardiogram documented AFl with variable atrioventricular conduction and a mean ventricular response of 120 bpm (Figure 2). She was scheduled for a second ablation procedure.

Surface electrocardiogram during atrial tachycardia. 12-lead electrocardiogram showing a regular, narrow QRS complex tachycardia with a mean ventricular rate of 120 beats per minute. Flutter waves with a “saw-tooth” morphology are observed, negative in leads II, III, and aVF, and positive in V1, with an atrial rate of approximately 270 beats per minute (cycle length of ∼224 ms) and variable atrioventricular conduction. These findings are consistent with atrial flutter, presumably atypical in the postsurgical context.

Under CARTO 3 guidance, a tachycardia with a cycle length (CL) of 224 ms was confirmed. Entrainment from the proximal coronary sinus showed a postpacing interval (PPI) equal to the CL (PPI−CL 0 ms), whereas, from the distal coronary sinus, the difference was 56 ms. A high-density activation map revealed a macroreentrant circuit with a zone of slow conduction in the intercaval region (Figure 3A and Supplemental Video 2). Entrainment at this site confirmed its participation in the circuit (PPI−CL 0 ms and concealed fusion) (Figure 3B).

Ablation at this location only succeeded in prolonging the CL to 264 ms (a 40 ms increase) without terminating the arrhythmia. This prolongation indicated modification of the circuit properties, likely altering the entrance or exit zone of the critical isthmus. Given this finding, the strategy was reassessed, and CTI ablation was performed.

During RF application in the isthmus, the flutter terminated abruptly, followed by a 3.952-second sinus pause that required the placement of a temporary pacing system in the right atrium for acute hemodynamic support (Figure 4 and Supplemental Figure 1). After stabilization, bidirectional isthmus block was confirmed, with transisthmus conduction times of 72 ms and 86 ms and a right atrial voltage map (Supplemental Video 3). The temporary pacemaker was removed 24 hours later, and the patient remained in a stable sinus rhythm with a mean HR of 92 bpm (Figure 5).

Discussion

This case exhibits the complexity of managing arrhythmogenic substrates in pediatric patients where structural CHD repairs,3 previous catheter ablations, and autonomic dysfunction intersect. Regarding AVNRT, it is 1 of the most frequent arrhythmias in pediatric patients.8 Although cryoablation is generally preferred for slow pathway modification in young children to mitigate the risk of permanent atrioventricular block, the use of RF guided by AI in this 6-year-old patient, coupled with high-definition mapping, provided precise and effective lesion delivery, balancing safety and efficiency.9 The patient’s history of syncope and the documented 4.4-second pause during wakefulness classified her as having clinically significant SND.10 This severe bradyarrhythmia would typically place a pediatric patient who has undergone complex surgical repair in the class II category for permanent pacemaker implantation.10 However, the positive atropine challenge, demonstrating a significant HR increase (>25%), definitively identified a significant functional component to the SND.6,11,12 This phenotype made her a candidate for CNA, aiming to avoid the need for multiple revisions associated with lifelong pacing in a growing child. The observation of a prominent sinus pause after AFl termination is evidence of an ongoing degree of primary sinus node impairment apart from CAN benefit. This phenomenon aligns with tachycardia-bradycardia syndrome, where chronic overdrive pacing from the tachycardia suppresses the sinus node, revealing its intrinsic weakness upon cessation of the rapid rhythm.2 It is important to acknowledge that the patient likely has a degree of intrinsic node disease13,14; however, the initial CNA successfully modulated the autonomic tone enough to compensate for this, avoiding the need for a pacemaker. We acknowledge the potential for the progression of SND and the future need for permanent pacing in this patient. However, the current interventions have helped avoid or delay permanent pacing, which carries significant long-term benefits for a growing child. The rapid recovery of the sinus node, demonstrated by the maintenance of a stable sinus rhythm after stabilization and the ability to remove the temporary pacemaker within 24 hours, validates this strategy. The AFl mechanism warrants careful analysis.15 Although AFl can be related to surgical scars, it is plausible that the initial slow pathway and CNA ablations contributed to iatrogenic substrate modification, facilitating a CTI-dependent macroreentry.4,16,12 The initial ablation in the intercaval region prolonged the CL, likely by altering the pivot point or entrance to the isthmus,15 but the ultimate termination at the CTI confirms the arrhythmia was CTI dependent.16 This supports the concept that, in complex substrates, multiple modifications may be required to interrupt the circuit. Follow-up and postprocedure monitoring are critical in patients with mixed SND and reentrant substrates. Currently, the patient is followed up with 24-hour Holter monitoring every 3 months. Although an implantable loop recorder would be the gold standard to assess for asymptomatic pauses or recurrence of high-rate arrhythmias, resource constraints in our setting have limited its use. However, the patient has remained asymptomatic with a mean HR of >90 bpm and no significant pauses on serial Holter monitors.

Conclusion

We present a case demonstrating the complex interaction among postsurgical substrates, iatrogenic modifications, and autonomic dysfunction. The coexistence of CTI-dependent flutter and SND (manifesting as tachycardia-bradycardia syndrome) highlights the need for a comprehensive approach. CNA provided a physiological solution that, although not correcting the intrinsic cellular defect, modulated the autonomic tone sufficiently to avoid device dependence. This case underscores the complementarity of autonomic and substrate-based electrophysiology therapies.
