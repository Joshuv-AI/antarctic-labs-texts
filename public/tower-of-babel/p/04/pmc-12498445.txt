Multiple detection method for SARS-CoV-2 with aptamer cocktails depending on their localization in paper-based assays
Authors: Dilek Çam Derin, Elif Gündüz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe nucleocapsid (N) and spike (S) proteins are key markers for SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis and treatment because of their antigenic regions. Aptamers targeting these proteins have been developed for various diagnostic platforms, including Lateral Flow Assays (LFAs). However, existing studies have focused mainly on the use of single aptamer or antibody/aptamer sandwiches in capture zones, lacking the advantages of using aptamer cocktails. The present study aimed to develop multiple aptamer cocktail-based lateral flow assays (mACLFAs) for rapid and accurate detection of SARS-CoV-2 in clinical samples.MethodsSpherical gold nanoparticles were synthesized and used as labels for aptamer conjugation for naked-eye analysis. Aptamers specific to the S and N proteins of SARS-CoV-2 were chosen and used as either capture or detection reagents for the sandwich assay model. A nitrocellulose membrane was used to develop the mACLFAs.ResultsModel 2 was the most efficient multiple-assay model for viral diagnosis, although models 1 and 3 correctly detected the virus in clinical samples. Model 4 and Model 5 had nonspecific interactions and were not preferred for multiple assay development. The specificity of models 1, 2 and 3 was 100%, and the sensitivity of models 2 and 3 was 100%, while it was 96.6% for Model 1.ConclusionOur findings demonstrated that the accuracy of viral detection was improved by the use of aptamer cocktails, as both the N and S proteins were captured in clinical samples. The results also highlighted the importance of the position of the detector aptamers in the capture zones in terms of the accuracy and interaction of the aptamers in the sandwich assay. Thus, these findings will be valuable for developing diagnostic tools to monitor and prevent future outbreaks, such as monkey pox virus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11751-3.Keywords: Multiple lateral flow assay, Aptamer cocktails, SARS-CoV-2, N protein, S protein

Introduction

The global spread of SARS-CoV-2 highlights the vital importance of effective diagnostic tools for controlling the pandemic. Studies have identified the N and S proteins as important markers for SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis and treatment because of their significant antigenic regions [1]. Since the N protein is conserved and abundant during infection, it is an ideal target for diagnostic assays. The high stability and quantity of the N protein in clinical samples improve the sensitivity of diagnostic assays [2]. The receptor-binding domain of the S protein (sRBD) is highly immunogenic and essential for both diagnostic and therapeutic interventions. The ability of the sRBD to bind to and penetrate host cells makes it an important target for the recognition of SARS-CoV-2 [3].

Traditional methods such as reverse transcription‒polymerase chain reaction (RT‒PCR) are efficient but time-consuming and require a specialized laboratory [4]. In addition, loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) [5], CRISPR-based detection [6], chest CT scans [7], enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) [8], serological tests [9] and electrochemical techniques have also been reported for SARS-CoV-2 detection [10]. However, these methods can result in false positives and require technical expertise, advanced settings, and radiation exposure [11–13] and serological assays are not suitable for early diagnosis. As alternatives to these methods, LFAs are fast, easy and cost effective. However, LFAs developed for SARS-CoV-2 detection frequently use antibodies that recognize specific viral antigens, and they need to be improved. For example, the sensitivity of LFAs was improved by the use of monoclonal antibodies for N protein detection [14]. Liu et al. developed a multiple LFA (mLFA) capable of concurrently detecting both the N and S proteins via a dual-antibody sandwich structure with fluorescent labeling to improve the sensitivity of the assay [15]. Despite their efficacy, antibody-based LFAs have drawbacks, including batch-to-batch variability, low stability and potential cross-reactivity [16].

Aptamers, which are synthetic oligonucleotides, represent a promising alternative to antibodies for diagnostic applications. During the COVID-19 pandemic, various aptamers have been developed for the specific recognition of SARS-CoV-2 [17, 18] and have been utilized in the development of electrochemical sensors [19], magnetic sensors [20], immunosensors [21], and lateral flow assays (LFAs) [22] for SARS-CoV-2 detection. However, these studies are based on the use of single-type aptamer sandwiches or antibody/aptamer sandwiches in the capture zones. Thus, the use of an aptamer cocktail targeting different epitopes on the same or separate targets provides substantial advantages over the use of a single aptamer [23, 24]. It offers enhanced adaptability, better target coverage, and greater resistance against mutations to recognize a wide range of target molecules or distinct epitopes [25, 26]. Although aptamer-based LFAs have been used to detect the N and S proteins of SARS-CoV-2, a limited number of aptamer pairs have been used in these assays, and detection has been performed on separate strips [27]. Therefore, mACLFAs that use a mixture of aptamers on a single strip to simultaneously detect the N and S proteins have not yet been developed and are still needed for viral detection. Because one of the key advantages of multiple LFA over traditional methods including RT-PCR, ELISA which are less feasible for low-resource settings [28] and single-target LFA is its ability to detect multiple viral antigens simultaneously [29]. Thus, since multiple LFA is cost-effective, portable, and does not require cold-chain storage, making it an ideal solution for mass screening in resource-limited environments [30] it plays a crucial role in improving diagnostic accuracy and pandemic control efforts.

In this research, we developed mACLFAs for the rapid detection of SARS-CoV-2 in clinical samples by using aptamers specific to the N protein and sRBD. Thus, the details of the design, optimization and validation of the mACLFAs were evaluated by comparing the aptamer localization on the point-of-care tests.

Experimental section

Reagents and apparatus

All chemicals used were of analytical grade. Alfa Aesar (Kandel, Germany) supplied the trisodium citrate and tetrachloroauric acid. Saline-sodium citrate (SSC) buffer and Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP) were supplied by Multicell (Lawrenceville, GA, USA) and Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), respectively. The size of the synthesized gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) was measured by scanning/transmission electron microscopy (STEM, Sivas University). The maximum absorption spectrum (λmax) of the AuNP/capture aptamer conjugates was read with an Epoch 2 plate reader/spectrophotometer (BioTek, CA, United States). Assay membranes with varying flow rates (E1–E2) were obtained from Whatman (GE Healthcare, Dassel, Germany). Aptamer sequences specific to the N protein (N APT3 and NP) [31] and sRBD (S APT1, S APT2) [32] were obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA, USA) with the desired modifications. Thiol-modified aptamers are called capture aptamers, whereas biotinylated aptamers are detection aptamers. Preparation of gold nanoparticle-labeled N–S aptamers

For the synthesis of AuNPs, HAuCl4.3H2O was reduced with sodium citrate. Specifically, 1 mM HAuCl4.3H2O was boiled, and 1% sodium citrate was added. The mixture was subsequently cooled to room temperature and centrifuged to obtain concentrated AuNPs. For conjugation, the capture aptamers S APT1, S APT2 and NP were individually activated by TCEP and incubated for 1 h. 4 μM for the NP aptamer and 8 μM for the S APT1 and S APT2 aptamers were used for conjugations, and activated reactions were mixed with 1 mL of AuNPs, separately. Then, 0.01 M PBS was added to the solutions for aging with saline. The conjugates were then centrifuged, and the pellet was washed with suspension buffer, which consisted of phosphate buffer containing 0.25% Tween 20, sucrose and bovine serum albumin, twice and stored at 4 °C in the same buffer.

Preparation of aptamer cocktails and design of mACLFA models

Strips consisting of a sample pad, a conjugated pad, a nitrocellulose membrane and an absorbent pad were prepared as described in our previous study [33]. Virus collection buffer (VNAT), SSC, and RB buffer comprising PBS, 0.1% Tween-20, and 2% BSA were used for flowing the sample, seperately. To prepare the N and S aptamer cocktail, AuNP/capture aptamer conjugates were mixed before loading onto the conjugate pad and drying. To generate the test line (TL) and control line (CL), biotinylated aptamers and complementary (comp) sequences to the capture aptamers were immobilized, respectively according to the principle of streptavidin‒biotin interaction. For the mACLFAs, 5 assay designs were prepared. The order of the detection aptamers used as the first test line (TL1) and second test line (TL2) was altered on the basis of the assay model. All the assay models were prepared by showing the capture lines as TL1-TL2-CL, respectively. For example, Model 1 was prepared as S APT1-N APT3-comp to NP, which shows the order of the lines as TL1-TL2-CL, respectively. Model 2 was designed as S APT2-N APT3-comp to NP, respectively. Model 3 was N APT3-S APT2-comp to NP; Model 4 was S APT2-N APT3-comp to S APT2; and Model 5 was designed as N APT3-S APT2-comp to S APT2, respectively. Figure 1 shows a schematic representation of the working principle of all mACLFAs models. The statistical significance of negative strips compared to positive strips was analyzed using ImageJ software. For each model, histogram graphs were generated in ImageJ by measuring the TL1-TL2 color intensities of positive and negative strips loaded with positive and negative samples, respectively. Statistical evaluation was performed using a Student’s t-test based on the mean values obtained.

Clinical sample preparation for SARS-CoV-2 detection

The detection of SARS-CoV-2 in clinical samples was performed at the BSL3 laboratory at Inonu University in Malatya, Türkiye, by applying 100 µL of nasopharyngeal/oropharyngeal swab in VNAT solution on the sample pad. A selection of positive and negative clinical samples confirmed by real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (rRT‒PCR) were randomly chosen without considering the age, sex or other clinical history of the patients. The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Inonu University, Clinical Research Ethics Committee (2023/74).

Results and discussion

Characterization of the AuNPs

The λmax of the synthesized AuNPs was 521 nm (Additional file Fig. 1). The size of the colloidal AuNPs with a uniform distribution was 13 nm (Fig. S1B). The subsequent binding of the capture aptamers to the AuNPs caused the λmax to shift from 526–527 nm, as expected, owing to the aptamer coating (Fig. S1, C-E). Since the concentration of capture aptamers on the surface of AuNPs significantly affects their stability, concentrations of 4 μM for NP and 8 μM for S APT1 or S APT2 were chosen for coating because of the lack of aggregation of AuNPs (data not shown) to ensure visibility with the naked eye.

Multiple detection of SARS-CoV-2 in clinical specimens

Clinical samples were directly taken from the tubes containing nasopharyngeal/oropharyngeal swabs and loaded onto the prepared mACLFAs. Within approximately 5–7 min, distinct lines formed in the capture zone, allowing for the visual determination of positive or negative results. Considering that the collection of the swab sample and its transfer into the viral transport solution takes approximately 2 min, and the subsequent sample loading onto the LFA, followed by conjugate migration and line formation, occurs within 5–7 min, the total time required for SARS-CoV-2 detection was approximately 9–10 min. The strip loading was performed at room temperature without the need for specific temperature conditions or optimization. On the basis of the preliminary binding assay, the NP binding intensity was stronger than that of the S-aptamers except for strip 2 (Additional file Fig. 2). Each assay model has its own characteristics depending on the buffer composition, aptamer pairings, and membrane configurations, and VNAT was preferred for further assays. Initially, validated clinical samples from the same patient were analyzed using at least five or ten strip tests for Model 1, 2 and 3 to evaluate the repeatability and effectiveness of the strips in detecting the target analyte (Additional file Fig. 3). Since nonspecific binding was observed in Models 4 and 5 (data not shown), we continued with Models 1, 2 and 3 because at least one TL correctly identified the virus. All the models worked correctly, as the CLs were visible. However, the CLs were more intense when designed to complement the NP aptamer (Figs. 2, 3 and 4) compared to when they were designed to complement the S aptamer conjugates for Models 4 and 5 (data not shown). Thus, these three models were further tested for specificity and sensitivity using 30 positive and 30 negative nasopharyngeal/oropharyngeal swab samples from individuals suspected of having COVID-19. Compared with single aptamer-based LFAs, the developed mACLFAs provide a comprehensive diagnostic tool confirming the presence of viruses via multiple types of targeting. This is crucial when the virus mutates and changes the antigenic region targeted by a single aptamer. Additionally, if a single line produces a weak signal, the other line can still confirm the presence of the virus. In SARS-CoV-2 variants, the highest mutations were observed in the N, nsp3, S, nsp4, nsp12, and M proteins, with the highest mutation frequencies in the S protein occurring in amino acids 508–635 and 381–508, while the highest mutation frequency in the N protein was reported in amino acids 164–246 [34]. Therefore, aptamer cocktails could be effective in diagnosing variants, or specific aptamer cocktails targeting the mutant regions of these variants could be developed for multiple variant detection in mACLFAs.

Model 1 worked more efficiently on the E1 membrane (Fig. 2, A-B) than on the E2 membrane (data not shown) in terms of the flow rate, binding capacity and sensitivity. Thus, E1 was preferred for all the models. As shown in Fig. 2, negative (A) and positive (B) samples were successfully recognized by the designed mACLFAs without nonspecific interactions. Although detection on the TL1 of strips 1, 7, 9, 11, 26, and 28 (Fig. 2B) was not detected, the TL1 of the remaining strips was clear. This might be related to variations in the viral load since the clinical swabs were collected as having different cycle thresholds (ct), < 25 (Table 2) or this variability may be attributed to differences in the composition of swab samples collected from different patients which may interrupt the interaction of the S-aptamers with the target. Furthermore, this may be caused by the use of the same aptamer as a capture and detector aptamer in the sandwich format. This can result in weaker detection than the dual aptamer sandwich format [35]. Notably, TL2 was clear compared with TL1, except for strips 26–27 (Fig. 2B). This might be caused by the high affinity and effective binding of N-aptamers, especially in cocktails. Because the N aptamer demonstrated stronger affinity, which may be due to the higher abundance of the N protein compared to the S protein, along with the strong binding affinity of N-specific aptamers and the relatively enhanced interaction between aptamers and N proteins in swab samples evidenced by the distinct LFA strips (Additional file Fig. 4) contribute to the observed differences in binding efficiency. Because specifically, the dissociation constants (KD) for the NP aptamer and N aptamer were determined to be 0.57 nM and 0.39 nM, respectively [36], whereas the S aptamers exhibited the KD values of 5.8 nM for S APT 1 and 19.9 nM for S APT 2 [32].

The mACLFAs developed by Model 2 correctly recognized the virus in the clinical samples (Fig. 3). However, for strips 27–28, the signals on TL1, TL2 and CL were weaker than those on the other strips. This may be due to the amount of virus particles in the clinical samples or the working efficiency of the prepared strip tests. Except for these strips, both the negative (Fig. 3A) and positive samples (Fig. 3B) were clearly detected on each line without nonspecific binding. The color intensities of all the lines were generally greater than those of Model 1. For TL1, this is most likely because of the use of S APT2, which binds to the target with high affinity and generates a stronger signal than S APT1 used in Model 1. In addition, capture was not adversely affected by the use of the same aptamer, S APT2, in the sandwich format compared with S APT1 in Model 1. These findings illustrate the importance of the type and affinity of the selected aptamers and their binding capacity under environmental conditions for mACLFAs. Although there was no significant difference between the S and N protein detection on TL1 and TL2, respectively the recognition of the N protein was slightly better than that of the S protein (Fig. 3B).

Model 3 also showed similar signal intensities on the TL and CL as those of Models 1 and 2 (Fig. 4). Nonspecific interactions were not observed in the negative samples (Fig. 4A). While all the positive samples were recognized, strips 21, 26, 27, 28 and 30 did not correctly bind to TL1. However, all the prepared assays worked correctly since the virus in the clinical samples was detected on TL2. Therefore, the detection accuracy was improved by preparing multiple lines even if one target was not recognized on a single line. Thus, this finding highlights the importance of using and developing mACLFAs instead of single TL-based LFAs [37] which can result in false negatives.

Interestingly, the binding of the N aptamer to the target becomes weaker when it is immobilized as TL1 (Fig. 4B) instead of TL2 (Fig. 3B). This can be caused by the short retention time and interaction of N proteins with the NP aptamer when it is present on TL1. However, this approach is not applicable for S protein recognition since the binding profile of S aptamers does not change when they are positioned on TL1 (Fig. 3B) or TL2 (Fig. 4B). Therefore, the localization of the detection aptamer was found to be significant and affected the assay efficiency depending on the aptamer type. Thus, these findings pioneer the development of multiple LFAs by optimizing the order and placement of aptamers, as most studies focus on single detection, the use of multiple antibodies, or the improvement of aptamer binding affinity and chemical modifications to increase the sensitivity [38].

The sensitivity and specificity of the developed mACLFA models were calculated as described in a previous study [39] based on the presence of at least one test line on the assay. Additionally, these results were compared with the specificity and sensitivity values obtained for the individual detection of N and S proteins on the same test strips. The analysis revealed that the specificity of all models was 100%. In terms of sensitivity, Model 1 exhibited a sensitivity of 96.6%, while the sensitivity for detecting S (TL1) and N (TL2) proteins individually was 80% and 93%, respectively. For Model 2, the overall sensitivity was 100%, which was consistent with the sensitivity observed for both S (TL1) and N (TL2) protein detection. Similarly, Model 3 demonstrated a sensitivity of 100%, whereas the individual detection sensitivities for N (TL1) and S (TL2) proteins were 93% and 83%, respectively. Thus, the sensitivity of LFAs is greater when they are designed in multiple formats than when they are designed in single-line formats, including aptamer-based LFAs [40] and their detection accuracy is greater than that of LFAs developed with aptamers [41, 42] or other reagents [43, 44] which are based on single-line detection without complex experimental steps. The p-value for the difference between positive and negative TLs was 0.004 for Model 1, < 0.001 for Model 2, and 0.002 for Model 3, demonstrating a statistically significant difference between negative and positive strips. Representative histogram graphs obtained from a positive and a negative strip for each model have been provided as Additional Fig. 5. The difference in line measurements between positive and negative strips is also clearly observable in the graphs. Thus, the developed mACLFAs offer high sensitivity and rapid visual detection of different viral surface antigens on the same strip, making it a ready-to-use and cost-effective alternative compared to existing SARS-CoV-2 diagnostic techniques such as LAMP, single LFA, and electrochemical sensors [45] require the design of nucleic acids and primers [46], signal enhancement [47], and the preparation of complex nanoparticles [48].

In addition, the importance of the aptamer duals and the position of the detection aptamers on mACLFAs were also observed. The capture of N proteins was lower in Model 3, in which N APT3 was placed on TL1, than in Model 2, in which N APT3 was placed on TL2. This result was also similar to that of S protein detection. The detection rate was lower when the S aptamer was used on TL2 (Model 3) than when it was used on TL1 (Model 2). Clinical samples were simultaneously selected and the limit of detection (LoD) was evaluated based on the detectability of samples with ct values ranging from 14.2 to 25.9 (Table 2). While the sample with the higher ct value was undetectable in Model 2 (Fig. 3B, strip 28), it was detected in Model 1 (Fig. 2B, strip 28) and Model 3 (Fig. 4B, strip 28). Thus, LoD depends on the developed mACLFA models. Interestingly, different clinical samples with the same ct value can exhibit varying line intensities when loaded onto strips prepared using the same model (Figs. 2B- 3B, strips 3, 18). As previously mentioned, this may be attributed to the potential impact of sample composition on detection.

To say that, based on the obtained data, the non-specific binding observed in negative samples for Model 4 and Model 5 suggested that the sequence arrangement of detector aptamers in the capture zone significantly influences their ability to bind to the target. This effect may be attributed to the influence of sequence arrangement on the secondary and tertiary structures of the aptamers, which could potentially alter the accessibility of their binding sites. Additionally, non-specific binding in assays may resulted from unintended interactions between aptamers and assay components, steric hindrance, and the influence of assay conditions on the conformational stability of aptamers caused by competitive binding. The spatial arrangement and density of immobilized aptamers may affect binding efficiency, potentially leading to cross-reactivity or background signals. Thus, these factors collectively compromising the assay specificity and sensitivity needs the careful optimization of aptamer sequence, assay buffer composition, and membrane functionalization strategies.

As a summary, in aptamer-based diagnostic tests, although aptamers exhibit a relatively high potential for cross-reactivity, our developed mACLFA assays have demonstrated, following optimization, that aptamers can be successfully employed in multiplex viral detection formats. Because the application of an aptamer cocktail may enhance antigen detection performance, as two aptamers shown in this study can bind to different moieties on a protein surface in a non-competitive manner [49]. Furthermore, the use of multi-aptamer strategies can help mitigate target heterogeneity, thereby improving detection accuracy [50]. Besides, aptamers are highly stable compared to antibodies in both conjugated and non-conjugated forms in a suitable temperature, a property that affords extended shelf life for rapid diagnostic tests. Moreover, due to the relative ease of modifying aptamers for covalent immobilization compared to antibodies, our mACLFA assays were not only rapidly developed but also exhibited excellent batch-to-batch consistency. Provided that the necessary instrumentation for aptamer synthesis and modification is available, and considering the robust performance of mACLFA assays in clinical samples, further optimizations including a high number clinical samples and verifications could render these assays applicable in real-world applications. However, limitations of this study may be that these assays are predominantly qualitative or semi-quantitative, which restricts their ability to precisely measure antigen levels. Furthermore, environmental conditions (e.g., temperature and humidity) and operator-dependent variability can adversely affect reproducibility and overall performance. Finally, challenges in synthesis, stability, scalability, regulatory approval, cost for producing large quantities with high specificity and affinity and structural instability under varying environmental conditions of the aptamers should be optimized carefully, for large scale production.

Conclusion

In this study, a cocktail of N and S aptamers on a single strip was used to create mACLFAs for rapid and accurate SARS-CoV-2 detection, firstly. High sensitivity and specificity were found by Models 1, 2 and 3, and the significance of the locations of the aptamers along with the cocktails used and the aptamer concentrations for mACLFAs were illustrated. Clinical specimens were diagnosed in 5–7 min, which is advantageous for viral detection to increase the accuracy of the test results. These findings could be adapted to rapid assays for multiple viral detection in possible outbreaks.
