Changes in epidermal thickness and their correlation with clinical characteristics in patients with vitiligo
Authors: Luling Huang, Weiwei Sun, Zhubiao Ye, Yu Liu, Kaiqiao He, Shuli Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Vitiligo is an autoimmune disorder characterized by epidermal melanocyte damage, with the typical clinical manifestation of white patches of skin. Keratinocytes, which work in concert with melanocytes to maintain the structural and functional integrity of the skin, are implicated in the progression of vitiligo. Recent studies have reported abnormal keratinocyte proliferation and epidermal thickening in some patients with vitiligo; however, the relationship between these changes and the clinical characteristics of vitiligo remains unclear. We assessed the changes in epidermal thickness in patients with vitiligo and their correlation with clinical characteristics. Compared to the non-lesional skins, the stratum corneum, viable epidermis, and full epidermis in the lesional skins were all significantly thicker. The thickness of the stratum corneum in the head, neck, and trunk was greatly lower than that in the extremities. The thickness of the stratum corneum in the sun-exposed area was higher than that in the sun-protected area, whereas the thickness of the viable epidermis decreased. In conclusion, our study found that the epidermis in the lesional skins of patients with vitiligo was significantly thickened, especially in the sun-exposed areas and extremities.

Introduction

Vitiligo is a chronic depigmentation skin disorder characterized by epidermal melanocyte damage, with an approximate global prevalence of 0.5-2% worldwide [1]. The main clinical features of vitiligo are white patches on the skin or mucosa, which may be accompanied by active manifestations such as inflammatory vitiligo and trichrome vitiligo [2]. Due to the incomplete understanding of its pathogenesis, there is a lack of effective clinical treatments. Keratinocytes work in conjunction with melanocytes to maintain the structural and functional integrity of the skin [3]. Keratinocytes are involved in vitiligo disease progression by secreting key chemokines that coordinate T cell migration and localization [4]. Recent studies have found that the abnormal proliferation of keratinocytes in vitiligo leads to epidermis thickening [5]. However, the relationship between the thickened epidermis and the clinical characteristics of vitiligo is not yet fully understood. Therefore, this study aims to analyze the changes in epidermal thickness in patients with vitiligo and their correlation with clinical characteristics.

Materials and methods

Study population

Clinical data of 191 patients diagnosed with vitiligo by skin biopsy between January 2017 and January 2023 were retrospectively collected from the vitiligo database of the Department of Dermatology, Xijing Hospital. Patients with vitiligo with complete pathologic and baseline information were included in this study. The exclusion criteria were patients with a history of vitiligo treatment within the past three months. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xijing Hospital of Air Force Military Medical University (approval number: KY20172030-1) and was conducted by the Declaration of Helsinki Principles.

Epidermal thickness measurement

Skin biopsy samples were obtained from both lesional and non-lesional skin of the same site in patients with vitiligo to measure epidermal thickness using hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining. The selection criteria for non-lesional skin were areas of normal-appearing skin surrounding the lesions, the normal number of melanocytes, and no lymphocytic infiltration. The HE-stained histopathological sections were scanned using a NANOZOOMER digital slide scanner. The NDP software was utilized to identify the lesional and non-lesional skins of vitiligo in the sections. Subsequently, the thickness of the stratum corneum (from the top of the stratum granulosum or stratum lucidum to the top of the stratum corneum), the viable epidermis (from the top of the stratum granulosum or stratum lucidum to the bottom of the stratum basale), and full epidermis (from the bottom of the stratum basale to the top of the stratum corneum) was measured using ImageJ software. Five different areas of both lesional and non-lesional areas were measured, and the average values were calculated. The measurement of the skin thickness was performed by three specialized dermatologists, and the average of the measurements of the three dermatologists represented the skin thickness.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data were expressed as frequencies or percentages. Numerical data that were normally distributed were shown as mean ± standard deviation. t-tests were used to compare normally distributed numerical data between two groups. An ANOVA was used to compare normally distributed numerical data between the three groups. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference. Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 software.

Results

A total of 191 patients with vitiligo were included in the study, including 83 males and 108 females. The mean age of the patients was 28.42 ± 18.43 years, and the mean disease duration was 39.79 ± 58.54 months. The most common biopsy sites were the trunk (42.91%), the extremities (32.39%), and the head and neck (24.7%). 83 lesions (43.32%) were in sun-exposed areas. Following the Vitiligo Global Issues Consensus Conference [6], patients were categorized into two groups based on disease progression: 156 with active vitiligo and 35 with stable vitiligo; and into four groups based on disease subtype: 111 with non-segmental vitiligo, 71 with segmental vitiligo, 8 with mixed vitiligo, and 1 with unclassified vitiligo. The thickness of the stratum corneum, viable epidermis, and full epidermis at the lesional skins was significantly higher than at the adjacent non-lesional skins in vitiligo (P < 0.05, Table 2). A pair of typical cases is presented in Fig. 1. Further analysis of lesions at different body sites revealed that the thickness of the stratum corneum at the face, neck, and trunk was lower than that at the extremities, and the full epidermis thickness at the trunk was also lower than that at the extremities (P < 0.05, Table 3). No statistically significant differences were observed in the thickness of the stratum corneum, viable epidermis, or full epidermis at other body sites (P > 0.05, Table 3). Additionally, the thickness of the stratum corneum in sun-exposed areas was substantially higher than that in sun-protected areas; however, the thickness of the viable epidermis in sun-exposed areas was lower than that in sun-protected areas (P < 0.05, Table 4). There was no statistically significant difference in the full epidermis thickness between these two groups (P > 0.05, Table 4). Furthermore, no significant differences were found in the thickness of the stratum corneum, viable epidermis, or full epidermis between patients with active vitiligo and those with stable vitiligo (P > 0.05, Table 5). Similarly, no significant differences were observed in the thickness of the stratum corneum, viable epidermis, or full epidermis between patients with segmental and non-segmental vitiligo (P > 0.05, Table 6).

Discussion

Our study found that compared to the epidermis of adjacent non-lesional skins, the stratum corneum, viable epidermis, and full epidermis in the lesional skins of patients with vitiligo were all significantly thickened. This is consistent with the findings of Jung et al. and Gniadecka et al. [7, 8]. Our results showed that, compared to sun-protected areas, the thickness of the stratum corneum in sun-exposed areas was greatly increased, while the thickness of the viable epidermis was reduced, with no significant difference in the thickness of the full epidermis. Ultraviolet (UV) irradiation can stimulate the proliferation of keratinocytes by damaging DNA and generating reactive oxygen species, leading to the thickening of the stratum corneum [3, 9, 10]. The increased keratinocytes secrete large amounts of chemokines such as CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL16, which recruit and activate melanocyte-specific CD8+T cells to kill melanocytes [11, 12]. As melanocyte destruction progresses, the subsequent activation of immune cells and the release of inflammatory mediators may further stimulate keratinocyte proliferation, creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates the progression of vitiligo [13]. This may partially explain why vitiligo is more likely to occur in sun-exposed areas. However, there remain different perspectives. Jung et al. suggested that the epidermis thickening in vitiligo might represent a photoprotective response, compensating for the reduction of melanin due to melanocyte deficiency [7].

The stratum corneum of the skin is the main barrier to transdermal absorption of topical drugs. The thicker the stratum corneum, the less efficient the absorption of topical drugs [14]. Clinically, it has been observed that the treatment effect of topical drugs on thickened lesions in psoriasis patients is poor, with the key reason being the abnormally proliferated stratum corneum that reduces the absorption efficiency of topical drugs [15]. We found that the thickness of the stratum corneum in the extremities of patients with vitiligo was significantly higher than that in the head, neck, and trunk. This may partially explain the reason why vitiligo in the head and neck responds well to treatment, while vitiligo in the extremities is more challenging to manage [16]. Studies have shown that dermabrasion can improve the therapeutic effect of locally applied calcineurin inhibitors, which may be because dermabrasion reduces the thickness of the stratum corneum, thereby promoting transdermal absorption of topical drugs [17, 18]. Phototherapy is a first-line treatment for vitiligo, and the dose of phototherapy is an important factor in determining the treatment effect of vitiligo. The consensus in the management of vitiligo recommends using the minimal erythema dose (MED) as the starting dose for phototherapy [19]. Previous studies have confirmed that thicker lesions have a larger MED [20]. However, in clinical practice, phototherapy is usually started with a fixed dose based on experience, which leads to thicker lesions receiving a lower effective dose of phototherapy, resulting in poorer therapeutic effects in areas with thicker stratum corneum, such as the extremities, compared to areas with thinner stratum corneum such as the trunk. Studies have shown that decreasing the thickness of the stratum corneum by specific interventions such as dermabrasion could improve the penetration of UV radiation, leading to an increase in dermal exposure and stimulation of melanocyte stem cell differentiation, which significantly promotes the repigmentation of white patches in the extremities [21]. Therefore, for areas with a thicker stratum corneum, increasing the dose of phototherapy and combining it with dermabrasion may help improve the effectiveness of phototherapy.

Our study found that the subtype and progression of vitiligo do not affect changes in epidermal thickness. Since skin histopathological examination is only performed in patients with diagnostic difficulties in clinical practice, our analysis included these patients, which inevitably introduces a selection bias to a certain extent. The results may not be universally applicable. Future studies should be specifically designed to include patients with different clinical characteristics for epidermal thickness analysis to clarify the relationship between changes in epidermal thickness and clinical features.

In summary, our study found that patients with vitiligo have significant epidermal thickening at lesions, especially in the extremities and sun-exposed areas. The thickened epidermis may mediate an abnormal localized immune microenvironment that promotes the development of vitiligo, as well as diminish the efficacy of phototherapy and pharmacotherapy in patients with vitiligo. Interventions to reduce epidermal thickness may be beneficial in the treatment of vitiligo.
