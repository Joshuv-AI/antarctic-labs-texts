The impact of powered circular staplers on anastomotic leak in left-sided colorectal cancer surgeries
Authors: Hayoung Lee, Yong Sik Yoon, Young Il Kim, Eun Jung Park, Min Hyun Kim, Jong Lyul Lee, Chan Wook Kim, In Ja Park, Seok-Byung Lim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSince the introduction of powered circular staplers in colorectal surgery, there has been growing interest in their impact on reducing complications, particularly anastomotic leakage. This study compared short-term postoperative outcomes between powered and manual circular staplers.MethodsThis retrospective study included colorectal cancer patients at the tertiary referral center from April to October 2023 who underwent anterior or low anterior resection (LAR) using a circular stapler. According to energy source, patients were divided into powered and manual groups, which used two powered and four types of manual staplers, respectively. All open, laparoscopic, and robotic approaches were included. Propensity score matching (PSM) analysis was used to reduce selection bias. Postoperative complications within 30 days, especially for anastomosis-related complications, were compared between the groups.ResultsAmong 511 patients, the powered group was 161 (32%). After PSM, 143 pairs of 286 patients were analyzed. The proportions of LAR were 53.8% and 51.0%, and initial diverting stoma rates were 23.1% and 22.4% for the Powered and Manual groups, respectively. Comprehensive complication rates were similar between the Powered group and the Manual group, without statistical significance (13.3% vs. 21.0%, P = 0.063). Anastomotic leakage was not different between the Powered and Manual groups (4.2% vs. 4.9%, P = 0.782). There was no significant difference in other complications, including anastomotic bleeding, ileus, surgical site infection, and intra-abdominal hematoma.ConclusionsThe study implies that powered circular staplers may not significantly reduce postoperative complications, including anastomotic leakages, compared to manual staplers in colorectal surgery of high-volume centers.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00464-024-11215-w.

In patients with left-sided colon and rectal cancer, circular staplers have been utilized since the 1990s, becoming the standard treatment for stapling. However, manual circular staplers, which rely on the surgeon's hand strength for firing, are associated with discomfort, especially for surgeons with smaller hands, and ongoing errors. This inevitable inconvenience induced the development of novel-powered instruments. According to one ex-vivo study, it was reported that there was a 37% decrease in the movement of the distal tip in powered circular staplers compared to the conventional manual staplers [1].

Given the focus on alleviating discomfort, there is increasing interest in the impact of these staplers on reducing complications, particularly anastomotic leakage in left-sided colorectal surgery. Anastomotic leakage can lead to significant patient and physician stress, resulting in various costs ranging from prolonged hospital stays to fatal sepsis [2]. While leakage rates vary, they can be as high as 20%, arising from multifactorial causes including technical, personal, and physician skills [3, 4].

Surgeons have pursued various methods to reduce anastomotic leakage rates due to its multifactorial nature, which stems from technical, personal, and physician skill factors [2]. These methods include tension-free anastomosis techniques such as splenic flexure mobilization, assessing blood supply using fluoroscopy post-anastomosis, discontinuing steroids for patients to improve outcomes, and change in anastomotic methods [5–7]. Circular staplers are recognized as one of the technical factors influencing anastomotic results. The introduction of powered staplers was an effort to enhance anastomotic integrity by addressing these needs.

Our institution has utilized two types of powered circular staplers since April 2023. While several retrospective studies have explored the usefulness of powered staplers, previous research designs primarily focused on a single kind of stapler. In our study, we conducted a comparative retrospective cohort analysis within a single center, comparing two different powered circular staplers to manual circular staplers in left-sided colorectal surgeries performed concurrently. This approach allowed us to minimize variables other than the type of stapling instrument used. We aimed to compare the Powered and Manual stapler groups’ clinical short-term outcomes, including anastomotic leakage rates.

Materials and methods

Patients selection

Patient data from left-sided colorectal cancer surgeries performed at Asan Medical Center from April to October 2023 were retrospectively collected through hospital database. Inclusion criteria encompassed patients undergoing anterior resection (AR) or low anterior resection (LAR), in which circular staplers were used, with or without stoma formation, during this period, and aged over 20 at the time of surgery. All procedures were conducted by seven colorectal specialists who had completed the learning curve in the colorectal department. Exclusion criteria comprised patients who underwent hand-sewn anastomosis such as coloanal anastomosis, operations for benign diseases, or other cancer types, including neuroendocrine tumors. The medical records of the study cohorts were thoroughly reviewed. The study proposal received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the Asan Medical Center (registration number: 2023–1634) and adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Additionally, it followed the reporting guidelines outlined in the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement [8] and modified guidelines for propensity score analysis[9].

Study groups and variables

We evaluated two types of powered staplers and four types of manual staplers, each selected based on the preferences of seven expert colorectal surgeons. In the Powered group, we utilized the ECHELON CIRCULAR™ Powered Stapler (ECP, Ethicon Inc., Cincinnati, OH, USA), which has been in use since April 2023 in our institution. Additionally, in August of the same year, we started utilizing the IntoCare™ Powered Stapling Platform (ICS, IntoCare Co., Suzhou, China), another type of powered circular stapler. The Manual group comprised four different staplers: the Chex™ Circular Stapler (CS, Frankenman International Ltd, Hong Kong, China), PROXIMATE® Curved Intraluminal Stapler (CDH, Ethicon Inc., Cincinnati, OH, USA), Premium Plus CEEA AutoSuture (CEEA, Covidien, Mansfield, MA, USA), and EEA™ Circular Stapler with DST Series™ Technology (DST, Covidien Inc., Mansfield, MA, USA). For comparison in this study between the Powered and Manual stapler groups, we categorized ECP and ICS as powered staplers and CS, CDH, CEEA, and DST as manual staplers.

All patients underwent routine mechanical bowel prep (MBP) the day before surgery, except in cases of bowel obstruction or preoperative bowel perforation, where MBP was omitted. MBP consisted of two bottles of picosulfate solution (170 mL each) with 1 to 2 L of water. On the day of surgery, patients received 2 g of cefoxitin before incision, with antibiotics discontinued within 24 h after the initial dose. Data collection included baseline characteristics, clinical features, and 30-day surgical outcomes.

Recorded variables included demographic characteristics (age at operation, sex, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, Body Mass Index (BMI), and comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes), preoperative obstruction, surgical approach (open, laparoscopic, or robotic), combined operations with other departments, and additional factors related to leakage (smoking status, preoperative chemoradiation therapy status, and presence of preoperative obstruction). Preoperative obstruction included cases of endoscopic partial obstruction impeding pediatric scope passage or total clinical obstruction. Combined operations referred to procedures involving departments other than Colon and Rectal Surgery. Smoking status was classified into three categories: never smokers, current smokers (those smoking at the time of surgery;), and past smokers (individuals who had quit smoking for at least two months before surgery, as recommended).

Surgical outcomes and complications

Surgical outcomes were recorded and compared by stapler types: location of anastomosis (AR or LAR), operation duration in minutes, postoperative hospital stay days, stoma diversion, and time to first flatus. Short-term surgical complications including leakage, bleeding, ileus, surgical site infection (SSI), and intra-abdominal hematoma within 30 days were also collected for comparisons.

The primary endpoint of this study was anastomotic leakage, defined and graded according to recommendations from the international study group on rectal cancer, indicating communication between intraluminal and extraluminal compartments [10]. Diagnosis was confirmed through changes in drainage, radiography, or intraoperative measures. In cases where patients exhibited symptoms such as fever, abdominal pain, leukocytosis, or altered drainage, postoperative CT or other radiographic methods were employed to detect anastomotic leakage. In cases where vital signs were unstable, surgical exploration was prioritized over radiographic evaluation. Anastomotic leakage diagnosis for patients after hospital discharge was confirmed if they presented with symptoms that led to radiologic tests showing evidence of a leak. Additionally, given that many patients undergo routine abdominopelvic CT follow-ups for oncologic reasons, even without symptoms, evidence of an anastomotic defect and communication found within the 30-day postoperative period was defined as leakage. Furthermore, our institution routinely performs a colon study using contrast and a CT scan before stoma take-down following adjuvant therapy, if applicable. However, leaks detected during these tests were considered only if they occurred within the 30-day postoperative period because this study specifically focuses on "short-term outcomes," and the potential influence of adjuvant cancer treatment cannot be disregarded. For patients with stoma diversion, the diagnosis of leakage within 60 days was also categorized as a short-term complication, attributed to the protective effect of the stoma. Data were retrospectively collected from medical records, including outpatient clinic visits and emergency department admissions.

Secondary endpoints included other postoperative complications within 30 days. Anastomotic bleeding refers to postoperative hematochezia necessitating transfusion or in-hospital interventions. Ileus was defined as prolonged diet restriction exceeding two days or cases requiring Levin tube insertion. SSI classification followed the diagnostic criteria outlined in the National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) guidelines from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), revised in January 2024 [11]. Reoperations and readmissions within 30 days postoperatively were recorded for any reason. Additionally, all complications were categorized according to the Clavien-Dindo classification system to assess severity [12].

Statistical analysis

For comparison between the two groups and each of the six staplers, the student’s t-test was employed for continuous variables, while the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was utilized for categorical variables. Propensity score matching (PSM) was conducted to address potential confounding factors and selection bias. To calculate the propensity score, we performed a logistic regression analysis using preoperative variables that could affect surgical outcomes and complications. These variables included age, sex, BMI, ASA score, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, tumor location, operation approach, presence of preoperative chemoradiation therapy (PCRT), smoking status, and preoperative obstruction. The patients were matched in a 1:1 ratio using the nearest-neighbor method with a caliper width of 0.2 based on their individual propensity scores without replacement, ensuring that matches were within a narrow range of propensity scores to enhance comparability. Standardized mean differences (SMD) below 0.1 were considered indicative of achieving good balance between the matched groups. Post-matching, we used the McNemar test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test to compare surgical outcomes between the matched groups. To compare postoperative complications, generalized estimating equations with logit link were used for binary outcomes such as leakage, bleeding, ileus, SSI, and intra-abdominal hematoma within 30 days. For categorical outcomes, such as the Clavien-Dindo classification of surgical complications, generalized estimating equations with cumulative logit link were used to fit the proportional odds model. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), particularly for PSM analysis using the MatchIt package®, and SPSS software (version 21.0; IBM Statistics, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Demographics

A total of 554 patients underwent either anterior or low anterior resection between April 1 and October 31, 2023. Exclusions from the study included 22 patients who underwent manual suturing without the use of surgical staplers, 18 patients with benign diseases, and 3 patients diagnosed with neuroendocrine tumors, according to the predefined exclusion criteria (Fig. 1). In a cohort of 511 patients who underwent AR or LAR, 161 utilized powered staplers, while 350 utilized traditional manual staplers. The six types of staplers were composed as follows: ECP was used by 99 patients, ICS by 62, CS by 132, CDH by 13, EEA by 182, and DST by 23. The male percentage was 65.8% in the Powered group and 60.3% in the Manual group, with rectal tumor locations accounting for 55.3% and 56.3%, respectively. PCRT was performed in 9.3% and 5.8% of the Powered and Manual groups, respectively, with no statistically significant differences observed (Table 1). Other clinical characteristics, including BMI, ASA score grouping (1–2 and 3–5), presence of hypertension and diabetes mellitus, smoking status, and preoperative obstruction, did not exhibit significant differences either. However, the Powered stapler group demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of robotic surgeries (46.0% vs. 17.4% in the Powered and Manual groups, respectively) and a lower proportion of laparoscopic approaches (50.9% vs. 78.6% in the Powered and Manual groups, respectively). Operative results

After PSM, 143 pairs were included in the analysis. The proportions of LAR were 53.8% and 51.0%, and initial diverting stoma rates were 23.1% and 22.4% for the Powered and Manual groups, respectively, without statistical significance observed (Table 2). The Powered group exhibited a longer median operation time of 120.0 min compared to 106 min in the Manual group (P = 0.044). However, there were no significant differences in stoma diversion or combined operation rates after PSM. Matched groups also showed no statistical differences in the time needed to pass flatus, with a median of 2 days (IQR 1.0, 2.0) in both groups (P = 0.540). Postoperative complications

After matching, the analysis of postoperative complications indicated that the complication rates were 13.3% for the Powered group and 21.0% for the Manual group. However, this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.063). The reoperation rates within 30 days were 4.9% for the Powered group and 3.5% for the Manual group (P = 0.768), while the readmission rates were 1.4% for the Powered group and 4.9% for the Manual group (P = 0.175), with no statistical significance in either case. When classified by the Clavien-Dindo scale, there was no significant difference between the groups: 14% vs. 8.4% for classes I–II, and 7% vs. 5.6% for classes III–IV (P = 0.266).

When comparing each complication, specifically leakage, the primary endpoint of this study, there were six cases (4.2%) in the Powered group and seven cases (4.9%) in the Manual group, showing no significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.782). Similarly, the incidence of anastomotic bleeding was comparable between the Powered and Manual groups, with two cases (1.4%) and one case (0.7%), respectively (P = 0.571). The incidence of ileus was 4.9% in the Powered group and 9.1% in the Manual group. However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.162). The surgical site infection rates were 8.4% in the Powered group and 11.9% in the Manual group, with no significant difference observed (P = 0.355). The incidence of intra-abdominal hematoma was similar between the two groups, with rates of 1.4% in the Powered group and 0.7% in the Manual group (P = 0.571) (Table 3). Additionally, when short-term postoperative complications were compared (Supplementary Table 1), across all six stapler types before PSM according to source of energy, the incidence of overall complications ranged from 16.1 to 23.1%, with no statistically significant differences observed (P = 0.792). Similarly, there were no significant differences in anastomotic-related complications among the stapler groups, including leakage (P = 0.835) and anastomosis bleeding (P = 0.617). When the severity of complications was compared, the Clavien-Dindo classification also showed no significant differences across the stapler types (P = 0.921).

Discussion

After PSM, the overall complication rates between the groups were similar and did not show a statistically significant difference in this study. Specifically, no significant differences were observed in anastomotic leakage rates between the Powered and Manual groups. Vignali et al. similarly reported no significant decrease in anastomotic leakage rates with powered circular staplers in laparoscopic colorectal resection and rectal anastomosis. However, they suggested a possible benefit in anastomotic bleeding rates, which was not evident in our results[13]. Nanishi et al. also found no statistically significant differences in anastomotic leakage rates or anastomotic bleeding rates despite emphasizing the benefits of powered staplers in increasing negative air leakage test rates [14].

Conversely, many studies have shown the beneficial effects of powered staplers on anastomotic leakage rates. Sylla et al. reported a significant reduction in postoperative complications in their single-arm study of 165 pairs matched with a historical cohort, with anastomotic leakage rates dropping to 1.8% in the powered group compared to 6.9% in the historical cohort (P < 0.001). Additionally, the postoperative ileus rate was 4.8% for ECP patients and 14.7% for manual patients (P < 0.001) [15]. Shibutani et al. also noted significantly lower leakage rates in the powered group (n = 68) compared to the conventional manual stapler group (n = 119) (4.4% vs. 14.3%, respectively, P = 0.048) [16]. Other studies reported similar results in lower leakage rates with even dramatic economic benefits of the powered groups with shorter hospital stays and fewer readmission rates although the powered stapler machine usually costs more [17–19].

One suggested reason for this beneficial effect is minimizing unnecessary movement thanks to stable force transmission. In traditional manual stapling for colorectal anastomoses, a critical stage involves the force required to fully penetrate staples through tissues. This can lead to unintended movements, often caused by the surgeon's uncomfortable position or insufficient grip strength. These factors are thought to contribute, at least in part, to technical errors observed during manual anastomotic procedures, possibly resulting in a high incidence of positive air leak tests or considerable technical error rate [1, 20, 21]. In contrast, novel-powered devices aim to enhance compression and stabilize the stapling head, potentially standardizing anastomotic procedures during surgery and improving outcomes regarding anastomotic integrity [22, 23]. For example, as mentioned above, Nanishi et al. reported a significant increase in negative air leak test with a powered circular stapler in their multivariate analysis (Odds ratio 6.74, confidence interval 1.35–33.56), P = 0.020) [14]. A potential reason for the reduced bleeding at the anastomotic site may lie in the uniform distribution of compression across the anastomosis and the absence of unnecessary traumatic movements, which could otherwise cause microvascular damage [20].

However, our study did not demonstrate a significant difference in overall postoperative complications. It is important to note that differences between staplers are multifactorial, extending beyond the powered or manual distinction. Staplers from different manufacturers often have unique shapes and operating mechanisms. Especially when the colon wall has been weakened due to colorectal cancer obstruction or preoperative chemoradiation therapy, the bowel wall can be edematous and fragile [24]. Son et al. reported a possible increase in compression injury in wet conditions and fixed type of stapler in their in-vitro study [25]. Their results suggest that over-compression might be induced in fixed-type staplers such as ICS, EEA, and DST in our study while current study showed no differences in complication rates across the stapler types. On the other hand, the size of the stapler or the number of stapler fires can impact anastomotic outcomes [26–28]. Overall, while the type of stapler used may partially assist in securing the anastomotic site, relying solely on one characteristic of the stapler, such as whether it is powered or not, to determine and judge surgical outcomes could be considered a hasty decision.

Moreover, among all the complications, anastomotic leakage is a complex occurrence influenced by various factors. Addressing it effectively requires considering multiple contributing factors rather than focusing on one aspect alone. Research on anastomotic leakage has identified more than dozens of factors associated with it, with traditional risk factors including male sex, low level of anastomosis, and age. Most studies emphasize the holistic nature of these factors, from patient characteristics to surgical techniques and hospital factors, in efforts to prevent leakages in various ways [29–31]. Sparreboom et al. introduced an integrated approach to prevent leakage through etiological aspects, communication, infection, and healing disturbances while Mcdermott et al. divided the risk factors according to preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative periods [32, 33]. While previously published studies on powered staplers have addressed multiple factors related to leakage, some factors such as smoking and PCRT status, were not consistently considered. Despite our efforts to incorporate various factors related to leakage, our study could not cover all aspects comprehensively.

Nevertheless, our single-center retrospective analysis revealed a similar rate of anastomotic leakage across powered or manual stapler types, contrasting with studies reporting a significant decrease in leakage rates with powered staplers [15–17]. Furthermore, our study involving six staplers did not yield statistical differences in postoperative complications. This might suggest an integrated stability in our surgical procedures setting despite the type of stapler used. It is also essential to highlight that our center is a high-volume tertiary center with extensive experience in performing over 1,000 rectal cancer surgeries annually for over 20 years. Therefore, given the variations observed in surgical outcomes as outlined above, it would be precarious to attribute the reduction in leakages solely to the stapling technique. The impact of powered staplers on surgeries conducted in an integrated center may not be as significant, as indicated by our findings.

In the present study, the Powered group showed longer operation time compared to the Manual group (120 min vs. 106 min, P = 0.044). Although Factors typically influencing operation time, such as operation approach, BMI, tumor location, and PCRT, were considered through PSM[34], confounding factors such as individual surgeon or tumor stage which we could not include in PSM might influence the different operation time of two groups. Another possible explanation for the longer operation time of the Powered group might be the time required to adapt to new powered staplers.

Furthermore, despite our study comprising a few hundred cases and yielding contrasting results compared to many other studies, it is noteworthy that in other investigations showcasing the favorable outcomes of powered staplers, the powered group typically encompassed nearly one hundred cases similar to ours. For instance, Bai et al. conducted a single-arm study with 168 cases in the ECP cohort, matched with a historical manual cohort while another study featured 218 cases in the manual group and 61 in the powered group within a double-arm study.[17, 18]. Similarly, Matsuhashi et al. examined 139 cases in the manual group and 99 in the ECP group [19]. Given our study's well-matched sample size of 143 pairs, we possess comparable statistical strength to report differing outcomes. However, given the retrospective nature of our study, we did not conduct a power calculation for sample size determination. While larger randomized trials are considered ideal, practical difficulties in enrollment and longer study periods can arise. Although the relatively small sample size can be a limitation of our study, the use of PSM was a notable strength. This method mitigated weaknesses in power by accounting for variations in surgical techniques and differences in the timing of operations, as different stapler types were used at the same center within a short period. Nevertheless, there remains a crucial need for detailed and comprehensive future randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes to conclusively address these issues.

The current study has several limitations that may introduce specific selection biases, primarily due to its retrospective design and lack of randomization, despite employing PSM to balance the two cohorts. While including a broad range of variables that could impact anastomotic leak rates, such as neoadjuvant treatment, smoking status, and differences in surgical approaches, enhances the robustness of our findings, certain variables, such as hypoalbuminemia and anemia, were not included in this study. At our institution, these conditions are managed through established protocols for elective surgeries, ensuring hemoglobin levels above 10 g/dL and albumin levels above 3.0 g/dL before the operation day, which helps minimize potential bias from these factors. However, there may still be hidden biases that we could not fully eliminate. Additionally, this study involved the evaluation of two different types of novel-powered staplers alongside four manual staplers. This variability in stapler types could introduce confounding factors due to differences in patient numbers and subsequent operational variations. However, the inclusion of various stapler types also prompts consideration of potential biases regarding the efficacy of specific powered staplers or products in reducing complications.

Conclusions

The study suggests that adopting powered circular staplers may not substantially reduce leakage rates or overall complications compared to manual staplers in colorectal surgery. Our institution consistently observed similar leakage rates regardless of stapler types, highlighting the importance of a holistic approach to secure anastomosis. This emphasizes the need to consider multiple factors beyond the type of a surgical stapler when assessing postoperative outcomes in colorectal surgery.
