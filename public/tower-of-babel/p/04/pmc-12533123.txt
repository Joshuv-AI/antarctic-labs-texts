Spatial modelling of groundwater potential zones in the Neyyar Basin using machine learning and morphometric analysis
Authors: R J Jerin Joe, V Stephen Pitchaimani, S Kaliraj, S Richard Abishek, A Antony Alosanai Promilton, Shankar Karuppannan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study employs morphometric analysis integrated with machine learning (Random Forest) to delineate groundwater potential zones (GWPZs) in the Neyyar Basin. A total of 60 morphometric parameters, categorized under linear, areal, relief, and drainage texture aspects, were extracted using SRTM DEM (30 m resolution) and analyzed to assess their influence on groundwater occurrence. Thematic layers such as geology, geomorphology, drainage density, lineament density, land use/land cover, soil type, slope, curvature, and rainfall were integrated with CGWB well yield and static water level data to train the Random Forest model. The results classified the basin into high (43.27%), moderate (21.78%), and low (34.96%) groundwater potential zones. High potential zones were observed in valleys, lower slopes, and fractured rock regions, where favorable infiltration and storage conditions enhance groundwater availability. Low potential zones were concentrated in steep terrains with high drainage density and low permeability, restricting recharge. The study confirms that morphometric parameters such as drainage density, bifurcation ratio, and relief characteristics are key indicators of groundwater occurrence, influencing infiltration, runoff, and storage capacity. By integrating detailed morphometric analysis with machine learning, this study enhances the accuracy of groundwater potential mapping, offering a robust approach for sustainable water resource management.Keywords: Morphometric analysis, Groundwater potential zones (GWPZs), Random forest model, Remote sensing and GIS, Neyyar BasinSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental impact, Geomorphology

Introduction

Morphometric analysis is a fundamental approach in geomorphology and hydrology that helps understand a drainage basin’s physical characteristics and influence on groundwater dynamics1. The drainage network of a basin plays a crucial role in controlling surface runoff, infiltration, and groundwater recharge2. Analyzing various morphometric parameters, such as drainage density, bifurcation ratio, stream frequency, and relief aspects, the hydrological behavior of a region and its potential for groundwater storage can be assessed3,4. These parameters offer quantitative insights into basin geometry, network patterns, and topographic features that govern subsurface water movement5. The morphometric analysis provides a basis for identifying groundwater-prone zones as the terrain morphology directly influences water movement and storage6,7. Regions with well-developed drainage systems, high bifurcation ratios, and low drainage density often indicate higher infiltration potential, making them suitable for groundwater recharge8. Meanwhile, areas with high drainage density and steep slopes tend to have rapid runoff, limiting groundwater availability9. Thus, integrating morphometric parameters with modern geospatial techniques and machine learning approaches enhances the accuracy and reliability of groundwater potential assessments.

Traditional approaches to groundwater potential zoning have relied on field investigations and hydrogeological surveys, which, although reliable, are often time-consuming, labor-intensive, and costly10. There is a growing need for advanced approaches such as remote sensing, geospatial techniques, and machine learning for efficient and accurate groundwater assessment. The application of machine learning (ML) models in groundwater potential zoning (GWPZ) has gained significant attention due to their ability to analyze complex spatial patterns and improve predictive accuracy11. Various algorithms, including logistic regression (LR), support vector machines (SVM), artificial neural networks (ANN), decision trees (DT), and ensemble models like Random Forest (RF) and Gradient Boosting (GB), have been employed to classify groundwater potential based on multiple hydrogeological and environmental parameters12–16. These models utilize well-distributed groundwater observation points for training, allowing them to establish nonlinear relationships between thematic layers and groundwater occurrence. Ensemble methods like RF have proven particularly effective by combining multiple decision trees to reduce variance and improve model stability while also providing insights into variable importance.

Horton17, was the first to establish the laws of drainage composition, introducing fundamental concepts such as stream order, bifurcation ratio, drainage density, and length of overland flow, which describe the organization and efficiency of a drainage network. Schumm18, expanded on Horton’s work by analyzing the relationship between drainage basin characteristics and geomorphic processes, defining parameters such as ruggedness number and texture ratio to assess basin morphology and their influence on infiltration and runoff processes. Strahler19, further refined Horton’s stream ordering system, introducing the widely used hierarchical stream classification method, which is essential for hydrological modeling and serves as the backbone for modern quantitative watershed analysis. Miller20 introduced the concept of ruggedness number, which quantifies the interaction between relief and drainage density, providing insights into the erosional potential of a basin and its implications for groundwater recharge. Melton21, contributed by defining relief ratio and relative relief, which help in assessing basin slope characteristics and their influence on water movement and storage. These early contributions established a strong theoretical framework for morphometric analysis, enabling its application in groundwater studies and watershed management.

Swarnakar & Channabasappa22, conducted a morphometric assessment of the Bhima Lower sub-basin using CartoDEM and GIS, highlighting the influence of Deccan basalt lithology on drainage characteristics. The study revealed that lower drainage density and stream frequency indicate high infiltration, whereas higher bifurcation ratios in certain watersheds suggest a higher risk of flooding. Kaliraj et al.,23 analyzed the Thamirabarani sub-basin in Kanyakumari District, concluding that the hypsometric integral and denudation rates indicate active erosional processes, which shape the basin’s evolving landscape and hydrological response. The results indicated that terrain characteristics significantly influence groundwater distribution, with the Frequency Ratio model demonstrating the highest predictive accuracy for groundwater resource management. The Dhodana watershed in central India, part of the Deccan Volcanic Province, exhibits a complex multi-layered aquifer system due to alternating hard massive and vesicular basalt flows24. Morphometric analysis, along with thematic layers such as lithology, slope, and lineaments, revealed that groundwater potential is highest in storage zones near drainage networks, while runoff zones exhibit lower recharge capacity. Groundwater potential mapping in the Subarnarekha River basin, India, using a hybrid FuzzyAHP, FuzzyDEMATEL, and Logistic Regression approach revealed that 22% of the area has very high groundwater potential, primarily in downstream alluvial regions25. In contrast, the central basin exhibited severe groundwater scarcity, emphasizing the role of morphometric and hydrologic factors in recharge variability. Rai et al., 26 analyzed the lower Kosi River Basin using ASTER DEM and GIS, highlighting the role of morphometric parameters in assessing flood susceptibility and groundwater recharge potential. Their findings emphasized that integrating morphometry with hydrological modeling enhances water resource management and flood mitigation strategies. Konduet al.,27, applied machine learning algorithms to delineate groundwater potential zones in the Rupnarayan Basin, West Bengal, using the LSM Tool Pack integrated with ArcGIS Pro. Their study identified 13 significant hydrogeological factors, with Random Forest achieving the highest prediction accuracy (92%) compared to other models such as Support Vector Machine and exTreme gradient boost.

Khan & Jhamnani28, utilized machine learning models to map groundwater potential zones in Kerala’s Idukki district, integrating 14 hydrogeological and terrain factors. Their analysis revealed that Random Forest (AUC = 0.92) provided the most reliable predictions, highlighting evenly distributed groundwater potential zones critical for disaster preparedness and sustainable water management. A study on coastal groundwater potential mapping utilized Remote Sensing, GIS, and the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) to assess nine thematic factors, with drainage density and geomorphology identified as key influencers 29. The weighted overlay method accurately classified groundwater potential zones, highlighting the significance of systematic decision-making for sustainable coastal water management. Using SRTM DEM and geospatial techniques, Suhara & Haghi30, analyzed the Nileswar sub-watershed in Kasaragod district, Kerala, assessing morphometric parameters to prioritize micro-watersheds. Their findings highlighted micro-watershed 6 as highly vulnerable, emphasizing the need for immediate soil conservation measures.

A morphometric analysis of the Neyyar sub-watershed utilized GIS, remote sensing, and DEM data to evaluate drainage characteristics, basin geometry, and relief parameters studied by Dinagara et al.,31. The study identified a dendritic drainage pattern with seven stream orders, where higher drainage density in the northern Charnockite-Gneiss terrain decreases towards the coastal plains. A study on sub-watershed prioritization in the Neyyar Basin employed morphometric analysis, Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and hypsometric analysis using remote sensing and GIS. Fifteen morphometric parameters were analyzed across 11 sub-watersheds, with SW1 consistently identified as the highest priority area due to its susceptibility to soil erosion, high runoff, and peak discharge32. A study on the Vazhichal watershed in the Neyyar basin applied morphometric analysis and land use/land cover (LULC) mapping using remote sensing and GIS to prioritize sub-watersheds for conservation33. The results indicated that morphometric analysis identified erosion-prone areas, while LULC analysis highlighted regions under intense land use pressure. A century-long land use/land cover (LULC) change analysis in the Neyyar River Basin (1914–2007) by Sheeja et al.,34 using remote sensing and GIS revealed significant transformations driven by urbanization, agricultural shifts, and deforestation. The study found a sharp decline in paddy fields, scrublands, and evergreen forests, with a corresponding increase in built-up areas, rubber plantations, and dense mixed forests. Badusha & Santhosh35, conducted a water quality assessment of the Neyyar River, analyzing physico-chemical parameters across six sampling sites to evaluate the impact of anthropogenic pollution and saltwater intrusion. Their investigation revealed deteriorating water quality in downstream reaches, attributed to pollutant loading from upstream settlements and seawater intrusion. The study documented reduced alkalinity, seasonal temperature fluctuations corresponding to monsoon patterns, and increased TDS, nitrate, and phosphate levels, highlighting the necessity for implementing effective pollution control measures and integrated watershed management approaches. Krishnakumar et al.,36 assessed heavy metal contamination in the bulk sediments of the Neyyar River across 17 locations during different seasons. Their findings indicated low contamination levels for most metals, except for cadmium (Cd), which showed moderate contamination at certain sites. Principal Component Analysis revealed both geogenic and anthropogenic sources of heavy metals, with Zn and Cd exhibiting minor enrichment, highlighting the impact of industrial discharges, agricultural runoff, and sand mining on sediment quality. The study by Suresh et al.,37 analyzed sediment deposition and reservoir siltation in the Neyyar catchment, revealing a 28.8 Mm3 loss in storage capacity over 38 years, with an annual sediment input of 58 m3/ha and a storage loss of 0.75 Mm3 (0.71%) per year. GIS-based analysis identified 17 km2 of high erosion-prone areas, while forest degradation was linked to reservoir expansion. The study warned that constructing an upper dam could submerge an additional 2.4 km2 of undisturbed forest, further accelerating environmental degradation.

Despite extensive research on the Neyyar Basin, previous studies have primarily focused on watershed prioritization, land use/land cover changes, sedimentation, and water quality assessments. While some morphometric analyses have been conducted, they have been limited in scope, often assessing a restricted set of parameters or focusing on sub-watersheds rather than the entire basin. Additionally, groundwater potential assessments in the region have largely relied on GIS-based multi-criteria decision-making approaches without leveraging advanced machine learning techniques for improved accuracy. This study aims to bridge these gaps by performing a comprehensive morphometric analysis of the entire Neyyar Basin, calculating 60 morphometric parameters encompassing linear, areal, relief, and drainage texture aspects, a significantly more detailed assessment than previous studies. By integrating these parameters with Random Forest machine learning modelling, this research seeks to delineate groundwater potential zones (GWPZ) with higher predictive accuracy than traditional approaches. The study also examines the relationship between morphometric characteristics and groundwater availability, identifying the key factors influencing recharge potential. Ultimately, this research will provide a data-driven approach to groundwater resource management, offering valuable insights for sustainable planning and decision-making in the Neyyar Basin.

Study area

The Neyyar River Basin is located in Thiruvananthapuram, the southernmost district of Kerala, bordered by Kollam to the north, Tirunelveli and Kanniyakumari districts to the east and south, and the Laccadive Sea to the west. Geographically, it extends between longitudes 76°41′ to 77°16′ E and latitudes 8°17′ to 8°47′ N (Fig. 1). The Neyyar River originates from the Agastyarkoodam hills and flows 56 km westward, joined by major tributaries such as Kallar, Mullayar, and Karavaliyar, before reaching its mouth near Poovar38. It serves as a vital water source for Neyyattinkara town and the surrounding areas. The basin features uneven topography, with the Western Ghats forming a rugged eastern boundary39. Geologically, the region consists of Late Cretaceous sedimentary formations, including sandstone, shale, limestone with fossiliferous horizons, and alluvial deposits, supporting agricultural activities40. The basin experiences a tropical climate with temperatures ranging from 24 °C to 33 °C and an annual rainfall of approximately 2800 mm, mostly received during the southwest monsoon (June–September). The dominant soil types include laterite, sandy loam, and clayey soils, which support diverse vegetation, including tropical evergreen and semi-evergreen forests, alongside cultivated plantations of coconut, banana, rubber, and pepper that play a significant role in the local economy41. As one of the three major drainage basins in Thiruvananthapuram, the Neyyar Basin has a population density of 1509 people per square kilometer.

Materials and methods

This study employed SRTM DEM data (1-degree arc resolution) for a comprehensive analysis of morphometric parameters of the Neyyar Basin. The analysis focused on four key aspects of morphometry: linear (15 parameters), areal (21 parameters), drainage texture (7 parameters), and relief (17 parameters)26. The DEM preprocessing was performed using ArcGIS 10.8, where hydrological correction was applied to remove depressions using the Fill tool. The flow direction and flow accumulation were derived using the D8 algorithm. The stream order was classified based on Strahler’s method, and morphometric parameters were extracted using Spatial Analyst and Hydrology toolsets in ArcMap 10.8. Standard morphometric equations were used to calculate drainage density, bifurcation ratio, stream frequency, elongation ratio, and relief aspects. Following the morphometric analysis, significant parameters influencing groundwater potential was identified (Table 1).

Lithological well data from CGWB (Central Ground Water Board), available via the India-WIRS portal, were incorporated into the study. This well data included static water level and discharge values, which were used for training the Random Forest (RF) model. Due to the limited number of lithological well data points, spatial interpolation was performed using Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) in ArcGIS to estimate values at unsampled locations. Extracted groundwater-related points were then used alongside morphometric parameters for further analysis. Thematic layers such as geomorphology, geology, drainage density, lineament density, land use/land cover (LULC), soil type, slope, and rainfall were selected as the inputs for identification of the groundwater potential zone using the Random Forrest ML model. To ensure consistency, these layers were standardized to a uniform spatial resolution (30*30) and projection (WGS 1984 UTM Zone 43). Although Random Forest does not require all input features to be in consistent units or standardized scales, standardization helps in maintaining uniformity across datasets42. Algorithms such as logistic regression and support vector machines (SVM), rely on distance-based calculations and are sensitive to feature scaling43. Random Forest operates by recursively splitting the data based on feature thresholds, making it invariant to differences in units or magnitudes44. The Random Forest (RF) algorithm was implemented using Python’s Scikit-Learn library, with Visual Studio as the development environment. The model was trained using 500 decision trees, each independently constructed using random subsets of input variables and training samples (Table 2). This ensemble learning approach enhances model robustness, minimizes overfitting, and improves generalization by aggregating multiple predictions. Each decision tree in the RF model operates by recursively splitting the dataset based on the most informative feature at each node, optimizing the classification of groundwater potential zones45. The Gini impurity criterion was employed to evaluate the quality of splits, ensuring maximum class purity at each decision node. The Gini index (G) is calculated using Eq. 1.

Results and discussion

The results of this study provide a detailed morphometric characterization of the Neyyar Basin, highlighting the influence of terrain parameters on hydrological processes. The calculated morphometric indices help in understanding the basin’s drainage behavior, surface runoff potential, and infiltration capacity, which are critical for groundwater recharge. The integration of Random Forest machine learning with hydrogeological and environmental factors has allowed for a data-driven classification of groundwater potential zones (GWPZs).

Morphometrical analysis

The morphometric analysis of the Neyyar Basin highlights significant variations in drainage patterns, basin geometry, and terrain characteristics, influencing runoff behavior and groundwater recharge potential. The linear, areal, drainage texture and relief aspects collectively reveal that the basin exhibits moderate to high surface runoff with limited infiltration capacity.

Linear aspects

The linear morphometric parameters provide insights into the drainage network structure, stream characteristics, and the hydrological response of the basin48. The stream order (So) of the Neyyar Basin follows Strahler’s classification, with stream orders ranging from 1’st to 5’th order (Fig. 3). The dominance of 1st and 2nd order streams (315 and 90) suggests a youthful stage of basin evolution, where the predominant Khondalite and Charnockite formations promote surface runoff over infiltration due to their relatively low permeability. The relationship between stream order and stream number follows Horton’s Law, where the number of streams decreases exponentially as stream order increases. This pattern suggests a well-drained and structurally controlled basin, with smaller tributaries contributing to higher-order streams (Fig. 4a). The stream length (Lu) exhibits an irregular pattern across different stream orders, with higher-order streams showing greater cumulative length (Fig. 4b). The total stream length is 522.75 km, indicating a well-drained basin. The stream length ratio (Lur), which represents the proportional relationship between successive stream orders, has a value of 1.0037, suggesting a nearly uniform lithological influence with minimal abrupt geological variations. The mean stream length ratio (Lurm) is 0.8814, and the weighted mean stream length ratio (Luwm) is 0.7714, indicating that stream segments tend to maintain a consistent gradient, contributing to a moderate runoff regime with some infiltration potential.

The bifurcation ratio (Rb), which expresses the degree of branching in the drainage network, ranges from 3.50 to 4.75, with a mean bifurcation ratio (Rbm) of 4.25. This indicates moderate structural control over drainage development consistent with the relatively homogeneous crystalline rocks in the region. A high bifurcation ratio (> 5) usually suggests strong geological control such as faulting, whereas the values in the Neyyar Basin indicate a semi-structurally controlled network with some influence of homogenous lithology. The weighted mean bifurcation ratio (Rbwm) is 3.89, further confirming a partially influenced structural setting, which may lead to moderate susceptibility to flooding in lower-order streams. The main channel length (Cl) is 55.87 km, representing the longest continuous stream in the basin, while the valley length (Vi) is 39.08 km, indicating the extent of fluvial erosion shaping the valley structure. The channel index (Ci) of 1.11 suggests a relatively elongated basin, with a well-defined main channel that efficiently drains the catchment. Similarly, the valley index (Vi) of 0.9 signifies a moderate level of valley development, reflecting a balance between erosional and depositional processes in the basin. The rho coefficient (p), calculated as 0.2072, indicates the storage capacity of the basin and its response to rainfall events. A lower rho coefficient (< 0.5) suggests limited water storage capacity within the basin, which is consistent with the crystalline bedrock geology where formations typically exhibit lower porosity and permeability, resulting in higher surface runoff and reduced groundwater recharge potential. This suggests that the Neyyar Basin has a quick hydrological response to precipitation, with limited water retention in surface formations (Table 3).

Areal aspects

These parameters help assess runoff characteristics, infiltration potential, and basin efficiency in water retention51. The basin length (Lb), measured at 55.87 km, represents the longest dimension of the basin, influencing the time required for runoff to reach the outlet. A mean basin width (Wb) of 8.90 km suggests that the basin is elongated rather than circular, which affects the time of concentration and flood response. The total basin area (A) is 497.30 km2, making it a medium-sized river basin, while the basin perimeter (P) is 137.14 km, reflecting the extent of the basin boundary and its interaction with surrounding landscapes. The relative perimeter (Pr), calculated as 1.735, indicates the degree of basin irregularity. A higher value suggests greater complexity in basin shape, which can influence runoff and erosion processes52. The length-area relation (Lar), with a value of 2.506, provides a measure of the elongation of the basin concerning its area, proving the observation that the Neyyar Basin is moderately elongated. The Lemniscate’s (k) value of 6.278 further confirms the elongated nature of the basin, as higher values indicate greater elongation and lower peak flood discharge.

The form factor ratio (Ff) is 0.1593, signifying a highly elongated basin. A lower form factor indicates a prolonged runoff period, reducing the risk of flash floods but increasing the time for water to reach the main channel. The shape factor ratio (Rs) of 6.278, being the inverse of the form factor, reinforces this observation, suggesting a long, narrow basin with an extended hydrological response time. The elongation ratio (Re), calculated as 0.4504, falls within the semi-elongated category, meaning the basin is less circular and more prone to delayed runoff and moderate infiltration. The ellipticity index (Ie) of 3.139 further supports the basin’s elongated nature, implying that the flow of water is more linear, with reduced chances of stagnation. The texture ratio (Rt) is 2.455, representing the drainage network density relative to the basin length. A moderate texture ratio indicates balanced runoff and infiltration characteristics. The circularity ratio (Re), with a value of 0.3323, confirms that the basin is not circular but rather elongated which may contribute to spreading runoff over a longer duration in the Neyyar Basin. Though the actual flood response is also influenced by the low permeability of the underlying rock formation affecting how water flows through the drainage system. A lower circularity ratio suggests that runoff is spread over a longer time, reducing peak flood potential. The compactness coefficient (Cc) of 1.735 provides additional confirmation of the basin’s shape and runoff efficiency, with values closer to 1 indicating a more compact basin with faster runoff. The fitness ratio (Rf) is 0.4074, showing how efficiently the basin is shaped for water flow. A lower value suggests moderate water retention potential in certain areas. The wandering ratio (Rw), calculated as 1.227, measures the meandering nature of the basin boundary, with higher values suggesting irregular terrain or structural influences. The watershed eccentricity (t), with a value of 6.198, indicates the degree of deviation from a perfect circle, which supports the observation that the Neyyar Basin is elongated with a linear flow direction. The drainage texture (Dt), calculated as 3.128, represents the density of the drainage network per unit perimeter length. The hydraulic sinuosity index (Hsi) and topographic sinuosity index (Tsi), calculated as 1.43, indicate that the main channel exhibits moderate meandering due to topographic variations. A sinuosity index value close to 1.0 suggests a straight river, while values above 1.5 indicate a highly meandering system53. The Neyyar River, with a Standard Sinuosity Index of 1.0, exhibits balanced channel sinuosity, showing that hydraulic and topographic controls influence its course equally. The stream frequency (Sf), calculated as 0.86, represents the number of streams per unit area. A lower stream frequency suggests well-drained terrain with moderate infiltration potential, whereas higher values indicate high surface runoff and low permeability54. The observed stream frequency reflects the basin’s drainage characteristics, where the dominant Lower Plateau (Lateritic) provides moderate drainage conditions, while the Denudational Hills in the highland contribute to higher drainage density due to limited infiltration capacity (Table 4).

Drainage texture analysis

These parameters help determine the erosion potential, groundwater recharge capability, and hydrological efficiency of the basin60. The Neyyar Basin’s moderate stream frequency suggests a stable drainage system with a mix of runoff and infiltration processes. The drainage density (Dd), with a value of 0.97 km/km2, indicates that the basin has a moderately developed drainage network. Lower drainage density (< 1.0 km/km2) suggests higher infiltration capacity and lower surface runoff, while higher values (> 2.0 km/km2) indicate impermeable terrain with high runoff potential61. The Neyyar Basin reflects the varied topographic conditions, where steep slopes (up to 72.24°) in the Western Ghats elevated areas promote rapid runoff and higher drainage development, while the gentler slopes in valley regions and lower basin areas enhance infiltration capacity.

The constant of channel maintenance (C), calculated as 1.03 km2/km, represents the inverse of drainage density and indicates the area required to sustain one unit length of stream channel. A higher value suggests greater infiltration capacity and lower surface runoff, meaning the Neyyar Basin has a good balance between surface flow and groundwater recharge. The drainage intensity (Di), calculated as 0.89, represents the ratio of stream frequency to drainage density and is an indicator of the impact of surface runoff on basin erosion. A lower drainage intensity suggests moderate runoff with lower chances of severe erosion, indicating that the Neyyar Basin is less susceptible to flash flooding or extreme erosional activity. This moderate erosional regime is spatially variable, reflecting the heterogeneous soil distribution across the basin. Areas underlain by Nitisols and Rendzinas, with their enhanced infiltration capacity, experience diminished erosional activity and improved water retention. Conversely, regions dominated by Acrisols exhibit increased erosional vulnerability due to their highly leached characteristics and limited permeability. The infiltration number (If), calculated as 0.83, is the product of stream frequency and drainage density and helps in understanding the infiltration capacity of the basin. A lower infiltration number (< 1.0) suggests moderate groundwater recharge potential, while a higher value (> 2.0) indicates poor infiltration and high surface runoff. The Neyyar Basin’s moderate infiltration number indicates that infiltration is possible in certain regions, particularly in areas with favorable lithology and soil conditions. The drainage pattern (Dp) is classified as dendritic, which is characteristic of homogeneous lithology and minimal structural control. A dendritic drainage pattern typically indicates a well-developed fluvial system, where streams have formed naturally without significant geological obstructions. The length of overland flow (Lg), calculated as 0.515 km, represents the distance water travels over the surface before entering a stream channel. A lower value (< 0.5 km) suggests higher runoff and lower infiltration, while a higher value (> 1.0 km) indicates more time for infiltration before water reaches the drainage network. The Neyyar Basin’s moderate length of overland flow suggests a balanced runoff-infiltration relationship, meaning that groundwater recharge can occur in specific zones, particularly in lower-gradient areas (Table 5).

Relief aspects

A high-relief basin is typically characterized by steep slopes, high runoff, and lower infiltration, while a low-relief basin supports slower runoff and increased water retention64. The height of the Neyyar Basin mouth (z) is 4 m, indicating the lowest outlet elevation where the Neyyar River meets the Arabian Sea, while the maximum basin height (Z) is 1818 m, corresponding to the highest peak in the Agastyarkoodam hills. This results in a total basin relief (H) of 1814 m, which represents the overall vertical drop within the basin. The relief ratio (Rhl), calculated as 32.46 m/km, represents the relative steepness of the basin and indicates the intensity of slope-driven surface runoff. Higher relief ratio values suggest rapid water flow, leading to increased erosion and minimal groundwater recharge. The absolute relief (Ra), which is equal to the maximum elevation (1818 m), confirms the presence of highly elevated terrains that contribute to significant topographic variations. The relative relief ratio (Rhp), computed as 13.23 m/km, provides a measure of the elevation difference relative to the perimeter of the basin. A higher value suggests the basin has a strong vertical gradient, promoting increased surface runoff. The dissection index (Dis), calculated as 0.9978, indicates that the basin is in an advanced stage of dissection. This proves that steep slopes in the highland region and deeply incised valleys in the midland region dominate the landscape. The gradient ratio (Rg), calculated as 32.46, quantifies the basin’s steepness and proves the terrain’s high-energy nature, which results in rapid runoff and increased sediment transport.

The watershed slope (Sw), with a value of 81.39%, indicates a highly sloping terrain, making the Neyyar Basin prone to soil erosion, flash floods, and low water retention, especially in the highland region. The ruggedness number (Rn), calculated as 1755.6, is a function of drainage density and basin relief, providing insights into terrain complexity. A higher ruggedness number (> 1000) indicates a highly dissected terrain, leading to intense erosion and limited groundwater recharge65. The Melton ruggedness number (MRn), calculated as 81.39, further supports this, suggesting that the basin is highly susceptible to landslides and soil loss due to its steep terrain. The total contour length (Ctl) is 1781.85 km, representing the total length of elevation contours mapped within the basin. A 50 m contour interval (Cin) was chosen to ensure detailed terrain classification and accurate slope analysis. The slope analysis (Sa), computed as 3.58, provides a measure of the overall slope steepness within the basin, influencing runoff velocity and soil erosion potential. The average slope (S), calculated as 179.2, confirms that the basin is dominated by steep slopes, further supporting the high-energy nature of the drainage system. The mean slope ratio (Sm), calculated as 0.0987, represents the average gradient of the basin relative to its area, indicating moderate to high runoff potential. The mean slope of the overall basin (0 s), calculated as 57.06, suggests that the majority of the basin consists of steeply sloping terrain, leading to high surface runoff and lower infiltration. The hypsometric curve of the Neyyar Basin indicates a convex-up shape, suggesting that the basin is in its youthful stage of geomorphic evolution. This implies active erosion, high relief, and limited sediment deposition, influencing surface runoff and groundwater recharge (Fig. 5). Geomorphology

The geomorphological features of the Neyyar Basin significantly influence groundwater recharge and surface runoff dynamics (Fig. 6). The Lower Plateau (Lateritic), covering 276.05 km2 (57.94%), forms the dominant landscape, characterized by moderate permeability, allowing partial infiltration and moderate groundwater potential. The Denudational Hills (98.15 km2, 20.60%) in the highland promote high runoff and limited infiltration, making these areas poor for groundwater recharge. Valleys (16.06%), particularly along the river course, serve as key recharge zones due to their low elevation and water-accumulating nature. Floodplains and young coastal plains, though covering a smaller extent, enhance infiltration, making them important for localized groundwater storage. Reservoir islands and water bodies contribute to surface water retention, indirectly influencing recharge in adjacent regions.

Geology (aquifer material)

The aquifer materials in the Neyyar Basin, based on CGWB observations, are found at depths of 20 m or more, significantly influencing groundwater availability and movement (Fig. 7). The Khondalites and Granulites, covering 90.40% of the basin, are hard, compact metamorphic rocks with low primary porosity, restricting groundwater storage. The fractures and weathered zones within these formations act as secondary aquifers, facilitating localized groundwater movement. Laterite and ferruginous concretions (8.19%), present in the lowlands of basin, allow moderate infiltration, but their water-holding capacity is limited. Despite its small extent, coastal alluvium (0.37%) is highly permeable, making it a potential shallow aquifer with good recharge capacity. Sandstone with clay (0.61%) supports moderate groundwater storage, while Migmatitic Gneiss (0.42%) has low permeability, restricting groundwater retention.

Drainage density

Drainage density (Dd) is an important parameter in groundwater potential assessment, as it reflects the extent of surface runoff, infiltration capacity, and subsurface water movement68. In the Neyyar Basin, drainage density values range from 0 km/km2 (no streams) to 7.94 km/km2 (highly dissected areas), indicating significant spatial variability (Fig. 8). High drainage density areas correspond to steep slopes and denudational hills, where impermeable rock formations and high runoff rates limit groundwater infiltration. These regions act as poor groundwater potential zones due to rapid surface water movement and minimal recharge. Low drainage density areas, mainly found in lower plateau regions, exhibit better infiltration potential, allowing for groundwater recharge. As observed in the morphometric analysis, the basin overall has moderate drainage density (0.97 km/km2), suggesting a balanced hydrological system where both runoff and infiltration occur, with recharge zones concentrated in low-relief, low-drainage-density regions.

Lineament density

Lineament density in the Neyyar Basin ranges from 0 to 0.29, with two distinct types: structural (geological) and geomorphic lineaments (Fig. 9). Structural lineaments, mainly joints and fractures, enhance subsurface water movement, making fractured zones potential groundwater conduits69. Geomorphic lineaments, aligned with drainage patterns, influence local infiltration in valleys and depressions. Areas with higher lineament density, especially where both types intersect, exhibit better groundwater potential, while low-density regions, often in steep terrains, restrict groundwater movement. Dense structural lineaments in weathered rock zones further improve groundwater retention, supporting localized aquifers. The regions with sparse lineament density and compact lithology experience higher runoff and minimal recharge potential.

The Random Forest model successfully classified the Neyyar Basin into three distinct groundwater potential zones: high, moderate, and low (Fig. 16). The low potential zones, occupying 174.21 km2 (34.96%), are concentrated in denudational hills and steep-sloped areas, where high runoff and impermeable bedrock restrict infiltration.

Validation of the model

The performance of the Random Forest model in classifying groundwater potential zones was evaluated using the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve. The ROC curve represents the model’s ability to differentiate between groundwater potential classes by plotting the True Positive Rate (TPR) against the False Positive Rate (FPR) at varying classification thresholds72. The Area Under the Curve (AUC) value was determined to be 0.7706, indicating good classification performance (Fig. 17). The standard error of the AUC was 0.0588, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) ranging from 0.6552 to 0.8860, confirming the model’s statistical reliability. The asymptotic probability (p-value) was < 0.0001, demonstrating that the model’s classification ability is significantly better than random chance. These results validate the effectiveness of integrating morphometric parameters with machine learning, confirming that the Random Forest model is reliable for delineating groundwater potential zones in the Neyyar Basin.

Morphometric controls on groundwater potential

The morphometric analysis reveals clear spatial relationships between basin characteristics and groundwater potential distribution in the Neyyar Basin. The observed bifurcation ratio values (3.5–4.0) indicate moderately branched drainage networks. It provides sufficient residence time for surface water infiltration, particularly important in the crystalline bedrock terrain where extended water–rock contact enhances recharge through fractured zones73. The moderate drainage density (0.97 km/km2) reflects an optimal balance for groundwater accumulation, being sufficiently developed for surface drainage while not overly dense to preclude infiltration opportunities. The strong correspondence between topographic parameters and the final GWPZ distribution is evident, where high potential zones align with areas of moderate slopes, lower drainage density, and favorable geological formations in the Lower Plateau regions. Conversely, the steep-sloped highland areas with high drainage density correspond to low potential zones, confirming that morphometric parameters effectively capture the fundamental hydrological processes controlling groundwater availability. The elongated basin shape contributes to extended flow paths that enhance infiltration opportunities in geologically suitable areas, while the relief characteristics delineate zones of rapid runoff versus areas conducive to groundwater recharge.

Limitations

This study offers valuable insights into the identification of groundwater potential zones (GPZ) within the Neyyar Basin, yet certain limitations must be considered. The analysis relies on available well data from CGWB, which, though comprehensive, represents point-specific measurements that were spatially interpolated using IDW to cover the entire basin. The temporal aspect of groundwater dynamics is not fully captured as the study uses static thematic layers representing conditions at specific time periods. Additionally, the Random Forest model’s performance is constrained by the spatial distribution and density of available well data points. Despite these constraints, the methodology provides a robust framework for regional groundwater assessment that can guide water resource management decisions and replicated in similar hydrogeological settings.

Conclusion

The findings demonstrate that morphometric characteristics, geological conditions, and hydrogeological factors collectively influence groundwater availability, and their integration through machine learning has resulted in a spatially refined groundwater potential map. The morphometric analysis revealed that the Neyyar Basin is a high-energy, steep-sloped terrain with strong vertical dissection, leading to high runoff and limited infiltration in elevated regions. The linear aspects confirmed a well-organized dendritic drainage system. The areal aspects highlighted that the basin has an elongated shape, contributing to moderate flood susceptibility but also supporting a well-balanced hydrological response. The drainage texture analysis further confirmed that the basin is moderately drained, with regions of low drainage density and moderate infiltration rates offering potential recharge zones. The groundwater potential analysis using Random Forest classified the basin into high (43.27%), moderate (21.78%), and low (34.96%) potential zones. High groundwater potential zones were primarily located in valleys, alluvial deposits, and fractured rock regions, where infiltration and water retention conditions were favorable.

The findings of this study have critical implications for sustainable groundwater management. Identifying high-potential recharge zones enables better groundwater conservation planning, especially in agricultural and urban expansion regions. The insights gained from morphometric and machine learning integration can assist in strategic groundwater extraction planning, reducing overexploitation and preventing resource depletion. For future research, field-based validation using geophysical surveys and groundwater monitoring wells can further refine the model. Additionally, the integration of remote sensing-derived soil moisture indices, climate variability data, and temporal groundwater fluctuations can improve long-term groundwater potential assessments. Sustainable water resource management practices, such as artificial recharge structures, check dams, and afforestation in low-recharge zones, should be prioritized to enhance groundwater availability in vulnerable regions.
