Chronic Acromioclavicular Joint Reconstruction With Semitendinosus Allograft: The X's Technique
Authors: Andrew S. Bi, Jacob T. Morgan, Tomas F. Vega, Logan D. Moews, Enrico M. Forlenza, Nikhil N. Verma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The acromioclavicular (AC) and coracoclavicular (CC) joints provide anteroposterior and superoinferior stability, respectively, of the scapula in relation to the lateral clavicle. Chronic or high-grade (≥IIIB) AC dislocations should be treated with a structured rehabilitation program, and if symptoms persist, they typically require reconstruction to restore alignment, relieve pain, and improve scapular function.Indications:This technique is indicated for chronic AC joint dislocations that are Rockwood grade IIIB-VI injuries that have failed conservative management. Patients should demonstrate both horizontal and vertical AC instability to use this technique.Technique Description:The senior author's “X’s” technique for AC joint reconstruction using semitendinosus (ST) allograft is detailed here. This arthroscopically assisted open approach uses diagnostic arthroscopy first to identify joint landmarks. The undersurface of the acromion is identified, and a brief bursectomy is performed. A tensionable button construct (TightRope and DogBone) is utilized to reduce the AC joint. Four all-suture anchors are used to form an X configuration and stabilize the AC joint. The ST allograft is passed through the anterolateral portal, looped around the coracoid, and secured over the top of the clavicle for additional fixation of the joint.Results:This technique does not yet have specific outcome data; however, similar techniques have demonstrated that ST allograft and autograft augmentation improve patient-reported outcomes and load resistance. Additionally, reconstructing the AC joint, in addition to the CC joint, improves patient horizontal instability. Allograft and autograft reconstruction techniques have low failure rates and help patients return to sport at their preinjury level.Discussion/Conclusion:AC joint reconstruction with an ST allograft using the “X’s” technique is a novel method of AC and CC joint treatment for patients with chronic AC joint dislocations that are Rockwood grade IIIB-VI, providing improved patient vertical and horizontal fixation via the 3 “X” constructs: the tensionable button construct, “X” of the all-suture anchors across the AC joint, the “X” of the ST graft around the coracoid and clavicle, and an optional “X” across the AC joint with the ST graft.Patient Consent Disclosure Statement:The author(s) attests that consent has been obtained from any patient(s) appearing in this publication. If the individual may be identifiable, the author(s) has included a statement of release or other written form of approval from the patient(s) with this submission for publication.

Background

The AC joint is a complex interaction between several osseous and ligamentous structures, with the AC joint providing primarily anteroposterior stability and the coracoclavicular (CC) joint providing superoinferior stability. 7 After an injury occurs to these ligaments, the scapula rotates inferiorly and internally while the clavicle remains relatively stable via the sternoclavicular joint, creating separation of the AC joint.14,15 AC joint dislocations can be classified according to the Rockwood classification, in which type 3s are borderline operative cases and types 4 through 6 have traditionally been described as operative cases. 17 A new subclassification was developed with type 3A injuries without horizontal instability treated conservatively, and type 3B injuries with horizontal instability treated initially conservatively but with operative management if needed.1,3

Initial treatment should focus on a patient-specific, structured rehabilitation program focused on scapular dyskinesia, such as the Mazzocca protocol, for types 1 through 6 injuries with special consideration for high-demand contact athletes. 11 Progression through the program is continuous and focused on function instead of time. A brief period of sling immobilization may be used to reduce pain, followed by range-of-motion (ROM) and scapular closed kinetic chain exercises. 11 Then, open kinetic chain shoulder strengthening focused on scapular function and sport-specific training may be gradually introduced. 11 Up to 78% of chronic type 3 injuries treated with scapular rehabilitation resolve dyskinesis 5 and achieve functional outcomes equivalent to those of surgical management, 10 demonstrating the importance of an initial conservative approach.

Indications

Failure of nonoperative treatment with refractory scapular dysfunction and persistent horizontal instability in patients with type 3B through 6 AC joint dislocations is indicated for surgical management.1,3 Overall, in chronic injuries compared to acute injuries, there is decreased healing potential of the CC ligaments with primary fixation only. 3 Acute injuries have primarily been described as presenting within 3 weeks of injury and can be treated with primary repair, whereas chronic injuries, as presenting after 3 weeks of injury, should primarily be treated with reconstruction.1,3,4 There are a wide variety of treatment options, with over 50 described techniques in the literature.8,9,12

The patient presentation is a 35-year-old male with a 2-year history of right shoulder pain after a motorcycle accident in which he fell over the handlebars. He has continued pain with overhead activity and a persistent deformity. On physical examination, he has an obvious deformity of the AC joint and pain with palpation. He has a full ROM with pain at the end range, mild discomfort on empty can, a positive O’Brien’s test, and is neurovascularly intact distally with excellent deltoid function. Multiple radiographs, including an Alexander view, demonstrate a type 5 Rockwood classification AC joint dislocation with evidence of horizontal instability. Magnetic resonance imaging is not required before surgical management; however, in chronic cases they can be helpful to rule out concomitant pathology, including rotator cuff tears or labral tears.

Technique Description

The patient is positioned in an upright beach chair with a pneumatic arm holder with room for a C-arm for fluoroscopy. The operative site is signed, and a timeout is completed. An initial diagnostic arthroscopy is performed, confirming no significant osteoarthritis in the glenohumeral joint or other concomitant pathology. A standard anterior portal is created in the rotator interval to allow for a subcoracoid decompression. The rotator interval is debrided superior to the subscapularis, medial to the coracoid, defining the undersurface of the coracoid. The scope is then moved into the subacromial space, and a lateral portal is created for debridement of the subcoracoid bursa. The scope is then moved into a lateral position, and an anterolateral instrumentation portal is created with an 8 mm cannula. This allows further instrumentation and debridement of the subcoracoid recess. Graft preparation is then performed with an ST allograft, with at least 2 cm of whipstitching at each end. The minimum graft length should be 15 cm, with 20 cm or more ideal. Given the X's reconstruction, the additional length of the limbs will be used for additional AC joint reconstruction.

An open utilitarian approach to the CC joint is performed, with mobile flaps created over the deltotrapezial fascia. A longitudinal split is then made over the clavicle with subperiosteal dissection anterior and posterior to the clavicle. An AC drill guide is then placed through the anterolateral portal and positioned on the superior surface of the clavicle. This should be positioned around 3 cm medial to the AC joint to recreate both the conoid and trapezoid, and slightly posterior to the AC joint to recreate the posterior-to-anterior trajectory of the CC ligaments.

A 2.4-mm initial guide pin is then drilled to allow a pilot hole through the clavicle and coracoid, followed by a 3.2-mm cannulated drill. A nitinol wire is then retrieved up the anterolateral portal and used to shuttle the sutures for the tensionable button construct (TightRope and DogBone, Arthrex). The undersurface of the coracoid contoured washer is then placed over the sutures and fed through the anterolateral 8-mm cannula with a long grasper (Kingfisher, Arthrex). This should be let-in upside down so the contour fits the undersurface of the coracoid. The tensionable button construct is then provisionally tightened over the superior clavicle for provisional reduction without full tensioning at this point.

Next, graft passage is performed. A switching stick is placed from posterior to the clavicle to medial to the coracoid. A dilator is then introduced over the switching stick, and the switching stick is removed and replaced with a shuttling suture. This suture is then retrieved from the anterolateral cannula and used to pass the first limb of the graft, which will recreate the conoid ligament. These steps are then repeated anterior to the clavicle and lateral to the coracoid to pass shuttling sutures that will then pass the other limb of the graft to recreate the trapezoid ligament. The scope can be kept in the lateral portal for viewing the graft passage.

At this point, as much tension as possible is performed through the tensionable button construct. This is assisted with raising the shoulder using a pneumatic arm holder (Spider2 Limb Positioner, Smither & Nephew) and an assistant pressing down on the clavicle with a broad instrument. Intraoperative fluoroscopy demonstrates a near reduction of the AC joint; however, residual superior translation remains.

At this point, the additional components of the X's reconstruction can be performed. Additional dissection is carried out laterally to the AC joint. The residual disc is then debrided with a rongeur. Once adequate exposure of the AC joint has been performed, four 1.8-mm knotless all-suture suture anchors (FiberTak, Arthrex) are then placed, with 2 suture anchors placed in the anterior and posterior acromion and 2 suture anchors placed in the anterior and posterior clavicle. These should be placed in a box orientation, angled toward each other, to allow for an oblique entry to avoid penetration of the subacromial space. The all-suture suture anchors are then converted, with the repair sutures placed into the loop suture of the opposite anchor across from it in an X configuration, providing 2 suture staple configurations across the AC joint. The final construct can be seen here, providing the second X of the X's reconstruction technique. These 4 anchors can be sequentially retensioned and alternated back and forth with the tensionable button construct over the CC joint. Final fluoroscopy demonstrated improved and excellent reduction of the AC joint at this stage.

Once final biomechanical stability has been achieved, the graft reconstruction can then be completed. The limbs of the ST allograft are tied over each other and secured using a series of figure-of-8 suture fixation using a number 2 high-tensile strength suture. The excess limbs of the ST allograft can then be brought over laterally, crossed over each other as the third X of the X's reconstruction, and fixed with the 4 repair remnant sutures from the all-suture anchors over the AC joint (Figure 1). This provides additional collagen augmentation of the AC joint.

The 3 Xs of the X's acromioclavicular joint reconstruction technique. From left to right: the all-suture anchor X construct overlying the superior aspect of the acromioclavicular joint; the vertically oriented semitendinosus allograft that reconstructs the conoid and trapezoid ligaments of the coracoclavicular joint in another X construct; the optional third X construct that uses the semitendinosus allograft to further augment the reconstruction and provide additional collagen fixation to the acromioclavicular joint.

Finally, the deltotrapezial fascia is closed, covering the graft reconstruction. Ideally, there should be no graft or implant exposed. The incision is then irrigated copiously and closed in a layered fashion.

Postoperatively, the patient is placed in an abduction sling for 6 weeks and made nonweightbearing for 3 months. At 6 weeks, physical therapy can be initiated with active and active-assisted ROM exercises in the scapular plane. At 3 months, strengthening can be initiated with gradual progression. Multiple radiographs are without evidence of peri-implant clavicle fracture.

Results

Complications of AC joint reconstruction are important to recognize, with a reported 8 incidence of up to 14.2%. Early complications include infection, hardware migration or failure leading to loss of reduction, and peri-implant fracture of the coracoid, distal clavicle, or acromion.6,8 For the X's technique, special attention should be paid to infection and peri-implant fracture, given the multiple implants used. Late complications include postoperative stiffness, graft elongation or failure, and implant tunnel-widening and osteolysis.2,6,8 Precautions should be emphasized intra- and postoperatively to prevent these complications.

Postoperative outcomes of anatomic AC joint reconstruction with ST allografts are extremely heterogeneous, given the variety of techniques within this treatment category. A meta-analysis of over 1700 patients comparing open versus arthroscopic techniques for treatment of AC dislocations demonstrated no differences in loss of reduction, complications, or revision rates. 8 However, there was a significantly greater rate of clavicle and coracoid fractures in open surgery compared to arthroscopic surgery. In this prospective comparative study of 12 Weaver-Dunn reconstructions versus 12 ST anatomic reconstructions, there was significantly better Constant Score and better stress-load resistance in the ST allograft reconstruction technique. 18 There have been no demonstrable differences between the use of allograft versus autograft for graft reconstruction techniques, and long-term follow-up of a small case series demonstrated zero failures with significant improvements and pre-to-postoperative patient-reported outcomes.13,16 Further biomechanical and clinical research is needed to evaluate the X's technique compared to other surgical techniques, of which there are numerous options.

The X's technique uses multiple implants, which increases procedural cost compared with nonoperative management or simpler reconstructions with fewer implants, such as the Weaver-Dunn or a single cortical suspensory fixation. The rationale for this construct is to provide multiplanar stability and biologic augmentation with an allograft, which may offer theoretical biomechanical advantages initially, support future biologic healing with the graft, and potentially reduce failure in select patients with chronic or high-grade instability.
