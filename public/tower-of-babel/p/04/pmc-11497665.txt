Multimorbidity measures associated with cognitive function among community‐dwelling older Chinese adults
Authors: Shuojia Wang, Yilin Chen, Lijiao Xiong, Nana Jin, Pengfei Zhao, Zhen Liang, Lixin Cheng, Lin Kang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONOlder adults with multimorbidity are at high risk of cognitive impairment development. There is a lack of research on the associations between different multimorbidity measures and cognitive function among older Chinese adults living in the community.METHODSWe used the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey from 2002 to 2018 and included data on dementia‐free participants aged ≥65 years. Multimorbidity measures included condition counts, multimorbidity patterns, and trajectories. The association of multimorbidity measures with cognitive function was examined by generalized estimating equation and linear and logistic regression models.RESULTSAmong 14,093 participants at baseline, 43.2% had multimorbidity. Multimorbidity patterns were grouped into cancer‐inflammatory, cardiometabolic, and sensory patterns. Multimorbidity trajectories were classified as “onset‐condition,” “newly developing,” and “severe condition.” The Mini‐Mental State Examination scores were significantly lower for participants with more chronic conditions, with cancer‐inflammatory/cardiometabolic/sensory patterns, and with developing multimorbidity trajectories.DISCUSSIONCondition counts, sensory pattern, cardiometabolic pattern, cancer‐inflammatory pattern, and multimorbidity developmental trajectories were prospectively associated with cognitive function.Highlights Elderly individuals with a higher number of chronic conditions were associated with lower MMSE scores in the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey data.MMSE scores were significantly lower for participants with specific multimorbidity patterns.Individuals with developing trajectories of multimorbidity were associated with lower MMSE scores and a higher risk of mild cognitive impairment. Keywords: cognitive function, elderly, multimorbidity, pattern, trajectory

BACKGROUND

Over the past few decades, life expectancy has significantly risen in nearly 60 countries, with adults now often living beyond 75 years. 1 Multimorbidity, characterized by the presence of more than two concurrent diseases, is influenced by factors like gender, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status. 2 Among Chinese elderly in the community, multimorbidity prevalence ranges from 6.4% to 76.5%. 3 , 4 The presence of multimorbidity can negatively impact the well‐being of older individuals, leading to a reduced quality of life and poorer self‐rated health. 5 Identification of multimorbidity patterns can provide clues to the prevention and treatment of Alzheimer's disease (AD) as well as improved prognosis. In addition, understanding the impact of multimorbidity on the risk of age‐related cognitive diseases is crucial for developing effective risk‐reduction strategies. 6

The measurement of multimorbidity is important for determining its impact on individuals and outcomes. There is a lack of standardized approaches for measuring multimorbidity, with most studies exploring the association between physical health conditions and cognitive decline, leading to a focus on single or specific chronic diseases, particularly cardiovascular diseases. 7 However, just counting the number of diseases does not provide a comprehensive assessment of their impact on patient health. In terms of the association between multimorbidity and cognitive performance, previous studies yielded inconsistent results. A study in the United States found that multimorbidity could contribute to an increased risk of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or AD (mean age 78.5 [SD 5.2]). 8 A study in Europe found subjects (mean age 75.1 [SD 6.9]) who reverted from MCI to normal cognition were more frequently affected by respiratory, urologic, and psychiatric diseases. 9 However, other research did not find an association between vascular risk factors and diseases or between cerebrovascular disease and disease progression in AD (mean age 73.3 [SD 8.8]). 10 Another study in the United States (mean age 76 [SD 5.5]) also found cardiovascular risk factors were not associated with cognitive decline. 11 It is possible that the impact of disease combinations on cognitive performance may not be simply reflected by counting the number of diseases, and the results vary for different multimorbidities.

Diseases often co‐occur in specific patterns, indicating shared pathophysiological mechanisms and risk factors. Research has demonstrated that certain disease patterns have different prognoses, with some of them playing a major role in the onset of dementia. 12 Few studies have examined the association of multimorbid patterns with cognitive function. Lisa et al. 13 found participants (mean age 53.7 [SD 6.5]) in the sleep class had the highest incidence of new‐onset cognitive decline. A study in Swedish showed that people with neuropsychiatric, cardiovascular, and sensory impairment/cancer multimorbidity had increased hazards for dementia (mean age 75.0 [SD 10.4]). 14 A study in England found memory scores tend to differ between different multimorbidity patterns (mean age 65.0 [SD 10.2]). 7 Most studies have been conducted in a Western population, with only one study conducted in an Asia population. 15 Given the differences in health‐related behaviors, dietary patterns, environmental factors, and disease spectrum, these findings may not be generalizable. Furthermore, existing approaches to assessing multimorbidity predominantly rely on cross‐sectional data and little consideration of temporal changes. 16 However, distinctive trajectories of multimorbidity can indicate dynamic changes. Understanding patterns and dynamic changes in multimorbidity can enhance the identification of risk and facilitate the development of personalized health management plans for older adults with multimorbidity.

In this study, we focused on community‐dwelling older adults with multimorbidity, who are the primary users of home healthcare services and are at high risk for the development of MCI. 17 Most previous studies investigated the effect of single chronic conditions on cognition, with limited exploration of multimorbidity patterns or trajectories in the Chinese elderly. Therefore, this study aimed to identify multimorbidity measures and their association with cognitive performance in Chinese community‐dwelling older adults.

METHODS

Data source and study population

The study utilized data from an ongoing nationwide survey of older adults in China, the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), from 2002 to 2018. Half of the counties in 23 provinces in mainland China were invited to participate in the study. Additional information regarding the survey was described elsewhere. 18 The present study initially included a total of 16,511 participants aged ≥65 years from 2002 to 2018. We included four waves of 3‐year survey data. After the exclusion of 2418 participants under 65 years of age, those lacking Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores during follow‐ups, or those diagnosed with dementia at baseline or the first interview, the final analysis included 14,093 participants (Figure 1).

Follow‐ups for different entrance years (a) and the inclusion and exclusion criteria (b) in the study.

Individual chronic conditions and multimorbidity

Data on 14 individual chronic diseases or conditions were collected at baseline and follow‐up waves. Ten of these conditions were self‐reported by participants, including diabetes, heart disease, stroke or cerebrovascular disease, respiratory disease (bronchitis, emphysema, asthma, pneumonia), tuberculosis, cancer, gastric or duodenal ulcer, Parkinson's disease, bedsore, and prostate diseases. Heart disease includes coronary artery disease, arrhythmias, heart failure, valve diseases, and other heart conditions. The response to each condition was coded as binary data. Blood pressure was measured using an electronic sphygmomanometer by a trained physician, and the mean of two repeated measurements was calculated. Blood pressure was also coded as a binary. Hypertension was defined as either a self‐reported condition or having a systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg or if patients self‐reported being diagnosed with hypertension. Participants were identified as having vision impairment if they were unable to see or distinguish the break in a circle, were blind, and/or reported having either glaucoma or cataracts. 19 Hearing loss was defined as requiring a hearing aid, and tooth loss was characterized by the absence of natural teeth.

Cognitive performance

Cognitive function was assessed using the MMSE, which was adapted into a Chinese version and tested through pilot survey interviews. 20 The MMSE comprises 24 items distributed across six dimensions: orientation, registration, naming, attention and calculation, recall, and language, with a total score range of 0 to 30. A higher score indicates better cognitive function, and MMSE was used as a continuous variable. Cognitive impairment was defined as a MMSE score less than 18. 21 , 22 , 23 Considering the standard cutoff value was not consistent, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using a cutoff of 24. 24 Additionally, dementia was defined as if the participant self‐reported having been diagnosed with the condition by a physician. In cases where the elders were unable to answer, their closest relative(s) or caregiver(s) provided the information. MCI and dementia were used as binary variables.

Sociodemographic and behavioral variables

The study considered sociodemographic variables including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), education, residence, place of birth, economic status, marital status, smoking habits, drinking habits, and exercise behavior. Height was estimated using knee height for men (height [cm] = 67.78 + 2.01 × knee height [cm]) and women (height [cm] = 74.08 + 1.81 × knee height [cm]) if height was not directly measured. 25 BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared (kg/m2) and categorized into four groups (<18.5 kg/m2, 18.5 to <23.0 kg/m2, 23.0 to <27.5 kg/m2, and ≥27.5 kg/m2) based on the World Health Organization classification for the Asian population. 26 Education levels were grouped into three categories: 0, <6 years, and ≥6 years. The place of residence was categorized as urban or rural. Self‐reported economic statuses were categorized as rich, normal, or poor. Marital status was classified as married (including cohabited) or single (separated, divorced, widowed, or never married). Smoking and alcohol consumption habits were measured by asking if they had ever smoked or consumed alcohol and were categorized as non‐smokers/drinkers, former smokers/drinkers, or current smokers/drinkers. Exercise behavior was categorized based on participants’ self‐reported regular exercise at present or ever (current, former, or never).

Statistical analysis

In this study, descriptive analyses were performed using frequencies (percentage) for categorical variables and means with standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. The chi‐squared test was utilized for categorical variables. Multimorbidity was assessed using condition counts, multimorbidity patterns, and multimorbidity trajectories.

Condition counts were categorized into 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and ≥5. Multimorbidity patterns were derived from the baseline (first wave) and the first follow‐up (second wave) using exploratory factor analysis (EFA), which was used to examine the underlying relationship among the 14 individual chronic conditions. 27 The principal factor method was applied based on the tetrachoric correlation matrix. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin method and Bartlett's test of sphericity were performed to estimate the adequacy of the model fit to the data. The optimal number of factors was determined by considering the eigenvalue, scree plot, and the interpretability of each factor. An oblique rotation of factor‐loading matrices was applied, with each resulting factor loading representing the strength of association between the condition and the latent factor. Conditions were retained in the pattern if they had the largest positive factor loading among all latent factors when labeling the patterns. A standardized composite factor score for each latent factor was calculated for each participant by multiplying the respective factor loading derived and each chronic condition at the penultimate interview record. Participants were then grouped into the pattern with the highest standardized factor score among all identified latent factors. In addition, those reporting no chronic conditions were grouped into a healthy pattern. 19

To examine the long‐term dynamic changes of multimorbidity, multimorbidity trajectories were fitted using data from baseline to the end of the follow‐up period. A group‐based trajectory model was used to identify potential multimorbidity trajectories using the Proc Traj procedure in the SAS software package (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). 28 The procedure fits a maximum likelihood function‐based semiparametric mixture model to longitudinal data. 29 Condition counts from baseline to the last interview were used to capture the maximum available follow‐up period in this survey research as a zero‐inflated Poisson outcome. Alternative models of two to six groups, with trajectory slope parameters varying through linear, quadratic, and cubic terms. The optimal model was determined based on the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and practical considerations of distinctiveness and interpretability of estimated trajectories (Supplemental Table S1).

A generalized estimating equation (GEE), linear regression, and logistic regression models were used to examine associations between multimorbidity measures and MMSE, MCI, or dementia. The models were adjusted for several covariates, including age, gender, residence, education, marital status, economic status, province, smoking habits, alcohol drinking habits, and exercise behavior. Given that BMI is closely linked to many cardiovascular diseases, we did not use BMI as a covariate. When the analysis outcome was the MMSE score, the models were additionally adjusted for the baseline MMSE score. In sensitivity analyses, we stratified the analysis by gender, further excluded participants with MCI at the baseline, and then repeated the analysis. Nevertheless, we used a MMSE cutoff value of 24 to analyze the association between multimorbidity with MCI as the standard is inconsistent. We also combined MCI and dementia in the analyses. All p values were two‐sided, and p < .05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed with SAS version 9.4 and R version 4.3.0.

Of the 14,093 participants, 6088 (43.2%) had multimorbidity (≥2 chronic conditions) at baseline and 11,868 (84.2%) had multimorbidity at the end of the study. Participants with more chronic conditions tended to have poorer self‐reported economic status and were more likely to live in urban areas (Table 1).

Multimorbidity measures

The EFA identified three multimorbidity patterns from the 14 chronic conditions included in the first and second waves, respectively (Table 2 and Supplemental Table S2). Patterns included cardiometabolic patterns (hypertension, diabetes, stroke or cerebrovascular diseases, and heart diseases), sensory patterns (hearing loss, vision impairment, and tooth loss), and cancer‐inflammatory patterns (respiratory disease [bronchitis, emphysema, pneumonia, or asthma], tuberculosis, cancer, gastric or duodenal ulcer, Parkinson's disease, and bedsore). The multimorbidity patterns in the first and second waves were relatively consistent. In addition to the aforementioned three patterns, we identified respiratory patterns in the third wave (data not shown). The evolution of multimorbidity patterns of older adults over 6 years includes both the change over time of disease patterns and the migration of participants from one pattern to another (Figure 2). During the follow‐up periods, the main shifts among patterns involved participants in the healthy group and those who primarily moved to the cancer‐inflammatory pattern at the first follow‐up.

Association between multimorbidity measures and cognition

The MMSE score was statistically significantly lower for participants with more chronic conditions (p for trend < .001). According to multimorbidity patterns and MMSE scores, participants in the cancer‐inflammatory, cardiometabolic, and sensory groups at baseline and the first follow‐up had lower MMSE scores compared with the healthy group. In addition, compared with the onset‐condition trajectory, newly developing (β = −0.95, SE = 0.12), and severe developing (β = −2.55, SE = 0.53) trajectories had lower MMSE scores (Table 3).

The risks of MCI during follow‐up were statistically significantly higher for participants with more chronic conditions and with developing multimorbidity trajectories. Compared with the onset‐condition trajectory, newly developing and severe condition trajectories had a higher risk of MCI. At baseline, the cardiometabolic (odds ratio [OR] = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.03 to 1.46) and sensory (OR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.41 to 1.95) patterns were significantly associated with a higher risk of MCI compared with the healthy group. At the first follow‐up, participants in the cancer‐inflammatory pattern, cardiometabolic pattern, and sensory pattern had a higher risk of MCI compared with the healthy group (Supplemental Table S3).

Participants with newly developing (OR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.34 to 3.03) and severe condition (OR = 7.13, 95% CI: 3.03 to 15.39) trajectories were associated with a higher risk of dementia compared with people with onset‐condition trajectory. However, only the number of chronic conditions above three was found to increase the risk of dementia, and the risk was higher with an increase in condition counts. In addition, we only found the association between cancer‐inflammatory patterns at baseline and sensory patterns at first follow‐up were associated with dementia (Supplemental Table S4).

Sensitivity analyses

The analysis stratified by gender was shown in Supplemental Tables S5 to S7. Similar associations were found in both sexes between condition counts, multimorbidity trajectories, and MMSE scores. With respect to multimorbidity patterns, participants in the sensory pattern showed similar results in both sexes compared with the total population. The association of multimorbidity with MCI stratified by gender was similar to the result of the association of multimorbidity with MMSE. The higher estimates of risk in women versus men suggest potential effect modification by gender. For the outcome of dementia, the association of multimorbidity patterns with dementia was different in different genders, and we did not find stable results between multimorbidity patterns and dementia in both sexes. Supplemental Tables S8 to S10 showed the results of the association of multimorbidity with MMSE, MCI, and dementia when excluding participants with MCI at baseline. The results were generally consistent with the results of the general population. Supplemental Table S11 showed the results of the MMSE cutoff value of 24, which were similar to the previous analysis with a cutoff value of 18. Supplemental Table S12 presented the results of combining MCI and dementia. In addition, the association of multimorbidity with MMSE excluding elderly with psychosis was shown in Supplemental Table S13. The results of combining MCI and dementia and excluding elderly with psychosis were similar to the overall analysis.

DISCUSSION

In this study, our results revealed that the MMSE scores were significantly lower for participants with a higher number of chronic conditions, with specific multimorbidity patterns, and those with developing and severe condition trajectories. These findings provide new insights into the multimorbidity factors that decrease cognitive performance and have important implications for healthcare planning and developing strategies to mitigate the impact of multimorbidity.

Tracking the evolution of multimorbidity patterns, we observed the number of participants grouped in the healthy group halved at the 6‐year follow‐ups as the majority transitioned toward the specific multimorbidity clusters identified at follow‐ups. Similarly, a study in Sweden on adults aged ≥60 identified a highly dynamic evolution of multimorbidity clusters at both 6 and 12 years and found participants’ transitions from one cluster to another. 30 Except for aging, two potential etiologic mechanisms may mainly explain the association of multimorbidity with cognitive impairment. One is through cardiovascular diseases such as hypertension, cardiac diseases, and coronary artery disease. Another potential mechanism is the presence of two or more chronic conditions that independently or in combination could synergistically promote or accelerate cognitive decline. 31 We also found the “healthy” group at 3 years included individuals who were initially classified as healthy, but a portion of the group originated from other categories. This dynamic nature can be attributed to participants transitioning from a previously identified sick state to a healthy state. Some diseases included in the study can be cured, including vision impairment due to cataracts, respiratory disease due to bronchitis, or pneumonia. In addition, self‐report disease diagnosis information may induce recall bias.

Our study extracted three types of multimorbidity patterns and determined that people with cancer‐inflammatory, cardiometabolic, and sensory patterns were associated with lower MMSE scores compared with healthy groups. The cognitive function decline may differ when related to the presence of sensory impairment versus cardiovascular and neuropsychiatric conditions and might even follow different pathways. 14 While not directly comparable, our study's findings align with those from previous research. A prospective cohort study in Taiwan found that women with cardiometabolic patterns were related to poor memory. 15 Another 12‐year Swedish cohort showed that individuals with cardiovascular pattern followed by sensory impairment were associated with elevated risks of dementia. 14 The postulated mechanistic pathways linking cardiometabolic pattern to cognition could be explained by chronic inflammation, energy metabolism, and reduced cardiac output. 32 Muddapu et al. 33 proposed that any decline in metabolic function could initiate a chain of cascading events, such as an excess of reactive oxygen species and heightened inflammation, which in turn could lead to the emergence of diverse neurodegenerative pathologies. In addition to multimorbidity associated with cognition, the length of time one has a condition also plays a role in its effects on cognition. Few studies explored the association between age at onset of disease and cognition. A longitudinal study found younger age at the onset of diabetes was significantly associated with a higher HR of dementia. They also found that late‐onset diabetes was not significantly associated with subsequent dementia. 34 Based on this, it is also important to consider whether disease comorbidity is associated with the onset of dementia in those with certain diseases. However, we were unable to access information about the age of disease onset or how well it is controlled with medications/treatments. Further studies are needed to explore the related effects.

Our findings are consistent with an investigation showing associations of multimorbidity trajectories with cognitive impairment; we found participants with developing multimorbidity trajectories associated with lower MMSE scores. A study among 5923 Health and Retirement Study participants showed that, compared to “no new condition,” the “rapid growth” trajectory was related to 32% (3% to 69%) higher dementia risk through an 8‐year follow‐up. 35 Therefore, besides condition counts, multimorbidity trajectories and patterns may provide more information for understanding multimorbidity and could be selected according to the purpose of preventing MCI and dementia. However, further research is required to comprehensively understand the underlying mechanisms and validate these findings. If future studies confirm the contribution of different multimorbidity patterns and trajectories of multimorbidity to longitudinal decline in cognitive performance, that might pave the way to the development of targeted strategies and patient management stratification approaches, particularly for community‐dwelling populations.

In this study, we considered several sociodemographic confounders as the social determinants of health that are likely causing/affecting chronic conditions. Existing evidence indicates that multimorbidity is more common and more severe in persons with low socioeconomic status. 36 Additionally, a prior study showed that the association between multimorbidity patterns and cognition tends to be smaller when individuals have higher education levels and engage in healthier behaviors. 7 According to life course epidemiology models, 37 it is believed that engaging in healthy behaviors has a protective effect on cognitive performance in later adulthood. The association between multimorbidity and cognition might be affected by sociodemographic factors. Previous studies showed that neuropsychiatric conditions might be associated with cognitive function. Moderate/severe anxiety was related to worse performance on semantic memory, attention, and executive function. 38 Another study reported depression to be a risk factor for dementia. 39 However, we were unable to obtain relevant disease information. Future studies are needed to consider neuropsychiatric conditions.

The strengths of our study include the large community‐dwelling study sample, the longitudinal study design, and multiple multimorbidity measures. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies in China to comprehensively examine the association of multimorbidity with cognitive performance in older adults. 15 However, several limitations need to be mentioned. First, disease diagnosis information was mainly from self‐report, and current data lack related clinical diagnosis information, which cannot provide precise delineation of MCI/dementia, and may lead to bias. Future studies are needed to use neuropsychological evaluation to validate MCI/dementia diagnoses and verify the results about the association between multimorbidity and MCI/dementia. Second, we cannot access the information about the age of disease onset or how well a disease is controlled with medications/treatments. Further study is needed to explore the effect of age of disease onset on cognition. Third, although the baseline survey inquired about common diseases of multiple systems, the number of diseases included was still limited, which may affect the results of the analyses. Fourth, there is a competing risk between mortality and cognitive decline; however, we cannot disclose the number of deceased individuals at each follow‐up and within each multimorbidity pattern as the CLHLS is still ongoing.
