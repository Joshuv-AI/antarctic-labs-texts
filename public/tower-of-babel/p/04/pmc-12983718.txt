Health workforce implications of integrating NCD prevention, screening and treatment into hospital-based MNCH services: perspectives from Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Viet Nam
Authors: Téa E Collins, Amanda Karapici, Blerta Maliqi, Daria Berlina, Svetlana Akselrod, Nuhu Yaqub, Wilson M Were, Anshu Banerjee, Julia Tainijoki, Aliina Altymysheva, Thi Quynh Nga Pham, Mekhri Shoismatuloeva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntegration of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) prevention, screening and treatment into maternal, newborn and child healthcare (MNCH) services has become increasingly important as countries address the dual burden of communicable and NCDs. While global policy attention has grown, practical experiences from low- and middle-income countries on how to operationalise this integration, particularly its implications for the health workforce, remain limited.This practice paper synthesises programme learning from Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Viet Nam, drawing on WHO-supported country assessments, programme reports and practitioner perspectives, with a particular focus on workforce challenges. Health workforce shortages, skills gaps, limited training capacity and uneven distribution remain major barriers to service integration in these countries. We apply Donabedian’s quality improvement model, encompassing outcome, process and structure, to elaborate on these challenges. We emphasise the importance of taking a systemic perspective in addressing health workforce issues and improving the quality of care. We recognise the need for additional research in key areas that are instrumental for strengthening the health workforce, particularly for the effective integration of NCD services into MNCH and strengthening primary healthcare. Our insights aim to assist in the development of integrated programmes and to promote advancements in the research agenda for the health workforce.

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs), such as cardiovascular disease, cancers, chronic respiratory conditions and diabetes, are responsible for the deaths of 43 million people annually, with 18 million of these occurring prematurely before the age of 70.1 The burden of NCDs is concentrated in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), where 82% of premature NCD mortality takes place.1 The challenges that NCDs and their shared risk factors pose to global health and development (economic, social and environmental), along with mental health conditions, were articulated in three United Nations General Assembly high-level meetings on the prevention and control of NCDs.2 These meetings have highlighted the necessity of a coordinated global response to achieve a one-third reduction in premature mortality from NCDs by 2030, through prevention, treatment and promotion of mental health, a key target of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).3

Despite increased global attention over the past two decades, countries’ efforts to prevent and control NCDs and mental health conditions remain insufficient.2 In response, the WHO has scaled up action and intensified its efforts at the global, regional and national levels, promoting a whole-of-government and whole-of-society approach to tackle NCDs and their shared risk factors through a strengthened multisectoral and multistakeholder response and integrated service delivery models, with consideration of specific country contexts. An example of WHO’s work is the project Improving the Quality of Hospital Care to Reduce Maternal, Newborn and Child Deaths and Accelerate the Achievement of the SDG Health Targets (the WHO quality improvement project) in Kyrgyzstan,4 Tajikistan5 and Viet Nam.6

In the lead-up to the fourth high-level meeting on the prevention and control of NCDs in September 2025, there is a growing acknowledgement of the pivotal role played by the health workforce (The term includes emergency, public health, polio, immunisation and other subgroups in the health workforce) in advancing the delivery of quality care and achieving global health targets. A strong health workforce is critical for delivering quality integrated care encompassing promotion, prevention, treatment and rehabilitative services for effective NCD prevention and control.7 Effective integration is crucial to ensure that everyone has access to a comprehensive and coordinated continuum of care that meets their needs across their life course. This integration should include a holistic approach to managing NCDs, focusing on both the prevention of risk factors and the treatment of NCDs. Additionally, NCDs should be integrated with mental health and maternal, newborn and child health (MNCH) services to provide more cohesive and effective care.

The argument that a strong, well-trained health workforce is essential to improving the quality of care is not new. Standard 7 of the WHO Standards for Improving the Quality of Maternal and Newborn Care in Health Facilities7 points out the important role of health workers in providing routine care and managing complications. Particularly, the availability, competencies, skills mix, team design and motivation of health workers are deemed critical components. Standard 8 recognises the importance of an enabling environment for health workers to operationalise care integration. The WHO Package of Essential Noncommunicable Disease Interventions for Primary Health Care (WHO PEN)8 recognises the role of a trained workforce and the importance of the availability of and adherence to guidelines and protocols of care to address the prevention and control of NCDs. These frameworks collectively provide valuable insights into the crucial role that the health workforce plays in the health system.

This paper aims to analyse health workforce challenges, as well as the factors that support quality integrated care for the prevention and control of NCDs and mental health. The analysis draws on implementation experiences, documented through WHO-supported country assessments conducted in Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Viet Nam. The data presented in those assessments were derived from a mixed-methods approach, including document review of national policies, health information systems and service statistics, as well as semistructured interviews and focus group discussions with policymakers, health managers and frontline health workers. For this manuscript, these country-specific findings were synthesised and supplemented with practitioner insights from global health workforce implementation, enabling the identification of cross-cutting themes and lessons relevant to integrating NCD services into MNCH platforms in LMICs.

We identify the common challenges encountered within these three countries, alongside country-specific insights, and propose a series of prerequisites essential for effective integration from the health workforce perspective. We use Donabedian’s quality improvement model to discuss health workforce challenges, considering three major dimensions of care8 9: (a) outcome—reduction of mortality, recovery and survival, and improved patient experience; (b) process—the way systems and processes work to deliver the desired outcome, such as services, diagnostics or treatments and (c) structure—characteristics of the health facility in which care takes place, including the modern protocols of care and treatment guidelines. We chose this framework due to its simplicity and ability to assess the health workforce holistically in shaping both process and structural quality to deliver the desired outcome.

Throughout the paper, we stress the importance of adopting a systemic approach and solutions that are context-specific and tailored to the part of the system responsible for addressing health workforce challenges in these countries. We conclude by outlining the direction of future efforts in the NCD health workforce agenda and identifying research priorities to inform the development of comprehensive programmes aimed at integrating NCDs within other areas of health services.

Integrating NCD prevention and control into MNCH programmes and services

NCDs and MNCH have historically been approached separately within global health policies, programming and resource allocation.10 This dichotomy contradicts the essential comprehensive strategy and integrated responses required for achieving universal health coverage as articulated in numerous high-level policy documents, such as the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, political declarations of several health-related United Nations General Assembly high-level meetings, and the WHO Fourteenth General Programme of Work 2025–2028.11

In the era of the Millennium Development Goals, infectious diseases and MNCH were given special attention and funding streams as global health priorities. NCDs were not considered part of the global health development agenda, irrespective of the huge economic and epidemiological burden they imposed.12 This led to a proliferation of disease-specific vertical programmes, which had targeted funding and often existed outside country health systems. Nowadays, verticalisation or siloing of healthcare is increasingly being replaced by integrated approaches to health service delivery by exploring synergies between vertically funded and provided services and already existing horizontal programmes within the health system, so the two become complementary.13 WHO advocates an integrated, people-centred approach to developing health systems capable of responding to emerging health challenges.14

From a systems perspective, separating NCDs from MNCH overlooks the interconnectedness of these health areas and misses opportunities for integrated care that addresses the broader determinants of health. Common risk factors between NCDs and poor MNCH outcomes, such as malnutrition in all its forms, alcohol consumption or tobacco exposure, combined with complex feedback mechanisms across different domains, underscore the need for an integrated approach.

Research suggests that successful integration relies on several enabling factors: early engagement of health workers1518; patient and community participation15; sustainable funding17 19; adequate team size1618; availability of necessary medical consumables such as medicines, supplies and medical equipment17; and regular follow-up.17 20 According to WHO, the development of new delivery models for integrated, people-centred services will require innovative and context-specific delivery models that support a multidisciplinary approach to provide comprehensive care across the life course.11 The literature strongly supports developing and training health workforce teams in multidisciplinary practices as critical for delivering effective integrated care.101618

Health workforce gaps within health systems in LMICs

WHO and other international organisations have highlighted a concerning trend in the global health workforce. For instance, a study using data from 2019 suggested that the global health workforce had a shortage of 6.4 million physicians, 30.6 million nurses and midwives, 3.3 million dentistry personnel and 2.9 million pharmaceutical personnel.21 The COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated this challenge, with the loss of lives of approximately 115 000 health workers.22 In 2021, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) projected a shortage of nearly 400 000 physicians across 32 OECD countries and a shortage of over 2.5 million nurses across 23 OECD countries by 2030.23 Additionally, data from 2024 projects a global shortage of 11 million health workers by 2030, primarily in LMICs.24

Despite these alarming statistics, they must be interpreted with caution due to the granularity of the available data that informs them, as well as the difficulty of comparing the estimates due to methodological differences. These limitations could lead to projections that may not fully represent the actual and future needs of the health system or address the complexities of health workforce requirements. Even if the projections can be taken at face value, simply having an adequate number of health workers required at the global or national level does not tell us much about workforce planning and management, and it tells us even less about structural quality.

Health workforce density requirements are determined by factors such as disease burden, demographics, skills mix, labour division and service distribution. WHO uses a threshold, not a recommendation, identifying a workforce (medical doctors, nursing personnel and midwifery personnel) density below 46 per 10 000 population and a universal health coverage service index below 80 as indicative of a relative health worker shortage on a global scale.25 However, these figures reveal little about the uneven distribution of the workforce, a significant challenge, particularly in LMICs. In high-income countries, the density of health workers is 6.5 times higher than in LMICs,26 even though this distribution is disproportionate to the burden of disease, given that 86% of premature NCD mortality occurs in LMICs.27

Economic, social and political instability are also significant factors that contribute to the migration of the workforce from low-income settings to high-income countries, exacerbating existing pressures on health systems. Migration exists within countries as well, from rural to urban areas, and from the public to the private sector.28 Patterns of mobility and migration are driven by multiple factors at multiple levels—from global, regional and national levels to institutional and individual levels—and may deepen existing inequalities. Despite the significant growth in health professionals’ education worldwide, between 2008 and 2018, whereby the number of medical graduates almost doubled and that of nursing graduates tripled, the increase in graduates outpaced the global population growth of 8% for the same period,29 showing possibly a mismatch between education strategies and future health system needs. However, this growth does not necessarily translate to an increase in the health professional density per 10,000 population. Instead, it highlights a lack of coordination between education strategies and health system planning and financing. For instance, in several African countries, the unemployment rate increases despite a lack of health professionals.30 Additionally, effective delivery of quality care is dependent on health workforce competencies, motivation and support by the health system, as the health workforce is an integral part of that system.31 Consequently, there is a need for a highly-skilled, well-trained and well-resourced health workforce to lead and implement actions to promote health and prevent and control NCDs. Such a workforce has been termed an ‘NCD-ready workforce’.32

In this context, policy and investment focus on transforming education programmes towards competency-based approaches aligned with population health priorities and skill gaps.31 Identifying skill gaps requires a systemic lens so capacity-strengthening initiatives can be tailored accordingly, accounting for individual, organisational, institutional and health system levels.33 Such a lens is essential, as different stakeholders, including national governments and donor agencies, often have varied interpretations of ‘capacity’ and how it manifests, which may include time constraints, lack of power or insufficient know-how.33

Special consideration should be given to the system itself, as it may inhibit effectiveness despite health workforce skills, training or resources. Potter and Brough33 suggest understanding capacity strengthening systemically as an iterative hierarchy of components to improve health workforce development and the use of resources for capacity enhancement. This hierarchy of components shows how the effectiveness of one form of capacity (eg, personal skills) depends on others (eg, workload), and in turn, facilitates (eg, performance) other forms of capacity, so resources can be allocated and interventions tailored accordingly to the part of the system responsible.

Merely providing ‘training’ may not be sufficient to bridge the gap between the supply of and demand for a skilled workforce.32 This is particularly evident when there are trained but unemployed healthcare workers or healthcare workers who work in other sectors.6 Therefore, it is essential for health workforce planning and management in LMICs to address the complex interplay between structural and process quality. This task is inherently not linear, relies on context and cannot be done in isolation. It needs to include considerations of health system governance, economic conditions, educational infrastructure and societal health needs. Even within the health workforce processes, planning and management need to account for the interplay dynamics between pre-service education, in-service training, turnover and the working environment.34 Moreover, leaders at different system levels have different perspectives on what workforce planning and management entail. Consequently, planning must address the requirements of the health system holistically, rather than focusing solely on occupational groups31 and necessitates collaborative efforts across various sectors and the community to ensure a health workforce equipped to deliver quality health services.

Perspectives from Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Viet Nam on integration of NCD into MNCH services

The majority of the population in Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Viet Nam live in rural settings (box 1).46 Their health systems remain largely state-owned, with ministries of health responsible for developing policies and technical guidelines and regulating health service provisions. Their health service delivery models, which are hospital-oriented and focused on episodic curative care, are not well suited to providing the continuous and person-centred care required for effective NCD integration into MNCH services. All three countries exhibit issues related to both structural and process qualities within their health systems, indicating challenges in the availability and distribution of the health workforce along with significant gaps in the actual delivery of care due to limitations in workforce skills, training and protocols.

Brief context on Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Viet Nam

Kyrgyzstan

Located in Central Asia, Kyrgyzstan has a population of 6.8 million million, with an average age of 26 years.46 Most of the population resides in rural areas (62.8%). The gross domestic product (GDP) per capita as of 2022 was US$1655.47 The country was part of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR) until 1991. Since then, the country has undergone several phases, reflecting broader national reform strategies and international influence to shift from a top-down approach to increased self-governance and accountability. The health system is mainly governed by the Ministry of Health and Social Development. The healthcare system is heavily reliant on hospitals, leading to underutilisation of primary healthcare (PHC) services. Investments have been focused on health facilities, information systems and electronic patient records. Despite achieving significant economic progress in the last decade, the country was notably impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic, resulting in increased socioeconomic challenges. The health system faces several challenges, including fragmented initiatives, lack of funding, lack of central coordination for quality improvements and ineffective data management. The ratio of doctors to population is much higher in urban areas and in the country’s capital than in rural areas; however, the ratio of nurses is adequate.36 Until 2022, no proper planning or strategy for the health workforce existed. Data from 2021 show 20.3 physicians and 49.8 nurses per 10 000 population, which was below the averages for the WHO European Region.36 Health workforce shortages are a critical issue, intensified by low remuneration and informal payment systems.

Tajikistan

Located in Central Asia, Tajikistan has a population of 10 million million, with an average age of 22.4 years.46 The percentage of the population residing in rural areas is 72.3%, representing the highest percentage among the three countries. The GDP per capita in 2022 was US$1054.47 Tajikistan was part of the USSR until 1991. Since its independence, the country has transitioned from a heavily centralised and input-based system towards a more output-oriented and performance-oriented model.37 The health system has largely relied on out-of-pocket payments, which have undermined equity and accessibility. The health sector is underfunded, which has hindered long-term strategies for strengthening PHC. Health financing mechanisms and reforms to improve health governance have been introduced. The country has a hospital-centric health service delivery model. Health care challenges include a critical shortage of health care workers and uneven health workforce distribution, due to migration and a lack of financial incentives. Data from 2021 show an average ratio of 76 doctors, nurses and midwives per 10 000 population, 37% lower than the WHO European Region average of 121 per 10 000 population.35

Viet Nam

Located in South-East Asia, Viet Nam has a population of 99 million million, with an average age of 32.5 years.46 The percentage of the population residing in rural areas is 61.9%. The GDP per capita in 2022 was US$4163.47 Though it was less affected by the COVID-19 pandemic compared with the other two countries, Viet Nam faces challenges related to rapid urbanisation and population ageing. The health system faces significant fragmentation, both at the policy level and in service delivery. The health care service and management system is organised into four administrative levels: central, provincial, district and commune.48 The country recognises the need for a shift towards integrated care to better manage chronic conditions and elderly care, which are prevalent due to demographic changes.49 However, health services follow a hierarchical public administrative structure, which is not conducive to integrated care.49 The country relies on hospital-centred care, resulting in inefficiencies in care continuity and high healthcare costs. Health worker shortages, uneven distribution and lack of skills for integrated care are present. Data from 2020 show 9.81 physicians and 2.88 pharmacists per 10 000 population.50

Structural quality

In all three countries, there are shortages of the health workforce. These shortages were attributed primarily to economic factors. In Tajikistan and Kyrgyzstan, health workforce salaries are lower than the national average salary.4 35 In Tajikistan, salaries for health workers working in hospitals are 23% lower than those working in PHC.35 In Viet Nam, although the salaries of health workers are comparable to other professional sectors, they are still insufficient given the highly demanding nature of the job.6

Low remuneration and lack of incentives have led to internal and external migration of the health workforce. In Tajikistan and Kyrgyzstan, the migration of health workers has mainly been to the Russian Federation due to the better pay, working conditions and career growth opportunities.36 37 In Viet Nam, health workers have left the health sector because of poor working environments, long working hours and overcrowding of facilities.6 Moreover, all three countries face an inequitable distribution of health workers, with rural and remote regions experiencing the greatest shortages. Additionally, the private health sector is growing and attracting more health workforce due to better working conditions, opportunities for professional development and the government’s encouragement of public-private partnerships.6 This also holds for Tajikistan and Kyrgyzstan.

Incorporating a gender perspective into NCD prevention and control is crucial, as it helps address the distinct health needs of both men and women across all age groups. Women, who represent almost 70% of the global health and social care workforce, often face the additional challenge of caring for others while encountering systemic barriers to their own NCD care, including prevention, screening and treatment. Gender dynamics have significantly influenced the patterns of the health workforce in all three countries. In Viet Nam, for instance, women make up the majority of MNCH staff at the district and commune levels. However, they encounter systemic barriers that hinder their access to leadership roles. Gendered expectations surrounding caregiving led to high workloads for female providers, particularly in tasks such as screening, counselling and patient follow-up during antenatal care.6 A similar situation exists in Kyrgyzstan, where low salaries and limited opportunities for career advancement have resulted in workforce attrition, often disproportionately affecting female staff.

In Viet Nam, the shortage of health workforce is further exacerbated by the inadequate supply of health workers from the educational system. Since 2015, the Government of Viet Nam has implemented several reforms to enable and catalyse innovations in medical education and integrate its educational programmes into a competency-based curriculum.38 However, challenges still exist due to large class sizes (400–600 medical students per year) and a low faculty-to-student ratio.38 In the other two countries, due to their political context as former Soviet countries, the educational system has been able to attract enough students to respond to the demands of the health sector. However, a shortage of health workforce still occurs due to the lack of retention of graduates in the health system.4 36 37

Process quality

The ministries of health in the three countries lead the development of national guidelines for preventing, diagnosing and treating NCDs, and determine which other parties will be involved in the guideline development process. Despite this central oversight, the process lacks a consultative approach and stakeholder involvement, meaning that input from various relevant stakeholders may not be considered during the formulation of policies. For example, in Tajikistan, the guidelines on the treatment of heart diseases in pregnant women were developed with input only from the cardiology association, without consultations with the association of obstetricians and gynaecologists, indicating a need for broader interdisciplinary consultations to optimise the quality and uptake of protocols and guidelines.5 There are noticeable gaps in policies and protocols related to integrated care and the appropriate training programmes for the health workforce to provide such care. These limitations are evident in the absence of multidisciplinary teams and concepts for joint patient management. The World Health Professions Alliance (WHPA) (representing the global organisations representing dentists, nurses, pharmacists, physiotherapists and physicians) states that primary healthcare covers so many facets of health and complex patient needs, it cannot be implemented by any single type of health worker alone.39 It requires a multidisciplinary collaborative approach involving qualified and regulated professionals, complemented by effective referral pathways to ensure patients receive the care they need from the right professional at the right time.

Findings from the three countries indicate that patients bypass the first level of care, leading to overcrowded hospitals due to inadequate quality of care, and a lack of competencies and skills of the health workforce at the PHC level.46 All three countries have reported a lack of continuous education and training opportunities for their workforce. In some cases, health workers had lost their skills over time, while in other cases, certain competencies and skills were never taught or not properly taught, emphasising the necessity for broader interventions extending beyond individual capacity strengthening, including system-level changes, to provide quality healthcare services.

In all three countries, the integration of NCD prevention and management into MNCH services remains more advanced at higher-level hospitals than at PHC facilities. For instance, in Kyrgyzstan, there is a lack of timely identification of pregnancy-related risk factors, routine hypertension screening and national protocols for the management of NCDs at the PHC level.4 Similarly, Tajikistan lacks protocols for managing conditions such as gestational diabetes, and medical records do not contain information on risk factors for NCDs or adequate monitoring of diabetes mellitus, even in cases of pregnancy with diabetes mellitus.5 In Viet Nam, most NCD screenings during pregnancy, such as hypertension, diabetes and screenings for breast or cervical cancer, are conducted at provincial or national hospitals, as PHC centres have limited diagnostic capacity and insufficient training.6

Moreover, to support the integration of NCDs into MNCH services, these countries are implementing digital health tools, although they are in their early stages. In Viet Nam, limited piloting of electronic medical records and telemedicine platforms has occurred at provincial hospitals, but PHC facilities often lack sufficient infrastructure or internet connectivity.6 In Tajikistan, ongoing donor-supported projects are exploring mobile platforms for maternal care tracking, but digital literacy among frontline providers remains a challenge.5 In Kyrgyzstan, efforts to link primary and hospital records are constrained by fragmented health information systems and under-resourced IT support.4

Strengthening structural and process quality

Fritzen40 discusses a variety of factors that influence health workers’ motivation and behaviour within the health system, which he categorises into ‘daylight’ or ‘shadow-side’ factors, distinguished by the degree to which they are overt and amenable to interventions. The factors that are directly related to the performance and motivation of healthcare workers are commonly known as daylight factors. These include such factors as salaries, performance management and growth opportunities, which align with structural quality.

Addressing such challenges requires a strategic approach to the attraction, recruitment and retention of health workforce professionals, especially within rural and remote settings. The WHO guideline on health workforce development, attraction, recruitment and retention in rural and remote areas41 advocates the development of policies that improve living conditions, ensure workplace safety, provide decent work, foster support networks, develop career pathways for all genders, facilitate knowledge exchange and elevate the profile of health workers in rural settings. In support of this, Positive Practice Environments,42 as defined by the WHPA Positive Practice Environment Campaign, are essential. These are healthcare settings promoting excellence and decent working conditions, helping to attract and retain staff, enhance patient care and strengthen health systems. The WHO Global Health and Care Worker Compact43 also emphasises the need to protect health workers’ rights and ensure safe, inclusive and discrimination-free workplaces, thereby promoting decent work and supportive environments, which are crucial for equitable, high-quality care.

Further, the educational system should be competency-based to equip students with skills to integrate social determinants of health and should be tailored to respond to the needs of the health system, disease burden, demographics, skills mix, labour division and service distribution. Improving structural quality, in this regard, will undoubtedly require interconnected, bundled interventions, designed and implemented in ways that respond to the scope of challenges and opportunities within each country.

However, there are also hidden or ‘shadow’ aspects of the organisational environment and cultural norms, like societal expectations and livelihood strategies that need to be acknowledged and addressed. These hidden factors can significantly motivate the health workforce to take on non-traditional roles and become agents of positive change within the health system. These two interlinked elements, where formal rewards interact with the underlying cultural values and organisational environment, influence health workers’ motivation and commitment to their role.40

Gender norms represent a critical ‘shadow-side’ dimension influencing how health workforce roles are perceived and distributed. Addressing structural and cultural barriers to gender equity is essential for workforce motivation, retention and progression. Additionally, in many LMICs, Community Health Workers (CHWs), who are considered a link between the health system and the community, are often expected to take non-traditional or expanded roles, such as NCD screening and counselling, without adequate preparation, supervision or support, which reduces the quality of care and risks patient safety. For example, in Vietnam, although CHWs are not formally integrated into NCD screening at higher levels, they play a role in health promotion, counselling and follow-up for high-risk women at the community level.6 These expanded expectations often reflect formal task-sharing policies (The rational redistribution of responsibilities among health workforce teams. Specific tasks or roles are shared, where appropriate, to less specialised health workers to make more efficient use of the available personnel. It should be accompanied by appropriate measures in terms of education, supervision, management support, licensing, regulation and remuneration44 due to systemic workforce shortages and informal gendered assumptions about caregiving roles. However, CHWs can play an important role in multidisciplinary teams, if suitably supervised by health professionals.

Addressing these gaps highlights the necessity to strengthen institutional capacities. This involves strategic initiatives in the process of recruitment, training and retaining diverse talent, with a focus on creating an environment for continuous learning and performance recognition that becomes a valuable reinforced part of the organisational culture.45 The transition requires shifting from an individual capacity approach, which emphasises personal skills and competencies, to an institutional capacity approach that focuses on structures, resources and organisational processes. This approach guarantees that the advantages of capacity strengthening remain within the institutions and are not lost with time or when health workers transition roles, exit the health sector or retire.

Interventions to strengthen health workforce capacities need to recognise the process of capacity strengthening systemically, as suggested by Potter and Brough,32 so the resources can be allocated, and the capacity needs addressed at the appropriate level to maximise benefits. Thus, capacity strengthening needs to be approached through a comprehensive strategy that addresses the entire life cycle of the health workforce, covering the spectrum from education (improving the pre-service education and postgraduate education curriculum) to the health labour market (in-service curriculum), rather than solely focusing on working individuals. Coordinated efforts across sectors, effective governance, sufficient funding, knowledge of context and needs in terms of workforce, and partnerships with the private sector and communities are also essential.31

The way forward: Research priorities

The global health community’s practical understanding of how to integrate NCDs into MNCH services is still nascent.19 20 Generally, national NCD strategies tend to focus on clinical protocols and service delivery. When they do address health workforce issues, greater attention is usually given to in-service training than to team design or pre-service education. In addition, national human resources for health plans rarely target NCDs directly. They are much more likely to address maternal and child health and infectious diseases such as HIV, malaria and tuberculosis.

Further research is required at the global and national levels to improve understanding and advance the implementation of integrated care models. At the global level, research should address the most frequent questions and challenges related to improving the capacity of the health workforce to provide NCD care within MNCH services. At the national level, research should focus on specific issues and challenges that are unique to a given context, or on which country decision-makers need information to make informed decisions and allocate resources. Incorporating primary data, like interviews with health workers or policymakers, is recommended for future work to better understand the challenges and opportunities in integrating NCD services into MNCH programmes.

Selection criteria for identifying priority areas for future research may include (a) the role that the research question plays in the integration of NCDs into hospital-based MNCH care, and at what level (global, national, local); (b) the cost and feasibility of the research; (c) the cost and feasibility of the potential solutions and (d) the perceived importance of the research question to all stakeholders.

Moving forward, many areas would benefit from additional research. These include the development of innovative models for integrating NCD care into MNCH services, approaches for effective training and capacity strengthening of the health workforce, and research into the impact of such integrated care models on patient outcomes and healthcare systems. Such research will offer meaningful insights into best practices, challenges faced in implementation and the scalability of successful models in different settings and populations.

To sustainably integrate NCD prevention, screening and treatment into MNCH, policymakers, implementers and system planners must move beyond isolated, short-term solutions and invest in coordinated, system-wide approaches. Strengthening both the structural and process dimensions of workforce capacity, through inclusive leadership, multisectoral collaboration and embedded learning systems, is essential to ensure quality, equity and sustainability in integrated service delivery. Countries aiming to advance universal health coverage must prioritise these foundational workforce investments as central to integrated, people-centred care.
