Evaluation of an integrated micro-skills training and clinical pathway model in surgical internship education
Authors: Yizhong Peng, Juanjuan Hu, Minjie Wang, Xing Zhou, Kaijian Bing, Song Li, Haiyan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical practical teaching (practical skills teaching) is a core component of the medical education system. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a synergistic integration of micro-skills training with the clinical pathway teaching method (CPTM) in surgical internship education.MethodsA total of 136 surgical interns were randomly assigned to an experimental group (synergistic micro-skills training-CPTM model) or a control group (traditional teaching). The experimental group received structured, pathway-guided training combined with video-based micro-skills training. Both groups underwent theoretical examinations, Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE), Mini-Clinical Evaluation Exercise (Mini-CEX), and Direct Observation of Procedural Skills (DOPS) assessments. A Likert-scale questionnaire was used to evaluate teaching satisfaction, skill improvement, and student engagement.ResultsThe synergistic model group demonstrated superior performance compared to the control group across all objective metrics. They achieved significantly higher scores in theoretical exams (79.13 ± 5.13 vs. 75.96 ± 5.28; mean difference 3.17, 95% CI [1.34–5.00]; Cohen’s d = 0.61, 95% CI [0.26–0.96]; P < 0.001) and OSCE (89.90 ± 4.00 vs. 84.99 ± 4.69; mean difference 4.91, 95% CI [3.35–6.47]; Cohen’s d = 1.11, 95% CI [0.74–1.47]; P < 0.001). Significant improvements were also observed across all domains of the Mini-CEX and DOPS assessments, as well as in questionnaire measures of satisfaction, clinical thinking, and engagement (all P < 0.05), with effect sizes ranging from moderate to large (Cohen’s d 0.36–1.59).ConclusionThe synergistic integration of micro-skills training and CPTM creates a powerful and effective educational model, significantly enhancing surgical interns’ theoretical knowledge, practical skills, clinical reasoning, and overall learning engagement. This integrated model provides a promising and innovative framework for modern surgical education.

Introduction

Surgical practical teaching (practical skills teaching) is a core component of the medical education system. Its fundamental goal is to help medical students efficiently improve their clinical operation skills and accumulate practical experience within a limited time, thereby systematically developing their comprehensive clinical competence (Dave et al., 2023). In China, medical undergraduates usually need to complete at least three years of systematic theoretical study before entering the clinical internship stage. However, the traditional teaching method, which focuses on demonstration and lecture, has increasingly shown limitations in meeting the complex and changing needs of clinical practice (Yang et al., 2023). The specific manifestations include students’ weak awareness of asepsis, insufficient mastery of operation details, and the relative shortage of clinical teaching staff, which makes it difficult to carry out targeted individualized guidance (Liu et al., 2023). These factors suggest that the traditional surgical practical teaching model may be insufficient to fully meet the evolving requirements of modern medical education for cultivating high-quality clinical talents.

With the continuous update of medical education concepts, a variety of advanced teaching methods have emerged, such as virtual reality video-guided learning, Train-The-Trainer curriculum, and artificial intelligence (AI)-guided surgical skills training. These methods have expanded the forms and boundaries of surgical practical teaching from different dimensions (Bradford et al., 2021; Fazlollahi et al., 2023; Vlachou et al., 2022). However, there is currently no practical teaching method that excels in both effectiveness and feasibility. Therefore, it is of urgent significance to explore an economical and easy-to-implement teaching method that can cover the entire cycle of theoretical teaching and practical operation.

In recent years, a distinct educational strategy has emerged that is particularly suited for procedural skill acquisition, that is video-based micro-skills training, denoted as micro-skills training. This approach draws on the broader concept of microlearning, which delivers educational content in small, focused units to reduce cognitive load and enhance knowledge retention (ALshammari, 2024; Román-Sánchez et al., 2023). When applied to psychomotor skills, microlearning is often implemented through short instructional videos that decompose complex procedures into discrete, observable steps. Learners can watch these videos repeatedly, practice each component, and receive structured feedback, a method that has shown effectiveness in fields such as computer programming (ALshammari, 2024), nursing education (Román-Sánchez et al., 2023), and vascular surgery (Qamar et al., 2025). Adapting this concept to surgical education, we define micro-skills training as a video-based, reflective approach in which students record their own performance of basic surgical procedures and engage in structured feedback. This model can be implemented based on digital technology. For example, tools such as mobile applications (m-learning), social media, or virtual reality (VR) platforms are used to digitally record the entire process of teaching and learning, thereby helping students fully review and reflect on their learning behaviors (Chandran et al., 2022; Fijačko et al., 2021; Nakhoul et al., 2024). This teaching method also lays a foundation for subsequent diverse teaching activities such as case discussions, group cooperation, and scenario simulations (Luo et al., 2021). Although micro-skills training has been applied in some clinical skills courses, it is still mostly carried out in traditional forms such as group discussions or micro-classes at present, and the potential of digital tools in supporting clinical practical learning has not been fully exploited (Geng, Zhu & Su, 2024; Román-Sánchez et al., 2023).

Clinical pathways are standardized diagnosis and treatment plans formulated for a specific disease or clinical problem. Their core goal is to transform international or national guidelines into specific operational procedures suitable for local medical scenarios (Muyama, Neuraz & Coulet, 2024). The clinical pathway teaching method (CPTM), based on clinal cases, introduces this concept into the clinical teaching process, and carries out teaching with the established clinical pathway as the framework, making the teaching process more professional, standardized, and systematic, thus effectively improving teaching quality (Chen et al., 2025; Tong et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). However, traditional clinical pathway teaching still faces certain challenges in practical application. For instance, it is not easy to control the teaching duration, and students’ teaching activities are easily intertwined with teachers’ clinical work, which may distract teachers’ teaching energy and affect students’ learning enthusiasm. To solve these problems, introducing a mechanism for real-time recording of the teaching process and supporting timely feedback and review has become an important way to improve teaching effectiveness.

Although micro-skills training has been applied in clinical skills training, its current implementation often relies on traditional formats such as group discussions or micro-lectures, with insufficient utilization of digital tools for structured, pathway-guided learning. Conversely, the CPTM provides a standardized diagnostic and treatment framework but faces challenges in real-time feedback, individualized skill practice, and student reflection due to its heavy reliance on instructor availability and clinical workload integration. When used in isolation, micro-skills training lacks the structured clinical context of a pathway, while CPTM lacks the granular, reflective skill reinforcement that micro-skills training offers. Therefore, integrating the two methods may overcome these respective weaknesses by embedding micro-skills training’s video-based, feedback-rich skill training within CPTM’s systematic clinical pathway framework, thereby providing both structure and personalized skill development.

This study investigates the organic integration of the micro-skills training method and the clinical pathway teaching method to construct a new teaching model. It aims to overcome the inherent limitations of traditional teaching by combining structured pathway guidance with micro-skills training, and use modern teaching methods to improve the learning efficiency and operation skills of intern students, thereby providing a new path for the overall improvement of medical education quality and the cultivation of clinical talent quality.

Materials & Methods

General information

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2025 Statement for randomized controlled trials (Hopewell et al., 2025). This study was approved by the ethics committee of Wuhan Union Hospital of Tongji Medical School of Huazhong University of Science and Technology [20230081-01].

A prospective sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1). The primary outcome measure was the theoretical examination score. Based on a pilot study conducted with 20 surgical interns prior to this trial (data not included in the main analysis), the expected mean difference between the experimental and control groups was 3.5 points, with a pooled standard deviation of 5.0, corresponding to a Cohen’s d of 0.7 (moderate to large effect). Using a two-tailed alpha of 0.05 and a power of 0.80, the required sample size was 52 students per group. Accounting for an estimated 20% attrition rate, we aimed to enroll 68 students per group. A total of 136 students who did their surgical internship at Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology from February 2023 to June 2024 were selected as the research subjects. They were divided into an experimental group and a control group with 68 students each using the random number table method.

Among the 68 interns in the control group, there were 40 males and 28 females. The maximum age was 24 years old, the minimum age was 21 years old, and the mean age was (22.22 ± 0.58) years. Among the 68 interns in the experimental group, there were 39 males and 29 females. The maximum age was 24 years old, the minimum age was 21 years old, and the mean age was (22.10 ± 0.55) years (Table 1). There was no statistically significant difference in the baseline data between the two groups, which met the requirements of this study. All research subjects gave informed consent and signed the informed consent form.

Randomization and allocation concealment

The unit of randomization was the individual intern. An independent research assistant (who was not involved in participant recruitment, baseline data collection, or outcome assessment) generated the random allocation sequence using a computer-based random number table (Microsoft Excel, RAND function). This was a simple randomization procedure.

To ensure allocation concealment, the independent research assistant placed group assignments (experimental or control) into sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. After a student signed the informed consent form and completed baseline demographic data collection, the research assistant opened the next envelope in sequence to reveal the group assignment. This process prevented both the enrolling staff and the students from knowing the allocation in advance, thereby eliminating selection bias.

Blinding

Outcome assessors (the clinical teachers who conducted the final theoretical examinations, Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE), Mini-Clinical Evaluation Exercise (Mini-CEX), and Direct Observation of Procedural Skills (DOPS) assessments) were blinded to group assignment. To achieve and maintain blinding, all assessment materials (e.g., examination papers, OSCE station instructions, video recordings of student procedures, and scoring sheets) were stripped of any group identifiers; students were identified only by study ID numbers. Assessors did not have access to the intervention status of any student, nor did they participate in the teaching process. No formal check of blinding success was performed, which we acknowledge as a minor limitation.

Due to the nature of the intervention (video recording of procedures and use of clinical pathway teaching forms), blinding of students and instructors was not feasible. This is a recognized limitation of the study, and the potential impact on performance and assessment is addressed in the Discussion section.

Instructors education

All instructors are attending surgeons with ≥5 years of clinical teaching experience. A 2-hour session was held one week before the start of the internship rotation. The session covered (a) overview of the study protocol and group assignments; (b) standard operating procedures for traditional teaching (control group); (c) detailed instruction on CPTM implementation, including use of clinical pathway forms and integration with daily clinical work; (d) micro-skills training techniques, including video recording protocols, feedback methods, and weekly video review discussion procedures; (e) avoidance of cross-contamination between groups. The training included a didactic lecture (45 min), case-based demonstrations (45 min), and a hands-on practice session (30 min) where instructors simulated a micro-skills training feedback session using a sample video. All instructors completed a brief competency check before the study began, which required them to correctly demonstrate (a) how to guide a student through video review, (b) how to provide structured feedback using the “feedback sandwich” model, and (c) how to complete the DOPS and Mini-CEX forms for a simulated student encounter. Instructors who did not meet the criteria (none in this study) would have received remedial training. A designated research assistant conducted random spot checks (unannounced, once every two weeks) during the internship to ensure instructors adhered to the assigned teaching model. No major deviations were identified.

Groups

Control group

Students in the control group completed a 16-week surgical internship following the traditional internship model (Fig. 1). Before starting the internship, students received the internship rotation plan and practical teaching schedule. On the first day of the internship, the surgical teaching and research section first conducted a centralized orientation for surgical interns. This orientation covered requirements for surgical internships, internship content, teaching formats, discipline, exit examination formats, and teaching evaluation. After the orientation, students were guided by teaching teachers from each department. The teaching content mainly involved clinical teachers instructing students to participate in daily clinical work. These activities were organized by the Surgery Teaching and Research Office and included small-group lectures, teaching ward rounds, case discussions, and skill operation courses. Each subspecialty was required to conduct at least one teaching ward round, one small-group lecture, and one case discussion per week to help students master basic knowledge of common and frequently-occurring diseases in that specialty. A total of 6–7 basic surgical skill operations covering essential procedures were arranged throughout the entire surgical internship. During the rotation in each subspecialty, students were required to complete 2 handwritten detailed medical records and undergo either a Mini-CEX (Mini-Clinical Evaluation Exercise) assessment or a DOPS (Direct Observation of Procedural Skills) assessment. Results of these assessments were fed back to students but not included in the statistical analysis of this study.

Study flowchart illustrating the design and implementation of the synergistic microteaching-clinical pathway teaching model compared to the traditional teaching model.

In the control group, participants received a standard orientation and followed the traditional apprenticeship model. Teaching was integrated into daily clinical work under the guidance of departmental teaching faculty. Formative assessments (Mini-CEX/DOPS) were conducted during rotations, with results provided as feedback. The outcome measures were the final summative assessments: a theoretical exam, an Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE), Mini-CEX, and DOPS, followed by a satisfaction questionnaire. In the experimental group, participants received the same standard orientation plus specific instruction on the Clinical Pathway Teaching Method (CPTM) and microteaching. Their training was structured around predefined clinical pathways for core diseases in each subspecialty. The microteaching component involved video recording of skill demonstrations by teachers and performances by students, followed by repeated self-review, written reflection, and weekly group video review sessions with facilitator feedback. Formative assessments (Mini-CEX/DOPS) were also conducted during rotations. The same suite of final summative assessments and questionnaire was administered to evaluate outcomes.

The total structured teaching time for the control group was approximately 8–10 h per week (including teaching ward rounds, small-group lectures, and case discussions). The control group received no clinical pathway teaching forms and no video-based micro-skills training (i.e., no recording of procedures, no video review, no weekly video discussion sessions). Their skill learning relied solely on traditional demonstration and supervised practice without structured reflection or feedback loops. Apart from these differences, both groups participated in the same daily clinical work, patient care, and orientation, and underwent the same final assessments.

Experimental group

The experimental group adopted a teaching model combining micro-skills training and CPTM (Fig. 1). To reduce bias in teaching delivery, all instructors were trained on the study protocol and encouraged to adhere strictly to the assigned teaching model.

Pre-internship preparation: One week before the internship, the surgical internship rotation plan was finalized. Clinical pathway teaching forms and practical teaching schedules were developed and sent to students.

Internship orientation: On the first day of the internship, the surgical teaching and research section conducted the same centralized surgical internship orientation as the control group. Meanwhile, students received department-specific clinical pathway teaching forms covering diseases in each subspecialty: Orthopedics: Femoral fractures, lumbar disc herniation, lumbar spinal stenosis, scoliosis, pelvic tumors, anterior cruciate ligament tears; Urology: Kidney stones, ureteral stones, renal cell carcinoma, prostate cancer; Hepatobiliary Surgery: Obstructive jaundice, cholelithiasis, liver cancer, cirrhotic portal hypertension; Gastrointestinal Surgery: Intestinal obstruction, gastric cancer, massive upper gastrointestinal bleeding, colon cancer, rectal cancer; Pancreatic Surgery: Chronic pancreatitis, pancreatic cancer, obstructive jaundice; Breast and Thyroid Surgery: Thyroid cancer, breast cancer; Neurosurgery: Brain tumors, craniocerebral injury, intraspinal tumors; Thoracic Surgery: Lung cancer, esophageal cancer, mediastinal tumors; Students were required to familiarize themselves with the diagnosis and treatment processes of these diseases.

Subspecialty teaching arrangement: When the teaching secretary of each subspecialty conducted the department-specific orientation, they emphasized the learning and application of clinical pathway forms. They also informed students that micro-skills training would be used for skill training and assigned teaching teachers. Each subspecialty rotation lasted 2 weeks.

Clinical pathway model structure and integration: The clinical pathway teaching forms were adapted from the Notice on Issuing Clinical Pathways for Relevant Diseases (2019 Edition) issued by the National Health Commission. For each disease, the pathway form outlined the following sequential steps: admission criteria, history and physical examination, diagnostic tests, treatment plan, surgical indications (where applicable), perioperative care, discharge criteria, and follow-up. Students used these forms throughout the patient’s hospital stay. The integration with micro-skills training was achieved by aligning video-recorded skill demonstrations (e.g., wound dressing) with the corresponding step in the clinical pathway (e.g., postoperative wound care). Weekly video review discussions explicitly referenced the clinical pathway step being taught that week.

Micro-skills training application: Under the guidance of teaching teachers, students learned to screen patients eligible for clinical pathway enrollment. Based on the clinical pathway content, they raised questions about the case, covering the entire process from initial diagnosis through inquiry, differential diagnosis via supplementary examinations based on symptoms, treatment plans, surgical indications, to discharge. After class, students researched relevant materials to gain in-depth understanding of the disease. During clinical diagnosis and treatment, micro-skills training was mainly applied to inquiry and surgical skill operations, including wound dressing change, gastric tube insertion, and catheterization. Each skill module was structured as follows: (a) a 10-minute live demonstration by the teaching teacher while students recorded the procedure using their mobile phones; (b) students practicing the skill under supervision while being recorded; (c) students reviewing their own video recordings at least three times after class, completing a structured reflection form; and (d) a weekly 30-minute group discussion (per ward) where students shared their reflections and received summary feedback from the teacher. Teachers provided summary guidance to ensure students mastered the key operation points and integrated humanistic care into clinical practice. The pedagogical methods employed included observation, deliberate practice, self-reflection, and structured feedback. To control for total learning time, the experimental group’s micro-skills training activities (video recording, review, and weekly discussions) replaced equivalent amounts of unstructured clinical observation and traditional demonstration time. The overall weekly structured teaching hours remained 8–10 h, identical to the control group. Hence, the two groups were balanced for time-on-task.

Internship duration and assessment

Both the experimental and control groups completed rotations in eight surgical departments, with 2 weeks per department, totaling 16 weeks. During the internship, after each department rotation, students underwent a Mini-CEX or DOPS assessment. Results were fed back to students but not included in the study’s statistics. After completing rotations in all eight subspecialties, both groups uniformly took Mini-CEX assessments, DOPS assessments, theoretical exit examinations, and practical skill examinations. These results served as the observation indicators of this study. A questionnaire survey was distributed after the assessments.

Mini-CEX: The Mini-CEX assessed seven domains, each scored on a 9-point scale (1–3 unsatisfactory, 4–6 satisfactory, 7–9 superior): Medical Interviewing (nine points): Logical history taking, use of open-ended questions, active listening, and efficiency in gathering relevant information. Physical Examination (nine points): Systematic approach, correct techniques, patient comfort, and identification of relevant findings. Humanistic Competence (nine points): Empathy, respect for patient dignity, attention to patient concerns, and effective communication. Clinical Judgment (nine points): Formulation of differential diagnoses, appropriate selection of investigations, and interpretation of results. Explanation and Patient Education (nine points): Clarity of communication, ability to explain diagnosis and treatment plans, and shared decision-making. Organizational Efficiency (nine points): Time management, prioritization of tasks, and workflow during patient encounters. Overall Performance (nine points): Global rating of the student’s clinical competence during the encounter.

DOPS: The DOPS assessed seven domains, each scored on a 6-point scale (1–2 below expected, 3–4 meets expected, 5–6 above expected): Humanistic Competence (six points): Patient comfort, respectful communication, and attention to patient dignity during the procedure. Relevant Knowledge (six points): Understanding of indications, contraindications, steps, and potential complications of the procedure. Preparatory Work (six points): Correct patient positioning, equipment preparation, and verification of informed consent. Patient Safety (six points): Adherence to safety protocols, infection control measures, and prevention of adverse events. Technical Skills (six points): Instrument handling, operative technique, and overall procedural proficiency. Asepsis Concept (six points): Maintenance of sterile field, proper hand hygiene, and use of sterile gloves/instruments. Post-procedure Management (six points): Completion check, proper specimen handling (if applicable), documentation, and post-procedure patient instructions.

Questionnaire: The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study. Its content was informed by a literature review and expert consensus (three senior surgical educators). Content validity was assessed by five independent experts who rated each item for relevance and clarity; the Scale-Level Content Validity Index (S-CVI) was 0.92, indicating excellent content validity. A pilot test was conducted with 20 surgical interns (not included in the main study), and internal consistency was good (Cronbach’s α = 0.87). The final questionnaire comprised five core items (overall satisfaction, interest in learning practical skills, progress in clinical thinking and practical skills, participation in teaching activities, and sufficiency of hands-on practice opportunities), each rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very dissatisfied/very inactive to 5 = very satisfied/very active). Two open-ended questions (strengths/weaknesses of teaching methods and memorable events) were also included to collect qualitative feedback. The full questionnaire is provided as a supplementary file.

All assessors (clinical teachers who conducted the final examinations) underwent a 90-minute training session that included reviewing the checklists, watching and rating three standardized video-recorded student performances (one for each assessment type), and discussing scoring discrepancies until consensus was reached. A mid-study refresher session (30 min) was conducted after the fourth subspecialty rotation to prevent rater drift.

Observation indicators

Scores of theoretical examinations and surgical skill examinations: After the internship, the surgical teaching and research section organized exit examinations, including a surgical theoretical examination and a practical skill examination. Both groups took theoretical examinations of the same difficulty level, which met the requirements of the teaching syllabus. The practical skill examination is based on the objective structured clinical examination (OSCE) (Khan et al., 2013). The content and evaluation criteria of OSCE were consistent for both groups, assessing students’ mastery of basic surgical knowledge and skills. Both examinations were scored out of 100.

Scores of Mini-CEX and DOPS assessments: After completing the 8th subspecialty rotation, students randomly selected disease types and surgical skills for assessment. Based on the selected disease type, students performed patient inquiry and physical examination. Teachers conducted Mini-CEX assessments on both groups to test students’ mastery of the inquiry process and related knowledge. Surgical skill operations were video-recorded; teachers watched the videos and scored them according to DOPS criteria to evaluate students’ proficiency in basic surgical operations. Both assessment methods were used to ensure the reliability of the results.

Questionnaire survey: The questionnaire covered satisfaction with teaching methods, improvement in practical skills and clinical thinking, student participation, and utilization of teaching resources. It was completed anonymously to investigate teaching satisfaction. The survey used a 5-point Likert scale: 5 points for “very satisfied”, 4 points for “satisfied”, 3 points for “moderately satisfied”, 2 points for “dissatisfied”, and 1 point for “very dissatisfied”.

Statistical analysis

All assessments were conducted by experienced clinical teachers who were not involved in the daily teaching of the participants to avoid assessment bias. To evaluate the adequacy of this sample size, we performed a post-hoc power analysis based on the primary outcome (theoretical examination scores). With the observed mean difference of 3.17 (79.13 vs. 75.96), a pooled standard deviation of approximately 5.2, a two-tailed alpha of 0.05, and equal group sizes (n = 68 per group), the achieved power exceeded 85%. Before applying parametric tests, we verified the underlying assumptions. Normality of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test for each comparison. All continuous outcome variables (theoretical examination scores, OSCE scores, Mini-CEX domain scores, DOPS domain scores, and questionnaire items) met the assumptions for parametric testing (Shapiro–Wilk p > 0.05 for all groups; Levene’s test p > 0.05 for all between-group comparisons). Therefore, no non-parametric alternatives or robust methods were required. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. Categorical data were expressed as n (%), and the chi-square test (χ2 test) was used for comparison. Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x¯ ± s), and the independent samples t-test was used for comparison. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Results of theoretical and OSCE

After the internship, the average theoretical examination score of students in the experimental group was significantly higher than that of the control group (79.13 ± 5.13 vs. 75.96 ± 5.28; mean difference 3.17, 95% CI [1.34–5.00]; Cohen’s d = 0.61, 95% CI [0.26–0.96]; P < 0.001). In terms of OSCE, the experimental group also achieved a significantly better score (89.90 ± 4.00 vs. 84.99 ± 4.69; mean difference 4.91, 95% CI [3.35–6.47]; Cohen’s d = 1.11, 95% CI [0.74–1.47]; P < 0.001) (Table 2). These results indicate that the teaching model combining micro-skills training and CPTM can effectively improve students’ mastery of theoretical knowledge and proficiency in practical skills.

Mini-CEX assessment results

The Mini-CEX assessment results showed that the experimental group scored (7.38 ± 0.75) in medical interviewing, which was higher than the control group’s (6.88 ± 0.89) (mean difference 0.50, 95% CI [0.21–0.79]; Cohen’s d = 0.60, 95% CI [0.25–0.95]; P = 0.001); in physical examination, the experimental group achieved a significantly better score (8.06 ± 0.73) compared with the control group’s (6.51 ± 1.15) (mean difference 1.55, 95% CI [1.21–1.89]; Cohen’s d = 1.59, 95% CI [1.20–1.98]; P < 0.001); and in the dimension of humanistic care, the experimental group also scored significantly higher (7.90 ± 0.74) than the control group (7.12 ± 1.03) (mean difference 0.78, 95% CI [0.47–1.09]; Cohen’s d = 0.86, 95% CI [0.50–1.22]; P < 0.001). Additionally, the experimental group showed significant advantages in clinical judgment (7.96 ± 0.68 vs. 7.50 ± 0.91, mean difference 0.46, 95% CI [0.19–0.73]; Cohen’s d = 0.57, 95% CI [0.22–0.92]; P = 0.001), patient education and explanation (8.50 ± 0.82 vs. 7.90 ± 1.02, mean difference 0.60, 95% CI [0.28–0.92]; Cohen’s d = 0.65, 95% CI [0.30–1.00]; P < 0.001), organizational efficiency (7.87 ± 0.73 vs. 7.47 ± 0.87, mean difference 0.40, 95% CI [0.12–0.67]; Cohen’s d = 0.50, 95% CI [0.15–0.85]; P = 0.005), and overall performance (7.99 ± 0.56 vs. 7.75 ± 0.74, mean difference 0.24, 95% CI [0.01–0.47]; Cohen’s d = 0.36, 95% CI [0.01–0.71]; P = 0.039) (Table 3). These results comprehensively reflect the improvement of students in the experimental group in all aspects of comprehensive clinical competence.

DOPS assessment results

Analysis of the DOPS assessment results showed that the experimental group scored significantly higher than the control group in operation-related knowledge (5.50 ± 0.53 vs. 4.87 ± 0.88, mean difference 0.63, 95% CI [0.37–0.89]; Cohen’s d = 0.83, 95% CI [0.47–1.19]; P < 0.001), pre-operation preparation (5.32 ± 0.70 vs. 4.78 ± 0.94, mean difference 0.54, 95% CI [0.26–0.82]; Cohen’s d = 0.64, 95% CI [0.28–1.00]; P < 0.001), patient safety (5.29 ± 0.71 vs. 4.90 ± 0.99, mean difference 0.39, 95% CI [0.10–0.68]; Cohen’s d = 0.45, 95% CI [0.09–0.81]; P = 0.009), technical competence (5.03 ± 0.65 vs. 4.71 ± 0.73, mean difference 0.32, 95% CI [0.09–0.55]; Cohen’s d = 0.46, 95% CI [0.11–0.81]; P = 0.007), aseptic awareness (4.96 ± 0.63 vs. 4.63 ± 0.81, mean difference 0.33, 95% CI [0.09–0.57]; Cohen’s d = 0.45, 95% CI [0.09–0.81]; P = 0.010), and post-operation management (4.81 ±0.63 vs. 4.56 ± 0.74, mean difference 0.25, 95% CI [0.02–0.48]; Cohen’s d = 0.36, 95% CI [0.01–0.71]; P = 0.036). Notably, there was no statistically significant difference in the score of humanistic literacy between the two groups (5.59 ± 0.60 vs. 5.47 ±0.61, mean difference 0.12, 95% CI [−0.09 to 0.33]; Cohen’s d = 0.20, 95% CI [−0.14 to 0.54]; P = 0.261) (Table 4), which may be due to the fact that students in both groups paid attention to doctor-patient communication and humanistic care during clinical practice.

Questionnaire survey results

The results of the teaching feedback questionnaire showed that the satisfaction score of the experimental group with the teaching method was (4.46 ± 0.61), significantly higher than that of the control group (3.93 ± 0.72) (mean difference 0.53, 95% CI [0.30–0.76]; Cohen’s d = 0.78, 95% CI [0.42–1.14]; P < 0.001). In terms of competence improvement, the experimental group had significantly better evaluations than the control group in the improvement of practical skills (4.56 ± 0.63 vs. 4.12 ± 0.82, mean difference 0.44, 95% CI [0.19–0.69]; Cohen’s d = 0.59, 95% CI [0.23–0.95]; P = 0.001) and clinical thinking (4.59 ± 0.58 vs. 4.01 ± 0.50, mean difference 0.58, 95% CI [0.38–0.78]; Cohen’s d = 1.08, 95% CI [0.71–1.44]; P < 0.001), and the student participation rate of the experimental group (4.63 ± 0.52) was also significantly higher than that of the control group (3.90 ± 0.79) (mean difference 0.73, 95% CI [0.50–0.96]; Cohen’s d = 1.07, 95% CI [0.70–1.43]; P < 0.001). However, there was no statistically significant difference in the evaluation of teaching resource utilization between the two groups (4.15 ± 0.74 vs. 4.24 ± 0.63, mean difference −0.09, 95% CI [−0.33 to 0.15]; Cohen’s d = −0.13, 95% CI [−0.47 to 0.21]; P = 0.454) (Table 5), indicating that the allocation and use of teaching resources were basically consistent between the two groups.

Discussion

Overall efficacy of the integrated model

Comparison of the surgical internship exit examination results showed that the experimental group achieved higher scores in both the theoretical exit examination and the practical skill examination than the control group, indicating that the combination of micro-skills training and CPTM can effectively improve students’ mastery of basic surgical knowledge and skills. A questionnaire survey of interns in the two groups revealed that compared with the traditional teaching method, students had a higher recognition of the teaching model integrating micro-skills training and CPTM.

Mechanisms and component actions (micro-skills training and CPTM)

CPTM is a highly recognized and scientific teaching approach. Its advantage lies in being based on well-developed clinical pathway templates and standardized diagnosis and treatment processes, which can effectively improve the clinical diagnosis and treatment behaviors of interns. From admission to prognostic rehabilitation, it provides interns with the most intuitive diagnosis and treatment process, effectively bridging the transition from textbook knowledge to practical patient care (Gao et al., 2025). CPTM can effectively align with the learning needs of interns, enabling them to complete corresponding learning content and achieve learning objectives within specific timeframes, thus avoiding the randomness and omissions of teaching content that may occur in traditional teaching (Naqib et al., 2018). At the same time, it can effectively optimize students’ time management and allow for efficient use of internship time (Wang et al., 2024). Micro-skills training miniaturizes courses, standardizes skill operations, decomposes and visualizes the teaching process (via audio and video), and closely integrates theory with practice, thereby enriching teaching formats (Román-Sánchez et al., 2023). In surgical training specifically, microlearning videos of less than three minutes have been used to teach end-to-side anastomosis, leading to significant improvements in resident performance and high learner satisfaction (Palmon et al., 2021; Qamar et al., 2025; Wakam et al., 2022). The integration of micro-skills training and CPTM allows for accurate positioning of interns’ learning needs, improves teaching effectiveness, offers greater flexibility and convenience in scheduling, strengthens practical operations, and incorporates formative assessment and evaluation with timely feedback (Qamar et al., 2025). This integration effectively enhances interns’ comprehensive surgical skills and enables the achievement of personalized learning goals for students. A study by Tong et al. (2023) found that the clinical pathway teaching method has significant effects in terms of communication skills in patient–doctor interactions, ability to record medical behavior, ability to guide hospitalized patients, and ability to manage and control diseases. These results are consistent with those obtained from the application of the micro-skills training-CPTM integrated model in clinical practical teaching.

Detailed discussion of assessment results (Mini-CEX and DOPS)

Mini-CEX and DOPS assessments are forms of formative evaluation and competence-oriented assessment systems, which can provide timely feedback on students’ mastery of clinical knowledge (Lörwald et al., 2018). Mini-CEX focuses on the logic and coherence of residents’ clinical thinking during clinical diagnosis and treatment, assessing their abilities in patient information collection, diagnostic and treatment thinking, clinical diagnosis and prognosis, and overall performance (Zaki et al., 2023). The Mini-CEX results showed that the experimental group outperformed the control group across all seven domains, particularly in physical examination, clinical judgment, and patient education. This indicates that clinical pathway teaching helps interns gradually develop a sound clinical thinking framework and adopts a problem-oriented approach to train students’ ability to proactively identify and solve problems, and enhances students’ initiative, divergent thinking, and exploratory skills in learning (Tervajärvi, Hutri-Kähönen & Rautiola, 2021). Additionally, CPTM clarifies the teaching objectives for instructors, makes teaching content clearer, aligns clinical teaching more closely with actual clinical practice, and standardizes teaching processes (Macke et al., 2022). This effectively reduces the guidance time required for instructors, enables targeted teaching for students, and improves teaching efficiency.

DOPS assessments can effectively evaluate both the objective and subjective abilities of interns (Naeem, 2013; Weimer et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2025). Similarly, DOPS results demonstrated superior technical skills, aseptic awareness, and post-procedure management in the experimental group, indicating that video-based micro-skills training with repeated review and feedback enhances procedural competence. Scientific and effective teaching methods help interns adapt better to their roles and improve internship efficiency (Roberts, 2020). Together, these findings confirm that the integrated model improves both cognitive and technical aspects of surgical training.

Non-significant findings and their implications

The absence of a statistically significant difference in humanistic literacy between the two groups warrants further consideration. One possible explanation is a ceiling effect (Jenks et al., 2023): both groups received basic training in doctor-patient communication and humanistic care during their clinical rotations, and the baseline level of humanistic competence may have been relatively high among all interns, leaving limited room for improvement. Additionally, the DOPS assessment of humanistic literacy relies on direct observation of discrete procedural encounters, which may not fully capture nuanced aspects of empathy, respect, and patient-centered communication that unfold over longer clinical interactions (Weimer et al., 2023). Future studies could incorporate more sensitive tools, such as patient-reported experience measures or video-recorded longitudinal encounters, to detect finer-grained differences in humanistic competencies between teaching models.

Study limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study, which may limit generalizability to other institutions. Although we performed a prospective sample size calculation confirming adequate power for the primary outcome, secondary outcomes may be less optimally powered. Besides, the follow-up was limited to the 16-week internship; long-term sustainability of the observed effects remains unknown. In addition, despite using validated tools (Mini-CEX, DOPS, OSCE), they may not capture all dimensions of clinical competence, and our self-developed questionnaire requires further validation in other settings. Potential biases such as the Hawthorne effect (students performing better due to novelty) and performance bias (non-blinding of participants and instructors) cannot be completely excluded, although assessor blinding and randomization helped mitigate detection and selection bias. In the application of micro-skills training, video recording was conducted solely in hospital wards. Due to environmental and resource constraints, the recorded videos may have been affected by interference or poor quality. It is recommended that in future teaching of basic surgical clinical skills, recording locations be shifted to skill training centers, and scenario simulation teaching be introduced to ensure teaching environment quality and teaching effectiveness (Childs, Manganiello & Korets, 2019). Additionally, video recording and playback in micro-skills training consume significant class time. Currently, the small number of students per subspecialty group allows the existing teaching process to function smoothly. However, an increase in the number of participating students would increase teaching workload, requiring the development of reasonable teaching plans or the addition of class hours (Wade et al., 2024). The scalability of the intensive micro-skills training component remains a major challenge for broader adoption. To address this, future implementations in resource-limited or large-class settings could consider the following strategies: (1) establishing a shared video library of standardized skill demonstrations to reduce repeated real-time recordings; (2) incorporating peer-assisted learning, where senior students or trained teaching assistants facilitate video review and feedback sessions; (3) adopting asynchronous online platforms to offload faculty time and allow students to complete reflective exercises flexibly. Furthermore, the quality of clinical pathway design affects the teaching process. Unreasonable clinical pathways may compromise teaching quality and even mislead students’ clinical thinking (Neubauer, 2020). Therefore, institutions should invest in regular updates and local adaptations of clinical pathways based on the latest evidence and local practice standards.

Based on the findings and limitations of this study, we offer the following practical guidelines for institutions considering adoption of the synergistic micro-skills training-CPTM model: For educators: Begin with a shared video library of standardized skill demonstrations to reduce real-time recording burden. Incorporate peer-assisted feedback (e.g., senior students as facilitators) to scale micro-skills training activities without excessive faculty workload. For hospital administrators: Prioritize phased implementation in high-volume core specialties (e.g., orthopedics, general surgery) before expanding to all rotations. Ensure clinical pathways are regularly updated and locally adapted to maintain teaching quality. For policymakers: Support the development of national or regional clinical pathway templates tailored for teaching purposes. Provide resources for faculty development programs on micro-skills training techniques and formative assessment methods such as mini-CEX and DOPS.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that integrating micro-skills training with the clinical pathway teaching method produces a synergistic effect in surgical practical teaching, significantly improving both theoretical knowledge and clinical skills among surgical interns. Beyond the immediate educational gains, this integrated model holds transformative potential for surgical medical education by offering a structured, scalable, and feedback-rich framework that bridges the gap between textbook learning and real-world clinical practice. To facilitate broader adoption, educators and institutions should consider the practical recommendations, including phased implementation, shared video resources, peer-assisted feedback, and regular clinical pathway updates. Future research should explore long-term outcomes and adaptation across different cultural and resource settings.
