Participatory systems modelling to improve mental health systems in Bogotá, Colombia: stakeholders’ perspectives, experiences and networks
Authors: Adriane Martin Hilber, Salvador Camacho, Mónica Natalí Sánchez Nítola, Dèbora L. Shambo-Rodríguez, Grace Yeeun Lee, Laura Ospina-Pinillos, Jo-An Occhipinti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParticipatory Systems Modelling (PSM) offers an opportunity to deliver robust, contextually relevant, and suitable models for informing strategic responses to complex public health challenges. For mental health care systems, PSM benefits from multi-sectoral participants’ social and emotional capital for building consensus on priorities and policy objectives that can influence decision-making.MethodsIn Bogotá, Colombia, we evaluated a PSM process that developed an interactive decision support tool that can inform policymaking on the selection of programs and services to mitigate social, economic, and health system drivers of youth mental health outcomes including suicidal behaviour. Using the CHaRL Framework, we explored changes in perceptions or beliefs of participants on the functionality of the (mental health) system and its drivers. Multi-sectoral participants including youth with lived experience participated in three workshops to collectively inform the building of the model. Pre-post-tests, a survey and observations of the PSM process were conducted.ResultsThrough the participatory process, participants increased knowledge and understanding of the mental health care system, its drivers and constraints of performance for youth in Bogotá. Due to active engagement, a credible and comprehensive tool was created, reflective of the complexity of the system, and focused on what participants and evidence show are high priority interventions. Participants’ concerns around trust in the model shifted positively over the course of the workshops. Skepticism in policymakers’ capacity to use the model increased participant recognition of the need to advocate for its use.ConclusionParticipatory processes help to overcome stakeholder hesitancy by offering an important starting point for building trust and partnership for effecting change in health systems. Consideration of participant bias at the start of the process could strength the validity and use of the results in the longer term.

Background

The systems science field has increasingly used Participatory Systems Modelling (PSM) approaches to inform policy making that are appropriate, relevant and evidence informed. In the field of mental health policy making, however, participatory systems modeling is relatively new with few assessments of the process conducted. This evaluative study aims to help fill this critical gap by exploring changes in participant learning, perceptions and knowledge that came about through the PSM process.

Systems science provides structured approaches to understanding complex public health challenges by examining the causal linkages that drive outcomes [24]. Within this discipline, systems modelling enables analysis of outcomes shaped by interacting factors at individual, social, and structural levels [29]. For complex issues such as mental health, systems modelling integrates diverse data and evidence into causal model that allows the simulation of programs and initiatives before they are implemented in the real world, thereby informing strategic decision making [2, 3].

Collaboratively developing system dynamics models with stakeholders enhances contextual relevance, transparency, and responsiveness to emerging evidence ([15]; Király & Miskolczi, 2019 [21]; [1]). Stakeholder participation, including policymakers, creates a deliberative space for building consensus on priorities, strengthening social capital and increasing the likelihood that modelling insights inform real-world policy and practice [5]. It further creates a peer review process to validate the emerging model, giving credence to the new priorities identified by the group and reflected in the model itself [11]. Similar observations [28] have been made using a community-based system dynamics (CBSD) process to build stakeholders’ capacity to learn and address complex problems collaboratively to generate knowledge across sectors and policy spheres.

PSM has been widely applied in environmental and health systems research, including in areas such as obesity, tobacco control, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and substance use ([1, 35]; Atkinson, 2018 [2]). More recently, PSM has demonstrated value in mental health planning, where participant engagement brings professional and lived experiences into discussions, but also personal, social and normative beliefs (and priorities) about how and where care is delivered [26]. Facilitation approaches that encourage reflection and storytelling enable incorporation of social-emotional factors, improving both participant learning and model credibility [4, 10, 13, 14]. Engaging diverse actors—from community members to policymakers and young people with lived experience of mental health conditions (or their carer) often brings contrasting visions of the system, highlighting its strengths and weaknesses as well as differing opinions on where improvements are needed [12]. This ensures that models capture system realities, inform service design, and provide credible platforms for policy dialogue.

A participatory systems modelling project on youth mental health in Bogotá, Colombia

Between 2021 and 2022, a PSM project [31] was conducted in Bogotá, Colombia, to guide investments and policymaking aimed at reducing youth suicide and improving mental health outcomes. Using a PSM approach, multidisciplinary stakeholders collaboratively designed a system dynamics model to identify key outcomes, drivers, and priority interventions for adolescent wellbeing and suicidal behavior. The model was informed by best available evidence and adapted from a sister project protocol from the Brain and Mind Centre in Australia [14, 22, 23, 29] The international collaboration involved the Computer Simulation & Advanced Research Technologies (CSART) at The University of Sydney, the Swiss Tropical and Public Health Institute, and Pontificia Universidad Javeriana, in partnership with Colombian stakeholders.

The PSM process involved three workshops with participants from health, education, and social sectors including both government, academic and civil society actors. The first workshop introduced systems thinking concepts, and then collectively identified and mapped key drivers (e.g., education, violence, access to care), and possible interventions. The second workshop presented a preliminary model (based on the drivers and interventions elaborated by the participants in Workshop 1) for feedback on structure, assumptions, and data sources, prompting discussion of causal pathways and intervention effects. In the third workshop, a revised model was presented that incorporated the perspectives of the participants related to the causal pathways and interventions from Workshop 2. It also included the available evidence, both from Colombia, the region and beyond to ensure the model was evidence based. In Workshop 3, participants reviewed and interacted with a near-final model, testing combinations of interventions and refining the interface and parameters. Further modifications were then made, and a final model was created and later shared for policy deliberation [31].

Critically, the PSM process relied on collaboration between researchers, policymakers, clinicians, community actors, and young people (and their carers) with lived experience of mental ill-health, alongside others familiar with Bogotá’s mental health and social systems. This diverse engagement ensured that the model reflected multiple perspectives and practical realities of service delivery and lived experience. Concretely, this was done iteratively throughout the process – as questions were raised, or concerns voiced, the group would then discuss how to address or mitigate the issue. For example, the initial model problematized mental health in the negative as ill-health – a notion the Colombian mental health community rejected preferring instead to focus on advancing mental health positively. The reframing of the discussion then led to the addition of variables that captured the contextual realities of Colombian youth that contribute to mental health generally. Once developed, simulation analyses compared projected mental health outcomes under various intervention scenarios against a status quo baseline. Findings indicated that without substantial change, youth suicide and self-harm rates would continue to rise over the next decade. The combination of school-based suicide prevention and gatekeeper training emerged as the most effective intervention strategy. For more information on the model see Ospina-Pinillos et al. 2024.

Learning from the participatory process

The evaluation of the PSM process sought to document the evolution of stakeholder perceptions of both the mental health system and PSM as a valid, policy-informing approach. Drawing on frameworks used in previous studies in Australia ([14]; [22]), we assessed stakeholder learning and perceptions before, during, and after the three participatory workshops. This design enabled examination of changes in understanding, beliefs, and perceived value of the PSM process in relation to youth mental health in Bogotá.

We hypothesized that the participatory process and prioritization exercise would change stakeholder knowledge and beliefs about the adolescent mental health care system and its functionality. We also hypothesized that the participation in the workshops might change participant perceptions of other actors in the sector, create potential for new partnerships, and innovation for improving mental health care for children and adolescents in Bogotá. The later hypothesis was explored through a social network analysis, reported separately (See Camacho et al., [6].

This article reports on the results of the evaluation of the PSM project. The evaluation assessed stakeholder learning, changes in perceptions and knowledge about adolescent mental health, and views on the model’s validity and utility for decision-making within Colombia’s mental health system. The evaluation fills a knowledge gap in understanding how a PSM process is appreciated by various stakeholders and the evolution of stakeholder perceptions throughout the PSM process.

Evaluation approach and methodology

Evaluation frameworks for PSM have evolved from assessing process efficiency and representativeness [18, 22, 25] to examining participant learning and socio-emotional dimensions. Zorilla et al., (2010) [34] and later Maskrey et al., [27] went further by developing process evaluation criteria for public participation that assesses participants’ evolving understanding of the system, including themes related to satisfaction, representativeness, responsiveness and trust. A few years later, Hamilton et al., [18] developed a more complex framework for evaluating processes, outputs and outcomes in a workshop format. During this period, emphasis shifted to put more emphasis on what participants themselves bring to the process, and how the process affects their views. This was well captured in Smajgl and Ward’s [34] Challenge and Reconstruct Learning (CHaRL) framework that focuses on how participants’ beliefs, perceptions, and decision-making evolve through engagement, measuring changes in understanding and influence on the model over time.

Our evaluation of the PSM process intervention applies an adapted version of the Challenge and Reconstruct Learning (ChaRL) Framework developed by Smajgl and Ward [34] to assess changes in participants’ perceptions and beliefs about the functionality of the mental health system and its drivers (see Fig. 1). The ChaRL Framework integrates concepts from complex systems theory, decision-making constructs, and the influence of learning (e.g., values, beliefs, and attitudes). It aims to understand how participatory processes foster an evolution in decision-making—beginning with participants’ initial expectations and beliefs, which are then shaped by evidence and analytical engagement, potentially leading to shifts in their perspectives and valuation of the resulting model or product.

Measuring this change involves both pre- and post-assessments of participant views and qualitative observations of how participants influenced, or were influenced by, the process. We selected this approach over more theoretical, criteria-driven frameworks because it emphasizes the evolution of participants’ understanding—from ex ante to ex post—in relation to the agreed objectives of the PSM process. In the adapted ChaRL model, participant’s value of the approach (Pending Decisions) and expectations are recorded before and after engagement at both the individual and group levels. These data are triangulated with documented and observed changes in stakeholder beliefs (mental models of the PSM process), coupled with the evidence generated collectively, that is then reflected in their confidence in the modelling process [34].

Based on the CHARL framework, we used mixed methods to explore the research questions including stakeholder’s expectations and learning from the PSM process both for them personally, and for the organisations or community where they work or live. We also explored stakeholder views of the mental health system, asking them to contribute to an analysis of the opportunities and challenges that exist from their perspective, and then collectively prioritise interventions they felt would mitigate some of the problems. Finally, we also explored through the workshops, stakeholders’ perspectives of the model investigated including: (i) model credibility; (ii) model utility; and (iii) perceived capability of the model to influence decision making and policy.

Evaluation methodology

Participant recruitment was informed by rapid stakeholder mapping to identify key actors across the mental health system. Eligible participants were those involved in service delivery, planning, research, or policymaking related to child and adolescent mental health in Bogotá. A total of 152 stakeholders were invited, including policymakers; clinical and non-clinical providers across care levels; academics; representatives from education, child protection, and other associated sectors; private and non-governmental actors (e.g., NGOs, insurers, and advocacy groups); and young people or caregivers with lived experience. Despite COVID-19 restrictions, there was strong interest, and 78 stakeholders participated in at least one workshop of the process.

Evaluative data was collected through pre and post semi-structured questionnaires and survey, and direct observations (see Fig. 2). Before participating, 41 stakeholders completed baseline interviews to capture initial perceptions and expectations, and 57 completed a pre-workshop survey. Following the final (third) workshop, 53 participants completed a post-survey. For the primary analysis, we included only those who attended all three workshops and consented to participate in evaluation activities (N = 24).

The survey questionnaire complemented the interviews asking similar questions to validate the qualitative data; it also contained additional questions to collect social network data from the stakeholders before and after the participatory workshops. The survey was created using the “RedCap” software and distributed by digital channels to all the participants before workshop 1, and after the workshop 3. In addition, two trained evaluators recorded systematic observations throughout the workshops, capturing verbal and non-verbal cues, group dynamics, and critical reflections shared during plenary and small-group sessions. These observations were supplemented by notes and feedback collected by the facilitation team.

All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed, checked for accuracy, and anonymized. The qualitative data were analyzed using Atlas.ti 8 software, following the principles of grounded theory [7, 8]. Coding and analysis were conducted iteratively, allowing categories and themes to emerge and evolve as data collection progressed. Constant comparison techniques were used to explore relationships between themes and triangulate findings from interviews, surveys, and observations.

Seventy-eight participants attended at least one of the three workshops. The initial workshop was attended by 57 participants; the second workshop by 42; and the third workshop by 54 attendees. A total of 26 respondents attended all 3 workshops and agreed to be included in the final analysis (N = 24). Together they represented 7 stakeholder group categories across the 3 workshops (see Fig. 3 below); and 25 of them also answered pre/post surveys. Learning from the participatory model building process

At baseline, most participants expected to learn how to construct a model (n = 20; 87%); at endline, 83% (n = 20) confirmed they had done so. Observational data indicated that participants valued the process for providing a “collaborative learning platform”, where expertise and experiences were exchanged and integrated into model design.

Confidence in the process

Initial skepticism stemmed from limited understanding of systems modelling and data availability. Over time, observations showed a shift from doubts about the model itself to lack of confidence in policymakers to commit sufficient political will and resources to make the changes to the system called for by the model – a shift in skepticism from the model to the human level factors that impede implementation.

Discussion

This evaluation explored stakeholder perceptions of a PSM approach, conducted using a workshop format, to inform youth mental health decision-making in Bogotá, Colombia. It examined the perceived value of participation, learning outcomes, and how engagement influenced stakeholder views of the model’s usefulness and potential to improve the mental health system. The evaluation was unique in capturing learning from participant interactions around a sensitive issue among diverse actors with differing perspectives of the problem and solutions. The PSM workshop approach and process helped participants to understand additional aspects of the mental health system, care pathway protocols, and, in many instances, the dysfunction that limits support for youth in need.

Using the “CHaRL framework”, the evaluation traced changes in participants’ perceptions of the causal pathways within the mental health system in Bogotá. Expectations regarding individual and organizational learning evolved as participants engaged in collective discussions and use of the data-driven model to explore solutions. This process generated confidence in the model and increased its perceived validity. For example, over the course of the workshops, through dialogue, voices of participants with lived experience appeared to hold greater weight than that of the mental health professionals, lending credence to school and community-based interventions over clinical services that proved to be most impactful in reducing suicide ideation and positive mental health outcomes.

PSM stimulates learning and knowledge creation

The participatory model-building process served as a “knowledge mobilization platform”, enabling iterative sharing of experience and evidence to create a contextually grounded model [14]. Participants deepened their understanding of system performance and the complexity of evidence-informed decision-making. Their active engagement enriched the model’s contextual accuracy and scope beyond what a desk-based exercise could achieve. Consistent with [19]; outcomes were “multi-directional, complex, highly relational and context dependent” – all necessary to capture the complexity of the Bogotá mental health system.

The participatory process

While participatory processes are not new in Colombia’s health sector, systems modelling represented an innovative application. In Bogotá, participation was widely supported, aligning with the Latin American tradition of participatory decision-making embedded in democratic processes ([33]; OECD, 2021. Colombia’s history of participatory action research [17] provided fertile ground for PSM, with stakeholders expecting to see their interests reflected in the model’s outcomes.

Initially, discussions were dominated by expert voices, in part because systems modelling was not intuitive for many participants and the purpose of the model was not immediately clear. This challenge was compounded by the technical language used to explain the model, which initially inhibited some participants to fully engage creating a hierarchy of participation in which ministry officials and academics led early discussions. During plenary sessions, evaluators and the facilitator actively observed these power imbalances and sought to mitigate their effects by encouraging all perspectives to be shared. As workshops progressed, facilitation strategies—including adapting the language used, mixed-group activities, smaller group work, and targeted feedback sessions—helped participants gain confidence, equalize participation, and foster more inclusive engagement across stakeholder groups.

Testimonies from caregivers and young people were particularly influential, grounding discussions in lived experience and shifting the focus from abstract debate to concrete needs. As engagement broadened, debates emerged around the role of communities in service provision, the contextual barriers faced by consumers, and the realities of when formal service protocols fail, demonstrating the growing influence of diverse stakeholders on the final model. Policymakers also expressed strong interest in applying the model to decision-making, leading to post-workshop discussions with the Bogotá Health District Department on capacity building and sustaining the approach. Overall, these dynamics underscore the intrinsic value of stakeholder participation in producing credible and robust models to inform policymaking, corroborating previous findings (Frerich et al., 2015; Király & Miskolczi, 2019 [20]).

Value of participatory systems modelling

Participants came to see themselves as “co-creators” of the model, guiding contextual adjustments and refining language and priorities. Community stakeholders advised that in Colombia, discourse emphasizes “mental health and wellbeing” rather than illness, and drew attention to social determinants—poverty, unemployment, education, and violence—that shape youth mental health. Recognizing these intersectional factors strengthened the model’s relevance and increased ownership as stakeholders had a sense of pride that their issues and expertise were a driver of the model.

Intentions to use PSM to inform decision-making

Stakeholders’ confidence in the model’s potential to forecast and inform decision-making grew over time. Initial hesitation reflected unfamiliarity with PSM, but by the third workshop participants viewed the model as a credible and useful decision-support tool. While participants acknowledged that the model itself cannot drive policy change without political commitment, they agreed it could identify effective and cost-efficient intervention strategies.

By the project’s conclusion, skepticism shifted from “the model’s reliability” to “concerns about political will” to act on its findings. Some participants, particularly policymakers, providers, and academics, expressed enthusiasm for using the model to improve system efficiency, while others doubted that policymakers would implement its recommendations, particularly because the interventions found to be most effective were ones localized at community level (e.g. school-based prevention programs) which requires public sector investments. Despite this, interest from local and national government agencies during dissemination events suggested strong institutional potential for uptake.

Stakeholder trust emerged as a key determinant of whether the model would be socialized and adopted. The PSM process provided a foundation for collective engagement and strategic system improvement, but implementation will depend on ongoing advocacy from the Bogotá mental health community. Future PSM projects should explicitly plan for dissemination and policy engagement as measures necessary for success.

Implications for PSM in LMIC contexts

Global disparities in mental health resources in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have been recognized since the ‘Lancet’ “Global Mental Health” series (Horton, 2007), which spurred the “Movement for Global Mental Health” [9]. While the movement emphasized evidence-based and rights-oriented care, critics have highlighted the lack of inclusivity and local participation [20]. They argue that “when communities actively participate in the governance, design or delivery of mental health interventions, they can be more acceptable and relevant to local needs as well as more cost-effective and of higher quality” [32]. They go on to stress that the process of participation can in itself be transformative and empowering. A recognized challenge both in the literature, and in our PSM evaluation is the role of power dynamics within the group of participants and the pre-existing biases, and scepticism they bring to the process [16]. To ensure participatory processes add value to the exercise, and are not skewed to participant bias, it may be necessary to explore participant perceptions and values early in the exercise to try and create a more level and open basis upon which to build a model intervention that can be implemented within the context by the stakeholders engaged in the PSM creation process.

Limitations

A key limitation was limited quantitative data—only 41 of 53 participants completed the endline survey—though this was mitigated by rich qualitative and observational data. Low survey response may have reflected time constraints and pandemic-related workload pressures. The workshops occurred as Colombia was emerging from COVID-19 restrictions, affecting both participation and willingness for in-person engagement.

The research team minimized participant burden by limiting activities outside workshop hours, which may have reduced opportunities for deeper follow-up but preserved high engagement during sessions. Despite these constraints, substantial insights were obtained from pre/post interviews and observation notes. Further exploration of how stakeholder roles and participation levels influenced learning would provide valuable understanding of how perceptions evolve through PSM.

Conclusion

This study evaluated participatory systems modelling (PSM) from the participant perspective, focusing on engagement, expectations, learning, and skepticism. Although it did not assess how shifts in participant perceptions directly influenced the model’s technical components, qualitative findings suggest that community-informed insights increased support for prevention-oriented and community-based interventions. The CHaRL framework proved useful in capturing both individual and collective learning outcomes; however, future evaluations would benefit from multi-level analyses that encompass actors across the broader mental health ecosystem. In this regard, the Comprehensive Multi-Scale Evaluation Framework for participatory modelling programmes represents a promising approach for further testing [22].

Overall, this PSM process evaluation demonstrates that stakeholder participation is essential for developing credible and contextually relevant models to inform policy decision-making, particularly in sensitive domains such as mental health. The Bogotá PSM initiative functioned as an effective platform for eliciting stakeholder expertise and generating insights to strengthen the city’s youth mental health system. Familiarity with participatory approaches supported active engagement and collective learning, and over the course of the workshops participants grew more confident in their contributions. Initial skepticism regarding the model’s realism gradually gave way to a sense of ownership and pride in co-producing a comprehensive, evidence-informed representation of Bogotá’s youth mental health system. Stakeholder involvement also enhanced the perceived validity and strategic relevance of the model’s findings.

Despite these gains, sustaining confidence in the model’s policy impact will require continued efforts to promote its socialization, institutional adoption, and multi-sectoral buy-in—particularly among policymakers. Ongoing skepticism related to political will, regulatory constraints, and intergroup distrust highlights that even highly participatory models cannot, on their own, overcome entrenched systemic barriers. Ensuring balanced representation across stakeholder categories, including individuals, families, and community organizations, therefore remains critical to fostering equitable participation and trust.

As Bogotá’s first PSM initiative, the participatory process represented an important initial step toward building trust and interdisciplinary collaboration within the city’s mental health system. It established a foundation for sustained partnership and policy dialogue. Encouragingly, following dissemination events, both the Ministry of Health and Social Protection and the Bogotá Health District Department expressed commitment to using the model to guide decision-making. Continued advocacy for the model’s application presents an opportunity to translate collaborative learning into concrete, evidence-based improvements in youth mental health care and overall system performance across Bogotá.
