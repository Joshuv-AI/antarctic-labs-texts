Impact of socioeconomic factors on cardiovascular diseases, diabetes and all-cause mortality in patients with uveitis: a Danish cohort study
Authors: Marie Ørskov, Henrik Vorum, Oliver Niels Klefter, Yousif Subhi, Steffen Heegaard, Maria Vittoria Cicinelli, Jimmi Wied, Piergiorgio Neri, Lasse Jørgensen Cehofski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeUveitis is a group of intraocular inflammatory conditions that may result in severe visual impairment. The potential interplay between uveitis and socioeconomic factors remains largely unexplored. This study investigated the association between socioeconomic factors and the risk of cardiovascular disease, diabetes and all-cause mortality in patients with uveitis.DesignNationwide cohort study.MethodsAll patients registered with a uveitis diagnosis at a Danish public hospital between 2000 and 2018 were included and stratified into low, intermediate or high educational or household income level. Data from different registers were linked using a personal identification number. Using the high levels as the reference, we estimated incidence rates and cumulative incidence. We quantified the differences using HRs with 95% CIs for cardiovascular disease, diabetes and all-cause mortality through Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsIn a cohort of 18 225 patients with uveitis, we observed increased rates and risks of all-cause mortality, peripheral artery disease, myocardial infarction, heart failure, ischaemic heart disease, stroke, and diabetes for lower educational or income level. The following HRs (95% CI) were elevated in patients with uveitis with low or intermediate socioeconomic status: all-cause mortality (low status: education: 1.58 (1.30 to 1.90); income: 2.09 (1.70 to 2.59); intermediate status: income: 1.56 (1.25 to 1.94)), diabetes (low status: education: 1.72 (1.33 to 2.23); income: 2.15 (1.65 to 2.79)), peripheral artery disease (low status: education 2.87 (1.77 to 4.68); intermediate status: income: 2.11 (1.35 to 3.30)), myocardial infarction (low status: education: 2.13 (1.32 to 3.44); intermediate status: income: 2.29 (1.40 to 3.75)), heart failure (low status: education: 1.99 (1.33 to 2.96); income: 2.05 (1.35 to 3.11)), ischaemic heart disease (low status: education: 1.62 (1.24 to 2.11)), and stroke (low status: income: 1.70 (1.19 to 2.43)).ConclusionPatients with uveitis with lower educational or income level faced a statistically significantly increased risk of cardiovascular disease, diabetes and mortality. Alongside the direct burden of uveitis, socioeconomic status should also be accounted for when assessing patient risk and designing management strategies.Keywords: education, Cardiovascular Diseases, Epidemiology, Public Health, Sociodemographic Factors

Introduction

Uveitis comprises a group of sight-threatening diseases caused by intraocular inflammation affecting the uveal tract, including the iris, ciliary body and choroid.1 Uveitis affects all ages, including children, and accounts for 5–10% of visual impairment worldwide.2 3 The incidence rate of uveitis is estimated at 24.9 per 100 000 person-years.4

Uveitis is classified as either infectious or non-infectious. Non-infectious uveitis can be associated with systemic autoimmune diseases but is often idiopathic.5 In chronic non-infectious uveitis, the persistent activation of immune pathways leads to elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, potentially promoting endothelial dysfunction. The systemic inflammatory burden in chronic non-infectious uveitis is a driver of atherosclerosis, increasing the risk of cardiovascular diseases. Previous studies have identified uveitis as a risk factor for myocardial infarction and arterial hypertension in patients with ankylosing spondylitis, likely through atherosclerotic mechanisms.6 7 Furthermore, uveitis associated with autoimmune diseases has been linked to an increased risk of atherosclerosis-related cardiovascular disease.8

Despite these findings, the broader relationship between uveitis and cardiovascular disease remains largely unexplored. In patients with retinal vein occlusion, we previously identified an association between socioeconomic factors and cardiovascular disease.9 Given the complexity of uveitis management, addressing cardiovascular risk factors may present additional challenges for affected patients. Socioeconomic status encompassing variables like educational attainment, income, occupation, employment status, social class, and geographic location is a potential determinant in the prognosis of cardiovascular events in patients with uveitis. However, no previous reports have examined the impact of educational or household income level on cardiovascular risk and mortality in this population. Using nationwide data from Danish registries, we investigated the association between socioeconomic determinants in patients with uveitis and the risk of cardiovascular diseases, diabetes and all-cause mortality.

Methods

Data sources

This cohort study used data from various Danish health registries, linked together through the personal identification number assigned to every Danish resident at birth or upon migration.10 Data sources included the Civil Registration System, which provided demographic details such as age, sex, cohabitation status and citizenship, and the Danish National Patient Register, which contains comprehensive records of diagnoses, medical procedures and surgeries performed at public hospitals.11 12 Additionally, the Danish National Prescription Database offered individual-level information on prescription medications dispensed by Danish pharmacies.13 The study also used data from the Income Statistics Registry, detailing household income, and the Population Education Registers, which recorded individuals’ educational attainment.14 15

The validity of the use of uveitis diagnosis codes from the Danish National Patient Register has been previously assessed, with a positive predictive value of 92.2% (95% CI 90.7 to 93.5%), indicating high reliability for research use.16

Data availability

The data is not publicly available as access to Danish national register data requires project-specific approval from the relevant Danish authorities. Danish law prohibits the deposit of person-level data. The data from the registers was pseudo-anonymised before access, making linking of data from different registers possible while averting identification of individual patients.

Settings

The study included all adult patients (≥18 years) with a diagnosis of uveitis in the Danish public healthcare system between 1 January 2000 and 31 December 2018. Patients with uveitis were included if they had a 10th revision of WHO’s International Statistical Classification of Disease and Related Health Problems (ICD-10) code for uveitis, including DH200, DH201, DH208, DH209, DH300, DH301, DH302, DH308, DH309 or DH441E. Subjects were excluded from the population if they were <18 years, died on the index date, immigrated to Denmark within the last year or had inconsistent demographic information. The index date was defined as the first date a uveitis diagnosis assigned in a Danish public hospital during the study period. Socioeconomic factors, including educational attainment and household income, were assessed at the index date and used as separate exposure variables. Each factor was treated independently, without combining education and income into a single composite measure (online supplemental figure S1).

Educational attainment was categorised into three levels, with the lowest group comprising individuals whose highest education level was primary school, the intermediate group including those who had completed high school or vocational education and the highest group consisting of individuals with higher education qualifications. Household income was stratified into tertiles, with each tertile representing low, intermediate and high household income level, respectively. Patients with missing values for a specific exposure were excluded from analyses involving that exposure but could still be included in other analyses if data were available. Patients were followed until the outcome of interest, the competing event of death or censoring due to emigration or administrative end of follow-up at 31 December 2018. The primary outcomes were all-cause mortality and major cardiovascular diseases, including stroke, myocardial infarction, peripheral artery disease, venous thromboembolism, atrial fibrillation, ischaemic heart disease, heart failure and diabetes. These disease classifications were based on ICD-10 diagnosis codes and Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical codes for prescription drugs. A complete list of disease classifications is available in online supplemental table S1.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics were summarised using descriptive statistics, with categorical variables presented as proportions and continuous variables as means with SD. Time-to-event analyses were conducted to assess the relationship between socioeconomic factors and the risk of developing cardiovascular diseases over a 5-year follow-up period from the index date. For each investigated outcome, all prevalent cases were excluded. Follow-up ended at the occurrence of death, emigration or the study’s conclusion (31 December 2018), whichever occurred first. The cumulative incidence of cardiovascular diseases was estimated using the Aalen-Johansen method, accounting for death as a competing risk, while overall mortality was assessed using Kaplan-Meier estimates.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate differences between socioeconomic groups. These analyses were performed both unadjusted and adjusted for age and sex, with age treated as a categorical variable in 10-year intervals. Furthermore, a model adjusted for all the investigated outcomes was performed. For income as exposure, a model adjusted for educational level was generated. The model adjusted for sex and age represented the total effect of the exposures on the outcomes, while the model adjusted for all the investigated outcomes represented an approximation of the direct effect of the exposure on the outcome. To ensure the validity of the Cox proportional hazards model, the proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and log-minus-log plots.

Clinically significant associations were defined as an HR change of at least 10%, either increasing or decreasing, when comparing socioeconomic groups using Cox models. The analysis focused on identifying robust differences with potential clinical implications.

Results

The study included 18 225 patients diagnosed with uveitis identified between 1 January 2000 and 31 December 2018 (table 1). The mean age at diagnosis was 49.2 years, with 52% of the cohort being female. Education and household income were distributed across the exposure groups as follows: 29% of the uveitis population had primary education, 40% had completed high school or vocational training and 26% had attained higher education. For household income, the lowest, intermediate and highest groups accounted for 26%, 25% and 26%, respectively. Missing values for educational level and household income were present, accounting for 4.5% and 23% of observations, respectively.

The overall rate during the 5-year follow-up period for the investigated outcomes ranged from 0.24 (95% CI 0.21 to 0.28) for myocardial infarction as well as peripheral artery disease to 1.47 (95% CI 1.39 to 1.56) for all-cause mortality. The same outcomes represented the lowest and highest overall cumulative incidence ranging from 1.2 (95% CI 1.0 to 1.3) for myocardial infarction and peripheral artery disease to 7.1 (95% CI 6.7 to 7.5) for all-cause mortality (online supplemental table S1). For all the investigated outcomes, rates and risks were higher in patients with uveitis with the low or intermediate educational or income level compared with those with the highest educational or income level during the 5-year follow-up period (table 2). For the 5-year follow-up period, elevated HRs were observed for all-cause mortality (low status: education: HR 1.58, 95% CI 1.30 to 1.90; income: HR 2.09, 95% CI 1.70 to 2.59; intermediate status: income: HR 1.56, 95% CI 1.25 to 1.94); diabetes (low status: education: HR 1.72, 95% CI 1.33 to 2.23; income: HR: 2.15, 95% CI 1.65 to 2.79); myocardial infarction (low status: education: HR 2.13, 95% CI 1.32 to 3.44; income: HR 2.07, 95% CI 1.24 to 3.46; intermediate status: income: HR 2.29, 95% CI: 1.40 to 3.75); heart failure (low status: education: HR 1.99, 95% CI 1.33 to 2.96; income: HR 2.05, 95% CI 1.35 to 3.11); peripheral artery disease (low status: education: HR 2.87, 95 % CI 1.77 to 4.68; intermediate status: income: HR 2.11, 95% CI: 1.35 to 3.30); ischaemic heart disease (low status: education: HR 1.62, 95% CI 1.24 to 2.11) and stroke (low status: income: HR 1.70, 95% CI 1.19 to 2.43) (table 3). These associations all exceeded a 10% increase for the lowest limit of the CI when comparing the lower educational or income levels with the highest educational or household income level. Furthermore, the observed associations persisted across all the conducted models. The lowest income group was associated with ischaemic heart disease and myocardial infarction, and the intermediate educational level was associated with peripheral artery disease and myocardial infarction, but these associations were not confirmed after adjustments for all investigated outcomes (table 3).

Discussion

Our findings indicate that patients with uveitis with a lower educational or household income level appeared to have a higher risk of adverse outcomes, including cardiovascular events, diabetes and all-cause mortality compared with those of higher educational or household income level.

Uveitis is a complex condition requiring individualised treatment, often involving multiple therapeutic approaches before optimal management is achieved.17 Successful disease control requires frequent follow-up visits and strict adherence to treatment, which may be more challenging for patients with lower socioeconomic status. Patients with lower socioeconomic status may have significant challenges in managing uveitis due to the complexity of treatment. Frequent follow-up visits and adjustments in therapy require time and financial resources, which can be particularly challenging for those with limited access to transportation or inflexible work schedules. This may lead to delayed treatment, poorer disease control and an increased risk of complications. The systemic inflammatory burden associated with uveitis may contribute to an increased cardiovascular risk. However, the added strain of managing uveitis could also interfere with adherence to treatments aimed at reducing cardiovascular risk factors, compounding the overall disease burden.18

The impact of immunosuppressive therapy on long-term health outcomes should also be considered. Many patients with uveitis, particularly those with non-infectious autoimmune forms, require systemic immunosuppression, including corticosteroids, antimetabolites, biologics or calcineurin inhibitors. These therapies have been associated with an increased risk of infection, and metabolic complications, potentially influencing mortality risk. Prolonged corticosteroid use, for instance, has been linked to diabetes, osteoporosis and cardiovascular disease.19

Denmark’s universal healthcare system provides equal access to medical care, making disparities in treatment availability an unlikely explanation for the observed differences in outcomes.20 Instead, factors such as delayed healthcare-seeking behaviour, comorbidities and lifestyle differences may contribute to the heightened cardiovascular risk observed in groups of lower socioeconomic status. In health systems without universal coverage, the financial burden of uveitis treatment can significantly exacerbate socioeconomic status-related disparities. Out-of-pocket costs for specialist consultations, diagnostic tests and expensive treatments are likely to place a disproportionate financial burden on patients of lower socioeconomic status. In contrast, universal healthcare systems aim to reduce these barriers by providing broader access to necessary treatments regardless of socioeconomic status.

Replicating this study in countries with different healthcare models and demographic compositions could offer further insights into the broader relationship between uveitis, socioeconomic status and cardiovascular disease risk. The generalisability of these results may be influenced by the specific healthcare model of the country in question.

A key strength of our study is the large cohort size and the comprehensive nature of Danish health registers, which capture data on all Danish residents, ensuring full coverage in terms of uveitis diagnoses and cardiovascular outcomes. One limitation of our study is the inability to distinguish between infectious and non-infectious uveitis, two distinct clinical entities with potentially different systemic health implications. A previous study found that approximately 90% of the Danish uveitis cases were non-infectious.16 Non-infectious uveitis, often autoimmune in nature, is associated with chronic inflammation, which could drive the increased cardiovascular and metabolic risks observed in this cohort. In contrast, infectious uveitis, which may arise in immunocompromised individuals, likely follows a different risk trajectory. Although we were unable to differentiate these subtypes, it is possible that a small subset of patients with infectious uveitis may have experienced increased mortality or cardiovascular risk due to underlying immunodeficiency. Additionally, it was not possible to stratify based on severity or duration of active inflammation. This study included patients with a hospital diagnosis of uveitis. In the Danish system, the majority of patients are examined by a general ophthalmologist who refers complex or severe cases to hospital for work-up. Patients with uveitis included in our study were all referred to a hospital setting having a higher proportion of moderate and severe cases of uveitis. Conversely, the patients with uveitis of our study may not be representative of mild uveitis cases with short-lived active inflammation.

Moreover, missing data on education and household income are more prevalent among immigrants and older individuals, thereby limiting the contribution from these groups.

Another common limitation in epidemiological studies is residual confounding. Adjustment for all confounders in the statistical models, is almost impossible. We conducted different adjusted models to investigate whether the associations remained in all the models.

Finally, our study may be limited by the dynamic nature of socioeconomic factors. While education is typically established early in life and remains relatively stable, household income fluctuates over time due to occupational changes and civil status variations. By incorporating education and income separately, we accounted for both early-life socioeconomic status and the financial situation at the time of uveitis diagnosis. The consistency of our findings across both measures suggests that socioeconomic status is a reliable predictor of future cardiovascular risk in patients with uveitis.

Conclusion

Patients with uveitis with lower educational and household income levels had increased rates and risks of all-cause mortality, diabetes and several cardiovascular diseases.

Our findings highlight the increased susceptibility of cardiovascular disease among uveitis patients with low socioeconomic status. Socioeconomic background should be recognised as a key factor in risk stratification, emphasising the need for targeted preventive measures and tailored management strategies to optimise cardiovascular health in this vulnerable population.
