Treatment satisfaction and patient preference for efanesoctocog alfa over previous hemophilia A treatment: Results from the XTEND-1 phase 3 clinical trial
Authors: Emil Spasov, Doris Quon, Katsuyuki Fukutake, Annemieke Willemze, Jennifer Dumont, Helena Palmborg, Nana Kragh, Emilie Gerard, Amanda Wilson, Jérôme Msihid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTreatments for Hemophilia A include prophylaxis with factor VIII (FVIII) replacement therapy. With standard and extended half-life therapies, patients still experience bleeds, and administration 2–4 times a week is needed, which affects adherence. Efanesoctocog alfa is a once-weekly FVIII replacement therapy approved to treat and prevent bleeding in patients with hemophilia A for all age groups.ObjectivesTo analyze patient treatment preferences and satisfaction comparing once-weekly efanesoctocog alfa with pre-study prophylaxis in adults and adolescents with severe hemophilia A in the pivotal Phase 3 XTEND-1 study (NCT04161495).MethodsExploratory post-hoc patient-reported outcomes were assessed by treatment preference at Week 52 (via a treatment preference survey) and patient satisfaction at baseline and up to Week 52 (via the 9-item Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaire for Medication [TSQM-9], covering: global satisfaction, effectiveness and convenience domains). Relationships between treatment satisfaction and preference versus bleeding and region were also explored.ResultsOf 130 patients who completed the preference survey, 90.0% preferred efanesoctocog alfa versus their previous prophylaxis regimen. Similar trends were observed by region. The most common reasons were ‘less frequent treatment’, ‘bleeds and bleed-related complications reduction’, and ‘feel better protected’. Of 115 patients who completed the TSQM-9 at Week 52, there was a statistically significant improvement from baseline in all three domains. By study end, an increase in the proportion of patients selecting the “best response” for all TSQM-9 items was observed. There were no strong correlations with treatment satisfaction improvements at Week 52 and improvement in quality of life or bleeding episodes.ConclusionsProphylactic efanesoctocog alfa was preferred to prior prophylactic regimens by most patients and improved patient satisfaction at 52 weeks.

Introduction

Hemophilia A is a rare, X-linked genetic bleeding disorder resulting from insufficiency or dysfunction of factor VIII (FVIII), a critical coagulation protein.1 Defects in these coagulation factors result in impaired clot formation causing bleeding episodes mostly affecting soft tissue, joints, and muscles.1 Individuals with severe hemophilia A can experience frequent and spontaneous hemorrhages, which cause debilitating pain and joint deterioration, resulting in reduced mobility and a poor quality of life.1, 2 Spontaneous bleeds can be life threatening.3

For patients with severe phenotype hemophilia A, prophylaxis is recommended to prevent bleeding; this includes clotting factor concentrates and non-factor therapies.3 According to the guidelines for the management of hemophilia published by the World Federation of Hemophilia (WFH), hemophilia care aims to prevent and treat bleeding episodes by replacing the deficient clotting factor.3 As such, one recommended treatment for hemophilia A includes intravenous replacement therapy with clotting factor concentrates (CFCs).3 Standard and extended half-life CFCs for prophylactic treatment are typically administered 2–4 times per week,3 leading to a substantial treatment burden on people with hemophilia and may affect adherence.4,5 This has been partly addressed by the development of a once weekly high-sustained CFC that achieves FVIII levels in the normal to near normal range for most of the week.6, 7

Patient satisfaction with treatment is important, and is influenced by attributes such as safety, bleeding protection, and dosing frequency; the latter has been reported as an important factor in treatment decision-making.8, 9, 10 In a cross-sectional, multicenter, observational study of 505 patients with hemophilia A, the frequency of drug administration had a significant negative correlation with treatment satisfaction (P < 0.05).11 Furthermore, in a survey of 63 people with hemophilia A, missed, forgotten, or delayed doses were reported by 46.4% of patients.12 It is important to understand the experiences of people living with hemophilia A, and their preferences for current treatments to fully inform treatment decisions and optimize levels of patient satisfaction.8, 9, 10, 11

Efanesoctocog alfa, a first-in-class high-sustained FVIII replacement therapy, is approved by the FDA, EMA and Pharmaceutical and Medical Devices Agency (PDMA) for use in all age groups with hemophilia A for once-weekly prophylaxis (50 international units [IU]/kg body weight),13, 14, 15 based on results of the pivotal Phase 3 trials XTEND-1 (NCT04161495)16 and XTEND-Kids (NCT04759131).17

The primary endpoint of the XTEND-1 trial was annualized bleeding rate (ABR). In this post-hoc analysis, we report the findings of exploratory endpoints evaluating the treatment preference and treatment satisfaction of once-weekly efanesoctocog alfa compared with pre-study prophylaxis in adults and adolescents with severe hemophilia A, assessed as the change in patient reported outcomes (PROs) and physical activity measurements after switching to efanesoctocog compared with their prior prophylactic treatment.

The objective of this study was to determine whether efanesoctocog alfa, a FVIII replacement product, which allows for less frequent prophylactic dosing, was preferred over previous FVIII treatment. Additionally, we sought to evaluate whether patients who switched to efanesoctocog alfa were more satisfied with their treatment, with the ultimate aim of optimizing treatment adherence and achieving patient treatment goals.

Methods

Trial design

Full methodologic details for XTEND-1 have been previously published.16 Briefly, XTEND-1 was an open-label, multicenter Phase 3 trial of efanesoctocog alfa in previously treated adults and adolescents ≥12 years of age with severe hemophilia A (<1 IU/dL [1%] endogenous FVIII activity), or a documented genotype known to produce severe hemophilia A. Patients were required to have had at least 150 previous exposure days to recombinant or plasma-derived FVIII concentrates or cryoprecipitates. Patients who were previously receiving standard-of-care FVIII prophylaxis were included in Arm A and received once-weekly prophylaxis with efanesoctocog alfa (50 IU/kg body weight) for 52 weeks. Patients who were previously on an on-demand treatment were included in Arm B and received on-demand efanesoctocog alfa for 26 weeks and then received once-weekly prophylaxis with efanesoctocog alfa for another 26 weeks. The primary endpoint in XTEND-1 was the estimated/modelled mean ABR. This analysis assessed exploratory endpoints in all patients who received at least one dose of weekly prophylactic efanesoctocog alfa.

Treatment preference and treatment satisfaction

Patient treatment preference was assessed at Week 52 using a two-item questionnaire. Preference was established by asking patients if they preferred to manage their condition with their previous treatment, current treatment, or if they had no preference. Once established, and if current treatment was their preference, they were asked to provide the top three reasons for their preference out of nine propositions. These included safer treatment option, increased sense of well-being, reduction in the number of bleeds and bleed-related complications, reduction in pain, improved joint mobility, less frequent treatment (once a week), shorter time of administration, feeling better protected, and ‘other reason’.

Patient satisfaction at baseline, Week 26, and Week 52 was assessed using the 9-item version of the Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaire for Medications (TSQM-9),18 a validated psychometric tool that provides a general measure of patient satisfaction with treatment. The TSQM-9 consists of three domains, each of which comprises three questions: global satisfaction (questions on confidence with taking medication; if good benefits outweigh the bad aspects; if the patient is satisfied or dissatisfied with the treatment), effectiveness (questions on the ability of the medication to prevent or treat the condition; the ability of the medication to relieve symptoms; the amount of time taken for the medication to start working), and convenience (questions on how difficult it is to use the medication; how difficult it is to plan medication use; how convenient it is to take the medication). The level of satisfaction, difficulty, convenience, confidence, or certainty were ranked from 1 to 7, with the highest score being the greater satisfaction (e.g., ‘extremely dissatisfied’ = 1, ‘very dissatisfied’ = 2, ‘dissatisfied’ = 3, ‘somewhat satisfied’ = 4, ‘satisfied’ = 5, ‘very satisfied’ = 6, and ‘extremely satisfied’ = 7). The recall period for TSQM-9 is 2–3 weeks, or since last medication. Results of the TSQM-9 questionnaire were summarized using three normalized (0–100) scores for the three domains.

Patient-reported outcomes

Several exploratory endpoints were assessed post-hoc in XTEND-1. These included patient-reported outcome (PRO) questionnaires including Haem-A-QoL, a quality-of-life assessment instrument, and PROMIS Pain Intensity 3a Worst Pain questionnaire.

Haem-A-QoL is a 46-item patient-completed questionnaire designed for measuring health-related quality of life (HRQoL) impairments attributed to hemophilia in the past 4 weeks, across 10 domains. The Haem-A-QoL Physical Health (PH) subscale includes five items pertaining to the frequency of hemophilia-related pain and physical/mobility difficulties: painful swelling, joint pain, ability to move without pain, ability to walk desired distance, and time needed to get ready. Items are scored on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“all the time”). Haem-A-QoL PH and total raw scores are computed as the sum of the item responses (for five and 46 items, respectively) and raw scores are transformed into a scale from 0 to 100, where higher values represent poorer HRQoL.

The intensity of pain at its worst score over the past 7 days was the first item from PROMIS Pain Intensity 3a Worst Pain and has a score selected by the patient, ranging from 1 (“Had no pain”) to 5 (“Very severe pain”).

Statistical methods

The analyses were performed on the Full Analysis Set (FAS), encompassing all participants who received at least one dose of study drug. Only patients enrolled in Arm A were included. In all analyses, assessments during major surgical or rehabilitation periods were excluded.

The treatment preference survey questions were summarized using descriptive statistics. For each TSQM-9 domain score, least squares mean changes from baseline, standard error (SE), 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values were estimated at each timepoint using a mixed-effect model with repeated measures (MMRM) with visit as fixed effect and baseline TSQM-9 score as covariate: a robust sandwich covariance matrix within a subject was used. Proportions of patients with the ‘best response’ for each of the nine items were also described at each timepoint.

The TSQM-9 domain data were also described by subgroups using descriptive statistics over time. A further subgroup, re-categorized treatment preference survey item 1 at Week 52: “Prefer current BIVV001 (rFVIIIFc-VWF-XTEN) treatment” vs “No preference or prefer old treatment” was also investigated.

The relationship between TSQM-9 and other PRO data was investigated for the subgroup with treated bleeds in patients with an evaluable efficacy period. The efficacy period reflects the sum of all intervals of time during which patients are treated with efanesoctocog alfa, excluding periods of pharmacokinetic evaluations, surgery/rehabilitation (minor and major), and large injection intervals (>28 days). The proportions of patients recording the best response for each of the nine items of the TSQM-9 was described for each of the nine items in the FAS and by subgroup at baseline and Week 52. Spearman correlations between TSQM-9 domain scores and other PRO scores were assessed at baseline, and on changes from baseline to Week 52 for Haem-A-QoL Total score, Haem-A-QoL Physical Health subscore, and PROMIS Pain Intensity 3a Worst Pain score. The interpretation rule for correlation coefficients (absolute values) was as follows: 0 to <0.2 = very weak, 0.2 to <0.4 = weak, 0.4 to <0.6 = moderate, 0.6 to <0.8 = strong, 0.8 to 1.0 = very strong.

Across all analyses, the significance level was 0.05. Because all analyses were conducted post hoc, no adjustment on multiplicity was performed and all p-values should be considered nominal. Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4.

Results

Analysis population

Of those who completed the questionnaire, 117 (90.0%) reported a preference for their current treatment with efanesoctocog alfa, 10 (7.7%) had no preference, and 3 (2.3%) preferred their previous treatment (Fig. 1). Similar trends were observed when data were analyzed by region (Fig. 1). Patient satisfaction

Overall, 122 (91.7%) patients completed the TSQM-9 questionnaire at baseline and 115 (86.5%) at Week 52. Mean (SD) TSQM-9 domain scores at baseline were 76.82 (18.62) for global satisfaction, 71.49 (21.87) for effectiveness, and 69.03 (16.25) for convenience. There were statistically significant improvements in TSQM-9 scores from baseline to Week 52 in all three domains (Fig. 3). The items with the highest percentage of patients selecting ‘very and extremely satisfied’ at baseline were ‘confident medication’ (84%) and ‘good outweighs bad’ (81%); these percentages increased at Week 52 to 93% and 90%, respectively. The percentage of patients selecting ‘very and extremely satisfied’ also increased for the other seven items from 35–50% at baseline to 61–86% at Week 52 (Supplementary Fig. 1A and 1B). Similar percentages of patients selecting the best response were observed between Europe and non-Europe regions at baseline, Week 26 and Week 52. Relationship between treatment preference and patient satisfaction

For the purpose of this analysis, the subgroups of patients who had no treatment preference or preferred their old hemophilia treatment were combined due to the small number of responses. TSQM-9 domain scores at baseline were broadly similar between patients who preferred current efanesoctocog alfa treatment (N = 107) and patients who had no treatment preference or preferred their old hemophilia treatment (N = 12) (mean [SD]: 76.50 [18.82] vs. 81.55 [11.58] for global satisfaction; 71.55 [21.83] vs. 73.61 [16.09] for effectiveness; and 69.31 [15.99] vs. 70.37 [15.41] for convenience). The three TSQM-9 domains were numerically improved at Week 52 in patients who preferred current efanesoctocog alfa treatment, whereas the three TSQM-9 domain scores were numerically worsened at Week 52 in the patients who had no preference or preferred their old hemophilia treatment (mean [natural 95% CI] change from baseline: 13.14 [9.84, 16.45] (N = 9) vs. -11.11 [-17.90, -4.32] (N = 106) for global satisfaction; 13.78 [8.70, 18.87] (N = 9) vs. -2.47 [-12.04, 7.11] (N = 106) for effectiveness; and 11.74 [8.65, 14.83] (N = 106) vs. -2.47 [-13.38, 8.44] (N = 9) for convenience). In addition, for the nine items of TSQM-9, higher percentages of patients with best response were observed among patients who preferred current efanesoctocog alfa treatment at Week 52 than at baseline.

Relationship between TSQM-9 and PROs

A weak association was observed between all TSQM-9 domains and baseline PROs (Haem-A-QoL Total score [global satisfaction: -0.36, P < 0.001; effectiveness: -0.30, P = 0.002; convenience: -0.31, P = 0.002], Haem-A-QoL Physical Health subscale score [global satisfaction: -0.37, P < 0.001; effectiveness: -0.33, P < 0.001; convenience: -0.20, P = 0.04], and PROMIS Pain Intensity 3a Worst Pain scores [global satisfaction: -0.30, P < 0.001; effectiveness: -0.28, P = 0.002; convenience: -0.28, P = 0.002]; -0.20 to -0.37) at baseline. The TSQM-9 domain with highest Spearman correlation coefficients was global satisfaction (Supplementary Table 1).

From baseline to Week 52, a very weak to weak association was observed between all TSQM-9 domains and PROs (Haem-A-QoL Total score [global satisfaction: -0.18, P = 0.082; effectiveness: -0.33, P = 0.001; convenience: -0.15, P = 0.136], Haem-A-QoL Physical Health subscale score [global satisfaction: -0.24, P = 0.017; effectiveness: -0.25, P = 0.014; convenience: -0.05, P = 0.661], and PROMIS Pain Intensity 3a Worst Pain scores [global satisfaction: -0.15, P = 0.111; effectiveness: -0.16, P = 0.081; convenience: -0.13, P = 0.178]; -0.05 to -0.33). The TSQM-9 domain with highest Spearman correlation coefficients was effectiveness (Supplementary Table 2). There was no statistically significant association for change from baseline at Week 52 between the TSQM-9 global satisfaction domain with improvement in quality of life, except a weak association (-0.24, P = 0.017) with Haem-A-QoL Physical Health subscore. There was no statistically significant correlation for the change from baseline in TSQM-9 convenience domain.

Discussion

In this exploratory post-hoc analysis, we investigated the treatment preferences and satisfaction of patients with severe hemophilia A treated with efanesoctocog alfa prophylaxis in XTEND-1. A key result of this analysis was that nearly all patients (90.0%) preferred treatment with efanesoctocog alfa to their previous prophylaxis treatment for severe hemophilia A, primarily due to a lower frequency of administration, fewer bleeds and bleed-related complications, and patients feeling better protected. This preference for efanesoctocog alfa versus prior prophylaxis is consistent with a previous analysis of exit interviews with participants enrolled in the XTEND-1 study, with all interview participants preferring efanesoctocog alfa to their pre-study treatment.19 Regarding treatment satisfaction in the current analysis, there were statistically significant increases in TSQM-9 scores from baseline to Week 52 in all three domains of global satisfaction, effectiveness, and convenience. TSQM-9 domain scores improved in patients who preferred current efanesoctocog alfa treatment, whereas these worsened in those who had no preference or preferred their old hemophilia treatment. No association was observed between TSQM-9 scores and bleeding history or bleeding during the trial. A weak association was observed between all TSQM-9 domains and baseline PROs.

Available treatments for hemophilia A include FVIII therapies that require frequent injections (due to the 8–19-hour half-life), which is challenging for patients and leads to substantial treatment burden. Efanesoctocog alfa was designed to provide higher factor levels with a longer half-life and less frequent doses than other FVIII therapies,20 thus enabling higher protection and alleviating treatment burden. Our finding of high treatment preference for efanesoctocog alfa versus previous treatment, with the most common reason being the lower frequency of the treatment, demonstrates that the once-weekly dosing may be a key factor informing patient treatment preference. In addition, the second most frequently chosen reason was the reduction of bleeding, followed by the feeling of being better protected, in line with the primary efficacy result of the XTEND-1 trial.16 These reasons for treatment preference are in line with previous studies on patient preferences for characteristics of prophylactic treatment in hemophilia A. Su, et al. conducted a discrete choice experiment via an online survey exploring sociodemographic characteristics and preferences in 113 patients with hemophilia A. They reported that the top three treatment attributes of prophylactic treatment in hemophilia were dosing frequency, bleed protection and safety concerns regarding excessive clotting.8 In addition, Furlan, et al. investigated the relative importance of various characteristics of prophylactic treatment in patients and/or parents of children with hemophilia A; they showed a preference in this trial group for reduced frequency of administration, followed by high efficacy.9

When comparing the patient preference with the patient satisfaction, baseline scores differed slightly for global satisfaction between patients who preferred current efanesoctocog alfa and those who had no treatment preference or preferred their old hemophilia treatment. However, as there is no meaningful difference between-group change threshold for TSQM-9 available in the literature, it is difficult to assess the impact of this difference here; additionally, the difference in group sizes renders comparison difficult. It may be that a small percentage of patients would prefer one treatment regimen over another based on their individual special needs. The benefit in efanesoctocog alfa treatment was realized by Week 26, with an increase from baseline in the proportion of patients selecting the best response across all nine of the TSQM-9 items. This proportion increased further in five of the nine items by Week 52 and was generally sustained in the other items. Patient satisfaction was higher at Week 52 in patients who preferred efanesoctocog alfa treatment than patients who preferred other treatments or had no preference. A worsening for the TSQM-9 global satisfaction domain at Week 52 was observed in patients who had no preference or preferred their old hemophilia treatment. Of note, the TSQM-9 questionnaire is not a widely used tool and there is a paucity of literature available for outcome comparison with our data. One identified study was a cross-sectional, multicenter, observational study of 505 patients with hemophilia A in Korea, where the mean TSQM scores reported in males were lower than those observed in our trial: 66.8 (SD 14.8) versus 85.9 (global satisfaction), 64.6 (SD 13.8) versus 85.1 (effectiveness), and 57.2 (SD 14.8) versus 82.5 (convenience).11 A Canadian prospective cohort study evaluated PRO measures in 58 patients with moderate or severe hemophilia A who either stayed on their standard half-life (SHL) FVIII treatment or switched to recombinant FVIII Fc fusion protein (rFVIIIFc) extended half-life products. Those who switched to rFVIIIFc experienced improved treatment satisfaction scores from 44/59 at baseline to 47/59 at 3 months and 48/59 at 24 months (60–70% improved over one-half of a SD), whereas those remaining on SHL-FVIII had no changes throughout the study.21 Similar improvements in treatment satisfaction were observed in our analysis.

Limited literature reporting TSQM-9 data hinders our ability to compare our findings with other studies. Moreover, there is no published threshold in these populations for clinically meaningful improvements in TSQM scores, and therefore the statistically significant improvements cannot be assessed. A high imbalance was also observed between subgroups leading to small sample sizes for ‘no preference’ or ‘prefer old treatment’, bleedings and North America subgroups, which may have confounded our findings. Additionally, a high number of TSQM-9 scores equal to 100 (the maximum) for each domain were observed, and this ceiling effect might have an impact on the investigated correlations. Finally, all analyses were conducted post-hoc, therefore prospective validation is warranted to confirm our findings.

Conclusion

New factor replacement products are shaping the hemophilia A treatment landscape by giving patients and caregivers treatment options with varying product attributes. Our exploratory analysis showed that efanesoctocog alfa, a high-sustained FVIII replacement product, which is efficacious and allows for less frequent prophylactic dosing, was preferred over previous FVIII treatment for nearly all patients in the XTEND-1 trial. This preference was reported across all regions analyzed: Europe, non-Europe, North America and non-North America. Patients who switched to efanesoctocog alfa were also found to be more satisfied with their treatment at Week 52 than at baseline in all three domains of the TSQM-9: global satisfaction, effectiveness, and convenience.

Substantial patient burden exists around hemophilia A and its treatment, particularly regarding the high frequency of infusions of standard of care therapies and suboptimal bleed rates. Our analysis shows that it is important for physicians to consider practices that take patient preference and satisfaction into account when making treatment decisions, in an effort to optimize treatment adherence and ultimately attain patient treatment goals.
