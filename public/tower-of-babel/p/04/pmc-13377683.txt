Attempted suicide: lived experiences of refugees in Rhino Camp, West Nile, Uganda
Authors: Robinson Ojwok, Judith Abal Akello, Jenifer Acio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicide is among the leading causes of death worldwide, and every year, more people die as a result of suicide than HIV, malaria, breast cancer, war, and homicide. Suicide rates are high among vulnerable groups that experience discrimination, such as refugees and migrants.PurposeThis study explored the lived experiences of refugee suicide survivors in Rhino Camp, West Nile, Uganda, using Thomas Joiner's interpersonal theory of suicide attempts.MethodsThis study employed an interpretative phenomenological research design and purposive sampling techniques to select 15 participants (seven key informants and eight refugee suicide survivors). Face-to-face interviews using in-depth interview guides and observations were used to gather data. Data were collected between June and July 2023.ResultsAmong refugee suicide survivors, saturation was reached after the seventh interview, and key informants reached saturation after the sixth interview, at which point no new themes emerged. Participants described socioeconomic deprivation, intimate partner conflict and gender-based violence, trauma loss and war experiences, health burdens and physical vulnerability, psychological distress and cognitive overload, suicide attempt as crisis responses, stigma, social judgment, and isolation as a broad set of interconnected subjective experiences and triggers shaping suicide attempts among refugees. Community rescue, institutional intervention, and psychosocial support were identified as supportive systems, while refugee suicide attempt survivors described meaning-making, coping, and recovery.ConclusionsSocioeconomic deprivation, psychological distress, gender-based violence, stigma, and isolation heighten vulnerability among refugees, leading to suicide attempts. Strengthening available supportive systems enhances support for refugee suicide survivors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27871-7.Keywords: Suicide, Refugees, Lived experiences, Mental health, Rhino Camp

Background to the study

Suicide is among the leading causes of death worldwide, and every year, more people die as a result of suicide than HIV, malaria, breast cancer, war, and homicide [1]. Worldwide, 800,000 people are projected to have completed suicide every year, with 75% of worldwide suicides occurring in low, and middle-income countries [2]. Suicide accounts for 1.4% of premature deaths worldwide. Males are predicted to die at rates roughly twice those of females (12.6 per 100,000 males compared to 5.4 per 100,000 females) [3–5]. Suicide rates worldwide are a significant public health problem that needs to be addressed urgently. Africa has six of the ten countries with the highest suicide rates in the world [6], and suicide kills about 11 individuals per 100,000 people in Africa each year, which is higher than the worldwide average of nine per 100,000 individuals [3]. It should be noted that for each suicide in Africa, there are an estimated 20 suicide attempts [3]. According to UNHCR, about 215 refugees attempted suicide, and 51 refugees completed suicide in Ugandan refugee camps [7]. In Rhino Camp, the cases of those who attempted and completed suicides between 2021 and May 2023 were 49 refugees (39 attempted and 10 completed suicides) out of 133,336 refugees majorly South Sudanese hosted [8, 9].

Suicide rates are high among vulnerable groups who experience discrimination, such as refugees and migrants, indigenous people, lesbians, gays, bisexuals, transgender, intersex (LGBTI) persons, and prisoners [10]. Suicide refers to intentional death resulting from self-inflicted injury [11]. A suicide attempt refers to the determination of an individual to terminate life by injuring their body, but their actions do not lead them to death [11].

Refugees may be at increased risk of suicide due to factors such as traumatic events [12–15], alcohol and other substance use abuse [3, 16–18], feelings of isolation [19], job loss [20, 21], chronic pain and illness [22], relationship conflicts [23–26], hopelessness [27], sense of isolation and lack of social support [28, 29], mental pain [30, 31] and need for love and belonging [32]. Losses associated with displacement such as separation from family and friends, loss of birthplace, diminished social respect, disruption of public connection, loss of property and income, and economic instability [33]. Suicide attempts can lead to adverse impacts, such as harm, prolonged sickness, and loss of freedom, and cause financial problems for families and communities [34].

Despite this, little is known about suicidal behaviors in these highly mobile populations, as collecting timely, relevant, and reliable data is challenging [35]. There have been increasing suicide attempts by refugees recently, with trauma, social isolation, and mental health services not widely available [36] and little attention paid to the lived experiences of refugee suicide survivors [37]

Understanding suicidal thoughts and emotions requires careful attention to the subjective views of individuals who experience suicide, as these insights are fundamental to enhancing the ability to explain, anticipate, and prevent suicidal behavior [38]. Exploring how people interpret and respond to suicidal ideation involves examining the subjective dimensions of these experiences, an approach that has received less emphasis within suicidology in recent decades despite earlier recognition of its importance [39]. A study conducted in 2019 reported that only a small proportion of participants (10 of 50) experienced relief or happiness immediately after surviving suicide attempts [40]. Most described difficult emotional reactions, including sadness, depression, disappointment, anger, emptiness, embarrassment, and shame [40]. Although many later shifted toward more positive emotions such as gratitude, hope, and relief, more than 30% continued to view their survival negatively at the time of the study [40].

Insights from survivors of suicide attempts offer critical experiential knowledge that can strengthen current prevention and intervention strategies. Their lived experiences can help to guide the development of mental health services that are accessible, relevant, and responsive to individual needs during periods of acute psychological distress [40]. Paying structured attention to the personal views of individuals faced with suicide can play an important role in guiding the development of more suitable forms of mental health support, broader societal decisions, and encouraging positive shifts within communities [41]. This paper reports on an interpretive phenomenological qualitative study that explored the lived experiences of refugee suicide attempt survivors.

Methods

Study design

This study used an interpretative phenomenological research design, an approach that highlights the value of a person's unique view and understanding, as it is predicated on the modes of thinking of firsthand knowledge and subjective experience [42]. Consequently, the design prioritizes participants’ perspectives while recognizing the researcher’s interpretative role in making sense of these accounts [43]. According to Prasad (2018), interpretivism involves understanding human experience, which requires attention to context, subjectivity, and meaning-making processes [44]. Interpretative phenomenology is clearly idiographic, requiring detailed analysis of each individual case before identifying shared patterns or themes across participants [45]. This commitment to depth over breadth guided all stages of the research process, including participant selection, data collection, and analysis. The study focused on examining the lived experiences of refugee suicide survivors within their personal and social contexts, allowing for an in-depth exploration of how meaning is constructed around complex and emotionally laden phenomena. Rather than seeking generalization, the approach emphasizes a rich, contextualized understanding of individual experiences [46]. Overall, the study design provided a coherent framework that guided the research process and ensured alignment between the methodological approach, data generation, and interpretation.

Study area

The study area was Rhino Camp, a refugee camp in the West Nile region of Uganda. Rhino Camp has geographic coordinates of 2.966°N, and 31.395°E [47]. The settlement spans an area of approximately 225 km2, which includes parts of the Terego District, Odupi, Omugo, and Uriama sub-counties, as well as the Rigbo Sub-County of the Madi-Okollo District [48]. Although the settlement is not a constant geographic area and has seven zones, the refugees still choose to live there in "wallets" [49]. Both the host communities and Rhino Camp can be described as rural, with some towns and urban areas included. The primary source of income for both the host community and the refugee population is crop farming. [50].

The choice to concentrate on suicide attempts and lived experiences among refugees living in Rhino Camp, West Nile, Uganda, was based on several factors. First, Rhino Camp is one of Uganda's largest refugee camps, housing thousands of refugees from nearby countries, such as the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) and South Sudan [51]. Second, there have been more suicide attempts by refugees recently, with trauma, social isolation, and mental health issues contributing to this [36]. Third, there is limited research on the actual experiences of refugee suicide survivors at Rhino Camp and other Ugandan settlements. Studies on refugee suicide have largely concentrated on the risk factors, prevalence, and interventions, with little attention paid to the individual experiences of refugee suicide survivors [37]. Thus, by focusing on Rhino Camp, this study aimed to clarify the unique experiences of refugee suicide survivors.

Study and target population

Study population

The population of the study was refugee suicide survivors in Rhino Camp, West Nile, Uganda. The cases of those who attempted and completed suicides in Rhino Camp between 2021 and May 2023 were 49 refugees (39 attempted and 10 completed suicides) out of 133,336 refugees [8].

Target population

All refugees in Rhino Camp, West Nile, Uganda. As of December 2022, Rhino Camp hosted 133,336 refugees, mainly South Sudanese [9].

Sample size and sampling

The study utilized purposive sampling to select 15 participants (seven key informants and eight refugee suicide survivors) because of a thorough in-depth analysis of qualitative research information [52]. This was the number at which “saturation” of the data was reached. The sample sizes in phenomenological studies vary from 3 to 25 [53].

Saturation was determined separately for refugee suicide survivors and key informants due to the distinct participant groups and use of different interview guides. Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently to allow ongoing assessment of thematic development and redundancy. Among refugee suicide survivors, saturation was reached after the seventh in-depth interview, at which point no new themes emerged. One additional interview was conducted to confirm redundancy and ensure adequate depth of the identified themes. For key informants, saturation was reached after the sixth interview, when no new institutional or contextual insights were identified. A seventh interview was conducted to verify the repetition and confirm saturation. Saturation was determined through iterative coding and constant comparison of transcripts. Hence, it was deemed that data collection had reached the saturation points. Following each interview, transcripts were reviewed and coded, and emerging codes were compared with existing categories to assess the emergence of new themes. Decisions regarding saturation were discussed during regular analytic meetings among the research team to ensure consensus and methodological rigor. Sampling of participants stopped at the point of saturation. Mason, M. (2010) asserted that saturation occurs when gathering fresh data, informed by both interviews and sampling, does not add any new information to our understanding of the problem. A qualitative sample reaches a point known as diminishing returns, which indicates that the data collection is complete or nearly complete [53, 54].

The research team had the following roles: the principal researcher led participants recruitment, trained translator, conducted all interviews, and performed initial coding and data management. The second researcher provided methodological oversight and contributed to the development and refinement of the coding framework. The third researcher participated in the discussions, reviewed emerging themes for coherence and credibility, and contributed to the interpretation of findings. Determination of data saturation, writing the manuscript, and approval of the final version were made by all the three research team members.

Seven key informants were purposively selected based on their professional expertise and direct experience working closely with refugee population in Rhino Camp. The seven key informants were identified through OPM, police, and mental health partner organizations. They included: two representatives from the Office of the Prime Minister—Yoro base Camp, one psychologist and one psychiatrist from mental health partners, one police member in the mental health department, and two refugee welfare council members. Eligibility criteria required that key informants: (1) had at least two years of professional experience working with refugee communities, and (2) were directly engaged in providing mental health and psychosocial support services, community support, or related services to refugees. The seven key informants were invited to participate in the study by clearly explaining the purpose of the research study and by signing a consent form. Selection was based on their capacity to provide informed institutional and contextual perspectives that complement, but do not replace, the lived experiences shared by refugee survivors.

The eight refugee suicide survivors were eligible to participate in the study if they were (1) above 18 years old, (2) attempted suicide in the last three years (2021- 2023), and (3) able and willing to describe their experience with a suicide attempt. Refugee suicide survivors with severe mental health distress, that could affect their ability to participate safely or provide informed consent at the time of data collection were excluded from the study. The eight refugee suicide survivors were recruited with the help of the OPM Regional Refugee Desk Office—Arua (RDO), OPM Settlement Commandant Rhino Camp, and Refugee Welfare Councils (RWCs), which linked the researchers to the refugee suicide survivors and their homes. The recruitment information for the eight refugee suicide survivors was obtained from the UNHCR—Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) implementing partner (TPO Uganda), which provided researchers with refugee suicide survivor’s details. The researchers followed all ethical standards to ensure that the refugee suicide survivors provided by TPO Uganda would be kept confidential to avoid violating the privacy of refugee suicide survivors. The eight refugee suicide survivors were affected by various factors, such as trauma, psychological distress, loss of connection, and social support. Refugee suicide survivors were of different ages, genders, and cultural backgrounds and had attempted suicide for different reasons.

Methodology rigor

Credibility was achieved by describing the lived experiences of suicide survivors from the perspectives of the participants and using the triangulation of different data collection methods. To ensure credibility and trustworthiness, the researchers’ knowledge of the phenomenon was bracketed to prevent preconceptions regarding refugee suicide survivors’ influence on outcomes and to allow the analysis of lived experiences. The quality of the research results was improved through thick, detailed descriptions and coding processes. Dependability was maintained through in-depth interview guide standards, data audits before analysis, and safeguarding the accuracy of the data. Verbatim collected from participants was transcribed by listening to the audio recording and making observations during face-to-face interviews. Confirmability was guaranteed through a review by the supervisor, data checking, and rechecking throughout data collection and analysis. To achieve the criteria of transferability, the process of research was explained in detail so that other researchers could use this technique [53].

Data collection

In-depth (face-to-face) interviews and observations of nonverbal behavior were conducted to collect data. The research team formulated interview guides in English. The research team consisted of the principal investigator, who holds expertise in mental health and psychosocial support, one public health expert, and a qualitative data specialist. The interview guide questions were informed by the literature review and theoretical framework of Interpersonal Theory (IPT) of Suicidal Attempts (SA) by Joiner, T. E, 2005. The principal researcher collected data using the interview guide, as it allowed the principal researcher to observe the verbal and nonverbal signs (body language and interest) of the participants, enabling them to provide accurate information, maintain participants' concentration, and ultimately capture participants’ emotions and behaviors. The in-depth interview guide had two components: the participant's sociodemographic characteristics and the participant's in-depth interview guide, per objective, to answer the questions. The seven key informants were interviewed in English. The eight refugee suicide survivors were interviewed with the help of a trained multilingual translator fluent in English, Lingala, Arabic, and Kakwa, given the linguistic diversity of the refugees in Rhino refugee settlement, and depending on the participant’s preferred language. The translator was trained in both qualitative research methods and mental health terminology, which supported accurate and context-sensitive interpretation. The interview guide for eight refugee suicide survivors was translated from English into Lingala, Arabic, and Kakwa before data collection. During interviews with the eight refugee suicide survivors, the translator interpreted the principal researcher’s questions by translating from English into the participants’ preferred language and translating participants’ responses back into English for the principal researcher. In this role, the translator functioned as both translator and interpreter. Key passages of translated transcripts were reviewed through back-translation procedures to enhance translation accuracy and to minimize potential loss or distortion of meaning. Selected passages were independently translated back into English by a second multilingual speaker to verify consistency of meaning. Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved by all three researchers to preserve participants’ original expressions and meanings. Forty-five to sixty minutes were allotted for each in-depth interview, which arrived at the end of the participant’s answer to the final question. All responses were audio-recorded after obtaining consent from the participants. Audio recordings were made in the participants’ preferred language of Lingala, Arabic or Kakwa, and English, as the multilingual translator concurrently translated into English, and transcriptions were made in English. The English transcripts were reviewed by the principal researcher alongside audio recordings to ensure accuracy in capturing participants’ intended meanings. Where clarification was needed, a follow-up consultation with the translator was undertaken. Although interviews required interpretation, care was taken to preserve participants’ original expressions and meanings. Quotations presented in the findings reflect the English translations of participants’ responses verified through the translation review process. The researchers’ interpretations were grounded in the translated transcripts and supported through team-based analytic discussions to enhance credibility. One clinical psychologist provided counseling to the refugee suicide survivor participants during interviews in case of distress to avoid traumatic recall; no session was terminated due to acute traumatic recall. The interview took place at the community counseling space/center, where several activities occurred, such as life skills training, various counseling sessions, and other training for refugees, hence scheduling participants on different appointment days and times to meet them at the counseling center one by one in case two or more participants were present. This maintained the privacy and confidentiality of the participants, which ensured that participants would not identify themselves and that other community members would remain unaware of what a particular participant was doing at the counseling center. This helped to manage community stigmatization or social harm. The welfare amount for eight refugee suicide survivors and seven key informants was 20,000/= Uganda shillings only each to reimburse transport costs since the participants’ locations were far from the counseling center. This amount was modest and aligned with local norms, ensuring it did not constitute undue inducement or influence participants’ decision to take part in the study. Data collection took place between June 2nd and July 31 st, 2023. In field editing, the information gathered from the field was reviewed, edited, documented, organized, and coded with participant pseudonyms and dates to identify patterns and related themes. Words, phrases, and open-ended responses in in-depth interviews were coded as they are easier to remember, skim, and organize. The participants’ exact words were used in quotes, and the researcher interpreted and commented on them. In-depth interviews with both key informants and refugee suicide survivors were pilot tested in the Imvepi refugee settlement.

Data management and analysis

There are varying processes by which interpretive phenomenologists analyze data, depending on the specific type of phenomenology used. These varying processes are hermeneutic phenomenological reflection, Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA), transcendental phenomenological reduction, and thematic approaches grounded in phenomenological interpretation. These approaches vary in their emphasis on bracketing, idiographic depth, or hermeneutic interpretation. For this study, the principal researcher analyzed each transcript using phenomenological analytic procedures described by Creswell and Poth [52], which provide a structured yet flexible framework suitable for interpretive phenomenological inquiry focused on meaning-making within lived experiences. This approach was selected because it allows systematic thematic development while preserving participants’ contextual narratives.

Ethical considerations

Approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, Lira University Research Ethics Committee (LUREC) no. LUREC-2023–18, and the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology (UNCST) no. HS3046ES before the study was conducted. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. The researcher sought clearance for data collection from the UNHCR and OPM after receiving approval from LUREC and a letter of introduction from the Faculty of Public Health, Lira University.

Informed consent

All participants were requested to provide their consent before the interview. The consent form was written because it provided an auditable record in the case of questions arose from data storage. Informed consent was obtained by describing the purpose of the study and procedures involved. During data collection, one clinical psychologist was available to speak to anyone upset after participating in this study. The interview process was voluntary, and the participants were free to leave the interview process without penalty.

Confidentiality

Participants were notified that the information from this study would be held and kept strictly private and used only for research purposes. The interviews were conducted in the community counseling space/center. To maintain the privacy of the participants, pseudonyms were used to report direct quotes. Furthermore, the participants remained anonymous (nameless) throughout the study. The records were kept electronically with a password, on paper, and behind locked doors.

Results

This study aimed to explore the lived experiences of refugees residing in Rhino Camp,West Nile Uganda, who had attempted suicide. According to the findings, sociodemographic characteristics of participants and themes were developed separately for refugee suicide survivors and key informants due to the distinct participant groups and use of different interview guides.

For seven key informants, five were male and two were female. Ages ranged from 28 to 52 years (M ≈ 39.7 years). Five participants were married, and two were single. Three participants held Master’s degrees (Clinical Psychologist and two OPM Representatives), and one held a Diploma (Psychiatric Clinical Officer), three participants had secondary-level education (Senior Four (Police Officer), Senior Three (RWC Representative), and Senior Two (RWC Chairperson 2). Experience of work within the refugee context ranged from 3.5 to 15 years as presented in Table 1.

Intimate partner conflict and gender-based violence

Participants described experiences of rejection, abandonment, domestic violence, forced and early marriages, substance abuse by partners (alcohol-related abuse), infidelity and relational mistrust, sexual violence (rape), and emotional humiliation as critical triggers for suicide attempts.

For example, one participant narrated her experience of marital abandonment:

“My husband rejected my children, and me, and I felt the world was not worth living in.” (R,23)

Domestic violence and physical abuse were also highlighted:

“He used to chase me with a panga… sometimes cut me with it.” (R,15)

“My husband kept on beating me. The situation was so bad that he even broke my hand which I took to Nimule hospital and the hand was fixed but when I came back again, he when a head to beat me until my spinal cord got a problem.” (R,21)

Early and forced marriages emerged as additional stressors:

“I was married at the age of 13 years in the form of rape… my husband kept beating me, and my life was unbearable.” (R,21)

On forced marriage, a key informant said:

“Some of the refugee families give out their daughters in marriage even when their daughters do not know.” (KII:4, Male, 44 years old)

Substance abuse by partners was reported by the participants and they said:

“My husband started smoking opium and misbehaving to me. Every day it was just a fight and quarrel with my husband until he bites my right ear. When he realized he ran a way to South Sudan.” (R,22)

“Things begun to change when He started drinking alcohol. Every time, he comes drunk, and chase me with panga. To my dismay, he is my torturer and made me his slave.” (R,15)

Sexual violence further compounded participants’ vulnerability:

“I was raped by three unknown men… the pregnancy from rape made me overthink and attempt suicide.” (R,19)

In addition, a key informant explained how domestic violence disproportionately affects women through various forms of violence, such as physical, sexual, emotional, and economic abuse, leading to triggers for suicide, as illustrated below:

“Issues of GBV have been ranked the highest trigger of suicides in Rhino Camp because of the nature of the refugees that we have. So they come with difficult psychological situations to deal with …inflict pain on women. What happens to someone who has been bitten by their closest family, where they are supposed to seek solace to avert suicides?.” (KII:2, Male, 34 years old)

While supporting the above voice, a participant narrated that:

“Domestic violence contributes to women committing suicide because when a man always tortures and abuses the wife, it makes the wife feel undermined in front of children and the community. Some of the women go through psychological and physical abuse, fighting and harm, and sexual abuse in marriage, and sex in homes is done by men without the wife’s concern. This practically happens in the refugee camps because here, we have about 64 ethnic groups.” (KII:4, Male, 44 years old)

Another key informant participant explained further the ordeals women go through in the Rhino Camp:

“Unwanted pregnancy, forced marriage, extramarital affairs, fights with the co-wife, man concentrating on one family, emotional violence in women. For example, there are some refugee men who come and impregnate their women and leave them without any support, and they go back to their country, leaving women to struggle with pregnancy and taking care of children.” (KII:1, Female, 36 years old)

Men also face silent emotional humiliation and abuse by their counterparts as one of the refugee suicide survivor narrated:

“My wife quarrels, abuse, and insults me. She does not have respect for me. I got annoyed and with too much thinking, I heard the voice speaking to me to kill myself. Immediately I had to pick a rope when to the bush, got a very tall huge tree with good branches, I climb it and tied the rope in one good branch and from there I tied the rope on my neck and threw myself down, the rope squeezed my neck good enough the rope broke.” (R,10)

Health burden and physical vulnerability

This study indicated that health burden, mental illness, and attempted suicide are closely related, and individuals with chronic illnesses, and mental health conditions are at an increased risk of attempting suicide, similar to the overall population. A key informant made the following assertions:

“There are clients on mental treatment, they stop taking treatment and symptoms come up. For example, voices become too loud, leading to relapse. Mental health clients, because of pain, sadness, conflict in their thoughts end up being triggered to suicide. Depression is being sad, with a loss of interest in activities. Trauma in acute form can stem from an events, causing psychological pains with bad dreams, and thoughts leading to Post Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD). Psychosis includes thoughts threatening to kill them. Autism, for instance families nursing a child who has autism, which requires behavioral management-they don’t know how to manage it, no special needs, no therapists, can’t keep in one place. Families gets confused, and can’t work because of caring for a child who destroys people’s properties, fights, and is aggressive. All the above triggers one’s suicide attempts.” (KII:1, Female, 36 years old).

Chronic illnesses such as HIV and Hepatitis B were reported, often accompanied by medication side effects and reduced physical strength.

“I am sick of HIV… the drugs weaken me.” (R,20).

“I have Hepatitis B.” (R,19).

“My condition is not okay.” (R,21).

To supplement on chronic illnesses, a key informant had this to said:

“There was a client who had been battling with diabetes for so long, but the treatment, medical bills, and seeing how the family struggled to keep him alive. He thought that instead of letting people suffer and tomorrow he still die, it is better to die and that one left a suicide note.” (KII:1, Female, 36 years old)

Socio-economic deprivation

Participants highlighted severe socio-economic challenges such as food insecurity, lack of shelter and poor living conditions, school fee challenges, caregiving burdens, financial difficulties, barriers in to accessing services, unemployment, poverty, lack of land, income, and livelihood opportunities and medical expenses as proximal stressors leading to suicide attempts in Rhino Camp.

One refugee suicide survivor reported prolonged periods without basic necessities as explained:

“I stayed in Tika for 2 years without shelter, no food… I was tired of looking for ways of survival by digging for people and my garden.” (R,23)

Care giving responsibilities was another common experience. Care giving burdens extended beyond children to elderly parents, intensifying economic pressure. Refugee suicide survivor explained:

“I am the only one taking care of my mother who is old and sick… no one could support me.” (R,15)

This study also revealed that inadequate food deliveries can have severe implications for individuals' physical and mental well-being, leading to heightened stress, hopelessness, and desperation. Refugee suicide survivors participants had this to say:

“Food is very little and no land for cultivation.” (R,15).

“Lack of clothing and food for children to eat.” (R,19).

A key informant shared how disputes or struggles over access to food or its distribution can create a severe emotional and psychological impact on those affected.

“Recently we had a reduction of food rations, and they categorized it into three categories: 1,2,3. Category 3, which is the worst means they will not be receiving any food, but where does a refugee get money to buy this food? Nowhere because they do not have work, no jobs, no employment opportunities in the settlement, they entirely rely on donor handouts and what partners provide. A reduction in food ratios has typically increased the suicide rates in Rhino settlement and partners implementing MHPSS in Rhino camp are finding a peak/rise in suicide cases, and the causes are usually around the food.” (KII:2, Male, 34 years old).

To supplement, two key informants stated:

“One of the reasons could be because of food reduction. The food given to them is not enough to feed their families and that also brings back the issues of suicidal attempts.” (KII:4, Male, 44 years old).

“Since then, when we were in our own country (home), we used to access our own food by ourselves, but now accessing food is also very hard, accessing land here is also very hard. All these things are the reasons why refugees attempt suicide.” (KII:6, Female, 28 years old).

School fee challenges were cited as a major contributor to suicides. Participants described feeling overwhelmed when children were sent home from school.

“My child was in S.4… I was looking for credit from people. There was no money.” (R,20)

“My child was sent home because of money.” (R,23)

“I got married because of lack of school fees.” (R,22)

This study found that financial difficulties were a contributing factor in attempted suicide. The stress and strain of financial problems cause individuals to experience feelings of hopelessness, desperation, and helplessness, making them vulnerable to suicidal thoughts and behaviors. For example, one refugee suicide survivors’ particpant said:

“There was no money, nobody helped me.” (R,20).

A key informant explained that:

“Chronic illnesses may lead to financial loss for example spending money on the treatment and medical bills. They are refugees, they have small incomes,….” (KII:1, Female, 36 years old).

According to another key informant,

“The level of poverty is too high in our settlement and because of such reasons, people end up attempting to commit suicide.” (KII, June 2023).

While supporting the above statement, a key informant argued that:

“The other trigger is unemployment. We all realize that everybody is yearning to be employed. Even the children who go to school, they go hoping that they will be employed. When one is jobless, they begin to think in two ways. When you overthink, Satan can drive you to one side and you may end up committing suicide.” (KII:7, Male, 42 years old).

A refugee suicide survivor shared her experience of lack of shelter and poor living conditions:

“I stayed without shelter, food, and support, and felt the world was not worth living in.” (R,23)

“No one can build for me. When it rains at night, we have to stand all night because the roof is leaking everywhere.” (R,15).

Traumatic loss and war experiences

Many participants shared experiences of violence, persecution, and war-related losses in their home countries including, the killing of spouses and siblings. Others reported rape during conflict or within the settlement, leading to traumatic pasts that affected their mental health. They experienced long-lasting effects on their mental health, which developed into depression, anxiety, and other psychological health issues.

Refugee suicide survivors had these to explained their experiences about the killing of their spouses during the war as below:

“I thought of the death of my first husband in the war, who was so nice to me, coupled with camp stressors. I went to drinking and abandoning my children alone. I drank alcohol for one full month without eating because my only wish was death, and even if people advised me to stop drinking, I would not accept.” (R,15)

“My husband was killed by unknown men and my husband had nobody even the brothers are not there. This pains me a lot always hence nothing left a part from suicide.” (R,19)

Another refugee suicide survivor explained her experience on war-related loss that:

“Back in South Sudan, I was very rich… all those things were taken.” And all those things contributed to the thought of suicide attempt. (R,20)

A refugee suicide survivor reported rape during conflict and within the settlement, leading to traumatic pasts that affected her mental health. She felt hopeless, powerless, and unable to envision a better future, increasing her vulnerability to suicidal thoughts and actions.

“I was raped by unknown men back in South Sudan during the war. The second rape happened to me in the settlement by three unknown men last year on 20th October when I went to Ocea Health Centre because I was sick on my way back. I got pregnant out of rape. I felt humiliated, hopeless and shameful.” (R,19)

Psychological distress and cognitive overload

Participants reported high levels of psychological distress due to hopelessness, overthinking, shame and humiliation, anger, confusion and feeling “worthless”, depression, trauma, PTSD symptoms, treatment discontinuation (relapse), and suicidal ideation and trauma memories as asserted by the voices below:

A 33 year-old female shared her experience:

“I felt very disappointed with life, sad, hopeless, and angry with myself. My husband rejected me and my children, the world is not worth my living in. So when all these things came into my mind, a plan of drowning myself and my children in the river came into my mind.” (R,23).

A 40-year-old female also shared her experience:

“I cried a lot, depressed, and traumatized for three days. I just picked the rope, locked the door, tied it in my house and climbed on the roof, only to find myself in Ocea Health Center. I went through severe pain. I felt shame, and guilt for what I had done, and embarrassed.” (R,15).

Similarly, a 44-year-old female expressed that:

“I was overthinking about the issue of school fees for my child. By then, my child was going for the final exams and I was looking for credit from people. There was no money, nobody helped me. That is when that incident started.” (R,20).

A 30 year-old male shared his experience:

“I had too much thinking preoccupied with thoughts of death, feeling depressed, hopelessness, sadness and anger.” (R,10)

A 33 year-old female stated that:

“I felt worthless, sad, tired of life, depressed, and traumatized where I became confused in my mind, and overthinking about death. Thought of ending my life so that I rest from this world. These leads me in to attempting suicide four times, twice in South Sudan and twice in Uganda.” (R,21)

A 26 year-old said:

“Out of frustration, anger and shame, over thinking that this man has bitten my ear off, I am different in the community, people are laughing and I am useless in this world. No support, sickness of the child and general suffering made me to attempt suicide using drugs for the first time.” (R,22)

Stigma, social judgement and isolation

Findings indicate that the community insults in the form of gossip and mockery, religious condemnation (sin), labeling (“bad heart,” “mad,” “drunkard”), withdrawal and isolation, self-blame, embarrassment, loneliness, crisis moments and lack of social support systems further exacerbate participants' mental health challenges as shared by the following statements:

“Some people were insulting me that I have a bad heart and others were saying I should have sought for help from them. Still after my plan failed and there was no assistance, I would have done it because at that state of my mind, I was very confused, having disturbing emotions that made me to feel like I was dreaming and only thinking of ways to die.” (R,23)

“People were sometimes laughing at me.” (R,20)

“Everyone is saying this is the woman who wanted to kill her child.” (R,21)

“People are laughing at me.” (R,22)

“The way some people talked caused me stress.” (R,19)

“It is sinful.” (R,20)

Cultural stigma

Participants expressed the existence of cultural stigmatization around mental health issues, which prevented them from seeking help and support. Some participants avoided visiting neighbors due to fear of judgment. Stigma not only followed attempts but also exacerbated vulnerability by reinforcing isolation and shame.

A participant shared her bitter experience below:

“There are so many who were even laughing sometimes at me. Stigmatization is present, because at times when I go to some funeral, people see me and start commenting about my body being thin, because when I was working as a businesswoman, I used to have a good body. But nowadays, you find people saying, “Is she the one or she is not the one?. People are just trying to give you hardship, and always those things are there. It even contributed to not visiting people, because always when I go, I think that maybe they are going to talk about me. That is why staying alone could all contribute to this thought that could bring me to attempt suicide again.” (R,20).

Suicide attempts as crisis responses

The participants’ narratives indicated that suicide attempts emerged as crisis responses to accumulated stress, were often planned and executed using various methods such as rope (hanging), ingestion of drugs, poison, knife and planned drowning. Three participants reported multiple attempts. Suicide attempts were sometimes planned in advance, while others were impulsive reactions following quarrels or acute humiliation.

The refugee suicide survivors’ participants’ explained:

“A plan of drowning in the river together with my children came into my mind… I prepared them and we walked to the riverside.” (R,23)

“I picked the drugs and swallowed… I felt unconscious. The second suicide attempt was using a rope where I am currently staying.” (R,15)

“I tied the rope around my neck and threw myself down… the rope broke, and I was rescued.” (R,10)

Community rescue, institutional intervention, and psychosocial support

Participants expressed that community rescue, police intervention, health facilities, counseling and psychotherapy, material assistance, religious/spiritual support, home/social visits and follow-ups are some of the support systems and interventions available for refugee suicide survivors. They have significant influence and are often the first point of contact for individuals seeking help. These leaders play a crucial role in building trust and facilitating community-related support for those dealing with mental-health challenges.

Community and family support

This study revealed various community structures put in place by organizations to provide assistance to individuals in need. Participant information provided a detailed account.

“You see the individuals who attempted suicide and also those who have completed suicides, they all come from the family. So, I can say that the family is the first support system that we can dwell on. From my experience in the sector is that, people who have suicide tendencies are best understood at the family level because if somebody starts to lament over issues that are very hard, when they start to behave in ways that they don't even know what they are doing, they are always isolated, and they want to stay alone. They can best be addressed at the family level if there are family members for example if there is a wife, husband, or children and so on. Family in totality is the first system that we know can support us in the management of attempted suicides.” (KII:3, Male, 42 years old).

Another participant suggested that:

“Community support systems for mental health vary in awareness, with some known to the community and others not. Organizations have established community structures, such as CRTs (Crisis Response Teams), VPAs (Volunteer Psychosocial Assistants), Community Based Sociotherapy Facilitators (CBS), Community Development Workers, and Village Health Teams, to aid during times of crisis and suicide attempts. Religious, cultural, and opinion leaders serve as trusted first contacts, while mental health professionals, including psychologists, social workers, and psychiatric clinical officers, offer counseling and therapies. Health facilities within and outside the community provide ongoing support, treatment, and follow-ups for individuals dealing with mental health conditions.” (KII:1, Female, 36 years old).

Relatedly, one more participant explained that:

“Community structures such as Crisis Response Teams, Volunteer Psychosocial Assistants, and Village Health Teams are empowered at the grassroots level. They receive training in Psychological First Aid and Medical First Aid, and have established reporting mechanisms and referral pathways. They work closely with community leaders and ambulances to respond to emergencies like suicide attempts and maternal health needs. Refugee Welfare Councils also play a supportive role. Trained professionals, including social workers, psychiatric clinical officers, and psychologists, provide comprehensive psychosocial support. They conduct mental health assessments, offer counseling and coping therapies to clients and their families. Cases are discussed in sector working groups and settlement coordination meetings to ensure appropriate support is provided.” (KII:2, Male, 34 years old).

“I reported to the Refugee Welfare Council, but he continued.”(R,15)

Role of religious, cultural, and opinion or other leaders

A participant had this to say about the support system:

“Religious, cultural, and opinion leaders are first contact/point of entry and Community trusts them”, (KII:1, Female, 36 years old).

Refugee suicide survivors shared that:

“My church organized Prayers; I gave my life to Jesus Christ.” (R,10, R,22)

“An old man came and stopped me from drowning my children and myself.” (R,23)

“My child ran to RWC and informed them, and they removed the rope from me.” (R,19)

In addition, a participant expressed that:

“We also have spiritual leaders who help provide counseling or talk to the families. We also have an African traditional setting where the elders in the community intervene in cases of suicide. We engage with different partners who intervene in the cases of attempted suicide and suicidal cases and provide follow-up with the affected persons to see how they are coping. We also involve police when necessary, at times, we need reinforcement when the situation requires a victim to be kept under safe custody.” (KII:4, Male, 44 years old)

Psychosocial support providers

This study revealed that psychosocial support providers play a vital role in helping individuals who have attempted suicide navigate through the complex emotional aftermath, providing them with the necessary tools and support to foster healing, resilience, and a renewed sense of hope for the future.

To illustrate, a participant highlighted that,

“Organizations dealing with psychosocial support have people who represent them within the community, for example, psychologists, social workers, and psychiatric clinical officers who provide counseling, advanced therapies, and treatment of mental health conditions, etc.” (KII:1, Female, 36 years old).

Similarly, another participant expressed that,

“The MHPSS partners come in to provide MHPSS aspects. The organization comprises trained professional social workers, psychiatric clinical officers, and psychologists. Their work is to carry out mental health assessments on the clients, profile, and assess cases, and take clients through counseling and coping therapies, not only for individuals who have attempted suicide but also immediate family members and the community because they all suffer this trauma. After profiling and clerking the clients’ case. The cases are forwarded to the sector working group meetings and conferences. These cases further go to the settlement coordination meeting after the root cause of suicide is ascertained, where all partners come to discuss and provide support according to the need.” (KII:2, Male, 34 years old).

Another participant shared that:

“Organizations like TPO provided home visits, counseling, and Psychological First Aid through Social Workers and Clinical Psychologists. Psychosocial support provided by the family, friends, and community members. I also got help from the health facility through the treatment and medication I received. Additionally, prayers were organized by my church at my home.” (R,10).

Health facilities support

Participants reported that health facilities play an essential role in supporting individuals with mental health issues both within and outside the community. They offer treatment, follow-up, and access to mental health professionals, who can provide support within the community.

In support of health facilities, participants had this to say,

“I woke up in the health center; treatment saved my life.” (R18)

“Health facilities within the community and outside communities that keep on supporting such people have mental health professionals who can easily go into the community and support. Health facilities offer treatment and follow-ups for clients” (KII:1, Female, 36 years old).

Like the voices above, another participant added that:

“Some of the services, include the health services, community health outreach programs by the health workers, and referrals by the partners. Rescue services in terms of ambulance services are available, for example, when one attempts to commit suicide and is not yet dead, the person is taken to the health center. From the health center, counseling and treatment of the wounds are provided. The services are also through the VHT systems. There's continuous follow-up on how this person is coping, and there is also reporting.” (KII:4, Male, 44 years old).

Meaning-making, coping mechanisms, and recovery

Coping mechanisms among refugees in Rhino Camp, West Nile, Uganda, refer to the strategies and resources that individuals use to navigate the challenges and stressors they face, including those related to their mental health. Given their limited access to formal mental health services, refugees often rely on various coping mechanisms to address their emotional struggles. Participants reported that they utilized coping mechanisms, such as support from family (motivation through children), spiritual practices like faith and spiritual transformation, which provided hope and moral grounding, livelihood engagement (farming and house construction), peer support, and cognitive reframing, to deal with their mental health struggles. Recovery involved gradual reconstruction of meaning. Some participants transitioned into roles supporting others, reflecting post-crisis growth.

To illustrate this, a 33-year-old woman said:

“Right now, the way I am coping, I managed to secure some land that I am digging, and I also constructed my houses alone, which has helped to reduce the thought of suicide. Friends helped me to cope with my suicide attempt.” (R,23).

Participants highlighted counseling, spiritual guidance, and material assistance as critical for recovery.

“TPO provided counseling… cognitive behavioral therapy helped me cope with my thoughts.” (R,21)

“My church organized prayers; I gave my life to Jesus Christ.” (R,10, R,22)

“They brought food, soap, beans, flour, and cooking oil… my heart calmed after the distribution.” (R,23)

Similarly, regarding livelihood engagement, participants shared their experiences that:

“Cultivation has given me happiness… it keeps me busy and reduces thoughts of ending my life.” (R21)

“I constructed my house alone.” (R,10).

On the peer support, a refugee suicide survivor shared that:

“I joined the peacemaker group in our community where we counsel and help people going through distress.” (R,21).

“ And then there are some phone calls that were also made to counsel me. Friends helped me to cope with a suicide attempt.” (R,20).

Discussion

Based on the shared views of the key informants and refugee suicide survivors who participated in this study, it was found that intimate partner conflict, and gender-based violence, and suicide are interconnected issues, especially for victims who are experiencing the devastating effects of abuse. Moreover, rejection, abandonment, domestic violence, forced and early marriage, and substance abuse by partners (alcohol-related abuse), infidelity and relational mistrust, sexual violence (rape), and emotional humiliation perpetuate this link. In these situations, relationships can result in exploitation, physical, emotional, and psychological harm, perpetuating inequalities and violating the rights of vulnerable individuals, especially women and children.. The above findings align with sources [24–26] and others that indicate that romantic companions or quasi-sexual assaults have been experienced by 35 percent of women worldwide. To address this, collaboration among various stakeholders, including prevention and early intervention such as awareness, education and the establishment of protective measures to ensure the safety and well-being of at-risk individuals, is necessary. Thomas Joiner's Interpersonal Theory of Suicidal Attempts (2005) provides a valuable lens for understanding the complex relationship between domestic violence and suicide vulnerability among victims. By applying this theory to develop interventions, stakeholders can work together to make the surroundings for those who are abused secure and compassionate to ultimately lower suicidality and promote healthier outcomes.

Turning to health burden and physical vulnerability: This study indicated that health burden, mental illness, and attempted suicide are closely related, and individuals with chronic illnesses and mental health conditions are at an increased risk of attempting suicide, similar to the overall population. A study of 105 cases (31 females and 74 males) of major depressive disorder, revealed a significant relationship between mental pain and suicidality [30]. By showing that those who attempted suicide were more likely to experience severe psychological anguish (odds ratios [ORs] between 1.02, and 1.17, P < 0.001) and the purpose of suicide attempt being unclear (ORs between 11.57, and 11.77; P < 0.001), additional evidence for the importance of psychological anguish alongside suicide risk was offered by [53]. Additionally, they were more likely to suffer from major depressive disorder (ORs between 1.62 and 1.70; P < 0.05) and personality psychological disorders (ORs between 2.65 and 3.01; P < 0.001; ORs for others between 1.96 and 2.28). A systematic review of 15 studies found that mental pain was a significant predictor of suicidal ideation and attempt vulnerability [31]. During adolescence, intense negative emotions such as guilt, shame, and hopelessness can cause psychological pain, and numerous studies have shown a link between psychological pain and the ability to commit suicide [31]. Thus, a comprehensive approach is needed to recognize warning signs, reduce stigma, promote mental health awareness, invest in mental healthcare, and implement suicide prevention programs. In addition, a compassionate society prioritizing mental well-being can save lives and support those in need. By understanding these factors, stakeholders can develop tailored interventions and create a compassionate society that prioritizes mental well-being, ultimately saving lives and supporting those in need among the refugee population in Rhino Camp.

This study highlighted socioeconomic deprivation such as food insecurity, lack of shelter and poor living conditions, challenges with school fees, caregiving burden, financial difficulties, barriers to accessing services, unemployment, poverty, lack of land, income and livelihood opportunities, and medical expenses as proximal stressors leading to suicide attempts in Rhino Camp. The stress and strain of socioeconomic deprivation cause individuals to experience feelings of hopelessness, desperation, and helplessness, making them vulnerable to suicidal thoughts and behaviors. The results of this study are broadly consistent with previous research on household foreclosure and financial insecurity, which are thought to be triggers that lead to an increase in suicide risk due to co-morbidity with additional risk factors such as depression [20, 21], anxiety, violence, and harmful alcohol use. Therefore, socioeconomic deprivation, such as those linked to individual hardships caused by job or asset losses, can be related to individual suicide risk. According to [54], those seeking asylum and refugees might be in danger of suffering poor social conditions upon arrival in another country; they might reside at the bottom of the social scale. Similarly, [55], asserted that anxiety and depression are closely associated with poverty and social exclusion among refugees and asylum seekers. These findings suggest that the relationship between poverty and suicidality is not only economic in nature but also deeply social and psychological, as displacement disrupts social identity, dignity, and coping capacity. In refugee settings such as Rhino Camp, prolonged dependency on humanitarian aid and limited livelihood opportunities may further intensify feelings of powerlessness and uncertainty about the future, thereby increasing emotional distress and vulnerability to suicidal behavior. This highlights the need for interventions that move beyond emergency relief to include sustainable livelihood and social protection strategies that restore autonomy, resilience, and hope among refugees. The study therefore suggests that fostering community-based support initiatives, including the integration of livelihood activities into Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) programs, may improve both refugee well-being and financial stability. Food insecurity emerged as a particularly important dimension of socioeconomic deprivation that significantly affected mental health and increased the risk of suicidal thoughts and attempts. Rather than treating food insecurity as a separate issue, the findings indicate that it operates within a broader cycle of poverty, psychological distress, and social instability. This supports previous results showing that food insecurity contributes to suicidal thoughts and actions through pathways such as chronic stress, poor nutrition, shame, stigma and worsening mental health outcomes. Additionally, undernutrition has been linked to food insecurity in older adults [56], and this condition is positively correlated with suicidality [57]. However, the findings of this study extend existing literature by illustrating how food insecurity in refugee settings may also undermine social roles and family functioning. For example, an inability to provide food for children or dependents may generate feelings of inadequacy, guilt, and perceived burdensomeness, particularly among caregivers and household heads. Such experiences may weaken social connectedness and increase emotional isolation, especially in contexts where displacement has already disrupted traditional family and community support systems. Thomas Joiner's Interpersonal Theory of Suicidal Attempts (2005) provides further insight into these findings by explaining how thwarted belongingness (feelings of social isolation or disconnection) and perceived burdensomeness (the sense that one is a burden to others) interact to heighten suicide risk. Among refugees, food insecurity and extreme poverty may reinforce perceptions of being unable to contribute meaningfully to one’s family or community, while displacement and loss of social networks intensify social disconnection. The chronic stress, anxiety, and depression connected with food insecurity further exacerbate mental health challenges, making the co-occurrence of these risk factors more likely. The findings underscore the importance of combining this theory with comprehensive measures and multisectoral interventions that address both socioeconomic deprivation and mental health needs may effectively reduce the risk of suicide in vulnerable populations affected by food insecurity. Efforts to alleviate poverty, improve food accessibility, expand livelihood opportunities, and provide access to land for cultivation through the Office of the Prime Minister and collaboration with host communities may help reduce suicide risk among refugees. Strengthening social support systems, integrating economic empowerment initiatives within MHPSS programs, and promoting early identification of mental distress are also critical. Collaborative approaches involving governments, humanitarian organizations, and local communities are essential to addressing the interconnected challenges of food insecurity, poverty, and mental health in refugee Camps.

This study revealed that traumatic loss and war experiences, inform of violence, persecution, and war-related losses in their home countries, including the killing of spouses and siblings, rape during conflict or within the Camp have a profound and lasting impact on refugees, making them more vulnerable to suicidal ideation and behaviors. Losses associated with displacement, such as separation from family and friends, loss of birthplace, diminished social respect, disruption of public connections, loss of property and income, and economic instability [33], lead to a lasting impact of trauma, which eventually prompts refugees to attempt or complete suicide. A study of the protracted mental health of war-affected refugees reported high levels of trauma, mental illnesses such as post-traumatic stress disorder and depression [12], and anxiety disorders in refugees ranging from 20% or more after resettlement. As a result, before, during, and/or after flight, suicidal thoughts and attempts are likely to occur among refugees and/or asylum seekers [15]. The probability of psychological illnesses evolving in displaced people is significantly increased due to the large number of traumatic situations that the majority of displaced people experience as a result of having to flee their country. According to [58–61], depression, anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder, and somatization seem to be the most prevalent disorders. Suicide attempts and thoughts are more common in people with mental illnesses and difficult life circumstances. Teenage girls have high rates of emotional disorders and suicidal thoughts. Individual and family initiatives may be beneficial for teenage girls who are at increased risk of suicide and mental trauma [12]. Addressing the mental health needs of refugees and providing comprehensive support and trauma-informed care are essential in fostering healing and resilience among those who have endured violence and loss. Recognizing the significance of mental well-being and offering appropriate resources can help refugees cope with their trauma and envision a more hopeful future.

Regarding psychological distress as a theme, the shared experiences from participants revealed that hopelessness, overthinking, shame and humiliation, anger, confusion, and feeling “worthless”, depression, trauma, PTSD symptoms, treatment discontinuation (relapse), and suicidal ideation and trauma memories lead to suicide attempts among refugees in Rhino Camp. The study findings are congruent with previous studies, according to [27], who found that suicide attempts were three times more likely to occur in people who felt extreme hopelessness than in those who did not. According to evidence, those who feel defeated have already forgotten the significance and intent of their lives. This can result in several mental illnesses and serious suicide attempts [62]. The findings of this study suggest that it is essential to recognize the indications of discouragement or despair and comprehend the pain that patients experience in therapy, provide necessities, foster a sense of community and family support, and offer culturally appropriate mental health services. This approach aims to help refugees cope with distress and rebuild their lives with hope and resilience. Collaboration among governments, humanitarian organizations, and local communities is essential for implementing sustainable solutions for the well-being of refugees.

Regarding stigma, social judgement and isolation as another theme, the study findings indicate that the community insults, in the inform of gossip and mockery, religious condemnation (sin), labeling (“bad heart,” “mad,” “drunkard”), withdrawal and isolation, self-blame, embarrassment, loneliness, crisis moments and lack of social support systems further exacerbate participants' mental health challenges leading to suicides. The study findings align with a study conducted in 2019, which states that people experience a sense of loneliness and a fear of abandonment when they feel cut off from their closest social group, consisting of spouses, relatives, acquaintances, as well as important nobles [63]. Depression, feelings of loneliness and despair, and isolation frequently co-exist. According to a systematic review on the topic, both familial and suicidal loneliness and disconnection are highly associated with one another [28]. However, the results vary slightly by region. Additionally, youth have received the most attention in the most frequent time frame, which is a main problem due to the inability to miss the youthful generations [28]. Other research on adolescents and young adults found a strong connection between subjective feelings of loneliness and suicidal outcomes, particularly suicide attempts and suicide ideation [29]. An indirect link between suicide and older age via hopelessness, depression, and unfavorable attitudes toward health has been suggested by the association between older age and social isolation. Physical illness did not increase the risk. According to multivariate analyses, depression, social exclusion, and unfavorable health attitudes had a full mediating effect on the effects of hopelessness on suicide [64]. Joiner et al. (2009) recognized a modest effect of indifference to thoughts of suicide on feelings as well as family social support [65]. Such results were comprehended by the authors in the suicide Interpersonal Psychological Theory context, which establishes that interpersonal concepts, purported distress, and frustrated belonging create the motivation to die as a necessary prerequisite to suicide attempts. This study supports the notion that having more social connections is protective against suicide, even though suicide attempts differ from suicidal thoughts or behaviors and are likely to be relatively common in adolescents. Creating opportunities for social interaction within refugee camps is crucial to improving mental well-being. This can include community events, support groups, and cultural exchange programs. Promoting tolerance and inclusivity as well as providing mental health support services are also important in mitigating social isolation and enhancing the overall happiness of refugees in the Rhino Camp. Research and humanitarian organizations support community-based approaches to address mental health challenges and promote resilience among refugees.

Still under the above theme, the study found that cultural stigma surrounding mental health among refugees can have significant consequences, hindering their ability to seek help and worsening their condition. The results of this study correspond with those of other studies on cultural stigma. Refugees and asylum seekers have a high chance of being at increased risk when they are cut off from their household members, culture, and religion [19]. The mental health of refugees and asylum seekers may be adversely affected by cultural identity loss and ties during relocation [66]. The Protective Culture Model [67] may be used to explain protective factors against suicide (such as religious convictions, strong family ties, and social support) that may be connected to one's culture of origin. This reduces the stress of acculturation. Even so, as time passes in the host nation, this protection wanes, leading to an increase in suicidal thoughts and actions in the second generation. Cross-cultural stress, encounters with discriminatory practices, cultural differences, and environmental factors are some of the factors that impact suicidality among refugees [67]. Although encounters before relocation powerfully affect psychological health and wellness, findings demonstrate that encounters in life after relocation may also have an impact on the psychological well-being of refugees and asylum seekers [66], with robust resettlement practices showing positive outcomes for mental health [68]. To address this, a multifaceted approach is essential, including education, awareness campaigns, and community involvement. Engaging community leaders and offering culturally sensitive mental health services can break down barriers and create a more supportive environment. Empowering refugees to advocate for their mental health further enhances their happiness in Rhino Camp.

Suicide attempts as a crisis responses emerged as the main theme. This study’s findings indicated that suicide attempt are crisis response to accumulated stress, were often planned and executed using a variety of methods such as rope (hanging), ingestion of drugs, poisoning, knife, and planned drowning. Refugee study participants reported having multiple attempts, and some were sometimes planned, while others were impulsive reactions following quarrels or acute humiliation.

Community rescue, institutional intervention, and psychosocial support as one of the main theme for this study. The study indicates that community rescue, police intervention, health facilities, counseling and psychotherapy, material assistance, religious/spiritual support, home/social visits and follow-ups are some of the support systems and interventions available for refugee suicide survivors.

From the shared views of the participants regarding community and family support, this study disclosed that the presence of well-organized and comprehensive mental health support systems within communities is vital for addressing suicide attempts and mental health challenges effectively. These systems involve various community structures, trained professionals, and families. While parental involvement is generally associated with reduced suicidal ideation and attempts through mitigation of depressive symptoms among non-refugees according to the latest findings involving 251 South-West Nigerian students from its two universities [69], in refugee settings, its protective effect may be limited. Many parents face high stress, competing survival priorities, and may have limited knowledge of mental health or the warning signs of suicidal behavior. According to Olatunji et al. (2020), family support is a form of communal support that provides practical, monetary, and affective assistance. The person's closest friends and family (couples, households, relatives, and significant individuals) have the greatest effect and may be helpful during difficult periods. A significant source may be friends and family, who provide communal, affective, and monetary support, which lessen the effects of external stressors. Resilience built from this aid reduces the risk of suicide associated with a traumatized childhood [70]. Relationships are especially protective for young people and the elderly, who have developed levels of dependency. However, potential gaps need attention, including addressing mental health stigma, improving access to remote areas, strengthening crisis response capacity, enhancing coordination and communication among support entities, ensuring long-term follow-up, promoting cultural sensitivity, establishing data and monitoring systems, and integrating mental health with primary healthcare. By addressing these gaps, mental health support systems can become more effective, inclusive, and responsive to communities’ diverse needs. Collaboration among stakeholders is essential for creating comprehensive mental health support systems that prioritize the well-being and resilience of individuals facing mental health challenges.

The study results revealed that religious, cultural, and opinion leaders in communities have a significant influence and are often the first point of contact for individuals seeking help for mental health challenges. They play a crucial role in building trust, breaking the stigma, and facilitating community-related support. These leaders provide culturally relevant and accessible mental health assistance, mobilize community resources, and promote a holistic approach to well-being. Refugee suicide attempt survivors frequently rely on divine principles and observe as opposed to formal clerical membership, according to [71] and [72]. These activities aid in overcoming despair, the most significant reliable risk factor for suicide, as well as a symbol in the hearts of individuals who have psychological disorders. They encourage people to seek personal fulfillment and a sense of belonging outside of the mainstream. Positive psychological health outcomes lead to a great deal of happiness and is typically associated with religious faith and spirituality [73, 74]. These relationships include having access to social support, being resilience to stress, emotional stability and calmness, inner fortitude and empowerment, feelings of happiness, and a lower likelihood to attempting suicide. According to trauma survivors, spirituality helps them to recover in part by transforming their anger and feelings of powerlessness into self-agency [75]. Collaboration between these leaders, mental health professionals, and community organizations is essential for creating a comprehensive and effective mental health support system that meets the diverse needs of individuals facing mental health challenges.

Based on the shared views from the participants who participated in the research identified that psychosocial support providers are instrumental in helping individuals who have attempted suicide navigate the emotional aftermath, fostering healing, resilience, and hope for the future. They create a safe and nonjudgmental environment where they actively listen to and validate individuals’ experiences. Their roles include offering coping strategies, exploring triggers, and addressing underlying issues. They build hope, provide long-term support; and adopt a holistic approach to address emotional, social, and spiritual aspects. Additionally, they may refer individuals to professional mental health services when needed and engage in suicide prevention initiatives. Compassionate support plays a crucial role in individuals' healing and contributes to suicide prevention. The study is congruent with other studies that revealed that suicide survivors who have been portraying real upsetting encounters or proactively taking chances to improve are backed by high, lasting, collectively strong partnerships with clinicians [76]. Individuals with psychiatric illnesses, even those who are unsociable and have little fun, may advance as well as practice helpful coping mechanisms to control psychological pressure and to safeguard and improve their health with the help of such assistance [77, 78]. A few researchers have emphasized the effectiveness of mutual treatment in suicide prevention due to the interdisciplinary team’s collaboration having a positive effect on the psychological well-being of sick people [79, 80]

The shared opinions of the participants revealed that collaborative and supportive approaches to dealing with attempted suicide involve combining the resources and expertise of health facilities with compassion and understanding of the community. It integrates professional mental health services with community support to effectively promote mental health and well-being. This approach includes providing integrated care, offering compassionate support, raising awareness through education, and implementing suicide-prevention strategies. By working together, stakeholders can create a comprehensive and caring support system that addresses mental health challenges and effectively promotes suicide prevention. Suicidal behavior can be decreased by receiving sufficient, prompt, and available therapeutic interventions to treat mental illness and substance abuse [81, 82]. Essential strategies to broaden access to healthcare services and policies across all health sectors, especially at community levels. The use of proper context by healthcare professionals, clear messaging to users about available services, and more streamlined client flow through the system are all examples of best practices. Health literacy policies and strategies should pay particular attention to mental health literacy [83, 84]. The wishes of survivors of suicide attempts include cooperative expertise, coordination of care, more follow-up endeavors, and very few transfers throughout the course of treatment [85]. According to [86] and [87], service users regularly believe that some experts are ill-equipped to address suicidality and stress the significance of early and regular opportunities for therapeutic inquiry into relevant subjects and biomedical initiatives to relieve suffering, with neither being seen as a replacement for the other. Related favorable and unfavorable opinions on psychotropic drugs have also been documented in previous research [86, 88]. The above point to the significance of a collaborative and supportive approach in dealing with attempted suicide, combining the resources and expertise of health facilities with compassion and understanding from the community promote mental health and well-being.

The last main theme was meaning-making, coping mechanisms, and recovery. Coping mechanisms among refugees in Rhino Camp, West Nile, Uganda, refer to the strategies and resources that individuals use to navigate the challenges and stressors they face, including those related to their mental health. Given their limited access to formal mental health services, refugees often rely on various coping mechanisms to cope with their emotional struggles. This study findings indicate that some participants used coping mechanisms, such as support from family (motivation through children), spiritual practices like faith and spiritual transformation which provided hope and moral grounding, livelihood engagement (farming and house construction), peer support and cognitive reframing, to deal with their mental health struggles. Recovery involved gradual reconstruction of meaning. Other studies have revealed that connection plays a crucial role in recovery, and social environments are crucial for presenting opportunities for doing and being in daily life [89]. Social structures help to reduce the aspirations of persistently disadvantaged groups, causing people to internalize constrained or unsuitable favorites, needs, and expectations to conform to what is practical. Strong interpersonal skills and self-confidence are nurtured in those with serious psychological disorders by proactive, problem-focused stress management techniques, such as a friendly mindset. These strategies have also been linked to self-help participation. These coping skills can be learned, modeled, and supported using cognitive-behavioral methods which are helpful in the practice of mental health and psychosocial support particularly in the management of one's mental health and well-being. To fight feelings of desperation, worthlessness, and loneliness among refugee suicide survivors, supportive networks and healthy coping strategies are therefore helpful in lowering the shame associated with thoughts of suicide.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Strengths of the study

This study is the first to explore the lived experiences of refugees who attempted suicide in Rhino Camp, West Nile, Uganda. This study adds to the body of scientific knowledge on mental health aimed at clarifying the unique experiences of refugee suicide survivors regarding displacement, trauma, and limited access to mental health services.

Study limitations and delimitations

This study was limited by several factors during the fieldwork and included:

First, this study should be interpreted within the context in which it was conducted. The research involved refugee participants from diverse linguistic and cultural backgrounds, and interviews were conducted in multiple languages to accommodate participants’ preferences. While this approach enhanced inclusivity, the use of interpretation and translation may have influenced the depth and nuance of some accounts, as certain meanings, expressions, or culturally embedded concepts may not have been fully captured during translation.

Conclusion

This study provides a nuanced understanding of the factors contributing to suicide attempts among refugees in settlement settings. Analysis of seven key informants and eight refugee suicide survivors’ participants’ narratives revealed that socioeconomic deprivation, intimate partner conflict, and gender-based violence, traumatic loss, war experiences, health burden, and physical vulnerabilities, psychological distress, stigma, social judgment and isolation, were primary drivers of suicidal behavior. Participants experienced extreme poverty, food insecurity, loss of property, caregiving burdens, and educational challenges, domestic abuse, forced and early marriage, and sexual violence, cognitive overload, and emotional confusion, traumatic experiences related to war, bereavement, and chronic illness which amplified stress and feelings of hopelessness. Participants described both planned and impulsive suicide attempts, often using ropes, drugs, poison, planned drowning, and knives.

Importantly, the study highlights the protective role of immediate rescue and psychosocial support interventions by family members, community leaders, police, health workers, and organizations such as TPO and UNHCR, which was instrumental in preventing completed suicides. Participants emphasized that counseling, spiritual support, livelihood engagement, and material assistance were central to recovery and building hope.

The findings underscore that suicide among refugee populations is a multi-dimensional phenomenon, shaped by the interplay of socio-economic, relational, health, and psychological factors. Preventive strategies must therefore be multi-sectoral, integrating mental health services, trauma-informed counseling, community protection, livelihood support, and stigma reduction. By addressing these determinants holistically, humanitarian actors can enhance resilience, promote psychosocial well-being, and reduce the risk of suicide in vulnerable refugee populations.

Declarations

Approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, Lira University Research Ethics Committee (LUREC) no. LUREC-2023–18, and the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology (UNCST) no. HS3046ES before the study was conducted. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. The researcher sought clearance for data collection from the UNHCR and OPM after receiving approval from LUREC and a letter of introduction from the Faculty of Public Health, Lira University.

Informed consent

All respondents were requested to provide their consent before the interview. The consent form was written because it provided an auditable record in the case of questions arising from data storage. Informed consent was obtained by describing the purpose of the study and procedures involved. During data collection, one clinical psychologist was able to speak to anyone upset after participating in this study. The interview process was voluntary, and the participants were free to leave the interview process without penalty.

Confidentiality

Participants were notified that the information from this study would be held and kept strictly private and used only for research purposes. The interviews were conducted in the community counseling space/center. To maintain the privacy of the participants, pseudonyms were used to report direct quotes. Furthermore, the participants remained anonymous (nameless) throughout the study. The records were kept electronically with a password, on paper, and behind locked doors.
