The role of intestinal microbiota in physiologic and body compositional changes that accompany CLA-mediated weight loss in obese mice
Authors: Meifan Zhang, Yue S Yin, Karolline S May, Shari Wang, Hayley Purcell, Xue-Song Zhang, Martin J Blaser, Laura J den Hartigh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveObesity continues to be a major problem, despite known treatment strategies such as lifestyle modifications, pharmaceuticals, and surgical options, necessitating the development of novel weight loss approaches. The naturally occurring fatty acid, 10,12 conjugated linoleic acid (10,12 CLA), promotes weight loss by increasing fat oxidation and browning of white adipose tissue, leading to increased energy expenditure in obese mice. Coincident with weight loss, 10,12 CLA also alters the murine gut microbiota by enriching for microbes that produce short chain fatty acids (SCFAs), with concurrent elevations in fecal butyrate and plasma acetate.MethodsTo determine if the observed microbiota changes are required for 10,12 CLA-mediated weight loss, adult male mice with diet-induced obesity were given broad-spectrum antibiotics (ABX) to perturb the microbiota prior to and during 10,12 CLA-mediated weight loss. Conversely, to determine whether gut microbes were sufficient to induce weight loss, conventionally-raised and germ-free mice were transplanted with cecal contents from mice that had undergone weight loss by 10,12 CLA supplementation.ResultsWhile body weight was minimally modulated by ABX-mediated perturbation of gut bacterial populations, adult male mice given ABX were more resistant to the increased energy expenditure and fat loss that are induced by 10,12 CLA supplementation. Transplanting cecal contents from donor mice losing weight due to oral 10,12 CLA consumption into conventional or germ-free mice led to improved glucose metabolism with increased butyrate production.ConclusionsThese data suggest a critical role for the microbiota in diet-modulated changes in energy balance and glucose metabolism, and distinguish the metabolic effects of orally delivered 10,12 CLA from cecal transplantation of the resulting microbiota.Keywords: Cecal microbial transplant (CMT), Conjugated linoleic acid (CLA), Glucose tolerance test, Insulin tolerance test, Short-chain fatty acids, Butyrate

Introduction

Obesity has reached epidemic proportions across the world [1], necessitating the development of novel weight loss strategies. Lifestyle modifications including exercise and caloric restriction are effective against obesity in the short term, yet obesity persists due to the high predilection for weight regain. As such, people attempting to lose weight often use readily available nutritional supplements such as conjugated linoleic acid (CLA). Trans-10, cis-12 CLA (10,12 CLA), a naturally-occurring dietary fatty acid found in foodstuffs deriving from ruminant animals, and also a major component of CLA weight loss supplements, promotes weight loss in animal models and obese human subjects [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. In mice fed a high fat high sucrose diet (HFHS), we have shown that 10,12 CLA supplementation promotes both weight and fat loss due to increased energy expenditure and adipose tissue fatty acid oxidation, promoting negative energy balance [2,6].

It is becoming increasingly recognized that the gut microbiota may contribute to the development of obesity, potentially by altering host energy metabolism. Gut microbiota composition is altered in both people and mice with obesity, and is also modified by weight loss [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. However, the mechanisms linking gut microbiota with body weight regulation are not well-defined. Whether changes in body weight are responsible for alterations in gut microbiota, or vice versa, remain unknown. We recently showed that host microbiota composition and metabolism are altered by oral 10,12 CLA supplementation, coincident with weight loss [12]. Oral 10,12 CLA administration enriched the gut for genera that produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFA), and increased gut-derived butyrate and circulating acetate [12]. Administration of SCFAs corrects diet-induced obesity [13], potentially by increasing energy expenditure and fat oxidation [14]. The similar phenotypes elicited by 10,12 CLA and by SCFAs suggests causal or synergistic roles in promoting body weight and fat loss.

To address the hypothesis that the previously observed changes in gut microbial composition and metabolism are necessary and sufficient for 10,12 CLA-mediated weight loss, we employed several approaches to perturb gut microbiota in obese mice. First, to determine whether gut microbiota are required for energy balance changes induced by 10,12 CLA, gut microbiota were perturbed by chronic administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics (ABX), then body weight, adiposity, and glucose metabolism were assessed. Next, to determine if gut microbes are sufficient for 10,12 CLA-mediated changes in energy metabolism, conventionally-reared and germ-free (GF) mice received transplants of cecal contents from donor mice that had undergone weight loss by 10,12 CLA supplementation. Mice in which the microbiota were disrupted by ABX exhibited blunted responses to 10,12 CLA, while transplantation of cecal materials from 10,12 CLA-treated mice improved glucose metabolism in recipients, indicating a critical role of the microbiota in mediating these metabolic effects.

Research design and methods

Mouse experiments

Antibiotics (ABX) study (Experiment 1)

Male 10-week-old low-density lipoprotein receptor knock out (Ldlr−/−) mice (Jackson Laboratories #002207) were used in this study (n = 5 mice/group) due to their propensity to develop a phenotype resembling human metabolic syndrome, including insulin resistance, inflammation, and obesity [15], augmenting the translational potential of this work. Mice were housed with a 14:10 light:dark cycle in Allentown IVC cages, and in identical cages within a standard humidity and temperature-controlled Sable System while undergoing indirect calorimetry measurements [2]. Group-housed mice were fed an HFHS diet (BioServ #F4997: 58.9% kcal from fat [lard], 26.2% kcal from sucrose, and 0.15% added cholesterol) (Table S1) [2] for 10 weeks (baseline), then singly housed and divided into two groups: one received an antibiotic cocktail administered ad libitum in the drinking water (ABX: 1 g/L ampicillin, 1 g/L neomycin, 0.5 g/L vancomycin, and 0.5 g/L metronidazole with sugar-free marshmallow flavoring), while the other received vehicle-containing water (VEH: sugar-free marshmallow flavoring). After 1 week of ABX/VEH treatment, mice were further divided into 4 groups (n = 5/group) as follows: (1) HFHS (VEH), (2) HFHS (ABX), (3) HFHS+10,12 CLA (VEH), and (4) HFHS+10,12 CLA (ABX) for a further 8 weeks. The 10,12 CLA was obtained from NuChek Prep (#UC-61-A) and admixed into the HFHS diet at 1% w/w (BioServ #F6586). This CLA supplementation of the mice mirrors the level that obese humans would consume during CLA supplementation [2]. Body composition was assessed using quantitative magnetic resonance spectrometry before and during each intervention period, as described below. Indirect calorimetry was performed using Sable Systems metabolic cages, as described below [2]. Mice were subjected to an intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (GTT, 1 mg glucose/kg body weight, 20% glucose solution) and an intraperitoneal insulin tolerance test (ITT, 1 U/kg lean body weight) one week prior to euthanasia by cervical dislocation and exsanguination under isoflurane anesthesia, as described [2]. See Supplemental Fig. S1 for a depiction of the study design. All experimental procedures were approved by the University of Washington Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and followed the NIH guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH publication no. 8023, revised 1978), and complied with the ARRIVE guidelines in accordance with the U.K. Animals Act.

Cecal transplant studies in conventionally-raised mice and germ-free (GF) mice (Experiments 2 and 3)

Preparation of cecal contents

Cecal contents for use as donor material were collected from male Ldlr−/− mice that had been fed an HFHS diet for 20 weeks with: (1) 1% 10,12 CLA added during the last 8 weeks of intervention (CLA-CT, 10,12 CLA cecal transplant donors, n = 8); or (2) without 10,12 CLA (Ob-CT, obese cecal transplant donors, n = 8). At euthanasia, ceca were opened longitudinally in an anaerobic chamber using a sterile blade, and contents were scraped into pre-reduced sterile PBS containing 50% glycerol (2 mL per cecum, n = 8 pooled cecal contents per treatment group). Cecal contents were filtered through sterile mesh (300 μm) and stored at −80 °C.

Cecal microbial transplant (CMT) in conventionally-raised recipients

Male 10-week-old Ldlr−/− recipient mice were fed an HFHS diet for 12 weeks to induce features of metabolic syndrome. To facilitate engraftment of cecal transplantation, all mice were then given ad libitum water containing ABX (1 g/L ampicillin, 1 g/L neomycin, 0.5 g/L vancomycin, and 0.5 g/L metronidazole with sugar-free marshmallow flavoring) during week 11 for 7 days, then changed back to untreated water. Mice were then inoculated with cecal contents from donor mice that had previously consumed a HFHS diet for 20 weeks with or without 10,12 CLA supplementation (CLA-CT or Ob-CT), described previously [16]. Control groups were given saline by oral gavage (Sal). Cecal contents (150 μL) were administered via oral gavage beginning one day after cessation of ABX treatment, with 6 gavages over the course of the next two weeks (n = 8 recipient mice per donor group). Body composition was assessed before and 2 weeks after the CMT. Mice underwent glucose and insulin tolerance testing 3 weeks after the CMT. See Supplemental Fig. S2 for experimental details.

CMT in germ-free (GF) recipient mice

Male C57BL/6J mice that had been reared germ-free (GF) were administered a single dose of cecal contents (100 μL) from the same donor mice described above (Ob-CT or CLA-CT) by oral gavage at 6 weeks of age. Control mice were inoculated with cecal contents from age-matched normal chow-fed Ldlr−/− male mice (Chow-CT). Inoculated mice continued to eat an irradiated normal chow diet (5LG4, LabDiet) until 10 weeks of age, then were changed to an HFHS diet (non-irradiated) until they were 14 weeks old. Glucose tolerance testing was performed at 9 and 13 weeks of age. See Supplemental Fig. S3 for experimental details.

Fecal PCR and SCFA analyses

Fecal DNA was extracted using a Power Fecal DNA Extraction Kit (MoBio). The presence of specific bacterial genera was determined by measuring DNA abundance using specific primers with comparison to the 16S rRNA gene (Universal, Integrated DNA Technologies), as described [[17], [18], [19], [20]] (Table S2). Fecal DNA content was log-transformed. Fecal and plasma SCFA were quantified from frozen samples by the University of Michigan Metabolomics Core as described [12]. Cecal SCFA were quantified using LC–MS/MS as described [21]. Briefly, plasma and dried cecal samples were extracted using methanol, then centrifuged and the clear supernatants collected. For derivatization, 40 μL of each supernatant was mixed with 20 μL of 200 mM 3-nitrophenylhydrazine in 50% aqueous acetonitrile and 20 μL of 120 mM N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodiimide in 50% aqueous acetonitrile with 6% pyridine. The mixture was incubated at 40 °C for 30 min. After reaction, the mixture was diluted to 2 mL with 10% aqueous acetonitrile. A 500 μL aliquot was mixed with 500 μL of an isotope-labeled internal standard mixture, and a 20 μL aliquot was injected for LC–MS/MS on a Waters Acquity I-class UPLC coupled to a Waters Xevo TQS-micro MS. Chromatographic separations were performed on a Waters BEH C18 (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm) UPLC column. Cecal data were normalized using the mass of the dried sample, taken prior to extraction.

16S rRNA sequencing and analysis

DNA was extracted from fecal pellets using the DNeasy PowerSoil HTP 96 Kit (QIAGEN, Valencia CA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. An amplicon library of the bacterial 16S rRNA V4 regions was obtained using barcoded fusion primers 515F/806R. The DNA concentration of each amplicon was quantified using the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) and the SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA). Samples were pooled and purified with the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN, Germantown, MD), and the pooled samples were quantified using the Quant-iT dsDNA Assay Kit, high sensitivity (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) on a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad CA), then combined at equimolar concentrations to form the sequencing library. Paired-end sequencing (2×150 bp) of the constructed library was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) at Azenta Life Sciences (South Plainfield, NJ) [22]. The 16S rRNA sequence datasets generated for this study have been deposited in the QIITA open-source microbiome database (https://qiita.ucsd.edu) under accession number 15509.

Raw paired end reads with perfect matching of bases between forward and reverse sequences were retained, demultiplexed, filtered, and analyzed using the QIIME 2 v2020.8 pipeline as previously described [23]. Briefly, the identification of operational taxonomic units (OTUs) was performed using the DADA2 plugin with quality filtering to trim the first six bases of each sequence with quality score <35. Taxonomy was assigned to sequences using the Naïve Bayes classifier compared against a SILVA 138 99% similarity OTUs [24,25].

Samples were rarefied at 4000 reads for assessing microbial α- and β-diversity differences. To evaluate species richness and evenness, α-diversity was computed by most commonly used metrics Shannon's index to estimate both richness and evenness in a single equation. To assess the overall microbial community variation between samples, unweighted UniFrac distance, Jaccard distance, and Bray Curtis matrices were used for β-diversity analysis. The multivariable association analysis with linear models (MaAsLin 2) was implemented in R to detect significant differences in relative abundances of microbial taxa between study groups [26].

Body composition analyses

Body composition analysis of body fat and lean mass was determined in live conscious mice using quantitative magnetic resonance spectroscopy (EchoMRI 3-in-1; Echo MRI, TX), with support from the NIDDK-funded Nutrition Obesity Research Center (NORC) Energy Balance Core at the University of Washington [27]. For the loss-of-function approach (Experiment 1, Fig. S1), measurements were collected at week 10 (baseline, after 10 weeks of HFHS feeding), week 14 (3 weeks after ABX +/− CLA), and week 18 (7 weeks after ABX +/− CLA). For the CMT approach in conventional mice (Experiment 2, Fig. S2), measurements were taken at week 12 (baseline, after all mice received ABX) and at week 17 (5 weeks following CT). For the CMT approach in GF mice (Experiment 3, Fig. S3), measurements were collected at weeks 9 and 14.

Indirect calorimetry

Ldlr−/− mice were singly housed one week prior to acclimation in metabolic cages. Energy expenditure was measured using a computer-controlled indirect calorimetry system (Promethion®, Sable Systems, Las Vegas, NV), with support from the UW NORC Energy Balance Core as described [2,28,29]. For each mouse, O2 consumption and CO2 production were measured at 5-minute intervals for 48 h. Respiratory quotient (RQ) was calculated as the ratio of CO2 production to O2 consumption. Energy expenditure (heat) was calculated using the Weir equation [30], and analyzed using a linear mixed model approach as recommended for the analysis of energy expenditure [31]. Ambulatory activity was measured continuously, with consecutive adjacent infrared beam breaks in the x-, y-, and z-axes scored as an activity count, with a tally recorded every 5 min. Data acquisition and instrument control were coordinated by MetaScreen v.1.6.2., and raw data were processed using ExpeData v.1.4.3 (Sable Systems) using an analysis script documenting all aspects of data transformation. Data were recorded over four consecutive 24-hour periods, with alternating 14-hour light and 10-hour dark cycles. Indirect calorimetry was only performed in the ABX study (Fig. S1).

Gene expression

RNA was extracted from frozen tissues using a RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia CA, USA), and reverse-transcribed using the Superscript III First Strand Synthesis System (ThermoFisher, Bothell, WA, USA). The resulting cDNA was amplified by qRT-PCR using an ABI 7900HT instrument. Taqman primer/probe sets for individual genes were obtained from ThermoFisher (Bothell WA, USA), and oligonucleotides were also used as indicated (Table S2). Gapdh was used as a reference gene. Relative expression of target genes was calculated using the ΔΔCt formula and expressed as fold change, as described [28].

Primary adipocytes

Inguinal white adipose tissue (IWAT) was harvested from male C57BL/6J mice (8–10 weeks of age) under aseptic conditions, minced and digested with collagenase II (1 mg/mL, #C2-28, Millipore Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 1 h at 37 °C with agitation. Digestate was filtered through sterile mesh (50 μm), and the preadipocyte-containing stromal vascular fraction (SVF) was collected by centrifugation. SVF cells were plated at 1.25 × 106 cells/well in DMEM/F12 + 20% FBS in 6-well plates. Once preadipocytes reached confluency, they were differentiated into primary adipocytes as described previously [32]. Once fully differentiated, primary adipocytes were treated with one of the following fatty acid preparations: (1) fatty acid-free BSA (control); (2) palmitic acid (PA, 250 μM conjugated to fatty acid-free BSA); (3) acetate (C2, 10 mM); (4) propionate (C3, 1 mM); or (5) butyrate (C4, 1 mM), replenished once daily for 2–3 days.

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and represented as means ± standard error. One- and two-way ANOVA were used to compare differences between mice receiving different treatments at different time points, with Tukey's post-hoc test to control for multiple comparisons unless specified. Indirect calorimetry data were analyzed using a linear mixed model with a compound symmetry covariance structure [31]. Measurements of microbial α-diversity and the relative abundance of bacterial communities and species were analyzed using the GraphPad Prism 9 software and displayed as mean ± standard error of mean in scatter plots, box-and-whiskers ± min-to-max, or heatmaps. Comparisons between study groups were assessed by Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test to account for additional variables. Statistical significance of inter- and intra-group β-diversity was determined by a permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was applied to create ordinations and to visualize the diversity between samples. A Benjamini–Hochberg-corrected q value < 0.25 was used by MaAsLin2 to determine statistical significance. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all other statistical analyses throughout this study.

Results

Experiment 1: antibiotic effects on SCFA and bacterial phylogeny

Prior work indicates that in addition to promoting weight and fat loss [2,6], 10,12 CLA supplementation alters gut microbial composition and increases fecal and plasma SCFAs in obese mice [12,33]. We therefore sought to determine whether these changes in gut microbiota are required for the weight loss effects of 10,12 CLA. We performed a loss-of-function study in which gut microbiota were perturbed by continuous ABX treatment just prior to and during 10,12 CLA supplementation in obese mice (Fig. S1). To assess the efficacy of ABX treatment, fecal samples were collected during the obesogenic period prior to ABX (8 weeks), and again during ABX or vehicle treatment (14 and 19 weeks). Expression of 16S rRNA was determined by qPCR from isolated fecal DNA samples using an established universal bacterial primer that targets the V3–V4 hypervariable region as the primer set used for Illumina MiSeq [18,19]. Total bacterial counts were significantly blunted by ABX, suggesting its effective suppression of intestinal bacteria populations (Figure 1A). Baseline levels of major bacterial genera previously shown to increase in abundance following 10,12 CLA supplementation, including Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Akkermansia [12,17], were assessed with validated primers [18] (Table S2). At baseline, levels of each were similar across treatment groups, as expected, and then all were blunted by ABX treatment (Figure 1A). Total fecal SCFA as well as individual measures of fecal acetate, propionate, and butyrate were decreased by ABX (Figure 1B), and there were reduced plasma levels of total SCFA and acetate, but without changes in propionate or butyrate (Figure 1C). Consistent with our prior report, 10,12 CLA supplementation increased fecal levels of Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Akkermansia ([12] and Figure 1A), with elevated fecal butyrate levels (Figure 1B), which were reduced by ABX treatment. Collectively, these results confirm that the ABX treatment effectively blunted intestinal bacterial populations and the measured fecal SCFA content, and reduced plasma acetate levels.

To more robustly assess the effect of ABX treatment in this model, 16S rRNA sequencing was performed on fecal samples collected prior to ABX (week 8), after 1 week of ABX or vehicle (week 11), after 2 weeks with or without CLA (week 14), and at the end of the study (week 19). Measures of alpha diversity were first assessed to determine whether microbial community richness and/or evenness were affected by ABX, 10,12 CLA, or the combination. Importantly, as expected, no differences in alpha diversity were detected at the baseline (8-week) timepoint (Fig. S4). Perhaps paradoxically, Mann Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis tests revealed a robust but transient ABX-induced increase in richness and evenness (Fig. S5. Table S3). The addition of ABX to 10,12 CLA-supplemented mice led to a modest reduction in fecal microbiota richness and evenness compared with 10,12 CLA + VEH. We next assessed fecal microbial community structure (beta diversity) over time by using Jaccard and unweighted UniFrac analyses to compare community differences. PCoA illustrated the significant effects of ABX for all metrics, evidenced by the starkly divergent clustering of fecal samples post-ABX (Fig. S6, Table S4). These analyses also show dissimilar community structures driven by 10,12 CLA compared to the vehicle groups in the presence of ABX. Collectively, these results indicate that the ABX exposure had a strong impact on alpha- and beta-diversity metrics, with smaller effects from the 10,12 CLA exposure.

Pairwise comparisons between the HFHS (VEH) group with the other treatment groups revealed several taxa that were differentially impacted by treatment. Mice given ABX had blooms from the Streptococcus and Geobacillus genera, an effect that was most evident at week 14 (Figure 2). By contrast, supplementation with 10,12 CLA led to a consistent reduction in Odoribacter taxa, as well as the blooming of Ruminococcaceae, Lactobacillus, and Alistipes across both time points, which reflects synergy between 10,12 CLA and ABX effects. Continuous consumption of HFHS led to the convergence of the relative abundances of these taxa by experiment end, as expected. However, the significantly lower levels of Odoribacter and higher levels of Alistipes in 10,12 CLA groups appear to be due to founder effects in the affected mice, while the blooming of Streptococcus is specific for the ABX groups (Fig. S7).

Experiment 1: ABX blunt the weight loss effects of 10,12 CLA

To determine if perturbing gut microbiota with ABX modulates 10,12 CLA-mediated weight loss, body weight and composition measurements as well as energy intake and expenditure were evaluated. ABX by themselves had no effects on body weight, adiposity, white adipose tissue (WAT) and liver mass when compared to vehicle treatment (Figure 3A). As previously reported, mice supplemented with 10,12 CLA lost substantial body weight and body fat, with increased liver mass (Figure 3A) [2]. Mice given 10,12 CLA in the presence of ABX also lost body weight, but the effect was delayed by ABX with subsequently less fat loss and liver gain (Figure 3A). Moreover, the 10,12 CLA-mediated increases in energy expenditure (body mass-adjusted heat production) were abolished in mice consuming ABX, with no changes observed in food or water intake between treatment groups (Figure 3B and C). Collectively, perturbation and suppression of gut microbiota with the ABX treatment delayed and dampened weight and fat loss induced by 10,12 CLA, providing evidence of involvement of gut microbiota.

Experiment 1: ABX have a strong effect on glucose metabolism

Previous work has shown that while 10,12 CLA supplementation has little discernable effect on glucose and insulin tolerance, it does not contribute to improvements expected with weight loss [2]. Consistent with this, fasting glucose and insulin levels were comparable between HFHS (VEH) and HFHS+10,12 (VEH) groups (Figure 4A), with no significant differences in glucose tolerance (Figure 4C, Table S5). However, glucose-stimulated insulin secretion and insulin tolerance were impaired by 10,12 CLA supplementation, effects that were reversed by ABX (Figure 4B–C). Plasma levels of serum amyloid A (SAA), an inflammatory chemokine associated with insulin resistance [34], was similarly increased by 10,12 CLA and reduced in the presence of ABX (Figure 4C). Interestingly, ABX treatment lowered fasting glucose levels while simultaneously worsening glucose tolerance (Figure 4A–C, Table S5), both in the absence and presence of 10,12 CLA, respectively. In summary, perturbing gut microbiota with ABX improved insulin tolerance and glucose-stimulated insulin secretion in response to dietary 10,12 CLA, indicating a role for gut microbes in glucoregulatory responses to 10,12 CLA. However, an independent role for ABX effects on glucose metabolism cannot be ruled out.

To further examine peripheral glucoregulatory metabolism, we assessed gene expression in inguinal white adipose tissue (IWAT). As we reported [2], the gene encoding adiponectin (Adipoq), an adipokine associated with insulin sensitivity, was reduced by 10,12 CLA supplementation, while the macrophage markers Lgals3 and Egr2, as well as the inflammatory cytokine Tnfa, were elevated (Figure 4D). Expression of genes associated with the browning of WAT, Cpt1b and Ucp1, were also increased by 10,12 CLA (Figure 4D). Perturbing the gut microbiota blunted the loss of adiponectin and the upregulation of Ucp1, Cpt1a, and Egr2 induced by 10,12 CLA (Figure 4D), which may contribute to restoration of insulin sensitivity in IWAT. Importantly, ABX treatment in the absence of 10,12 CLA had no effects on IWAT mRNA expression. Experiments using cultured primary adipocytes revealed similar gene expression patterns in response to SCFA, with acetate (C2) blunting Adipoq while increasing Cpt1a (Fig. S8). This implies that some effects of 10,12 CLA on WAT could be mediated by SCFA, and are blunted by ABX-mediated suppression of SCFA. Examination of the liver did not reveal an overt hepatic phenotype in response to 10,12 CLA, but showed a strong trend for ABX to increase Cpt1a gene expression (Figure 4E), suggesting that the ABX treatment increased hepatic fatty acid oxidation. Collectively, perturbation of gut microbiota by ABX improved glucose metabolism, with notable improvements in mice co-treated with 10,12 CLA. The results of this loss-of-function study demonstrate that 10,12 CLA-mediated weight loss can be partially attributed to changes in gut microbiota.

Experiment 2: cecal transplant (CT) in conventionally-raised recipients

Since results from the loss-of-function experiment suggest that 10,12 CLA-mediated weight loss and glucoregulation are partially dependent on changes in gut microbiota, we next asked whether these gut microbiota changes were sufficient to drive 10,12 CLA-mediated phenotypes. Male Ldlr−/− mice were fed a HFHS diet for 12 weeks before being inoculated with cecal contents (cecal transplant, CT) from other Ldlr−/− mice that had been fed an HFHS diet with or without 10,12 CLA (CLA-CT or Ob-CT, respectively) for 20 weeks [16] (Fig. S2). Recipient mice were given an ABX cocktail in drinking water 1 week prior to inoculation to allow for the donor microbiota to engraft, then the ABX water was replaced with normal water for the remainder of the study, as described [35]. Recipient mice were fed an HFHS diet continuously before, during, and after ABX and inoculation, and control mice were given saline gavage (see Fig. S2; experimental design). Body weights did not differ between groups before or after CT (Figure 5A). Body composition analysis showed that mice given saline or Ob-CT tended to gain fat and lean mass over the course of the study, an effect not observed in mice given CLA-CT (Figure 5B). At sacrifice, mice given CLA-CT had smaller epididymal white adipose (EWAT) tissue depots and more liver mass than the Ob-CT mice (Figure 5C), consistent with the phenotype of 10,12 CLA-supplemented donor mice [2]. In summary, CLA-CT partially recapitulated the loss of adiposity observed in donor mice.

We next examined glucose metabolism in these mice. Fasting glucose levels tended to increase over time in the Sal- and Ob-CT groups, while CLA-CT tended to decrease fasting glucose levels (Figure 5D). Despite the modest effects on body composition, CLA-CT mice showed improved glucose tolerance compared to Ob-CT mice, with lower area under the curve (AUC) and glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (Figure 5E, Table S6). CLA-CT mice also had improved insulin tolerance over Ob-CT mice (Figure 5F, Table S6). Administering to mice either of the Ob- or CLA-CT microbiota transplants resulted in worse glucose and insulin tolerance than saline control mice, consistent with the strong microbiota perturbation observed. In summary, recipient mice inoculated with cecal contents from donor mice that had previously been supplemented with 10,12 CLA were resistant to further fat gain and displayed improved glucose and insulin tolerance than comparable recipient mice inoculated with cecal contents from donor HFHS-fed mice without 10,12 CLA, effects that can be directly attributed to 10,12 CLA. These results further imply that mice receiving CT from donor mice undergoing weight loss have improved glucose metabolism that may be dissociated from 10,12 CLA-mediated effects on body weight.

Experiment 3: cecal transplant in germ-free (GF) recipients: impact on energy balance

We next conducted a complimentary experiment using germ-free (GF) mice as recipients to remove the effects of differences in the founder populations in the recipient mice. C57BL/6J mice that had been reared GF were inoculated at 6 weeks of age with the same cecal contents as above (Ob-CT or CLA-CT), or with an age-matched control donor group that had been fed normal chow (Chow-CT). Recipient mice continued to consume an irradiated normal chow diet until 10 weeks of age, then were changed to an HFHS diet until euthanasia at 15 weeks of age (Fig. S3). Following HFHS diet, body fat percent markedly increased in a subset of all three experimental groups (Figure 6A), as expected, with no differences between groups. There were no differences in body weight between different recipient mouse groups on either the chow or HFHS diets (Figure 6B). Similarly, no differences in EWAT, IWAT, liver weight, or fasting glucose levels were noted between groups (Figure 6C–D) Glucose tolerance tests conducted at 9 weeks of age (while recipient mice consumed a chow diet) revealed few differences between groups, but a modest improvement in mice that received Ob-CT (Figure 6E, Table S7). However, by 13 weeks of age (with recipient mice now consuming an HFHS diet), mice given CLA-CT showed a marked improvement in glucose tolerance compared to Chow-CT and Ob-CT mice (Figure 6E, Table S7). We next assessed mRNA expression of several genes in IWAT and liver related to metabolic status. Ob-CT increased mRNA expression of Adipoq and Lgals3 in IWAT, indicating an adipogenic and inflammatory phenotype (Figure 6F). In contrast to our previous study with oral 10,12 CLA supplementation [2,6], we did not observe significant reductions in IWAT Adipoq in response to CLA-CT. However, mice that received CLA-CT had overtly increased Ucp1 mRNA expression and decreased Lgals3, a macrophage marker, consistent with previous observations [2]. In the liver, there were no differences in expression of genes involved in fatty acid synthesis (Fasn), oxidation (Cpt1a), or inflammation (Saa2) (Figure 6G). However, Pck1 and Slc2a2, genes important for hepatic glucose production and glucose uptake, respectively, were increased in CLA-CT mice (Figure 6G). Pck1 plays a crucial role in hepatic energy metabolism, and is known to promote hepatic gluconeogenesis and restrict hepatic steatosis [36]. Slc2a2, which encodes GLUT2 protein, is a major regulator of hepatic glucose flux and is required for glucose-sensitive gene expression [37]. Collectively, the CT results in GF mice support our observations in conventional mice to suggest that improved glucose metabolism that accompanies weight loss can be transferred by microbiota.

Experiment 3: cecal transplant in germ-free (GF) recipients: 16S rRNA, cecal and fecal SCFA, and fecal bacterial analyses

We next conducted analysis of 16S rRNA sequencing of donor cecal samples and fecal samples of GF recipient mice collected at 10, 12, and 14 weeks. As expected [7], the donor materials from the mice that received the HFHS diet had reduced alpha diversity compared to the materials from the chow-fed mice (Fig. S9). Compared with the initial materials, alpha diversity in the Chow-CT, Ob-CT, and CLA-CT recipient mice decreased during the chow consumption period. Alpha diversity increased in the HFHS-donor mice during the HFHS consumption period, with all groups reaching similar levels by experiment end after five weeks of HFHS diet (Fig. S9, Table S8). Next, we assessed beta-diversity using the Bray Curtis and unweighted UniFrac metrics. In both analyses, donor cecal contents from chow-fed mice were dissimilar from HFHS-fed mice, which clustered together, similarly as the GF recipients at week 10 (Fig. S10, Table S9). The introduction of HFHS feeding led to convergence of the population structures of the four groups over time (Fig. S10, Table S9). Phylogenic analysis of the recipient mouse feces revealed blooming of particular taxa over time. Mice that had received CLA-CT had increased abundance of Mucispirillum and Akkermansia, at all time-points examined, with Blautia and Alistipes transiently enriched (Figs. S11–S13). Other taxa were selected against in the CLA-CT group, including Lachnospiraceae and Odoribacter, the latter also reduced by oral 10,12 CLA in Expt. 1.

Levels of plasma and cecal SCFA, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate, as well as fecal bacterial DNA content, were assessed in recipient mice at the end of Experiment 3. GF mice given CLA-CT exhibited elevated plasma and cecal butyrate levels (Fig. S14A–B), with a trend towards increased plasma and cecal acetate, consistent with results from Experiment 1 and prior work [12]. Fecal DNA analysis revealed similar total bacterial counts in all treatment groups (Fig. S14C, 16S). Mice inoculated with CLA-CT had a trend towards increased Bifidobacteria DNA content with no change in Lactobacillus (Fig. S14C). There was a stark increase in Akkermansia DNA content in CLA-CT mice, consistent with 16S rRNA sequencing data from these mice (Figs. S11–S13). Because CLA can be synthesized by microbial taxa in the gut, we next measured fecal linoleic acid isomerase (LAI) DNA content. CLA-CT mice had increased LAI DNA levels (Fig. S14C), suggesting the potential for gut microbes exposed to dietary CLA to select for bacteria that can convert linoleic acid into CLA in a feed-forward manner. Collectively, fecal analyses revealed similar bacterial taxon enrichment and intestinal and plasma butyrate production patterns between donor mice undergoing weight loss due to 10,12 CLA supplementation and recipient mice given CLA-CT.

Discussion

We have previously shown that obese mice undergoing weight loss due to 10,12 CLA supplementation develop altered gut microbial composition, with enrichment of SCFA-producing genera and elevated levels of fecal butyrate and plasma acetate [12]. We therefore hypothesized that these changes in gut microbiota were necessary and sufficient for the weight loss effects of 10,12 CLA. We now show that altering the gut microbiota using broad-spectrum ABX blunts 10,12 CLA-mediated weight and fat loss, while introducing microbiota from 10,12 CLA-supplemented mice has more modest effects on these parameters. These observations provide evidence that the gut microbiota are required, but are not necessarily sufficient, for the 10,12 CLA-mediated weight loss. Moreover, the ABX-mediated disruption and CT-mediated introduction of 10,12 CLA-selected microbiota reversed and recapitulated the 10,12 CLA-mediated changes in glucose metabolism, respectively, suggesting important roles of the gut microbiota in glucoregulation.

While the goal of Experiment 1 was to deplete gut microbes using ABX, this strategy could have introduced ABX-mediated effects independent from changes in gut microbe composition. Low-dose ABX have been used by the livestock industry for decades as a method of increasing body mass (and therefore profitability for farmers) [38,39]. In the current study, we did not observe any differences in body weight, fat or lean mass, or energy expenditure between ABX-treated and untreated control groups, suggesting little or no effects of high-dose ABX treatments themselves on indexes of energy balance in these adult mice. However, despite reduced fasting glucose levels elicited by ABX, we observed a striking worsening of glucose tolerance in the mice receiving the high-dose ABX treatment. While we did not directly measure insulin responsiveness in peripheral tissues, the lack of an effect of ABX on WAT genes associated with insulin sensitivity (i.e. Adipoq) and insulin resistance (i.e. Lgals3, Tnf) suggests that peripheral glucose regulatory pathways were not impacted by ABX. These results contrast with those from a previous study in which chronically administered vancomycin and metronidazole reduced body weight, body fat, and improved glucose metabolism in mice fed a high fat diet, findings that were due to a combination of their gut microbiota-modulating effects and direct effects on host metabolism [40]. Indeed, we observed a reduction in circulating SAA levels in the mice treated with ABX, suggesting an anti-inflammatory effect of the high-dose ABX [41]. While fecal bacterial counts were dramatically reduced by the high-dose ABX, as expected, measured bacterial richness was increased. We interpret this to be consistent with the suppression of a small number of dominant taxa that were inhibitory to a larger number of minor taxa, which bloomed in their absence.

Host diet is a primary driver of microbiota composition [42,43]. SCFA are produced to varying degrees based on dietary composition, and work by us and others indicates that a 10,12 CLA-containing diet increases SCFA production in the gut [12,33]. In the current study, while all fecal SCFAs were markedly reduced by ABX treatment, plasma levels were only slightly affected. A dichotomy between the effects of vancomycin on fecal and plasma SCFA levels has been observed in humans [44], although the mechanisms are unclear. Possible explanations include that plasma SCFA levels are tightly regulated, and with decreased gut microbial populations, SCFA harvest by colonic enterocytes is increased in a compensatory manner, maintaining plasma SCFA homeostasis. Hepatic acetate production also could be increased, as the liver may convert free fatty acid-derived acetyl-CoA to acetate [45,46]. Regardless, in the current study ABX treatment significantly attenuated both fecal and plasma acetate levels, which is the most abundant plasma SCFA, and therefore may have greater biological impact than plasma propionate or butyrate. Indeed, acetate may beneficially impact hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism [47]. Thus, the blunting of acetate production due to ABX effects may be one mechanism by which glucose tolerance was worsened. Similarly, the potential introduction of SCFA-producing microbes in the CLA-CT mice could have contributed to improved glucose tolerance in the CMT models.

Perturbation of gut microbiota can profoundly impact energy balance and glucose metabolism independent of SCFA effects. While high fat feeding has immediate effects on gut microbial composition [48], effects on body weight, composition, and glucose metabolism related to these microbial changes can lag for weeks [49]. This could explain why CLA-CT mice did not show improved glucose tolerance at 3 weeks post-CT, but only after 6 weeks. That this improved glucose tolerance was independent from major changes in body weight implicates a direct effect of gut microbiota and not weight loss per se. However, the weight loss in the donor mice likely impacted the microbiota, as CLA-CT mice showed comparable improvements in glucose metabolism as weight-matched mice undergoing caloric restriction (CR) in our prior study [2] Moreover, mice transplanted with gut microbiota from humans who had consumed a strict very low-calorie diet similarly showed improved glucose tolerance [50]. However, other studies have reported adverse effects of CR on gut microbiota in both animal models and humans [[51], [52], [53]], implying that the degree of CR may be critical to the gut microbiome effects. Collectively, the combined ABX and CMT studies suggest that 10,12 CLA-mediated effects on glucose metabolism are dependent on gut microbial changes, whereas changes in body weight and fat mass are less dependent.

Several taxonomic changes evoked by manipulation of the gut microbiota were consistent across the experimental models, and consistent with previous work by others [[54], [55], [56]]. Most notably, oral 10,12 CLA delivery (Expt. 1) as well as 10,12-CT (Expt. 3) led to increased abundance in taxa from the genus Akkermansia. Species of Akkermansia, particularly A. muciniphila, appear to be able to improve glycemic control in people and in mice [[57], [58], [59], [60], [61], [62], [63]], reduce inflammation, and increase the secretion of incretins such as glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) and peptide Y (PYY) [[64], [65], [66], [67], [68]]. A. muciniphila has been negatively correlated with body fat mass and glucose intolerance in obese mice and rats given a high fat diet [[69], [70], [71]]. Another genus selected by 10,12 CLA in both experiments was Alistipes, a taxon whose abundance has been inversely associated with inflammatory conditions, including cardiovascular disease [[72], [73], [74], [75], [76]], colitis [77], and hepatic inflammation [[78], [79], [80], [81]] in mice and humans. Blautia species, also enriched by 10,12 CLA, also have been inversely associated with obesity and visceral fat mass [[82], [83], [84]], and increased in abundance during weight loss [85]. The abundance of Blautia species have been inversely correlated with diabetes [84,86,87], whereas pharmacotherapies that improve diabetes increased Blautia abundance [88]; such observations imply that increasing intestinal Blautia abundance could improve glucose metabolism. Importantly, species of Akkermansia, Alistipes, and Blautia all are known SCFA producers [[89], [90], [91], [92]], suggesting that strategies to increase their relative abundance could provide metabolic benefits.

An important consideration is that our studies highlighted the effects of 10,12 CLA supplementation and microbial perturbation primarily in the context of HFHS diet feeding. Lean chow-fed mice and rats similarly lose body weight and fat mass in response to oral CLA supplementation [54,[93], [94], [95], [96]], suggesting that 10,12 CLA efficacy is independent of dietary composition and/or baseline adiposity. However, we chose the obese HFHS diet-fed model to study 10,12 CLA as a potential weight loss strategy with translational appeal. Importantly, a component of Experiment 3 examined the impact of weight loss-altered microbiota (via 10,12 CLA) on chow-fed recipients, with few phenotypes observed until mice were switched to a HFHS diet, further justifying our use of HFHS diet models. Indeed, one prior study has similarly transplanted fecal material from donor mice that had been engaged in active CLA-mediated weight loss, with prominent weight loss and glucose metabolic phenotypes observed when recipient mice were fed a high fat diet [55], supporting the similar results using our HFHS-fed models.

While oral CLA supplementation has a direct effect on gut microbiota, the converse is also plausible, that endogenous microbial CLA production could exert host effects. Thus, suppression of the gut microbiota not only modulated SCFA-mediated host effects, but also may have suppressed endogenous CLA production. CLA is produced exclusively by bacterial biohydrogenation of linoleic acid by linoleic acid isomerase [97,98], and is a bacterial product similar to SCFA. Thus, suppressing the gut microbiota likely minimized contributions of endogenously-produced CLA to the observed changes in energy metabolism, and, conversely, introducing microbial contents from CLA-fed mice may have augmented endogenous CLA-production capacity. This is supported by the increased fecal linoleic acid isomerase content observed in CLA-CT mice (Experiment 3). Mice given probiotics that include CLA-producing Lactobacillus or Bifidobacter strains were resistant to diet-induced obesity [99,100], consistent with a role for endogenously produced CLA impacting body weight. In a similar study in obese humans, increasing the relative abundance of CLA-producing bacteria in the gut by administering the oral prebiotic inulin-type fructans was inversely correlated with serum cholesterol and hemoglobin A1C levels [101]. Ablation of linoleic acid isomerase in particular gut bacterial species resulted in reduced gut immunity by limiting distinct populations of CD4+ intraepithelial lymphocytes, suggesting an important role of endogenous gut bacterial CLA production on intestinal immune homeostasis [102]. Such findings suggest that strategies to increase endogenous CLA production could lessen diabetes and cardiovascular disease diatheses. Moreover, it is possible that the impact of endogenously-produced CLA by gut microbes could elicit metabolic effects in the host that are distinct from the effects of oral CLA delivery. This notion is supported by the similar insulin tolerance test results evoked when gut microbes were perturbed by either ABX (Experiment 1) or CMT (Experiments 2 and 3).

Limitations to this study included that only male mice were studied, which limits translational potential in females. We chose to only study males because they more reliably become obese than female mice [103,104], and female mice also are more resistant to CLA-mediated weight loss (unpublished observations). Further studies are needed to assess whether gut microbiota are necessary or sufficient for weight loss in female mice. In addition, while all mice used in these studies had the C57BL/6J background, mice used in Experiments 1 and 2, as well as all cecal donor mice, were deficient in the low-density lipoprotein receptor (Ldlr); we used these mice to facilitate atherosclerosis evaluation for other studies [16,105]. However, the GF mice used in Experiment 3 expressed Ldlr. Because Experiments 2 and 3 were complementary despite differing founder genotypes, the Ldlr deficiency should be considered a minor limitation. In addition, it has been shown that increased dietary and systemic cholesterol has minimal impact on gut microbiota [106], and that dietary perturbations explain almost 60% of the total structural variance in gut microbiota, whereas host genetic mutations account for <12% [107]. Moreover, rearing Ldlr−/− mice germ-free had no impact on atherosclerosis phenotypes compared to conventionally-raised Ldlr−/− mice [108], suggesting that the microbiota have little added impact on atherosclerosis in an Ldlr−/− host. Thus, we do not anticipate that the use of Ldlr−/− mice influenced our results. Another potential limitation is that the ABX used in Experiment 1 may have elicited host effects that were independent from their effects perturbing gut microbiota. Finally, we used cecal contents for microbiome transplantation, which could contain host mammalian cells or gut microbiota-derived metabolites that may have been transplanted in addition to gut microbes; determining whether fecal transplantation would have elicited similar phenotypes could yield important insights.

Conclusions

Results from this study suggest that 10,12 CLA-mediated weight and fat loss require an intact gut microbiota. Weight loss and the accompanying improvements in glucose metabolism due to caloric restriction also require an intact gut microbiota, suggesting that gut microbiota adopt a distinct weight loss phenotype. The ability to harness and replicate a particular microbial signature associated with weight loss in humans could lead to novel avenues for precision medicine. Moreover, enhancing our understanding about how SCFA- and CLA-producing probiotics could modulate body weight and energy balance could lead to novel probiotic/synbiotic approaches to target obesity and diabetes. We conclude that weight loss driven by particular dietary strategies, caloric restriction or 10,12 CLA supplementation, are associated with distinct gut microbial phenotypes.
