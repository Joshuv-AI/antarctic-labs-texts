Red flags in asymmetric axonal neuropathy revealing MPO-ANCA–associated microscopic polyangiitis: a case report
Authors: Yu Wang, Na Liu, Chunmei Yang, Ju Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMononeuritis multiplex is a recognized, though sometimes overlooked, clinical feature of myeloperoxidase antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (MPO-ANCA)-associated vasculitis. When renal or pulmonary involvement is initially mild, the condition may mimic common neurological disorders and diagnosis can be delayed.Case presentationA 51-year-old woman presented with a five-month history of progressive bilateral lower-limb pain and edema, followed by three weeks of rapidly progressive asymmetric weakness affecting all four limbs. Nerve conduction studies showed severe multifocal axonal neuropathy with marked side-to-side compound muscle action potential asymmetry, consistent with mononeuritis multiplex. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated anemia, hypoalbuminemia, proteinuria, microscopic hematuria, MPO-ANCA positivity, and a subsequent creatinine rise from 76 to 121 μmol/L, indicating early but clinically evident systemic involvement. Chest computed tomography showed right-sided nodular/parenchymal pulmonary lesions with bilateral chronic/interstitial changes. Treatment with glucocorticoid-based induction therapy together with a documented cyclophosphamide-containing discharge regimen was associated with marked early improvement in pain, strength, inflammatory markers, and renal indices.ConclusionsThree red flags should prompt early ANCA testing in patients with rapidly progressive asymmetric axonal neuropathy: multifocal asymmetric axonal loss with relatively preserved conduction velocities on nerve conduction studies; early renal abnormalities such as hematuria, proteinuria, or rising creatinine; and concordant systemic findings including hypoalbuminemia, elevated inflammatory markers, or pulmonary imaging changes. Background

Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis (AAV) is a necrotizing small-vessel vasculitis that includes microscopic polyangiitis (MPA), granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA), and eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) [1]. Peripheral nervous system involvement is common in MPA and most often presents as mononeuritis multiplex caused by ischemic injury to the vasa nervorum [2].

The clinical challenge in this setting is not that vasculitic neuropathy is unknown, but that AAV may not initially be considered when neuropathy is the dominant presentation. Early symptoms can mimic lumbar radiculopathy, diabetic neuropathy, Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS), or chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP), particularly before renal or pulmonary manifestations become prominent [3–5].

We report a neurologically predominant case of MPO-ANCA-associated MPA presenting as rapidly progressive asymmetric axonal neuropathy. The aim is to highlight the diagnostic red flags that prompted systemic evaluation and early treatment.

Case presentation

A 51-year-old woman was admitted after a five-month history of progressive bilateral lower-limb pain, initially treated elsewhere as lumbar radiculopathy without improvement. During the three weeks before admission, she developed rapidly progressive asymmetric weakness affecting the feet and hands, together with distal numbness and bilateral lower-limb edema. She became unable to walk independently.

She had no known diabetes, toxin exposure, recent infection, or family history of hereditary neuropathy. On neurological examination, weakness was distal-predominant and asymmetric. Upper-limb proximal strength was preserved, but distal strength was reduced, more prominently on the left. In the lower limbs, the right ankle was plegic (Medical Research Council grade 0), whereas the left ankle retained partial movement. Pinprick and vibration sensation were reduced in both feet in a patchy, non-length-dependent distribution. Bilateral lower-limb edema was present. Cervical and lumbar magnetic resonance imaging showed degenerative changes but no compressive lesion sufficient to explain the pattern or severity of the deficits.

Nerve conduction studies showed severe multifocal axonal neuropathy consistent with mononeuritis multiplex. The most discriminating findings were the marked left-right asymmetry of median compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitudes (left 2.5 mV vs. right 7.1 mV), absence of bilateral peroneal motor responses, and a severely reduced right tibial CMAP (0.50 mV) without an F-wave. Sensory nerve action potentials (SNAPs) were markedly reduced in the left median and ulnar nerves and absent in the lower limbs. Conduction velocities were relatively preserved. This pattern favored axonal ischemic neuropathy rather than a primary demyelinating process and argued against GBS or CIDP. Needle electromyography demonstrated acute denervation in the bilateral tibialis anterior muscles and left abductor pollicis brevis. Sympathetic skin response and repetitive nerve stimulation were normal (Table 1).

Systemic evaluation was then pursued and demonstrated inflammatory, renal, immunological, and pulmonary abnormalities. Key findings included anemia, hypoalbuminemia, elevated inflammatory markers, proteinuria with microscopic hematuria, marked MPO-ANCA positivity with p-ANCA positivity and PR3-ANCA negativity, and an in-hospital creatinine rise before treatment. Chest computed tomography showed right-sided pulmonary nodular/parenchymal lesions together with bilateral chronic/interstitial changes (Fig. 1). These findings supported a systemic small-vessel vasculitic process with early but clinically evident renal involvement and pulmonary abnormalities. After mimics had been excluded and a small-vessel vasculitic syndrome established clinically, the patient fulfilled the 2022 American College of Rheumatology / European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology classification criteria for MPA by virtue of MPO-ANCA positivity (+ 6; classification threshold ≥ 5) [1].

Treatment was initiated promptly because of rapidly progressive neurological deterioration together with evolving systemic disease. Intravenous methylprednisolone sodium succinate was administered at approximately 200 mg/day for the initial nine days, then tapered stepwise to 160 mg/day for three days, 120 mg/day for three days, 80 mg/day for three days, and 60 mg/day for three days, followed by oral prednisone 50 mg/day at discharge. Nadroparin, a low-molecular-weight heparin, was used for venous thromboembolism prophylaxis because the patient was largely bedridden, had bilateral leg edema, and had markedly elevated D-dimer levels; lower-extremity venous ultrasonography did not demonstrate deep vein thrombosis.

Supportive treatment also included albumin supplementation, red-cell transfusion for symptomatic anemia, neuropathic pain control, antihypertensive therapy, gastric protection, and electrolyte management. According to the medication record, compound cyclophosphamide tablets (cyclophosphamide 50 mg per tablet) were prescribed at 2 tablets twice weekly during hospitalization and continued at discharge. Because this schedule does not correspond to a standard induction or maintenance protocol for AAV, it is reported here exactly as documented and is not interpreted as a guideline-based regimen.

The patient improved clinically during hospitalization. Neuropathic pain decreased within the first week after glucocorticoid treatment. Distal strength improved progressively, and by discharge the upper limbs were near normal while the lower limbs had improved to approximately Medical Research Council grade 4+ with the aid of a walking device. Laboratory indices improved in parallel, with MPO-ANCA declining progressively, creatinine normalizing, urine protein becoming negative, and inflammatory markers resolving; details are summarized in Table 3.

At short-term follow-up (3 months), pain and edema had resolved, distal numbness had improved but not fully disappeared, and she remained ambulatory with assistance. Prednisone had been tapered to 30 mg/day. No major adverse effects of immunosuppression were documented during the available follow-up period.

Discussion

Vasculitic neuropathy phenotype and diagnostic message

This case does not aim to establish novelty of axonal neuropathy in AAV, because vasculitic neuropathy is already a recognized manifestation of MPA and other forms of AAV [2, 6]. What this case illustrates is how a neurologically predominant presentation can defer consideration of systemic vasculitis when renal indices appear near-normal and the initial complaint looks indistinguishable from lumbar radiculopathy or diabetic neuropathy.

The neurological phenotype was typical for vasculitic neuropathy: painful, rapidly progressive, asymmetric, and axonal [2, 7]. In contrast to diabetic polyneuropathy, which is usually length-dependent and relatively symmetric, this patient had marked side-to-side differences and multifocal deficits. In contrast to Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) or chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP), the electrophysiological study showed severe amplitude loss with relatively preserved conduction velocities, favoring axonal ischemic injury of the vasa nervorum rather than a primary demyelinating process. This electrophysiological pattern—multifocal, asymmetric, axonal, with relatively preserved velocities—was more compatible with ischemic nerve injury than with a primary demyelinating neuropathy.

Systemic clues directing toward vasculitis

The systemic findings, taken individually, were each easy to overlook. Proteinuria and microscopic hematuria could be dismissed as incidental; a creatinine of 76 µmol/L appeared normal; hypoalbuminemia might be attributed to malnutrition. Taken together, however, they described a different picture: early but clinically evident renal involvement, not incidental urinary findings. The pulmonary changes on CT reinforced this. None of these abnormalities explained the neuropathy; all of them pointed toward a systemic process.

MPO-ANCA at 223.43 RU/mL—more than tenfold above the upper reference limit—with concurrent p-ANCA positivity and absent PR3-ANCA, in a patient without eosinophilia, made MPA the most parsimonious diagnosis. After exclusion of mimics, the patient met the 2022 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for MPA [1]. This distinction was clinically important because non-systemic vasculitic neuropathy would not adequately explain the renal and pulmonary findings and would imply a different risk profile and management strategy [4, 8].

The early clinical response in this case was favorable, but neurological recovery in AAV is often incomplete and may lag behind systemic biochemical improvement [2, 6]. This outcome should not be taken as representative. Neurological recovery in AAV-associated neuropathy is frequently incomplete, and the trajectory seen here—near-normal upper limb strength and Medical Research Council grade 4+ lower limb strength at three months—likely reflects the relatively short interval between symptom onset and treatment rather than any intrinsic feature of the disease.

Treatment decisions and transparency

Treatment in this case was individualized. The glucocorticoid schedule recorded during hospitalization did not follow a standard pulse protocol and has therefore been reported exactly as documented. The available record documented a cyclophosphamide-containing discharge regimen rather than a conventional intravenous pulse schedule; therefore, the regimen is reported descriptively and should not be interpreted as a guideline-based induction protocol. Regarding alternative immunosuppressive options, current guidelines support remission induction with high-dose glucocorticoids together with either rituximab or cyclophosphamide in patients with severe GPA/MPA [12]. Rituximab is an established alternative to cyclophosphamide for remission induction and is also widely used for remission maintenance. In contrast, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is not considered standard induction therapy for AAV-associated neuropathy. Its use is generally limited to selected situations, such as refractory disease, temporary adjunctive therapy when conventional immunosuppression is contraindicated, or immunoglobulin replacement in patients with hypogammaglobulinemia and recurrent severe infections.

Additional reviews, case reports, and clinicopathological studies have similarly emphasized that diagnosis and management of AAV with peripheral nervous system involvement require integration of ANCA serology, systemic organ clues, neuropathy phenotype, and, when feasible, histopathological confirmation [13, 14, 15, 16, 17].

Renal biopsy and limitations

Renal biopsy was not performed during the acute hospitalization. Histology would have provided stronger diagnostic confirmation and prognostic stratification, particularly regarding the extent of active versus chronic glomerular injury. However, given the severity of neurological deterioration and the strength of the clinical and serological evidence, treatment was started without waiting for kidney histology. This is an important limitation of the report and limits inference about long-term renal prognosis.

The difference between the admission MPO-ANCA value (223.43 RU/mL) and the pre-treatment Day 0 value (201.68 RU/mL) was modest and was not interpreted as a treatment effect before induction therapy. It more likely reflects short-term biological or assay-related variation. Both values remained markedly abnormal and supported the diagnosis of active MPO-ANCA-associated vasculitis.

This report has two main limitations. First, renal biopsy was not available. Second, follow-up was limited to the early post-discharge period. Although the patient showed clear short-term improvement in pain, strength, and laboratory abnormalities, longer follow-up would be required to determine the durability of neurological recovery, renal outcome, and relapse risk.

Conclusion

This case highlights three practical red flags for early ANCA testing in patients with rapidly progressive asymmetric axonal neuropathy: electrophysiological evidence of multifocal asymmetric axonal loss with relatively preserved conduction velocities; early renaland systemic clues such as hematuria, proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, inflammatory marker elevation, or rising creatinine; and serological confirmation with marked MPO-ANCA positivity in the appropriate clinical context. Recognizing this constellation early may help avoid misattribution to more common neuropathic disorders and allow treatment during a potentially reversible stage of vasculitic nerve injury.
