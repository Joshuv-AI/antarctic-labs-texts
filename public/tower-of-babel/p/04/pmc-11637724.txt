Piecing together the intricate puzzle of organ recovery in AL amyloidosis
Authors: Mario Nuvolone, Giovanni Palladini
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn AL amyloidosis, the factors hindering organ recovery despite deep haematological responses to anti‐clonal therapy remain elusive. The study by Staron and colleagues identifies tissue‐specific factors influencing the lack of organ response, including host‐related characteristics, the properties of the amyloidogenic precursor, the extent of organ compromise at diagnosis and the dynamics and depth of haematological response. While this study begins to unravel the complex mechanisms underlying organ recovery in AL amyloidosis, it also raises critical questions that warrant further investigation through future collaborative research.Commentary on: Staron et al. Factors impeding organ recovery despite a deep haematological response in patients with systemic AL amyloidosis. Br J Haematol 2024; 205:2268‐2272.Keywords: light chain (AL) amyloidosis, monoclonal gammopathies, organ response

In their paper, the authors from the Boston University Amyloidosis Center investigate the factors that can hinder the recovery from organ compromise in light chain (AL) amyloidosis. 1 This disease, the prototype of monoclonal gammopathies of clinical significance, arises when a typically small B‐cell or plasma cell clone produces patient‐specific, unstable, amyloid forming light chains (LCs) that deposit in tissues and lead to organ dysfunction and damage. Current treatments focus on targeting the underlying clone to reduce the production of amyloid LCs. Clinical observations have shown that lowering amyloid LC levels correlates with improved organ function, 2 even without a substantial reduction in amyloid deposits. This finding suggests that organ impairment is not only driven by the disruption of tissue architecture caused by amyloid deposits but is also maintained by the direct toxicity of circulating amyloid precursors. Combined measurements of circulating free light chains (FLC) and biomarkers of organ dysfunction and damage provide a reliable means to monitor treatment response and form the basis of response criteria that predict survival outcomes. Achieving a deep haematological response is linked to more significant organ recovery, which may be delayed but continues to improve for up to 2 years after treatment. 3 , 4 Complete reversal of organ impairment is possible, and the survival of patients who reach this milestone is comparable to that of the age‐matched general population, suggesting that AL amyloidosis can be ‘operationally cured’. 5 Yet, in some patients, organ compromise persists despite complete haematological response.

Staron and colleagues shed light on this puzzling observation in a retrospective series of patients who received contemporary treatments. They found that cardiac recovery is less likely in those whose amyloid LCs are of the lambda isotype. While the reasons underlying this observation are not clear, it is intriguing to postulate that this might reflect intrinsic differences in cardiotoxicity among different LC germline genes. Sequence determinants of LC toxicity are still poorly understood, but emerging evidence is indicating that key residue(s) in a given LC are major drivers for its amyloidogenicity and toxicity. 6 In this context, access to individual patients' LC sequences might help gaining insight into this complex phenomenon. 7 When considering the kidney, several factors were identified as hindering renal recovery, including advanced renal damage and dysfunction at baseline, as well as delayed achievement of a complete haematological response. These findings suggest that amyloid kidney involvement may progress to an irreversible stage. This underscores the importance of early diagnosis of AL amyloidosis and timely intervention with treatments capable of rapidly inducing a deep haematological response. Additionally, males were significantly less likely to achieve renal response, suggesting the presence of sex‐related, possibly tissue‐specific factors that may either promote kidney repair after complete haematological response or protect the kidney from irreversible damage for a longer period. Although, differently from the heart, LC isotype did not seem to affect renal recovery, patients with low baseline FLC levels were less likely to attain renal response, while those with low amyloid FLC concentrations at the time of response assessment were more likely to do so. This seemingly contradictory observation could reflect varying toxicity of different LCs, as well as challenges in evaluating haematological response in patients with low initial FLC levels and/or the influence of reduced glomerular filtration rate on FLC measurements. This finding warrants further investigation using more precise analytical methods for detecting and measuring amyloid FLC at low concentrations, such as mass spectrometry. 8 Indeed, even in patients who achieve a complete haematological response, the persistence of very low, undetectable levels of toxic LCs may still impede organ recovery. Earlier studies on minimal residual disease (MRD) in patients with AL amyloidosis who reached complete haematological response indicated that MRD‐negative individuals were more likely to achieve cardiac and renal response. However, this finding was not confirmed in the study by Staron and colleagues, who did not observe a major impact of MRD status on organ recovery. Nevertheless, a recent meta‐analysis of published studies reaffirmed that MRD negativity is associated with a higher likelihood of both cardiac and renal response. 9

Could amyloid deposits contribute to persistent organ impairment even in the absence of residual circulating amyloid LCs? This seems plausible but is difficult to prove, as we currently lack methods with sufficient analytical sensitivity to measure changes in tissue amyloid load in vivo. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging suggests that reductions in extracellular volume may indicate a decrease in the amyloid burden within the heart. Although relatively rare and often occurring later after treatment discontinuation, such reduction is associated with improved organ function and better survival outcomes. 10 Thus, immunotherapy targeting amyloid deposits to promote amyloid clearance may potentially accelerate or enhance organ recovery. Ongoing clinical trials are investigating the efficacy of different anti‐amyloid antibodies in combination with chemo‐immunotherapy in advanced cardiac amyloidosis (NCT04973137, NCT04512235, NCT04504825), and the results are eagerly awaited.

In addition to residual circulating toxic LCs and amyloid deposits, other factors—possibly host‐related, such as sex, or associated with concomitant diseases—may also hinder organ recovery in AL amyloidosis (Figure 1). Assessing comorbidities is, of course, crucial when determining whether to initiate rescue therapy to promote organ response. 11 The paper by Staron et al. has begun piecing together this intricate puzzle, but it also raises new questions. Like most previous studies addressing delayed organ response, this study is limited by its retrospective design, which makes it difficult to account for the time dependence of organ recovery. For instance, in the paper by Staron and colleagues, the time since complete haematological response was longer in individuals who attained cardiac recovery. To make further progress, we need large, international, prospective studies that leverage advanced technology for disease assessment and data analysis. The EUREKA project (NCT06205953), a European registry and sample‐sharing network now open to international partners beyond Europe, is one such effort. By investigating why some patients fail to achieve organ response despite deep haematological response, we can delve deeper into the mechanisms of organ impairment in this disease, potentially identifying additional therapeutic targets.
