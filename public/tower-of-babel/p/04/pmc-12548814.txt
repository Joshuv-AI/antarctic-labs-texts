Implantable Capsular Hook Technique for Sutureless Scleral Fixation of the Capsular Bag in Lens Subluxation
Authors: Chunming Chen, Yan Shao, Hong Zhong, Chengyao Qin, Zehao Liu, Wenxiu Sun, Min Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.

Lens subluxation is a complex ophthalmic condition that is not uncommon in clinical practice. Although various surgical techniques have been reported, there is currently no standardized surgical approach. Maintaining the stability and centration of the lens capsular bag is essential for implanting an intraocular lens (IOL), as close as possible to its physiological location, thereby enhancing optical outcomes for the affected eye.1 For lens dislocations with limited zonular rupture (less than 4 o'clock positions), simple implantation of a capsular tension ring (CTR) can stabilize the capsular bag, resulting in outcomes similar to cataract surgeries with a normal capsular bag.2,3 For managing lens dislocation with moderate to severe zonular rupture, two primary surgical approaches are generally used. One approach involves total lens extraction combined with vitrectomy, using various techniques for IOL implantation.3 The other involves focuses on the fixation of the capsular bag, using modified capsular tension rings, capsular tension segments,4–8 capsular anchors,9 and implantable capsular hooks.10–13 Various capsular bag refixation techniques provide the advantages of in-the-bag IOL implantation with minimal surgical trauma. However, many of these techniques are technically complex, challenging to learn, and require specialized materials that may not be readily available. In this study, we used a 7-0 polypropylene suture to create an implantable capsular hook for scleral fixation of the capsular bag in lens subluxation surgery. A 26-G needle was used to guide the 7-0 polypropylene suture into the eye, where the suture was pulled out to shaped into a hook, exerting vertical traction on the capsular bag at the site of the continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis (CCC). This technique results in minimal trauma, eliminates the need for conjunctival and scleral flaps, and does not require suture knotting, making it suitable for moderate to severe lens subluxation without adding complexity when fixing at the 6 o'clock position.

Surgical Technique

The capsular hook was made from a 7-0 polypropylene suture with a built-in suture needle (Prolene, Polypropylene Suture, Ethicon, Johnson & Johnson. The suture is produced by Johnson & Johnson, located in Illinois, USA. The Chinese import medical device number is 201222154), which is approved for ophthalmic surgery. A thermal cautery device was used near the bending point of the suture to thermally shape it into a hook. The surgery was performed by one of the authors (C.C.), as demonstrated in Figure 1 and Supplemental Digital Content 1 (see Video, http://links.lww.com/IAE/C552). The surgery was performed under retrobulbar anesthesia, and a corneal incision was made, preferably on the side with healthy zonules. After completing the CCC, a temporary capsular hook was placed according to the extent of zonular rupture to stabilize the capsular bag, followed by emulsification and aspiration of the lens nucleus. In cases with vitreous prolapse was present, a limbal vitrectomy was performed before the surgery to prevent vitreous traction–related complications. If pars plana vitrectomy was combined due to retinal disease, lens extraction was performed first, followed by procedures in the vitreous cavity. Then, the capsular hook was implanted, and finally, gas or silicone oil filling was performed, as demonstrated in Supplemental Digital Content 1 (see Video, http://links.lww.com/IAE/C553). After the lens nucleus was aspirated, a CTR was implanted to correct capsular bag collapse, followed by further aspiration of the cortex. Finally, an IOL was implanted in the capsular bag.

The investigated technique began by threading a 7-0 polypropylene suture into a 26-G needle. The needle was then advanced 1.5 mm to 2 mm posterior to the limbus, parallel to the iris plane, and positioned in front of the capsular bag. An intraocular forceps was then inserted into the anterior chamber to pull the suture out, and a thermal cautery device was then used to shape the suture into a 3-mm–long hook. A needle holder clamped the built-in suture needle and passing it through the superficial scleral layer at the puncture site one or two times, ensuring a passing distance greater than 4 mm. Both hands coordinated the following procedure: The suture was pulled externally, while the hook was advanced internally into the eye. Once the hook engaged with the capsular bag at the site of the CCC, the intraocular forceps was released, and the tension on the hook was adjusted as necessary, allowing it to exert stable vertical traction on the capsular bag at the CCC (Figure 1H). The suture was then trimmed flush with the scleral surface, and its end tucked into the scleral tunnel.

Results

A total of 11 eyes from 11 patients (8 men and 3 women) with lens subluxation were included (see Table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/IAE/C551). Patients with autoimmune or other rheumatic diseases were excluded. Limited zonular rupture occurred iatrogenically in patient 9 during phacoemulsification, while the other cases resulted from trauma. The lens subluxation involved the 5 to 7 o'clock positions. Three patients had localized retinal detachment with hemorrhage and underwent pars plana vitrectomy. The vitreous cavities of Patients 5 and 7 were filled with air (Figure 2C), which was absorbed within 2 weeks postoperatively. Patient 3 underwent silicone oil filling, and the silicone oil was removed 4 months after surgery, with the IOL–capsular bag complex remaining stable and centered (Figure 2B). The best-corrected visual acuity improved from a preoperative mean of 1.15 ± 0.39 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution [logMAR] (Snellen 20/206) to 0.18 ± 0.15 logMAR (Snellen 20/29) at the final follow-up (paired t-test, P < 0.001). The primary intraoperative complication was minor ciliary body bleeding after intraocular puncture, which was fully absorbed within 1 week after surgery. Three eyes experienced transient intraocular pressure elevation postoperatively, which returned to normal after 1 week of medical treatment. The mean follow-up period was 7.5 months ± 3.6 months (ranging from 5 to 15 months). Throughout the follow-up period, the hook exerted stable vertical traction on the capsular bag at the site of the CCC (Figure 2A), with good IOL positioning and no postoperative complications, such as capsular hook slippage, retinal detachment, or macular edema.

Discussion

In surgeries for lens subluxation with moderate to severe zonular rupture, preserving the capsular bag poses a significant challenge, necessitating scleral fixation to prevent dislocation of the IOL–capsular bag complex. Various capsular fixation techniques have been reported.4–14 The modified capsular tension rings and capsular tension segments feature specialized suture holes for scleral fixation. Some reports describe the use of sutures for fixation,4,5 while others describe the use of IOL haptics or flanged scleral fixation with 5-0 polypropylene sutures.6,7 Capsular hook fixation techniques using glue-assisted or flanged scleral fixation have also been described.11,13 In addition, capsular anchors, resembling a “paper clip,” have been secured using scleral sutures.9 However, these techniques are generally complex and require significant manipulation of the conjunctiva and sclera. Steps such as passing long curved needles through the anterior chamber, suture knotting, and fibrin glue adhesion, can increase surgical time and the risk of long-term suture-related complications. Flange fixation techniques require an accurately shaped flange, and creating a “mushroom-shaped” flange that does not slip or protrude through the conjunctiva presents a challenge. Achieving precise control over the flange size, shape, and tension necessitates considerable skill.15 Furthermore, the persistent tension on the capsular bag and the lack of frictional support from an effective suture tunnel increase the risk of flange slippage.

A sutureless capsular hook technique using 5-0 polypropylene sutures has been reported.14 However, the 5-0 polypropylene suture is thick, stiff, and highly rigid. If the hook twists during surgery, it could potentially tear the capsular bag. In addition, the hook may exert oblique traction at a large-angle on the capsular bag at the site of the CCC, increasing the risk of hook slippage and rupture of healthy zonules. Another drawback is the uncontrolled intraoperative risk associated with using a large, long, curved needle for intraocular puncture. In this study, we modified the capsular hook technique to offer several advantages: 1) the 7-0 polypropylene suture is inserted into the eye using a 26-G needle, and intraocular forceps are used to pull the suture out to form a hook. The hand-guided needle offers good control, even when performing fixation at the 6 o'clock position; 2) the 7-0 polypropylene suture is soft, and the hook exerts vertical traction on the capsular bag at the site of the CCC (Figure 2A), making slippage unlikely and avoiding tangential traction on healthy zonules; 3) the technique is simple, time-efficient, with no need for a conjunctival incision or scleral flap creation; 4) the capsular hook provides sufficient and adjustable traction, enhancing the stability and centration of the IOL–capsular bag complex. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to use capsular hooks made from 7-0 polypropylene sutures for lens subluxation.

For cases of moderate to severe zonular rupture, implantation of a CTR is essential. The CTR offers circumferential support to the capsular bag, restoring its round contour and preventing collapse and laxity. The combined use of a CTR and capsular hook stabilizes the capsular bag and maintains the optical center of the IOL. For patients with lens subluxation complicated by retinal diseases who require combined pars plana vitrectomy, 1 to 3 capsular hooks were implanted based on the extent of zonular rupture. The IOL–capsular bag complex, together with the iris, acts as a barrier to prevent anterior chamber fluid loss and to block the entry of gas or silicone oil from the vitreous cavity (Figure 2, B and C). Among 11 cases in this study, 3 underwent combined pars plana vitrectomy, all with IOL implantation. The vitreous cavity was filled with air in two cases and with silicone oil in one case.

The key aspect of the capsular hook technique is that the intraocular forceps holding the hook should not be released until it is confirmed that the hook has securely engaged the capsular bag at the site of the CCC. After this, the external scleral suture should then be gradually tightened. If an obtuse angle forms where the hook engages with the capsular bag at the CCC, it indicates effective engagement. In addition, if the hook maintains a vertical position relative to the capsular bag at the CCC, slippage under normal conditions is unlikely. Postoperatively, gradual fibrosis of the capsular membrane envelops the hook, increasing its stability. Therefore, patients are advised to avoid pressing on the affected eye during the early postoperative period. Another critical factor is the distance the suture needle travels within the scleral layer; a distance greater than 4 mm is believed to provide sufficient friction. To increase the surgeon's confidence, passing the needle through the scleral layer one more time or creating a small flange at the end of the suture using thermal cautery may be used. This technique is minimally invasive with few complications. In this study, three experienced a small amount of anterior chamber hemorrhage intraoperatively, which resolved within one week. No postoperative complications, such as suture breakage, hook slippage, or IOL displacement, were observed during the follow-up period.

In conclusion, the implantable capsular hooks made from 7-0 polypropylene sutures can serve as an alternative method for stabilizing the capsular bag in surgeries for moderate to severe lens subluxation. This technique is simple, minimally invasive, and time-efficient. Our experience indicates that this method is safe and has achieved satisfactory outcomes in treated patients. However, the limitations of this study include the small sample size and short follow-up period. Further observation and comparison with other methods are needed to confirm the long-term effects.
