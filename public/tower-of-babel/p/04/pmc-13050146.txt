Modelling healthy ageing in South Africa: a network analysis of intrinsic capacities
Authors: Lawrence Ejike Ugwu, Erhabor Sunday Idemudia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe rapidly increasing older adult population in sub-Saharan Africa necessitates an understanding of how health domains interact to shape healthy ageing. Existing approaches overlook complex interdependencies between cognitive, physical, emotional, and functional capacities. This study applies network analysis to model these relationships in a large cohort of older adults in rural South Africa.MethodsWe analysed data from 4783 participants (aged ≥40) in the 2021–2022 wave of the HAALSI study. Mixed Graphical Models (MGMs) were estimated from a mixed correlation matrix to assess conditional associations among 12 variables spanning cognitive function, depressive symptoms, physical capacity, self-rated health, and socioeconomic status. Central and bridge nodes were identified, and gender-stratified networks were compared.ResultsThe network revealed strong interconnectivity among intrinsic capacity domains. Cognitive function (delayed recall) and functional ability (ADL limitations) emerged as the most central nodes, with self-rated health and depressive symptoms as key bridges. While global network strength and structure did not differ significantly by gender, men’s networks were anchored around cognitive function, whereas women’s were more centred on physical functioning. Depressive symptom clustering was denser among women.ConclusionsIntrinsic capacity domains are tightly coupled in rural South African older adults; cognition and ADL limitations are pivotal. Subtle gender differences in network structure suggest a need for tailored, multidomain interventions. These findings support the WHO Healthy Ageing framework and highlight the utility of network models for identifying intervention leverage points in low-resource settings.Keywords: intrinsic capacity, network analysis, cognitive function, activities of daily living (ADL), sub-Saharan Africa, gender differences, functional ability, rural health, older people

Introduction

Population ageing is accelerating globally, with low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) experiencing rapid increases. In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the population aged 60 and older is projected to grow by over 218% between 2019 and 2050 [1]. South Africa exemplifies this demographic transition, with the proportion of individuals aged 60 and above increasing from 7.0% in 1996 to 9.8% by 2022 [2]. This shift places significant strain on already under-resourced health systems, particularly in rural regions marked by high HIV prevalence, poverty, and limited access to care. As older adults in these contexts face an increasing burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), disability, and cognitive decline, there is an urgent need for comprehensive frameworks to understand and promote successful ageing [3].

Successful ageing is a multidimensional construct. Classical models such as that of Rowe and Kahn [4] emphasised physical health, cognitive functioning, and active engagement with life. Contemporary frameworks have expanded this view to include subjective well-being, social participation, and economic security [5]. The World Health Organisation (WHO) operationalises this broader understanding through its Healthy Ageing Framework, which centres on ‘functional ability,’ the interaction between an individual’s intrinsic capacity and their environment [6]. Intrinsic capacity encompasses domains such as cognition, mobility, psychological well-being, vitality, and sensory function. Understanding how these components interact dynamically is particularly important in resource-limited settings, where constraints may shape ageing trajectories in distinct ways.

Traditional analytic methods, such as regression or latent variable models, often treat health indicators as downstream effects of unobserved constructs, thereby masking the potential interdependence between individual symptoms or capacities [7]. This limitation is increasingly recognised in ageing research, where domains such as cognition, mood, and physical function frequently co-occur and may reinforce one another. Network theory, particularly the Network Theory of Mental Disorders, provides a helpful lens for conceptualising health not as the outcome of latent variables but as the emergent property of interacting symptoms or traits [7]. This perspective aligns well with the WHO’s emphasis on the interconnected nature of intrinsic capacity and its real-world manifestations.

Network analysis, and Mixed Graphical Models (MGMs) in particular, provide empirical tools for modelling these complex interrelationships. MGMs estimate partial correlations among observed variables, visualising them as networks where nodes represent symptoms or traits and edges represent conditional dependencies [8]. These models enable the identification of central nodes (variables with the most influence) and bridge nodes that connect otherwise separate clusters, offering insight into how interventions targeting specific capacities may reverberate across the system [9].

Although network methods are increasingly applied in psychiatric and psychosomatic research, their application to broader ageing-related health in LMICs remains limited. Most studies to date originate from high-income settings and focus on specific conditions, such as depression or frailty [10], with little exploration of how multiple domains of healthy ageing interact in diverse populations.

This gap is especially relevant in SSA, where structural inequities and distinct life-course exposures likely shape network structures differently than in high-income contexts [11]. Moreover, gender differences in ageing trajectories are well-documented. Women often live longer than men but report higher levels of disability, depressive symptoms, and poorer self-rated health, a phenomenon known as the male–female health–survival paradox [12]. Despite this, few network studies have stratified analyses by sex to explore potential divergences. This study addresses these gaps using data from the Health and Ageing in Africa: A Longitudinal Study of an INDEPTH Community (HAALSI), a population-based cohort in rural South Africa [13]. As one of the few population-based studies on ageing in the region, HAALSI provides the rich, multidomain data on cognitive, physical, and emotional capacities required to construct such a network model.

Guided by the WHO Healthy Ageing Framework and grounded in Borsboom’s network theory, this study applies Mixed Graphical Modelling to examine the interrelationships among key domains of intrinsic capacity (cognitive, physical, emotional, and functional) in a large, rural South African cohort of older adults. Specifically, we aim to [1] identify central and bridging nodes within the health domain network, [2] assess whether cognitive or functional markers occupy dominant structural positions, and [3] evaluate gender-based differences in network architecture. By elucidating how symptoms and traits interact within and across domains, our analysis provides a systems-level understanding of ageing in an underrepresented setting, offering critical insights for targeted, context-sensitive interventions.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study is a cross-sectional analysis based on Wave 3 of the Health and Ageing in Africa: A Longitudinal Study of an INDEPTH Community (HAALSI), conducted in 2021–2022. HAALSI is a population-based cohort of adults aged 40 years and older residing in the Agincourt Health and Socio-Demographic Surveillance System (HDSS) site in rural Mpumalanga, South Africa [13]. The Agincourt HDSS, managed by the Medical Research Council/Wits Rural Public Health and Health Transitions Research Unit, provides a well-characterised demographic platform for longitudinal ageing research.

Participants

A total of 5059 respondents participated in HAALSI Wave 3. For this study, we included participants with complete data on selected psychosocial, cognitive, physical, and socioeconomic variables. After excluding cases with excessive missingness or survey skip patterns, the final analytic sample comprised 4783 adults (54.2% female), with a mean age of 61.7 years (SD = 13.2, range: 40–95).

Measures

We selected 12 variables (nodes) aligned with multidimensional models of healthy ageing, spanning five key domains. Each variable was treated as a node in the network model.

Depressive Symptoms (6 nodes): Six items from the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) were used [14, 15]. Participants provided binary (yes/no) responses to assess symptoms in the past week: ‘felt depressed,’ ‘everything was an effort,’ ‘restless sleep,’ ‘felt lonely,’ ‘felt happy,’ and ‘enjoyed life.’

All continuous variables were standardised before analysis.

Analytical strategy

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.1 [16] using the bootnet, qgraph, and NetworkComparisonTest packages.

Given the mixed data types (binary, count, and continuous), we estimated a Mixed Graphical Model (MGM). This was achieved by first computing a mixed correlation matrix (using the cor_auto function in bootnet) that automatically estimated the appropriate bivariate correlations for each pair of variables (i.e. Pearson, polychoric, or polyserial).

The resulting correlation matrix was used to estimate a regularised partial correlation network using the graphical least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (glasso) with extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) model selection (γ = 0.5) to ensure a parsimonious (sparse) network.

Edge-weight accuracy was evaluated via nonparametric bootstrap (2000 resamples), and the stability of centrality metrics was assessed using a case-dropping subset bootstrap. We report the correlation stability coefficient (CS-C), with values >0.5 considered acceptable [8]. Strength centrality (sum of absolute edge weights) was used to identify influential nodes. Bridge centrality was also computed to assess the role of nodes linking different domains.

Networks were estimated separately for men and women using identical tuning parameters. The NetworkComparisonTest package was used to assess:

Global Strength Invariance: Difference in total edge weight.

Global Strength Invariance: Difference in total edge weight.

Network Structure Invariance: Permutation-based test of edge distribution differences.

Edge-Specific Differences: Identification of significant edge disparities between male and female networks.

Sensitivity analyses

We examined network robustness at multiple tuning parameters (γ = 0.25 and 0.75) and repeated estimation, excluding highly skewed or rare variables.

Discussion

Our network analysis revealed a highly integrated structure of factors underlying healthy ageing in this rural South African sample. Cognitive function (delayed recall) and functional ability (ADL limitations) emerged as the most central hubs in the overall network. This suggests these domains are pivotal, with strong connections to both physical and affective measures.

Our finding of cognition as a key hub differs from some network studies in high-income cohorts, such as Koivunen et al. [17], which identified walking speed as the most central node. This discrepancy may highlight significant contextual differences; in a rural South African setting with different life-course exposures, cognitive capacity may play a more central organising role in the health network than locomotion.

Furthermore, we found that self-rated health and depressive symptoms acted as key bridges connecting different health clusters. The role of affective symptoms as a bridge is particularly notable, reinforcing a growing body of research. For instance, Levin et al. [10], in a network analysis of older adults, also demonstrated strong interconnections between late-life anxiety, depression, and physical health, supporting this view of mental health as a critical link between health domains.

Gender-specific patterns

We found subtle sex differences in network structure. Statistical tests indicated no significant overall disparity in network topology or global connectivity between men and women, but individual nodes and edges differed in importance by gender. In the men’s network, cognitive function had the highest strength, whereas in women, ADL limitations topped the list. This suggests that cognitive performance plays a relatively larger role in men’s well-being networks, while functional mobility is comparatively more central for women.

The distinct topological structures observed in men and women may be interpreted through the lens of customary gender roles in the Agincourt sub-district. In this rural setting, older women frequently bear a ‘triple burden’ of caregiving, domestic maintenance, and subsistence activities, tasks that are physically demanding, such as fetching water and wood [13]. Consequently, any limitation in physical function (ADLs) immediately disrupts their ability to fulfil these primary social roles, thereby radiating rapidly to other domains such as self-rated health and depressive symptoms. In contrast, older men in this community often transition into roles centred on household governance, livestock management, and community adjudication (traditional courts), where cognitive competence is the primary currency of social capital. Thus, cognitive function serves as the central anchor for the male health network, whereas physical function is the linchpin for women.

One possible interpretation is that in this setting, men’s livelihood or social roles may hinge more on cognitive decision-making, whereas women’s daily tasks (often involving household or community support roles) amplify the impact of physical limitations. Consistent with this, we observed that edges linking depressed mood and happiness, as well as between sleep difficulty and happiness, differed by gender: for example, the negative associations between feeling sad and feeling happy were significantly stronger in women than men. These gender-specific patterns hint that the interplay of psychological and functional factors may manifest differently for men and women, even though the overall network of ageing remains similarly interconnected in both groups. Our findings suggest tailoring interventions by gender—for instance, focusing on mobility and ADL assistance (e.g. assistive devices, home supports) may particularly benefit women, whereas cognitive training and mental stimulation might yield outsized gains for men. At the same time, the lack of major structural differences implies that broad strategies (addressing multiple domains simultaneously) are generally applicable to both sexes.

As reported, while we found no significant global differences in network structure, the analysis revealed subtle sex differences in network architecture. In the men’s network, cognitive function had the highest strength, whereas in women, ADL limitations were the most significant. To our knowledge, this is the first study to identify this specific gender-based divergence in node centrality within a network model. However, this finding is highly consistent with the well-documented ‘male–female health–survival paradox,’ where older women, while living longer, consistently report higher rates of disability and functional limitations than men [12]. Our finding provides a novel, systems-level insight into this paradox, suggesting that functional limitations may be a more dominant and organising factor in the overall health system of older women.

Theoretical integration

Alignment with the WHO healthy ageing framework

This result resonates with the World Health Organisation’s healthy ageing framework. WHO defines healthy ageing as ‘the process of developing and maintaining the functional ability that enables wellbeing in older age’ [18]. In our network, nodes correspond to intrinsic capacities (e.g. cognitive ability, locomotion) and their interconnections illustrate how losses in one capacity can propagate to others. For example, the strong ADL–cognition links imply that a decline in one of these capacities is likely to affect the other, reflecting what WHO terms a loss of intrinsic capacity.

A network theory perspective on intrinsic capacity

Borsboom’s [7] (2017) network theory of psychopathology provides insight into these interdependencies. It posits that if variables are causally connected strongly enough, they can create self-sustaining feedback loops. Analogously, our network implies that ageing-related deficits can become mutually reinforcing: cognitive decline may impair physical coordination (leading to ADL difficulties), which in turn may exacerbate cognitive load and depressive symptoms, entrenching a frail state. In network terms, highly central nodes (such as cognition or ADL) act as hubs through which perturbations spread, providing a clear mechanism for interventions.

Integrating with network models of resilience

Finally, our study connects with intrinsic capacity research applying network methods. By viewing healthy ageing through the combined lenses of WHO’s framework and network theory, we gain a richer understanding of why cognitive function appeared so central: it represents a significant intrinsic capacity whose decline can percolate through multiple health dimensions. This network-based view is consistent with resilience models of ageing; just as Brinkhof et al. [19] interpret factors promoting resilience as linked pathways in their network, we see that protective capacities and risk factors for decline intertwine.

Policy and practice implications

These findings have concrete implications for interventions and policies in ageing. A key insight is that efforts to promote healthy ageing should be multifaceted. Because cognitive function serves as a central hub, programs that enhance cognitive reserve (such as adult education or memory training) may have ripple effects on physical health and mood. Similarly, initiatives to maintain physical capacity (e.g. community exercise classes, fall prevention) are likely to support better cognitive and emotional well-being.

We recommend integrated interventions that simultaneously target cognitive, physical, and psychosocial domains. Given women’s networks centred on ADL, policies could prioritise supports that reduce physical burdens for older women (e.g. home-based care, age-friendly infrastructure). For men, initiatives that maintain cognitive engagement may yield particular benefits. In both cases, health workers should be aware of the central role of these capacities and screen accordingly.

Health systems in South Africa should incorporate routine functional assessments. For instance, integrating tools for cognitive screening and ADL evaluation into HIV or chronic disease clinics could identify at-risk elders early. Training community health workers to recognise the signs of network distress (e.g. co-occurring memory and mobility issues) will help target interventions before negative feedback loops intensify.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that offer avenues for future research. First, the cross-sectional design prevents any causal interpretations. The network edges represent conditional dependence, not directed influence. Future research should use longitudinal network models with HAALSI’s subsequent waves to untangle these directional relationships.

Second, the network is, by definition, limited to the 12 nodes included. Omitting other key variables (such as social engagement, sensory function, or biomarkers) could alter the network’s structure.

Third, the findings are from a specific rural cohort in South Africa and may not be generalizable to urban populations or other contexts in sub-Saharan Africa.

Finally, some measures had statistical limitations, such as the strong floor effect in the ADL limitations variable, which may have attenuated its connections within the network.

Conclusions

Our results directly support the aspirations of the WHO Decade of Healthy Ageing. By identifying leverage points in the network of intrinsic capacities, we provide evidence to guide interventions that keep older adults functional and valued in their communities. Investing in cognitive health and functional independence (for example, through education, physical rehabilitation, and social programs) will advance the Decade’s aim to improve older adults’ lives worldwide. In this way, our study links detailed empirical findings back to the grand challenge of promoting healthy, successful ageing for all.
