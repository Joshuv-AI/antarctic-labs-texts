Low uric acid predicts severe outcomes in neuronal surface antibody-mediated autoimmune encephalitis
Authors: Ying-Zhe Shao, Ning Zhao, Qiu-Xia Zhang, Lin-Jie Zhang, Li Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutoimmune encephalitis (AE) mediated by neuronal surface antibodies is a severe neuroinflammatory disorder with heterogeneous clinical manifestations. Early identification of patients at risk for severe outcomes, such as intensive care unit (ICU) admission, remains a challenge. Uric acid (UA), known for its dual role in inflammation as both a danger-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) and an antioxidant, has been implicated in various autoimmune diseases, but its prognostic significance in AE has not been systematically explored.MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled 90 patients with neuronal surface antibody-positive AE. Serum UA levels were measured at admission, and clinical outcomes, including Clinical Assessment Scale for Autoimmune Encephalitis (CASE), discharge modified Rankin Scale (mRS) and ICU admission were analyzed. Statistical methods included Spearman correlation, logistic regression, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to evaluate predictive value of UA.ResultsPatients requiring ICU admission had significantly lower UA levels (median 193.0 vs. 250.0 µmol/L, p = 0.008). UA negatively correlated with CASE scores (r = -0.237, p = 0.024) and discharge mRS (r = -0.267, p = 0.011). Logistic analysis identified UA as a potential adjunct biomarker of ICU admission (yes/no) (OR = 0.985, 95%CI:0.971-1.000, p = 0.044), with an optimal cutoff of 213.5 µmol/L (AUC = 0.703, sensitivity 63.9%, specificity 77.8%). Patients with UA < 213.5 µmol/L were older and had higher CASE scores and worse functional outcomes (p < 0.05).ConclusionDecreased serum UA at admission is associated with increased risk of ICU admission in neuronal surface antibody-mediated AE, and correlate with a more severe disease severity and poorer functional outcomes, suggesting its potential as a prognostic biomarker, possibly reflecting its neuroprotective role.Keywords: Uric acid, Autoimmune encephalitis, Neuronal surface antibodies, Biomarker, ICU admission, Neuroinflammation

Introduction

Autoimmune encephalitis (AE) represents a heterogeneous group of neuroinflammatory disorders characterized by immune-mediated attacks against central nervous system antigens, leading to diverse clinical manifestations such as seizures, cognitive dysfunction, and psychiatric disturbance [1]. The early identification of patients at risk for severe disease progression or intensive care unit (ICU) admission remains a critical challenge in clinical practice. While antibody testing and neuroimaging aid in diagnosis, there is an urgent need for readily accessible biomarkers that can predict disease severity and guide therapeutic decision-making [2].

Uric acid (UA), the end product of purine metabolism, has garnered increasing attention for its role in immune regulation. As an endogenous danger-associated molecular pattern (DAMP), UA can activate innate immune responses through mechanisms such as NLRP3 inflammasome activation and Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling, promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [3, 4]. Paradoxically, UA also exhibits antioxidant properties at lower concentrations, suggesting a dual role in modulating inflammation. Elevated UA levels have been linked to disease severity in various autoimmune and neurological conditions, including neuromyelitis optica [5, 6], myasthenia gravis [7], and chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) [8]. However, its clinical significance in AE, particularly in cases mediated by neuronal surface antibodies, remains unexplored.

Given the neuroinflammatory pathogenesis of AE and the immunomodulatory effects of UA, we hypothesized that serum UA levels could serve as a prognostic biomarker for disease severity and outcomes. This study aimed to investigate the association between UA levels and clinical features, including ICU admission, respiratory failure, and functional disability, in a retrospective cohort of patients with neuronal surface antibody-positive AE. Our findings may provide new insights into the role of UA in neuroinflammation and offer a practical tool for risk stratification in AE management.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective cohort study included 90 patients diagnosed with AE with positive neuronal surface antibody treated at the Department of Neurology, Tianjin Medical University General Hospital between August 2013 and April 2025. Eligible participants met the following requirements: (1) age ≥ 16 years; (2) definite AE diagnosis based on current diagnostic guidelines [1]; (3) confirmed presence of neuronal surface antibodies in either blood or cerebrospinal fluid samples; (4) comprehensive medical documentation. The exclusion criteria included: (1) coexisting acute neurological conditions; (2) preexisting movement disorder diagnoses or other conditions that influence the disability and functional score; (3) major chronic illnesses affecting hematologic, renal or hepatic systems; and (4) recent immunotherapy within 28 days before hospitalization. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University General Hospital (IRB2025-YX-308-01).

Data collection

Clinical data were systematically retrieved from electronic medical records, including age at onset, sex, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia), time from onset to admission, clinical manifestations, and outcome metrics. Disease severity was assessed using two validated clinical instruments: the Clinical Assessment Scale for Autoimmune Encephalitis (CASE), and the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at discharge [9]. The mRS Score is evaluated based on the following criteria: completely asymptomatic, completely normal neurological function, and no impact on daily life: 0 points. Despite symptoms, there is no obvious functional impairment and the ability to complete daily work and life: 1 point. Mild disability, unable to complete all activities before illness, but no assistance is needed and can take care of one’s own daily affairs: 2 points. Moderate disability, requires partial assistance, but can walk independently: 3 points. Moderate to severe disability, unable to walk independently, and requires care in daily life: 4 points. Severe disability, bedridden, incontinence of urine and feces, abnormal life, completely dependent on others: 5 points. Death: 6 points. The CASE scale consisting of 9 items (seizure, memory dysfunction, psychiatric symptoms, consciousness, language problems, dyskinesia/dystonia, gait instability and ataxia, brainstem dysfunction, and weakness) was developed. Each item was assigned a value of up to 3 points. No epileptic seizure: 0 points; Controllable epilepsy: 1 point; Refractory epilepsy: 2 points; Epileptic status: 3 points. No memory dysfunction: 0 points; Mild memory dysfunction (not affecting daily life): 1 point; Moderate memory dysfunction (affecting daily life): 2 points; Severe memory dysfunction (unable to communicate or recent memory loss): 3 points. No psychiatric symptoms: 0 points; Mild psychiatric symptoms (not affecting life, no need for medication): 1 point; Moderate psychiatric symptoms (requiring medication treatment): 2 points; Severe psychiatric symptoms or inability to cooperate with examinations: 3 points. Consciousness clear: 0 points; Drowsiness: 1 point; Coma: 2 points; Unconsciousness: 3 points. No language problems: 0 points; Mild language problems (slow, but able to express sentences): 1 point; Moderate language problems (unable to express complete sentences): 2 points; Severe language problems (unable to communicate): 3 points. No dyskinesia and dystonia: 0 points; Mild dyskinesia or dystonia (not affecting daily life): 1 point; Moderate dyskinesia or dystonia (affecting daily life): 2 points; Severe dyskinesia -induced secondary medical problems (self-harm, rhabdomyolysis, requiring restraint, damage to venous access): 3 points. No gait instability and ataxia: 0 points; Mild gait instability or ataxia (no support needed for walking): 1 point; Moderate gait instability or ataxia (assisted walking): 2 points; Severe gait instability or ataxia (unable to walk): 3 points; No brainstem dysfunction: 0 points; Pupillary paralysis: 1 point; Gastric tube treatment: 2 points; Ventilator treatment due to central hypoventilation: 3 points. No weakness (muscle strength level 5): 0 points; Mild weakness (muscle strength level 4): 1 point; Moderate weakness (muscle strength level 3): 2 points; Severe weakness (muscle strength less than level 3): 3 points.

Laboratory parameters included serum uric acid, creatinine and urea measured within 24 h of admission and prior to the initiation of immunotherapy. Serum and/or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples were subjected to standardized antibody testing using indirect immunofluorescence assays. The ICU admission criteria encompassed patients exhibiting at least one of the following clinical indications: (1) acute neurological deterioration requiring intensive monitoring, including status epilepticus, coma, or severe encephalopathy; (2) respiratory compromise necessitating invasive ventilatory support; (3) circulatory collapse demanding vasoactive medications; (4) systemic inflammatory response with organ failure; (5) intracranial hypertension requiring therapeutic management; or (6) Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II)II scores ≥ 15 suggesting critical illness. Consistent with critical care research protocols, we only classified patients as ICU cases if their intensive care duration exceeded 48 h.

Statistical analysis

Conduct the Shapiro-Wilk normality test for the continuous variables. Non-normally distributed data were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR), and comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are represented by the number (percentage), and the differences are compared using the chi-square test. Spearman correlation was used to assess the relationship between uric acid and disease severity scores. Sex, age, time from onset to admission, uric acid, urea, creatinine, hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia were included in the linear regression model with CASE score as the dependent variable by the backward elimination, and the variables related to the CASE score were screened out. The sex, age, time from onset to admission, CASE score, uric acid, urea, creatinine, hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia were included in the logistic regression model as dependent variables by backward (LR), and the predictors of ICU treatment (yes or no) were screened out. The discriminative performance of uric acid in predicting ICU admission was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the curve (AUC) and 95% CIs were reported for uric acid, with optimal cutoff values determined using Youden’s index. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Graphical visualizations were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 6.01; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 90 patients diagnosed with AE with positive neuronal surface antibody were retrospectively enrolled between August 2013 and April 2025. The cohort exhibited a male predominance (64.4%, n = 58), with a mean age at onset of 57.0 (31.0) years. Antibody profiling identified anti-leucine-rich glioma-inactivated 1 (LGI1) antibodies as the most prevalent subtype (40.0%, n = 36), followed by anti-N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) (33.3%, n = 30), anti-γ-aminobutyric acid B receptor (GABABR) (12.2%, n = 11), and anti-contactin associated protein 2 (CASPR2) (4.4%, n = 4). The remaining patients (10.0%, n = 9) were positive for other neuronal surface antibody AE-associated antibodies. The median time from the onset of the disease to admission of the patients was 20.0 (51.0) days. Disease severity at admission was assessed using the CASE, with a median score of 5 (9), indicating moderate severity. Functional outcomes at discharge, evaluated by the mRS, revealed a median score of 2 (2), reflecting generally favorable short-term recovery. However, 34.4% (n = 31) of patients exhibited significant disability (mRS ≥ 3), and 20.0% (n = 18) required admission to the ICU during hospitalization (Tables 1 and 2).

Association between uric acid and disease severity

Patients with age < 60 had higher uric acid than patients with age ≥ 60 (270.5 vs. 209.0, p = 0.020). The serum uric acid had no significant difference between male and female. Patients requiring ICU admission had markedly decreased serum uric acid levels (median 193.0 vs. 250.0 umol/L, p = 0.008) compared to non-ICU patients. While among patients with respiratory failure and poor functional outcomes (mRS ≥ 3), there were no significant changes with patients without respiratory failure and good functional outcomes (mRS < 3) (200.0 vs. 242.0 umol/L, p = 0.136; 203.0 vs. 253.0, p = 0.066) (Table 2). Spearman correlation analyses further clarified the associations (Fig. 1). Uric acid negatively correlated with CASE and discharge mRS (r = -0.237, p = 0.024; r = -0.267, p = 0.011).

Predictive value of uric acid for ICU admission

Sex, age, time from onset to admission, uric acid, urea, creatinine, hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia were included in the linear regression model with CASE score as the dependent variable by the backward elimination, results showed that UA (β = -0.016, 95% CI: -0.029- -0.004, p = 0.013), urea (β = 0.596, 95% CI: 0.155-1.036, p = 0.009) and age (β = -0.088, 95%CI:-0.156- -0.020, p = 0.012) was associated with the CASE score. The sex, age, time from onset to admission, CASE score, uric acid, urea, creatinine, hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia were included in the logistic regression model as dependent variables by backward (LR), the results showed that UA (OR = 0.985, 95% CI: 0.971- 1.000, p = 0.044) and CASE score (OR = 1.391, 95% CI: 1.203–1.609, p < 0.001) were predictors of ICU treatment (yes or no).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis confirmed the strong discriminative power of uric acid (AUC = 0.703; 95% CI: 0.573-0.832, p = 0.008) for ICU admission (yes/no) (Fig. 2). The optimal uric acid cutoff of 213.5 umol/L yielded a sensitivity of 63.9% and specificity of 77.8%.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that decreased serum UA levels at admission are significantly associated with an increased risk of ICU admission and are correlated with a more severe disease severity and poorer functional outcomes in patients with neuronal surface antibody-mediated autoimmune encephalitis (AE). These findings suggest that UA may serve as a clinically useful prognostic biomarker for disease severity in AE, possibly reflecting its neuroprotective and antioxidant properties.

UA has been well-established as a marker linked to vascular and metabolic syndrome, its role in immune-mediated disorders remains complex and context-dependent. In systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), higher serum uric acid levels are associated with global damage in patients with SLE [10]. Elevated serum UA levels are commonly observed in cardiovascular and renal diseases, linked to renal arteriolopathy, and predict poor prognosis in patients with lupus nephritis [3]. Additionally, in rheumatoid arthritis (RA), higher serum UA levels are frequently observed and positively correlated with disease severity [11]. Studies have shown that both urate crystals and soluble uric acid can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18, thereby amplifying systemic inflammation [12, 13].

In contrast, a growing body of evidence suggests that UA may exert protective effects in neuroimmune diseases. Meta-analyses and clinical studies have shown significantly lower serum UA levels in patients with myasthenia gravis [7], Guillain-Barré syndrome [14], multiple sclerosis and neuromyelitis optica [5, 6, 15]. In MS, low UA levels have been linked to increased disease activity, relapses, and compromised blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity [16, 17]. Notably, similar trends are observed in both serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), indicating a systemic and central role for UA in modulating neuroinflammatory processes [18].

UA is the end product of purine metabolism and functions as a potent endogenous antioxidant, capable of scavenging peroxynitrite and other reactive oxygen species (ROS). This antioxidative capacity is believed to underlie its neuroprotective effects. In animal models of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, UA administration reduced CNS infiltration of inflammatory cells and ameliorated disease severity in a dose-dependent manner. These findings highlight the potential for UA to modulate neuroinflammatory responses in central nervous system autoimmune diseases [19, 20].

Research specifically examining UA in AE is still limited, primarily focusing on anti-N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) encephalitis. Prior studies involving 58 anti-NMDAR encephalitis patients have reported lower serum UA levels during acute disease phases, which normalize after treatment and correlate with clinical severity [21]. Moreover, CSF UA concentrations are associated with serum levels and disease burden, while the CSF/serum UA ratio has been shown to correlate with the BBB index, supporting a role for UA in reflecting neuroinflammatory activity and BBB disruption in AE [22].

The observed association between lower UA levels and ICU admission aligns with the known role of UA as an antioxidant. As an antioxidant, UA scavenges singlet oxygen, hydroxyl radicals, hydrogen peroxide, and peroxynitrite [23]. Studies have found that human physiological concentrations of UA modulate the activity of SOD1 and SOD3 by preventing their inactivation by hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) [24]. UA can also form stable complexes with metal ions such as iron and copper, converting them into less reactive forms and thereby inhibiting their ability to catalyze free radical reactions [25]. However, the complex relationship between neuronal surface antibody-mediated AE and low serum UA levels remains unclear. It is uncertain whether the decrease in UA is due to its consumption in response to oxidative stress from inflammation or low UA levels contribute to insufficient neuroprotection, thereby correlating with disease severity. Further mechanistic studies are needed to elucidate this relationship. Interestingly, we found that UA levels were significantly higher in younger patients (< 60 years), which may reflect age-related differences in purine metabolism or inflammatory responses [4].

Notably, UA showed stronger predictive value for ICU admission than for functional outcomes (mRS ≥ 3) or respiratory failure. This may be because ICU admission criteria in AE often reflect acute, life-threatening complications that are more directly linked to inflammatory burden, whereas long-term disability is influenced by additional factors like treatment response and neural repair mechanisms [2]. The optimal UA cutoff of 213.5 µmol/L demonstrated moderate predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.703), suggesting it could be combined with other biomarkers (e.g., CSF cytokines) or clinical scores (CASE) for improved risk stratification [9].

Several limitations warrant consideration. CSF UA levels were not measured in this study, which may more directly reflect CNS-specific oxidative and inflammatory activity. The observational design precludes causal inferences about the role of UA in AE pathogenesis. Future research should investigate whether modifying UA levels through therapeutic interventions might influence outcomes in patients with severe or relapsing AE, as suggested by experimental neuroinflammatory models [8]. Furthermore, this study did not include other factors that might affect the UA level, such as diet, nutritional status, and medications. This affects the generalizability of the study’s conclusions. Future research will incorporate these confounding factors to enhance persuasiveness of the research conclusions. Additionally, data on pro-inflammatory cytokines of the research subjects, such as IL-1β, IL-6, or TNF-α, were not available in this study. Future research will explore the association between UA and these inflammatory factors. Finally, this study included patients with multiple positive antibodies. There may be heterogeneity among different antibody types. Therefore, larger sample size studies are needed in the future to verify whether UA can be a potential adjunct biomarker in patients with different antibody types.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study identifies serum uric acid as a promising prognostic biomarker for ICU admission in neuronal surface antibody-mediated autoimmune encephalitis. These findings highlight a potential interplay between purine metabolism and neuroinflammation and underscore the need for further studies to explore the mechanistic role of UA and therapeutic potential in AE.
