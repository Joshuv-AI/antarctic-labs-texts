Unsolicited Patient Complaints in Spine Surgery: An In-Depth Study of How and Why They Occur
Authors: Harsh Jain, Hani Chanbour, Iyan Younus, Omar Zakieh, Soren Jonzzon, Campbell Liles, William O. Cooper, Julian G. Lugo-Pico, Raymond J. Gardocki, Richard Berkman, Amir M. Abtahi, Byron F. Stephens, Scott L. Zuckerman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES:The high-risk yet elective nature of spine surgery can often lead to patient complaints. Examining these complaints can improve care quality and experience. This multi-institutional study analyzed unsolicited patient complaints (UPCs) in spine surgery to: (1) compare mean complaints among spine surgeons, other surgeons, and nonsurgeon physicians; (2) categorize patient complaints; and (3) assess whether complaints were linked to surgeon-patient interactions.METHODS:A multi-institutional cross-sectional study across 200 hospitals (2014-2017) analyzed deidentified UPCs regarding neurosurgery/orthopedic spine teams, categorizing complaints as surgeon- or nonsurgeon-related, focusing on communication, professionalism, complications, and hospital infrastructure. Descriptive and bivariate statistics were performed.RESULTS:Of 42 380 physicians, 525 were spine surgeons, averaging 7.2 ± 8.3 UPCs, significantly higher (P < .001) than nonspine surgeons (5.6 ± 6.3) and nonsurgeons (3.3 ± 5.4). Of 149 UPCs, 43% met multiple categories, 42% were not directly related to surgeon-patient interactions, and 58% occurred preoperatively. Reasons for complaints were as follows: Communication (64%): 63.2% stemmed from lack of communication rather than poor communication (36.8%). Attending surgeons accounted for 70.5% of communication issues. Lack of empathy was reported in 10.1%. Professionalism (26%): 86.8% were attributed to the attending surgeon. Postoperative complications (24%): Permanent deficits (4%) and dissatisfaction with outcomes (28.9%) were reported. Hospital infrastructure (14%): Cost/insurance-related issues (4.0%) were reported. People not offered surgery (12%): Reasons included the following: infrastructure/personnel (33.3%), risks-outweighing-benefits (27.8%), patient-related health reasons (11.1%), and perceived discrimination (5.6%). Clinic issues (12%): Dissatisfaction with not being seen by the surgeon (5.4%), long waiting times (6.0%), and appointment without previous imaging (1.3%) were reported.CONCLUSION:Among spine surgeons, 42% of complaints were unrelated to direct surgeon-patient interactions, and 58% occurred before any surgery was performed. Communication (64%) was the most common issue, with 70% being surgeon-related. Nonsurgeon-related complaints involved hospital infrastructure, not offering surgery, seeing advanced practice providers, and clinic inefficiencies. A collaborative effort of the healthcare team is crucial to address systemic issues and enhance care quality.

Given the complexity and unpredictability of medicine, patient complaints commonly arise,1,2 and often stem from issues concerning communication, accessibility, unmet expectations, complications, or administrative concerns, some of which may be beyond the physician's control.3 Patient complaints have also been shown to correlate with surgical complications and malpractice claims.4-8 Understanding and addressing these complaints can foster improvements in care and the overall patient experience.

Spine surgery often involves elective procedures aimed at improving pain and quality of life, yet even the smallest operations carry significant neurological risk. Existing research indicates that neurosurgeons receive a higher volume of patient complaints compared with other medical specialties, with spine surgery cited most frequently.3 In orthopedic surgery, up to 13% of the complaints are attributed to spine surgeons.9 Furthermore, trauma patients have been shown to be at a higher risk of adverse outcomes when treated by consulting neurosurgery and orthopedic surgery services with unprofessional behaviors.10 Despite the high frequency of patient complaints related to spine surgery, there remains a significant gap in understanding the full spectrum of these complaints.11 Moreover, most studies about patient complaints are confined to a single specialty,3,12-14 and none combine orthopedic and neurosurgery spine surgeons, to fully capture the field of spine surgery.

Although the importance of addressing patient complaints is well recognized, the existing literature provides nonspecific, vague, and generic conclusions, which are inadequate to drive meaningful changes in clinical practice. Complaints are often binned into broad categories that fail to define their precise nature.11,15 In spine surgery, complaints often arise from factors beyond the attending surgeon's direct control, including hospital infrastructure, administrative challenges, or natural course of recovery. In addition, several opportunities exist for spine surgeons to improve their communication skills when delivering suboptimal news.

Given the elective yet high-risk nature of spine surgery, it was evident that this area was ripe for future study. In this multi-institution study of unsolicited patient complaints (UPCs) related to spine surgery, we sought to: (1) compare mean complaints of spine surgeons vs nonspine surgeons, and nonsurgeon physicians; (2) report the reason for each complaint; and (3) determine if the complaint was directly related to the surgeon-patient interactions or not.

METHODS

Study Design

A multi-institutional, cross-sectional study was conducted, analyzing UPCs regarding neurosurgery and orthopedic spine surgery teams from January 01, 2014 to December 31, 2017. Data were obtained from the institutional Center for Patient and Professional Advocacy (CPPA), which maintains a national database of patient complaints from over 200 hospitals. Through its Patient Advocacy Reporting System (PARS), CPPA partners with hospitals and medical groups to identify and intervene with physicians with elevated numbers of UPCs. Physicians are included in the PARS database through institutional participation agreements with the CPPA, whereby attending physicians with active credentials at participating hospitals are eligible for inclusion.

Study Population

Neurosurgeons and orthopedic surgeons enrolled in the PARS database with active credentials during the study period were manually reviewed to identify spine surgeons. Surgeons were categorized as spine surgeons based on institutional credentialing and documented clinical practice focus within the PARS database. The designation as a spine surgeon did not require an exclusive spine-only practice, and surgeons with mixed clinical practices were included if spine surgery constituted a substantial component of their clinical practice.

A 4-year audit period was used to examine the UPCs received. Patients aged younger than 18 years or older than 89 years were excluded. Complaints against resident/fellow physicians were also excluded.

Categorization of Complaints

Based on the PARS database, complaints are classified into 6 categories: (1) care and treatment, (2) communication, (3) humanistic concern for the patient, (4) accessibility, (5) environment, and (6) billing issues.3,16 Although these broad categories are helpful, there may be additional detail in patient complaints that could suggest targeted areas for improvement. Thus, this initial categorization was used as a starting point for more spine surgery-specific categories.

Spine-Specific Complaint Categorization

The 3 attending neurosurgery and orthopedic spine surgeons, at the primary research site, convened to discuss the categorization of UPCs. After reviewing the initial 50 complaints, several subcategories were chosen that were most relevant to a spine surgeon's practice. Each investigator reviewed the complaint narratives and discussed emerging patterns to develop a set of spine-specific categories that expanded on the broader PARS classification framework. Disagreements in categorization were resolved through discussion and consensus review of the complaint narratives. Complaints as Surgeon-Related or Not

Within the aforementioned categories, each complaint was determined to be surgeon-related or not. Surgeon-related was a priori defined as a complaint that was directly a result of a surgeon's individual actions, such as professionalism or poor communication. Examples of nonsurgeon-related complaints were hospital infrastructure problems, professionalism of hospital personnel, or clinic/hospital inefficiencies out of the surgeon's control.

Statistical Analyses and Ethical Considerations

Descriptive statistics were used to report frequencies and proportions of complaints across categories and subcategories. The frequencies were further analyzed as surgeon-related and nonsurgeon-related. The patient and physician data were deidentified before study initiation, by a third party not involved in the conduct of the study. The Institutional Review Board (Vanderbilt University Medical Center (IRB#221114)) approved the study as nonhuman subjects research and waived the consent requirement.

RESULTS

Physician Demographics

Of 42 380 physicians, 525 were spine surgeons and 8038 were nonspine surgeons. Among spine surgeons, approximately 30% were neurosurgeons whereas 70% were orthopedic surgeons. Most spine surgeons were male (484/92.2%) and practiced in an academic medical center (347/66.1%). A higher proportion of spine surgeons attended medical school (470/89.5%) and completed residency training in the United States (500/95.2%).

Among the study cohort, 69% of physicians practiced in academic settings. The geographic distribution of physicians included 16% from Eastern United States, 31% from Midwest, 15% from the South, and 39% from Western United States, reflecting broad national representation. Physicians practicing in regional/community settings had higher rates of at least one UPC, as compared with those practicing in academic settings (61.0% vs 48.4%), whereas formal PARS interventions were uncommon in both groups (3.5% vs 2.1%) (Table 1).

Patient Complaints

During the study period, the 525 spine surgeons in the cohort were collectively associated with 3780 UPCs. Spine surgeons received a mean of 7.2 ± 8.3 complaints, significantly higher than nonspine surgeons (5.6 ± 6.3) and nonsurgeon physicians (3.3 ± 5.4, P < .001) (Figure 2). Spine surgeons were also more likely to receive multiple patient complaints (P < .001, Figure 3). Although the graph shows a higher rate of complaints for spine surgeons, it is important to note that these data are not controlled for patient volumes. Spine surgeons see a larger number of patients in the clinic and are therefore at risk of receiving higher number of complaints as compared with their surgical and nonsurgical colleagues. As patient-volume denominators were not available in the PARS database, these comparisons should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, identifying the common themes underlying these complaints remains important for informing quality improvement efforts and improving patient care.

To enable detailed qualitative analysis of complaint content, complaints were further evaluated based on narrative detail. A total of 1210 complaints originating from spine surgeons in the top 2 deciles for complaint volume were reviewed for qualitative relevance. From these, 149 complaints contained sufficient narrative detail and were selected to represent a distribution across all 6 complaint domains for in-depth thematic evaluation. Of these 149 complaints, 85 (57.0%) were confined to a single domain, whereas 64 (43.0%) spanned multiple domains. The reasons for complaints in descending order were as follows: communication (63.8%), professionalism (25.5%), postoperative complications (24.2%), hospital infrastructure (14.1%), not offered surgery (12.1%), and clinic issues (12.1%) (Table 2). Of all complaints, 86 (57.7%) were surgeon-related, and 86 (57.7%) occurred before surgery. Relevant examples for each complaint category have been provided in Table 3.

Examples of Patient Complaints for Each Category

Communication

An issue with communication was reported by 95 patients (63.8%). A lack of communication was cited by 60 of 95 patients (63.2%) and poor manner of communication by 35 patients (36.8%). Inadequacy in postoperative communication was cited in 20 complaints (21.1%) and lack of empathy in 15 (15.8%). The attending surgeons accounted for 67 communication issues (70.5%), with the remaining 28 (29.5%) being attributed to the care team.

Professionalism

Unprofessional behavior was reported by 38 patients (25.5%) with the attending surgeon (86.8%) more commonly being held responsible as compared with the care team (13.2%).

Postoperative Complications

A total of 36 patients (24.2%) complained about postoperative complications. Of these, 6 patients (16.7%) had irreversible complications whereas remaining 30 (83.3%) had a reversible complication.

Hospital Infrastructure

A lack of or inadequacy of hospital infrastructure leading to delay in patient care was reported in 21 complaints (14.1%). Cost and insurance-related issues were reported by 6 patients (4.0%).

People Not Offered Surgery

Surgery was not offered or denied to 18 patients (12.1%). The reasons for denial were as follows: 6 (33.3%) inadequate infrastructure or personnel, 5 (27.8%) risks outweighing the benefits of surgery, 2 (11.1%) patient-related health reasons, and 1 (5.6%) perceived discrimination.

Clinic Issues

An issue related to the clinic was reported by 18 patients (12.1%), with the most common being dissatisfaction due to not being seen by the attending surgeon (8/18, 44.4%) and long waiting time for the appointment or at the clinic (9/18, 50.0%). In addition, 2 patients (11.1%) were denied an appointment without prior imaging.

DISCUSSION

In a large series of UPCs among orthopedic and neurosurgery spine surgeons, spine surgeons received significantly more complaints than their surgery counterparts. Moreover, 42% of complaints were related to issues outside the surgeon's direct interactions, and 58% occurred before any surgical intervention. Communication was the most frequently cited issue, with 64% of patients citing a lack/poor manner of communication. Professionalism was reported by 26% of patients, mostly attributed to attending surgeons. Postoperative complications comprised 24% of complaints, and most were reversible. Some patients experienced delays in care because of inadequate hospital infrastructure (14%). In addition, 12% of complaints were because of patients not being offered surgery, often due to infrastructure limitations or high surgical risks. Clinic-related issues were reported by 12% patients, mainly due to not seeing the attending surgeon and long wait times.

A significant proportion (42%) of complaints stemmed from issues beyond the surgeon's direct control, highlighting the multifaceted and complex nature of patient dissatisfaction in spine care. As patient complaints are often initially attributed to the attending surgeon, surgeon attribution in this study relied on detailed qualitative adjudication of individual complaint narratives rather than default administrative assignment, allowing differentiation between complaints attributable to surgeon actions and those arising from system-level or care-team–related factors. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have identified factors outside the surgical procedure as key contributors to patient complaints.11,15 Patient experiences are heavily influenced by the hospital infrastructure, personnel, and administrative processes.11 Accordingly, improving patient satisfaction requires a collaborative effort among surgeons, hospital staff, and administrators to address systemic and infrastructural issues, thereby enhancing care quality and reducing patient complaints.

Not being offered surgery led to 12% of patient complaints, with cited reasons including inadequate facilities, risks outweighing benefits, and even perceived discrimination. Denial of surgery can lead to patient frustration and confusion, underscoring the need for clear communication and transparency in surgical decision making. A well-recognized challenge in spine practice is that most patients presenting with back pain do not need surgery, yet this can be very challenging to explain to patients,17 particularly given the emotional and cognitive burden associated with chronic pain. During these discussions, professionalism and clear, empathetic communication are essential to prevent perceptions of bias or discrimination.10 Although spine surgeons are likely to believe they are acting in the patient's best interests, it is possible that room for improvement exists in how this nuanced message is delivered.

Postoperative complications were reported in only 24%, lower than expected given the high-risk nature of spine surgery.3,11 While complaints reported permanent deficits (4%), the low rates highlight the importance of preoperative counseling and risk stratification, ensuring the patients understand the risks and have reasonable expectations. Previous studies have reported higher rates of complaints that reported postoperative complications with inadequate communication cited as a common complaint.11,18 Furthermore, providing adequate postoperative care and support, especially to patients with complications can reduce complaints.11

Lack of empathy by the attending surgeon was reported in 10% of communication-related complaints. Empathy is a critical aspect of patient care but is often overlooked. A lack of empathy while interacting with patients can have a significantly adverse impact on patient satisfaction and trust.18 Effective communication skills with appropriate display of empathy can greatly improve patient outcomes and foster a strong patient-physician relationship. Furthermore, studies have shown that empathetic communication leads to greater patient satisfaction and decreases the likelihood of malpractice claims.19,20

This study has several limitations. To enable in-depth qualitative analysis, we purposefully focused on surgeons with the highest complaint burden, reviewing 1210 UPCs originating from spine surgeons in the top 2 deciles of complaint volumes, of which 149 complaint excerpts with sufficient narrative detail were sampled to represent 6 predefined domains. As the qualitative sample was restricted to surgeons with higher complaint volumes and complaints containing sufficient narrative detail, the analyzed complaints were not randomly selected and may not fully represent the distribution of complaint types across all spine surgeons. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than as population-level estimates of complaint prevalence and are intended to inform quality-improvement efforts. The retrospective, complaint-based design introduces reporting and attribution bias, and the absence of linked clinical outcomes precludes causal inference. In addition, UPCs are inherently subjective and reflect patient perceptions rather than objective assessments of care. Although structured narrative adjudication was used to improve interpretability and distinguish surgeon-attributable from system-level issues, these data should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating, intended to inform quality-improvement initiatives. Complaints about residents/fellows were excluded, which may underestimate their role in academic care centers. Patients with poor outcomes are more likely to register complaints to their hospital, leading to a potential bias in the data. The study was further limited to institutions participating in the PARS database, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. In addition, there exists a difference between institutions in ways patient complaints are collected and reported, which can potentially lead to bias. Furthermore, institution-specific hospital characteristics were not available, precluding stratification by individual hospital size, academic designation at the institutional level, or listing of participating hospitals. Although our data show that spine surgeons receive a significantly larger number of patient complaints, it is important to note that these comparisons are based on absolute complaint counts and are not controlled for patient volumes. The database does not capture denominators such as the number of outpatient clinic visits or operative cases, precluding calculation of volume-adjusted complaint rates. Differences in patient volumes across specialties may therefore partially contribute to the observed differences in complaint frequency. Future studies should control the analysis for patient volumes, given the differences in the number of patients seen in the clinic by different specialists. Finally, although we classified complaints as related to hospital infrastructure or administrative issues, categorized as nonsurgeon-related, might indirectly affect perceptions of surgical care. These findings should be interpreted as descriptive indicators of where patient dissatisfaction most commonly arises in spine care rather than as measures of surgical quality or technical performance. Notably, most complaints occurred preoperatively, and many were unrelated to direct surgeon actions, emphasizing the importance of system-level and team-based interventions. Potential quality-improvement initiatives include standardized previsit expectation setting, clearer communication when surgery is not recommended, structured postoperative follow-up communication, and clinic workflow optimization to address access and wait times.

CONCLUSION

In a study of UPCs in spine surgery, spine surgeons received significantly more complaints than their neurosurgery and orthopedic surgery nonspine colleagues. Moreover, 42% of complaints were unrelated to the surgeon's interactions with the patient, and most complaints occurred before any surgery was performed. Communication was the most common complaint category. A collaborative effort of surgeons, hospital staff, and administrators should be undertaken to address the infrastructure and systemic issues to improve care and reduce patient complaints.
