Radiotherapy as a potential risk factor for delayed anastomotic leakage after rectal surgery: a meta-analysis
Authors: Jau-Jie You, Abe Fingerhut, Yi-Chang Chen, William Tzu-Liang Chen, Ming-Yin Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadiotherapy is a well-recognized risk factor of acute leakage after colorectal surgery. However, its relationship with delayed leakage remains unclear.The aim of this systemic review and meta-analysis was to determine its association with delayed anastomotic leakage (DAL) after rectal surgery.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted across seven international databases (PubMed, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, ClinicalKeys, EBSCOhost-CINAHL, Web of Science, and Google Scholar), five Chinese/Taiwanese databases, the ProQuest Dissertations, and three Grey literature databases. All studies that reported DAL where radiotherapy and nonradiotherapy study groups were included.The random-effect model was used to combine data. Data were presented as odds-ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The main outcome measured was DAL.ResultsSix comparative series involving a total of 4991 patients were analyzed. DAL was identified in 323 cases, occurring between 22 and 2059 days post-operation. The results revealed a statistically significant increase in the risk of DAL following radiotherapy (OR = 5.208; 95% CI = 3.241-8.371; p < 0.001). Heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (Cochran Q = 12.769 (p=0.026), I2=60.844%, Tau2 = 0.188, predictive interval (PI) = 5.208, 95% PI = 1.313-20.665).ConclusionsRadiotherapy is a potential risk factor for DAL after anterior resection and low anterior resection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03247-3.Keywords: Radiotherapy, Anterior resection, Low anterior resection, Delayed leakage

Introduction

Advances in surgical techniques, neoadjuvant radiotherapy, and an increased tendency to use shorter distal resection margins have contributed to higher rates of sphincter preservation in rectal surgery [1]. While these advancements have bolstered the potential for cure, they have also increased the likelihood of anastomotic leakage [2].

Although most anastomotic leaks are diagnosed during the initial postoperative period, delayed anastomotic leakage (DAL) has been reported after discharge from hospital, during examination before the closure of a protective stoma, or even after its closure, in fact up to more than 20 years post-operation [3–6]. While the overall anastomotic leakage rate varies greatly depending on the definition, the time of onset, the associated risk factors, the surgical technique and patient status, the reported population-based prevalences of symptomatic anastomotic leakage for colorectal and coloanal anastomoses at least one year after surgery range from 20.1% to 24.2% [7, 8].

Delayed anastomotic leakage typically presents as extravasation observed during a contrast enema, pelvic abscess as seen on computer tomography (CT), chronic sinus or entero-vaginal fistula, and entero-cutaneous fistula, among others, after what is defined as the immediate post-operative period. While most patients are generally stable (due to localized inflammation), there are still some cases that present with severe sepsis, classified-grade C by the International Study Group of Rectal Cancer (ISREC) [9, 10] or grade IIIb/IV by the Clavien-Dindo classification [4, 11–18]. In general, DAL is slower to develop, multifactorial, and related to healing deficiencies. Proposed risk factors for DAL include low tumor location or lower anastomosis location, chemoradiotherapy, high comorbidity, and the presence of a diverting stoma [2, 4, 7, 12, 13, 15, 16, 18–24].

The aim of this study is to perform a meta-analysis focusing on radiotherapy as a potential risk factor for DAL.

Search strategy and study selection

In August 2025, we searched across seven international databases (PubMed, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, ClinicalKeys, EBSCOhost-CINAHL, Web of Science, Google Scholar) and five databases from China/Taiwan (China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) - KNS55, National Taiwan Library and Information System Dissertation and Thesis Knowledge Value-added System (NTLTD), Airiti Library, Wanfang Data, and the Journal of the Colorectal Surgical Society of the Republic of China (crs.org.tw)). We utilized the following MeSH terms (anastomotic leakage, radiotherapy, low anterior resection) and EMTREE terms (rectum, surgery, low anterior resection, chemoradiotherapy, radiotherapy, anastomosis leakage, delayed leakage, delayed leak, late leakage, late leak) and their combinations. We imposed no language restrictions and applied no filters. The Chinese equivalents of the search terms were used in Google Scholar and the five China/Taiwan databases. The Google Scholar search was confined to the first 100 results for screening with each combination of search terms. The ProQuest Dissertations database was also searched as a source for doctoral dissertations and master’s theses. Additionally, three grey literature databases were reviewed: the Grey Literature Report, OpenGrey, and ScienceDaily.com.

We further hand-searched the references of the retrieved full-text articles to include additional relevant publications.

Selected articles were reviewed and assessed for methodological quality using a the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [25]. This review was registered in the PROSPERO registry [26] under ID: CRD42024529331.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Two authors (JJ, MA) independently reviewed all abstracts. After review, we retrieved full-text copies of studies that potentially met the inclusion criteria. If both authors concurred that a study did not meet the eligibility criteria, we excluded it. In cases of disagreement, we resolved the conflict through discussion and consensus, or by consulting a third reviewer (WM or AF). Inclusion criteria included: (1) patients with rectal lesions undergoing low anterior resection or total mesorectal excision with colorectal or coloanal anastomosis; (2) treatment with or without radiotherapy; (3) presence of DAL. Studies not meeting these criteria were excluded.

Anastomotic leakage was defined according to the ISREC and modified by Chen and Fingerhut [9, 10]. We included articles which defined DAL as leaks occurring after at least 14 days post-operation [19]. We separated patients into preoperative radiotherapy and non-radiotherapy groups.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Each author independently extracted data from the studies and then compared their findings, resolving any disagreements through discussion. Two authors (JJ, MS) assessed the methodological quality of all included studies using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [25]. For studies that did not provide details on DAL, we contacted the authors to obtain the missing data.

Data synthesis and analysis

We conducted this meta-analysis using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software, version 4.0 (CMA 4.0). A random-effect model was employed to estimate the overall pooled incidence of DAL among patients in either radiotherapy or non-radiotherapy groups. We calculated the odds ratios (OR) and differences in means with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for dichotomous and continuous variables. If the literature only provided a median with range or interquartile range, we calculated the mean with standard deviation using the method described by Luo et al. [27]. A two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. We calculated the Cochrane Chi² test (Q-test) [28, 29], the I2 [30, 31], the Tau² [32] and 95% predictive intervals (PI) for the assessment of heterogeneity [30, 33]. Sensitivity testing was conducted with the one study removed method [33]. To assess for publication bias, we examined the funnel plot and conducted Egger’s regression tests [34]. Meta-regression was conducted with the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method [35, 36].This review adheres to the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [37] and is reported in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [38].

Results

A total of 2,842 potentially eligible studies were identified through the initial search. From the 243 records identified in PubMed, 13 articles were deemed potentially relevant after title and abstract screening. A similar process was applied to other databases Moreover, we contacted nine authors to clarify unclear data. Of these, six responded, but only two were able to provide clear and complete data.

After compiling 22 articles from 18 resources and removing 16 duplicates, we reviewed the cited references in them and retracted another 11 candidate studies. This process led to the enrollment of 34 articles. From these 34 full-text articles, we eliminated: 2 posters, 4 case series, 9 studies focusing on early leakage, 1 on vaginal fistula, 2 on acute leakage repair, 3 that combined both colonic and rectal leakage cases without distinction of rectal cases, 2 where the surgical method employed was not clear, 1 without radiotherapy, and 4 with different design from the others due to the rarity of non-radiotherapy patients [8, 14–16]. Finally, a total of six articles [2, 8, 17, 18, 22, 23] were retained for this meta-analysis. (Fig. 1, Appendix 1) A comprehensive description of each study is provided in Table 1.

The study sample sizes ranged from 141 to 1,903, covering a total of 4,991 patients, including 2,057 radiotherapy patients. DAL was identified in 323 cases. DAL occurred 22 to 2,059 days postoperatively, with a prevalence ranging from 1.8% to 10.1%. The prevalence was higher in patients having undertone radiotherapy, ranging from 8% to 23.8%. Although we could not correct for gender and age due to data limitations, there appeared to be a trend towards higher prevalence in men and younger patients (Table 1).

The studies exhibited moderate to severe heterogeneity according to the I2 statistic (I2 = 60.844%), favoring an association between radiotherapy and delayed leakage (Fig. 2) (OR = 5.208; 95% CI = 3.241–8.371; p < 0.001). The Cochran Q = 12.769 (degree of freedom, df = 5) was statistically significant (p = 0.026 meaning we reject the null hypothesis that the true treatment effect is the same across studies and variations simply by chance; the Tau2 = 0.188. Sensitivity testing is listed as Fig. 3. The prediction interval (PI) ranged from 1.313 to 20.665, much (approximately three-fold) larger than the CI (3.241–8.371), attesting to the heterogeneity. However, neither the PI nor the CI crossed the line of null hypothesis (Figs. 2 and 3), reinforcing the value of this meta-analysis.

Discussion

Our meta-analysis found that radiotherapy may be a risk factor for DAL. Nonetheless, substantial heterogeneity was observed among the studies (Cochran Q = 12.769, I2 = 60.844%, Tau2 = 0.188) and the width of the PI (1.313–20.665) was almost three-fold that of the 95% CI (3.241–8.371) which means that no formal conclusion can be made and further analyses are required.

Although the I squared statistic is the most widely used today [31], there is an on-going debate on the use of this metric to assess heterogeneity in meta-analyses. Even the author of the I2 statistic admits today that PI are more appropriate and that the I2 statistic should not be used anymore for that purpose [30, 39]. This is why we used the PI as an indicator. (Figures 2 and 3) and the fact that both the CI and PI were to the right of the line of no effect provides some strength to our overall result even though there were only five, retrospective cohort studies available for analysis.

The most likely reasons for heterogeneity are different radiotherapy modalities (long course, short course), different or absence of chemotherapy regimens, variable interval time between radiation to operation, type of surgery (AR, LAR, SSS, open, minimal invasive, robotic), surgical anastomosis method (handsewn, stapled, end-to-end, side-to-end), with or without protective stoma, stoma closure times and follow-up protocols (examination interval and technique). Sensitivity analysis and logistic regression revealed that that DAL increased over diagnostic time, but not statistically significantly (p = 0.4811) but as there were only six studies in this meta-analysis, no formal conclusions can be made [40].

However, the central debate surrounding DAL revolves around the absence of a definitive consensus regarding the precise time cutoff for its definition [19]. In general, DAL is often defined as occurring beyond 30 days post-surgery. However, some authors have employed alternative criteria, including events that occur after discharge, beyond 3 weeks, or even exceeding 60 days as their chosen cutoff points [19, 41–43]. Although the exact cut-off is difficult to define with precision, we agree with Škrabec et al. [19] that AL occurring before and after 14 days post-operative define two different entities: (a) the former occurs in relation to technical factors whereas (b) the latter is related to impaired healing. Further confusion exists as to whether the definition is based on the time of actual breakdown or the diagnosis.

Another confounding factor that is not clearly outlined in the literature is the influence of a defunctioning stoma at the time of the initial surgery: it is theoretically possible that a stoma may obscure minor acute leakages, thereby delaying the diagnosis. While the interaction of stoma on the AL rate is well studied for acute leakage [44, 45], the effectiveness of a defunctioning stoma in reducing DAL is controversial : two studies stated that stomas had a protective effect [18, 23] while others [8, 14–16] did not. From the population-based study by Rutegård, partial mesorectal excision for upper rectal tumors with a stoma is associated with higher DAL (7.5%). After adjustment, there was no statistically significant difference in DAL between patients with or without a stoma (HR 1.69; 95%CI, 0.59–4.85) [8]. The underlying mechanism is unclear. Surgeons may decide to perform a stoma because they judge the patient at a higher risk of leakage, making the stoma appear to be associated with DAL. Since these reports primarily focused on the effects of radiotherapy, we are unable to obtain detailed information on intra-operative factors (such as tissue perfusion and anastomotic tension) or post-operative recovery characteristics (such as nutrition and Enhanced Recovery After Surgery protocols). We can only observe the phenomenon without identifying the contributing cofactors.

As many as 39% of patients with DAL may be asymptomatic, in contrast to just 1% in cases of acute leakage [18]. Arumainayagam et al. also noted that 9% of asymptomatic leaks could be identified using routine contrast enema imaging studies before the closure of the defunctioning stoma [3]. This underscores the value of routine imaging surveys before proceeding with the closure surgery of defunctioning stomas.In agreement with our meta-regression analysis, DAL increased over diagnostic time, but not statistically significantly (p = 0.4811): a precise diagnostic time frame is therefore not feasible and AL or its consequences can be diagnosed up to 20 years after rectal surgery [46].

Surgical intervention accounted for approximately 30.8% to 53% of cases [13, 17, 18, 23]. Once DAL is identified, adopting an aggressive surgical or multimodal treatment approach as well as stoma reversal can result in improvements in 10-year survival rates and functional outcomes [13]. Ponholzer noted that aggressive treatment may relieve pain symptoms (relative risk (RR) = 5.25; p = 0.031), improve bowel continuity (RR = 3; p = 0.026), and lead to longer mean survival in the stoma reversal group (98.4 months vs. 56.7 months; p = 0.089) [13]. However, some patients still remain a permanent stoma (39.6%, 48.7%) [13, 23].

In addition to the effects of RT, what is the impact of chemotherapy? Qin et al. observed that chemotherapy has a lesser impact on acute leakage (p = 0.02) and on delayed stenosis (p < 0.001) [47] to RT or CCRT. While there are case reports of Bevacizumab associated with DAL [48, 49], other chemotherapy regimens (5FU, FOLFOX) have not shown any statistically significant effects on leakage or stenosis, whether in multivariable or univariate analysis [47]. In our analysis, we could not confirm these findings (p = 0.1796).

The time interval between RT/CCRT and OP has been a subject of academic debate. Performing elective rectal surgery within 4 days following preoperative short-course RT has been reported to be associated with an increased risk of anastomotic leakage [50] while a longer interval of more than 8 weeks after CCRT before rectal surgery did not show an increase in the risk of acute anastomotic leakage [51, 52]. Nonetheless, there are no available data specifically addressing the impact of the time interval between RT and OP on DAL.

Our study presents several limitations: (a) all included studies were cohort studies, and as such, no causality can be implied; (b) the individual sample sizes in each study was small, (c) only six studies were included, confounding the evaluation of heterogeneity [45].Last, it would have been of interest to further evaluate other related outcomes in patients who developed DAL after radiation (healing status related to DAL, oncologic outcomes, short- and long-term prognosis, and other complications). However, not all the articles retrieved for our main endpoint “DAL related to radiation therapy” contained enough details for any meaningful analysis on these other interesting outcome measures, and therefore it was not reasonable to analyze the available data within meta-analysis.

Because of these limitations, no formal conclusions can be made to support radiotherapy is a risk factor for the development of DAL, in spite of the apparent higher rate of late AL associated with RT. There is a need for further studies of risk factors for both early and late AL. Although this was not the goal of our study, our overall recommendation is that physicians should remain vigilant for symptoms and signs indicative of leakage, as it can have considerable consequences for patients. Therefore, timely diagnosis and treatment of symptomatic cases is of utmost importance.
