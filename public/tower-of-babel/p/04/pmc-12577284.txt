Tuberculosis patients’ delay in healthcare access and its associated factors in Shandong Province, China
Authors: Yuqi Duan, Minghao Sun, Chuanlong cheng, Shengnan Yu, Sihao Song, Jun Cheng, Xiujun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesDelays in tuberculosis (TB) patients are a major obstacle to TB control, patients’ delays during diagnosis and treatment increase the potential of TB transmission and lead to an increased disease burden. The aim of this study was to explore the factors that influence patients’ delay at the ecological level as well as at the individual level.MethodsThe study utilized tuberculosis patients’ data identified in Shandong Province, China between 2016 and 2020 from China’s National Disease Reporting Information System, including demographic, clinical, and health service information. Ecological factors such as GDP and medical institution density came from the Shandong Statistical Yearbook, while age structure and gender ratio data were sourced from WorldPop. Multiple methods were employed in this study to investigate the determinants of patients’ delay in tuberculosis diagnosis. Geographically weighted regression (GWR), spatial Durbin model (SDM), spatial lag model (SLM), and spatial error model (SEM) were utilized to analyze the ecological factors influencing patients’ delay at the county and district levels. Additionally, logistic regression and variable importance measures (VIM) were employed to identify individual-level factors associated with patients’ delay.ResultsA total of 134,975 tuberculosis patients were included in the study. Of these patients, 67.9% experienced patients’ delay with a median delay time of 29 days (IQR:10–60). Ecological-level patients’ delay rates were influenced by the age group 60 + and the number of healthcare facilities. Patient delay was also influenced by individual factors such as gender, age, occupation, sputum smear results, census registration, patient source, classification of treatment, and type of tuberculosis. Among them, workers (OR = 0.821), students (OR = 0.747), retired (OR = 0.871) and the unemployed (OR = 1.033) differed from farmers. Direct clinic visits (OR = 5.230), follow-up identified (OR = 6.108), and referrals (OR = 3.945) had significantly higher odds than proactive inspection. Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the world’s most significant infectious diseases, posing a serious burden to public health and safety. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) 2022 report, China ranks third in the estimated number of TB cases among the 30 high TB burden countries, behind India and Indonesia, accounting for 7.4% of the global total [1]. China is expected to have 780,000 TB cases in 2021, with an estimated incidence rate of 55 cases per 100,0001. The WHO’s End TB Strategy aims to reduce the incidence of TB by 90% and the number of deaths by 95% by 2035 [2]. As one of the high tuberculosis burden countries, China is facing a daunting task of preventing and controlling the disease [3].

Delays in the diagnosis and treatment of TB patients are a major obstacle to TB control, and early detection and timely treatment of TB patients are crucial for effective TB control, as highlighted in the WHO’s End TB strategy [4]. The patient delay time was defined as the time interval from the date of first symptom onset to the date of first consultation, and the patient delay was defined as a patient delay of more than 14 days [5]. This definition is consistent with established metrics used in TB research, allowing for valid comparisons with previous studies. Patients’ delay during diagnosis and treatment increase the likelihood of TB transmission, leading to an increased disease burden [6, 7]. And patients’ delay can also lead to poorer outcomes and higher mortality rates, as the longer the period of non-treatment, the higher the risk of exacerbations and death [8, 9].

Current research shows that both individual-level factors and ecological-level factors can affect delay in TB patients. TB patient delay at the individual level may be influenced by gender [10], occupation [11], presence of underlying diseases [12], socioeconomic status [13], and education level [14]. Patients’ delays at the ecological level may be attributable to factors such as demographics, socioeconomics, and healthcare capacity [15].

While numerous studies have investigated TB patients’ delay, the majority focus exclusively on either individual or ecological factors, rather than integrating both. Actual tuberculosis prevention and intervention is cascade by cascade and is conducted through a multi-tiered healthcare system [16, 17]. Therefore, a multi-level research framework was proposed in this paper. Therefore, a multi-level analytical framework is proposed in this study. The first stage, conducted at the ecological level, was designed to understand the spatial distribution of patients’ delay rates and to explore area-level factors associated with high delay rates. The second stage, carried out at the individual level, aimed to examine individual risk factors for patients’ delay. Our research fills this gap by implementing an integrated, two-level (individual and ecological) analytical framework combined with spatial modeling to simultaneously identify who is at risk and where and why delays cluster. The findings provide actionable insights for crafting targeted interventions, directly contributing to Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.3 to end the TB epidemic by 2030. The aim of this study was to analyze the risk factors of TB patients’ delay at different levels. These are essential for formulating healthcare and patient-level interventions and proposing local prevention and control strategies. This approach is more in line with the current prevention and control situation and can provide a more comprehensive policy basis and recommendations for disease control departments at all levels.

Materials and methods

Study area

Shandong Province (Shandong) is a coastal province in eastern China with a land area of 155,800 square kilometers. It is the second most populous province in China. In 2021, the permanent resident population of Shandong Province was 101.700 million, an increase of 172,400 from the previous year. The situation of tuberculosis prevention and control remains critical due to its large population base.

Data source

Data for this study were obtained from the daily reported cases of tuberculosis in Shandong Province from 2016 to 2020 in the National Disease Reporting Information System. The data contained demographic data of TB patients such as age, gender, occupation, patient source, and nationality; clinical characteristics such as sputum smear results, classification of treatment (initial treatment or retreatment), treatment outcome; health service-related Information such as date of TB symptoms onset, date of first visit and date of being reported. The patients’ delay rates for each district and county were also calculated for spatial and ecological level analysis. The reporting process for tuberculosis within the National Disease Reporting Information System begins with the initial diagnosis by a healthcare provider, who completes the Infectious Disease Report Card immediately upon identifying a suspected or confirmed case. This is followed by review and online direct reporting by the medical institution’s epidemic response manager. The report is then promptly audited and validated by the county- or district-level Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), with feedback provided as necessary. Finally, the CDC coordinates with primary healthcare institutions to conduct epidemiological investigations, supervise medication adherence, and perform follow-up management. The entire process emphasizes the principle of first-contact responsibility, legally mandated reporting, and multi-sectoral collaboration.

Data on GDP, disposable income, number of medical institutions and population density in the ecological factors were obtained from the Statistical Yearbook of Shandong Province (city-level data are used instead because the number of medical institutions at the district and county level was not available). Considering that municipal-level data reflects a unified policy planning framework and offers greater standardization and comparability, its use as a substitute is a feasible option under the current circumstances. Gender ratio and the proportion of the population over 60 years old were obtained from the Worldpop project (https://www.worldpop.org/) China Population Age Structure Dataset.

Stage 1: ecological level analysis methods

A global spatial autocorrelation analysis was performed by mapping patients’ delay rates by district and county to test for spatial aggregation. Subsequently, the Getis-Ord Gi* index was used to determine the level of local spatial autocorrelation and to identify the location of cold or hot spots [18].

The spatial autocorrelation of dependent or independent variables can violate the classical assumption of ordinary least squares (OLS), so their regression coefficient and goodness of fit R2 can produce bias and large variance [19, 20]. Therefore, in ecological level studies, considering the possible spatial dependence caused by spatial adjacency, we used a spatial modeling approach to explore the influencing factors. Geographic weighted regression (GWR) and three common spatial econometric models—spatial lag model (SLM), spatial error model (SEM), and spatial Durbin model (SDM)—were used in this study. The four models—GWR, SLM, SEM, and SDM—represent distinct spatial mechanisms: GWR captures local variation in relationships, SLM models spillover effects in the outcome, SEM corrects for spatially correlated omitted variables, and SDM incorporates spillovers from both outcomes and predictors. The detailed introduction of these four methods can be found in the supplementary document. Since it is unclear which specific spatial process the data conforms to, we cannot directly select an appropriate spatial model. The best model was then selected for analysis based on the highest coefficient of determination (R²) values and the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) to ensure both goodness of fit and model simplicity.

Spatial econometric model

SLM considers an autoregressive model of spatial variables, which is also known as a spatial autoregressive model. SEM considers the spatial correlation of random interference items (error items) and reflects the error process through the spatial covariance of different regions. SDM is a comprehensive extension of the spatial lag model and the spatial error term model. The spatial Durbin model considers not only the spatial autocorrelation of the dependent variable but also the spatial autocorrelation of the independent variable.

Geographic weighted regression

Geographic weighted regression is an extension of linear regression model. Spatial elements are generally heterogeneous, and spatial regression models with a single-parameter structure often fail to capture region-specific characteristics. To show the heterogeneity of spatial regions, a parameter model of spatially variables is introduced, namely the spatial geographic weighting model (GWR) [21]. These models were used to examine the associations between the proportion of delayed tuberculosis patients in each district or county (dependent variable) and key ecological predictors (independent variables).

Stage 2: individual level analysis methods

Logistic regression

The logistic regression model uses the maximum likelihood method to construct the relationship between predictor variables and classification results. It is widely used because of its computational simplicity, easy interpretation of results, and high reliability. In this study, binary logistic regression was used to analyze the influencing factors using the presence or absence of patient delay as the dependent variable. Univariate logistic regression was used to explore the effects of gender, age, nationality, occupation, sputum smear results, census registration, patient source, classification of treatment and type of tuberculosis (Supplementary Material Table S2.). Independent variables with P < 0.05 were included in a multi-variate logistic regression model for further analysis, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Variable importance measures

The random forest variable importance measures (VIM) were used in the study to evaluate the importance of the features used above and to quantify the contribution of each feature to the classification performance of the random forest model. In this study, the Gini index was used as an evaluation metric [22]. Random forest is an ensemble learning method in machine learning, which is a classifier containing multiple decision trees.

The data analysis in this study was conducted using RStudio (version 4.2.2) for statistical modeling and ArcGIS Pro (version 3.0) for spatial data processing and mapping.

Results

Results of ecological level analysis

Between 2016 and 2020, a cohort of 135,185 tuberculosis patients were identified in Shandong Province, China. After exclusion of cases with incomplete information, a total of 134,975 patients were included in the study, among whom 91,595 (67.9%) exhibited patients’ delay, with a median delay time of 29 days (IQR:10–60). The rate of patients’ delay varied considerably across districts, with the Dongchang District of Liaocheng City reporting the lowest delay rate at 19.3%, while the highest rate of 96.2% was observed in Sishui County of Jining City. The spatial distribution of patients’ delay ratios at the district and county levels in Shandong Province was shown in Fig. 1 (a). The global autocorrelation coefficient was 0.429 (P < 0.001), which indicates that there is a positive spatial correlation between the patients’ delay rates in each district and county. This finding supports the use of spatial analysis methods over ordinary least squares (OLS) regression. Figure 1 (b) depicted the results of the hot and cold spots analysis, in which the cold spots were mainly concentrated in Jinan, Liaocheng and Dongying City, while the hot spots were distributed in the south of Qingdao and Fei County, Linyi City.

Results of ecological level analysis

To conduct ecological-level research, the dependent variable used was the proportion of delayed patients in each district and county, while the independent variables were ecological factors. In accordance with the OLS regression results (Supplementary Material Table S1), statistically significant variables were retained, and the two variables, the number of medical institutions and the proportion of population over 60 years old, were included in the subsequent ecological level study. The spatial distribution of these two variables is shown in Supplementary Material Figure S1. Four spatial statistical models were fitted, and the best model was selected as the GWR model based on the R2 and AIC values (Supplementary Material Table S3.).

The results of GWR were shown in Table 1, and the median coefficients of both variables are positive. Figure 3 showed the spatial variation of the estimated values of each parameter of the GWR model. This study found that the estimated coefficients of environmental association factors exhibit spatial variation.

Results of individual level analysis

The results of logistic regression and the number of days delayed by patients in various population were shown in Table 2. The median patients’ delay time with active inspection group was the smallest which was five days; The median patients’ delay time with extrapulmonary tuberculosis was the largest which was 78 days. A significant association of all variables except gender on patients’ delay was found in the univariate logistic regression (P < 0.05). All variables except for gender, were included in the multivariate analysis. Based on epidemiological expertise, established important covariates (e.g., gender) were forced into the model to ensure comprehensive adjustment for potential confounding factors. A significant effect of all variables except nationality was found in the multivariable regression (P < 0.05). The most striking finding was that the patient source and occupation were the strongest determinants of patients’ delay. Compared to farmers, workers (OR = 0.821), students/children (OR = 0.747), retired (OR = 0.871) and the unemployed (OR = 1.033) differed from workers. Direct clinic visits (OR = 5.230), follow-up identified (OR = 6.108), and referrals (OR = 3.945) had significantly higher odds than proactive inspection. This conclusion further supported by the random forest variable importance analysis.

Discussion

This study proposed a two-stage analysis framework to explore the influencing factors of patients’ delay at two levels. At the ecological level, the delayed medical visit rates in each district and county were affected by the number of medical institutions and the proportion of the population over the age of 60, with the coefficient estimates of the influencing factors varying over time and space. At the individual level, patient delay was influenced by a variety of factors: gender, age, occupation, sputum smear results, census registration, patient source, Classification of treatment and type of tuberculosis, among which occupation and patient source were the two most important influencing factors. It suggests that effective interventions must be multi-pronged: at the ecological level, policies should focus on optimizing the distribution and efficiency of healthcare resources rather than merely increasing their number; at the individual level, prioritizing active case-finding among high-risk occupational groups and streamlining referral pathways from clinics could yield substantial reductions in delay.

There was spatial autocorrelation in the delay rate of patients. A spatial analysis model was used in this study to explore the factors influencing the delay rate of ecological patients. The mean coefficient between the number of medical institutions and the patients’ delay rate was − 0.044. This relatively weak association may be partly attributable to data limitations: since medical institution data were only available at the city level rather than the county level, the analysis could not account for intra-city variations in healthcare resources. The lack of granularity likely reduced the accuracy and spatial resolution of the estimated relationship, as finer-scale differences in medical service distribution were not captured. The results showed that the coefficient estimates of environmental influencing factors varied with spatial location, which could partially explain the spatial variation in patients’ delay rates. Previous studies have found that the patients’ delay rates across regions are related to the degree of aging and health service capacity [15], which was consistent with the model results. In addition, we found that the delay rate of patients in areas with a moderate number of health care institutions is less affected by the number of health care institutions. This might be attributed to the fact that in regions with a low number of medical institutions, patients live farther away from the health facilities, resulting in longer patients’ delays [23, 24]. However, areas with a high number of medical institutions also have higher rates of delayed visits. This may be due to regions with a large number of medical institutions coinciding with regions of high population density, where there are usually queues at the medical institutions, which prevents patients from being seen on time [25]. This highlights the importance of not only increasing the number of health institutions but also optimizing their service efficiency and patient flow management. It also suggests that the relationship between medical resource availability and patient behavior is nonlinear and context-dependent, influenced by urban population dynamics. Building upon these findings, our study proposes a multi-tiered policy framework to mitigate TB patients’ delays through spatially targeted interventions. In regions characterized by a low density of medical institutions—where geographical inaccessibility constitutes the primary barrier—policy efforts should prioritize enhancing physical availability through the deployment of mobile clinical units, the establishment of telemedicine hubs in underserved communities, and the introduction of transportation support programs to facilitate referrals. Conversely, in high-density areas where operational inefficiency and congestion contribute significantly to delays, strategies must shift toward optimizing service delivery rather than expanding infrastructure. Such measures may include implementing digital appointment systems, extending operating hours to distribute patient volume, and reinforcing primary healthcare capacity to allow for effective triage and reduce burden on specialized facilities. Collectively, these recommendations emphasize the health policy implication that effective TB control requires precision public health interventions tailored to local health service landscapes—distinguishing between regions suffering from absolute resource shortages and those affected by suboptimal resource utilization—to design context-specific, equitable, and cost-effective strategies.

The proportion of the population aged over 60 was selected as an indicator because the World Health Organization (WHO) defines individuals aged 60 and above as elderly; thus, this variable reflects the proportion of the aging population. The mean coefficient between the proportion of the population over 60 years old and the patients’ delay rate was 0.154. Substantial spatial heterogeneity was observed in this association, with coefficients ranging from − 0.974 to 0.933 across different regions. In contrast to other studies that typically consider higher rates of aging as a risk factor for patients’ delay [15, 26], we found that in Shandong province regions with a larger proportion of the population over 60 years old were less affected by the degree of aging. This counterintuitive finding may be attributed to Shandong’s relatively advanced and comprehensive TB prevention and control system [27]. In this situation, stronger promotion of and adherence to early TB prevention and treatment among the elderly [28]. This might be explained by the relatively advanced public health infrastructure in Shandong Province, where TB prevention and control programs are more comprehensive. Elderly residents may be better informed and more likely to engage with primary health services due to established chronic disease management systems.

This finding challenges the assumption that aging is uniformly associated with delayed care and underscores the need for region-specific strategies. In contrast to other studies, the patients’ delay rate in Shandong Province was not significantly affected by factors such as population density, GDP, gender ratio, and disposable income per capita [15, 29–31]. One possible explanation is that Shandong’s overall achievements in TB control—reportedly ranking third lowest nationally—and its robust public health campaigns may have mitigated the influence of these broader socioeconomic determinants at the population level.

At the individual level, patients’ delay was influenced by many factors, of which patient source and occupation are the most significant. Direct clinic visits (OR = 5.230), follow-up identified (OR = 6.108), and referrals (OR = 3.945) had significantly higher odds than proactive inspection. The results showed a significantly lower risk of delay in patients with proactive screening, which is consistent with other studies [32, 33]. It suggested that active screening and other means for reducing patients’ delays could be actively pursued in high-risk groups of tuberculosis, strengthening the dissemination of the importance of active medical examination and other means [34–37]. This suggests that actively identifying high-risk populations through routine checkups, contact tracing, and community-based outreach could be a critical intervention to reduce delays. It also highlights the importance of TB health education in improving medical-seeking behavior. Compared to farmers, workers (OR = 0.821), students (OR = 0.747), retired (OR = 0.871) and the unemployed (OR = 1.033) differed from workers. The risk of patients’ delay was significantly higher among farmers and unemployed groups of different occupations, which may be related to their lower income and lower education level. Studies have indicated that low income and insufficient awareness of the risks of tuberculosis may be factors affecting the timely treatment of tuberculosis patients [38, 39]. This group may also face barriers such as transportation difficulties, lack of paid sick leave, and limited trust in health institutions, all of which can further delay care-seeking. Therefore, future strategies should incorporate targeted support for occupationally and economically vulnerable populations. This study revealed that the risk of delayed treatment increases with age. This may be attributed to the change in smoking prevalence and other respiratory diseases such as chronic bronchitis that occur with aging [40–42]. This could lead to a tendency to normalize or dismiss new respiratory symptoms as part of aging, thereby reducing their urgency to seek care. Conversely, a negative sputum smear test or the absence of such testing is a protective factor against patients’ delay. This may be due to a higher proportion of asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic patients among the pathogen-negative population [43]. These patients are more likely to be identified through active screening methods such as health checkups or contact tracing, resulting in a relatively shorter time to seek medical treatment. In contrast, pathogen-positive patients tend to exhibit symptoms for a longer duration before seeking medical treatment.

There are still some limitations in this study. Firstly, the data were obtained from the infectious disease surveillance system, which is greatly influenced by the quality of surveillance data reporting. Secondly, the judgment of the timing of first symptom onset is largely affected by the subjective perceptions of patients and the physicians’ first visit approach. Third, although our study incorporated both individual- and ecological-level factors, it was constrained by limited sample size and data precision. Consequently, we were unable to perform more granular analyses—specifically, ecological-level analyses in high-incidence areas and individual-level analyses in regions with severe delays in medical care. This limitation may have reduced our ability to identify context-specific determinants. Lastly, many important and potentially relevant factors were not considered due to limited data sources. Future work should prioritize building larger and richer linked datasets and performing stratified and multilevel analyses that explicitly focus on ecological drivers in high-incidence settings and individual-level determinants where care delays are severe.

Conclusions

This study used a multilevel research model to explore the factors that influence patients’ delay, demonstrates that tuberculosis patients’ delay is influenced by ecological factors such as the proportion of the elderly population and the number of healthcare facilities, as well as individual factors including gender, age, occupation, and sputum smear results, with patient source and occupation identified as the most significant determinants of delay. It helps the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) to identify high-risk areas and high-risk populations which can provide a policy rationale and scientific basis for the tuberculosis prevention and control efforts at multi-level government departments.
