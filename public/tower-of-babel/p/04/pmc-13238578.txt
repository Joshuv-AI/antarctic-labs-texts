Synergistic Effects of Normal‐Range Serum Sodium and Potassium on Mortality: The Modifying Role of Inflammation From a NHANES Cohort Analysis
Authors: Haofeng Zhang, Fudong He, Zhenger Fang, Guangjun Zheng, Biying Zhou, Xia Chen, Mingliang Liu, Guang Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the associations of serum sodium and potassium levels within the normal range with cardiovascular (CV) and all‐cause mortality, and to further explore the potential roles of oxidative stress and inflammatory biomarkers in these associations.MethodsWe analyzed NHANES 1999–2018 data from 32,837 adults with normal baseline serum sodium (135–146 mmol/L) and potassium (3.5–5.0 mmol/L), stratified by tertiles. Weighted Cox models assessed associations with CV and all‐cause mortality, and bootstrap‐based causal mediation analysis assessed nine oxidative stress and inflammatory biomarkers.ResultsThe lowest tertile of serum sodium was associated with an increased risk of all‐cause mortality (HR: 1.17; 95% CI: 1.03–1.32; p = 0.017), while the highest tertile of serum potassium was associated with an increased risk of CV (HR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.00–1.58; p = 0.052) and all‐cause mortality (HR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.02–1.29; p = 0.025). Serum sodium and potassium showed a statistically significant interaction for all‐cause mortality (p = 0.044), with the lowest risk at sodium 138–139 mmol/L and potassium 3.5–3.8 mmol/L (HR: 0.46, 95% CI: 0.31–0.68; p < 0.001). Mediation analysis showed that inflammatory biomarkers accounted for 0.8% to 1.5% of the associations between serum sodium/potassium and CV or all‐cause mortality.ConclusionIn the general population, lower normal‐range serum sodium was associated with higher all‐cause mortality, while higher serum potassium was linked to increased cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality. Inflammatory biomarkers may partially mediate these associations. Our findings provide more evidence that the “safest” sodium and potassium interval needs to be reconsidered.

Introduction

Sodium and potassium are essential electrolytes for maintaining basic physiological functions, especially for the cardiovascular (CV) system [1]. Normal serum sodium and potassium range, defined as the interval that 95% of the reference healthy population fall into, lies between 135 and 146 mmol/L and between 3.5 and 5.0 mmol/L, respectively [2, 3, 4]. These ranges are widely applied in clinical practice under the implicit assumption that values within these intervals carry minimal clinical risk. However, emerging evidence challenges this assumption.

Even mild deviations in electrolyte levels have been implicated in adverse biological processes. Previous studies have suggested that both hyperkalemia and hyponatremia trigger inflammatory reactions [5, 6, 7], with hyponatremia specifically inducing oxidative stress responses [8]. Hyponatremia also may activate the Renin–Angiotensin–Aldosterone system, leading to increased levels of aldosterone, adrenaline, and noradrenaline, which in turn may elevate the mortality rate from CV diseases in hypertensive patients [9]. Two international multicenter studies reported that disturbances in potassium levels were independent predictors of cardiovascular mortality in patients with acute coronary syndrome or heart failure [10, 11]. The Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis in the United States also reported that serum potassium concentration ≥ 5.0 mEq/L was associated with CV and all‐cause mortality in community‐living individuals [12].

Growing data suggest that small variations within “normal” electrolyte ranges may still carry clinical implications. The Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities study in the United States showed that high‐normal serum sodium is associated with accelerated biological aging, chronic diseases, and premature mortality in the general population [13]. Another study based on community‐based elderly in Taiwan, China also reported that low‐normal serum potassium level is associated with higher risks of CV and all‐cause mortality [14]. These findings imply that sodium and potassium may influence health outcomes in a dose‐response manner even within normal clinical limits, and that the current definitions of “normal” may not optimally reflect physiological or prognostic thresholds.

Sodium and potassium work together to regulate critical functions, including blood pressure regulation, nerve and muscle function [15, 16, 17]. Given their physiological interplay, it is plausible that these electrolytes may have combined or interactive effects on CV risk, which cannot be fully captured by examining each ion in isolation. Yet, to date, no study has evaluated the synergistic effects of normal‐range sodium and potassium on CV or all‐cause mortality. Moreover, alterations in inflammation and oxidative stress represent biologically plausible pathways linking electrolyte homeostasis to adverse CV outcomes, even within the conventional normal range. Understanding whether inflammatory or oxidative biomarkers mediate these associations would help clarify the underlying mechanisms and strengthen causal inference.

Therefore, the present study, utilizing data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), was designed based on the causal hypothesis that variations in serum sodium and potassium within normal ranges contribute to CV and all‐cause mortality, with oxidative stress and systemic inflammation playing a mediating role in these associations. To test this hypothesis, we aimed to (1) assess the independent and joint associations of serum sodium and potassium with mortality risk in the general population, and (2) explore whether oxidative stress and inflammatory biomarkers mediate these associations. By addressing these questions, our study seeks to re‐examine long‐standing assumptions about “normal” electrolyte levels and provide new insights into their prognostic significance for CV health.

Methods

Study Participants

Data were obtained from NHANES (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/Default.aspx), which is a nationally representative survey of nutrition and health status conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in the United States. All study protocols were approved by the Research Ethics Review Board of the National Centre for Health Statistics. The procedures adhered to the guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. We linked the baseline data from 10 NHANES cycles (1999–2018) with death certificate records from the National Death Index (NDI) through December 31, 2019, including a total of 116,876 participants. Detailed technical specifications of the matching methodology are available from the NCHS website (corrected link: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data-linkage/mortality-public.htm). All participants were followed until December 31, 2019, with follow‐up time calculated from their respective baseline survey dates to the date of death or the cutoff date (whichever occurred first). Participants who were still alive at the cutoff date were treated as right‐censored. The median follow‐up duration for the entire cohort was 9.98 years. After excluding those lacking serum potassium and serum sodium data, 73,425 individuals remained. Subsequently, after excluding missing information on CV and all‐cause mortality, participants with serum sodium levels below 135 mmol/L or above 146 mmol/L, serum potassium levels below 3.5 mmol/L or above 5.0 mmol/L (according to the American Academy of Family Physician guidelines [18, 19]), and urine albumin‐creatinine ratio (UACR) above 30 mg/g, further excluded participants with missing information on covariates including age, race/ethnicity, sex, education level, marital status, smoking status, the ratio of family income to poverty (PIR), body mass index (BMI), physical activity, total cholesterol levels, history of CV disease, dietary sodium, and dietary potassium. Finally, 32,837 participants were included in the study (Supporting Figure S1).

Measurements of Serum Sodium and Potassium Levels

Serum samples were refrigerated appropriately before transport to the Collaborative Laboratory Services Center in Ottumwa, Iowa for analysis. Sodium and potassium levels were measured using the indirect ion‐selective electrode method. This system measures sodium and potassium levels by detecting the voltage generated through ion exchange. In healthy individuals, the normal range for serum sodium and serum potassium, defined as the interval within which 95% of a reference healthy population falls, is 135–146 mmol/L and 3.5–5.0 mmol/L, respectively [13, 20].

Determination of Mortality Outcomes

According to the 2017 ACC/AHA hypertension guideline recommendations, hypertension is defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 80 mmHg, or taking antihypertensive drugs [21]. For mortality status, we used the NHANES public‐use linked mortality file as of December 31, 2019, which linked the certificate records from the National Death Index (NDI) through a probability matching algorithm. The disease‐specific death was determined using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10), and the NCHS classified heart diseases (054–068).

Oxidative Stress and Inflammation Biomarkers

The study quantified oxidative stress using four established biomarkers: gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT), uric acid (UA), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), and the uric acid‐to‐HDL ratio (UHR). Systemic inflammation was assessed through five hematological indices derived from complete blood counts (CBC): neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (MLR), neutrophil‐monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NMLR), systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII), and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI). Detailed calculation methods are provided in Supporting Table S1.

Covariates

The covariates include sociodemographic factors, dietary factors, and health behaviors and conditions. Sociodemographic factors comprised age, race, sex, education level (less than high school, high school graduate or above), marital status (married, unmarried), and PIR. Dietary factors encompassed the average daily intake of sodium, potassium, and moisture. Health behavior factors included physical activity (sedentary, moderate, vigorous) and smoking status (never smoked, former smoker, current smoker), while health conditions included BMI (kg/m2), medical history, and clinical examination data. Clinical examination‐related data included urine albumin‐creatinine ratio (mg/g) and total cholesterol levels (mmol/L). Data related to CV disease history (defined as coronary heart disease, heart failure, or angina pectoris), hypertension, and diabetes are also included.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables are represented as counts (n) and percentages (%). The weighted Cox proportional hazards model was used to estimate hard ratios and 95% CIs. As recommended in the NHANES survey design documentation, sampling weights were applied to ensure nationally representative estimates. The Taylor series linearization method was used for variance estimation, which is the default and recommended approach in Stata's svy suite of commands. Model 1 was the crude model; Model 2 adjusted for age and sex; Model 3 further adjusted for race/ethnicity, education, marital status, PIR, smoking, BMI, physical activity, total cholesterol, history of CV diseases, hypertension, and diabetes; Model 4 additionally adjusted for urine albumin‐creatinine ratio, dietary sodium, dietary potassium, and moisture. Furthermore, Kaplan‐Meier was used to estimate CV and all‐cause mortality for the tertiles of serum sodium and serum potassium. Additionally, nonlinear correlations (R RCS package) were explored using restricted cubic spline (RCS) COX regression with four knots RCS (5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th) [22]. We conducted stratified analyses across all covariates and explored their potential interaction effects.

We first identified oxidative stress and inflammatory biomarkers that were statistically associated with serum sodium and potassium levels. Next, we screened these biomarkers to select those statistically significantly associated with CV and all‐cause mortality. Finally, we conducted a causal mediation analysis for each selected oxidative stress and inflammatory biomarkers to decompose the total effect. Specifically, the total effect of serum electrolytes on mortality was estimated using a Cox model adjusted for confounders without including the mediator, while the direct effect was estimated from a model that additionally included the mediator. The indirect effect was calculated as the product of the coefficients from the two aforementioned associations. The proportion of the total effect mediated was derived by dividing the indirect effect by the total effect. Furthermore, we evaluated the mediation effects of statistically significant inflammatory markers using the percentage of excess risk mediated (PERM) [23]. We investigated whether oxidative stress and inflammatory biomarkers may influence all‐cause and CV mortality through their effects on CV‐related risk factors, including triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low‐density lipoprotein (LDL), fasting blood glucose (FBG), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), systolic blood pressure (SBP), hypertension, type 2 diabetes (T2D), and obesity: PERM=100×HR(confunder adjust)−HR(confunder and mediator adjust)HR(confunder adjust)−1

We performed several sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of our findings: (1) to address potential reverse causality, we excluded participants who died within the first 2 years of follow‐up. (2) Although the methods for measuring serum sodium and potassium remained consistent, we further accounted for potential inter‐survey variations by stratifying the analysis into three NHANES cycles (1999–2004, 2005–2010, and 2011–2018). (3) Serum sodium‐to‐potassium ratio was categorized into tertiles. (4) Including the participants with a UACR between 30 and 299 mg/g. (5) Excluding the participants with self‐reported chronic kidney disease or heart failure.

All analyses were performed using Stata software version 16 (STATA Corp., TX, USA) and R 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Associations of Serum Sodium and Serum Potassium Within the Normal Range With Risk of CV and All‐Cause Mortality

Over 327,809.75 person‐years of follow‐up, there were 1041 CV mortality and 3495 all‐cause mortality. The cumulative mortality rates for CV and all‐cause over time to tertiles of serum sodium and serum potassium levels are shown in Supporting Figure S2.

In the crude model, the highest tertile of serum sodium and potassium levels was statistically significantly associated with increased risks of CV mortality and all‐cause mortality. Further adjustment for age and sex, the lowest tertile of serum sodium and the highest tertile of potassium were associated with increased all‐cause mortality risk (Na: HR: 1.12; 95% CI: 1.03–1.21; p = 0.009; K: HR = 1.18; 95% CI: 1.08–1.28; p = 0.001). The highest potassium tertile also showed elevated CV mortality risk (HR: 1.20; 95% CI: 1.02–1.41; p = 0.026). Further adjustment for sociodemographic and clinical covariates attenuated but maintained significance for all‐cause mortality (Na: HR: 1.17; 95% CI: 1.03–1.33; p = 0.013; K: HR: 1.13; 95% CI: 1.01–1.26; p = 0.038), while CV mortality associations became marginal (K: HR: 1.23; 95% CI: 0.98–1.54; p = 0.079). Additional adjustments for urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio, lipids, and dietary electrolytes yielded similar trends for all‐cause mortality (Na: HR: 1.17; 95% CI: 1.03–1.32; p = 0.017; K: HR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.02–1.29; p = 0.025) and CV mortality (K: HR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.00–1.58; p = 0.052; Tables 2 and 3).

The Mediation Effects of Inflammation Biomarkers

Serum sodium levels were statistically significantly associated with all oxidative stress and inflammation biomarkers, while serum potassium levels were only statistically significantly associated with MLR and SII, showing borderline statistical significance with NMLR (Supporting Tables S4 and S5). Further screening of oxidative stress and inflammatory biomarkers associated with CV and all‐cause mortality, we found that MLR, SII, and NLR all statistically significantly increased CV mortality. Additionally, GGT and all inflammatory biomarkers (except UHR) were associated with elevated all‐cause mortality (Supporting Table S6).

We subsequently conducted causal mediation analysis with 1000 bootstrap iterations on these selected oxidative stress and inflammatory biomarkers. Mediation analysis results showed that NLR, MLR, NMLR, SIRI, and SII accounted 0.8% to 1.5% of the risk between serum levels and all‐cause mortality (Figure 3), with 1.55% of the excess risk accounted by these five biomarkers (Supporting Table S7). Meanwhile, we found that MLR accounted 1.4% of the association between serum potassium levels and CV mortality, and 1.1% for all‐cause mortality, while SII accounted 0.9% of the association with all‐cause mortality (Figure 4). However, we did not observe a combined mediation effect of MLR and SII in the association between serum potassium and all‐cause mortality (Supporting Table S8).

Our further analyses showed that inflammatory biomarkers (NLR, MLR, NMLR, SIRI, SII) may influence all‐cause mortality by affecting diastolic BP, LDL, TC, hypertension, and T2D. Specifically, the lowest serum sodium levels showed direct statistically significant associations with DBP, LDL, TC, and T2D, while the highest serum potassium levels demonstrated a direct statistically significant association with TC (Supporting Tables S9–S11). Sensitivity Analysis

After excluding participants with died within 2 years of follow‐up, there were no statistically significant changes observed in the associations of serum potassium and serum sodium levels with CV and all‐cause mortality (Supporting Figure S4). Additionally, the trends across these three time periods (1999–2004, 2005–2010, and 2011–2018) were generally consistent (Supporting Figure S5). Furthermore, we found that the lowest tertile of the sodium‐to‐potassium ratio was statistically significantly associated with an increased risk of all‐cause mortality (HR: 1.16; 95% CI: 1.03–1.30; p = 0.014) (Supporting Table S12). Similar results were observed in other sensitivity analyses (Supporting Tables S13–S16).

Discussion

In this study, using data from the NHANES survey, we observed that lower serum sodium levels within the normal range are associated with an increased risk of all‐cause mortality. Meanwhile, higher serum potassium is associated with an elevated risk of CV and all‐cause mortality with a J‐shaped relationship, with MLR mediating CV and all‐cause mortality, and SII mediating all‐cause mortality. Maintaining serum sodium at 138–139 mmol/L and potassium at 3.5–3.8 mmol/L within the normal range statistically significantly reduced all‐cause mortality. Stratified analyses and sensitivity analyses corroborated these findings.

We found that lower serum sodium levels within the normal range statistically significantly increased the risk of all‐cause mortality, a finding that aligns with most previous studies. For instance, a cross‐sectional study conducted among 4697 adults identified a J‐shaped relationship between low levels of serum sodium and all‐cause mortality [24]. Additionally, in a study of patients with Parkinson's disease and two studies of patients with heart failure, similar results were also reported [25, 26]. However, a prospective cohort study with 11,255 participants found that people whose middle‐aged serum sodium exceeds 142 mmol/L faced an increased risk of being biologically aged, developing chronic diseases, and dying at a younger age [13]. This study, however, did not consider important individual dietary risk factors such as daily intake of potassium, sodium, and fluid intake. We also observed a negative association between higher serum sodium within the normal range and all‐cause mortality, but it was not statistically significant.

Dyskalemias are linked with an increased risk of all‐cause and CV mortality [27]. The Danish National registries showed that lower and upper levels of serum potassium within the normal range were associated with a significantly increased short‐term risk of death in chronic heart failure patients [20]. Two studies involving hypertensive patients reported that high levels of serum potassium increase the risk of all‐cause mortality [28, 29]. Observational studies in patients with hypertension also suggested a U‐shaped association between serum potassium concentrations and all‐cause mortality [30]. In this study, we further reported that higher serum potassium levels, even within the normal range may increase the risk of CV and all‐cause mortality.

Interestingly, as important biomarkers of electrolyte balance and physiological function, this is the first study to demonstrate an interaction between serum sodium and potassium to all‐cause mortality. Our study found that within normal ranges, maintaining serum sodium at 138–139 mmol/L and serum potassium at 3.5–3.8 mmol/L was associated with a significant 54% reduction in all‐cause mortality risk. These findings raise an important question: Do the currently defined normal ranges for serum sodium and potassium remain appropriate? Further research is needed to repeat our results and to redefine these reference intervals.

Previous studies have elucidated the mechanism underlying the association between serum potassium levels and mortality, which is likely predominantly attributed to the potassium homeostasis mechanism. Extracellular potassium levels are strictly controlled to maintain the resting membrane potential of excitable cells. The most reasonable mechanism by which elevated serum potassium levels lead to death is through inducing fatal arrhythmias. It has been reported that elevated serum potassium levels increase the risk of ventricular arrhythmias in patients with acute myocardial infarction, suggesting a potential mechanistic explanation for the association of elevated serum potassium levels with all‐cause mortality [31]. The mechanism by which low sodium levels within the normal range increase the risk of all‐cause mortality may be due to the direct toxicity of severe hyponatremia to the brain, leading to cerebral edema, brain herniation, encephalopathy, seizures, and coma [32, 33]. Additionally, even mild decreases in serum sodium levels may have adverse effects on the central nervous system, causing imbalance, gait abnormalities, increased risk of falls, and risk of fractures [25, 34, 35, 36, 37]. In line with previous studies [5, 6, 7], we further reveal that even high‐normal potassium and low‐normal sodium levels within the normal range may increase CV and all‐cause mortality risk through inflammatory pathways.

Inflammatory biomarkers (NLR, MLR, NMLR, SIRI, and SII) may partially mediate the association between the lowest‐tertile serum sodium (within the normal range) and all‐cause mortality. Specifically, MLR and SII accounted for the relationship between the highest‐tertile serum potassium and CV mortality, while MLR also accounted for all‐cause mortality. Mechanistically, elevated sodium and potassium can promote pro‐inflammatory macrophage polarization, Th17/Treg imbalance, monocyte activation, inflammasome signaling, and chronic NF‐κB/JAK‐STAT/MAPK activation, sustaining vascular inflammation and contributing to endothelial dysfunction, atherogenesis, and CV events [38, 39, 40]. These immune perturbations may also exacerbate traditional CV risk factors such as diastolic BP, LDL, TC, T2D, and hypertension, partially explaining the observed associations with all‐cause and CV mortality [41, 42, 43, 44, 45].

This study is among the first to explore the associations between serum sodium and potassium levels within normal ranges with CV mortality and all‐cause mortality in the general population, while also investigating the potential mediating roles of oxidative stress and inflammatory biomarkers, along with the involvement of CV‐related risk factors in these associations. However, our study has some limitations. First, the observational design precludes causal inference, and observations such as the low risk in the 138–139 mmol/L range may stem from statistical instability. Therefore, our results must be validated in randomized clinical trials. Second, the serum sodium and potassium levels were at a single point in time, which may not account for the changes over time, especially for the serum potassium levels. Finally, our findings are specific to the US population, and the generalizability of our findings to other backgrounds, cultures, and populations is not guaranteed. Additionally, our interaction and mediation analyses were exploratory in nature, and the study was not powered to robustly detect subgroup differences, a critical constraint that warrants acknowledgment.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we reported that lower serum sodium levels within the normal range are associated with an increased risk of all‐cause mortality. Meanwhile, higher serum potassium is associated with an increased risk of CV and all‐cause mortality with a J‐shaped relationship. The lowest all‐cause mortality was observed in participants with serum sodium at 138–139 mmol/L and potassium at 3.5–3.8 mmol/L. Furthermore, inflammatory biomarkers accounted for a small portion of the associations of serum sodium and potassium levels with CV or all‐cause mortality. Our results provided more evidence for reconsidering the “optimal safety ranges” for serum sodium and potassium.
