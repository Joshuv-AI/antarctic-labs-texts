Predictive value of prognostic nutritional index in patients undergoing gastrectomy for gastric cancer: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Huachu Deng, Yiqiang He, Gaofei Huang, Yuetong Huang, Jiaheng Wu, Xingan Qin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:The value of prognostic nutritional index (PNI) in gastrectomy remains controversial. This meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the predictive value of PNI in patients undergoing gastrectomy for malignancy.Methods:We retrieved studies from medical literature databases to analyze the endpoints of overall survival, cancer-specific survival, recurrence-free survival, and clinicopathologic features. The hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were used to access the survival prognostic value of PNI in patients after gastrectomy. Odds ratio and mean difference were used to evaluate the relationship between the low PNI and clinicopathologic features.Results:In total, we included 38 articles (39 trial comparisons) which contained 23,756 gastrectomy patients. The results showed that low PNI was associated with shorter overall survival (HR: 1.82, 95% CI 1.62–2.03), shorter cancer-specific survival (HR: 1.44, 95% CI 1.24–1.67), and shorter recurrence-free survival (HR: 2.52, 95% CI 1.41–4.47). Besides, patients with low PNI had a higher risk of postoperative complications compared with high PNI (HR: 1.65, 95% CI 1.30–2.09). And low PNI group was found to be related to older, lower BMI, larger tumor size, deeper tumor invasion, poorer differentiation, more advanced tumor stage, total gastrectomy, and the presence of lymph node metastasis, lymphatic invasion, and vessel invasion.Conclusion:PNI was significantly associated with survival and postoperative complications of gastric cancer patients undergoing gastrectomy. Therefore PNI has the potential to be a prognostic predictor for gastrectomy.

1. Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is one of the most prevalent malignant malignancies of the digestive system.[1] GC incidence and death rates are ranked 4th and 5th, respectively, in the worldwide cancer statistics for 2020.[2] The treatment of GC includes surgery, endoscopy, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy. Surgery is still the main treatment for GC.[3] Therefore, prognosis and survival prediction of postoperative patients with GC is crucial, which is conducive to preoperative guidance of gastrectomy.

Immune factors and nutritional status are closely related to surgical risk in cancer patients.[4] Recent studies have found that these 2 factors are related with long-term prognosis of some solid cancers.[5] Prognostic nutritional index (PNI), the Glasgow prognostic score, neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio, and platelet/lymphocyte ratio are some contemporary indices for assessing the nutritional and immunological health of cancer patients.[6] Among them, the study of PNI is the most mature. PNI was first proposed and confirmed by Onodera. The PNI is calculated by serum albumin (ALB) and total lymphocyte count (TLC). Both of them are easily obtained before surgery. Many studies have shown a correlation between preoperative PNI and surgical risk assessment and long-term prognosis for a variety of abdominal tumors, such as colorectal cancer, esophageal cancer, liver cancer, and so on.[7–9]

Besides, the role of preoperative PNI in postoperative complications and prognosis of GC had been demonstrated in previous studies. There are 2 meta-analyses studying the role of PNI in the GC patients undergoing gastrectomy. But all of them have certain limitations. In 2016, 1 meta-analysis by Yang et al[10] included 10 studies with 3396 patients. The study demonstrated a correlation between clinicopathological characteristics of GC patients and PNI, a predictor of survival and postoperative complications. There were, however, not enough research and patients included. According to Li et al,[11] individuals with low PNI undergoing gastrectomy for cancer may have a poor prognosis and a greater incidence of postoperative complications (POCs). But they are mainly focused on the relationship between PNI and POCs. A number of clinical trials on the predictive role of the PNI and gastrectomy for cancer had been published for the past 3 years. It is questionable whether the conclusions of the previous meta-analyses are still reliable. So it is necessary to update the previously published meta-analyses. The purpose of this study was to comprehensively evaluate the role of preoperative PNI in survival and complications after GC resection based on the latest available evidence.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Search strategy

This meta-analysis was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA).[12] In order to study the predictive value of PNI in patients undergoing gastrectomy for malignancy, we conducted a systematic search for studies in databases such as the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Embase, Pubmed, Google Scholar, Baidu Scholar, and other databases. We searched for relevant studies published up to February 20th, 2022 with language restriction to English. Combining the main keywords and free words, the complete search strategy was as follows: (“PNI” OR “prognostic nutritional index” OR “Onodera index”) AND (“stomach cancer” OR “gastric cancer” OR “GC” OR “gastric carcinoma” OR “gastric tumor” OR “gastrectomy”). We also reviewed the reference list of retrieved articles to look for other potential experiments.

2.2. Study selection

The studies were considered to assess the predictive efficacy of PNI in patients having gastrectomy for malignancy. Following were the criteria for inclusion: (1) cancer patients having gastrectomy surgery. (2) Endpoints (overall survival [OS], cancer-specific survival [CSS], recurrence-free survival [RFS], and POCs) were available. (3) If several studies have overlapping samples, the studies with large sample size or high quality were selected. (4) Patients in the study were divided into 2 groups based on PNI.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) summary studies, animal, cellular studies, or low-quality studies were excluded. (2) Gastrectomy combined with other operations at the same time. (3) GC patients without surgical treatment. (4) Comparison of the value of PNI and other prognostic indicators. (5) Gastroesophageal junction cancer.

2.3. Data extraction

The following information was independently extracted by 2 authors (H.D. and Y.H.) from each of the studies that were included: study period, publication year, country, simple size, age, type of surgery, cutoff value, follow-up, pathological stage, OS, CSS, RFS, and clinicopathological features. When differences arise, all the authors negotiate together until the differences are resolved.

2.4. Quality assessment of study and evidence

To assess the methodological quality of the included case–control studies, we used the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). NOS criteria include selection, comparability, and exposure in experimental and control groups. There are 4 entries and 3 entries for selection and exposure, respectively, with 1 point for each entry, and comparability is only 1 entry for 2 points per entry. So the highest quality score is 9 points. A score of ≥7 was designated as a high-quality study and <7 were designated as low-quality studies.[13]

2.5. Statistical analysis

The survival predictive significance of PNI in patients who had had gastrectomy was investigated using the hazard ratio (HR). The association between low PNI and clinicopathologic characteristics was assessed using odds ratio (OR) and mean difference. The heterogeneity of the included studies was evaluated using the Q test, where the test threshold was set at 0.05. P > .05 suggested that there was study heterogeneity. The Higgins I2 statistic was also looked at. I2 values >50% and >75%, respectively, indicate that there was significant heterogeneity and high heterogeneity in the trials. We used a random-effects model when we saw considerable heterogeneity. A fixed-effects model was selected in all other cases. If there were more than 10 studies that evaluated a single outcome, we used Begg and Egger test to investigate causes of heterogeneity and look at publication bias. Additionally, we examined potential impacts of publication bias using the nonparametric “trim and fill” methods developed by Duval and Tweedie. In order to assess the causes of heterogeneity, we performed a subgroup analysis. In order to assess the dependability and stability of the combined results, sensitivity analysis was utilized. For sensitivity analysis, we employed the “leave-one-out” strategy. After first excluding one of the included studies, a meta-analysis was performed to combine the remaining investigations. The original meta-analysis findings were assessed to determine if there were any significant changes as a result of the effect of omitted studies by evaluating the changes of the merged results. Software called STATA 12.0 was used for the statistical analysis. Without any particular considerations, a threshold of two-sided P < .05 was regarded as significant.

3. Results

3.1. Literature search process

According to PRISMA guidelines, 316 related literatures were included in the preliminary search. Two hundred twelve literatures with the same author and data were excluded. Forty-five literatures were excluded for lack of required outcome indicators. Ten literatures excluded for data unavailable or research in the same center. Finally 38 literatures[14–51] were included, containing 23,756 patients as shown in Figure 1.

3.2. Included study characteristics

The characteristics of the included studies were described in detail in Table 1. All included studies were single-center prospective or retrospective controlled trials with a minimum sample size of 90 and a maximum sample size of 7781. Thirty-two studies were retrospective studies, 4 were prospective studies, and 3 did not report the type of study. The included studies were from China, Japan, Korea, and Italy, and most of them were from Japan and China, accounting for 87%. Twenty-nine studies reported OS, 8 studies reported CSS, 5 studies reported RFS, and 15 studies reported POCs. The NOS score of these 38 included studies ranged from 6 to 8.

3.3. PNI and OS

Twenty-nine studies, with a simple size of 18,596 patients, reported on the relationship between PNI and OS. The pooled result showed that low PNI was related to shorter OS (HR: 1.82, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.62–2.03, Fig. 2). Due to middle heterogeneity between the studies (I2 = 55.7%, P < .01), a random-effects model was applied. In addition, we did subgroup analyses based on the publication time, country, cutoff value, and sample size of the included studies. The results showed that in the country subgroup analysis, low PNI was related to shorter OS in Chinese, Korean, and Japanese patients (HR: 1.66, 95% CI 1.37–2.00; HR: 1.85, 95% CI 1.20–2.85; HR: 1.97, 95% CI 1.70–2.29), but not significantly different in Italian patients (HR = 1.92, 95% CI 0.96–3.85). And PNI was an independent predictive factor for OS in other subgroups. Studies heterogeneity still persisted after subgroup analysis. The results are shown in Table 2.

3.4. PNI and CSS

Eight studies, with a simple size of 4032 patients, reported on the relationship between PNI and CSS. The pooled HRs showed that low PNI patients had shorter CSS compared with high PNI patients (HR: 1.44, 95% CI 1.24–1.67, I2 = 0.0%, Fig. 3). A fixed-effects model was applied.

Forest plot of the HR for association between low PNI with CSS in patients after gastrectomy. PNI = prognostic nutritional index, OS = overall survival, CSS = cancer-specific survival, RFS = recurrence-free survival, HR = hazard ratio.

3.5. PNI and RFS

Five studies, with a simple size of 9527 patients, reported on the relationship between PNI and RFS. The pooled HRs demonstrated that patients with low PNI had shorter RFS compared with high PNI (HR: 2.52, 95% CI 1.41–4.47, I2 = 91.2%, Fig. 4). A random-effects model was applied.

Forest plot of the HR for association between low PNI with RFS in patients after gastrectomy. PNI = prognostic nutritional index, OS = overall survival, CSS = cancer-specific survival, RFS = recurrence-free survival, HR = hazard ratio.

3.6. PNI and POCs

Sixteen studies, with a simple size of 12,339 patients, reported on the relationship between PNI and POCs. The pooled OR revealed that patients with low PNI had a higher risk of POCs compared with high PNI (OR: 1.65, 95% CI 1.30–2.09, I2 = 66.7%, Fig. 5). A random-effects model was applied.

Forest plot of the OR for association between low PNI with POCs in patients after gastrectomy. PNI = prognostic nutritional index, POCs = postoperative complications, OR = odds ratio.

3.7. PNI and clinicopathological features

Patients with low PNI were older and had BMI that were lower than those with high PNI. Furthermore, it was discovered that low PNI groups were associated with lymph node metastases, lymphatic invasion, deeper tumor invasion, poorer differentiation, advanced tumor stage, complete gastrectomy, greater tumor size, and deeper tumor invasion (Table 3).

3.8. Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

We performed sensitivity analyses by deleting each included study at a time (Fig. 6). Results showed that excluding any of the studies did not significantly alter the effect of PNI on OS in a comprehensive meta-analysis. It means our results are reliable and robust. In the meta-analysis for PNI and OS, Egger test (P = .084) and Begg test (0.108) revealed that there was no publication bias (Fig. 7). Besides, we performed a trim-and-fill approach to correct potential publication bias. By this method we obtained symmetric funnel plots (Fig. 8). And the adjusted HR remained significant (HR: 1.55, 95% CI 1.35–1.79, P < .001), indicating that our results were reliable.

4. Discussion

In 1984, Japanese scholar Onodera first proposed the concept of PNI after gastrointestinal surgery in malnourished cancer patients.[52] The PNI is calculated by ALB and TLC. ALB is synthesized by the liver, and its decline can be caused by long-term insufficient protein intake, which can be used as an indicator of protein malnutrition in chronic diseases. ALB reflects the body’s nutritional status. Serum ALB undertakes physiological functions such as tissue repair, material transport, and osmotic pressure maintenance. Preoperative hypoalbuminemia will lead to a series of complications such as postoperative infection and delay wound prognosis. More importantly, the consumption of ALB by malignant cancer, and gastrointestinal cancer may affect the body’s absorption of protein, which may lead to the low ALB state of patients with malignant cancer. Studies have demonstrated that the serum ALB level is a significant predictor of the progression, metastasis, recurrence, and survival of patients with malignant cancer and is an independent factor impacting the prognosis of patients with a range of malignant cancers. TLC is a simple parameter reflecting immune function. Malnutrition and low cellular immune function can cause its decline. The number of TLC is related to the immune level of the body. When the number of lymphocytes decreases, it indicates that the immune function of the body decreases. The immune surveillance effect on the cancer decreases, and the occurrence of immune escape of cancer cells increases.[53,54] PNI is a comprehensive indicator for assessing immune and nutritional status. PNI = serum ALB (g/L) + 5 × TLC (×109/L). If PNI is more than 50, it indicates that the patient is in a normal nutritional state; when PNI is between 45 and 50, it indicates that the patient has mild malnutrition, but the resection and anastomosis of digestive tract were still safe; between 40 and 45, it indicates that the patient is obviously malnourished and there is a risk of gastrointestinal resection and anastomosis; when PNI is <40, it indicates that the patient is seriously malnourished and cannot tolerate the operation.[52]

The PNI value is a convenient and practical indicator, and relatively easy to obtain. It does not require a lot of human or financial resources, which only need serum ALB and TLC. So PNI has the potential to be a prognostic indicator. For patients with low PNI, giving necessary nutritional supplements before surgery could not only reduce POCs, but also reduce the incidence of adverse reactions of postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy. For patients with severely low PNI, we can adjust chemotherapy regimen to reduce adverse reactions (such as single-drug chemotherapy), or appropriately reduced the dose of chemotherapy drugs to avoid serious adverse reactions, so as to achieve individualized treatment. Moreover, the PNI can be used to predict survival and the risk of POCs of GC after gastrectomy. So PNI can be used clinically as a routine item to guide treatment.[55]

PNI has steadily evolved into a novel indicator in recent years for assessing the prognosis of patients with lung cancer, gynecological malignancies, and urologic neoplasms.[56] In addition, the prognosis judgment of non-cancer patients such as fractures, heart failure, and cerebral infarction has also been increasingly used. However, PNI was initially used to examine patients’ nutritional and immunological statuses following gastrointestinal surgery.

Thirty-eight studies[14–51] with a total of 23,756 GC patients having gastrectomy were included in this meta-analysis. We discovered that PNI, an independent factor, had an impact on the survival and prognosis of patients with stomach cancer. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses confirmed the validity of our results, which were trustworthy and solid. A stratified meta-analysis revealed that despite variations in groups’ publication years, sample sizes, study designs, and cutoff values, low PNI was remained substantially linked with POCs. Although Italy and South Korea were not statistically significant in the country subgroup analysis, this may be caused by the small number of studies and sample sizes in these 2 countries. While this was going on, we further corrected publication bias in OS studies using a trim-and-fill strategy. The findings demonstrated that there is still a strong correlation between low preoperative PNI and short OS, supporting the validity of our conclusions. We looked into the relationship between PNI and POCs in further detail. The findings demonstrated that a high POC risk was consistently correlated with low PNI. Additionally, the sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the results of the meta-analysis of POCs were accurate and that there was no publication bias in the meta-analysis of complications. Additionally, we discovered a strong correlation between low PNI and subpar CSS/RFS in GC. As a result, PNI may be regarded as a reliable signal for estimating POCs and the prognosis for survival.

Our meta-analysis has the following potential clinical implications: (1) this is a meta-analysis to assess the predictive function of PNI for postoperative survival and complications in patients undergoing gastrectomy. Compared to previous studies, we included 38 studies that contained a large sample size of 23,756 participants. (2) Sensitivity analyses and subgroup analyses were conducted to explore heterogeneity. We found no source of heterogeneity through sensitivity analysis. And we successfully decreased the heterogeneity of OS by performing a subgroup analysis according country, publishing time, cutoff value, sample, and study design. (3) All included studies were case–control studies with high quality. (4) Only 2 studies[15,50] had a sample size of <100. (5) There was a low probability of publication bias for the included studies. (6) These studies were all published after 2010, and a large number of studies have been published in the past 3 years, which further improves the reliability of the findings. (7) OS as an endpoint may contain non-cancer-related deaths, so we also included CSS and RFS endpoints to assess cancer-associated survival, which was better to evaluate the value of PNI in patients after gastrectomy for cancer.

The limitations of our study are as follows: (1) most included studies are retrospective clinical studies that lead to selection bias and recall bias. (2) Most of the included studies were from East Asia (Japan, China, and Korea), so the global applicability of the findings remains to be studied. So large sample size, multicenter and long-term follow-up RCTs are necessary to designed to further evaluate the value of PNI. (3) After subgroup analysis by country, publishing time, cutoff value, sample, and study design, the heterogeneity of OS in our meta-analysis decreased, but the heterogeneity was still large. (4) Most of the patients were with different stage GC. Based on the data of published article, it was unable to assess the predictive role of PNI in patients after different stages of GC resection.

5. Conclusions

In GC patients following gastrectomy, PNI was substantially correlated with survival, postoperative complications, and clinicopathologic characteristics. Therefore, PNI has the potential to be a prognostic predictor for gastrectomy.
