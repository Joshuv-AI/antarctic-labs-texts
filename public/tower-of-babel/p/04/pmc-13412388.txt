Case series: the right parasternal view and its prognostic value in detecting pathological abnormalities on transthoracic echocardiography
Authors: Neve Moore, Alyssa Lacerna, Juan Ortegarestrepo, Roxy Senior, Rajdeep Khattar, Wei Li, Sitali Mushemi-Blake
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTwo-dimensional echocardiography is the most widely used imaging modality for immediate diagnosis, due to its easy access and cost-effectiveness. International guidelines recommend four standard views to perform a comprehensive minimum dataset for every adult patient, namely left parasternal, apical, subcostal, and suprasternal views.Case summaryIn this case series, we revisit the right parasternal view (RPV). Although it is infrequently used in routine clinical practice, it provides valuable complementary information, particularly when abnormalities are found on the minimum dataset or when measurement results are inconsistent. Including this view in the imaging protocol adds only a few extra minutes but can yield important additional diagnostic information.DiscussionPresented are four cases in which the complementary use of RPV provided decisive diagnostic information that was either missed or underestimated by standard echocardiographic windows and influenced clinical management, re-emphasizing the value of adding this view during a routine examination.

Recognize the right parasternal view as a non-conventional but valuable addition to a comprehensive minimum echocardiography dataset.

Introduction

The right parasternal view (RPV) technique can be applicable in many cardiac diseases, namely aortic pathologies or congenital heart diseases, for example, atrial septal defect or Fontan conduits. 1–3 Shimamura et al.4 compared Doppler measurements from apical and RPV and found that the severity of aortic stenosis (AS) was underestimated in 10% of their patients when measurements were from the apical views alone.

To obtain the RPV, the patient should lie in a deep right decubitus position, with the right arm extended above the head.1–3 The transducer should be placed in the second or third intercostal space, higher than the standard parasternal view. The transducer probe’s index marker should be pointing towards the patient’s right shoulder (Figure 1A).

Right parasternal views on transthoracic echo. (A) Orientation of transducer position on the patient’s chest. (B) Right parasternal view of the ascending aorta in long axis. (C) Right parasternal view showing interatrial septum.

The high RPV allows for clear visualization of the ascending aorta.5,6 Aortic flows are in alignment to the transducer, thereby yielding the highest Doppler velocities for assessments of aortic pathologies. Lowering the probe into the super-inferior orientation with marker pointing to the left shoulder (short axis) allows for clear assessment of the interatrial cavity.7

Patient 1: aortic root dissection

A 32-year-old female with normal habitus (height 1.57 m, body surface area 1.54m2), no past medical, family history of heritable thoracic aortic disease or features of Marfan syndrome presented to the emergency department with intermittent chest pain, and shortness of breath over the past 8 months. The patient had previously been diagnosed with pericarditis. Blood results revealed mildly raised troponin of 24–47 ng/l and C-reactive protein (CRP) of 27 mg/l.

Echocardiography performed at another institution revealed a small pericardial effusion, and the ascending aorta was not clearly visible; however, it appeared to be significantly dilated with severe aortic regurgitation.

They were later referred to our institution for consideration of aortic valve (AV) intervention. A repeat transthoracic echocardiogram was performed. Measurements from the left parasternal view revealed a sinus of Valsalva of 58 mm and sinotubular junction of 69 mm. The ascending aorta was not well seen from this view.

In alignment with our laboratory protocol and guidelines, interrogation in the RPV raised suspicion of a dissection flap in the ascending aorta (Figure 2). There was severe aortic regurgitation associated with severely dilated left ventricle (129 ml/m2, consistent with chronic severe aortic regurgitation), increased diastolic filling pressures, preserved ejection fraction of 54% by 3D imaging, and normal global longitudinal strain (−18.9%). The left atrium was normal size (20 ml/m2). The right heart was normal with mild tricuspid regurgitation and an estimated pulmonary artery (PA) systolic pressure of 34 mmHg. A small circumferential pericardial effusion was noted. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the thorax and transoesophageal echocardiography revealed the presence of a 70 mm aneurysm of the ascending aorta.

Surgical findings during sternotomy revealed a huge aneurysmal ascending aorta measuring 70 mm and areas of extreme thinning consistent with impending rupture. The ascending aortic aneurysm was excised, and a 25 mm On-X composite graft was implanted into the native aortic annulus (Table 1). The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery and discharged 11 days post-surgery. Subsequent follow-up visits showed normal left ventricular size (46 ml/m2) and function with good progress. The patient was also referred to our genetics clinic.

Patient 2: aortic stenosis

A 70-year-old female with a history of progressive dizziness on standing, episodes of dull chest pain, and worsening shortness of breath on exertion, leading to reduced exercise tolerance. Patient reported 2 syncopal episodes in 6 months.

On examination, they had an ejection systolic murmur not radiating to the carotids. Their chest was clear, and there was minimal peripheral oedema. They were in sinus rhythm with a resting blood pressure of 136/80 mmHg. Patient’s full blood count, urea and electrolytes, liver function test, thyroid function test, coagulation and CRP were within the normal range, and B-type natriuretic peptide was 22 ng/l. Their coronary angiogram revealed unobstructed coronary arteries.

Their transthoracic echo (TTE) showed normal left ventricular size and function, mild left ventricular hypertrophy, and normal biventricular systolic function. The AV was trileaflet and heavily calcified. There was reduced leaflet excursion associated with calcification. The apical views yielded a peak gradient of 32 mmHg, mean gradient of 17 mmHg, and AV area of 1.1cm2. The stroke volume was 64 ml, and the transvalvular flow rate was 232 ml/s. The image quality from the apical five-chamber view was sub-optimal, yielding an incomplete AV Doppler signal. The RPV was attempted, and Doppler signal was found to have a more complete envelope with peak gradient of 61 mmHg, mean gradient of 30 mmHg, and AV area was 0.9 cm2. Readings obtained from the RPV were significantly higher compared to the apical views (Figure 3).

The AV severity eventually advanced, and the patient underwent successful surgical intervention with a 21 mm Edwards Magna Ease Bioprosthetic valve. They were discharged 7 days post-surgery. The patient made good recovery and resumed an active lifestyle.

Patient 3: sinus venosus defect

A 48-year-old female presented with a 2-year history of progressive shortness of breath on exertion, palpitations and dry cough, mosaicism, and possible ground-glass changes on initial CT pulmonary angiogram.

They subsequently underwent a saline microbubble contrast echocardiogram. During the examination, the patient was in fast atrial fibrillation (120 b.p.m.) with blood pressure of 93/66 mmHg. Technically challenging off-axis views were obtained, which confirmed findings of a severely dilated right heart (right atrial volume 95 ml/m2, right ventricle (RV)—base 62 mm, RV—mid 51 mm, RV—length 51 mm) with moderate to severely reduced systolic function (fractional area change 23%, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion 21 mm, S’ 14.8 cm/s). There was an aneurysmal and mobile interatrial septum, and saline contrast images demonstrated a left-to-right shunt; the origin was difficult discern. The RV had features of volume and pressure overload. Additionally, there was moderate tricuspid regurgitation, raised estimated PA systolic pressure of 33 mmHg, and a short acceleration time of the pulmonary flow of 83 ms, all indicative of elevated pulmonary pressures. The main PA and branches were significantly enlarged. Both mitral leaflets were billowing with moderate mitral regurgitation. The left ventricle was of normal size and function.

Use of the RPV, visualized in the long-axis orientation (marker pointing to the right shoulder), with the probe angulated inferiorly to align with the inferior vena cava to right atrium (RA) junction, ensuring the atrial septum was most perpendicular to the ultrasound beam, clearly confirmed the presence of a large inferior sinus venous atrial septal defect (3D area 5.65 cm2) with a left-to-right shunt (Figure 4). The posterior location of the defect was not detectable in conventional views; however, the complementary use of the RPV clearly delineated the sinus venosus defect (SVD). Subsequent cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed the presence of a large inferior SVD defect, quantifying Qp/Qs ∼6.7, PA flow 14.1 l/min, and aortic flow ∼ 2.1 l/min. Right heart catheterization showed the patient had high oxygen saturation in the PA (90%), and lower RA (88%). The mean PA pressure was 28 mmHg, with mean pulmonary wedge pressure of 21 mmHg.

The SVD was repaired using a fenestrated patch and Cox Maze and the inferior vena cava was redirected into the RA (Table 2). Transoesophageal echocardiography confirmed a good surgical result. The patient made a good recovery and was discharged 9 days later. Subsequent serial echocardiography showed a gradual reduction in RV size and improvement in systolic function with no residual shunt.

Patient 4: Fontan circulation

Tricuspid atresia is a rare complex congenital heart disease that reduces longevity and requires immediate surgical intervention. The Fontan palliation is a surgical procedure first described in 1968 that separates the pulmonary and systemic cardiac circulation via a conduit without needing the subpulmonary ventricleto pump deoxygenated blood to the lungs. Adult patients who undergo a Fontan procedure are documented to have a complex clinical course with complications such as hepatic dysfunction (75% autopsies), diastolic dysfunction (72%), chronotopic incompetence, thromboembolic-coagulation abnormalities (15%–25%), arrhythmias (20%–40%), heart failure, and sudden death.8–9

Here, we present a 34-year-old female patient who was born with tricuspid atresia, hypoplastic RV, small ventricular septal defect, severe pulmonary stenosis, and patent foramen ovale. During the first years of life, the patient underwent multiple complex operations with final conversion to a Fontan circulation (Table 3). They were on long-term follow-up by the adult congenital heart team in our institution.

In 2023, the patient presented with syncope and had a single chamber VVI permanent pacemaker surgically implanted. A two-dimensional (2D) echocardiogram was requested to rule out pericardial effusion following pacemaker box change.

Baseline observations during TTE were as follows: BSA 1.8 m2 and blood pressure 100/51 mmHg. Two-dimensional echocardiogram showed levocadria position, situs solitus, tricuspid atresia with absent right atrioventricular connection, normal-sized left atrium connecting to a dilated left ventricle with low normal ejection fraction (53%). The RA appeared dilated with a rudimentary RV. The Fontan connection was unobstructed with laminar phasic Doppler flows. There was no pericardial effusion.

Complementary RPV is superior to conventional views because the ultrasound probe sits nearer to the right heart chambers. As the RA lies more anteriorly and rightward in the chest wall, the ultrasound beam reaches the chamber with minimal intervening tissue and less attenuation. In addition to this, the inferior vena cava entering the RA may align perpendicular to the beam from the right side, thereby improving assessments of RA size, RA masses/thrombi, interatrial septal shunts, etc. In this case, utilization of the RPV technique demonstrated a severely dilated RA with spontaneous contrast, raising suspicion of a mass in the RA (Figure 5). Further imaging from the RPV using a bolus of an ultrasound-enhancing agent (sulphur hexafluoride microbubbles) confirmed the presence of a large thrombus measuring 48 mm × 36 mm.

The new findings were escalated, and the patient was treated with a high target of INR between 3 and 4. Subsequent echocardiography follow-up assessments incorporating the RPV technique demonstrated the absence of thrombus in the RA.

Discussion

Two-dimensional TTE is a widely utilized non-invasive imaging modality owing to its unique capability to provide safe, portable, and rapid clinical diagnosis. This article aimed to remind readers of the ‘forgotten’ RPV, and the value it adds to a comprehensive minimum dataset when an echocardiogram needs to answer a specific clinical question.

International guidelines recommend the RPV as the complementary window of choice for estimating the true size of the ascending aorta, as imaging from the left parasternal, apical three long-axis, and modified five-chamber views yield sub-optimal lateral resolution.5 As demonstrated in our case, the complementary RPV enabled visualization of the mid to distal portions of the ascending aorta, including the presence of a tear. In a similar case to ours, Sonaglioni et al.10 were able to identify a tear in the lumen of the ascending aorta using the complementary RPV, underscoring the additional diagnostic value of this approach.

In cases of significant aortic stenosis (AS) where Doppler measurements remain discordant, the RPV technique may serve as a complementary problem-solving tool to aid differential diagnosis. Benfari et al.11 demonstrated that incorporating the RPV technique improved reproducibility of results, particularly in the assessment of haemodynamic changes in progressive AS, with feasibility rates of >75% (2017).

Pulsed-echo Doppler flowmeter, commonly known as PEDOF probes, use continuous wave Doppler and is extremely useful in acquiring high-velocity gradients.12 Use of the PEDOF probe in the RPV is advocated within the guidelines as it can increase the peak gradients recorded.13 A study by Shin et al.14 states that using standard Doppler echocardiography from the apical window can underestimate the true gradient in AS due to poor Doppler alignment and suggests that using the PEDOF probe can enhance diagnosis. In another study, a PEDOF probe in the RPV was reportedly used during stress echocardiography in a case of low-flow, low-gradient AS.15

In adults, the greater distance between the ultrasound transducer probe on the chest wall and the atrial septum in subxiphoid imaging views can result in sub-optimal foreshortened views of the RA. A complementary RPV approach can provide a more longitudinal cut through the right heart chambers, depicting a truer representation of the size and morphology of atrial defects. Studies whereby RPV has been used in addition to other windows to assess the interatrial septum report increased sensitivity from 85.2% to 90.9%.16 Use of 3D in atrial-septal defects is documented to play a useful role in imaging-led interventions.17

SVD is categorized as the ‘deficiency of the superior auricular septum adjacent to the orifice of the superior vena cava and the associated anomalous entry of the right pulmonary veins to the heart’.7,18 Conventional TTE may not adequately detect an SVD, and a transoesophageal approach is needed. However, our case highlights how the adjunct use of RPV not only gained better visualization of the defect but also provided advanced 3D images.

Indeed, the RPV is a complementary window that should be incorporated into the minimum dataset when standard transthoracic windows are poor. It should be systematically attempted in suspected or known AV disease, especially when AS data is discordant, in low-flow/low-gradient AS, and in suboptimal Doppler alignment from apical windows. The RPV yields the highest aortic velocity because the beam aligns more parallel to the systolic flow through the AV. Furthermore, the proximal and mid ascending aorta are often better visualized from the RPV than the left parasternal view, making it an adjunctive view in aortopathy assessment. In atrial defects, this view may align the septum more perpendicular to the ultrasound, thereby improving structural definition. Finally, in congenital heart disease, the RPV window can align better with low-velocity venous flows in surgically altered pathways.

Limitations

Despite the advantages of the RPV, it is important to acknowledge the limitations that can be encountered. To obtain good images the patient is required to be in a steep right-hand side position, which for patients with limited mobility may prove difficult. Obesity, lung, and rib artefacts are also factors that may inhibit the ability to obtain an adequate RPV. Aside from physical limitations, time constraints can limit the incorporation of the RPV in a standard examination but may be overcome by increased awareness of its value and appropriate training to improve efficiency.

Conclusion

In summary, current guidelines universally recommend acquiring images from four sequential views to complete a comprehensive TTE minimum dataset. When underlying pathology is suspected or measurements conflict with clinical findings, the RPV can provide a useful complementary view. Reports of using this view remain scarce in the literature due to its under-utilization in routine practice. In this case series, we describe four different pathologies in which the adjunct use of RPV altered clinical management and contributed to favourable patient outcomes, further emphasizing its value in a complete TTE study.
