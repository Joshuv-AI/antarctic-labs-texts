Policy snapshots cannot establish journal AI-policy failure
Authors: Yibo Wang
Published: 2026

He and Bu (1) provide valuable large-scale evidence of a sharp rise in AI-associated writing signals in scholarly publications and rare public full-text disclosures of AI use in their full-text sample. The concern is narrower: their data document diffusion and public disclosure but do not establish the headline inference that journal AI policies have “failed to promote transparency or restrain AI adoption.”

The issue is not that these limitations are absent from the article; it is that they are incompatible with a title-level and abstract-level policy-failure claim. The analysis relies on 2025 policy snapshots rather than journal-specific adoption dates. The authors acknowledge that policy release dates are unavailable and that publication lags can last months or longer. Papers published before a journal adopted a policy remain useful for describing baseline trends, but cannot be unambiguously assigned to policy exposure. Because authors encounter journal rules at submission or acceptance, publication date is an imprecise stand-alone proxy for policy exposure.

Treatment and outcome definitions further weaken the inference. The principal inferential comparison collapses “strict prohibition,” “open,” “disclosure required,” and “not mentioned” into a binary with/without-policy comparison, although the policy group is overwhelmingly disclosure-oriented rather than prohibitory. In the January 2025 classification, the authors report only 27 strict-prohibition journals and 2 open-policy journals, compared with 3,556 disclosure-required journals, and 96.8% of disclosure-policy journals allow writing and editing support. If most policies permit writing assistance, continued growth in estimated AI-like writing is not evidence against their stated objective. Compounding this, the study’s core metric, MLE-estimated AI content proportion, cannot distinguish between language polishing or editing, which the article describes as typically permissible, and substantive text generation that may violate policy rules. For disclosure-oriented policies, the relevant estimand is disclosure behavior after exposure to the policy in force at submission or acceptance.

Nor is public full-text disclosure equivalent to compliance with the policy presented to authors. Full-text mining is a valuable descriptive measure, but the authors’ policy classification records disclosure locations that include submission systems and cover letters. For that subset, absence of a public full-text statement may reflect a nonpublic disclosure channel, no policy at submission, or ambiguity about whether language polishing required disclosure. Even where manuscript disclosure is required, full-text mining remains a proxy for compliance rather than direct observation of it.

A snapshot comparison can therefore document rapid AI diffusion and rare public disclosure, but it cannot separate ineffective policies from late adoption, publication lag, selective policy adoption after prior AI diffusion, mismatch between policy requirements and measured outcomes, or growth in permitted assistance vs. noncompliant generation. Testing journal AI-policy failure as an intervention would require, for example, adoption dates, policy-type distinctions, submission- or acceptance-date exposure, and within-journal event-study or staggered difference-in-differences designs. Until such analyses are conducted, the descriptive trends documented by the authors cannot support a causal claim of journal AI-policy failure.
