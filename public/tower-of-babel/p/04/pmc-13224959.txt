Left atrial appendage occluder thrombosis caused embolic strokes: a case report
Authors: Gabriel C Riedemann, Ilko L Maier, Sören Brandenburg, David Oxborough, Duygu Kocyigit Burunkaya, Ali Nazmi Calik, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeft atrial appendage (LAA) occlusion is an alternative for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation (AF) patients who are unsuitable for long-term oral anticoagulation due to bleeding risks. While intended to reduce haemorrhagic stroke, LAA occluder devices can rarely lead to thrombus formation and subsequent embolic events.Case summaryA 75-year-old woman with paroxysmal AF and cerebral amyloid angiopathy had an LAA occluder implanted due to concerns for high bleeding risk. She presented with mild left leg weakness; magnetic resonance imaging revealed embolic strokes in the left cerebellum and right cerebral hemisphere. Importantly, transoesophageal echocardiography identified a floating thrombus on the LAA occluder as cause of embolic strokes. Given the high risk of bleeding, an interdisciplinary decision was made collaboratively with the patient to initiate anticoagulation therapy with apixaban. A rigorous follow-up protocol was established to monitor her condition. During an 18-month follow-up, imaging showed effective thrombus resolution without bleeding complications.DiscussionOur case report underscores the need for vigilant monitoring after LAA occluder implantation and reconsideration of anticoagulation therapy, even in patients with high bleeding risks. The findings emphasize the limitations of LAA occlusion alone and the necessity for improved device design and management strategies to enhance patient safety.Keywords: Left atrial appendage occluder, Stroke, Device-related thrombosis, Atrial fibrillation, Cardiac devices, Cerebral amyloid angiopathy

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common cardiac arrhythmia and significantly increases the risk of stroke.1 The European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines 2024 recommend surgical closure of the left atrial appendage (LAA) as an adjunct to oral anticoagulation in patients with AF undergoing cardiac surgery to prevent strokes (Class I, Level of Evidence B).2 For patients unsuitable for surgery or long-term oral anticoagulant (OAC), percutaneous LAA occlusion serves as an alternative (Class IIb, Level C).2

Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is a cerebrovascular disorder characterized by the deposition of amyloid-beta peptides in the walls of cerebral arteries, leading to vessel fragility and an increased risk of intracerebral haemorrhage.3

This case report presents a rare occurrence of late thrombus formation on a LAA occluder device resulting in embolic strokes in an elderly patient, in whom oral anticoagulation was avoided due to a high perceived risk of intracerebral haemorrhage.

Case presentation

A 75-year-old female with a complex medical history, including paroxysmal AF, arterial hypertension, prior cerebral ischaemia, and diagnosed CAA, presented to our hospital with new-onset mild weakness in her left leg. Her cardiovascular risk profile was significant, yielding a CHA2DS2-VA score of 7. Due to the high risk of intracerebral haemorrhage associated with CAA and oral anticoagulation, a percutaneous LAA occluder (Abbott Amplatzer Amulet 28 mm) was implanted 2 years ago. Post-procedure transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE) confirmed complete sealing of the LAA without signs of device-related thrombosis (DRT). She received clopidogrel 75 mg and acetylsalicylic acid 100 mg daily for 3 months, followed by acetylsalicylic acid daily long-term. A 3-month follow-up TOE showed again a persistent sealing of the LAA without signs of a DRT.

At the time of presentation, the patient was taking acetylsalicylic acid 100 mg daily. Otherwise, the patient was taking atorvastatin, levetiracetam, L-DOPA, ramipril, and torasemide. Vital signs were within normal limits. Neurological examination confirmed mild left leg paresis without additional focal deficits.

Diagnostic imaging with cranial CT showed no signs of acute ischaemia, haemorrhage, or proximal vessel occlusion. Subsequent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain revealed acute embolic infarcts in the right lenticulostriate region and the left cerebellar hemisphere (Figure 1). Duplex ultrasonography of the carotid arteries excluded significant stenosis. Importantly, TOE uncovered a floating, pedunculated thrombus attached to the centre of the LAA occluder device (Figure 2; Supplementary material online, Video S1).

Considering the thrombus as the source of the embolic strokes, an interdisciplinary team evaluated management options. RCT-Data is limited. Surgical removal of the thrombus and LAA ligation was deemed high risk. Despite the elevated risk of intracerebral haemorrhage, the decision to initiate immediate therapeutic anticoagulation with heparin, followed by long-term therapy with apixaban 5 mg twice daily, was made. Vitamin K antagonists demand frequent monitoring and pose a higher intracranial bleeding risk, driving the choice of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs). Among these, apixaban offers a particularly favourable intracranial bleeding profile compared to other DOACs. A small trial favoured low-dose DOAC over dual antiplatelet therapy for prophylaxes after implantation4; this is in line with a recent metanalysis.5 The larger ANDES trial did not show a significant reduction in DRT with short term DOAC compared with DAPT after LAAC, although overall safety favored DOAC.6

The patient was placed under a rigorous follow-up regimen, including TOE examinations to monitor the thrombus and strict control of arterial hypertension. A semi-annual cranial MRI protocol was implemented to assess for any intracerebral haemorrhage. Two months after having started anticoagulation therapy, TOE showed resolution of the free-floating thrombus on the LAA occluder device. A thin cap over the device remained (see Supplementary material online, Video S2). This was interpreted as a residual thrombus, so anticoagulation was continued. Over an 18-month follow-up period, the patient remained free from bleeding complications and recurrent cardioembolic events. A 6-month follow-up shows a near complete resolution of the thrombus with only a minimal thrombogenic mass remaining (see Supplementary material online, Figures S1 and S2). Follow-up MRIs confirmed the absence of intracerebral haemorrhage.

Discussion

Managing stroke risk in patients with AF who have contraindications to OAC therapy presents significant clinical challenges. The AF guidelines of the ESC 2024 recognize percutaneous LAA occlusion as an alternative for stroke prevention in patients with AF who are ineligible for OAC.2 Randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the non-inferiority of LAA occlusion compared to warfarin in reducing stroke risk, with potential for lower bleeding rates during long-term follow-up.7,8 More recently, CLOSURE-AF did not demonstrate non-inferiority of LAAC versus best medical therapy in high-risk patients with AF.9

Our case report reflects some of the practical challenges and limitations of LAA occlusion, which are acknowledged but not thoroughly addressed in the ESC guidelines. One crucial point highlighted by our case is the ESC guidelines’ emphasis on the non-inferiority of LAA occlusion primarily in the prevention of haemorrhagic stroke, with a trend towards more ischaemic strokes. The underlying meta-analysis by Holmes et al. concludes that the incidence of haemorrhagic stroke was significantly reduced when comparing LAA occlusion to warfarin. However, the device group experienced a higher rate of ischaemic strokes.10

DRT is a significant predictor of subsequent stroke or systemic embolism, with reported incidences ranging heavily from 2% to 25%.11 In our patient, the thrombus was detected 2 years after the procedure.

Several risk factors for DRT have been identified, including hypercoagulable states, deep device implantation, non-paroxysmal AF, renal insufficiency, and specific device design features such as exposed screws.12 In our patient, the thrombus was likely associated with the exposed proximal screw mechanism of the LAA occluder, which may promote thrombus formation.

While TOE has been the gold standard for diagnosing DRT, cardiac CT can identify hypoattenuated thickening on the device surface, which may be an early indicator of DRT formation.13 Incorporating standardized imaging protocols and adopting multimodal imaging strategies could enhance early detection of thrombus and improve patient outcomes.

The occurrence of DRT 2 years post-implantation raises important questions about the optimal timing of surveillance imaging following LAA occlusion. The ESC recommends performing a transthoracic echocardiogram prior to discharge to confirm proper device positioning and to exclude pericardial effusion.11 Additionally, surveillance imaging with TOE or cardiac CT angiography is advised 6–12 weeks post-procedure to assess for DRT and peri-device leaks.11 Given that late (>12 months) occurrences of DRT have been reported, repeat imaging at 12 months or later may be considered.14

Recent studies highlight variability in DRT detection based on imaging modalities and the timing of follow-up.15 Differentiating normal endothelialization from actual thrombus formation on the device can be challenging; however, mobile or protruding thrombi are considered more susceptible to embolization, necessitating reinforced anticoagulation therapy.15 Given the known risk of thrombus recurrence after DRT, continuous imaging surveillance is crucial to detect reformation and guide long-term anticoagulation strategies. Thrombus recurrence after DRT has been observed in a significant proportion of patients after LAA closure.16 Asmarats et al.16 reported that DRT reoccured in 35%.

The treatment of DRT remains a topic of ongoing debate, with no universally accepted standardized approach. Anticoagulation is the primary treatment strategy, with observational data suggesting that it can resolve DRT in over 90% of cases. However, the optimal duration of anticoagulation therapy and the risk of recurrence upon cessation remain uncertain.

Conclusion

The late formation of a thrombus on a LAA occluder device highlights the limitations of current devices and the need for extended surveillance beyond the initial post-procedural period. The literature and our experience suggest that in selected high-risk patients, cautious reintroduction of anticoagulation therapy may be beneficial. This case advocates for further research to optimize device designs, develop standardized long-term monitoring protocols, and refine management strategies in this complex patient population.
