Positivity rate of Aspergillus spp. in patients with tuberculosis-like symptoms in Yaoundé, Cameroon and antifungal resistance profile

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulmonary aspergillosis represents a significant public health concern resulting from the proliferation of Aspergillus species within the respiratory tract. This infection is frequently underdiagnosed in comparison to tuberculosis during the management of pulmonary infections, especially among individuals living with HIV. The objective of this study was to determine the positivity rate of Aspergillus spp. from sputum and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) samples of patients presenting with tuberculosis-like symptoms at Jamot Hospital in Yaoundé, Cameroon, and to evaluate their antifungal resistance profiles.MethodsA total of 114 samples (104 sputum and 10 bronchoalveolar lavage) were collected for mycological analysis. Data were verified, coded, and analyzed using StatView v5.0 and GraphPad v8.0.2 for Windows. Pearson’s chi-square test of independence and multivariate logistic regression models were used to compare percentages, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsAspergillus spp. were detected in 12.3% of samples, with two species identified. Aspergillus niger was predominant (57%0), followed by Aspergillus flavus (29%). A. niger isolates tested (5) exhibited resistance to polyenes, with 80% resistant to amphotericin B and 40% to nystatin, and 20% resistant to the azole fluconazole. All A. niger isolates were susceptible to econazole and clotrimazole. Among the five A. flavus isolates tested, all demonstrated resistance to amphotericin B and fluconazole, 60% to nystatin, and 20% to clotrimazole.ConclusionAspergillus spp. is present in the respiratory tract but often overlooked, and can lead to a misdiagnosis of tuberculosis. Although the very small number of isolates tested, Aspergillus spp. could be also affected to the antimicrobial resistance observed in recent years.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-12990-8.

Background

Aspergillosis is an infection caused by microscopic fungi of the genus Aspergillus, whose airborne spores are readily inhaled and disseminated by humans [1]. As opportunistic pathogens, these fungi typically do not cause disease in immunocompetent individuals. Individuals with compromised immune systems, such as those with tuberculosis, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), cancer, or diabetes, are at increased risk [2].

The World Health Organization (WHO) prioritizes fungal pathogens based on their capacity to cause acute or subacute systemic infections and on resistance to drugs or other treatments [3]. This framework classifies fungal pathogens into three priority groups, with Aspergillus placed in the highest, or critical, group alongside pathogens such as Cryptococcus neoformans, Aspergillus fumigatus, Candida auris, and Candida albicans [3].

Aspergillus spp and Mycobacterium tuberculosis are both involved in lower respiratory tract infections; although M. tuberculosis is often highlighted in the infectious process, Aspergillus acts silently but presents the same symptomatology as M. tuberculosis, thus leading to diagnostic errors [1]. Aspergillus fumigatus is the most commonly isolated species, responsible for 80–90% of invasive Aspergillus-related infections [4]. In 70–80% of cases, the pathogen invades the lung parenchyma, resulting in pulmonary aspergillosis. The infection may subsequently disseminate via the bloodstream, causing significant damage to vital organs, including the brain, heart, and skin [5].

Mycotoxins, which are low-molecular-weight secondary metabolites, have been linked to Aspergillus virulence and the associated organ damage [6]. The global prevalence of pulmonary aspergillosis varies by type, socioeconomic conditions, and the presence of comorbid lung diseases, particularly tuberculosis, which facilitates its growth [7]. Aspergillus species are responsible for more than 300,000 cases of invasive aspergillosis worldwide each year [8]. Mortality rates increase significantly with azole-resistant A. fumigatus infections, ranging from 47% to 88%, and in some reports, reaching 100% [2].

The clinical presentation of pulmonary aspergillosis closely resembles that of tuberculosis. Consequently, pulmonary aspergillosis is frequently overlooked in the management of pulmonary infections among both HIV-positive and HIV-negative patients, in contrast to tuberculosis [9]. This underdiagnosis was demonstrated in a French study, which found that 35.7% of tuberculosis cases had a concomitant positive culture for Aspergillus spp., yet only 6% received treatment for pulmonary aspergillosis [10].

In sub-Saharan Africa, the resurgence of tuberculosis during the HIV epidemic has contributed to increased susceptibility to aspergillosis [11]. In Cameroon, data on pulmonary aspergillosis remain limited, primarily derived from two studies. The first, conducted in Buea, reported a 15% prevalence of Aspergillus in the sputum of patients with suspected tuberculosis [12]. The second identified a low prevalence (2.4%) of chronic obstructive pulmonary infections among HIV-positive patients in Yaoundé [13].

The present study aims to determine the positivity rate of Aspergillus spp in patients presenting with tubercolsis-like symptoms and to assess the antifungal resistance profile of some Aspergillus isolates.

Methods

Study design and setting

Study area

A cross-sectional study was conducted over six-month period (from July to December 2023). Participants were recruited at the Jamot Hospital in Yaoundé (HJY), and the collected samples were analyzed at the Bacteriology Laboratory of the Military Hospital in Yaoundé (MHY),

Both HJY and MHY are referral hospitals in Cameroon. HJY specializes in the management of major endemic diseases, particularly respiratory diseases such as tuberculosis. MHY, located on Reunification Boulevard in the annex of the Ministry of Defense of Cameroon, also specializes in the care and treatment of various pathologies, including respiratory tract infections. Both hospitals are situated in Yaoundé, the capital of the Centre Region, approximately 200 km from the Atlantic coast, at a latitude of 4° North and a longitude of 11°35’ East. Yaoundé covers an area of 475,777 km2 and has a population of 4,509,000 inhabitants [14]. Recent statistics indicate that the prevalence of HIV infection was approximately 18% in 2020 [15].

The sample size was calculated based on a 15% prevalence reported in a study conducted in Buea, Cameroon, on respiratory aspergillosis in the sputum of patients suspected of tuberculosis (9). Participant recruitment and sample collection

Any patient hospitalized at Bronchoscopy and Pulmonology departments of HJY who met the following criteria was included in the study: (1) any patient with symptoms similar to tuberculosis (cough, anorexia, fever, dyspnea), not under antifungal treatment; (2) any patient whose chest X-ray suggested tuberculosis with a prescription for a sputum examination or bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL); (3) any patient who gave consent to participate in the study. Any patient who did not meet the conditions mentioned above was excluded from our study. Each participant’s enrolment was preceded by the signing of an informed consent form during a confidential interview, which aimed to explain the importance of the study and to collect sociodemographic and clinical informations using a previously established questionnaire.

After explaining the sputum collection protocol to the participants, they were given a sterile, sealed container to collect the sputum themselves upon waking, on an empty stomach, before brushing their teeth. A specialized physician performed the BAL sampling; after explaining the sampling process to the participants, he performed a fibroscopic puncture to obtain higher-quality samples, which were placed in sterile, hermetically sealed pots. For both sample types, we followed the procedure described by Saadi et al. [16]. All samples thus collected were immediately transported to the laboratory in a cooler under strict aseptic conditions for analysis.

Mycological analysis of samples

Isolation and identification of Aspergillus spp.

Sputum and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) samples were inoculated onto Sabouraud agar supplemented with chloramphenicol and transferred into test tubes. The tubes were incubated at 37 °C for 2 days to 1 week, as previously described [16]. Suspected colonies were subcultured onto Sabouraud agar with chloramphenicol and subsequently transferred to Petri dishes at 37 °C to obtain pure colonies. The initial cultures were carried out in test tubes to limit contamination often observed in fungal culture. In addition, we handled the samples under a microbiological safety hood.

Quality control of culture medium was performed by testing sterility and fertility process using the reference strain of A. flavus ATCC available in laboratory. Macroscopic identification of Aspergillus was based primarily on colony cultural characteristics, including morphological appearance, pigmentation, coloration on both the upper and reverse sides, growth rate, and mycelial features [17]. For microscopic identification, a few drops of methylene blue were placed on a sterile slide, adhesive tape was applied to the mold culture, and the culture was affixed to the slide. The preparation was examined under an optical microscope at 40x magnification to identify structures characteristic of each species: (1) conidiophores (color, structure, vesicle) and (2) properties of the Aspergillus head (vesicle, phialides and metulae, conidia) [16]. The lack of molecular identification is a limitation in this study.

Detection of aflatoxin-producing Aspergillus isolates

Aflatoxin-producing isolates were detected by the coconut test. Briefly, fresh and mature isolates of pure molds were inoculated onto Petri dishes containing 20 mL of CEA (Coconut Extract Agar). Then, the dishes were incubated at 25 °C for 3 to 7 days, and fluorescence was measured under ultraviolet light (365 nm) using a UV lamp. It has been shown that aflatoxin diffusion zones exhibit blue fluorescence under ultraviolet radiation [18].

Acid-Fast Bacilli (AFB) detection by Ziehl-neelsen staining

To determine participants’ status regarding M. tuberculosis infection, we performed the gold standard in the diagnosis of tuberculosis: Ziehl-Neelsen staining of the samples using the previously employed protocol (18). The stained slides were observed under a 100x optical microscope to detect acid-fast bacilli (AFB), which appear red-violet against a blue background [19].

Aspergillus antifungal susceptibility test

Preparation of fungal inoculum

The inoculum was prepared from fresh, mature cultures (2–5 days old) on potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates at 35 °C. In some cases, prolonged incubation (up to 10 days) was required for good sporulation of the isolates. The colonies were overlaid with 1 mL of sterile distilled water supplemented with 1% tween 20 to expose conidia or spores. These conidia were carefully collected using a micropipette and transferred to a sterile tube. The suspension was then homogenized for 15 s using a vortex mixer. The inoculum was filtered using a sterile nylon filter (11 μm mesh) to retain hyphal clumps. This step was repeated as many times as necessary to obtain an inoculum with fewer than 5% fungal structures other than conidia, as determined using the hemocytometer (30). The inoculum was adjusted by adding sterile distilled water to a spore concentration of 1 × 106 CFU/mL for the initial inoculum. A 1:10 dilution with sterile distilled water was made to obtain a final working inoculum of 1 × 105 CFU/mL [20]. The different concentrations were determined using a hemocytometer (Malassez cell). Due to budget constraints, only 5 isolates of each identified species will be tested.

Preparation of antifungal solutions and determination of MICs

In this exploratory study, five commercially available antifungal agents, commonly prescribed for the treatment of fungal infections, were selected: Amphotericin B (10 g/mL oral suspension), Nystatin (105 µg/mL oral suspension), Fluconazole (10 mg/mL powder for oral suspension), Econazole (30 g powder for oral suspension for 0.3 g), and Clotrimazole (100 g powder for oral suspension for 1 g). A stock concentration (64 µg/mL) was prepared from each molecule using potato dextrose broth (PDB) and series of ten two-fold microdilutions was performed in 96-well microtiter plates according to the EUCAST 2022 guidelines [20]. Each well containing 100 µL of PDB and for each antifungal agent and isolate, 100 µL of the stock solution (32 µg/mL) was added to the first well to achieve a concentration of 16 µg/mL, followed by serial dilutions to reach a final concentration of 0.031 µg/mL. Subsequently, 100 µL of spore suspension (1 × 105 spores/mL) was added to all wells except well 11, which served as the negative control (PDB plus 100 µL of antifungal). Well 12 functioned as the positive control and contained 100 µL of spore suspension and 100 µL of PDB. The microplates were sealed with film and incubated at 35 °C for 48 h as recommended EUCAST 2022 [20]. Each antifungal sensitivity test was conducted in triplicate for every Aspergillus strain. Quality controls were performed using the reference strain of A. flavus ATCC available in laboratory.

Statistical analysis

Data collected were entered into an Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft Office 2016, USA), verified, and coded for consistency. Statistical analysis was performed using Statview v5.0 for Windows (SAS Institute, Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA) and GraphPad v8.0.2 for Windows (GraphPad PRISM, IBM, Inc., San Diego, California, USA). Qualitative variables were presented as frequency (n) and percentages (%), while quantitative variables were presented as means ± standard deviation (SD).

Pearson’s chi-square test of independence and Fisher’s exact test were used to compare percentages, with a statistical significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were constructed to assess the strength of the association between the dependent variable (Aspergillus spp. carriage) and the explanatory variables of the models (e.g., gender, age, clinical signs, etc.). This strength was estimated by calculating the crude odds ratio (ORb) and adjusted odds ratio (ORa), their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), and the threshold for statistical significance. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of participants

The final sample size of 114 compared to the calculated 196 in this study can be explained by the fact that some sampling such as BAL is devastating and requires highly qualified personnel for its execution, which in our context is low; also due to the inability of some participants to provide respiratory samples. Furthermore, we received no funding for this study; consequently, our budget was very limited for this pioneering research. A total of 114 out of 120 contacted participants were recruited, comprising 104 individuals from the pulmonology department (91.2%) and 10 from the bronchoscopy department (8.8%). The sample included 62 females (54.4%) and 52 males (45.6%), resulting in a male-to-female sex ratio of 0.83. Participant ages ranged from 19 to 80 years, with a mean ± SD of 41.6 ± 11.5. Individuals aged 30–40 years represented 31.6% of the sample. Ten occupational categories were identified, with small traders (21.3%) and housewives (20.2%) being the most common (Table 1). Clinical presentations varied, with asthenia (74.6%), cough (73.7%), anorexia (52.6%), and hemoptysis (17.5%) being the most frequently reported symptoms (Fig. 1).

Aspergillus spp. positivity and species identified

Among 114 samples analysed in this study (104 sputum and 10 BAL), 14 (12.3%, 95% CI 7.5–19.6%) were positive for Aspergillus spp. (Fig. 2A). Depending on the sample type, the proportion of Aspergillus spp. was 10.6% in sputum and 30% in BAL samples (Fig. 2A). two species were identified, namely A. flavus and A. niger, observed as single infections or co-infections. A. niger identified in 8 samples (57%) was predominant compared to A. flavus identified in 4 samples (29%); 2 cases (14%) of co-infection were observed (Figs. 2B).

Macroscopic examination revealed that A. flavus samples exhibited a white color on the reverse, progressing to yellow or yellow-green, and on the reverse displayed dark red, pinkish to brown, or colorless hues (Fig. 3A and B). In contrast, A. niger samples showed a white-yellow to blackish coloration and a granular texture on the front, with a pale yellow to colorless appearance on the reverse (Fig. 3C and D).

This initial identification based on macroscopic observation was confirmed using microscopic characterization under a 40x objective on slides prepared using the Scotch tape test method by identifying the specific structures for each species. A. niger: Aspergillus head: Biseriate, radiate; Conidiophore: Long (1.5–3 mm), wide (15–20 μm), smooth, colorless to brown; Vesicles: Spherical (30–100 μm); Conidia: Large and globose (3.5–5 μm), brown, echinulate (Fig. 3G-H). A. flavus: Aspergillus head: Uni or biseriate, radiate; Conidiophore: Long (up to 2.5 mm), often verrucous, colorless, thick-walled; Vesicles: Spherical (25–45 μm); Conidia: Large (3.5–4.5 μm) globose, pale green, echinulate (Fig. 3E-F).

Detection of aflatoxin-producing Aspergillus isolates

Fourteen isolates, including 8 A. niger and 6 A. flavus, were tested using the Coco test to detect aflatoxin production. Six isolates tested positive for aflatoxin production, including 3 A. niger (30%) (Fig. 4A) and 3 A. flavus (50%) (Fig. 4B).

AFB positivity

Ziehl Neelson staining revealed red rods on a blue background under a 100-lens optical microscope. Thus, 21 out of 114 sputum samples (including 104 sputum samples and 10 BAL) analyzed for AFB were positive, yielding a 18.42% positivity rate. No AFB was detected in the BAL samples. In addition, 2 cases of tuberculosis-aspergillosis coinfection (1.92%) were reported; therefore, 19 out of 104 sputum samples (18.26%) were positive for tuberculosis alone, and 83 were negative (79.80%) (Fig. 4C).

Association between aspergillosis positivity and participant characteristics

Pearson’s chi-square tests of independence were used to examine the association between aspergillosis positivity and participants’ sociodemographic and clinical features. All significance threshold values (P) were above 0.05, indicating no statistical link between aspergillosis and participant characteristics (Table 2).

Antifungal susceptibility testing of Aspergillus isolates

Ten isolates, including five from each Aspergillus species identified in this study, were tested for susceptibility to five antifungals belonging to two classes: polyenes (amphotericin B and nystatin) and azoles (fluconazole, econazole, and clotrimazole). Since antifungal susceptibility varies by class, we considered an isolate susceptible to the azole class if the MIC was less than 8 µg/mL, and to the polyene class if the MIC was less than 2 µg/mL [20].

According to [21], as isolates are subjected to antifungals of the azole and polyene families, the MBC cannot be calculated, thus the strains will be + considered resistant or susceptible solely on the basis of the MIC. A. flavus isolates were resistant to these two classes of antifungals, i.e. 100% (n = 5) resistance to amphotericin B and 60% (N = 3) to nystatin (polyenes); 100% (N = 5) to fluconazole, 60% (N = 3) to econazole and 20% to clotrimazole (azoles). Four A. niger isolates were resistant to amphotericin B (80%) and two isolates were resistant to nystatin (40%). Discussion

Our study included 114 samples collected at HJY, including 104 sputum samples and 10 BAL samples. We obtained Aspergillus spp. positivity rate of 12.3%, which is higher than that reported by Pefura-Yone & Fodjeu in 2015 (2.4%) in Yaoundé for chronic pulmonary aspergillosis in HIV-positive patients from the African continent [13]. Several parameters could explain this discrepancy, including the methodological approaches used in the two studies, the nature of the study populations, the sample sizes, and confusion at the diagnostic level with tuberculosis.

Aspergillus spp. positivity rate in sputum was higher than that of BAL. The significant difference in the numbers between these two types of samples would account for the observed discrepancy in positivity. This can be explained by the fact that the BAL is an examination that is rarely requested of the patient because it is painful to perform and requires a great deal of expertise, unlike the sputum examination. Three BAL samples were positive out of 10 analysed, compared with 11 positive sputum samples out of 104 analysed; this suggests that BAL samples are better indicated for the detection of positivity rate of Aspergillus spp.. This is supported by Guegan et al. [22], who, using PCR, found A. fumigatus in 60% and 52% of BAL and sputum samples, respectively.

The most frequently identified Aspergillus species was A. niger, 57%, compared with 29% for A. flavus, unlike the study by Balajee et al., where A. fumigatus was the dominant isolated species [23]. In contrast, two studies reported A. flavus dominance [24, 25]. Geographical and population-driven epidemiological specificities could explain this difference in the profile of dominant fungi.

Faurteen isolates, including 8 A. niger and 6 A. flavus, were subjected to the Coco test to detect aflatoxin production. Six isolates tested positive for aflatoxin production, including 3 A. niger (37%) and 3 A. flavus (50%), which is consistent with the results obtained by the Meijer et al. ’s team on maize, cereals, animal feed, and sorghum [26]. The high rate of aflatoxin production by A. flavus and A. niger isolates could be due to dietary factors. These foods, which could serve as reservoirs of mycotoxins, are an essential part of the dietary habits of Cameroonian populations, including our study population. This assumption is worth investigating in our setting over the coming years.

The results of the chi-square test showed no statistically significant link between Aspergillus positivity and participant parameters, as the p-value was greater than 0.05 (P > 0.05). Multivariate logistic analysis revealed an association between Aspergillus carriage and participant age; the risk of Aspergillus spp. carriage was reduced by 91% (ORa = 0.09; 95% CI 0.01–0.90; p = 0.04) in participants over 50 years of age compared to patients under 30 years of age. This may be explained by the fact that, in our context, young people may have less frequent sexual activity and therefore a higher risk of contracting HIV and subsequently opportunistic infections such Aspergillosis colonization. Previous study was reported association between several factors and development of invasive Aspergillosis, including: chronic renal failure (OR 2.1; 95% CI [1.17; 3.68], p = 0.013), history of infection or colonization with Aspergillus sp (OR 12.2; 95% CI [2.73; 54.37], p = 0.001), … [27].

Antifungal assays demonstrated that 80% and 40% of A. niger isolates were resistant to amphotericin B and nystatin, respectively, both polyene antifungals. Only one isolate showed resistance to azole antifungals, while all isolates remained sensitive to econazole and clotrimazole. This observation aligns with previous findings [28]. The observed resistance to polyenes may be attributed to the presence of resistance genes, which are more frequently detected in A. niger compared to other species within the genus, particularly in relation to spore production. For A. flavus, 100% of isolates were resistant to amphotericin B and 60% to nystatin, supporting the findings of McGinnis et al., who reported high amphotericin B resistance rates (up to 80%) in A. flavus [29]. Although the sample size in this study is limited, the results indicate reduced efficacy of polyene antifungals for the treatment of aspergillosis, likely due to the acquisition of resistance phenotypes. Among azole antifungals, all A. flavus isolates tested were resistant to fluconazole, with 60% resistant to econazole and 20% to clotrimazole. These findings are consistent with those reported in other studies [30]. This suggests that Aspergillus spp. strains, like all other microorganisms, are experiencing the emergence of antimicrobial resistance observed in recent years.

Conclusion

The objective of this study was to determine the positivity rate of Aspergillus spp. among Cameroonian patients with suspected tuberculosis and to evaluate the antifungal resistance profiles of some isolates. A positivity rate of 12.3% was found, and two species, A. niger and A. flavus were identified. Some isolates of these two species tested showed resistance to antifungals from the polyene family, such as amphotericin B and nystatin, as well as to azoles, including fluconazole.

The findings indicate that Cameroonian patients were carriers of Aspergillus spp. which could induce tuberculosis-like symptom and may result in misdiagnosis. Additional studies are warranted in Cameroon to assess the true risk of Aspergillus spp cariage, particularly among immunocompromised individuals, given the increased likelihood of co-infections with opportunistic pathogens and resistance to frontline antimicrobial.
