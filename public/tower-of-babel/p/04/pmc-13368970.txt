Suture-suspension single-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy versus conventional multiport laparoscopic cholecystectomy: a single-center, prospective, randomized controlled clinical study
Authors: Yingan Zhao, Shuai Han, Jinghui Zhang, Yini Xu, Jianfeng Lu, Siming Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSingle-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy (SILC) shows superior cosmetic outcomes but greater technical difficulty. This study aimed to compare the short-term outcomes of the suture-suspension SILC versus conventional conventional multiport laparoscopic cholecystectomy (CMLC) and to analyze the risk factors of difficult SILC.MethodsFrom October 2023 to September 2024, 120 patients with symptomatic cholelithiasis were enrolled and randomly assigned to either the suture-suspension SILC group (60 patients) or the conventional CMLC group (60 patients). The primary outcome was operative time. Secondary outcomes included intra-operative blood loss, postoperative hospitalization duration, time to first flatus, 24-hour postoperative pain score, incidence of postoperative complications (bile leakage, hemorrhage, wound infection), and subjective satisfaction.ResultsCompared to the conventional CMLC, suture-suspension SILC showed no significant differences in operative time (P > 0.05) or intra-operative blood loss (P > 0.05). However, the time to first flatus (1.20 ± 0.443 days), duration of postoperative hospitalization (1.58 ± 0.889 days), and the 24-hour postoperative pain score (3.92 ± 1.225) in the suture-suspension SILC group were all significantly lower than those in the CMLC group (P ≤ 0.05). The subjective satisfaction score (3.98 ± 0.431 points) in the suture-suspension SILC group was significantly higher than that in the CMLC group (3.32 ± 0.469 points, P < 0.001). No postoperative complications occurred in either group. Thickened gallbladder wall, elevated white blood cell (WBC) count, and pericholecystic effusion were identified as factors associated with the operative time of SILC (P ≤ 0.05). A gallbladder wall thickness exceeding 4.375 mm was associated with an operative time longer than the average for the suture-suspension SILC procedure.ConclusionThe suture-suspension method renders SILC short-term outcomes comparable to those of conventional CMLC. However, the choice of surgical technique should be made cautiously in cases of severe gallbladder inflammation.Keywords: Patient satisfaction, Postoperative recovery, Single-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy, Suture-suspension SILC, Three-port laparoscopic cholecystectomy

Introduction

Single-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy (SILC), as an emerging surgical technique, has gradually gained worldwide attention and recognition. Numerous Chinese and international studies indicate that, compared to conventional multiport laparoscopic cholecystectomy (CMLC), SILC shows advantages such as reduced trauma, less postoperative pain, and superior cosmetic outcomes [1, 2]. However, some scholars have raised concerns that this technique may be associated with several issues, including a longer operative time, increased intra-operative blood loss, and a higher rate of postoperative complications [3, 4]. The enhanced technical difficulty has also limited the widespread application of SILC. Despite these concerns, the potential benefits of SILC in accelerating postoperative recovery and improving patient satisfaction warrant further investigation. To date, evidence from prospective randomized controlled trials comparing SILC with conventional three-port laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) remains limited, particularly regarding the role of auxiliary techniques in reducing technical difficulty. Furthermore, few studies have systematically analyzed the preoperative risk factors associated with prolonged operative time in SILC, such as gallbladder wall thickness, white blood cell count, and pericholecystic effusion, which are critical for patient selection.

In response, our institution applied the suture-suspension method to SILC and conducted a prospective randomized controlled study to compare with conventional three-port laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC), aiming to investigate the safety and feasibility of the suture-suspension SILC procedure.

Materials and methods

Study data

In this prospective study, patients who underwent “laparoscopic cholecystectomy” for “cholelithiasis with cholecystitis” at The First Affiliated Hospital of Ningbo University from October 2023 to September 2024 were enrolled. The sample size was calculated using PASS software (version 15.0) based on the primary outcome of operative time. According to our preliminary pilot study results, the mean operative time for conventional CMLC was 45.6 ± 8.3 min, and we considered a difference of 6 min in operative time between the two groups as clinically significant. With a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05 and a power (1-β) of 0.80, the calculated sample size was 52 patients per group. Considering a 15% potential dropout rate, we planned to enroll 60 patients per group. In sequential order of admission, patients were randomly assigned by the numerical sequence method to either the clinical group (underwent suture-suspension SILC) or the control group (underwent conventional CMLC), with 60 patients in each group. To minimize selection bias, the random allocation sequence was generated by a statistician not involved in patient enrollment or surgery using a computer-generated random number table. Allocation concealment was achieved using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE), which were opened only after patient enrollment was confirmed. Given the nature of the surgical procedures, blinding of surgeons was not feasible; however, outcome assessors and statisticians were blinded to group allocation. Additionally, data analysts were kept unaware of the grouping information until the final analysis was completed. Inclusion criteria: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria for cholelithiasis [5], with age of 18–80 (inclusive) years old; (2) indicative for surgery and physically tolerable for surgery; (3) accepting surgical treatment and voluntary to participate in the clinical study. Exclusion criteria: (1) presence of common bile duct stones or pancreatitis; (2) highly preoperative suspicion of gallbladder malignancy; (3) uncorrected coagulation disorders; (4) pregnancy or lactation. Informed consent was obtained from all patients and their families for this study. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Ningbo University (Approval No.: Ningbo First Hospital Ethics Review 2023 Research No. 034).

Method

Control group: Conventional CMLC was performed. Patients were placed in the supine position, and general anesthesia was induced following endotracheal intubation. A 10-mm incision was made at the lower edge of the umbilicus for the insertion of a puncture needle and establishment of pneumoperitoneum (parameters: CO2 flow rate 40 L/min, intra-abdominal pressure 10–12 mmHg). A 10-mm trocar was introduced through the infraumbilical incision. Subsequently, two additional 5-mm incisions were made under the xiphoid process and below the right costal margin at the midclavicular line, respectively, for the placement of 5-mm trocars. Laparoscopic exploration of the abdominal cavity was performed. After confirming no abnormalities, the gallbladder was retracted with a non-traumatic grasper held in the left hand to expose Calot’s triangle. A hook cautery and dissecting forceps, used alternately in the right hand, were employed to isolate the cystic duct and cystic artery. These structures were then clipped with polymer clips and transected. The gallbladder was isolated completely from the liver bed, placed into a retrieval bag, and extracted by slightly enlarging the observation port incision. The incisions were then closed layer by layer, with the skin approximated via subcuticular suture. The operative time and intra-operative blood loss were accurately recorded.

Clinical group: Suture-suspension transumbilical SILC was performed. Patients were placed in the supine position, and general anesthesia was induced following endotracheal intubation. A 15-mm incision was made at the lower edge of the umbilicus. The abdominal cavity was entered layer by layer, a single-port trocar was placed, and pneumoperitoneum was established (parameters: CO2 flow rate 40 L/min, intra-abdominal pressure 10–12 mmHg). The lower channel of the trocar served as the camera port, while the upper channel served as the operating port. Following laparoscopic exploration of the abdominal cavity, a straight needle with a No. 7 suture was introduced. The seromuscular layer of the gallbladder fundus was sutured laparoscopically. The needle was then externalized through the abdominal wall below the costal margin to retract the gallbladder and the inferior liver edge. Another straight needle with a No. 7 suture was introduced through the abdominal wall to the right of the xiphoid, used to suture the seromuscular layer of the gallbladder infundibulum. After tying the suture, the needle was externalized at the level of the umbilicus on the right anterior axillary line. By traction on these sutures, the anterior and posterior aspects of Calot’s triangle were exposed (see Fig. 1). A hook cautery and dissecting forceps, used alternately, were employed to isolate the cystic duct and cystic artery. These structures were then clipped with polymer clips and transected. The gallbladder was completely separated from the liver bed and extracted through the single port. The umbilical incision was closed layer by layer, with the skin approximated via subcuticular suture. The operative time and intra-operative blood loss were accurately recorded.

Surgical observation indicators and evaluation

Preoperative imaging measurements: All patients underwent preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) within one week before surgery. Gallbladder wall thickness was measured on axial T2-weighted images at the level of the gallbladder body, defined as the maximum distance from the mucosal surface to the serosal surface, measured in millimeters (mm). Gallbladder size (length) was measured on coronal images as the maximum longitudinal diameter from the gallbladder fundus to the neck, also measured in millimeters (mm). All measurements were performed independently by two radiologists blinded to the study protocol, and the average value was used for analysis.

(1) Indicators of surgical efficacy included: Operative time: defined as the time interval from skin incision to completion of skin closure, measured in minutes. Intra-operative blood loss: defined as the total estimated blood loss during the procedure, measured in milliliters, estimated by the suction canister volume and gauze weighing method. Postoperative recovery indicators included: Postoperative hospitalization duration: defined as the time interval from the date of surgery to the date of discharge, measured in days. Time to first flatus: defined as the time interval from the completion of surgery to the first passage of flatus postoperatively, measured in days. 24-hour postoperative pain score: defined as the patient-reported pain level at 24 h after surgery, assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst possible pain). Incidence of postoperative complications: defined as the proportion of patients experiencing any of the following events within 30 days postoperatively: bile leakage (presence of bilious fluid in the abdominal cavity or drainage), hemorrhage (bleeding requiring transfusion or reoperation), or wound infection (purulent discharge from the incision site with positive culture). Hospitalization costs: defined as the total direct medical expenses incurred during hospitalization, measured in Chinese Yuan (RMB). (2) Patient experience evaluation: Patient satisfaction with the incision was assessed via telephone follow-up 1–3 months postoperatively. Patients were asked to rate their satisfaction based on the physical impact of the surgery and the surgical scar, using a 5-point scale (1–5): very dissatisfied, dissatisfied, satisfied, quite satisfied, and very satisfied. Satisfaction score was defined as the patient-reported rating on this 5-point scale.

Statistical methods

Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 27.0). Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the t-test; enumeration data were expressed as rates and compared using the chi-square test, with the test level set at α = 0.05. Binary logistic regression analysis was employed to analyze factors influencing the operative time of SILC (prolonged operative time defined as operative time exceeding the mean operative time of the SILC group). The results were presented as regression coefficients (B), standard deviations, odds ratios (OR = Exp(B)), and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the OR, with a P value ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to examine factors related to the operative time of SILC, and the area under the curve (AUC) with its 95% CI was calculated. The Youden index method was applied to calculate the critical value for independent variables and to determine the optimal cutoff value.

Results

The CONSORT flow diagram of the study is presented in Fig. 2. A total of 120 patients were assessed for eligibility, all of whom met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled. No patients were excluded at the screening stage. The 120 patients were randomly assigned to either the suture-suspension SILC group (n = 60) or the conventional CMLC group (n = 60). All patients received the allocated intervention, and none were lost to follow-up or discontinued the intervention. All 120 patients were included in the final analysis.

Incidence of complications

No severe postoperative complications, such as biliary stricture, intra-abdominal infection, bile leakage, or hemorrhage, occurred in either patient group.

Perioperative outcomes of the two groups

No significant differences were observed between the clinical group and the control group regarding operative time (53.47 ± 8.544 min vs. 48.83 ± 9.186 min) or intra-operative blood loss (6.63 ± 1.311 mL vs. 6.88 ± 1.446 mL). Factors related to operative time in SILC

Univariate logistic regression analysis results indicated that gallbladder wall thickness (OR = 21.63, 95% CI: 2.481–188.718, P = 0.005), the presence of pericholecystic effusion (OR = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.046–0.860, P = 0.031), and white blood cell count (OR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.041–1.803, P = 0.025) were risk factors for a prolonged operative time in SILC. In contrast, gallbladder size (OR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.146–1.344, P = 0.151), the presence of an impacted stone in the gallbladder neck (OR = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.085–1.314, P = 0.117), BMI (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 0.942–1.543, P = 0.138), preoperative total bilirubin (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.986–1.189, P = 0.097), preoperative direct bilirubin (OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 0.956–1.370, P = 0.140), preoperative indirect bilirubin (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.952–1.225, P = 0.234), preoperative ALT (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.979–1.011, P = 0.526), preoperative AST (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.979–1.030, P = 0.750), preoperative prothrombin time (OR = 1.54, 95% CI: 0.887–2.657, P = 0.126), and preoperative albumin (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 0.870–1.199, P = 0.793) were not identified as risk factors for prolonged SILC operative time. Multivariate logistic regression analysis results identified thickened gallbladder wall as an independent risk factor for prolonged operative time in SILC (OR = 14.86, 95% CI: 1.574–140.136, P = 0.018). Preoperative white blood cell count (OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 0.808–1.568, P = 0.458) and pericholecystic effusion (OR = 2.13, 95% CI: 0.325–13.896, P = 0.431) were not identified as independent risk factors in the multivariate analysis (Table 4). ROC curve analysis using the Youden index showed that a gallbladder wall thickness exceeding 4.375 mm significantly influenced the operative time of SILC (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Driven by increasing aesthetic demands and advancements in medical technology, SILC has been progressively adopted and implemented in major medical institutions. SILC is technically more challenging than CMLC, as it consolidates the multiple dispersed operating ports of traditional LC into a single umbilical incision. Large-scale data reports indicate that, compared to CMLC, SILC faces the following challenges: (1) the “coaxial view” where the limited visual field shares the same axis as the operating instruments; (2) the “chopstick effect”, referring to interference between instruments due to their coaxial arrangement; and (3) “restricted exposure” of the surgical field caused by the coaxial instruments, thereby increasing surgical risks [6–8]. The suture-suspension SILC technique we employed utilizes sutures to suspend the gallbladder fundus and infundibulum. This method replaces the grasping forceps used in conventional LC, thereby fully exposing Calot’s triangle while avoiding the problem of instrument conflict inherent in multi-port approaches. Compared to SILC procedures utilizing two grasping forceps, this technique allows the operating surgeon to control the laparoscope with one hand. This enables the operating surgeon to adjust the visual field according to his/her needs and preferences during the procedure. The suture-suspension method reduces the number of operating ports required, further minimizing the total incision length. Concurrently, it imposes fewer technical demands on the surgical assistant.

Although multiple studies have demonstrated the advantages of SILC in terms of postoperative recovery and patient satisfaction, comparisons between different techniques and methodologies remain contentious. In particular, a consensus has yet to be reached on the specific technical execution of SILC, and the application of the suture-suspension method still lacks systematic data support. In previous studies, the operative time for SILC was often longer than that for CMLC [3, 4, 9]. Some literature suggests that the suture-suspension method can effectively simplify the surgical process, yielding comparable or even superior outcomes to CMLC regarding operative time, intra-operative blood loss, and postoperative recovery [10, 11]. However, these findings were not supported by prospective studies, and patient selection was subject to statistical bias. Our study is prospective in design, with two groups of patients randomized by numerical sequence, avoiding the influence of surgeon subjectivity in case selection on the study results. This study yielded the following findings: (1) Regarding surgical feasibility, no statistically significant difference was observed in operative time between the suture-suspension SILC and CMLC. The authors posit that an effective gallbladder suspension technique can adequately replace traditional grasping forceps in exposing Calot’s triangle, potentially offering the primary surgeon an even simpler and more expedient operative process. Furthermore, based on our institutional experience, the suture-suspension technique features a relatively short learning curve, allowing junior surgeons to achieve proficiency rapidly. (2) Concerning surgical safety, attributable to satisfactory exposure of Calot’s triangle, the suture-suspension SILC demonstrated no statistically significant differences compared to CMLC in terms of intra-operative blood loss or the incidence of postoperative complications. 3. Pertaining to postoperative recovery, the suture-suspension SILC proved superior to CMLC in measures of postoperative pain scores, duration of postoperative hospitalization, and patient satisfaction with the incision. These findings coincided with the results reported in other similar studies. Although the cost of the single-port trocar used in SILC is higher than that of the trocars used in CMLC, the total hospitalization costs showed no significant difference owing to the shorter hospital stay and reduced postoperative medication requirements in the SILC group.

But it is worth noting that SILC carries a certain probability of conversion to open surgery or the need for additional trocars during the procedure, which presents challenges to its success rate. A meta-analysis by Yamazaki et al. [12], encompassing 102 articles, reported that 7.2% of SILC procedures ultimately required conversion to laparotomy or addition of trocars. Similarly, a multicenter randomized controlled trial by Arezzo et al. [13] involving 600 patients reported a 5% conversion rate and a 6% rate of additional trocar placement in the SILC cohort. In the present study, only 2 out of 60 SILC cases required the addition of 1 trocar. This suggests that the success rate of our institution’s suture-suspension technique may be higher than rates cited in the literature [12, 13]. Furthermore, because gallbladder suspension replaces one grasping forceps, typically only one additional operating port under the xiphoid process is needed, still resulting in fewer total incisions than conventional CMLC. For institutions initially adopting the suture-suspension cholecystectomy technique, starting with a two-port approach, i.e., utilizing the subxiphoid incision for suturing the gallbladder infundibulum, can mitigate the interference between instruments and the laparoscope, thereby reducing the technical difficulty of the suturing step.

The recognition and avoidance of specific risk factors for SILC is crucial for enhancing procedural success rate and patient safety. Literature reports [14] identify previous preoperative drainage procedures (such as PTCD), histopathological findings (e.g., acute cholecystitis or empyema), the surgeon’s technical expertise, and a body mass index (BMI) > 30 kg/m2 as factors affecting surgical difficulty. Other scholars [15] have specifically indicated that a high BMI, acute cholecystitis, and a resident as the operator are major contributors to prolonged operative time in SILC. Among these, BMI and the severity of gallbladder inflammation are recurrent factors across multiple studies [14–16], although these studies often lack a quantitative analysis of inflammatory severity. This study performed a quantitative analysis of gallbladder inflammation, including gallbladder wall thickness, the presence of pericholecystic effusion, gallbladder size, the presence of an impacted stone in the gallbladder neck, and WBC count. The results demonstrated that a thickened gallbladder wall is an independent risk factor for prolonged operative time in SILC. Specifically, a gallbladder wall thickness exceeding 4.375 mm was associated with an operative time longer than the average for the SILC procedures in this study. Based on the surgical experience of our institution, increased gallbladder wall thickness and the presence of pericholecystic effusion significantly impair the exposure of Calot’s triangle during surgery, substantially elevating the procedural difficulty. This observation aligns with the assessment criteria for acute cholecystitis outlined in the Tokyo Guidelines [17]. However, in this study, BMI was not identified as a factor influencing SILC, which may be attributed to the absence of enrolled patients with high BMI (BMI > 30 kg/m2).

SILC presents a certain technical threshold and learning curve, a point highlighted in multiple studies [4, 6, 7] that recommend this procedure be undertaken by surgeons with adequate experience. The suture-suspension SILC also carries a technical prerequisite, demanding proficiency in laparoscopic suturing from the operating surgeon. It requires the precise suturing of the gallbladder’s seromuscular layer using a straight needle. Sutures placed too superficially risk cutting through the gallbladder tissue, while excessively deep sutures may puncture the gallbladder lumen, leading to intra-operative bile leakage. Our institution has practiced this technique for several years. Based on accumulated experience, if bile leakage occurs due to gallbladder puncture during the initial phase of the procedure, placing gauze beneath the operative area can effectively absorb the bile and prevent its dispersion. Furthermore, postoperative irrigation with saline solution and thorough drying with gauze are recommended. None of the 60 patients underwent SILC in this study experienced complications related to intra-operative bile leakage. Therefore, this procedure can be adopted by surgeons with a foundational skill in laparoscopic suturing and offers a relatively high margin for error during its initial implementation. Numerous publications [18, 19] also report that for surgeons proficient in laparoscopic techniques, SILC is associated with a relatively short learning curve. This study analyzed potential factors influencing the operative time of SILC and results suggested that gallbladder wall thickness and WBC count were correlated with prolonged SILC operative time. The operative time was significantly prolonged when the gallbladder wall thickness exceeded 4.62 mm. This finding can serve as a reference for novice surgeons planning SILC procedures. Caution should be exercised in selecting SILC for patients with elevated WBC count, pericholecystic inflammatory effusion, or a gallbladder wall thickness exceeding 4.62 mm.

Limitations of this study: First, incisional hernia is a well-recognized concern in SILC due to the enlarged umbilical fascial defect. The umbilical incision in SILC (15 mm) is larger than that in conventional CMLC (10 mm), which theoretically increases the risk of incisional hernia formation. The follow-up duration in this study was limited to 1–3 months postoperatively, which is insufficient to fully evaluate the incidence of incisional hernia, as these complications may present months or even years after surgery. Longer-term follow-up beyond 12 months is necessary to accurately assess the true incidence of incisional hernia following suture-suspension SILC. Second, Gallbladder wall thickness measurements were manually obtained by researchers from MRI images, potentially introducing minor measurement discrepancies. Third, this study was not prospectively registered in a clinical trial registry. We acknowledge these limitations. Future studies should adopt standardized, automated measurement tools or artificial intelligence-based image analysis techniques to obtain more objective and precise measurements of gallbladder wall thickness, thereby reducing observer-related variability. Additionally, multi-center studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate the cutoff value of gallbladder wall thickness (4.375 mm) identified in this study and to further explore its clinical applicability in patient selection for SILC. Furthermore, future studies should be registered prior to patient enrollment in a clinical trial registry to enhance transparency and reproducibility. Future studies should also incorporate extended follow-up protocols (e.g., ≥ 12 months) to adequately assess the risk of incisional hernia and confirm the long-term safety profile of suture-suspension SILC.

Conclusion

In summary, the suture-suspension method for SILC minimizes instrument collision and interference, facilitates intra-operative visualization and manipulation, and is safe, feasible, and manageable. It is suitable for implementation in hospitals possessing adequate laparoscopic surgical expertise and can be considered for any patient eligible for conventional laparoscopic cholecystectomy. However, in cases of a difficult gallbladder, the timely addition of ports or conversion to laparotomy is imperative. In balancing minimally invasive cosmetic benefits against surgical safety, safety must always remain the paramount priority.
