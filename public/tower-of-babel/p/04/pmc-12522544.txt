Comparison of urinary prolactin and serum/urine prolactin ratio in hyperprolactinemic and macroprolactinemic patients
Authors: Behnam Ein-Mozaffari, Akbar Aliasgharzadeh, Farzad Najafipour, Azam Moslemi, Mitra Niafar, Vahideh Sadra, Nasser Aghamohammadzadeh, Jalil Houshyar, Halimeh Amirazad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBiologically, the monomeric form of prolactin is the most active. In measurement methods, all forms of prolactin are included in the assay; therefore, the presence of macroprolactin, causes a false increase in prolactin and misdiagnosis of hyperprolactinemia. It seems that if an alternative method can be used to check macroprolactin, it would be helpful. The study aims to investigate the urinary level of prolactin and its relationship with serum levels in cases of suspected macroprolactinemia and determine whether these tests can serve as a novel, non-invasive, accessible, and cost-effective tool for diagnosing macroprolactinemia.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, patients who visited the Tabriz Imam Reza hospital clinic (2020–2021) with elevated prolactin levels. Prolactin and creatinine were checked in the urine and blood samples. After treating the serum with polyethylene glycol to precipitate macroprolactin, serum prolactin was measured again. The patients were divided into two groups. The first group, consisting of 19 individuals, with a recovery rate of more than 60%, formed the hyperprolactinemia group. The second group, comprising 36 individuals with a recovery rate of less than 60%, formed the macroprolactinemia group.ResultsThe participants in this study were 50 individuals, comprising 10 men and 40 women. The mean urinary prolactin levels in the hyperprolactinemia group are 43.27 ± 15.53, and in the macroprolactinemia group, they are 32.58 ± 16.27. This difference was statistically significant. The median ratio of urinary prolactin to urinary creatinine is 0.03 in the hyperprolactinemia group and 0.02 in the macroprolactinemia group (p<0.05). The median ratio of serum prolactin to urinary prolactin in the hyperprolactinemia group is 32.6, and in the macroprolactinemia group, it is 45.5 (p < 0.01).ConclusionFindings showed that the difference between urinary prolactin in true macroprolactinemic and hyperprolactinemic patients is significant, such that the amount of urinary prolactin in macroprolactinemic patients is less than in hyperprolactinemic patients, and the ratio of serum to urinary prolactin is higher. These parameters can be used to detect macroprolactin in cases of increased serum prolactin level, and this finding could provide a basis for the idea that these parameters can be used as an alternative to the PEG sedimentation test for diagnosing macroprolactin.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-025-02052-6.Keywords: Prolactin, Hyperprolactinemia, Creatinine, Macroprolactinemia, Diagnosis

Background

Prolactin is secreted from pituitary lactotroph cells. There are three forms of prolactin in blood circulation: monomer prolactin, prolactin dimer, or big prolactin, and macroprolactin, another form of big prolactin, that binds to immunoglobulins. The biological functions of monomeric form of this hormone, including its roles in reproduction, metabolism, immunity, and lactation, are essential and specific Prolactin levels increase in various physiological conditions such as pregnancy, stress, and exercise, as well as in pathological conditions including pituitary and hypothalamic tumors, systemic diseases like kidney failure, polycystic ovary syndrome, and cirrhosis. Prolactin levels also rise in response to certain medications, such as neuroleptic drugs. Measuring it to diagnose and treat diseases such as pituitary tumors, such as prolactinoma, reproductive problems, and amenorrhea is essential. In modern prolactin measurement methods, all forms of prolactin are included in the measurement. The presence of macroprolactin, which is biologically inactive and unrelated to the patient’s symptoms, can cause a false increase in prolactin levels, leading to the misdiagnosis of hyperprolactinemia and inappropriate treatments [1]. Therefore, all cases of hyperprolactinemia should be evaluated for the presence of macroprolactin to reduce the unnecessary use of imaging, avoid inappropriate dopamine agonist treatments, and save time and money [2, 3].

In most healthy individuals, the monomeric form of prolactin accounts for 65–85% of the total prolactin in blood circulation, while the dimeric form comprises 10–20%, and macroprolactin naturally makes up less than 10% [4–6]. The prevalence of macroprolactinemia has ranged from 15% to 46% [4, 7]. The gold standard method for determining macroprolactin is gel filtration chromatography (GFC). However, it is time-consuming, complex, expensive, and not widely available. Alternative techniques based on serum prolactin immunoassays before and after the removal of macroprolactin through ultrafiltration, IgG immunoabsorption using protein A or anti-human IgG, or polyethylene glycol (PEG) precipitation have been described [8–10]. Studies have shown that PEG precipitation is superior, exhibiting the best correlation with gel filtration chromatography. Additionally, it has the advantage of precipitating dimeric prolactin [11] and is 27 times less expensive than gel filtration chromatography [12, 13]. Different protocols for performing the PEG precipitation test exist, and variations in serum volume, type of PEG used, mixing time, and temperature/centrifugation/incubation times, can lead to differences in laboratory results [2]. These protocols increase the potential for human error, thereby reducing the reproducibility of the test and its consistency in results. Nevertheless, PEG precipitation remains the most widely used method for detecting macroprolactin [3, 14]. There is a need for a convenient and inexpensive test to identify macroprolactin in cases of hyperprolactinemia.

Current methods, such as chromatography, are expensive and challenging, while the commonly used PEG test has limitations and lacks standardization. This study suggests a potential new method for diagnosing macroprolactinemia, based on the fact that macroprolactin, unlike monomeric prolactin, is not excreted in urine, resulting in lower urinary prolactin levels. This method would calculate the presence of macroprolactin by comparing serum and urinary prolactin levels. This approach could offer a non-invasive, accessible, and less expensive diagnostic tool for macroprolactinemia.

Methods

Participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted among 50 individuals (40 women and 10 men) aged 18–50 years. A sample size of 50 patients was calculated using a 95% confidence coefficient, 80% power, with d = 0.02, and σ2 = 0.07 as follows: [15].

The mean age of the participants was 29 ± 8 years. The mean serum prolactin level was 1714 mIU/L, with a minimum of 399 mIU/L, a maximum of 6912 mIU/L, and a standard deviation of 1269 mIU/L.

This study was conducted using a convenience sampling method. This study’s participants were selected from patients of reproductive age—both men and women—who, between 2020 and 2021, were referred to the endocrinology clinic of Imam Reza Hospital due to elevated prolactin levels, or in whom elevated prolactin was confirmed during evaluations at the center.

Inclusion criteria for the study included men and women of reproductive age with elevated prolactin levels. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy and breastfeeding, the presence of disorders such as elevated creatinine, chronic diseases such as kidney failure, and unwillingness to participate in the study.

Measurements

Blood and urine samples were collected simultaneously in the morning after an overnight fast. The following tests were performed on them: Prolactin and creatinine levels were measured in the urine samples.

In the blood sample, after separating the serum, both creatinine and prolactin levels were checked. Subsequently, serum prolactin was re-measured using polyethylene glycol to precipitate macroprolactin. The recovery percentage of prolactin after polyethylene glycol treatment was calculated using the following formula:

Patients were then divided into two groups: those with hyperprolactinemia (predominantly involving monomeric prolactin) and those with macroprolactinemia. An extensive literature review was conducted to determine the optimal cutoff value for the study.

The results of the studies [14, 16–18] showed that a consistent cutoff value was not used for the PEG test. However, based on a broader consensus around the value of 60%, we adopted the same threshold in our study. Specifically, serum samples with more than 60% recovery were considered to contain monomeric prolactin (genuine prolactin). Samples with less than 60% recovery were classified as containing macroprolactin. Based on the 60% cutoff, they were divided into two groups. The first group consisted of 19 participants with more than 60% recovery, classified as having hyperprolactinemia. The second group consisted of 31 participants with less than 60% recovery, classified as having macroprolactinemia. This approach allows us to study and measure the effect of macroprolactin and dimeric prolactin on urinary prolactin secretion in the macroprolactinemia group compared to the hyperprolactinemia group, which mainly consists of monomeric prolactin. The urinary prolactin level was assessed by measuring the ratio of urinary prolactin to urinary creatinine (primary objective), as well as the ratio of serum prolactin to urinary prolactin, and the ratio of serum prolactin to the urinary prolactin-to-creatinine ratio (secondary objective). These ratios were investigated and compared between the two groups.

Materials and devices used

In this study, PEG (6000 g/mol) was used. Prolactin levels were measured using the LIAISON Prolactin (xt) 312,171 device (DiaSorin, Italy), which employs the sandwich chemiluminescence immunoassay method. The device follows the WHO 3rd International Reference Preparation standard (84/500). The normal reference range for this device is as follows: Reference Range (5th-95th percentile) mIU/L.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics software version 24 (IBM SPSS Statistics, Armonk, USA). To compare the parameters between the two groups, the independent t- test and the Mann-Whitney U test were used.

The ROC curve and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) were used to evaluate the diagnostic power of the urinary prolactin variable, the urinary prolactin-to-urinary creatinine ratio, and the serum prolactin-to-urinary prolactin ratio. Also sensitivity and specificity report.

Discussion

This study was conducted to investigate the effect of serum macroprolactin on urinary prolactin levels. The prolactin levels in two groups, hyperprolactinemia and macroprolactinemia, were compared in terms of urine prolactin and the serum-to-urinary prolactin ratio. The results aimed to explore whether urinary prolactin levels could help differentiate hyperprolactinemia from macroprolactinemia. The results showed that urinary prolactin levels and the ratio of prolactin to urine creatinine were significantly higher in the hyperprolactinemia group compared to the macroprolactinemia group. Additionally, this finding aligns with the studies by Anderson et al. [6], who suggested that the large molecular size of the macroprolactin complex inhibits its passage through the capillary endothelium. Our findings align with those of Pekkolay et al. [15], who reported that median urinary prolactin levels were lower in the macroprolactinemia group compared to normal subjects. However, the Pekkolay et al.‘s studies acknowledges two major limitations: a small sample size (30 participants in total across both hyperprolactinemia and macroprolactinemia groups) and the absence of urinary prolactin correction based on creatinine levels. Our study is the second in this field and has the advantage of not only measuring urinary prolactin but also calculating the ratio of urinary prolactin to creatinine, which provides a more precise assessment of prolactin secretion in random urine samples. We compared this ratio between the two groups, and this variable more evidently emphasized the difference between hyperprolactinemia and macroprolactinemia.

In this study, the median serum-to-urinary prolactin ratio in the macroprolactinemia group was higher than in the hyperprolactinemia group, and this difference was statistically significant. Therefore, this variable can also be used as a distinctive factor between the two groups. In cases of increased serum prolactin levels, this variable is higher in macroprolactinemic individuals than in individuals with true hyperprolactinemia and can identify macroprolactinemia.

The study by Pekkolay et al. found that the serum-to-urine prolactin ratio was highest in the hyperprolactinemia group, lowest in the control group, and intermediate in the macroprolactinemia group. Their results showed a higher ratio in the hyperprolactinemia group compared to the macroprolactinemia group, which contrasts with the current study.

The results of Pekkolay et al.‘s studies also showed that an increased serum-to-urine prolactin ratio in hyperprolactinemia suggests the presence of more monomeric prolactins in the urine.

However, since urinary prolactin is in the denominator, an increase in urinary prolactin should lower the ratio.

In the current study, despite similar mean serum prolactin levels, the macroprolactinemia group had a higher ratio due to lower urinary prolactin levels. This difference could be attributed to the larger sample size. Additionally, the study introduced a new ratio comparing serum prolactin to the ratio of urinary prolactin to urinary creatinine, which was significantly higher in the macroprolactinemia group, but no other studies were found for comparison.

Given the significant difference in the mentioned variables between the two studied groups and their ability to distinguish macroprolactinemic from hyperprolactinemic individuals in cases of elevated serum prolactin levels, ROC curves were generated to determine the distinguishing values for these groups, and the sensitivity and specificity of these values were examined.

Based on the serum-to-urinary prolactin ratio, a cut-off point of > 34.65 was calculated, with a sensitivity of 0.71 and a specificity of 0.58. This indicates that in cases of increased serum prolactin, a ratio higher than 34.65 can detect macroprolactinemic individuals with relatively good sensitivity and moderate specificity. The ROC curve result of Pekkolay et al.‘s study showed that simultaneous measurement of serum and urine prolactin could distinguish between normal individuals and patients with macroprolactinemia and hyperprolactinemia, which is consistent with the results of our study.

Although the sensitivity and specificity of the attained values were higher than in our study, they provided a more representative comparison between macroprolactinemic, hyperprolactinemic, and normal individuals. Investigating the difference in urinary prolactin levels among these groups is an appropriate approach. However, the primary goal of this study is to distinguish macroprolactinemic patients within groups with elevated serum prolactin levels, rather than identifying them among normal individuals. Our data helps identify macroprolactinemic patients in cases with raised serum prolactin levels, offering a more practical approach. Our goal is to develop a reliable method for diagnosing macroprolactinemia, bringing us closer to improving the diagnosis of true hyperprolactinemia.

The results of the ROC curve based on the standard urinary prolactin, showed that People with urinary prolactin levels higher than 39.3 (with a sensitivity of 0.63 and a specificity of 0.55) are diagnosed as hyperprolactinemia.

The results of the ROC curve, based on the ratio of urinary prolactin to urinary creatinine, with a value higher than 0.025 (sensitivity 0.68, specificity 0.58), indicate a hyperprolactinemia group.

In our review of the literature, we did not find comparable findings for the two observations mentioned above, making our research the first to report on this. Our study demonstrates that urinary prolactin levels and the ratio of urinary prolactin to urinary creatinine may help identify true hyperprolactinemic patients in cases of elevated serum prolactin levels.

Limitations

Although our study addressed the limitations of previous research [15] regarding the number of participants and the consideration of urinary prolactin levels based on urinary creatinine, as well as examined more parameters in this field, further research is needed to develop a new laboratory method and to establish cutoff values and reference ranges for the detection of macroprolactin, specifically, such a study should, first, include a larger number of participants, and second, use chromatography as the gold standard for prolactin measurement instead of the PEG method.

Conclusions

Our findings showed that the differences between urinary prolactin, the urinary prolactin-to-creatinine ratio, and the serum-to-urinary prolactin ratio in macroprolactinemic and hyperprolactinemic individuals are statistically significant. So that the urinary prolactin level or urinary prolactin to creatinine ratio is lower in macroprolactinemic individuals than in hyperprolactinemic individuals, and the serum to urinary prolactin ratio is higher, and therefore these parameters can be used to diagnose macroprolactin in cases of increased serum prolactin levels. This finding could provide a basis for the idea that these parameters can be used as an alternative to the PEG sedimentation test for diagnosing macroprolactin, a method that would likely avoid the problems and limitations of PEG and would be more accessible, cost-effective, and convenient.
