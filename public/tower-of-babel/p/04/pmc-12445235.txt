Activatable companion theranostics for dual-modality imaging-escorted pyroptosis-propelled synergistic cancer therapy
Authors: Yilin Wan, Yurong Liu, Weihua Wen, Ting He, Chunying Li, Guohua Wang, Lijun Jin, Meng Li, Yumeng Wu, Rong Wen, Yifan Zhang, Lian-Hua Fu, Jing Lin, Peng Huang, Daxiang Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCompanion diagnostics (CDx) plays a pivotal role in precision medicine for cancer treatment. However, conventional CDx are often limited by their inability to provide real-time monitoring of cancer progression and therapeutic responses. Herein, we develop a dual-modality imaging-based companion theranostic (CTx) nanoplatform (LET-Cl@GOx), which integrates activatable photoacoustic (PA) and fluorescence (FL) imaging to enable the enhanced diagnostic accuracy and real-time therapeutic feedback, while demonstrating cascade-amplified photothermal/starvation synergistic therapy. The LET-Cl@GOx is designed by the assembly of glucose oxidase (GOx) with a pH-activatable near-infrared (NIR) dye (LET-Cl), enabling the turn-on of PA/FL imaging within the acidic tumor microenvironment (TME). The dynamic alterations of PA/FL imaging signals provide real-time feedback on TME acidification, enabling accurate monitoring of GOx catalysis progression and precision timing of photothermal therapy (PTT) intervention. Furthermore, the GOx-mediated tumor starvation reduces adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels, leading to the diminished heat shock protein expression and consequently enhanced the sensitivity to PTT. Concurrently, the photothermal effect reciprocally enhances the catalytic activity of GOx, establishing a triple closed-loop system with positive feedback amplification. This multiscale-augmented synergistic therapy triggers robust pyroptosis via the Caspase-3/gasdermin E signaling pathway, demonstrating remarkable therapeutic efficacy of tumors in vivo.Keywords: Companion theranostics, Cascade synergistic therapy, Pyroptosis, pH-responsive cyanine, Glucose oxidase

Introduction

Companion diagnostics (CDx) serve as essential tools for providing precise pathology information, facilitating the identification and optimization of personalized treatment regimens [[1], [2], [3]]. The majority of clinically implemented CDx are based on in vitro diagnostic methods [[4], [5], [6]], which detect specific biomarkers or genetic traits to predict a patient's response to a given drug [[7], [8], [9]]. Nevertheless, these conventional approaches are inherently limited in their capacity to offer real-time monitoring of dynamic molecular events within living systems. In contrast, molecular imaging-driven CDx systems enable non-invasive, real-time, and quantitative assessment of molecular events in vivo [10,11]. A representative example is FerriScan, an imaging-based CDx platform for clinical quantification of hepatic iron concentration using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in non-transfusion-dependent thalassemia patients undergoing deferasirox treatment [12,13].

Activatable molecular imaging, unlike the conventional “always-on” type that emits signals continuously, exhibit signal switching in response to specific targets or stimuli (such as pH variations, enzymatic cleavage, glutathione reduction, or endogenous reactive oxygen species (ROS) oxidation within the tumor microenvironment (TME)) [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]], thereby improving both the signal-to-noise ratio and detection sensitivity [19,20]. Activatable photoacoustic (PA)/fluorescence (FL) dual-modality imaging-guided CDx integrates the advantages of both the high spatial resolution of FL signal and the deep tissue penetration capacity of PA signal, providing comprehensive multiscale information on lesions and enhancing diagnostic precision [[21], [22], [23]]. However, most current CDx systems predominatly focus on diagnostic functionalities, lacking the capability for simultaneous tumor therapy or real-time therapeutic feedback. To address these limitations, companion theranostics (CTx) is emerging as a research trend that integrates diagnostic imaging and therapeutic functions within a single platform, enabling tumor detection, real-time monitoring, and selective tumor ablation in one system [24]. Although pH-responsive phototherapy systems hold great promise for imaging-guided cancer therapy, they are also subject to certain limitations [[25], [26], [27]]. For instance, tumor heterogeneity leads to variations in the pH of different TME, affecting drug activation efficiency, in the meanwhile phototherapeutic efficacy is normally hindered by limited light penetration depth and tumor resistance mechanisms such as thermotolerance. Compared with conventional pH-responsive phototherapies, the CTx platform not only possesses tumor-selective activation and imaging-guided therapeutic capability but also enables real-time imaging feedback during the therapeutic process [28]. This design facilitates the integration of multimodal strategies, wherein tumor-selective metabolic or starvation therapy can be combined with phototherapy to achieve synergistically therapeutic outcomes [29,30]. The imaging function can monitor both the process of starvation therapy and the activation efficiency of the phototherapeutic probe. Once the probe activation reaches its maximum efficiency, imaging feedback signal can be used to trigger phototherapy, thereby ensuring optimal treatment timing, thus maximizing therapeutic efficacy.

Herein, we developed a triple closed-loop-logic-based CTx nanoplatform (LET-Cl@GOx) for pH-activatable dual-modality imaging-guided cascade photothermal/starvation and pyroptosis-propelled synergistic cancer therapy (Scheme 1). Initially, a pH-activatable near-infrared (NIR) PA/FL probe (namely LET-Cl) was synthesized by our previous method [22]. This probe demonstrated strong binding affinity with GOx (−8.1 kcal mol−1). Using GOx as a carrier, LET-Cl was loaded to construct the LET-Cl@GOx nanoplatform via hydrophobic interaction. The LET-Cl@GOx exhibited minimal NIR PA and FL intensity along with the weak photothermal effect at the physiological pH (7.2–7.4), but showed remarkable activation in acidic TME, demonstrating strong PA/FL dual-modality imaging signals and photothermal effect for precision photothermal therapy (PTT). Simultaneously, GOx catalyzed the oxidation of the intratumoral glucose, generating substantial hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and gluconic acid, thereby intensifying oxidative stress and acidity within tumor tissues. The amplified tumor acidity 1) augmented NIR PA/FL imaging signals of LET-Cl, indicating the initiation of GOx-mediated starvation therapy, and 2) enhanced the photothermal effect of LET-Cl, which in turn facilitate the GOx-catalyzed reaction. Thus, one cascade loop with real-time feedback of therapy was established. Furthermore, the elevated oxidative stress improved the aforementioned PTT effect, completing the second cascade therapeutic loop. Additionally, GOx-induced starvation therapy suppressed energy metabolism and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production through glucose depletion in tumor cells, resulted in the downregulation of heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) expression, which consequently further reinforced the PTT efficacy of LET-Cl. This enhancement synergistically complemented the starvation therapy, finalizing the third closed-loop of cascade therapy amplification.

Additionally, the variation in hemoglobin oxygen saturation levels, induced by the consumption of oxygen during the GOx catalytic process, was also monitored through multispectral PA imaging in vivo. The PA/FL dual-modality imaging of LET-Cl@GOx enabled real-time visualization of in vivo diagnostic process, which exhibited strong correlation with the catalytic process of GOx and dynamic pH alterations. This correlation enables in vivo CTx, facilitating precise determination of optimal therapeutic windows and providing essential feedback for treatment optimization. Under 808 nm laser irradiation, the enhanced photothermal/starvation synergistic therapy effectively triggered Caspase-3/gasdermin E (GSDME)-mediated pyroptosis of tumor cells, resulting in significant tumor eradication efficacy. LET-Cl@GOx intergates microenvironment response, cancer diagnosis, targeted therapy, treatment progression monitoring and feedback, sparking further research into this kind of intelligent programmable CTx platform.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of LET-Cl@GOx

LET-Cl was synthesized according to our previous method (Fig. S1) [22]. A cyanine backbone was selected due to its strong absorption in the NIR region, particularly around 800 nm. To enhance molecular stability, a cyclohexenyl ring was incorporated into the heptamethine chain. A nucleophilic hydroxyl group was introduced at the indole fragment site to function as a pH-responsive element, capable of reversible activation. In contrast to the conventional protonation mechanism, this pH-responsive approach relies on the cleavage of specific covalent bonds. The synthesized LET-Cl initially adopted a ring-closed conformation, and the ether bond was progressively breaking to form the ring-open derivative (LET-Cl’) under acidic condition (Fig. 1a), showing a typical cyanine configuration, leading to the activation of intramolecular charge transfer, and enhanced NIR absorption and FL emission. The chemical structures of these compounds were verified using nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR and 13C NMR) and high-resolution mass spectroscopy (HR-MS) (Fig. S2 and S3).

Subsequently, GOx and LET-Cl were successfully assembled via hydrophobic interactions to form LET-Cl@GOx [31,32]. The hydrated particle sizes measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and the polydispersity index (PDI) of LET-Cl@GOx with varying mass ratios of LET-Cl were recorded, and a 20 % ratio was selected for subsequent experiments based on optimal particle size distribution and homogeneity (Fig. S4). The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image clearly revealed the spherical morphology of the synthesized LET-Cl@GOx nanoparticles (Fig. 1b). In phosphate buffer solution (PBS, pH 7.4), LET-Cl@GOx exhibited prominent Tyndall scattering and displayed an average hydrodynamic diameter of 100.8 nm, with a PDI of 0.17 (Fig. 1c). Furthermore, the size and PDI of LET-Cl@GOx remained relatively unchanged over a period of 7 days, showing its good stability under physiological conditions (Fig. S5). The surface zeta potential of LET-Cl@GOx (−7.3 ± 0.6 mV) was comparable to that of GOx (−9.8 ± 1.9 mV) (Fig. S6). LET-Cl@GOx exhibited similar absorption peaks to those of LET-Cl while a slight red shift was observed, which may be attributed to π-π stacking interactions of LET-Cl within the assembly process (Fig. 1d). Besides, LET-Cl@GOx showed protein bands for GOx in sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) characterization (Fig. 1e). These findings validated the successful assembly of LET-Cl@GOx nanoparticles. The encapsulation efficiency of LET-Cl in LET-Cl@GOx reached 44.2 %, when the mass ratio of LET-Cl to GOx was 20 % (Fig. S7). To better understand the assembly mechanism of LET-Cl@GOx and the interactions between LET-Cl and GOx, non-covalent molecular docking simulations were performed. These results indicated that LET-Cl exhibited a favorable docking score and binding energy with GOx, suggesting a strong affinity between two components (Fig. 1f). The binding sites of LET-Cl on GOx were modeled to involve multi residues Tyr68, Tyr515, and Arg512, which were situated in close proximity to the catalytic cofactor flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) (Fig. S8). These residues contributed to the structural stabilization of the active center and played important roles in maintaining optimal catalytic function [33,34]. Consequently, the interaction of LET-Cl with these residues might perturb the local environment of FAD, potentially inhibiting the enzymatic activity of GOx. Subsequently, the enzymatic activity of LET-Cl@GOx was evaluated using H2O2 assay kit, with an equivalent amount of free GOx serving as the control. After the addition of glucose, the H2O2 concentration in both solutions increased over time (Fig. 1g and Fig. S9a). However, in the LET-Cl@GOx group, the increase rate was slower compared to the GOx group, demonstrating that the enzymatic activity of GOx in LET-Cl@GOx was inhibited, which will be beneficial for in vivo applications with the reduction of toxicity [32]. Additionally, similar changes in the pH values were recorded (Fig. 1h and Fig. S9b). Then, we investigated the pH-responsive behavior of LET-Cl@GOx. TEM images and pH-responsive release profile unveiled that the nanoparticles could degrade under acidic conditions, which facilitated the release of LET-Cl in the acidic tumor microenvironment in vivo (Fig. 1i and Fig. S10).

In vitro pH-activatable and GOx-amplified PA/FL imaging and photothermal properties

Building upon the activatable property of LET-Cl, the pH-responsive optical behavior of LET-Cl@GOx was analyzed [22]. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, the absorption of LET-Cl@GOx at around 808 nm gradually elevated as the pH decrease. The absorption of LET-Cl@GOx at 808 nm exhibited a 1.68-fold increase at pH 5 compared to its intensity at pH 7. The FL signal intensity of LET-Cl@GOx was also intensified with pH decrease, accompanied by a slight blue shift (Fig. 2b). The PA spectra exhibited similar results, emphasizing its capability for pH-responsive PA imaging (Fig. S11). Accordingly, the PA signal intensity of LET-Cl@GOx at 808 nm progressively increased with pH decrease (Fig. 2c). The photothermal conversion property of LET-Cl@GOx was assessed by thermal imaging system. Under 808 nm laser irradiation, the temperature of the solutions was elevated more rapidly as the pH declined, demonstrating its pH-responsive photothermal effect (Fig. 2d). A pronounced concentration-dependent photothermal effect was observed upon laser irradiation of LET-Cl@GOx solutions at pH 5.5 (Fig. S12). After five cycles of heating and natural cooling, the photothermal performance of LET-Cl@GOx displayed minimal variation, proving its superior photothermal stability (Fig. S13a). The photothermal conversion efficiency of LET-Cl@GOx was calculated to be 36.8 % (Fig. S13b). Subsequently, to confirm the activity of GOx within the assembly, the absorption profiles and FL signal of LET-Cl@GOx were analyzed in the presence of glucose. Upon the addition of glucose, the GOx-mediated catalytic reaction gradually lowered the pH value of the solution, leading to a progressive increase in the absorption of LET-Cl@GOx around 808 nm (Fig. 2e). The FL intensity also exhibited an upward trend over catalytic time (Fig. 2f). A catalytic time-dependent elevation was also observed in PA intensity, which displayed a 2.9-fold amplification at 8 h after the addition of glucose (Fig. 2g). Furthermore, the photothermal effect of solutions was enhanced over catalytic time (Fig. 2h). Subsequently, correlations between FL/PA signal intensities and temperature during GOx catalytic time were analyzed and calculated. As illustrated in Fig. 1i, all evaluated parameters demonstrated robust correlations, with coefficients exceeding 0.9. Specifically, the PA amplitude and FL intensity demonstrated a high correlation coefficient of 0.98, suggesting the suitability of LET-Cl@GOx as a PA/FL duplex imaging agent. Temperature, as the direct indicator of photothermal effectiveness, displayed significant correlations with PA amplitude (Pearson coefficient: 0.95) and FL intensity (Pearson coefficient: 0.94). These high correlation values strongly support the utility of PA and FL signals as imaging-based CDx to provide real-time feedback, accurately guide PTT, and facilitate a CTx strategy. Additionally, previous studies have shown that the enzymatic efficiency of GOx is modulated by temperature, with an ideal performance observed around 50 °C [35,36]. To explore the impact of the photothermal effect on LET-Cl@GOx's catalytic activity, we conducted further investigations. Under laser illumination, LET-Cl@GOx generated more H2O2 compared to the non-irradiated group, confirming that the photothermal effect could enhance the catalytic activity of GOx (Fig. S14). All of above results verified that LET-Cl@GOx exhibited pH-activatable PA/FL imaging and PTT.

In vitro synergistic therapeutic effect

The therapeutic effect of LET-Cl@GOx was determined on A549 tumor cells. Firstly, cellular uptake of LET-Cl@GOx was investigated according to the FL signal emitted by LET-Cl. As the incubation time progressed, the cellular FL signal gradually intensified, suggesting that LET-Cl@GOx was effectively internalized by A549 cells (Fig. 3a). Additionally, the cellular penetration of nanoparticles was further evaluated using a 3D spheroid model. Following 8 h of incubation with the LET-Cl@GOx, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was utilized to measure FL intensity at 20 μm intervals from the apex to the equator. The result showed that the nanoparticles successfully penetrated the spheroids to a depth of 60 μm, confirming their potential for achieving deep tissue penetration in vivo (Fig. 3b). Next, LET-Cl@HSA (only PTT effect) was synthesized as a control to investigate the therapeutic efficacy of photothermal/starvation synergistic therapy mediated by LET-Cl@GOx [32]. TEM images and DLS results indicated that the synthesized LET-Cl@HSA exhibited similar morphology and size distribution to LET-Cl@GOx (Fig. S15). The viability of cells treated with LET-Cl@HSA remained above 90 % at various glucose concentrations, whereas LET-Cl@GOx exhibited glucose concentration-dependent cell death due to GOx-mediated glucose depletion and increased oxidative stress (Fig. 3c and Fig. S16). The gluconic acid and H2O2 produced by the GOx-catalyzed reaction can reduce the intracellular pH and trigger the oxidative stress, thereby promoting the efficacy of pH-responsive PTT. As depicted in Fig. S17, the LET-Cl@HSA plus H2O2 (+) treatment group resulted in lower cell viability than LET-Cl@HSA (+) group, suggesting the oxidative stress induced by H2O2 improved the PTT efficacy. The intracellular pH value was tested by 2′,7′-Bis-(2-Carboxyethyl)-5-(and-6)-Carboxyfluorescein Acetoxymethyl Ester (BCECF-AM, pH probe). Compared to the control and LET-Cl@HSA groups, the LET-Cl@GOx-treated group displayed strong green FL signals, indicating more acidic pH level (Fig. 3d and (Fig. S18). The reduction of pH level was also supposed to improve the photothermal effect of LET-Cl@GOx, thereby augmenting the PTT effect. As expected, LET-Cl@GOx group showed most cytotoxicity upon laser irradiation (Fig. 3e). The live/dead cell staining images (Fig. S19) showed that weak red FL signals (dead cells) were observed in both the LET-Cl@HSA with laser irradiation and LET-Cl@GOx without laser irradiation groups. In contrast, strong red FL signals were detected in the LET-Cl@GOx with laser irradiation group, demonstrating the superior antitumor effect of the combined therapy. Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis revealed that the apoptosis rate induced by LET-Cl@GOx plus laser irradiation (65.3 %) was markedly higher than that of LET-Cl@HSA with laser irradiation (35.7 %) and LET-Cl@GOx without laser irradiation (27.5 %) (Fig. 3f and (Fig. S20). Additionally, LET-Cl@GOx also exhibited efficient uptake in LLC cells and demonstrated a robust synergistic therapeutic effect upon laser irradiation (Fig. S21). Compared with tumor cells, which preferentially utilize the Warburg effect and exhibit markedly higher glucose uptake efficiency, normal cells rely predominantly on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and possess lower glucose uptake efficiency [37,38]. This metabolic disparity limited the availability of glucose for the LET-Cl@GOx in normal cells, resulting in weak LET-Cl activation and low cytotoxicity (Fig. S22). Consequently, LET-Cl@GOx exhibited a notable selectivity toward tumor cells.

Subsequently, we explored the changes in intracellular oxidative stress associated with apoptosis. To monitor the increase in oxidative stress, we employed a commercial ROS probe, 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). As depicted in Fig. 3g and Fig. S23, LET-Cl@GOx (+) group induced the highest intracellular ROS levels, as confirmed by the bright and intense FL signals of DCF in the cells, which was 1.4 times higher than that in LET-Cl@HSA (+) group and 1.9 times higher than in LET-Cl@GOx (−) group. The mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) serves as a crucial occurrence of mitochondrial function and can be used to assess the early stages of cell apoptosis [39,40]. As illustrated in Fig. 3h and Fig. S24, a notable decrease in the FL intensity ratio between JC-1 aggregates and monomers was detected in LET-Cl@GOx plus laser (2.67) compared with LET-Cl@HSA plus laser (1.79) and LET-Cl@GOx without laser irradiation (1.21). In contrast, no conspicuous reduction could be observed in the other groups, indicating that ROS generation led to a substantial decline in intracellular ΔΨm. To investigate the cell proliferation and migration in real time during apoptosis, a high-throughput imaging system was employed to monitor and quantify the dynamics of individual cells under various treatments. Cell migration is primarily influenced by displacement and velocity, where displacement refers to the direct distance between the initial and final positions of a cell [41,42]. The trajectory of cell movement was recorded every hour over a 12-h period (Fig. S25). As shown in Fig. 3i, the trajectory of LET-Cl@GOx (+) treated cells within 12 h was notably shorter than that of the other groups. The displacement distance, which quantifies cell movement, indicated that cells treated with LET-Cl@GOx plus laser remained closer to their initial positions (Fig. 3j), suggesting a reduction in displacement (Fig. 4k). The velocity of cell movement was considerably decreased following LET-Cl@GOx (+) treatment (Fig. 4l). Overall, LET-Cl@GOx (+) treatment notably diminished both cell displacement and speed, suggesting that cell migration was inhibited. Cell proliferation was assessed by quantifying cell numbers. As demonstrated in Fig. S26, LET-Cl@GOx (+) treatment resulted in a stable cell count with no increase, signifying a considerable suppression of cell proliferation. Additionally, the temporal changes in cell morphology were examined, as a reduction in cell area may serve as an indicator of cellular stress or apoptosis, ultimately leading to decreased proliferation. As expected, the cell area in LET-Cl@HSA with laser irradiation and LET-Cl@GOx without laser irradiation showed a certain degree of reduction, while LET-Cl@GOx plus laser resulted in the most pronounced decline (Fig. S27). Collectively, these results confirmed that LET-Cl@GOx-mediated photothermal/starvation synergistic therapy significantly impeded both cell migration and proliferation.

In vitro synergistic therapeutic mechanism

To gain a deeper understanding of the antitumor mechanism mediated by LET-Cl@GOx, we explored the role of it played in regulating tumor cell energy metabolism and modulating cell death pathways. Previous studies have demonstrated that GOx-mediated starvation therapy substantially diminishes the glycolytic capacity of tumor cells [31,43]. The extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) was analyzed using the Seahorse glycolysis stress test kit to assess glycolytic activity. The specific ECAR response profiles to various stimuli, including glucose, oligomycin, and 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) (Fig. 4a). When mitochondrial ATP production was suppressed by oligomycin, LET-Cl@GOx (+) group exhibited only a minimal increase in ECAR, reflecting a 6.15-fold and 4.37-fold decrease in glycolytic capacity compared with control group and LET-Cl@GOx group, respectively (Fig. 4b). The metabolic adaptations of tumor cells were further investigated by analyzing their mitochondrial aerobic respiration. The oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was assessed to determine the level of OXPHOS using the seahorse mitochondrial stress test kit. As illustrated in Fig. 4c, the specific OCR response profiles to various stimuli, including oligomycin, carbonyl cyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone (FCCP), rotenone (Rot), and antimycin A (AA). Notably, the LET-Cl@GOx (+) group exhibited a diminished response to FCCP, with maximal respiratory capacity reduced by 3.46-fold and spare respiratory capacity decreased by 3.73-fold compared to the control group (Fig. 4d). Following these observations, the ATP production rate was further assessed to determine the capacity for ATP synthesis. As shown in Fig. 4e, the LET-Cl@GOx (+) group displayed a significant decrease in the ATP synthesis rate, with values 4.63-fold and 3.49-fold lower than those observed in the control and LET-Cl@GOx groups, respectively. This notable reduction in ATP production further underscores the substantial impact of LET-Cl@GOx-mediated synergistic therapy on energy metabolism of tumor cells. As far as we know, tumor cells typically upregulate heat shock proteins (HSPs) as a protective mechanism to counteract heat stress and reduce cellular damage induced during PTT process [29,30]. More importantly, when ATP content is reduced, the expression of ATP-associated HSPs in tumor cells are correspondingly diminished. The results revealed that LET-Cl@GOx reduced both OCR and ECAR in A549 cells, leading to a decrease in ATP content, thereby resulting in a decline of HSP70 level (Fig. S28). Next, we observed the morphological changes of the cells using a high-throughput imaging system. Remarkably, tumor cells exposed to LET-Cl@GOx (+) displayed a swollen, rounded morphology, which was subsequently followed by membrane rupture and blister formation, a hallmark characteristic of pyroptosis (Fig. 4f). Pyroptosis is an inflammatory form of programmed cell death distinguished by rapid cell swelling, plasma membrane rupture, and the release of pro-inflammatory intracellular contents [44,45]. Mechanistically, it is initiated by the activation of caspases, which cleave gasdermin family proteins (e.g., GSDME) to release their N-terminal domains [46,47]. These domains insert into the plasma membrane, forming large pores that disrupt cellular osmotic balance, leading to lytic cell death and the release of tumor-associated antigens, danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), and pro-inflammatory cytokines [48,49]. This immunogenic process can further stimulate dendritic cell maturation, enhance antigen presentation, and activate cytotoxic T lymphocytes, thereby contributing to antitumor immune response [50,51]. Additionally, Annexin V-Fluorescein Isothiocyanate (FITC)/Propidium Iodide (PI) co-staining images indicated that foam formation and cell membrane rupture were strikingly observed in the LET-Cl@GOx (+) treatment groups. A slight red FL signal from PI was observed in the LET-Cl@HSA (+) and LET-Cl@GOx groups. However, prominent red FL signal was detected in the LET-Cl@GOx (+)-treated cells, indicating that the cell membrane integrity was disrupted and pores were formed (Fig. 4g). The activation of caspase-3 generally serves as the upstream signal for initiating pyroptosis [46,51,52]. The confocal results demonstrated that LET-Cl@GOx (+) treatment primarily activated the caspase-3 pathway due to the mitochondrial damage (Fig. 4h and Fig. S29). As shown in Fig. 4i and S30, the Western blot results indicated that LET-Cl@GOx (+) treatment upregulated the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein Bcl-2-associated X (BAX) and downregulated the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2). The shift in the balance between these proteins further corroborated mitochondrial damage [53,54]. Subsequently, the activated caspase-3 enzyme cleaved the GSDME protein at a specific site, leading to the release of its N-terminal fragment (GSDME-N) into the cytoplasm [50,55,56]. This cleavage event is a critical step in the process of pyroptosis, as GSDME-N is known to translocate to the cell membrane, where it forms pores, resulting in cell membrane rupture [44,45,57]. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), a well-established marker of pyroptosis, was found to be elevated in the cell culture supernatants of the LET-Cl@GOx, LET-Cl@HSA (+), and LET-Cl@GOx (+) groups (Fig. 4j). These findings validated that LET-Cl@GOx-mediated synergistic treatment induced pyroptosis in A549 cells. Overall, LET-Cl@GOx depleted glucose and induced oxidative stress, thereby compromising the glycolytic and OXPHOS capacities of A549 cells, which resulted in a decrease in ATP synthesis. The diminished ATP levels suppressed the expression of HSP70, thereby weakening resistance to hyperthermia [30,58,59]. Under laser illumination, the LET-Cl@GOx-mediated synergistic therapy activated the Caspase-3/GSDME signaling axis, culminating in pyroptosis of the cells (Fig. 4k).

In vivo multiscale imaging

In vivo imaging capability is critical for CTx. In vivo dynamic imaging of LET-Cl@GOx was explored by sequential integration of PA imaging, FL imaging, and infrared thermal imaging. The LET-Cl@HSA or LET-Cl@GOx was administered via intratumoral injection (i.t.) into mice bearing xenograft A549 tumors. As illustrated in Fig. 5a–d, a moderate increase in both FL and PA signals was observed in the LET-Cl@HSA group due to its TME-responsive property. In contrast, the LET-Cl@GOx group exhibited a more pronounced enhancement in these signals, reaching peak intensities at 120 min post-injection (p.i.), which were 1.7-fold and 2.2-fold higher than those observed in the LET-Cl@HSA group, respectively. This indicated that LET-Cl@GOx possessed robust in vivo PA/FL imaging capability within acidic TME. Furthermore, the elevated glucose levels within the tumor interacted with GOx, resulting in enhanced TME acidification and subsequently amplifying the PA/FL signals. Consequently, a time-dependent increase in PA and FL intensities observed in the LET-Cl@GOx group indicated the continuous progression of GOx-mediated starvation therapy. The increased PA brightness reflected a stronger photothermal effect. Afterwards, we investigated the photothermal effects of LET-Cl@GOx under the guidance of PA/FL imaging. The tumors were treated with laser irradiation at 5-min or 120-min p. i. of LET-Cl@GOx, respectively (Fig. 5e). After 5 min of laser irradiation, the temperature in the 5-min and 120-min p. i. groups increased by 19.69 °C and 27.12 °C, respectively, whereas the 0-min p. i. group exhibited only a modest rise of 5.85 °C (Fig. 5d). This manifested that the in vivo PA/FL signals of LET-Cl@GOx were positively correlated with its photothermal effect, making it a potential CTx indicator for offering valuable real-time feedback to optimize laser irradiation time window (Fig. 5g). In addition, the laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI) system was applied to achievenon-invasive monitoring of tumor microvessels and blood flow during the therapy. As shown in Fig. 5h, the microvascular network within the tumor appeared relatively intact prior to drug injection. However, 2 h after i. t. injection of LET-Cl@GOx, the vascular network was disrupted, and blood flow was evidently reduced (Fig. S31). This effect was further exacerbated following laser irradiation, indicating that the LET-Cl@GOx-mediated synergistic therapy efficiently damaged the tumor vasculature and diminished blood flow.

Subsequently, systemic administration of LET-Cl@GOx was conducted on A549 tumor-bearing mice to further investigate its in vivo imaging performance. The hemolysis assay, along with histological analyses of major organs at 12 and 24 h post-intravenous injection of LET-Cl@GOx, confirmed its good hemocompatibility and biocompatibility (Fig. S32). After systemic administration, the PA intensity of the LET-Cl@GOx group gradually elevated, reaching a peak at 12 h (Fig. 6a and b). Owing to the enhancement mediated by the catalytic effect of GOx, the PA signal of LET-Cl@GOx group was stronger than that of the LET-Cl@HSA group, showing a 1.4-fold increase according to the ex vivo measurement (Fig. S33). As previously noted, the catalytic reaction of GOx requires oxygen consumption, which consequently influences the oxygen saturation (sO2) levels within tumor tissues [32,60]. Multispectral PA imaging was conducted at various time intervals following intravenous injection of LET-Cl@HSA and LET-Cl@GOx, capturing the PA signal intensities of oxyhemoglobin (OxyHb) and deoxyhemoglobin (DeoxyHb) for each group. As depicted in Fig. 6c and Fig. S34, the deoxyhemoglobin signal in LET-Cl@GOx group gradually increased, whereas no significant change was observed in LET-Cl@HSA group. The sO2 level was determined by quantifying the concentrations of OxyHb and DeoxyHb. The observations showed that LET-Cl@GOx induced an apparent reduction in sO2 level, dropping from 45.65 % to 25.19 % within 12 h (Fig. 6d). The decrease in sO2 level reflected the continuous progression of GOx-mediated starvation therapy. Moreover, the results from FL imaging were in accordance with those observed in PA imaging. Following systemic administration, the FL signal in the tumor region of LET-Cl@GOx group peaked at 12 h, showing a 1.59-fold increase compared to LET-Cl@HSA group (Fig. 6e and f). After 24 h of monitoring, the mice were euthanized, and the major organs and tumors were harvested for biodistribution analysis. Ex vivo images revealed significant accumulation of LET-Cl@GOx in tumor tissues. Additionally, intense FL signals were observed in livers, suggesting that both LET-Cl@GOx and LET-Cl@HSA were metabolized primarily by livers (Fig. S35).

In vivo synergistic therapeutic efficacy

Inspired by the high tumor enrichment, confirmed in vivo enzymatic activity, and superior in vitro therapeutic performances, we proceeded to assess the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of LET-Cl@GOx. A549 tumor-bearing mice were intravenously administered with LET-Cl@GOx or LET-Cl@HSA, followed by treatment with or without local 808 nm laser irradiation. Under the navigation of PA/FL dual-modality imaging, laser irradiation was applied to the tumor regions after 12 h i. v. injection of nanoparticles. Upon exposure to laser, tumor temperatures in mice treated with LET-Cl@GOx or LET-Cl@HSA elevated progressively by 16.27 °C or 9.59 °C, respectively, whereas the control group exhibited only a 4.93 °C increase (Fig. 7a and b). The results not only validated the in vivo photothermal conversion capability of LET-Cl@GOx, but also emphasized the augmented PTT facilitated by the catalytic activity of GOx. Then, the tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into six groups: Control, Control (+), LET-Cl@HSA, LET-Cl@HSA (+), LET-Cl@GOx, and LET-Cl@GOx (+) to monitor the tumor growth after different treatments over a 14-day period (Fig. 7c and d). In the absence of laser irradiation, tumor growth in LET-Cl@HSA-treated mice was comparable to that in the Control group, while mice treated with LET-Cl@GOx exhibited a slight delay in tumor progression, attributed to the starvation therapy. PTT effectively hindered tumor burden in the LET-Cl@HSA-treated group. Excitingly, the tumor growth in mice treated with LET-Cl@GOx plus laser irradiation was significantly suppressed over the 14-day monitoring period, with some mice experiencing complete tumor regression without recurrence. The photographs of mice and their excised tumors on day 14 were collected and the weight of their tumors were measured (Fig. 7e and Fig. S36). Negligible changes in body weight were observed across the different treatment groups (Fig. 7f). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining images showed a marked downregulation of HSP70 and an upregulation of cleaved caspase-3, aligning with in vitro results (Fig. 7g and Fig. S37). To assess therapeutic efficacy, tumors tissues were harvested on day 3 for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Ki-67 immunohistochemistry, and TdT-mediated dUDP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining assays (Fig. 7g and Fig. S38). Compared to other groups, tumor sections from LET-Cl@GOx (+) group exhibited the most severe karyopyknosis and plasmatorrhexis in H&E staining images, along with a pronounced decrease in cell proliferation as evidenced by Ki-67 immunostaining. TUNEL images also unveiled the large-scale cell apoptosis in LET-Cl@GOx (+) treatment group. Moreover, H&E staining images of major organs from all treatment groups showed no substantial evidence of damage or inflammation (Fig. S39). The serum biochemical indexes of liver function (alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST)), kidney function (creatinine (CREA) uric acid (UA), and urea nitrogen (BUN)) exhibited no viewable changes after LET-Cl@GOx treatment (Fig. S40). These results confirmed that the LET-Cl@GOx-mediated synergistic therapy exhibited superior therapeutic efficacy. In contrast to traditional pH-sensitive PTT, the efficacy of LET-Cl@GOx was amplified by the catalytic reaction of GOx, overcoming the limitations imposed by the mildly acidic TME. Additionally, the catalytic effect of GOx induced tumor starvation by depleting glucose, lowering ATP levels, and reducing HSP70 expression, thereby weakening the thermal tolerance of tumor cells. Furthermore, the local hyperthermia in turn accelerated the catalytic reaction of GOx, achieving a multistage-enhanced photothermal/starvation cascade synergistic therapy effect. Moreover, LET-Cl@GOx incorporated a synthesized small molecule and a commercially available enzyme with reasonable cost, and its straightforward synthesis was readily scalable for large-scale production. Regarding biosafety, the combination of an organic small molecule with a biocompatible enzyme supported a favorable safety profile. Importantly, the LET-Cl@GOx nanoplatform integrated starvation therapy and PTT, thereby overcoming the inherent limitations of each monotherapy. Collectively, these properties suggested a great potential for the clinical translation of the LET-Cl@GOx.

Conclusion

In summary, we present a programmable CTx for multiple closed-loop logic-based cascade synergistic therapy and feedback amplification. Specifically, in the acidic TME, pH-responsive LET-Cl@GOx exhibits an elevation in NIR absorption and FL signals, simultaneously activating both PA/FL duplex imaging and PTT. The catalytic reaction of GOx amplifies the imaging-escorted phototherapeutic efficacy through the three ways: 1) enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio of PA/FL duplex imaging and intensifying the PTT effect of LET-Cl by further acidifying the local microenvironment; 2) improving the sensitivity of tumor to PTT by generating ROS (H2O2); 3) promoting tumor starvation by glucose-consumption. In this case, the PA/FL signals were directly correlated with local pH levels, allowing real-time monitoring of the GOx-based catalytic process as well as the pH-responsive PTT effect. The oxygen consumption driven by GOx catalysis was also visualized through in vivo PA imaging of oxygen saturation. During GOx-based glucose-consumption, the ATP synthesis rate was decreased, leading to the downregulation of heat shock proteins (e.g. HSP70) expression and thereby enhancing the PTT efficacy. Additionally, the photothermal effect of LET-Cl further reinforced the catalytic activity of GOx, forming a closed-loop therapeutic amplification. Moreover, this multiscale-augmented synergistic therapy induces strong pyroptosis by Caspase-3/GSDME signal axis, achieving complete tumor eradication in vivo. Building on these findings, future researches will extend efficacy validation to additional tumor models, elucidate immune responses activated by the LET-Cl@GOx nanoplatform, and explore synergistic integration with immunotherapy. Parallel development of molecular subtype-stratifying CTx platforms will further advance precision targeting. These pathways represent critical milestones toward clinical translation of CTx strategy in precision cancer treatment.

Experimental section

Materials and reagents

5-chloro-2,3,3-trimethyl-3H-indole, 3-iodopropyl acetate were purchased from Energy Chemical Co., Ltd (China). 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) and Glucose oxidase (GOx) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM) and Fetal bovine serum (FBS) were provided by Gibco (USA). Calcein acetoxymethyl ester (Calcein-AM), and propidium iodide (PI) were purchased from Biolegand (USA). Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit were obtained from Invitrogen (USA). BCECF-AM probe was purchased from Yeasen Biotechnology (China). Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test Kit, Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit, Seahorse XF Real-Time ATP Rate Assay Kit were purchased from Agilent (USA). Hoechst 33342, Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit, ATP Assay Kit, Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) Assay Kit, Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) Cytotoxicity Assay Kit, and BCA Protein Assay Kit were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology (China). Anti-DFNA5/GSDME (Cat. ab215191) and anti-HSP70 (Cat. ab31010) were purchased from Abcam (UK). Anti-caspase-3 (Cat. 9662S), anti-cleaved caspase-3 (Cat. 9661S), Bax (Cat. D2E11), Bcl-2 (Cat. 3498S) and β-actin (Cat. 8457S) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (USA). Anti-caspase-3 p11 Alexa Fluor® 488 (Cat. sc-271759) were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (USA). A549 cell lines were purchased from Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (China).

Characterizations

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were recorded on HT7700 (Hitachi Electronic, Japan). Zeta potential and Dynamic Light Scattering were carried on Zetasizer Nano-S (Malven, England). UV/vis absorption spectra were measured on a Cary 60 UV/Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, USA). Fluorescence (FL) spectra were collected on a Thermo Scientific Lumina fluorescence spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., USA). Cell experiments were conducted on High Content System (PerkinElmer, America). PA imaging was performed on Vevo LAZR-X photoacoustic imager (VisualSonics, America).

Synthesis of LET-Cl@GOx

The nanoparticles was synthesized according to our previous simple method [32,61]. Initially, 10 mg of GOx was dissolved in 5 mL of PBS (pH 8.5) buffer under stirring for 2 min, followed by the addition of GSH. Subsequently, 500 μL of LET-Cl (4 mg mL-1, dissolved in ethanol) was slowly introduced into the GOx solution while stirring. The final mixture was then gently stirred for 30 min. Free LET-Cl and GSH were removed through ultrafiltration centrifugation.

Enzyme activity

The catalytic activity of GOx was evaluated by monitoring the generation profiles of H2O2 and pH changes in the solution. Time-dependent variations in H2O2 and pH were measured during the reaction between LET-Cl@GOx (GOx: 54.3 μg mL−1) and glucose (10 mM). Additionally, H2O2 and pH changes were assessed for reactions involving LET-Cl@GOx (GOx: 54.3 μg mL−1) and glucose at concentrations of 0, 1, 2, 5, 10, and 20 mM. H2O2 concentrations were determined using a H2O2 Assay Kit (Beyotime, China), while pH values were monitored using a pH meter (ThermoFisher, USA). The in vitro photothermal-enhanced catalytic activity was assessed by incubating LET-Cl@GOx (GOx: 54.3 μg mL−1) with 10 mM glucose at room temperature for 10 min, followed by exposure to an 808 nm laser (0.6 W cm−2) for an additional 10 min. The control group was maintained at room temperature with 10 mM glucose but without laser irradiation.

In vitro photothermal performance

To assess the concentration-dependent photothermal effect, 200 μL of LET-Cl@GOx solutions at varying concentrations of LET-Cl in PBS (pH 5.5) were illuminated by a laser (808 nm, 0.6 W cm−2) for 5 min, and the temperature changes were captured using an infrared thermographic camera (FLIR A310).

For the evaluation of the pH-dependent photothermal effect, 200 μL of LET-Cl@GOx solutions (LET-Cl: 40 μM) with different pH values were irradiated under identical conditions, and the temperature of the solution was recorded using the FLIR A310.

To investigate the GOx-augmented photothermal effect, 20 mM glucose was added to LET-Cl@GOx (LET-Cl: 40 μM) solutions with initial pH values of 6.5, followed by incubation for 12 h. Afterwards, the solutions were then exposed to a laser (808 nm, 0.6 W cm−2) for 5 min, and temperature changes were monitored using the infrared thermal camera. LET-Cl@GOx solutions (LET-Cl: 40 μM) without glucose supplementation served as control groups. The photothermal conversion efficiency (ηT) of LET-Cl@GOx was calculated following a previously established method [62].

To evaluate photostability, LET-Cl@GOx solution (200 μL, LET-Cl: 80 μM) was irradiated with an 808 nm laser (0.6 W cm−2) for 5 min, followed by natural cooling upon cessation of irradiation. This heating-cooling cycle was repeated five times, and the solution temperature was continuously captured using the FLIR A310.

Cellular experiments

The A549 lung cancer cells were cultured in DMEM (containing 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin) under 37 °C and 5 % CO2 humidified atmosphere.

For cellular uptake, A549 cells were seeded into confocal dishes (1 × 105 cells per dish) for 24 h incubation. Following incubation, the culture medium was replaced with glucose-free DMEM containing LET-Cl@GOx (LET-Cl: 20 μM). After varying incubation periods, the cells were stained with Hoechst for 10 min to label the nuclei. A549 cells were seeded in an ultra-low adsorption culture plate (5 × 104 cells per well) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, half of the culture medium was refreshed daily until the spheroids reached a defined volume. Fluorescence images of cells were captured by confocal scanning system (Zeiss, Germany).

For cellular pH detection, A549 cells were seeded into 96-well CellCarrierTM microplate (8 × 103 cells per well) for 24 h incubation. Following incubation, the culture medium was replaced with fresh DMEM containing LET-Cl@HSA (LET-Cl: 40 μM) or LET-Cl@GOx (GOx: 54.3 μg mL−1, LET-Cl: 40 μM). After 6 h incubation, the cells were stained with BCECF-AM probe. Fluorescence images of cells were obtained by High-content system (PerkinElmer, USA).

For phototoxicity assay, A549 cells were seeded into 96-well plates (7 × 103 cells per well). After 24 h incubation, the cells were replaced with DMEM media containing a serial concentration of LET-Cl@HSA or LET-Cl@GOx. After 6 h incubation, the cells were replaced by fresh DMEM media and irradiated for 10 min by an 808 nm laser (0.6 W cm−2). The phototoxicity were measured by CCK-8 assay kit.

For Live/dead staining assay, A549 cells were seeded into 96-well CellCarrierTM microplate (1 × 104 cells per well) and incubated overnight. Subsequently, the culture media were removed. The cells were incubated with fresh media containing LET-Cl@HSA or LET-Cl@GOx (at equivalent 40 μM of LET-Cl) for 6 h, followed by treating with or without an 808 nm laser irradiation (0.6 W cm−2, 10 min). Afterwards, the cells were co-stained with Calcein-AM and PI, then washed by PBS twice and observed by High-content system (PerkinElmer, USA).

To detect cell apotosis by flow cytometry, A549 cells were seeded into 12-well plates (5 × 104 cells per well) and incubated for 24 h. Afterwards, the cells were incubated with LET-Cl@HSA or LET-Cl@GOx (at equivalent 40 μM of LET-Cl) for 6 h and then irradiated by an 808 nm laser (0.6 W cm−2, 5 min) or shielded in the dark. After further incubation for 1 h, the cells were harvested and stained with Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit, and analyzed by a flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA).

For reactive oxygen species (ROS) observation, A549 cells were seeded into 96-well CellCarrierTM microplate (1 × 104 cells per well). After 12 h incubation, the cells were treated with fresh DMEM containing LET-Cl@HSA or LET-Cl@GOx (at equivalent 40 μM of LET-Cl) for 6 h. Then, the cells were washed with PBS three times and irradiated by an 808 nm laser (0.6 W cm−2, 5 min). Afterwards, the A549 cells were stained with DCFH-DA (10 μM) for 30 min. Subsequently, the fluorescence images were captured by High-content system (PerkinElmer, USA).

For ΔΨm evaluation, A549 cells were cultured in 96-well CellCarrierTM microplate (1 × 104 cells per well) overnight. Afterwards, the cells were incubated with DMEM containing LET-Cl@HSA or LET-Cl@GOx (at equivalent 40 μM of LET-Cl) for 6 h. Then, the cells were irradiated by an 808 nm laser (0.6 W cm−2) for 5 min. Afterwards, the cells were stained Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (Beyotime, China).

Cell proliferation and migration was evaluated by High-content analysis system (TCO, 37 °C and 5 % CO2). A549 cells were cultured in 96-well CellCarrierTM microplate (2 × 103 cells per well) overnight. Afterwards, the cells were treated with LET-Cl@HSA or LET-Cl@GOx (at equivalent 20 μM of LET-Cl) for 6 h and irradiated by a 808 nm laser (0.6 W cm−2) for 5 min. Cell trajectory, speed, displacement and number were recorded under digital phase contrast model. Images were acquired for 13 h and recorded every 1 h. All data were processed through Harmony 4.8 Software.

The level of ATP detection

A549 cells were seeded into 6-well plates (1 × 105 cells per well). After various treatments, the cells were collected and measured by ATP assay kit.

Western blot analysis

A549 cells were lysed via RIPA lysis buffer in an ice bath. Then, the protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay kit, followed by immunoblotting of cell lysates using antibodies against caspase-3, cleaved caspase-3, GSDME, Bcl-2, Bax, and β-actin according to standard protocols. A chemiluminescence imaging system was used to monitor the expression levels of different proteins (Fluor Chem E).

Immunofluorescence in vitro

To detect the change of caspase-3 expression, A549 cells were seeded into 96-well CellCarrierTM microplate (8 × 103 cells per well) and incubated overnight. Afterwards, the cells were treated with LET-Cl@HSA or LET-Cl@GOx (at equivalent 40 μM of LET-Cl) for 6 h, further irradiated by a 808 nm laser (0.6 W cm−2, 5 min). Mouse monoclonal caspase-3 p11 Alexa Fluor® 488 was chosen for immunofluorescence.

Hemolysis assay

The Red blood cells (RBCs) were isolated from serum by centrifugation of 2 mL whole blood sample at 4500 rpm for 10 min. Following five washes with PBS, the purified RBCs were resuspended in a final volume of 10 mL. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of the diluted RBC suspension was mixed with 0.5 mL of PBS containing varying concentrations of LET-Cl@GOx. The mixtures were vortexed and incubated at room temperature for 4 h. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged, and the absorbance of the supernatants was measured at 541 nm using a microplate reader (Biotek, USA). RBCs treated with deionized water and PBS served as positive and negative controls, respectively.

Animals

Female nude mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from the Guangdong Medicinal Laboratory Animal Center (Guangzhou, China) and handled in compliance with the regulations of the Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Shenzhen University (AEWC-SZU, maximum tumor size: <2000 mm3). All experimental mice were housed under standard conditions (temperature: ∼22 °C, humidity: 40–70 %, 12-h light-dark cycle) with unrestricted access to sterile food and water. To establish a subcutaneous tumor model, A549 cells (∼5 × 106 cells/mouse) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of BALB/c mice. The mice were utilized for in vivo therapeutic experiments once the tumor volume reached approximately 80 mm3 and for in vivo fluorescence (FL) and photoacoustic (PA) imaging when the tumor volume reached ∼150 mm3.

In vivo dynamic imaging

Following the intratumoral administration of 50 μL PBS (Control), LET-Cl@HSA (LET-Cl: 1 mg kg−1) or LET-Cl@GOx (LET-Cl: 1 mg kg−1), PA imaging was performed using the Vevo LAZR-X system and the IVIS Spectrum system (PerkinElmer, USA; λex = 745 nm, λem = 820 nm) at different time points (0, 5, 15, 30, 60, and 120 min). The system parameters of LAZR-X system were configured as follows: frequency = 40 MHz, wavelengths = 808 nm, PA gain = 40 dB, B-mode gain = 20 dB, pulse repetition rate = 20 Hz, and laser power = 20 mJ cm−2. Laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI) was captured by RFLSI Ⅲ system (RWD, China).

In vivo FL/PA duplex imaging

For PA imaging, after intravenously administered 150 μL of LET-Cl@HSA (LET-Cl: 5 mg kg−1) or LET-Cl@GOx (LET-Cl: 5 mg kg−1), the Vevo LAZR-X system was employed in PA-Mode 2D/3D mode (wavelengths = 808 nm, PA gain = 45 dB) to obtain the PA signal within tumor region. The variation of OxyHb/DeoxyHb and content of oxygen saturation (sO2) within tumor regions were measured by Vevo LAZR-X system using PA-Mode 2D/3D (Multiwavelength, PA gain = 45 dB).

To perform FL imaging, mice were intravenously injected with 150 μL of LET-Cl@HSA or LET-Cl@GOx, corresponding to a LET-Cl dose of 5 mg kg−1. FL images were captured at predetermined time points (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h) before and after injection using an IVIS Spectrum system (PerkinElmer, USA; λex = 745 nm, λem = 820 nm). The system was used to calculate the integrated signal intensity from the acquired spectra. Furthermore, three mice were euthanized and their tumor tissues along with major organs were collected at 24 h for observation and analysis using the IVIS Spectrum system.

Antitumor efficacy

To evaluate the LET-Cl@GOx-mediated synergistic therapy, A549 tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into 6 groups (n = 5/group), including (1) Control, (2) Control + Light, (3) LET-Cl@HSA, (4) LET-Cl@HSA + Light, (5) LET-Cl@GOx, and (6) LET-Cl@GOx + Light. The dose of LET-Cl for group (3)–(6) were 5 mg kg−1, and 100 μL PBS was as a control. After 12 h of injection, the mice of group (4) and (6) were irradiated with 808 nm laser (0.6 W cm−2) for 10 min. The tumor sizes and body weights of the mice were recorded every two days. The tumors sizes were measured with a digital caliper and the tumor volumes were calculated in accordance with the formula: Vx = Lx × Wx2/2 (Vx, Lx, and Wx indicate the volume, length and width of tumors after different treatments at x days). The tumors in various groups were harvested for immunofluorescence staining (HSP70, Cleaved caspase-3), hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, TUNEl satining, and Ki-67 staining. All the mice were sacrificed after 14 days of treatment, their tumors and main organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were harvested, and the tumors were photoed and weighed. The main organs were separated out and stained by H&E for tissue biocompatibility assessment. These slices were analyzed using TEKSQRAY Slide Scan System SQS1000 (Shenzhen Shengqiang technology Co. Ltd., China).

For evaluation of blood biochemical index, healthy mice were intravenously injected with 150 μL of LET-Cl@GOx (LET-Cl: 5 mg kg−1). After 14 days, the blood samples were obtained by orbital blood extraction from the representative mice.

Statistical analysis

The quantitative data analysis was presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical difference was tested by Student's t-test or one way ANOVA by using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). The P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001).
