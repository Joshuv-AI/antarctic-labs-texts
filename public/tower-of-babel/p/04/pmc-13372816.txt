Engineering in vitro models to replicate the complexity of chronic wounds
Authors: Lucas Argandoña, Kristina Ivanova, Tzanko Tzanov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic wounds represent a major clinical challenge driven by sustained inflammation, proteolytic imbalance, microbial colonisation, and impaired tissue regeneration. This review critically examines 2D and 3D in vitro models used in chronic wound research, evaluating their capacity to recapitulate chronicity across venous leg ulcers, diabetic foot ulcers, and pressure ulcers. Current systems lack the biochemical complexity required to reproduce key features of the non-healing phenotype. Building on quantitative clinical wound exudate data, we propose a feature-driven, aetiology-stratified framework that provides modular design considerations for in vitro models and chronic wound simulating media.

Introduction

Chronic wounds (CWs) impose a substantial medical and financial burden on healthcare systems worldwide1,2. CWs are highly heterogeneous across multiple dimensions, including aetiology, pathogenesis, anatomical location and host-related factors, which constrain robust epidemiological characterisation and undermine accurate assessment of their global burden3,4. Despite their diversity, CWs share several pathological features that distinguish them from acute wounds, including prolonged inflammation, persistent microbial colonisation, excessive protease activity, oxidative stress, and impaired cellular responses to reparative stimuli5,6. Reported global prevalence ranges from 1.67 to 2.21 cases per 1000 individuals, with women aged 70–80 years most affected; chronic leg ulcers alone show prevalence rates around 1.08% with incidence up to 1.33%, increasing markedly with age4,7–9. Associated costs are estimated at 2–4% of total national healthcare expenditure, with annual wound care expenditures exceeding $22.5 billion in the USA, where CWs affect nearly one in six Medicare beneficiaries; in Europe, 1.5 to 2 million individuals are affected, with pressure ulcers showing a median prevalence of 10.8% and ranging from 4.6 to 27.2% across the continent10–14. The three most prevalent CW types, venous leg ulcers (VLUs), diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) and pressure ulcers (PUs), differ not only in aetiology and anatomical location but in their underlying wound microenvironments5.

In CWs, the normal repair sequence of haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodelling is disrupted at multiple levels, preventing progression towards tissue regeneration5. The nature and relative contribution of these disruptions differ across wound types: VLUs are characterised by a protease-rich, alkaline exudate driven by venous hypertension; DFUs by a hyperglycaemic, oxidative milieu that disrupts immune function and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodelling; and PUs by oxidative bursts from repeated ischaemia-reperfusion injury15–17. Wound chronicity is therefore not a single pathological state but a group of aetiology-specific failures of normal repair.

Despite longstanding academic interest in wound modelling, the field still lacks a standardised and widely accepted in vitro model of CWs. Animal models remain the preclinical standard but fail to naturally reproduce CW pathology and cannot recapitulate the distinct biochemical profiles of VLU, DFU and PU, limiting their translational value18. Increasing ethical and regulatory pressures to reduce animal experimentation have further accelerated the development of in vitro alternatives19,20.

Against this backdrop, a diverse range of in vitro models has emerged, spanning 2D monolayer cultures, co-culture systems, 3D scaffolds, organotypic skin equivalents, and advanced bioprinted constructs20–24. These models vary substantially in their capacity to mimic key features of wound chronicity, and no consensus framework currently exists to guide model selection or development25,26. A fundamental constraint has been insufficiently recognised: no single model can fully recapitulate the biochemical complexity of all CW types simultaneously, as wound exudate (WE) composition varies substantially across wound types, patient populations and disease stages27–29. A modular, aetiology-stratified approach, in which a core set of shared pathological features is complemented with wound-type-specific biochemical modules, is therefore more appropriate than any universal formulation.

This review critically evaluates the current landscape of in vitro CW models, building on a systematic compilation of quantitative biochemical data from clinical WE studies to propose a feature-driven framework that maps dominant pathological processes to modular, aetiology-stratified design considerations for in vitro models and culture media formulations.

Key criteria for developing in vitro CW models

CWs are distinguished from acute wounds by the failure of the normal repair sequence, haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodelling, to resolve in an orderly and timely fashion5. This failure is sustained by the co-occurrence of multiple interdependent pathological processes. Persistent neutrophil infiltration drives sustained reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, with myeloperoxidase (MPO) generating hypochlorous acid that directly inhibits TIMPs and perpetuates ECM degradation (Fig. 1)5. Elevated matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activity degrades fibronectin, vitronectin and growth factors, impairing keratinocyte and fibroblast function and hindering re-epithelialisation30–32. Microbial colonisation, predominantly by S. aureus and P. aeruginosa, frequently culminates in polymicrobial biofilm formation, present in 78.2% of CWs, which amplifies inflammation through TLR2 and TLR4 overexpression while conferring marked resistance to host defences and antimicrobial treatment33–35. Hypoxia, driven by vascular insufficiency or infection, disrupts redox homeostasis and further impairs antioxidant defences, creating a self-reinforcing inflammatory cycle36. ECM stiffness alterations additionally affect fibroblast activation and matrix remodelling, factors absent from most simplified models31. Wound exudate

The choice of culture media is a critical factor in in vitro CW model design, as it directly determines the biochemical environment to which cells, pathogens and biomaterials are exposed37. WE is a complex biological fluid that plays a central role in tissue repair and immune regulation. Under physiological conditions, exudate contributes positively to healing by maintaining a moist environment and supporting immune cell migration38. In CWs, however, its composition and biological activity become dysregulated, reflecting a persistent inflammatory state: WE is characterised by elevated MMPs, pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, and a disrupted balance between proteolytic enzymes and their inhibitors (TIMPs), favouring excessive ECM degradation5,31,39. An ideal culture medium should therefore simulate this biochemical milieu, encompassing high protease activity, oxidative stress mediated by ROS, persistent pro-inflammatory cytokines, degraded ECM fragments and blood-derived substances40–42. Models that omit these stressors risk oversimplifying the CW environment and overestimating therapeutic efficacy, including the resilience of bioengineered scaffolds under proteolytic conditions32,39.

Standard media such as DMEM lack these wound-specific features. Wound-simulating media (WSM), defined culture formulations typically based on meat-derived broth enriched with mammalian serum, designed to partially replicate the nutritional and biochemical composition of wound fluid, provide a more physiologically relevant nutrient profile and are particularly useful in biofilm research26. However, WSMs do not contain the full spectrum of proteolytic enzymes, ROS, or cytokine profiles characteristic of CW exudate, and further refinement and standardisation of culture media formulations remain essential26.

CW exudate is not characterised by a single elevated biomarker but by a converging pattern of simultaneous biochemical dysregulation across multiple axes (Table 1). Proteolytic imbalance is the most consistently documented feature: in non-healing VLUs, MMP-2 and MMP-9 reach 944 and 483 ng/mL respectively, while TIMP-1 falls to 2.5 ng/mL, an 11-fold reduction relative to healing conditions43. MPO activity in infected WE reaches 92.2 U/mL, approximately 50-fold above non-infected levels, generating hypochlorous acid that further inhibits TIMPs and perpetuates ECM degradation44. Inflammatory mediators are elevated by up to two orders of magnitude relative to acute wounds, while albumin falls to 17 g/L, glucose to 1.2 mmol/L and pH rises to 7.9–8.729,45,46. Two features of this dataset are critically important for model design: the pathological state is defined by the co-occurrence of these disruptions rather than any single parameter, underscoring the need for multi-component systems; and the available data are limited in scope, with DFU and PU parameters represented primarily as fold-changes or qualitative observations rather than absolute concentrations27,47. 2D and 3D CW in vitro models

CW models can broadly be categorised into two main formats: 2D and 3D models (Fig. 2). This classification reflects increasing efforts to balance experimental simplicity with physiological relevance. 2D models typically involve mono-cultures of keratinocytes, fibroblasts or immune cells23,48,49. They are straightforward to handle, allow high-throughput screening, and are highly reproducible. However, they fail to replicate the complex spatial architecture of the skin, lack ECM interactions, and do not allow the formation of chemical gradients, such as those of oxygen or inflammatory mediators. In contrast, 3D models introduce depth and structure, enabling more accurate cell–matrix and cell–cell interactions20,50,51. These models include scaffold-based constructs (e.g. collagen gels, decellularised matrices), self-assembled skin equivalents, and bioprinted tissues. Importantly, they allow the integration of pathophysiological elements such as biofilms, inflammatory gradients and enzymatic activity into a defined spatial context50,52,53. 2D models

2D in vitro models have long been a cornerstone of biomedical research due to their simplicity, cost-effectiveness and ease of use48. Since their first introduction and widespread adoption in early cell biology and wound healing studies, these platforms have been extensively applied to CW in vitro testing, providing fundamental insights into cell behaviour, drug responses and basic biological processes under tightly controlled conditions26,42,49. However, despite their longstanding and widespread use, 2D models rely on flat, rigid substrates that fail to capture the three-dimensional architecture, biochemical gradients and dynamic microenvironment that define CWs49. Consequently, their limited physiological relevance has become increasingly apparent, highlighting a clear need for more advanced and biomimetic in vitro models. Even though 2D models cannot fully capture the spatial and molecular complexity of CWs, they remain indispensable tools for early mechanistic studies and therapeutic screening.

Wound healing assay

In vitro wound healing assays are commonly based on 2D monolayers of dermal fibroblasts or keratinocytes, in which a cell-free area is generated by mechanical, thermal or chemical disruption of a confluent layer, and closure is monitored via microscopy, fluorescence or impedance-based platforms49. The assay offers experimental simplicity and compatibility with high-throughput screening, making it a practical platform for early-stage evaluation of compounds influencing cellular migration and proliferation. However, it does not capture the chronic inflammatory environment or the biochemical stressors present in non-healing wounds. Persistent cytokine exposure, oxidative stress and elevated proteolytic activity are absent, and wound closure under standard culture conditions occurs rapidly, masking the impaired cellular responses that characterise chronic pathologies.

Recent refinements have sought to address this acute wound bias by introducing sustained inflammatory stimulation. In one representative study, a delayed closure model was established in HaCaT keratinocytes and human dermal fibroblasts using a pro-inflammatory cocktail of IFN-γ (15 ng/mL), PMA (25 ng/mL) and LPS (500 ng/mL), producing consistent closure inhibition accompanied by induction of 39 cytokines including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-17 at greater than fivefold increases, recapitulating key features of the clinical CW cytokine landscape55. Validation with a mesenchymal stem cell-derived secretome restored closure in keratinocytes but not fibroblasts, illustrating cell-type-specific responses and a broader limitation of simplified 2D systems: despite capturing selected inflammatory features, these models lack immune cell populations, oxidative stress mediators, active proteolytic activity and ECM interactions. Delayed scratch closure assays are therefore best positioned as first-line mechanistic screening tools for isolating the contribution of individual inflammatory mediators to cell behaviour, but are insufficient as standalone models of CW pathophysiology49. Their translational value increases substantially when biochemical stressors such as active proteases, ROS-generating systems and aetiology-specific culture media are incorporated.

Host cell–biofilm interaction models

A range of in vitro models have been developed to study the interaction between human skin cells and bacterial biofilms, aiming to replicate the persistent infection and inflammation characteristic of CWs26,56–59. Thaarup et al. identified eight defining components of such models, of which WSM, host matrix composition, continuous nutrient supplementation, polymicrobial biofilms and physicochemical gradients were considered most relevant for mimicking CW conditions26. Studies using biofilm-conditioned medium (BCM) have provided mechanistic insights into how biofilm secretions impair wound healing independently of direct bacterial contact. S. aureus biofilms grown from clinical CW isolates reduced keratinocyte scratch closure from 68.7 ± 5.1% in controls to 7.3 ± 4.7% at 24 h, with progressive expansion of the wound area by 72 h (p < 0.0001), and induced apoptosis in 90.4 ± 1.2% of exposed keratinocytes58. BCM alone reproduced these effects with statistical equivalence to direct co-culture, confirming that biofilm-derived soluble factors rather than nutrient depletion or pH changes are responsible58. In fibroblasts, BCM and planktonic-conditioned medium induced distinct proteolytic responses: BCM drove elevated TNF-α and suppressed MMP-3, while planktonic medium elevated IL-6, IL-8, MMP-1 and MMP-3, suggesting that biofilm-derived factors differentially modulate the fibroblast secretome in ways that contribute to the protease imbalance of non-healing wounds57. Biofilm-derived soluble factors alone are sufficient to impair keratinocyte and fibroblast function across multiple axes: S. aureus BCM reduced keratinocyte proliferation to 30.3 ± 5.7% of control values, while P. aeruginosa BCM was too cytotoxic to permit completion of migration assays, collectively recapitulating several features of the dysregulated cellular environment of non-healing wounds59.

A complementary approach uses unprocessed clinical WE applied directly to healthy donor fibroblasts as a functional readout of chronicity60. Doerfler et al. demonstrated that exudates from non-healing wounds of diverse aetiologies consistently inhibited fibroblast proliferation and ECM deposition, correlating with elevated MPO and neutrophil elastase activities rather than overall MMP activity60. Transcriptomic analysis revealed convergent induction of inflammatory cytokine pathways and the unfolded protein response, yet individual blockade of IL-1 signalling nor chemical inhibition of endoplasmic reticulum stress rescued proliferation, indicating that the pathological effect cannot be attributed to any single mediator, reinforcing a central premise of the present framework: the non-healing phenotype is sustained by the co-occurrence of multiple interdependent biochemical drivers, and models isolating individual components will systematically underestimate both biological complexity and therapeutic resistance. However, most of these setups still lack essential components of CW pathology, including inflammatory cytokines at clinically relevant concentrations, active proteolytic enzymes, such as MMP-2 and MMP-9, and oxidative stress mediators, such as MPO. Their translational value could be substantially enhanced by incorporating these biochemical stressors alongside the host–microbe interface, either through enriched culture media or integration with more complex multicellular platforms.

Lubbock CW biofilm model

The Lubbock chronic wound biofilm (LCWB) model provides an in vitro representation of multispecies biofilms within a fibrin-based matrix, supporting rapid biofilm maturation within 48 h at densities up to 7.05 × 106 CFU/mg42. Its utility for antimicrobial screening has been demonstrated across multiple studies: salicylic acid selectively inhibited S. aureus while xylitol preferentially targeted P. aeruginosa, illustrating the model’s capacity to resolve species-specific responses within a polymicrobial community, and silver-containing dressings reduced S. aureus biofilm by one to seven log CFU compared to negligible activity from non-antimicrobial formulations61,62. Translational extensions include in vitro-to-in vivo biofilm transplantation onto murine wounds, generating stable polymicrobial infections that remained heterogeneous across four species for at least 12 days, with bacteria showing 2.3- to 3-fold greater tolerance to bleach and gentamicin compared to monospecies contexts, and P. aeruginosa itself demonstrating 2-fold increased tolerance when grown polymicrobially63. The model has also been adapted for veterinary applications, with a dual-species canine wound variant demonstrating species-specific spatial organisation consistent with human CW biopsies64. Despite this breadth of application, the LCWB model lacks host tissue architecture, immune cell components, and the biochemical stressors that define the CW microenvironment, including active MMPs, oxidative mediators and inflammatory cytokines. It therefore functions best as a biofilm-focused screening tool within stepwise experimental pipelines, rather than as a standalone model of CW pathophysiology.

Flat-bed perfusion model

The flat-bed perfusion biofilm wound model studies biofilm behaviour under dynamic conditions that partially simulate the exudative environment of CWs, culturing P. aeruginosa and S. aureus within cellulose matrices under continuous perfusion with WSM65,66. Steady-state biofilms reach densities of approximately 107 CFU per matrix within 24 h, with real-time tracking enabled by bioluminescent P. aeruginosa65. The model has demonstrated utility for differentiating bactericidal from bacteriostatic effects of topical dressings: an iodine dressing reduced P. aeruginosa biofilm by 2 log-fold within 3 h with partial recovery by 24 h, while a silver dressing produced a more gradual 1 log-fold reduction sustained over 24 h but showed minimal efficacy against S. aureus, contrasting with its known activity against planktonic cultures and illustrating how the biofilm phenotype alters treatment susceptibility67. Bioluminescence monitoring correlated strongly with viable counts across a 3-log-fold range, enabling non-invasive kinetic tracking. However, the model is acellular and relies on single-species biofilms within an inert scaffold, precluding host cell interactions and failing to capture the polymicrobial dynamics, pH gradients, hypoxia and oxidative and proteolytic landscape characteristic of non-healing wounds. Its translational relevance would increase substantially through the incorporation of host cells, polymicrobial communities and the biochemical stressors of CW exudate.

Outlook: designing mechanistically and pathologically relevant 2D CW models

Taken together, the 2D models evaluated offer complementary but partial perspectives on CW pathophysiology (Table 2). Scratch assays provide experimental control over individual cellular responses but lack biochemical complexity. Dynamic perfusion models introduce physiologically relevant flow conditions but remain acellular. A critical limitation lies not in excessive superposition of pathological factors but in insufficient integration of the features that define CW pathophysiology. MMP-2, MMP-9, MPO, ROS and pro-inflammatory cytokines are largely absent from existing systems, despite their well-documented roles in tissue damage, cell senescence and microbial persistence32,68,69. Their exclusion compromises the biological relevance of observed cellular behaviours30,31. Crucially, these features must not be implemented in isolation: MMP-driven ECM degradation releases DAMP fragments that sustain TLR-mediated cytokine signalling; cytokines upregulate further MMP expression; ROS generated by MPO directly inhibit TIMPs; alkaline pH enhances protease catalytic function; and biofilm-associated TLR ligands independently maintain the inflammatory state 70. Models incorporating only one or two of these features will capture neither the emergent biology nor the therapeutic resistance of non-healing wounds. A feature-driven approach to in vitro CW model design

Building on the biochemical evidence presented in Table 1, a feature-driven design framework is proposed for in vitro CW models. Rather than imposing fixed medium formulations, it identifies six pathological axes, namely proteolytic imbalance, persistent inflammation, oxidative stress, altered biochemical context, ECM degradation and DAMP signalling, and microbial colonisation, and maps each to key biomarkers, wound-type-specific design considerations, and recommended experimental readouts (Table 3). The proposed readouts support multidimensional evaluation of CW-like states rather than serve as universal validation thresholds, as consensus quantitative criteria for defining a reproduced non-healing phenotype across CW aetiologies have not yet been established. Where quantitative clinical exudate data are available, primarily for VLU parameters, these values serve as evidence-based benchmarks rather than direct formulation targets. Clinical exudate measurements reflect dynamic in vivo environments sustained by ongoing cellular activity and endogenous regulatory mechanisms, conditions that are not fully recapitulated in static culture systems. Consequently, quantitative values should be interpreted as clinically informed benchmarks to guide model development, while further experimental validation remains necessary to assess the extent to which CW-like states have been reproduced. Where data are absent or represented only as fold-changes, as is the case for most DFU and PU parameters, the corresponding parameters are presented as mechanistically informed guidance derived from related experimental literature or mechanistic inference. The availability of quantitative data currently differs across CW aetiologies, with VLU representing the most developed evidence base. Accordingly, DFU and PU parameters should be interpreted as mechanistically informed guidance to inform model refinement as additional quantitative datasets emerge. These considerations provide a basis for developing biochemically informed culture conditions; the quantitative gaps identified in Table 1 simultaneously constitute a call for prospective, multiparameter characterisation of WE across VLU, DFU and PU to advance the evidence base for CWSM development.

The pathological axes described in Table 3 are not intended to be interpreted as independent or uniformly implemented modules. Rather, they represent interacting features of CW biology whose relative contribution and degree of integration are expected to vary according to wound aetiology, research objective and model complexity. The framework is therefore intended to support progressive and modular incorporation of pathological features, rather than simultaneous implementation of all components.

The same biochemical deficit that constrains in vitro CW models is equally apparent in the SWF used to evaluate wound dressings and biomaterials. Existing SWF formulations (Table 4) range from simple electrolyte solutions to protein-containing biomimetic preparations, but none include active proteolytic enzymes, pro-inflammatory cytokines, oxidative mediators such as MPO, or wound-type-specific biochemical components70. As a consequence, dressings and biomaterials are routinely evaluated under conditions that systematically underestimate the hostile biochemical environment they will encounter clinically. The CWSM framework presented in Table 3 provides a basis for developing SWF formulations that reflect this complexity across VLU, DFU and PU contexts.

VLU, DFU and PU share a common core of pathological features, but their dominant biochemical drivers differ substantially, precluding a single universal medium. By separating a shared core from wound-type-specific modules, the framework allows the same experimental platform to address different research questions without requiring entirely independent protocols, facilitating incremental validation of individual components. Ultimately, the value of these platforms will depend not on complexity alone, but on the precision with which they translate available quantitative evidence on CW exudate into experimentally defined and reproducible conditions.

3D models

3D in vitro models address several structural limitations of 2D systems by incorporating spatial complexity, heterogeneous cellular organisation and ECM-like scaffolds, enabling the study of cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions, nutrient and oxygen gradients, and the influence of architectural cues on cellular behaviour24. Human skin equivalents (HSEs), collagen-based biofilm matrices, 3D-bioprinted constructs, and microfluidic wound-on-chip platforms represent the principal approaches in this category, each offering improved physiological relevance compared to monolayer cultures71–73. However, structural complexity alone does not confer pathological relevance: the same biochemical deficits identified in 2D systems, absence of sustained proteolytic activity, oxidative stress, persistent inflammatory signalling and wound-type-specific biochemical context, persist in current 3D models, limiting their capacity to recapitulate the non-healing phenotype of CWs.

Human skin equivalents

HSEs provide an advanced 3D platform incorporating a stratified epidermis on a fibroblast-populated dermal matrix, replicating key architectural and functional characteristics of human skin74. TERT-immortalised HSEs eliminate donor variation while maintaining keratinocyte proliferation indices and cytokine profiles similar to primary constructs72. Their utility for studying infection dynamics has been demonstrated through bacterial colonisation studies: fibrin-based HSEs supported stable colonisation by S. aureus at densities of 102–106 CFU SE−1, with S. aureus inducing a substantially broader innate immune response than S. epidermidis, upregulating 480 transcripts including TLR2, IL-1β, IL-17C and TNF75–77. Wounded Graftskin supported time-dependent biofilm formation by both S. aureus and P. aeruginosa, with S. aureus forming architecturally complex biofilms penetrating the dermal layer at earlier time points, recapitulating spatial features observed in clinical wound biopsies77.

Despite these advances, current HSEs lack persistent cytokine signalling, sustained MMP activity, oxidative stress and progressive ECM degradation72,74. TNF-α and IL-1α remain low or undetectable under basal conditions, and VEGF and HGF fall below detection limits72. Recent incorporation of patient-derived cells has begun to address these gaps: HSEs containing DFU-derived fibroblasts and blood-derived monocytes demonstrated M1 macrophage polarisation with significantly elevated IL-1β, IL-8 and TNF-α, and DFU fibroblasts specifically drove IL-6 secretion independently of macrophage source (p < 0.0001)78. However, such models occupy a conceptual boundary between in vitro and ex vivo systems, introducing inter-patient variability that complicates reproducibility and mechanistic interpretation. Despite recent advances in cellular complexity, the absence of active proteases, oxidative mediators, and wound-type-specific biochemical context means that current HSEs reproduce acute rather than chronic wound biology.

Matrix-based in vitro wound models

Matrix-based in vitro wound models provide 3D scaffolds that support biofilm formation within a structurally relevant semi-solid environment, better approximating the tissue matrix of infected wounds22. Collagen-based perfusion models support reproducible steady-state biofilms: Slade et al. demonstrated P. aeruginosa biofilms reaching 6.0–8.0 × 1010 CFU slide−1 by 30 h, with ceftazidime at 40 mg/L achieving only a 1.2–1.6 log reduction, leaving viable bacteria at 109 CFU g−1, far exceeding the 105 CFU g−1 clinical infection threshold66. Real-time metabolic monitoring detected ammonia at 2273 ppb and hydrogen cyanide at 138 ppb, volatile markers undetectable in static models66. Thaarup et al. refined this approach using collagen matrices with WSM perfusion, sustaining a stable polymicrobial S. aureus and P. aeruginosa community over 6 days, with spontaneous development of alkaline pH ≥8.0 and anoxic conditions at 0.4–0.8 mm depth, closely resembling non-healing wound microenvironments22. Composite collagen-fibrin hydrogels further demonstrated temporal biofilm maturation with upregulation of adhesion genes icaA, icaD, clfA and clfB at 48 h (p < 0.05), and species-specific antimicrobial tolerance patterns consistent with clinical observations73. Despite these capabilities, matrix-based models remain predominantly acellular and do not reproduce sustained inflammatory signalling, protease overexpression, oxidative stress, or progressive ECM degradation. Their representation of chronicity is limited to biofilm persistence, spatial organisation and microenvironmental gradients, without capturing the host–microbe biochemical interplay that drives the non-healing phenotype22,79. They are therefore best positioned as infection-focused screening tools for antimicrobial efficacy testing under wound-like physicochemical conditions.

3D printed models

3D bioprinting enables the precise, layer-by-layer deposition of living cells, ECM components, and bioactive molecules into defined spatial configurations, offering structural and cellular complexity beyond conventional models50. Digital light processing (DLP) based bioprinting using composite silk fibroin-gelatin methacrylate bioinks has enabled fabrication of full-thickness skin constructs incorporating fibroblasts, keratinocytes and endothelial cells, with EGF treatment producing significantly higher re-epithelialisation rates and upregulation of cytokeratin 13, FGF and CD31 compared to untreated controls at weeks 1 and 2 (p < 0.05)80. Incorporation of DFU-derived fibroblasts demonstrated impaired wound-healing capacity and M1 macrophage polarisation compared to non-diabetic controls, recapitulating key hallmarks of the DFU microenvironment74. Drug delivery functionality has also been integrated: PCL-based scaffolds loaded with levofloxacin demonstrated sustained antibiotic release over 4 weeks with inhibitory activity against S. aureus and E. coli81. While structural complexity has improved, most bioprinted constructs are designed to model acute wound repair rather than the persistent inflammatory and proteolytic milieu that defines chronicity. Persistent cytokine signalling, MMP overexpression, oxidative stress, and microbial biofilm colonisation are largely absent, and no existing system simultaneously integrates all pathological features of non-healing wounds24. Technical challenges, including shear stress-induced cell damage, unpredictable scaffold degradation, and absence of standardised fabrication protocols, further constrain reproducibility50,54. Incorporation of biochemically defined CWSM and real-time monitoring of pathological readouts represents the most promising direction for advancing these platforms towards CW pathophysiological relevance.

Microfluidic and skin-on-chip models

Recent advances in microfluidics and organ-on-chip engineering have enabled increasingly sophisticated in vitro skin models integrating dynamic perfusion, multicellular organisation and controlled microenvironmental cues82–85. Sriram et al. developed a fibrin-based full-thickness skin-on-chip in which dynamic perfusion enhanced epidermal differentiation, dermo-epidermal junction formation, and barrier function compared to static culture, with integrated permeability testing directly on-chip82. To address immune responsiveness, Ramadan and Ting developed a microfluidic co-culture of keratinocytes and dendritic-like immune cells enabling real-time cytokine profiling under inflammatory stimulation, though the system remains restricted to an epidermal-immune interface without fibroblasts, ECM degradation, or microbial challenge83. Mori et al. engineered a full-thickness skin equivalent containing endothelialised microchannels with size-selective permeability, providing a foundation for modelling vascular inflammation and impaired perfusion85. Biglari et al. developed a multicellular wound-on-chip co-culturing fibroblasts, endothelial cells and macrophages, in which TNF-α stimulation induced elevated IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-8 secretion, endothelial junction disruption, and impaired vascular network formation, while dexamethasone restored endothelial integrity and reduced cytokine levels84.

These platforms share common limitations: most lack polymicrobial biofilms, protease-rich environments, hypoxia and oxidative stress, and few capture the transition from acute injury to non-resolving chronicity. Future developments should prioritise modular platforms integrating immune-competent cellular layers, perfusable microvasculature capable of modelling ischaemia-reperfusion cycles, polymicrobial biofilms, and the biochemical features of CW exudate, including elevated MMPs, ROS and pro-inflammatory cytokines.

Outlook: towards mechanistically and pathologically relevant 3D CW models

A critical conceptual distinction has yet to be fully resolved across the 3D model landscape: the difference between using advanced fabrication technologies to construct in vitro CW models versus using them to fabricate therapeutic constructs subsequently evaluated as proof-of-concept material assessments50,51,86. Most studies employing bioprinted constructs fall into the latter category, reporting cellular viability, re-epithelialisation kinetics and drug release profiles that characterise the material rather than the pathology, advancing material fabrication at the expense of pathological modelling50,81.

Microfluidic and skin-on-chip platforms represent a more direct attempt at pathological modelling, yet share the same fundamental gap: the absence of polymicrobial biofilms, proteolytic mediators, and the biochemical complexity of CW exudate82–85. The most advanced current platforms approach chronicity from a single axis: infection models capture biofilm persistence but lack host response; HSEs incorporating patient-derived macrophages reproduce inflammatory polarisation but lack microbial colonisation and sustained proteolytic activity; wound-on-chip systems recapitulate endothelial dysfunction but omit wound-type-specific biochemistry. To address this gap, a shift from fabrication-driven to pathology-driven design is required. The feature-driven framework proposed (Table 3) and the comparative analysis of SWF formulations (Table 4) provide a basis for this approach, which requires integrating at least four components: an immune-competent cellular compartment with wound-aetiology-specific phenotypes; perfusable microvasculature capable of modelling ischaemia and endothelial dysfunction; a polymicrobial biofilm component within a biochemically defined WSM; and real-time readouts for pH, oxygen tension, MMP activity and cytokine secretion. Progress will require not only technical integration but methodological standardisation, encompassing reproducible fabrication protocols, validated readouts, and consensus criteria for what constitutes an in vitro CW phenotype.

Conclusions

CWs remain a significant clinical challenge precisely because their pathology is sustained by the co-occurrence of multiple interdependent processes: persistent inflammation, proteolytic imbalance, microbial colonisation and impaired tissue regeneration. The in vitro models reviewed here have advanced considerably in structural and cellular fidelity, yet none reproduces this co-occurrence in a biochemically defined and aetiology-specific manner. 2D systems offer experimental simplicity but lack the spatial and biochemical complexity of non-healing wounds. 3D platforms introduce structural relevance without resolving the underlying deficit: most approach chronicity from a single axis, and the field has progressively advanced material fabrication at the expense of pathological modelling. No existing system simultaneously integrates the inflammatory, proteolytic, microbial, and vascular features that define wound chronicity across VLU, DFU and PU contexts.

Bridging this gap requires a shift in design logic, from structural mimicry towards pathology-driven model construction informed by quantitative clinical evidence. The quantitative synthesis of clinical WE data presented here, and the CWSM framework derived from it, provide a basis for this reorientation, translating available quantitative evidence on CW exudate into experimentally defined and reproducible conditions stratified by wound aetiology. Standardised biochemical conditioning, validated readouts, and modular multicellular architectures will be necessary to develop platforms capable of reproducing key features of the non-healing phenotype in vitro with sufficient fidelity to support mechanistic research and preclinical therapeutic evaluation. As regulatory frameworks increasingly demand human-relevant preclinical evidence, the development of biochemically defined, aetiology-stratified CW models represents not only a scientific priority but a translational imperative for the wound care field.
