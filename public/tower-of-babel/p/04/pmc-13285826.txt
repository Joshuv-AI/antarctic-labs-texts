Update of germ line RUNX1 variant curation rules: version 3.1∗†
Authors: Zachary Hattig, Tilda E Carlelycke, Amagoia Ruiz Martin, Taylor Walker, Nancy A Speck, Paul Liu, David Wu, Lucy A Godley, Myeloid Malignancy Variant Curation Expert Panel
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR:

Within the Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen), Variant Curation Expert Panels (VCEPs) specify variant classification criteria for individual genes.1 The Myeloid Malignancy VCEP (MM-VCEP) establishes and updates curation guidelines for genes associated with myeloid malignancies, initially focusing on RUNX1 and its associated conditions, hereditary thrombocytopenia and hematopoietic malignancies.2, 3, 4, 5 The precise classification of RUNX1 variants remains a central challenge in the diagnosis of hereditary hematopoietic malignancies.6

Following the initial guidelines in 20192,3 and their revision in 2022,5 further work on RUNX1 has prompted refinement of the interpretive tools, particularly for variants of uncertain significance (VUS). Therefore, we developed a second update to RUNX1 curation rules, version 3.1 (Figure 1; supplemental Table 1), which introduces modifications to 7 key evidence codes (PM1, PM5, PM2, PM4, PVS1, PS1, and BP7). These modified curation rules reflect current understanding of RUNX1 and result in the following reclassifications: 4 likely pathogenic (LP) variants were upgraded to pathogenic (P; supplemental Table 2); 13 VUS were upgraded to LP (supplemental Table 3); and 84 VUSs were downgraded to likely benign (supplemental Table 4). Below, we outline the rationale for these updated rules and provide some context for each change.

The PM1 criterion focuses on critical residues within the runt-homology domain, essential for DNA binding together with its cofactor core binding factor beta.7 To better capture the functional impact of amino acid substitutions in this region, PM1 has been upgraded to PM1_strong for missense variants affecting 13 highly conserved residues essential for DNA binding: R107, K110, A134, R162, R166, S167, R169, G170, K194, T196, D198, R201, and R204 (amino acid numbering is according to NP_001001890.1; supplemental Table 1). Substitutions at these residues are almost invariably associated with pathogenicity, primarily through disruption of protein conformation and functional integrity, thereby justifying elevation of the evidence strength from moderate to strong (Figure 2). To prevent overweighing pathogenicity, PM5 or PS1 should not be applied if PM1 is used. When evaluating missense variants, we revised PM5 to account for Grantham scores,8 which prevent overweighing conservative substitutions.9 We also reclassified variants at 1 hot spot residue: c.486G>C (p.Arg162Ser) and c.486G>T (p.Arg162Ser) have moved from LP to VUS (supplemental Table 5).

With the fivefold expansion of gnomAD version 4.1.0 data and the consequent more accurate assessment of rare variants, we adjusted PM2_supporting by elevating the allele frequency threshold to an upper frequency of ≤0.00005. In the original specifications, variants had to be completely absent from all population databases to meet the PM2_supporting criterion. With the larger current database, this specification is too stringent, because known P variants not found in earlier versions are now observed in gnomAD version 4.1.0. This updated threshold was derived empirically by defining the upper bound using the most frequent P variant, p.Arg204Gln. To maintain quality control, we require a mean gene coverage of ≥20× at the position. When available, we recommend using the GroupMax filtering allele frequency, or we require that all subpopulations meet the PM2_supporting threshold.

If in-frame deletions/insertions involve the PM1-defined hot spot residues, PM4 is elevated to PM4_strong (supplemental Table 1). The criterion now also encompasses stop-loss variants, which comprise a known mechanism of disease.10

Following recommendations to adjust the REVEL score threshold to 0.644,11 we conducted a validation study on RUNX1 missense variants (supplemental Figure 1), reflecting previous observations regarding the inherent limitations of in silico predictors.12 If we accept a REVEL score threshold of 0.644, then among the 736 ClinGen-classified missense variants, 62 would be upgraded from VUS to LP, 13 from LP to P, and 1 from likely benign to VUS, corresponding to 10.32%. Therefore, the Pejaver et al11 threshold overestimates RUNX1 pathogenicity. Given the strong performance of our previous receiver operating characteristic analysis, the MM-VCEP retained the original REVEL threshold of ≥0.88 for PP3.

We adopted variable-strength codes that integrate RNA functional data, including PVS1_variable and BP7_variable, according to ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation Working Group recommendations.13 PVS1_variable allows the strength level to be modulated based on the functional impact of an aberrant mRNA. PS1 now includes splicing variants with RNA evidence predicting the same functional defect as a known P/LP variant, following the same rationale as for missense variants (supplemental Figure 2).13 A canonical splice site variant (c.508+2T>C) was reclassified from P to LP.

BP7 updates ensure application only to synonymous or intronic variants with minimal likelihood of functional impact. Due to its limited predictive value, conservation is no longer required. Instead, BP7 now relies on a low predicted splicing impact (SpliceAI Δ score ≤0.20)14 for intronic variants at or beyond +7 or −21 from the canonical splice site. Synonymous variants are excluded if located within the last 3 or the first nucleotide of an exon preceding a canonical splice site (supplemental Table 1; supplemental Figure 2).13 We introduced a BP7_variable code for variants with experimental RNA data showing no splicing effect.

The updated RUNX1 version 3.1 variant classification guidelines enhance diagnostic accuracy. By leveraging large-scale population resources, such as gnomAD version 4.1, and refining gene-specific evidence criteria, these guidelines provide a more consistent and reliable framework for variant interpretation. The updated variant classifications underscore how data-driven rule revision reduces uncertainty, enhances diagnostic precision, and supports informed clinical decision-making. Looking ahead, we can expect further refinement from emerging technologies, such as gene-wide functional approaches and multiplexed assays of variant effect,15 which hold promise for further minimizing uncertainty in variant interpretation.
