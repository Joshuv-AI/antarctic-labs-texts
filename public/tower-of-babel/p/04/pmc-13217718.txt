Knowledge and perceptions on dengue among internship medical students and rural physicians in Ecuador: a cross-sectional study, 2025
Authors: Manuel Calvopiña, Juan S Izquierdo-Condoy, Jorge Vasconez-Gonzalez, Alice Gaibor-Pazmiño, William Cevallos, Leonardo Bravo, Esteban Ortiz-Prado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDengue is a public health concern in Ecuador, with sustained transmission in the Pacific Coast, Amazon, Galapagos Islands, and emerging in the Andean region. Adequate knowledge and perceptions among medical students and physicians are essential for diagnosis, appropriate management, patient education, and control. This study evaluated dengue-related knowledge and perceptions among Ecuadorian students in the internship program (interns) and physicians in the rural medical service program, and to identify factors associated with higher knowledge scores.MethodsA nationwide cross-sectional study was conducted between January and July 2025 using a self-administered online questionnaire. The 44-item survey covered knowledge of dengue (0–27 points) across four domains—aetiology/pathophysiology (6 questions), clinical features (6), diagnosis (5), and management/treatment (10)—as well as training experiences and perceptions (14 questions). Descriptive statistics and inferential analyses were performed using χ² tests, Student’s t-test, one-way ANOVA, and Pearson’s correlation, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsA total of 811 responses were analysed (455 interns and 356 rural GPs; mean age 25.8 ± 3.4 years; 61.3% female). The overall mean knowledge score was 14.0 ± 3.4/27 points, slightly above the midpoint. Scores were highest for aetiology/pathophysiology (4.3 ± 1.0/6) and diagnosis (2.7 ± 0.9/5), but lowest for clinical features (2.4 ± 1.2/6) and management/treatment (4.5 ± 1.7/10). Higher scores were significantly associated with prior lectures in tropical medicine and previous experience diagnosing or treating dengue cases. No statistically significant differences were observed in overall knowledge between the two groups. However, physicians were more likely than interns to report dengue-specific training in the past year (64.8%; p = 0.001), familiarity with the dengue official guidelines (64.3%; p = 0.001), and prior clinical experience managing dengue cases (62.4%; p = 0.001). Over half of participants perceived insufficient dengue training during medical education and believed that health authorities do not adequately train physicians.ConclusionsThis first nationwide study reveals critical gaps in dengue knowledge, particularly in clinical recognition and management, among Ecuadorian medical interns and rural physicians. Strengthening tropical medicine curricula and continuing professional education is needed to improve clinical preparedness and enhance the healthcare system’s capacity to respond to ongoing dengue transmission and outbreaks.Keywords: Cross-Sectional, Dengue, Ecuador, Education, KAP, Medicine

Introduction

Dengue is the world’s fastest-growing mosquito-borne disease, with 2024 marking a record high of over 14 million reported cases and 9,500 + deaths globally, representing a 12-fold increase since 2014. The Americas region bears over 90% of the burden, though cases are rising worldwide [1]. In Ecuador, despite dengue infection is endemic and considered a significant public health concern by the Ministry of Health [2], no study have assessed the knowledge and perceptions on internship medical students and physicians doing the Rural Medical Service Program, leaving a gap in evidence on the preparedness of future and recent graduated physicians to address dengue. One study has assessed the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) on dengue in healthcare professional’s nurses and physicians working in Machala city [3]. In February 2024, Organización Panamericana de la Salud (OPS) declare the Epidemiological Alert due to increase in dengue cases in the Americas, and Ecuador remains among the countries with sustained transmission [4]. Endemicity and outbreaks are reported in rural settlements of Ecuador [5–7].

Dengue transmission in Ecuador occurs year-round with seasonal peaks during the rainy months in the warm Coast and Amazon, including the Galapagos Islands, and has recently expanded into Andean region [6–8]. Reported cases have increased in recent years, from 16,017 in 2022 to 27,838 in 2023—comprising 86.5% dengue without warning signs, 13.1% with warning signs, and 0.4% severe dengue—and reached to 61,329 cases in 2024. By the epidemiological week 15 of 2025, 15,814 cases had already been recorded, predominantly without warning signs [9]. Some areas of the country are currently hyperendemic, with all four DENV serotypes detected in circulation [4, 10]. The vector Aedes aegypti, is present in all subtropical and tropical regions of continental Ecuador (Coastal and Amazon) and in the Galapagos Islands [11, 12]. It is estimated that 70% of the country’s land area has environmental conditions for the reproduction of the Ae. aegypti; furthermore, Ae. albopictus, a potential vector for DENV, is present in the coastal and Amazonian regions and in the foothills of the Andes Mountain [13].

Medical training in Ecuador, includes a one-year mandatory Rotating Internship Program for final-year students or medical interns [14], during which they rotate through key specialties under the supervision of certified physicians in public and private hospitals located in large cities. This program bridges theoretical knowledge with clinical practice and culminates in the degree of general physicians. Graduates must then pass the national Qualifying Examination for Professional Practice in Medicine (EHEP), administered by the Council for Quality Assurance in Higher Education (CACES), which assesses competencies across medical disciplines, covering tropical diseases including dengue. Successful candidates are legally required to complete a one-year Rural Medical Service [15], providing primary healthcare in suburban and rural areas under the Ministry of Public Health (MoH). This rural service equips new physicians with practical experience in resource-limited settings, strengthening their diagnostic, community health skills, and diseases prevention. Tropical medicine is a relevant subject in medical education in Ecuador, given the country’s geographic location and the prevalence of tropical diseases [16]. However, not all universities include this discipline in their curriculum. Of the 30 universities in Ecuador that offer a medical degree, only 16 teach tropical medicine —10 of them are public institutions, with 9 located in the Andean region [17]. Inadequate knowledge on tropical vector borne diseases, vector ecology, misinterpretation of case definitions and classification, or suboptimal preventive counselling may hinder national control strategies, clinical worsening and fatalities [18–20].

In Ecuador, although severe dengue represents a smaller proportion of total cases, it continues to cause hospitalizations and deaths, particularly among children and vulnerable populations [21]. The co-circulation of other arboviruses such as chikungunya, Zika, and more recently Oropuche has further complicated clinical diagnosis, management, and surveillance [22, 23]. Ecuador’s MoH adopts PAHO/WHO guidelines for dengue case definitions, clinical phases, diagnosis, management, and prevention, classifying cases as dengue without warning signs, dengue with warning signs, and severe dengue [24]. Laboratory confirmation relies on detection of NS1 antigen by ELISA, while viral isolation and RNA detection by PCR are recommended within the first five days of symptoms onset. All NS1-positive samples are referred for confirmatory testing [25].

Medical students, particularly those in their final year (interns), and physicians labouring in suburban and rural settings represent a crucial segment of the healthcare workforce, as they often serve in primary care facilities and in remote communities where laboratory confirmation may be limited. Hence, this survey using an online questionnaire aims to assess and compare the knowledge on epidemiology, diagnosis, clinical management, and prevention of dengue, together with training experiences and perceptions of dengue, among medical interns and rural physicians in Ecuador. Understanding their preparedness is important to identify educational gaps, enhance medical curricula, reinforce clinical training, and design targeted interventions that improve both clinical and preventive competencies, ultimately strengthening the capacity to respond effectively to dengue.

Methods

Study design

This is a nationwide cross-sectional study aimed to assess the level of knowledge on dengue as well as training experiences and perceptions, among Ecuadorian medical students in the final-year (Internship Program) and physicians working in rural health centers of Ecuador, based on an online, self-administered, closed-ended questionnaire.

Country of study

Ecuador is in northwestern South America and is geographically divided by the Andes Mountains into three main continental regions: the Pacific coastal lowlands, the Andean highlands, and the Amazon basin, in addition to the Galápagos Islands. Approximately 70% of its continental territory consists of tropical and subtropical lowlands, particularly along the Pacific coast and in the Amazon region, where climatic conditions favour the transmission of vector-borne diseases such as dengue. Climate variability is strongly influenced by the El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO), which is associated with increased rainfall and flooding in coastal areas and western Andean slopes, factors that contribute to fluctuations in dengue transmission patterns [26, 27].

Participants

One group were the final-year medical students or medical interns performing the mandatory year named Rotating Internship Program [14]. Students rotate in both public and private hospitals placed in the four ecoregions of the country. The second group were the recently graduated physicians doing the mandatory one-year Rural Medical Service Program, administered by the MoH. GPs are posted in primary health care centers located in suburban and in remote rural or underserved communities [15]. Completion of this program is a legal requirement for obtaining professional accreditation and the right to practice medicine in Ecuador.

Questionnaire and data measurement

The questionnaire was developed by the research team based on the dengue diagnostic, treatment, and prevention guidelines from PAHO, the Ecuadorian MoH [24, 25], and from previously validated questionnaires in similar studies [18–20] adapted to the Ecuadorian context. Content was reviewed and validated by two independent experts in tropical medicine and public health. A pilot test with 25 potential respondents assessed comprehension and structure; wording and layout were refined accordingly. The final instrument comprised 44 closed-ended items organized into five sections: (1) sociodemographic characteristics (3 questions); (2) university training and workplace information (4 questions); (3) dengue-related preparation and experience (6 questions); (4) knowledge on dengue (27 questions), and (5) perceptions (4 questions). Knowledge was measured with 27 items across four domains: etiology and pathophysiology (6 points), clinical features (6 points), diagnosis (5 points), and management and treatment (10 points). Each correct answer scored 1 point and each incorrect answer 0 (range 0–27 points). To assess internal consistency of the 27-item knowledge scale, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated. Given the dichotomous nature of the items (correct/incorrect responses), this coefficient is equivalent to the Kuder–Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20).

Data collection and management

The online platform SurveyMonkey® was used to administer the questionnaire. Data was collected between January 15 and July 31, 2025. Participants accessed the survey through a unique link distributed via national official communication groups of students in internship and rural physicians in Ecuador. The survey was disseminated nationwide through institutional coordination networks targeting individuals officially enrolled in the Rotating Internship Program and the Rural Medical Service Program during the 2025 academic cycle. The exact total number of eligible participants nationwide was not publicly accessible; therefore, a precise response rate could not be calculated. No financial or academic incentives were offered, and no reminder messages were sent after the initial distribution. Eligibility was restricted to individuals officially enrolled in either program at the time of data collection.

The introductory section outlined the study’s objectives, ensured confidentiality, and included an electronic informed consent form. The survey closed automatically in 10 min. To ensure data quality, all answers were carefully reviewed for completeness and internal consistency. Of the initial 1,131 responses, 100 respondents were excluded due to incomplete data, 54 due to inconsistencies, and 166 because participants did not provide informed consent. After this filtering process, a total of 811 valid responses were retained for analysis, comprising 455 students in internship or medical interns and 356 rural physicians (Fig. 1).

Bias and quality control

Potential biases were addressed at multiple stages. Selection bias was mitigated by nationwide dissemination across public and private institutions and both target groups; nonetheless, convenience sampling may favor respondents more engaged with dengue or tropical medicine. The self-report format may introduce recall and social desirability biases; to reduce the latter, investigators emphasized strict anonymity and the importance of honest reporting. Duplicate responses were restricted by IP control. To minimize information bias, items were grounded in guidelines and refined after expert review and piloting. Data cleaning was performed independently by two researchers; discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and proportions, while continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For categorical variables not related to knowledge scores, associations were evaluated using chi-square (χ²) or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. For the knowledge score (0–27 points) and its domains, quantitative analyses were performed. Comparisons of mean knowledge scores between two groups were conducted using Student’s t-test, while comparisons across more than two groups used one-way ANOVA with post-hoc Bonferroni correction when applicable. Correlations between knowledge scores and continuous variables such as age or months in current position were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethical approval was obtained from the Comité de Ética de Investigación en Seres Humanos of Universidad de Las Américas (CEISH-UDLA, Quito, Ecuador). The study was classified as exempt given its observational design and use of anonymous data (Protocol Code: 2023-EXC-008). All participants provided informed electronic consent prior survey completing.

Results

General characteristics

A total of 811 questionnaires were analysed, 455 (56.1%) from students in internship and 356 (43.9%) rural physicians. The mean age of participants was 25.8 years (± 3.4). The 497 (61.3%) were female. The 420 (51.8%) had completed their undergraduate training in public universities with 717 (88.4%) studying in institutions located in the Andes region. At the time of the survey, participants reported a mean of 5.9 months (± 3.6) of academic or professional practice, predominantly within public healthcare institutions (742, 91.5%) (Table 1).

The 27-item knowledge scale demonstrated moderate internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.67, indicating acceptable reliability for an exploratory cross-sectional study.

Dengue-related training

Overall, 71.3% (95% CI: 68.2%–74.4%) of total respondents reported receiving formal lectures on tropical medicine during their undergraduate education, with higher proportions among rural physicians (77.5%; 95% CI: 73.2%–81.8%) compared with students in internship (66.4%; 95% CI: 62.1%–70.7%). In terms of specific dengue-related training, 51.9% (95% CI: 48.5%–55.3%) had completed at least one dengue-focused course or certification in the past year. This proportion was markedly higher among rural physicians (76.7%; 95% CI: 72.3%–81.1%) than among internship students (32.5%; 95% CI: 28.2%–36.8%).

Attendance at scientific congresses or conferences on dengue was reported by 20.1% (95% CI: 17.4%–22.8%) of participants. Less than half (43.9%; 95% CI: 40.5%–47.3%) had read the Manual of Procedures of the Epidemiological Surveillance Subsystem SIVE–ALERTA-MoH, Quito, 2nd Edition [25]. Although nearly all participants (98.8%; 95% CI: 98.0%–99.6%) had previously heard of dengue, 43.6% (95% CI: 40.2%–47.0%) reported having ever diagnosed or treated patients with dengue (Table 2).

Perceptions

More than half of the total participants (56.1%; 95% CI: 52.7%–59.5%) stated that they had not received sufficient training on dengue during their undergraduate medical education. Regarding institutional response, 52.9% believed that the Ministry of Public Health (MSP) does not adequately train physicians on dengue, while only 47.1% (95% CI: 43.7%–50.5%) considered that training was adequate. Similarly, 59.3% (95% CI: 55.9%–62.7%) believed that the MSP lacks sufficient resources to manage dengue cases. In addition, 54.3% reported that control and prevention measures were inadequately implemented, with only 45.7% (95% CI: 42.3%–49.1%) perceiving these measures as adequate.

Differences between medical students in internship versus rural physicians

As expected, rural physicians were older (27.1 ± 4.2 years) compared with medical students (24.8 ± 2.0 years). Regarding training, rural physicians were more likely than internship students to have received dengue-specific certifications in the past year (76.7% vs. 32.5%; p = 0.001), to have attended scientific congresses on dengue (28.1% vs. 13.4%; p = 0.001), and to have read the SIVE–ALERTA 2014 guidelines (64.3% vs. 27.9%; p = 0.001). Moreover, rural physicians more frequently reported having diagnosed or treated dengue patients (62.1% vs. 29.2%; p = 0.001) compared with medical interns (Tables 1, 2, and 3).

Regarding training-related variables, participants who reported receiving undergraduate tropical medicine lectures had a mean knowledge score of 14.1 ± 3.3 compared with 13.7 ± 3.6 among those who did not (mean difference: 95% CI: −0.14 to 0.94; p = 0.046). Likewise, participants who had heard about dengue showed a mean score of 14.0 ± 3.4 versus 11.6 ± 5.4 among those who did not (mean difference: 2.4 points; 95% CI: −1.47 to 5.87; p = 0.009). However, the wide confidence interval—driven by the small size of the comparison group—indicates instability of this estimate. Similarly, those with experience diagnosing or treating dengue patients had a mean score of 14.2 ± 3.3 compared with 13.9 ± 3.5 among those without such experience (mean difference: 0.30 points; 95% CI: −0.17 to 0.77; p = 0.036), again reflecting a modest and imprecise association (Table 4).

Discussion

The present study, conducted in dengue-endemic Ecuador among medical interns and physicians completing rural service, demonstrates significant deficiencies in clinical recognition and case management despite an overall acceptable theoretical understanding of dengue etiology, pathophysiology, and diagnostic principles. These findings indicate that theoretical exposure alone is insufficient and that both formal curricular reinforcement and supervised clinical experience are essential to ensure adequate preparedness to manage dengue in endemic settings. Unlike previous research, which was limited to a single city (Machala) located in the Coast and focused on physicians and nurses [3] our study was conducted nationwide, including participants graduated from several universities working in both tropical and temperate regions. Importantly, the groups assessed here occupy critical transitional stages in medical training and provision of professional services. Interns, in their sixth and final year, rotate for one year across public and private hospitals located in large cities, while rural physicians, having completed their internship, graduated, and passed the national professional qualification exam (CACES) provide primary care in peri-urban and remote rural primary health centers [15, 28]. OPS issued the Epidemiological Alert due to the increase in dengue cases in the region of the Americas [4, 29], recommending effective diagnosis, treatment, prevention, and control measures. Therefore, adequate levels of KAP among health personnel should be assessed to comply with these recommendations.

Our results showed that despite nearly collective awareness of dengue, knowledge scores revealed important gaps. The overall mean score was 14 out of 27 points, slightly above the midpoint, reflecting a generally insufficient level of knowledge. The weakest of the four domains were recognition of clinical features (2.4/6 points) and knowledge of management/treatment (4.5/10 points), which are crucial for early diagnosis and case management. In contrast, knowledge of etiology and pathophysiology (4.3/6 points) and diagnostics (2.7/5 points) were relatively better, surpassing slightly the midpoint of their scales. These results suggest that theoretical aspects of the disease may be better covered in undergraduate training, whereas applied clinical competencies remain insufficiently emphasized. Comparable deficiencies have been documented in prior Ecuadorian studies among medical interns and physicians, identifying suboptimal radiology knowledge, limited disaster preparedness and perceived readiness, and insufficient performance in basic and advanced life support [30–32]. These convergent findings point to a persistent gap between theoretical instruction and executable clinical action.

Subgroup analyses revealed no statistically significant differences in overall dengue knowledge between medical interns and rural physicians, nor between participants from public and private universities. This suggests that knowledge gaps are widespread across different stages of training and institutional backgrounds. One possible explanation is the relatively short time in the activities of interns and rural physicians, averaging only 5.9 months (± 3.6). It is likely that more substantial differences would emerge when comparing these groups with physicians who have worked for several years on clinical practice. In this regard, the only previous KAP study conducted in a dengue endemic city (Machala) with Ecuadorian health professionals, reported high levels of knowledge concerning dengue symptoms and treatment; nonetheless, the authors emphasized the need for additional training in prevention strategies, diagnostic criteria, and patient admission guidelines [3]. These findings reinforce the importance of strengthening dengue education at both undergraduate and postgraduate levels. Additionally, geographic analysis where participants were laboring did not show statistically significant differences, though lower mean scores were observed in highland provinces where dengue is not endemic, compared to higher scores in Galápagos Islands, Napo, and El Oro. This suggests that contextual exposure and local epidemiology may influence knowledge acquisition, further supporting the need for standardized training across all regions.

Importantly, better knowledge was positively associated with training-related variables. Participants who reported having received lectures on tropical medicine scored higher than those who had not (14.1 ± 3.3 vs. 13.7 ± 3.6; p = 0.046). Likewise, participants with prior experience diagnosing or treating dengue patients demonstrated slightly higher knowledge scores compared with those without such experience (14.2 ± 3.3 vs. 13.9 ± 3.5; p = 0.036), underscoring again the value of both academic preparation and practical exposure in strengthening competencies. However, although these associations reached statistical significance, the magnitude of the observed differences was modest, suggesting that their practical or clinical relevance should be interpreted with caution. Hence, medical education on tropical diseases should be strengthened within undergraduate curricula. We recommend that medical students be exposed during the internship program to primary health centers where dengue and other tropical diseases are commonly managed [16]. Concerningly, nearly 30% of respondents indicated they had not received tropical medicine discipline during medical school and 56.1% reported insufficient preparation during their medical education, a significant gap in a country where more than 70% of the territory presents suitable conditions for Aedes aegypti proliferation [33] and where dengue is considered a significant public health problem with increasingly cases, morbidity and mortality [2, 21].

Perceptions regarding the responses of the MoH on dengue training, management and control, were also significative. More than half of the participants believed that the MoH does not adequately train physicians on dengue. Furthermore, the majority expressed skepticism about the adequacy of vector control and prevention measures. These perceptions align with the documented challenges of dengue control in Ecuador, including resource constraints and the expansion of transmission into previously unaffected areas and to rural communities of the Coast and Amazon [7, 8, 10, 34] and repeating outbreaks [9, 35].

Sources of information on dengue reported by participants provide further insight into these gaps. Undergraduate classes (76.7%) and health centers (55.2%) were the main sources of knowledge, while mass media and digital platforms played a minor role. This reliance on formal education and institutional practice highlights both the opportunity and the responsibility of universities and the health system to provide comprehensive training. However, students perceive such preparation as insufficient, this reflects structural weaknesses in both academic curricula and continuing medical education. Academia and MoH should encourage reading and applying of the PAHO Dengue Guidelines [24] and the official MoH guidelines [25].

Taken together, the findings of this study, with knowledge levels only slightly above the midpoint (14.0 ± 3.4/27 points), highlight the urgent need to reinforce tropical diseases education in Ecuadorian medical schools and to expand continuing professional training for young physicians. Currently, only 16 of the 30 universities in Ecuador include tropical medicine as a subject, typically taught in the sixth semester [17]. We recommend that the Consejo de Educación Superior (CES), the governing body responsible for planning, regulating, and coordinating higher education, evaluate and update medical curricula to ensure that Tropical Medicine is a compulsory discipline to all universities.

Providing all medical students with comprehensive training on dengue is critical for improving early diagnosis and clinical management, especially as transmission spreads to new geographic areas driven by climate variability and vector adaptation [7, 13, 33]. Aligning medical education with the realities of an endemic context will not only strengthen individual competencies but also enhance the health system’s overall capacity to respond effectively to recurrent outbreaks.

In addition to curricular reform, artificial intelligence (AI)–based educational tools may serve as complementary strategies to strengthen training in tropical diseases. Recent evidence shows that large language models demonstrate moderate diagnostic accuracy in infectious disease scenarios but remain limited in therapeutic decision-making, indicating that AI can support knowledge acquisition without replacing clinical judgment [36]. When ethically integrated into structured pedagogical frameworks, AI-driven platforms may enhance case-based learning and continuous knowledge reinforcement; however, their implementation must safeguard critical thinking and avoid cognitive overreliance [37, 38].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences between training experiences and knowledge levels. The reliance on a self-administered, self-reported questionnaire may have introduced recall bias and social desirability bias, as participants might have overestimated their knowledge or provided responses perceived as academically or professionally desirable rather than reflecting their actual clinical practice. Self-reported measures are also inherently limited in their ability to objectively assess real-world competencies. Additionally, the online administration of the survey may have restricted participation among certain subgroups, particularly individuals with limited internet access or lower digital literacy, potentially underrepresenting physicians working in remote or resource-constrained settings in Ecuador. This modality may therefore have contributed to sampling bias. The voluntary participation design further exposes the study to potential selection bias, as individuals with greater interest in tropical diseases or those working in dengue-endemic areas may have been more inclined to respond. Although the sample size was large and geographically diverse, it was limited to medical interns and recently graduated GPs and may therefore not be representative of all healthcare providers in Ecuador. Furthermore, the non-probabilistic recruitment strategy increases the risk of selection bias and limits generalizability. Finally, although the instrument was comprehensive, it may not fully capture practical clinical competencies, diagnostic reasoning, or real-time decision-making skills required in dengue case management. The internal consistency of the knowledge scale was moderate (Cronbach’s α = 0.67), which, although acceptable for exploratory research, suggests that further refinement of the instrument could enhance measurement precision in future studies.

Conclusions

While most participants relied on undergraduate classes and health centers as their main sources of information, nearly one-third reported not having received formal training in tropical medicine, underscoring a critical gap in medical education in a tropical and endemic country for Neglected Tropical Diseases. Higher knowledge scores were associated with prior exposure to tropical medicine lectures and clinical experience treating dengue patients, highlighting the importance of both academic preparation and practical training. These findings emphasize the urgent need to strengthen undergraduate curricula and continuing professional education in tropical medicine to ensure that young physicians are adequately prepared to confront dengue, particularly as the disease continues to expand geographically in Ecuador. Future surveys should include other health professionals (e.g., nurses in endemic areas and senior physicians), community workers, and local inhabitants.
