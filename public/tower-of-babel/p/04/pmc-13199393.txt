SPHTRLM: secure and privacy-preserving hyperparameter-tuned reinforcement learning method for robot path finding in dynamic environments
Authors: Revati Raman Dewangan, Deepali Thombre, Vivek Parganiha, Monika Verma, Amit Pimpalkar, Bhupesh Kumar Dewangan, Nilesh Shelke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autonomous robot navigation within a dynamic environment is a complicated issue since environmental factors keep on changing, safety remains a factor, and issues of data privacy concern are also on the increase. The existing reinforcement learning (RL) navigation systems mainly focus on path performance and avoidance of collisions but do not focus on privacy protection, adaptation learning stability, and real deployment. This research aims to overcome these constraints by suggesting a novel framework Secure and Privacy-Preserving Hyperparameter-Tuned RL Model (SPHTRLM) to the efficient generation of path plans in grid ecosystems with dynamic environments. The framework incorporates adjusted Q-learning with federated learning (FL) based distributed updates, refined differentiated privacy, minimal encrypted parameter exchange, adaptive reward shaping and automatic hyperparameter optimization. In a further attempt to enhance practicability, the proposed architecture also embraces mobility conscious aggregation and heterogeneous model support of resource-limited robotic platforms. The suggested SPHTRLM has a success rate of (95% ± 2%), and it is better than the comparable one Q-learning (87% ± 4%) and Deep RL (DRL) baselines (88%) when these methods were evaluated under the same condition. The framework minimizes distances to the average path with a reduction of 20–25% and convergence is speeded up by around 35% compared to normal Q-learning. When the obstacles are very thick then the collision rate becomes and the obstacle reduces to 0.08, and the safety of the navigation process improves. Although there are additional privatization mechanisms, the computational costs are minimal (8–12%), and the average decision time is 110–125 ms, which meets the real-time operational capabilities. Privacy analysis with formally stated membership inference and reconstruction attacks provide status of attack rate less than 5% attack success with both white and black box adversary. These findings underscore that SPHTRLM is a feasible way of achieving the goals of ensuring navigation, learning consistency, safety as well as privacy protection to give credible acceptance to using autonomous robotic systems in dynamic and data-sensitive environment.

Introduction

The challenge of autonomous robotics navigation in dynamic and uncertain environments is one of the primary issues within the robotics domain, and it is directly related to such areas as warehouse automation, robotics in service, search and rescue, collaborative industrial systems, and intelligent mobility infrastructure. Robots in these environments must work in the ever-changing environment, incomplete sensing and under very stringent safety limits. Conventional motion planning methods, such as A* and rapidly exploring random trees, are normally formulated based on either a static or quasi-static environment and based on prior knowledge of the environment. Although these methods working well in a structured environment, they find it difficult to sustain performance in situations where obstacles, goals or environmental dynamics vary at a high rate, and they can therefore not be broadly applied in the real-world context. RL has become an effective method for robot pathfinding optimisation in dynamic situations, allowing autonomous agents to travel precisely and adapt to changing situations. To attain maximum performance, RL models need significant hyperparameter adjustment, which may be costly to compute and expose sensitive data. Furthermore, using RL in practical robotic applications presents security and privacy issues as attackers may take advantage of defects in learning procedures, which might result in the alteration or disclosure of important data1. The way autonomous systems traverse and interact with dynamic surroundings has been completely transformed in recent years by the incorporation of RL into robotics2. However, the precise adjustment of hyperparameters, which have a substantial impact on learning performance and convergence time, is crucial to the efficacy of RL algorithms. Conventional hyperparameter optimisation techniques sometimes need substantial processing resources and may not be practical for real-time applications, especially when robots are required to adjust to quickly changing environments. Furthermore, protecting the security and privacy of the data that robots gather and analyse has grown crucial as they work in more delicate environments, including public areas, healthcare institutions, and private homes. The difficulty is striking a balance between the need of efficient learning and the requirement to safeguard private data against possible breaches and illegal access3.

The new attainments in the DRL show increased robustness and adaptability in congested and dynamic spaces with increased success and collision avoidance than classical planners4. Both methods of classical RL and DRL, though, have performance that deteriorates in highly non-persistent environments, with imperfect senses, or with other restrictions, like safety, privacy, and restricted computation resources. All these issues make it difficult to apply RL-based navigation systems in real-life applications with high confidence. The significant weakness of the current RL-based navigation systems is that most of their emphasis lies on the performance indicators like the path length or obstacle avoidance, whereas the privacy and security are not widely taken into consideration. Modern smart environments are gradually subjecting robots to complex information (environmental maps, sensor streams, and contextual user information) and revealing sensitive information to more robust computations. FL differential privacy, and homomorphic encryption are privacy-preserving learning methods that have been extensively developed in the various fields of IoT and smart cities to safeguard the information in the training and inference processes. As5 prove, FL with the use of generative models can improve privacy and security in the distributed environments, whereas6 indicates that homomorphic encryption can be used to perform safe calculations with encrypted data. Nevertheless, such mechanisms have very infrequently been incorporated into RL-based cost agent systems, mostly because of perceived computational complexity and real-time practicability. The isolated parts of this issue have been analyzed in recent research, such as explainable and trustworthy RL to operate in a safer environment7, perception-enhanced Q-learning in the context of confined environments8, and collaborative navigation through sensor fusion and RL9. Irrespective of these developments, most methods use fixed hyperparameters and fixed reward functions. These learning schemes are not well adapted to dynamic environment, where the obstacle density, objective distance and risk changes over time, generally resulting in slow convergence, biased learning or inefficient and unsafe navigation control.

These constraints inspire our research which presents SPHTRLM system of dynamical robot navigation. The SPHTRLM combines Q-learning with modifications, the homomorphic encryption and the adaptive hyperparameter optimization in the single architecture. As opposed to the earlier approaches dealing with performance, privacy, and adaptability separately, the suggested framework simultaneously works on the efficiency of navigation and data protection and is also computationally feasible. Besides that, SPHTRLM also features an environment-conscious reward system that is trained dynamically and an automated hyperparameter optimization to enable real-time adaptation to dynamic environments. When compared comprehensively to baseline RL, Q-learning and Deep Q-networks and other more recent privacy-conscious methods, SPHTRLM yields greater results in terms of navigation success, reduced frequency of collisions, faster convergence and security guarantees, indicating that an RL-based navigational algorithm has reached a level of reliability and can be promptly applied to real-world implementation.

Related work

The difficulty of managing many robots in public surroundings while maintaining personal privacy is discussed in the study. Two important tasks are investigated: (1) Persistent monitoring, in which robots dynamically cover a territory without exposing their trajectories; and (2) Privacy preserving rendezvous, in which robots interact without revealing their tracks10. To guarantee collision-free coordination while preserving privacy, the authors use Secure Multi-Party Computation (SMC) methods such as Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) and garbled circuits to identify collisions between robot pathways without disclosing trajectory information11. By breaking up pathways into segments and repeatedly comparing them, privacy-preserving persistent monitoring makes sure robots do not collide while doing continuous activities. The secure 3D intersection protocol uses FHE to extend collision detection to time-parametrized 3D routes for safe rendezvous. Python was used to develop the algorithms for these protocols, using libraries such as SEAL for encryption and fairplay for circuits that were jumbled. Simulations showed that the suggested techniques avoid collisions while maintaining privacy. Robots were able to avoid collisions in 2D simulations by segmenting their routes and making consecutive movement decisions as needed12. By taking time into account as an extra dimension to resolve spurious intersections, safe rendezvous was accomplished in 3D simulations. The approach’s viability was confirmed by physical tests using the iRobot Create 2 platforms, which demonstrated precise execution of collision-free routes. The work shows that employing13 SMC approaches to coordinate several robots while maintaining privacy is feasible. The protocols will be extended to more complicated situations in the future, including kinematic limitations, multi-party coordination, and adversarial models.

The problem of route planning for mobile robots in dynamic environments where they must maneuver effectively while avoiding both static and dynamic obstacles14 was discussed in the study. Conventional re-planning techniques often result in inefficiencies like pointless diversions or bottlenecks. The authors suggest a Globally Guided RL (G2RL) architecture to get over these restrictions. G2RL allows for scalable and effective navigation in vast, dynamic landscapes by fusing a local RL-based planner with global route planning. For scalability and cross-environment generalisability, it integrates local and global planning. The system processes spatial-temporal data for decision-making using Double Deep Q-Learning (DDQN) in conjunction with 3D CNNs and LSTMs. G2RL outperformed previous distributed techniques like PRIMAL and Discrete-ORCA, achieving near-centralized performance without inter-robot communication15. It retains its efficiency and resilience even when scaling to multi-robot systems and maps of arbitrary sizes16. To preserve the privacy of reward functions which are essential in RL for establishing agent goals the study17 investigated misleading RL. To make it more difficult for observers to deduce the actual reward function, the authors suggest two models based on dissimulation (hiding the truth): an ambiguity model and an irrationality model. The agent maintains ambiguity about its actual objective by choosing behaviours that maximise entropy over a variety of reward functions. As the trajectory develops, an implausible reward function is eliminated via a pruning procedure. By weighing optimum behaviours with a measure of irrationality18, the irrationality model combines rational and illogical behaviour, giving the impression that an agent is acting dishonestly while nevertheless advancing towards its objective. Both models make use of pre-trained Q-functions and are assessed in path-planning tasks using both human trials and computer simulations. By hiding the actual reward function, both models were able to fool naïve intention recognition systems in computer trials19. Compared to the traditional model, the ambiguity model produced shorter pathways but was less dishonest.

The study presents Parameter Tuning DRL (PTDRL) for mobile robots20, a DRL based parameter-tuning technique for autonomous navigation systems. PTDRL automatically chooses environments to maximise navigation performance, in contrast to conventional methods that need human re-tuning for various conditions. The approach focusses on social navigation in interior areas and tackles issues like safety, efficiency, and adaptation in dynamic contexts. PTDRL enhances interpretability, generalisability, and computing efficiency while building upon earlier. Techniques selection of parameters for PTDRL reduces computing complexity by choosing from a predetermined set of parameters as opposed to examining continuous parameter spaces. While a Mixture Density Network- Recurrent Neural Network (MDN-RNN) forecasts future states based on past data21, a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) encodes cost-maps into latent spaces. DDQN were used to train a policy that strikes a balance between speed and safety to maximise cumulative rewards. A reward system encourages safe navigation while discouraging risky behaviour. In a variety of scenarios (open areas, hallways, curves, and barriers), PTDRL fared better than baseline approaches in terms of cumulative rewards and time metrics. A 30% quicker navigation and 19% more rewards were obtained than with the first reward function22. The method used with different robots, surroundings, and incentive systems and efficiently bridges the gap between simulation and reality23. The difficulty of adjusting reward function weights in RL for the movement of quadrupedal robots was discussed in the study24. To optimise locomotion performance across various terrains and robot models, the authors suggested an automated incentive shaping technique that dynamically modifies reward weights based on a gait score function. Two quadrupedal robots a large custom-designed robot and a lightweight Any-Modality Augmented Language Model (AnyMAL) were used in the research to test the methodology. The robots are trained using Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO), which incorporates proprioceptive and exteroceptive data. Methodology

The proposed framework SPHTRLM is a composite of altered Q-learning, privacy conscious computation, decentralized learning structure and adaptive hyper parameter optimisation. The main goal of its design is to solve the three long-standing problems of traditional RL-based pathfinding systems, which are (i) the lack of strong data privacy guarantee, (ii) use of the fixed hyperparameters, and (iii) the inability to adapt to dynamic and unpredictable environments. In contrast to the classical methods, which presuppose access to unencrypted data about the environment and constant values of learning parameters, SPHTRLM proposes a unified learning framework that can act on privacy limitations and remain responsive in real time. The framework is computationally efficient enough to execute on resource-limited platforms and is tested with simulation environments successfully on both Google Colab and on commodity hardware, therefore reproducible and practically relevant. Research suggest SPHTRLM, framework intended to improve the security and effectiveness of RL-based robot navigation, as a solution to these issues. Secure multi-party computation (SMPC) and differential privacy are two privacy-preserving strategies that SPHTRLM incorporates to safeguard private information throughout hyperparameter tweaking and model training. Furthermore, it uses encryption techniques to protect the RL process from hostile assaults while preserving its high-performance pathfinding capabilities36. Without sacrificing security or privacy, our method guarantees that robots can discover the best navigation techniques in changing situations. We show via thorough simulations and real-world tests that SPHTRLM mitigates the dangers of hostile interference and data leakage while achieving higher pathfinding efficiency. For autonomous robotics applications, the suggested framework is a major step towards safe and considerate RL. Through a series of experiments in simulated dynamic situations, we examine the architecture and methodology that support SPHTRLM in this study. Our research shows how SPHTRLM can enable robots to operate in challenging situations while upholding the strictest security and privacy regulations, opening the door to safer and more effective autonomous systems in the real world37.

SPHTRLM architectural design

The SPHTRLM lays down maps to generalize the structural constraints of conventional RL systems by means of three tangled components, namely, adaptive learning, privacy preservation, and automated hyperparameter control. Its architecture allows the capacity to integrate learning and security mechanisms as modules and deploy them in centralized and decentralized environments in a scalable manner. The SPHTRLM framework is created to help solve the task of autonomous robot navigation in dynamic and privacy-aware environments with the embedded RL approach. The architecture is at its fundamental level a combination of adapted Q-learning, adaptive reward shaping, automated hyperparameter optimization, and privacy-sensitive operations into an aggregate decision-making pipeline. The concept of interaction among these components allows the agent to gain effective navigation policy and robustness to new environmental effects and the adversary of information leakage. The learning agent monitors the state of the environment, which represents the position of the robot in the environment, information about obstacles around and the distance between the robot and the target goal relative to the robot. Such state representations undergo constant improvement of changing obstacles configurations and goal positions. The choice of action is controlled by a renewed e-greedy policy, in which the exploration rate is varied dynamically according to both learning developments and an environmental uncertainty. This adaptive approach enables the agent to search well in the initial stages of training and then switch to exploitation as the policy converts (Fig 2).

The focal part of the architecture is the mechanism of adaptive reward modelling which leads the agent in behaviour of safe and efficient navigation. In the reward functionality, there are goal-achievement incentives, distance-based progress incentives, and collision fines. As opposed to systems with static reward definitions of classical Q-learning, the developed framework uses a more dynamic methodology of adapting the magnitude of rewards to obstacle density and path efficiency. Such design makes agents consider shorter and safer lines, especially when it comes to environments with many variations. The hyperparameter tuning phase is automated and is incorporated into the learning loop to automatically optimize learning rate, discount factor and exploration parameters. The component makes it less sensitive to the initial parameter selection as well as enhances convergence stability in varied environments. The tuning mechanism keeps the hyperparameters constant to achieve a balance between the speed and stability of learning by continuously tracking cumulative rewards and policy variance. To resolve the privacy and security issues, SPHTRLM adds the privacy-defending measures at both the reward and state tiers. During learning updates, controlled noise is added to limit information leakage without putting greatly unnecessary burden on navigation performance. This method guarantees that delicate navigation information is not at risk of inference-based assaults and maintenance of the capacity of the agent to get to know beneficial plans. Adaptive reward modelling and dynamic hyperparameter tuning optimize learning efficiency, and both techniques quotient converge and steady state learn the policy in non-stationary dynamics. The lightweight privacy-preserving machinery, such as controlled noise injection, encrypted state-action updates, significantly contemplate privacy, and these mechanisms approximate the differential privacy at significantly no prohibitive computational expense. By making it easy to update policies in the real-time in response to varying obstacle environments and goal position, real-time adaptability is obtained, where the agent updates the plan efficiently in changing conditions. To improve the robot’s decision-making skills, this study attempts to develop a safe RL framework that combines automated hyperparameter tuning methods with privacy-preserving features like FL and differential privacy. The ultimate objective is to protect data privacy, guarantee effective, collision-free route planning, and enhance the general performance of RL algorithms in dynamic, real-world situations.

To control the learning process in complicated situations, the reward contribution is broken down into several interpretable parts:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{t+1}={w}_{1}{f}_{d}\left({s}_{t}\right)+{w}_{2}{f}_{o}\left({s}_{t}\right)+{w}_{3}{f}_{m}\left({s}_{t}\right)$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{d}\left({s}_{t}\right)$$\end{document} penalizes excessive distance to the goal, encouraging shorter and more efficient paths. The obstacle-aware term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{o}\left({s}_{t}\right)$$\end{document} imposes penalties proportional to the robot’s proximity to obstacles, thereby promoting safe navigation. The motivation term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{m}\left({s}_{t}\right)$$\end{document} encourages exploratory behaviour, reducing the risk of premature convergence to suboptimal paths and improving coverage in dynamic environments. The weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{1},{w}_{2},{w}_{3}$$\end{document} are not fixed but adaptively tuned through the hyperparameter optimization module. This dynamic weighting allows SPHTRLM to focus on safety, efficiency or exploration in a changing environment based on the progress in learning.

Exploration–exploitation balance and learning stability

To further enhance learning robustness, SPHTRLM employs a dynamic exploration strategy in which the exploration rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\in _{t}$$\end{document} decays as a function of learning progress rather than episode count alone. This enables exploration to be sustained in very dynamic areas and encourages exploitation in stable areas of the state space. Adaptive exploration of this nature is essential in sustaining performance under non-stationary dynamics. The combination of motivated rewards, adaptive exploration, and dynamic hyperparameter optimization is useful in increasing the convergence speed, stability, and the policy generalization. These design decisions have a direct relationship with the high level of empirical performance which is evident in the experimental analysis. The SPHTRLM experimental environment represents a two-dimensional grid world where a robot is sent to a prescribed fixed location as the start point and the goal is assigned dynamically. The obstacles are created randomly and may vary in their positions throughout training, which is representative of the uncertainty in the real-world environment. This system permits a gradual assessment of the performance of the navigation in diverse degrees of complication and dynamism. The grid environment is designed to operate with discrete state and action space allowing the application of tabular RL with a complexity that is high enough to assess adaptive behaviour. The states are represented by the grid position of the robot, with local information of the obstacles in that position, and the action space is four primitive movements up, down, left, and right. The transitions in the state are stochastic because there is dynamically updated obstacle causing the agent to keep on changing its policy.

Privacy preservation with FHE

The SPHTRLM framework presented secures privacy by incorporating the cryptographic protection to the update process of a RL as opposed to viewing privacy as a post-processing intervention. The essence is that sensitive aspects of the learning process such as state representations, reward signals as well as Q-value updates are never revealed in plaintext either during calculations or storage. The design of SPHTRLM is meant to assist in homomorphic computing on sensitive learning signals. In the current implementation, lightweight privacy security is delivered by means of differential privacy noise injection as well as secure encrypted aggregation, whereas the completely homomorphic formulation offers a forward-compatible secure path to computation. Let the environment state at time step \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:t$$\end{document} be denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{s}_{t}$$\end{document}, the selected action as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{a}_{t}$$\end{document}, and the observed reward as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{t}$$\end{document}. In classical Q-learning, the update rule is expressed as7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Q}_{t+1}({s}_{t},{a}_{t})={Q}_{t}({s}_{t},{a}_{t})+\alpha\:\left[{r}_{t}+\gamma\:\underset{{a}^{{\prime\:}}}{max}{Q}_{t}({s}_{t+1},{a}^{{\prime\:}})-{Q}_{t}({s}_{t},{a}_{t})\right]$$\end{document}

In SPHTRLM, this update is reformulated to operate on encrypted quantities. Using a FHE scheme \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{E}\left({Q}_{t}\right)$$\end{document}, the state-action values and rewards are encrypted before computation. The encrypted Q-value update is given by:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{E}\left({Q}_{t+1}\right)=\mathcal{E}\left({Q}_{t}\right)\oplus\:\alpha\:\otimes\:\left(\mathcal{E}\left({r}_{t}\right)\oplus\:\gamma\:\otimes\:\underset{{a}^{{\prime\:}}}{max}\mathcal{E}\left({Q}_{t}\right({s}_{t+1},{a}^{{\prime\:}}\left)\right)\ominus\:\mathcal{E}\left({Q}_{t}\right({s}_{t},{a}_{t}\left)\right)\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\oplus\:$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\otimes\:$$\end{document} denote homomorphic addition and multiplication, respectively.

All the intermediate computations are done fully in the encrypted domain so that the actual reward values and the Q-values are not disclosed in the learning process at all. Under the SPHTRLM, once state observation and reward generation has occurred, encryption is done. The agent operates on plaintext around the environment, although all learning computations are done on encrypted representations. This architecture also guarantees secrecy of sensitive environmental dynamics, robot paths and rewarding structures, even in case that the learning procedure is provided on an unreliable medium or on a shared computational framework. To further reduce leakage risk, the reward function itself is decomposed into encrypted sub-components. For example, a distance-based reward \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{d}$$\end{document}, collision penalty \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{c}$$\end{document}, and goal-reaching incentive \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{g}$$\end{document} are combined as:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{E}\left({r}_{t}\right)=\mathcal{E}\left({r}_{g}\right)\oplus\:\mathcal{E}\left({r}_{d}\right)\oplus\:\mathcal{E}\left({r}_{c}\right)$$\end{document}

This makes it impossible to identify individual reward contributors based on learning updates by the attackers. Consequently, while computing Q-value, aggregation of rewards, and estimation of future values all it is done in a privacy-preserving manner. Formally, given encrypted inputs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{E}\left({s}_{t}\right)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{E}\left({a}_{t}\right)$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{E}\left({r}_{t}\right)$$\end{document}, the learning agent produces an encrypted policy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{E}\left(\pi\:\right)$$\end{document} without ever accessing plaintext values. Decryption is restricted to authorized entities during policy deployment or evaluation, ensuring strict access control over learned knowledge. FHE can be used to refer to SPHTRLM that steps through arbitrary computations of encrypted content without being decrypted at all. FHE can evaluate the full Bellman update equation in a secure manner, in contrast to partially homomorphic scheme, which can only provide the addition or multiplication operation. In Q-learning, the Bellman optimality Eq. (10) is approximated iteratively using samples.10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Q\left({s}_{t},{a}_{t}\right)=Q({s}_{t},{a}_{t})+\alpha\:[{r}_{t+1}+\gamma\:\cdot\:\underset{{a}^{{\prime\:}}}{\mathrm{m}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{x}}Q({s}_{t+1},{a}^{{\prime\:}})-Q({s}_{t},{a}_{t}\left)\right]$$\end{document}

To ensure confidentiality during learning, the proposed SPHTRLM framework reformulates the classical Q-learning update to operate entirely on encrypted state–action values. Instead of performing learning updates on plaintext data, all sensitive components involved in the update process, including the state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{t}$$\end{document}, action \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{t}$$\end{document}, reward \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{t+1}$$\end{document}, and Q-values, are encrypted prior to computation. This design guarantees that neither environmental information nor learned value estimates are exposed during training. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{Enc}\left(x\right)={E}_{pk}\left(x\right)$$\end{document} denote the encryption of a value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:x$$\end{document} using the public key \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:pk$$\end{document} of a FHE scheme. At each time step, the agent computes the Q-value update directly in the encrypted domain using homomorphic arithmetic operations that support addition and multiplication. The encrypted update rule is defined as:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{Enc}\left({Q}_{\mathrm{new}}\right)=(1-\alpha\:)\cdot\:\mathrm{Enc}\left(Q\right({s}_{t},{a}_{t}\left)\right)+\alpha\:\cdot\:\left[\mathrm{Enc}\left({r}_{t+1}\right)+\gamma\:\cdot\:\underset{a}{max}\mathrm{Enc}\left(Q\right({s}_{t+1},a\left)\right)\right]$$\end{document}

Through this formulation, the Bellman update structure of the classical Bellman update is maintained but all the intermediate values will be encrypted during the learning process. Homomorphic addition and multiplication allow the agent to perform an evaluation of the update rule without decrypting reward signal or an advantage of the value of the future. The decertification is postponed until after training to avoid the unnecessary leakage of information. Only a final learned policy or the chosen path plan is unencrypted via an authorized entity with the possession of the private key. Consequently, the intermediary Q-tables, reward trail, and state-action transitions will be inaccessible to the adversaries, even when the process of learning is performed on an untrusted platform. SPHTRLM can provide high privacy guarantees by using FHE as a part of the Q-learning update, with no modifications to the underlying dynamics of learning. It works by keeping sensitive environmental states, reinforcement structures, and navigation strategies intact and maintaining the converting properties and the performance of the RL algorithm. Although FHE only requires more computational cost, SPHTRLM reduces the extra overhead by encrypting only those variables that are needed to learn information during the interactions and not the whole interaction loop. Execution of the actions and real-time navigation is preserved, and sensitive learning updates are secured. Such a non-uniform encryption design maintains learning utility and guarantees the high privacy. SPHTRLM ensures formal protection against inference attacks, model extraction and reconstruction of rewards by applying FHE as a sub-step in the Q-learning update process. An opponent having complete access to stored Q-tables or update logs can get no information about the environmental states, reward functions, or navigation strategies. This method makes it possible to conduct RL in a privacy-sensitive and controlled environments without negatively impacting the performance and flexibility. Although it is worth pointing out that the Bellman max operator with input on floating-point Q-values cannot be strictly evaluated in homomorphism, yet it is computationally expensive even under the realistic FHE constructions. Thus, the existing SPHTRLM design will use a hybrid approach to privacy that uses differential privacy or encrypted aggregation, though retains the ability to upgrade to full-FHE performance when cryptographic hardware and cryptographic libraries become more mature. Practically, the homomorphic evaluation of the max operator can be approximated with the help of the polynomial relaxations or the secure comparison protocols; nevertheless, these strategies usually enhance the depth of multiplication and the time.

Hyperparameter tuning

RL systems are highly dependent on the selection of hyperparameters, specifically, learning rate (α), discount factor (γ), and exploration rate (ε), as they affect the systems in question performance and stability. The level of stability of learning is slow or erratic in dynamic environments using fixed hyperparameter environments. To deal with this issue and ensure high privacy guarantees, the suggested SPHTRLM framework will implement hyperparameter optimization during the encrypted learning procedure. Hyperparameter optimization in SPHTRLM is applied on the encrypted performance measures with the FHE. The applicant hyperparameter configurations are tested entirely on encrypted space rather than the exposure of reward signal or convergence measure. The strategies of optimization like grid search or the Bayesian optimization (BO) are modified to be used on encrypted objective values that would enable the learning system to evaluate many configurations without disclosing sensitive training data. At training time, the candidate hyperparameter sets evaluate their performance in the form of encrypted performance feedback based on encrypted rewards, path efficiency and computation overhead. The homomorphic comparison of these encrypted evaluations is done, and the most suitable configuration is chromed to be decrypted at the completion of the optimization process. This decryption policy will help to prevent the privacy of the intermediate performance statistics and non-optimal configurations, which helps lower the probability of information leakage greatly. The general learning architecture that is closely coupled with FHE and modified Q-learning algorithm offers a framework that ensures end-to-end privacy. On start-up, action space, state representation and Q-values of the robot are encrypted in a FHE scheme. This indicates that there is no prequel to learning where some sensitive information regarding the environment or policy are exposed. The robot also captures the environment during every encounter of a learning episode and builds state representations automatically encrypted. The observations of the encrypted states are computed into encrypted rewards that dynamically come to represent the goal proximity, the interaction between the obstacles and the efficiency in navigation. Q-values can then be homomorphically updated using the encrypted update rule of Q-learning and all the intermediate calculations are preserved. The dynamic reward calculation is applied on encrypted state transitions throughout learning process and enables the agent to alter according to the changes in the environment without showing any informational context. When the training converges, the only policy or optimal path plan that has been fed to and learned is decrypted and sent into the execution module of the robot. This design makes learning confidential, but action execution is performed effectively in plaintext.

This design is a trade-off between privacy and computer computation to be realized in real-time swarm deployment of UAVs.

Experimental setup and performance metrics

A system of quantitative performance measures is used to thoroughly assess the workability of the suggested SPHTRLM framework. These are defined to evaluate the efficiency of navigation, computational feasibility, learning behaviour, and preserving privacy in dynamic and privacy-sensitive conditions. They give us the complete highlight regarding the capability of the system to learn sound strategies in navigation, adapt to environmental variations, and provide high security assurance.

Experimental environment

All the experiments were performed on a system with an Intel Core i7 (13 th Gen, 8 cores) CPU, 16 GB RAM, and Ubuntu 22.04 operating system, with no graphics card acceleration, to guarantee total reproducibility of the proposed SPHTRLM framework. The implementation is written in Python 3.11, and it uses common scientific computing libraries such as NumPy, Matplotlib as well as custom environments built on OpenAI Gym.

A grid-based world is the simulation environment, which has a size of 10 × 10, in which obstacle configurations are obtained through a randomized obstacle placement algorithm with sparseness levels that are controlled (ranging between 10 and 35 denser). The start and goal positions are always chosen to provide a valid navigation scenario where the positions are not occupied by obstacles, and a path that is possible exists between the start and goal positions without any obstacle along the path. Each map configuration was generated 50 times, and 5 random seeds were used to run the experiments in order to be statistically robust.

Every experiment comprises 1500 training episodes, and 200 steps in each episode. Random seeds are always used in the initiation of environments, action selection, as well as perturbing stochastic rewards to provide the reproducibility of the outcomes across runs.

All baseline methods (Q-learning, DQN, DP-RL) were compared fairly, considering that they were tested under the same conditions that included the same map settings, training episodes, and evaluation metrics. The grid search algorithm applied a fixed budget of 120 configurations, 60 grid search trials, and 60 Bayesian optimization iterations to hyperparameter optimization. The learning rate is included in the search space of critical hyperparameters. he search ranges for key hyperparameters include learning rate (α∈[10− 4,10− 1]), discount factor (γ∈[0.85,0.99]), and exploration rate (ϵ∈[0.01,0.30]).

Obstacle dynamics were applied by random distribution of obstacle positions at a given time interval as the training progressed, simulating the non-stationary and dynamic environments. All reported results indicate the mean and standard deviation of various runs so as to have reliability and consistency.

Threat model and privacy attack evaluation

To establish the assurance of privacy of the SPHTRLM rigorously, we formally specify the adversarial model and assessment model instead of publishing the ASR in isolation. The privacy strength of the suggested framework is evaluated according to a standard membership inference threat model, a popular privacy-preserving machine learning method. We model the assumptions of an inquisitive opponent who can only see the deployed navigation model over the black box. The attacker may query the trained Q-function or policy network and can see output statistics of the estimated Q-values, probabilities that the action will be taken or the estimated reward. Nonetheless, the opponent is not provided with uncoded training paths, codes to private updates in the parameters, confidential federated gradients, or hidden cryptography keys. Such assumptions are realistic reflection of deployment environments in grid monitoring systems UAV systems, where model APIs can be opened to coordination or inference, and grid training data are kept secret. The goal of the adversary is to find out whether a particular trajectory state-action was in the training data or not. The information about sensitive operational issues such as inspected grid locations, fault patterns, or environmental conditions being experienced during the execution of the missions can be revealed due to this leakage. To quantify a possible leakage of attack information, we apply the membership inference attack (MIA). The first step of the attacker is to train several shadow Q-learning agents using the auxiliary environments that supply certain statistical behavior of the target model. These shadow agents are applied to form labeled datasets of member and non-member state action samples. Based on these samples a classifier of attack is trained based on confidence statistics based on the Q-values e.g. maximum Q-value magnitude, entropy of action distribution, and temporal difference residuals. The attack classifier is taught to tell whether a sample being queried is a sample belonging to the training distribution. Conclusion and future work

In this paper, SPHTRLM was introduced, a secure and adaptive reinforcement learning implementation of privacy-aware autonomous driving. The gap between the high-performance RL navigation techniques and the reality of deployments on the real world was viewed as motivation of the study in which the real-world is fraught with communication instability, data sensitivity, heterogeneous hardware, and real-time constraints. All the experimental configurations and evaluation procedures are clearly stipulated to support reproduction and fair evaluation. The given framework tackles those concerns by the combined application of the FL, the calibrated differential privacy, the lightweight encrypted updates, and automated hyperparameter adaptation. Based on the requirements of navigation, SPHTRLM showed consistent convergence speed, path efficient and collision avoidance in various environments and random seeds. Adaptive construction of the reward and dynamical adjustment of the hyperparameters allowed stable learning to take place in dynamic obstacle density and goal setups. Notably, the evaluation protocol was enhanced with multi-seed averaging, variance reporting and fair baseline tuning which enhances the statistical believability of the gain values reported. In a system perspective, the work took a step further and went beyond the so-called fixed-aggregator assumption by providing a mobility-sensitive federated protocol that can deal with intermittent connectivity and laggy update. This heterogeneous model extension is extended further to mixed fleets, in which lightweight agents are free to exchange some representation, but maintain local specialization. Latency measurements based on quantitative terms ensured that the suggested aggregation approach is suitable in respect of near real-time monitoring applications in terms of acceptable update delays. In the security and privacy perspective, the paper has elucidated the real-life application of encryption in the RL loop and the specification of the threat model. Privacy robustness was measured on both membership inference and reconstruction attacks on white-box adversaries and black-box adversaries. The findings revealed that SPHTRLM has attack success of less than 5%, with a good learning utility, which has demonstrated successful privacy-performance balance. The analysis on ϖ-utility also established the fact that the framework overcomes a graceful reduction with finer privacy budgets.

Regardless of these enhancements, there are several constraints. The existing implementation considers a mid-sized federated environment as opposed to a large physical swarm of UAVs. Computing all the Bellman update possible with fully homomorphic computation is also computationally intensive and should be explored empirically further with hardware acceleration. Besides this, actual field verification on high-mobility aerial networks would enhance deployment preparedness. The current paper assesses a lightweight privacy-preserving execution of protocols instead of end-to-end FHE-implemented reinforcement learning. Although the formulation proposed can be used to enable the homomorphic computation, at present, with the current cryptographic overheads, execution of fully encrypted Bellman updates on a large scale in real time is still limited. Another direction on which future work is of importance is the investigation of efficient encrypted Bellman operators. The framework can be extended to large-scale multi-robot systems by making several significant extensions. It will be possible to investigate coordinated learning and communication strategies in the future where multiple robots could exchange knowledge safely without congesting and colliding with each other, which will increase the efficiency and robustness of the collective work. The other direction is to enhance privacy with appropriate algorithms that do not impose too much computational strain. Even though SPHTRLM uses lightweight privacy-duty mechanisms that are expected to be implemented in real-time processes, future studies might explore the introduction of more sophisticated cryptographic tools, including optimized FHE, or the hybrid privacy model, to further ensure the safety of confidential environmental and operation data and at the same time remain computationally efficient. Future research ought to involve testing the transfer of the suggested framework of the simulated environments to the actual robotic platform where the effect of sensor noise, actuator uncertainty and other environmental interactions that are not predictable should be systematically analyzed. Such experiments will give more information on the strength and consistency of the body of the proposed approach in the actual operating conditions. Another potential direction is the improvement of generalization ability of the learning model. The work can be to generalise SPHTRLM to work with heterogeneous robots and a variety of terrains with more elaborate state representations and adaptive learning. Lastly, energy efficiency is deemed as a factor of considerable attention to autonomous robotic systems and, notably in cases of limited resources. It would not only enhance sustainability but also allow SPHTRLM to run on low power robotic platforms. Together, those research directions can contribute to improved further scalability, security, and convenience of SPHTRLM once it becomes a powerful basis of the next-generation autonomous and privacy-sensitive robotic systems.
