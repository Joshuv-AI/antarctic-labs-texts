Moderating effects of social capital on the relationship between fear of falling and depressive symptoms among community-dwelling older adults
Authors: Yeong-Mi Seo, Eun Sook Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFear of falling (FoF) and depression substantially affect the health outcomes of older adults. Previous studies have established an association between FoF and depression; however, the moderating role of social capital in this relationship remains underexplored. We aimed to investigate the moderating effect of social capital on the relationship between FoF and depressive symptoms in older adults.MethodsData from 773,861 adults aged ≥ 65 years who participated in the 2019 Korean Community Health Survey (KCHS) were used for this cross-sectional study. The assessment tools included measures of FoF, the Patient Health Questionnaire-9, and indicators of social capital (neighborhood trust, neighborly reciprocity, neighborhood satisfaction, social networks, and social participation). Data were analyzed using a linear regression model with the complex survey module, which accounts for stratification, clustering, and sampling weights inherent in the KCHS design.ResultsHigher levels of FoF were significantly associated with increased depressive symptoms (B = 0.94, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.88, 1.00). Social capital significantly moderated this FoF–depression relationship, with the strongest effects observed for social participation (B = -0.21, 95% CI = -0.26, -0.15) and social networks (B = -0.16, 95% CI = -0.23, -0.10), followed by neighborhood satisfaction (B = -0.12, 95% CI = -0.17, -0.06). These interactions indicate that higher social capital attenuates the positive association between FoF and depressive symptoms.ConclusionsThe critical role of social capital in moderating the relationship between FoF and depressive symptoms in community-dwelling older adults was empirically validated. Using a nationally representative dataset and complex sampling design, the generalizability of the findings was enhanced. The findings underscore the need for social capital enhancement strategies, such as strengthening social networks, expanding social participation opportunities, and developing age-friendly environments, to prevent and manage FoF and depression among community-dwelling older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01714-0.Keywords: Aged, Depression, Fear of falling, Social capital, Cross-sectional studies

Background

Falls are common among adults aged ≥ 65 years and are a major cause of both fatal and non-fatal injuries [1]. The estimated global prevalence of falls among older adults is 26.5% [2], with 15.9‒25.1% of community-dwelling older adults in Korea experiencing falls annually [3]. Falls are the primary cause of injury-related mortality among older adults [4] and can result in severe physical and psychological consequences, making fall prevention a critical public health concern [2].

In older adults, fear of falling (FoF) and depression have been identified as psychological factors closely correlated with fall risk [5–7]. A systematic review and meta-analysis where the relationships between FoF, depression, and fall risk in later life were examined suggested that interventions aimed at reducing fall incidence should simultaneously target both FoF and depression [8]. FoF, defined as a psychological concern related to falling, is highly prevalent among community-dwelling older adults [9]. In another systematic review and meta-analysis, 153 studies conducted across 38 countries were evaluated, and the global prevalence of FoF among older adults and community-dwelling older adults was estimated to be approximately 50% [10]. Depression is another considerable public health issue, affecting over one-third of older adults globally [11]. Depression in later life is intricately linked to physical health deterioration [12], functional limitations, and socioeconomic changes [13]. Given its association with elevated fall risk in older adults [6] and negative outcomes such as suicide and reduced quality of life, proactive intervention is strongly required [14, 15].

Cross-sectional studies involving community-dwelling older adults have consistently reported a positive association between FoF and depression [7, 16, 17]. Moreover, longitudinal studies on older adults in Korea have found that FoF and the resulting daily activity restrictions increase the risk of developing depressive symptoms [18], with baseline FoF serving as a predictor of future depressive symptoms [19]. Some inconsistencies exist among systematic reviews on the factors related to FoF in older adults [20]; however, a recent scoping review indicated that a positive link between FoF and anxiety/depression was found in 19 out of 20 included studies [21]. Literature generally supports a positive correlation between FoF and depression; nevertheless, the strength of the interconnection between these two psychological conditions varies across studies. One meta-analysis concluded that FoF is significantly associated with both depression (odds ratio [OR] = 1.516, confidence interval [CI] = 1.35‒1.70) and depressive symptoms (OR = 1.93, CI = 1.39‒2.68) [10]. Another meta-analysis revealed a markedly stronger association between FoF and depression (OR = 2.85, CI = 1.66–4.90) [8], suggesting the potential influence of moderating variables and other factors [22]. However, to date, studies have primarily focused on identifying the direct relationship between FoF and depression, with limited studies examining the mediating or moderating factors in this association. Previous studies where such factors were investigated were predominantly focused on physical activity [14], fall efficacy, activity level [23], and activities of daily living (ADL) disability [24]. Therefore, identifying the factors that influence the relationship between FoF and depression is essential for gaining a more comprehensive understanding of this relationship and developing effective intervention strategies.

Social capital has been identified as a social determinant of depression [25, 26]. It encompasses both formal and informal social connections, as well as the quantity and quality of social relationships, including norms of reciprocity and trust within a community [27]. Social capital is a multidimensional concept classified according to its forms and functions. It is generally divided into two dimensions: cognitive social capital, which encompasses subjective attitudes such as interpersonal trust and norms of reciprocity, and structural social capital, which represents behavioral aspects such as social networks and civic engagement [28, 29]. Additionally, social capital operates at multiple analytical levels, with a distinction between individual and ecological social capital [29, 30]. Individual social capital reflects personal perceptions of access to and engagement in social relationships, while ecological social capital captures collective community characteristics through aggregated individual responses [29]. Social capital is associated with public health outcomes not only at individual levels but also at collective levels (including neighborhoods and workplaces) [27, 28]. Both individual- and community/workplace-level social capital positively affect indicators such as mortality, self-rated health, and depression [28]. Specifically, cognitive social capital acts as a protective factor against mental disorders, including depression [29]. Longitudinal studies have revealed that older adults with more social capital are more likely to experience a decrease in depression over time [31]. Furthermore, in a study where the impact of neighborhood environments on depression in older adults was analyzed, settlement inclusivity, which comprises interdependence, participation, safety, and accessibility, was found to be associated with lower levels of depression [32]. These findings highlight the significance of social capital in mitigating depression and promoting mental health.

Previous studies have confirmed the independent associations between FoF and depression, as well as social capital and depression; however, studies where the interactions between these variables have been investigated remain limited. Elucidating the potential role of social capital as a mediator in the relationship between FoF and depression could offer critical insights into designing targeted interventions for older adults to prevent falls and enhance their mental health. FoF leads to restrictions in daily activities [18, 24, 33], and reduced activity levels influence depression [18, 24]. Conversely, social capital plays a crucial role in improving ADL performance, with higher levels of social capital reducing ADL disability and enhancing independence and health-related quality of life in older adults [34]. Based on this evidence, we hypothesized that social capital functions as a moderating factor that attenuates the association between FoF and depression by supporting continued engagement in daily activities.

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to examine the moderating effect of social capital on the relationship between FoF and depressive symptoms among community-dwelling older adults in Korea. Given the established associations between social capital and health outcomes at both individual and ecological levels, we focused on individual-level social capital, specifically neighborhood trust and reciprocity, neighborhood satisfaction, social networks, and social participation, as measured in the Korea Community Health Survey (KCHS). The nationally representative nature of the KCHS dataset facilitates population-level analysis of individual social capital perceptions and behaviors. The findings will provide evidence for developing integrated interventions targeting FoF, depression, and fall prevention in older adults, while highlighting the critical role of social capital in mental health promotion.

Methods

Study aim

We aimed to examine the moderating effect of social capital on the relationship between FoF and depressive symptoms among community-dwelling older adults in Korea using nationally representative survey data. The study hypotheses were: (1) FoF is positively associated with depressive symptoms among community-dwelling older adults. (2) Social capital, comprising neighborhood trust, neighborly reciprocity, neighborhood satisfaction, social networks, and social participation, moderates the FoF–depressive symptoms relationship, such that higher levels of social capital will attenuate the positive association.

Study design

A cross-sectional descriptive methodology was employed to analyze the moderating effect of social capital on the FoF–depressive symptoms relationship in community-dwelling older adults.

Study setting

Data were obtained from the 2019 KCHS, which includes information on FoF and social capital.

Study population

The KCHS targets adults aged ≥ 19 years, using stratified sampling based on administrative divisions (districts/towns) and housing types, followed by a two-stage probability sampling method with systematic sampling within selected households [35]. Among the 229,099 participants in the 2019 KCHS, we focused on adults aged ≥ 65 years, resulting in 74,547 potentially eligible individuals. After excluding 686 individuals with incomplete FoF or depressive symptom data, the final analytic sample comprised 73,861 participants (Fig. 2).

Flow diagram of study participant selection from the 2019 Korea community health survey

Measurement instruments

Assessment of depressive symptoms

The Korean version of the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) was used to assess psychological distress. The Korean PHQ-9, translated and standardized by Choi et al. [36], consists of nine criteria, each scored on a four-point Likert scale, representing the frequency of symptomatic experiences. This comprehensive assessment allows for the quantification of depressive manifestations, with aggregate scores ranging from 0 to 27. Higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms.

Measuring fear of falling

FoF was evaluated using a single-item question: “Do you usually feel afraid of falling?” with three response categories: ‘not at all afraid,’ ‘a little afraid,’ and ‘very afraid.’ This single-item measure has been widely utilized in previous research to assess FoF [21] and has shown high test-retest reliability [37]. To examine the linear correlation between FoF and depressive symptoms, the variable was transformed into a continuous metric ranging from 0 to 2, with higher scores corresponding to a greater FoF.

Social capital

Trust, reciprocity, social networks, and social participation have been identified as key components of social capital [38]. Based on prior research, social capital in this study was classified into cognitive and structural components [27–29]. Cognitive social capital encompasses subjective perceptions and attitudes, including neighborhood trust, neighborly reciprocity, and neighborhood satisfaction, while structural social capital represents behavioral aspects of social relationships, comprising social networks and participation.

Social capital was measured using data from the KCHS [15, 39, 40]. Neighborhood trust was assessed using the question, “Do neighbors trust each other?” with responses categorized as either ‘yes’ or ‘no’ (dummy-coded with ‘no’ as the reference). Neighborly reciprocity was measured using the question, “Do neighbors help each other during major life events?” with responses similarly categorized and dummy-coded. Neighborhood satisfaction was measured using five items assessing satisfaction with safety, the natural environment, living conditions, public transportation, and medical services. These items were dichotomized, and a total score ranging from 0 to 5 was generated via summation, with higher scores representing greater satisfaction with the neighborhood environment. Social networks were assessed using three questions regarding the frequency of contact with close relatives, neighbors, and friends. Responses were categorized as ‘at least once a month’ or ‘less frequently’ and summed (range: 0‒3), with higher scores reflecting larger social networks. Social participation was evaluated using four items measuring participation in religious activities, social gatherings, leisure/recreational activities, and charitable work. Responses were categorized based on regular participation (at least once a month) and summed (range: 0‒4), with higher scores indicating more active social participation.

Covariates

Covariates included demographic factors such as sex, age, educational attainment (elementary school or lower, middle school, high school, college or higher), monthly income (< 1 million South Korean Won [KRW], 1–3 million KRW, 3–5 million KRW, ≥ 5 million KRW), and health-related factors including chronic disease status (based on physician-diagnosed hypertension and diabetes, which are the only chronic conditions available in the KCHS dataset, scored from 0 to 2 according to the number of diagnoses), fall experience (none, one fall, and two or more falls, based on self-reported falls in the past year), and physical activity level. Physical activity level was assessed using the Korean version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [41], and calculated in metabolic equivalent task (MET)-minutes per week. Following established IPAQ guidelines, participants were classified into three categories: inactive (< 600 MET-min/week), minimally active (600–2,999 MET-min/week), and health-enhancing physical activity (≥ 3,000 MET-min/week).

Data collection and ethical considerations

This study was granted an exemption by the Institutional Review Board of the affiliated institution (IRB No. GIRB-D25-NY-0019). The original data used in this study were obtained in de-identified form from the KCHS website following the submission and approval of the data usage agreement and research plan.

Data analysis

The KCHS employs a complex sampling design, necessitating the application of sample weights to ensure the representativeness of the findings [42]. Accordingly, in this study, we incorporated the sampling design and weights into all analyses to enhance generalizability to the Korean community-dwelling older adult population. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA 19 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) with the complex survey module, which incorporates stratification, clustering, and sampling weights to account for the multistage stratified cluster sampling design of the KCHS. This design-based approach applies the Taylor series linearization method to estimate standard errors, correcting for intra-cluster correlation and design effects, thereby ensuring valid statistical inference [43].

Descriptive statistics and frequency analyses were performed to examine the distribution of participant characteristics and study variables. Multicollinearity was assessed using correlation analysis and the variance inflation factor (VIF) values; all correlation coefficients were below 0.5, and VIF values ranged from 1.01 to 1.05, confirming the absence of multicollinearity concerns.

To test the study hypotheses, multiple linear regression models were used, and moderation regression analysis, as suggested by Baron & Kenny [44], was performed within the complex survey module. For Hypothesis 1, a direct association model was fitted to examine the relationship between FoF and depressive symptoms, controlling for all covariates. For Hypothesis 2, a moderation analysis was conducted using a two-step hierarchical approach. In Step 1, the main effects model included control variables, the independent variable (FoF), and moderating variables (social capital components). In Step 2, interaction terms between FoF and each social capital component were added to test for moderation effects. Given the examination of five interaction terms (FoF × each social capital component), the Holm–Bonferroni sequential correction method was applied to control for multiple comparisons while maintaining adequate statistical power [45]. Covariates, including age, sex, education level, monthly income, number of chronic diseases, fall experience, and physical activity level, were controlled for in all models. Statistical significance was set at α < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Demographics and characteristics

The average age of the individuals included in the analysis was 73.85 years; 44.6% were male and 55.4% female. Elementary school graduates represented the largest educational group at 47.8%, and 44.6% of the individuals fell into the highest-income category. Regarding chronic disease diagnoses (hypertension and diabetes), 46.0% had one condition, whereas 15.4% had both. In addition, 11.3% of individuals had experienced one fall in the previous year, while 6.0% had experienced two or more falls. In terms of physical activity levels, 42.4% of the participants were classified as inactive, 42.8% as minimally active, and 14.8% as engaging in health-enhancing physical activity.

Analysis of the primary variables yielded the following mean scores: FoF, measured on a scale of 0–2, had an average score of 0.78, whereas depressive symptoms, measured on a scale of 0–27, had a mean score of 2.64. Among social capital factors, 74.4% reported neighborhood trust, and 54.0% reported neighborly reciprocity. The mean scores for neighborhood satisfaction (0–5), social networks (0–3), and social participation (0–4) were 4.26, 1.68, and 1.17, respectively (see Table 1).

Step 2: Moderation Effects.

The adjusted model in step 2 confirmed the moderating effect of social capital factors. Specifically, the interaction terms FoF × neighborly reciprocity (B = -0.15, 95% CI = -0.29, − 0.02), FoF × neighborhood satisfaction (B = -0.12, 95% CI = -0.17, -0.06), FoF × social networks (B = -0.16, 95% CI = -0.23, -0.10), and FoF × social participation (B = -0.21, 95% CI = -0.26, -0.15) were statistically significant, while FoF × neighborhood trust was not statistically significant (B = -0.04, 95% CI = -0.20, 0.12). After applying Holm–Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons among the five interaction terms, three interaction terms remained statistically significant: FoF × neighborhood satisfaction, FoF × social networks, and FoF × social participation (all adjusted p < 0.001). The interactions involving neighborhood trust and neighborly reciprocity did not retain significance after correction. These findings indicate that the positive association between FoF and depressive symptoms was attenuated by higher levels of neighborhood satisfaction, social networks, and social participation, partially supporting Hypothesis 2. Among these, social participation (B = -0.21) and networks (B = -0.16) exhibited the strongest moderating effects (see Table 3).

The interaction plots showing the moderating effects of neighborhood satisfaction, social networks, and social participation on the FoF–depressive symptoms relationship are illustrated in Fig. 3. In all three cases, the positive association between FoF and depressive symptoms was stronger among individuals with lower levels of these social capital components, indicating that higher levels of neighborhood satisfaction, social networks, and social participation consistently attenuated the FoF–depressive symptoms relationship.

Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to establish foundational data to support the enhancement of mental health and the development of fall prevention programs for community-dwelling older adults. Using nationwide survey data, we examined the moderating effect of social capital on the relationship between FoF and depressive symptoms. The main findings are summarized below.

Relationship between fear of falling and depressive symptoms

Higher levels of FoF in older adults were associated with increased depressive symptoms. This result aligns with those of previous studies that have consistently reported a positive association between FoF and depressive symptoms [7, 8, 10, 16, 17, 21]. A longitudinal study where the relationship between FoF, activity restriction, and depressive symptoms in older adults was analyzed reported that those with FoF had a 2.08-fold greater risk for depressive symptoms than those without. This risk increased to 2.51-fold when FoF was accompanied by activity restriction, confirming the negative impact of FoF and consequent activity restriction on depression [18]. In addition, another longitudinal study in which national panel data was used reported that FoF directly affects the prevalence of depression 2 years later and indirectly affects it through mediating factors such as ADL disability [24]. FoF is a prevalent health issue among community-dwelling older adults, regardless of their history of falls, indicating the necessity for early screening and appropriate interventions to prevent and manage depression.

Relationship between social capital and depressive symptoms

Social capital factors, including trust and reciprocity among neighbors, satisfaction with the neighborhood environment, social networks, and social participation, were negatively associated with depressive symptoms. These findings corroborate those of previous studies that demonstrated an inverse correlation between depression and cognitive social capital (such as trust, reciprocity, sense of belonging, and social cohesion) [26, 40, 46].

Cognitive social capital (neighborhood trust, reciprocity, and satisfaction) showed significant inverse associations with depressive symptoms, consistent with longitudinal evidence indicating that higher levels of reciprocity and satisfaction with social relationships are associated with more rapid decreases in depressive symptoms over time [31]. Research on neighborhood environments and mental health consistently show that neighborhood satisfaction, safety, and accessibility are protective factors against depression among older adults [32, 40, 47]. Because the neighborhood environment serves as the primary social context for older adults [32], cohesive neighborhoods can contribute to mental health protection by reducing stress and promoting healthy behaviors [46]. Therefore, strategies to enhance cognitive social capital should be considered to prevent depression in older adults, such as strengthening social cohesion with neighbors and creating age-friendly living environments.

Structural social capital (social networks and participation) also showed significant protective effects against depressive symptoms, consistent with findings from previous studies reporting that diverse social networks and civic participation are important social resources for preventing depression in older adults [48, 49]. Globally, social isolation and loneliness are major mental health risk factors affecting approximately 25% of community-dwelling older adults [50]. Therefore, maintaining active social connections and participation may be particularly important for preventing depression by enhancing social support and sense of belonging.

Moderating effect of social capital on the relationship between fear of falling and depressive symptoms

The central finding of this study was that three social capital components—neighborhood satisfaction, social networks, and social participation—significantly moderated the FoF–depressive symptoms relationship, with the association being weaker among individuals with higher levels of these social resources. This finding supports those from previous research suggesting that social capital can act as a protective factor against depression among older adults [29]. A study in which the moderating effect of social capital was analyzed reported that among older adults with chronic arthritis, those with more frequent contact with neighbors, friends, and children, as well as higher participation in social activities, had lower levels of depression despite poor subjective health status [51]. Another study confirmed that community satisfaction, the frequency of social contact, and participation in social activities among community-dwelling older adults interacted with depressive symptoms and functioned as moderators of suicide [15]. In our study, social participation and networks exhibited the strongest moderating effects, suggesting that structural dimensions of social capital may be particularly important in the Korean context. This finding aligns with previous research suggesting that structural dimensions of social capital tend to be more influenced by specific cultural contexts and behavioral norms, whereas cognitive dimensions may have more universal applicability in mental health promotion owing to their foundation in fundamental human experiences such as trust, reciprocity, and belonging [29]. These findings have important implications for intervention design, indicating that programs for older adults experiencing FoF should prioritize creating opportunities for active social engagement and network building.

In contrast, the moderating effect of trust and reciprocity among neighbors was not statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons. Several factors may explain this finding. First, our single-item measures of neighborhood trust and reciprocity may have lacked the sensitivity to capture the multidimensional characteristics and intensity of trust and reciprocity. Second, the relationship between cognitive social capital and FoF may be more complex than initially hypothesized, potentially involving mediating pathways rather than direct moderation. The sociocultural characteristics of South Korea, including its collectivist culture and family-centered support structures [52, 53], may have influenced the levels and effects of social capital observed in this study. Therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to populations in different sociocultural contexts, and future studies should examine whether similar moderating effects of social capital are observed in countries with different cultural and social structures.

Our findings complement existing research on FoF-related social and environmental factors. Both social resources (close relationships, family support) and environmental barriers (poor neighborhood conditions, limited access to facilities) have been identified in previous studies as protective and risk factors for FoF, respectively [17, 54–56]. Our results extend this literature by showing that these factors can moderate the mental health consequences of FoF, suggesting that social and environmental interventions may have dual benefits—reducing FoF itself and mitigating its psychological impact.

Significance and limitations of the study

This study makes several contributions to the literature on aging, mental health, and social determinants of health. First, it provides the first empirical evidence of social capital’s moderating role in the FoF–depressive symptoms relationship among Korean community-dwelling older adults, extending theoretical understanding beyond direct associations to include protective mechanisms. Second, by differentiating between cognitive and structural dimensions of social capital, this study offers nuanced insights into which specific social resources are most effective in buffering psychological distress related to physical health concerns. Third, the use of nationally representative data with a complex sampling design enhances the external validity and generalizability of the findings to the broader Korean older adult population. Fourth, the application of rigorous statistical methods, including design-based estimation and multiple comparison corrections, strengthens the reliability of the observed moderating effects. These findings have important implications for public health practice and policy. Strategies to enhance social capital, such as strengthening social networks, providing opportunities for social participation, and creating age-friendly environments, are necessary for preventing and mitigating FoF and depression among community-dwelling older adults. The results suggest that interventions targeting structural social capital—particularly social networks and participation opportunities—may be more effective than those focusing solely on cognitive aspects.

Despite these contributions, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, methodological limitations arise from the study’s cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inferences about the directionality of observed associations among FoF, social capital, and depressive symptoms. While the theoretical framework suggests that social capital moderates the FoF–depression relationship, alternative causal pathways (such as depression affecting both FoF and social capital) cannot be ruled out. Second, measurement limitations present additional constraints. The single-item assessment of FoF, while pragmatic for large-scale surveys, may not capture the multidimensional nature of this construct, including activity-specific fears, fear intensity, and associated behavioral restrictions that validated instruments such as the Falls Efficacy Scale measure. Similarly, our measurement of social capital was constrained by available KCHS indicators, potentially limiting construct validity. The single-item measures of neighborhood trust and reciprocity may have been particularly inadequate for capturing these complex constructs, potentially explaining their non-significant moderating effects. Third, conceptual and contextual limitations warrant consideration. Social capital operates across multiple levels; however, in this study, we focused exclusively on individual-level perceptions. Ecological-level social capital characteristics may provide additional explanatory power for understanding the FoF–depression relationship. Additionally, social capital is inherently dynamic, influenced by life events and evolving social contexts [57], yet the cross-sectional design does not allow for the capture of these temporal dynamics or an examination of whether the moderating effects vary across different stages of aging or in response to changing health status.

Future research should address these limitations through several approaches. Longitudinal studies are needed to establish temporal precedence and examine bidirectional relationships among study variables. Multi-level analyses incorporating both individual and ecological-level social capital measures would provide a more comprehensive understanding of social determinants. The use of validated multi-item scales is recommended to better capture the complexity of FoF and social capital constructs. Finally, cross-cultural research is needed to examine the generalizability of these findings across different cultural contexts where social capital may operate differently.

Conclusions

This study provides empirical evidence for the protective role of social capital in mitigating depressive symptoms among community-dwelling older adults experiencing FoF. Using nationally representative data from South Korea, we demonstrated that social capital components differentially moderate the FoF–depressive symptoms relationship, with structural social capital showing stronger protective effects than cognitive social capital did. Specifically, neighborhood satisfaction, social networks, and social participation significantly attenuated the positive association between FoF and depressive symptoms. Conversely, neighborhood trust and reciprocity did not show significant moderating effects after correction for multiple comparisons. These findings advance our theoretical understanding of how social resources function as protective mechanisms in the context of physical health concerns and mental health outcomes among older adults. The results have implications for intervention design and public health policy. Intervention strategies should prioritize: (1) facilitating the development and maintenance of diverse social networks through structured social programs; (2) creating accessible opportunities for meaningful social participation; and (3) enhancing neighborhood satisfaction through age-friendly environmental modifications. At the policy level, investments in age-friendly infrastructure and community-based social programs may yield dual benefits by simultaneously addressing fall-related concerns and promoting mental health among older adults. However, some limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The cross-sectional design limits causal inferences, and the cultural specificity of the Korean context may affect generalizability to other populations. Future longitudinal and cross-cultural research is needed to establish temporal relationships and examine the universality of these protective mechanisms across different sociocultural contexts.
