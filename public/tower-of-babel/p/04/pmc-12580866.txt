Low‐Density Lipoprotein Receptor‐Related Protein 11 Promotes Proliferation in Lung Adenocarcinoma
Authors: Takumi Kiwaki, Hiroyuki Tanaka, Makiko Kawaguchi, Yoshiko Umekita, Koji Yamamoto, Tsuyoshi Fukushima, Hiroaki Kataoka, Yuichiro Sato
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLow‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 11 (LRP11) is reported to be overexpressed in various cancers; however, its functional role in lung adenocarcinoma remains poorly understood. This study aimed to elucidate the tumor‐promoting function of LRP11 in lung adenocarcinoma. We assessed the expression and function of LRP11 in lung adenocarcinoma cell lines through both silencing and overexpression experiments. RNA sequencing was performed to identify genes associated with LRP11 expression. The clinical relevance was evaluated using public datasets (The Cancer Genome Atlas and the Singapore Oncology Data Portal). LRP11 was overexpressed in lung adenocarcinoma cells and promoted their proliferation in vitro. RNA sequencing identified multiple genes negatively correlated with LRP11, all of which contained predicted C/EBPβ binding motifs in their promoter regions. Clinically, high LRP11 expression was associated with poor prognosis in patients with lung adenocarcinoma. In conclusion, LRP11 promotes lung adenocarcinoma progression by enhancing cell proliferation and modulating transcriptional activity. These findings suggest that LRP11 may serve as a potential therapeutic target and prognostic biomarker in lung adenocarcinoma.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer and a leading cause of cancer‐related deaths worldwide, with approximately 2.5 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths reported in 2022 [1]. Lung cancer is broadly classified into two categories: non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which accounts for approximately 85% of all cases, and small cell lung cancer [2]. NSCLC is further subdivided into three major histological types: lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), squamous cell carcinoma, and large cell carcinoma [3]. The prognosis of patients with lung cancer remains poor, with a 5‐year survival rate of approximately 20% in most countries [4]. Especially, LUAD is the most common subtype of lung cancer [5], highlighting the need for further improvements in therapeutic strategies.

In addition to conventional treatments for LUAD, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, targeted therapies based on molecular alterations in cancer cells have emerged over the past decade [6]. Several driver gene alterations are well characterized in LUAD, with the most common being KRAS and EGFR mutations, followed by ALK, ROS1, BRAF, RET, MET, and others [5]. Treatments targeting these genetic changes, particularly EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors, have substantially improved patient outcomes. However, several challenges remain, including a lack of effective therapies for certain patient groups and the development of acquired resistance to targeted drugs [7]. More recently, immunotherapies targeting tumor immune evasion have transformed the treatment landscape for lung cancer [8]. Although immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting PD‐L1 and CTLA‐4 have significantly improved survival in patients with NSCLC [9, 10], further therapeutic advancements are needed. To achieve this, it is critical to understand how tumor‐promoting factors contribute to malignant phenotypes such as proliferation in LUAD.

Previously, we demonstrated that hepatocyte growth factor activator inhibitor type 1 (HAI‐1) functions as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting membrane‐anchored proteases [11]. In this study, we identified low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 11 (LRP11), which shares a domain structure similar to that of HAI‐1. LRP11 is a member of the low‐density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) family [12]. Several studies have reported that LRP11 is overexpressed in various cancers, including cancers of the uterine cervix, prostate, and liver [13, 14, 15], but its precise role and underlying molecular mechanisms remain largely unclear.

Here, we demonstrated that LRP11 is highly expressed in LUAD and promotes cell proliferation in vitro. Additionally, we identified transcriptomic changes correlated with LRP11 expression. Finally, we explored the clinical relevance of LRP11 in LUAD using publicly available datasets.

Materials and Methods

Protein Homology Analysis

Protein homology was assessed using NCBI BLAST. The protein sequence of HAI‐1 (assertion number: NP_001027539.1) was used as the query, and homologous proteins were identified using the protein–protein BLAST algorithm. Domain structures of HAI‐1 and LRP11 were retrieved from the NCBI database.

Cell Culture

Human LUAD cell lines A549, HLC‐1, and PC9 were obtained from RIKEN BRC (Tsukuba, Ibaraki, Japan). VMRC‐LCD cells were purchased from the JCRB Cell Bank (Osaka, Japan), and Calu‐3 and H1650 cells from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). The LC‐2/ad cell line was established as described previously by our group [16]. A non‐neoplastic human bronchial epithelial cell line, BEAS‐2B, was also obtained from ATCC. BEAS‐2B cells were cultured in a mixture of Airway Epithelial Cell Basal Medium (ATCC) and Bronchial Epithelial Cell Growth Kit (ATCC), according to the supplier's instructions. All other cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), penicillin G, and streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Evaluation of In Vitro Cell Proliferation and Migration

To evaluate in vitro cell proliferation, cells were seeded in 96‐well tissue culture plates (A549, 500 cells/well; H1650, 1000 cells/well; PC9 and VMRC‐LCD, 5000 cells/well) and cultured for 7 days. Relative cell numbers were determined using the CCK‐8 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical, Osaka, Japan). Each experiment was performed independently in triplicate.

A wound healing assay was conducted to evaluate cellular migration. Cells were seeded in 12‐well culture plates. Once confluent, the cell monolayers were scratched using a 1000‐μL pipette tip and washed with PBS to remove cell debris. The cells were then incubated for 24 h (A549) or 8 h (PC9) in culture medium. Wounds were photographed before and after incubation, and wound length was measured to calculate the rate of closure. The experiment was performed independently in triplicate.

Cell Cycle Analysis

Cell cycle distribution was analyzed by flow cytometry using the Guava Cell Cycle Reagent (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested, washed with PBS, and fixed in 70% ethanol at 4°C overnight. The fixed cells were subsequently stained with the Cell Cycle Reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA content was measured using a Guava EasyCyte Mini Base System (Merck) and data were analyzed using the Floreada.io cloud‐based analysis platform. The experiment was independently performed in triplicate.

Quantitative RT‐PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), followed by DNase I treatment (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan), or using the NucleoSpin TriPrep kit (Macherey‐Nagel, Düren, Germany). For reverse transcription, 3 μg of total RNA was processed using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). Real‐time PCR was performed on the Thermal Cycler Dice Real‐Time System II (Takara Bio) using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara Bio). ACTB mRNA served as the internal control. The sequences of primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Immunoblotting

Cellular proteins were extracted using Nucleospin Triprep (Macherey–Nagel) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Equal amounts of total protein were separated using SDS‐PAGE under reducing conditions on 4%–12% Bolt Bis‐Tris Plus Mini Protein Gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and transferred onto Immobilon membranes (Merck). After blocking with 5% nonfat milk in TBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBS‐T), the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibodies, as described below. The membranes were then washed and incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (diluted in TBS‐T containing 1% BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence reagent (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). The primary antibodies used were as follows: sheep anti‐human LRP11 polyclonal antibody (AF8355; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), rabbit anti‐human caspase‐3 polyclonal antibody (#9662; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), rabbit anti‐human cleaved caspase‐3 polyclonal antibody (#9661; Cell Signaling Technology), and mouse anti‐human β‐actin monoclonal antibody (A5316; Merck). The secondary antibodies used were HRP‐conjugated goat anti‐mouse IgG polyclonal antibody (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA), swine anti‐rabbit IgG polyclonal antibody, and rabbit anti‐sheep IgG polyclonal antibody (Dako, Glostrup, Denmark).

Immunofluorescence

Cultured cells seeded on a 4‐well Slide and Chamber (WATSON, Tokyo, Japan) were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min. After permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X‐100 in PBS for 5 min and blocking with 1% BSA in PBS for 20 min, the cells were incubated with rabbit anti‐human LRP11 polyclonal antibody (GTX87202, GeneTex, Irvine, CA, USA) for 1 h. After washing, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 633‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min and counterstained with DAPI (Merck). The cells were visualized using a SpinSR10 spinning‐disk confocal microscope (Evident, Tokyo, Japan). All steps were performed at room temperature.

Forced Expression of LRP11

The full coding sequence of the human LRP11 gene, followed by a C‐terminal MTC/DDK tag (pEBM‐LRP11) and enhanced GFP (EGFP; pEBM‐LRP11‐EGFP) was cloned into KpnI‐BamHI site of the Epstein–Barr virus‐derived vector pEBMulti‐Neo (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical). The vectors were transfected into cultured cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by selection with G418 (1000 μg/mL).

Knockout of LRP11

The CRISPR/Cas9 system was employed to knock out LRP11 in A549 cells. The Cas9 and single guide RNA (sgRNA) expression plasmid, pSpCas9(BB)‐2A‐Puro (PX459), was obtained from Addgene (Watertown, MA, USA). The sgRNA target sequence, 5′‐GTCGCGCGCTTACCCTGCCG, was designed on the antisense strand of Exon 1 of the LRP11 gene (Figure 3A). The sgRNA DNA was inserted into the BbsI site of PX459. The plasmid was transfected into subconfluent cells using Lipofectamine 3000. After 24 h, the transfected cells were selected using 1 μg/mL puromycin. LRP11 knockout was confirmed via genome sequencing. For the functional rescue of defective LRP11, pEBM‐LRP11 was re‐introduced as described above.

Knockdown of LRP11

Two pre‐designed siRNAs (Silencer Select, Thermo Fisher Scientific), S39655 and S39656, were used as siLRP11#1 and siLRP11#2, respectively, for the transient silencing of LRP11. The target sequences were as follows: siLRP11#1 (S39655), 5′‐CUACGACUGGUGAAACAGAtt‐3′; siLRP11#2 (S39656), 5′‐AGAGGAAAGUUAUAUAUUUtt‐3′. The siRNAs were transfected using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After 24 h, transfected cells were seeded at 1000/well, and the remaining cells were used for RNA extraction. Relative cell numbers were measured 5 days after seeding using the CCK‐8 assay.

RNA Sequencing, Detection of Differential Expression Genes and Transcription Factor Analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol and treated with DNase I. The mRNA was isolated using the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and a strand‐specific library was prepared using the NEBNext Ultra I Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs). Pair‐end 150 bp sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Mapping and quantification of the read sequences were conducted using the Rhelixa RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) pipeline on the NIG supercomputer, consisting of FastQC (v0.11.7), Trimmomatic (v0.38), HISAT2 (v2.1.0), Samtools (v1.9), RSeQC (v3.0.1) and featureCounts (v1.6.3). The read counts were adjusted for gene length and normalized to transcripts per million. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the TCC‐GUI software [17]. To explore interactions between C/EBPβ and the promoter region of each DEG, potential C/EBPβ binding motifs (frequency matrix: MA0466.4) were identified using JASPAR 2024 [18]. P‐values for the binding sites were calculated using the TFM‐PVALUE R package [19].

Gene Expression and Prognosis Analyses Using Public Database

mRNA expression data of LRP11 in cancer cell lines were obtained from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) [20]. LRP11 mRNA expression and clinical data of patients with LUAD were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, N = 490) [21] and Singapore Oncology Data Portal (OncoSG, N = 169) [22]. mRNA expression data were normalized against those of the diploid samples using z‐scoring transformation. All data were accessed via cBioPortal [23].

In addition, to compare LRP11 mRNA expression between tumor and normal lung tissues, we analyzed 57 paired LUAD samples from the TCGA dataset. For cases with multiple tumor samples, the one with the highest LRP11 expression was selected. Expression data in transcripts per million (TPM) were retrieved from UCSC Xena [24].

For reanalysis of publicly available single‐cell RNA sequencing data, we retrieved datasets from the original publication [25] and processed them according to the methods described therein. Analyses were performed using R (v4.2.2) and the Seurat package (v4.3.0). Statistical significance between each cluster and other clusters was assessed using Fisher's exact test for detection rate and the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for average expression levels.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.2.3) and RStudio (v2023.06.1) with R (v4.2.2).

Results

LRP11 Is a Membrane Protein, Highly Homologous to HAI‐1, That Is Overexpressed in Lung Adenocarcinoma

Protein homology analysis identified 24 proteins homologous to HAI‐1, including other serine peptidase inhibitor Kunitz type (SPINT) family members such as HAI‐2 and SPINT3 (Figure 1a, Table S2). Among these, we focused on LRP11, which exhibited a 79% query cover and an E‐value of 1e‐24. Comparison of the domain structures of HAI‐1 and LRP11 revealed that both are single‐pass transmembrane proteins sharing a motif at the N‐terminus with eight cysteines (MANEC), a polycystic kidney disease (PKD) domain, and a low‐density lipoprotein receptor class A (LDLRA) domain. However, LRP11 lacks the Kunitz‐type protease inhibitor domains (KDs) present in HAI‐1 (Figure 1b). Forced expression of pEBM‐LRP11‐EGFP in the LUAD cell line A549 demonstrated localization of LRP11 to the plasma membrane and cytoplasm (Figure 1c). HAI‐1 is recognized as a tumor suppressor, presumably through its protease inhibitor function [11]. However, LRP11 lacks KDs, which are responsible for the protease inhibitor activity, while retaining the other structural domains of HAI‐1. Given this structural similarity, we hypothesized that LRP11 may competitively oppose HAI‐1 and potentially promote tumor progression.

Notably, analysis of the CCLE datasets revealed elevated LRP11 expression (z‐score > 2.0) in 43 of 1156 cell lines, with 30.2% (13/43) of these being lung cancer cell lines (Figure 1d, Table S3). Furthermore, the proportion of LRP11‐high cells among lung cancer cell lines was significantly higher than that in other cancer types (13/198 vs. 30/958; p = 0.020, chi‐squared test). Among the four major histological subtypes of lung cancer, adenocarcinoma exhibited a higher LRP11 expression, although the differences were not statistically significant (mean z‐scores: adenocarcinoma 0.5861, squamous cell carcinoma 0.4582, small cell carcinoma 0.3976, and large cell carcinoma 0.2452) (Figure S1).

RT‐PCR analysis showed that all seven LUAD cell lines examined in this study (A549, Calu‐3, H1650, HLC‐1, LC‐2/ad, PC9, and VMRC‐LCD) exhibited higher LRP11 expression compared to the immortalized non‐neoplastic bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS‐2B (Figure 1e). In the TCGA LUAD cohort, analysis of 57 paired tumor and adjacent normal lung tissue samples showed a trend toward higher LRP11 expression in tumor tissues (mean TPM: 1.078 vs. 0.958), although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.1743, paired t‐test; Figure 1f). Reanalysis of a publicly available single‐cell RNA‐seq dataset of non‐small cell lung cancer (including LUAD and squamous cell carcinoma) revealed that LUAD‐specific subsets, such as CXCL1 and LAMC2 cancer clusters [25], exhibited significantly higher LRP11 expression compared with the normal alveolar cluster (Fisher's exact test and Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, p < 0.001). In contrast, squamous cell carcinoma‐associated clusters, including CDKN2A, proliferating, and SOX2 cancer clusters, showed lower LRP11 expression levels.

LRP11 Enhances Proliferation of Lung Adenocarcinoma Cell Lines In Vitro

Next, we explored the function of LRP11 in LUAD cells. Among the seven LUAD cell lines used in this study, A549 and H1650 cells, which exhibited high LRP11 expressions, were selected for knockout and knockdown experiments, respectively. PC9 and VMRC‐LCD cells, which showed relatively low LRP11 expression, were chosen for forced expression experiments.

To investigate the effect of LRP11 silencing, we generate LRP11 −/− sublines of A549 cells using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. Three LRP11 −/− sublines (A549_dLRP11_#3, #4, and #5) and two control sublines (A549_mock_#2 and #3) were established. Genome sequencing confirmed a frameshift mutation in LRP11 followed by a premature stop codon (Figure 2a). Immunoblotting and immunofluorescence staining demonstrated decreased LRP11 expression in these LRP11 −/− sublines (Figure 2b,c), although the interpretation of the immunoblotting results was complicated by high background signals. Among these, A549_dLRP11_#3 and #5 and mock_#3 were used in subsequent in vitro experiments, while A549_dLRP11_#4 and mock_#2 were used only for RNA‐seq. Cell proliferation assays showed that LRP11 silencing suppressed cell growth (Figure 2d), and wound‐healing assays revealed that LRP11 ablation also inhibited cellular migration (Figure 2e). We also performed LRP11 knockdown using siRNA in H1650 cells and observed a reduction in cell proliferation (Figure 2f–h). Re‐expression of LRP11 in A549_dLRP11_#3 rescued the growth inhibition caused by LRP11 silencing (Figure 3a–c), and a similar trend was observed in A549_dLRP11_#5 (Figure 3d–f). These findings suggest a direct role for LRP11 in promoting LUAD cell proliferation.

Collectively, these experiments involving silencing and overexpression of LRP11 in four distinct LUAD cell lines indicate that LRP11 contributes to malignant traits in LUAD by promoting cancer cell proliferation and migration. No significant activation of caspase‐3 was observed in association with LRP11 modification, suggesting a limited effect of LRP11 on apoptosis (Figure S2).

To assess whether LRP11 influences cell cycle progression, we conducted flow cytometric analysis (Figure S3). In A549 cells, LRP11 knockout did not significantly alter cell cycle distribution (A549_dLRP11_#3), and only a minimal shift toward the G2/M phase was observed in another knockout clone (A549_dLRP11_#5), which did not correlate with the results of proliferation assays. Similarly, overexpression of LRP11 in PC9 cells had no discernible impact on cell cycle phase distribution. Collectively, these findings indicate that LRP11 does not play a significant role in cell cycle regulation in LUAD cells.

We also examined whether LRP11 influences epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT), given its potential relevance to cell migration. Quantitative RT‐PCR analysis revealed partial alterations in EMT marker expression following LRP11 knockout in A549 cells; however, these changes were not consistent with the migration assay results, and substantial clonal variability was observed (Figure S4). In PC9 cells, LRP11 overexpression induced modest changes in several EMT‐related transcription factors, but a clear EMT phenotype was not evident (Figure S4). These findings suggest that the migration phenotype associated with LRP11 is not attributable to consistent modulation of EMT.

Gene Expression Changes Associated With LRP11 Expression

To explore the mechanisms underlying the tumor‐promoting role of LRP11 in LUAD, we performed RNA‐seq on PC9 and A549 cells. In LRP11‐overexpressing PC9 cells, we identified 544 DEGs, of which 158 were positively correlated and 386 were negatively correlated with LRP11 (Figure 5a). Similarly, in LRP11‐silenced A549 cells, 805 DEGs were detected, including 422 upregulated and 383 downregulated genes (Figure 5b). By examining genes that fluctuated in relation to LRP11 in both PC9 and A549 cells, we identified seven genes that were negatively correlated with LRP11: C1R, DEFB1, GBP2, LOXL4, MRPL18 (a pseudogene), PDZK1IP1, and UBA7 (Figure 5c). In contrast, we observed no genes elevated alongside LRP11, except for the pseudogene MT1P1.

LRP11 Is Related to Poor Prognosis in the Lung Adenocarcinoma

Finally, we investigated public LUAD datasets to validate the tumor‐promoting function of LRP11 in a clinical setting. We analyzed data from two independent LUAD cohorts: TCGA and OncoSG. The TCGA dataset revealed a significantly shorter overall survival in the high LRP11 expression group than in the low LRP11 expression group (Figure 6a). Similar results were observed in the OncoSG cohort (Figure 6b). LRP11 expression tended to increase with clinical stage progression, with stage III and stage IV cases exhibiting significantly higher LRP11 expression than stage I cases (Figure 6c). Notably, among patients with stage I LUAD, LRP11 expression did not correlate with prognosis. However, in patients with stage II–IV LUAD, those with high LRP11 expression had a significantly poorer prognosis (Figure 6d,e). These findings suggest a pivotal role for LRP11 in the later stages of LUAD progression. Among the six genes (C1R, DEFB1, GBP2, LOXL4, PDZK1IP1, and UBA7) identified as correlated with LRP11 via RNA‐seq analysis, only UBA7 showed a significant correlation in clinical samples (Figure 6f,g).

Discussion

In the LDLR family, which includes LRP11, some members such as LRP5, LRP6, and LRP8 are known to promote the progression of various cancers through the regulation of diverse intracellular signaling pathways [26]. However, the role of LRP11 in cancer remains largely unclear. Although previous studies have implicated LRP11 in the progression of cervical, prostate, and liver cancers [13, 14, 15], its involvement in lung cancer has not yet been elucidated. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first to demonstrate the role of LRP11 in lung cancer. In this study, we demonstrated that LRP11 is overexpressed in LUAD cell lines compared with BEAS‐2B non‐neoplastic bronchial epithelial cell line. It should be noted, however, that BEAS‐2B is an immortalized bronchial cell line and may not accurately reflect the characteristics of alveolar epithelial cells, which are considered the presumed origin of LUAD. To address this limitation, we also analyzed paired LUAD and adjacent normal tissue samples from the TCGA dataset and observed a trend toward higher LRP11 expression in tumor tissues, although the difference was not statistically significant. Consistently, reanalysis of a public single‐cell RNA‐seq dataset further supported this trend, showing higher LRP11 expression in LUAD clusters compared with normal alveolar epithelial cells. We demonstrated LRP11's growth‐promoting role through both overexpression and knockdown experiments using LUAD cell lines. One limitation of this study is the absence of in vivo experiments to validate the function of LRP11. Nevertheless, we found that higher LRP11 expression is significantly associated with poor prognosis in two independent LUAD cohorts, suggesting a potential contribution of LRP11 to LUAD progression. Another limitation of this study is the genetic heterogeneity among the LUAD cell lines used. For instance, A549 cells harbor a KRAS mutation, while PC9 cells carry an EGFR alteration [27]. These differences in oncogenic driver mutations may influence intracellular signaling and potentially modulate the functional effects of LRP11.

We identified LRP11 as a protein highly homologous to HAI‐1, a membrane‐anchored serine protease inhibitor [11]. Both proteins share three domains: MANEC, PKD, and LDLRA. LRP11 is shorter due to the absence of two Kunitz domains. In contrast, LRP11 possesses only the LDLRA domain among the three core domains of the LDLR family: LDLRA, EGF‐like, and YWTD [28]. This structural distinction suggests that the functions of LRP11 differ from those of other LDLR family proteins. We previously reported that the PKD domain of HAI‐1 is essential for the proper folding and chaperone function of target proteases [29]. Additionally, it is known that the MANEC domain structure of HAI‐1 resembles the PAN/apple domain family, which plays active roles in intra‐ and intermolecular interactions [30]. Given the close structural similarity between LRP11 and HAI‐1, knowledge of HAI‐1's domain functions might be useful for elucidating the functions of LRP11.

Although LRP11 has been reported as a poor prognostic factor in several cancers [13, 15], little is known about the mechanism by which it promotes tumor progression. Wang et al. reported that the expression of cell cycle‐related proteins such as CDK2/4, cyclin D1/E1, MMP‐2/9, and VEGF correlates with LRP11 in cervical cancer [13]. However, in our study, LRP11 expression did not alter cell cycle progression or apoptotic cell death pathways in LUAD cells. These findings suggest that the proliferative effects of LRP11 may be mediated through mechanisms other than direct regulation of the cell cycle or apoptosis.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate transcriptomic changes directly associated with LRP11 modulation. Notably, our RNA‐seq analysis found no overlap between previously reported genes and those associated with LRP11 expression. This discrepancy suggests that LRP11 may exert distinct roles depending on the primary organ, cancer type, and experimental context. Our transcriptomic analysis revealed a set of genes consistently downregulated in response to LRP11 expression. While the specific targets may vary across cancer types, we found that all genes negatively correlated with LRP11 shared predicted C/EBPβ binding motifs in their promoter regions. This suggests that LRP11 may influence transcriptional programs via pathways involving C/EBPβ, a transcription factor known to integrate inflammatory and proliferative signals in cancer cells [31]. Notably, Sun et al. reported that LRP11 physically interacts with MAPK13/p38δ, a component of the signaling cascade upstream of C/EBPβ activation [32]. These findings raise the possibility that LRP11 regulates gene expression through modulation of the MAPK‐C/EBPβ axis. Although the exact mechanism by which LRP11 influences gene transcription remains to be elucidated, our data suggest that LRP11 contributes to LUAD progression by altering gene expression patterns associated with cancer cell proliferation and migration. Further investigation is needed to determine whether LRP11 functions through direct transcriptional regulation, modulation of signaling cascades, or other epigenetic mechanisms.

Among the genes negatively correlated with LRP11 expression, UBA7 is particularly notable due to its established tumor‐suppressive role in lung cancer [33, 34]. UBA7 encodes an E1 enzyme essential for ISGylation, a ubiquitin‐like protein modification system involved in both cancer regulation and immune responses [35]. In LUAD, UBA7 expression is frequently downregulated, and restoration of UBA7 has been shown to inhibit tumor cell proliferation through ISGylation‐dependent degradation of cyclin D1 [33, 34]. In our analysis, UBA7 showed a consistent negative correlation with LRP11 in both cell lines and clinical LUAD datasets, suggesting that it may be one of the key downstream effectors of LRP11. While the mechanism of this regulation remains to be clarified, the inverse relationship between LRP11 and UBA7 expression may partly account for the tumor‐promoting role of LRP11 in LUAD.

Although immune‐related mechanisms were not investigated in the present study, several previous reports have suggested a potential link between LRP11 expression and tumor immunity, including associations with PD‐L1 expression, reduced infiltration of immune cells, and, conversely, enhanced antitumor responses in tumor‐infiltrating T‐cells [14, 32, 36]. The relevance of LRP11 to tumor immunity in LUAD remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

In summary, our study demonstrated the tumor‐promoting function of LRP11 in LUAD and suggested its involvement in transcriptional regulation. LRP11 may serve as a promising therapeutic target and potential biomarker for predicting tumor aggressiveness.
