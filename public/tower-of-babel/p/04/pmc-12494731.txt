Parasitic Pachypygus gibber poses a silent threat to reproduction and development in Ciona robusta
Authors: Sebastiano Scibelli, Mirko Mutalipassi, Iole Di Capua, Nadia Ruocco, Geoffrey A. Boxshall, Maria Costantini, Antonio Terlizzi, Valerio Zupo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pachypygus gibber, an ascidicolous copepod of the family Notodelphyidae, is commonly found within the pharyngeal basket of Ciona robusta, a pivotal model species in marine biology. Pachypygus gibber was traditionally viewed as a filter feeder, but its ecological role —whether commensal, kleptoparasitic, or parasitic—remains debated. We investigated, through controlled laboratory experiments, the impact of P. gibber on the reproductive and developmental fitness of C. robusta. We compared infested (C+) and non-infested (C−) parental lines, recording egg production, hatching rates, larval settlement, and juvenile growth and survival. Results revealed no significant differences in egg production per unit body length between C + and C-; however, hatching and settlement rates were significantly reduced in infested individuals. Moreover, offspring of infested parents exhibited marked growth impairment and elevated mortality over four weeks. These findings provide evidence that P. gibber acts as a parasite sensu lato, exerting substantial negative effects on host reproductive output and juvenile viability. Our results partially redefine the ecological role of P. gibber, with implications for C. robusta population dynamics. In addition, we demonstrated that experimental settings where unnoticed infestations are present, might confound biological studies involving this model organism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-18125-4.

Introduction

Copepods are among the most diverse and abundant crustacean taxa in the seas, playing several ecological roles; in fact, they can be primary consumers, carnivores or even symbionts and parasites of both invertebrates and fish, taking part in complex ecological networks1,2. As described by Poulin (2007), parasitism may be defined as an interspecific symbiotic association between two organisms in which one organism (the parasite) has a nutritional dependence on the other (the host) and has a negative impact on the fitness of the host3. For this reason, parasitism is typically identified by evidence of detrimental effects on the host, which may include tissue damage, alterations in physiological condition, reduced reproductive output, or increased mortality. Without such evidence, distinguishing parasitism from commensalism or other interactions can remain ambiguous3,4.

Copepods, which have independently developed parasitic lifestyles several times, have an exceptionally wide host range that includes 14 different metazoan phyla and display remarkable morphological diversity5,6. Parasitic copepods evolved various strategies to exploit the resources of their hosts, from a simple ectoparasitism to complex endoparasitic lifestyles7,8. These interactions have often broader ecological implications, affecting host population dynamics, community structure, and biogeochemical cycles9–11.

Within the realm of ascidicolous copepods—those associated with ascidians—a multitude of species have been identified, each with variable host preferences, anatomical locations, and ecological roles12–15. While some species exhibit strict specificity towards their hosts16, others are capable of infesting multiple ascidian species17. The copepod order Cyclopoida Burmeister, 1834, contains seven families which primarily or exclusively utilize ascidians as hosts5 and these are clustered together in the suborder Oithonida Khodami, Mercado-Salas, Tang & Martinez Arbizu, 20196,18. They can exhibit distinct body forms and diverse oral morphologies, reflecting the diversity of their lifestyles19. Some species, for example, Haplostomella australiensis Gotto, 1970 inhabit the stomach or the intestine of their host, the simple ascidian Cnemidocarpa radicosa (Herdman, 1882), while others, e.g., Notopterophorus papilio Hesse, 1864, can have large alate processes on the body and are found in the pharyngeal sac of their ascidian hosts, primarily species of Ascidia, Phallusia, and Ciona20. Multiple copepod species can be found in association with particular ascidians21. Phallusia nigra Savigny, 1816, for example, serves as host to both Styelicola omphalus Kim I.H., Cruz-Rivera, Sherif & El-Sahhar, 2016 and Bonnierilla projecta Stock, 1967, in the Red Sea22. The former was attached to the visceral mass of its host, while the latter occurred in the pharyngeal sac. These copepods vary not only in their anatomical location within the host, but also in their ecological relationships. Interactions with their hosts may vary from commensalism to parasitism, however, the exact nature of copepod-ascidian relationships often remains unclear due to the lack of direct evidence of parasitic behavior23. As such, these copepods represent valuable models to study the possible spectrum of host-symbiont interactions, which adds levels of complexity to our understanding of dynamics in marine ecosystems.

Ciona intestinalis (Linnaeus, 1767) and Ciona robusta Hoshino & Tokioka, 1967 are hosts for various copepod species, including Pachypygus gibber (Thorell, 1859)24. As tunicates positioned at the base of the chordate lineage, Ciona robusta and Ciona intestinalis are key models in studies of developmental biology, neurobiology, and marine ecotoxicology, providing crucial insights into gene regulation and body plan evolution across chordates25,26. Pachypygus gibber is classified in the family Notodelphyidae Dana, 1853, a group recognized for its transition to parasitic lifestyle. The Notodelphyidae, together with the Archinotodelphyidae Lang, 1949, was recovered as one of 14 independent transitions to parasitism within copepods6. Pachypygus gibber has been the subject of numerous studies. It has a complex life cycle, involving a nauplius phase of six stages, followed by a copepodite phase of six stages, of which the sixth and final stage is the adult27. The copepod exhibits three sexual phenotypes: the female, plus two types of males referred to as “typical” and “atypical”28. The atypical male form possesses a unique cephalic sense organ, hypothesised to be a chemosensor involved in such complex behavioral patterns as host and sex recognition29.

The ecological role of P. gibber is poorly understood. Michaud and colleagues (2004)30 reported that P. gibber may act as a kleptoparasite, relying on the host’s food string to access nutrients, whereas Hipeau-Jacquotte (1986)29 concluded that it is a filter-feeder, based on analysis of its mouthparts. She also proposed that the atypical male form of P. gibber might be haematophagous29, based on its mouthpart anatomy, which she inferred was better suited to a sucking-type of feeding. Generally, symbiotic associations31 are defined in terms of cost-benefit relationships. Hipeau-Jacquotte (1988)32 presented evidence indicating that P. gibber consumes the same planktonic algae as its host, including such species as Diacronema lutheri (Bendif & Véron, 2011), reinforcing the suggestion that this copepod employs a filter-feeding mechanism32. However, data from a subsequent investigation suggested that P. gibber does not affect the host’s survival, growth, or resistance mechanisms33. This study indicated that, while P. gibber may not significantly affect its host survival, its presence could have more subtle, yet unexplored implications on the host’s reproductive capabilities. Despite the existing knowledge of its ecology and behavior, a notable gap remains in the literature regarding its specific impact on the reproductive performance of its hosts. If P. gibber does not impair host fecundity, it would more appropriately be classified as a commensal rather than a parasite.

Given the limited understanding of these symbiotic associations, it is crucial to investigate the role of P. gibber in C. robusta, especially considering: (i) the widespread use of this ascidian as one of the most important model organisms across various research fields, and (ii) the efforts for optimizing Ciona culturing systems34, for example using existing automatized systems35.

This study aims to fill key gaps in our understanding of the effects of P. gibber infestation on reproductive and developmental features of C. robusta, including egg production, hatching success, larval settlement, growth, and survival of both larvae and juveniles. To this end, we designed and conducted controlled laboratory experiments specifically aimed at testing whether P. gibber exerts measurable negative effects on its host—thus evaluating its ecological role as a potential parasite. To our knowledge, this is the first study to experimentally assess the impact of P. gibber—and more broadly, of any ascidicolous copepod—on host reproductive performance. Our findings contribute to a better understanding of host–symbiont dynamics in marine systems, with implications for parasitology, marine ecology, and aquaculture practices.

Materials and methods

Collection

Adult Ciona robusta specimens were collected in April 2024 from mussel farming ropes at depths of 6 to 20 m in the Ionian Sea, near Taranto, Italy. Field temperatures at these depths ranged between 15 and 16 °C. Only individuals with no visible external damage were selected at the collection site, based on the absence of lesions, deformities, or other apparent abnormalities. These specimens were placed in cooled containers (at a temperature of about 16 °C +/- 0.5) and transported to the Stazione Zoologica facility in Naples, Italy, where they were maintained in 400 L open-cycle aquariums. The sea squirts were cultured under controlled conditions until they were ready for gamete collection.

Morphological identification

Six adult females and one “typical” male of the copepod were preserved in ethanol and used for morphological examination. Specimens were observed on a low power stereomicroscope (Leica M165C), and HD-images of each speciment were taken (Fig. 1). For dissection, speciments were immersed in lactic acid for 24 h and dissected using electrolytically-sharpened tungsten needles. Dissected limbs were examined as temporary mounts in lactic acid using a microscope (Olympus BH2) equipped with differential interference contrast. Identification was made by detailed comparison with the redescription of Pachypygus gibber in the recent monograph on Notodelphyidae36 which also contained descriptions of 37 new genera and 178 new species from ascidian hosts.

As listed by Kim & Boxshall (2020)36, the diagnostic traits of P. gibber are: (1) the terminal claw on the caudal ramus is distinctly larger (about 3 times) than the other 3 claws and the 2 caudal setae are positioned at about 60% and 80% of the ramus length, (2) the antennule is 8-segmented with 3 setae on the first segment, (3) the allobasis of the antenna is armed with a single small seta on outer margin and the free endopod is 1-segmented, (4) the mandibular endopod has a setal formula of 4 and 10, (5) the maxillulary endopod is armed with 4 and 3 setae on the first and second segments, respectively, (6) the third exopodal segment of legs 2 to 4 is about 2.5 times longer than wide and bears reduced inner setae on leg 2, but lacks inner setae on legs 3 and 4, and (7) the free exopodal segment of the female leg 5 is about 2.8 times longer than wide. The material from this study shares all of these diagnostic features. A high-definition image in lateral view of an adult female was taken using a Leica HD camera (DMC5400). This adult female voucher specimens have been stored in the collection of the Natural History Museum, London (Registration Number NHMUK 2025.1793).

Selection of parental individuals and in vitro fertilization

The maturity of Ciona robusta specimens was determined by examining the morphology and pigmentation of the gonoducts through the transparent tunic. Reddish to brownish structures indicated mature oocytes and oviducts, whereas whitish coloration was associated with sperm and sperm ducts. To prevent spontaneous spawning before gamete collection, continuous illumination was provided. In total, 20 individuals were selected, consisting of 10 infested (C+) and 10 non-infested (C-) parental individuals, as biological replicates for the parental groups. They were dissected under a stereomicroscope (Leica M165C, 8x to 100x magnification range) with a scalpel to check for the presence of P. gibber, taking care to avoid damage to the gonads. After dissection, those hosting the copepod were assigned to the (C+) group, while those without the copepod were categorized as (C-). During this process, the total body length (TBL) of each individual was measured under a stereomicroscope using a manual caliper, as the longest distance from the basal attachment site to the end of the oral siphon, with a precision of 0.1 mm. The number of eggs produced was normalized to total body length (number of eggs/TBL).

Gametes were collected from the oviducts and sperm ducts located ventrally, using the same incision. Eggs were collected with a Pasteur pipette and pooled into a 200 mL glass vessel. Sperm was obtained from the sperm ducts of three individuals, pooled, and diluted with 2 mL of filtered seawater (Millipore 0.22 μm). The concentration of gametes was determined using a cell-counting chamber. Fertilization was initiated by adding sperm to a pool of 2,000 eggs in Petri dishes containing 150 mL of filtered seawater, ensuring a sperm-to-egg ratio of approximately 5,000:1. This ratio falls within the effective range reported for Ciona intestinalis, although at the lower end, and is sufficient to ensure successful fertilization while minimizing the risk of polyspermy37. Embryos were incubated in a thermostatically controlled chamber at 18 °C under a 12:12 h light-dark cycle for three days. Temperature stability was monitored throughout the incubation, with fluctuations kept within a tolerance range of ± 0.5 °C, and the temperature value was periodically confirmed using a laboratory-grade thermometer (King Scientific).

After settlement, filtered seawater was used to provide partial water changes, and 150 µL of suspended food (Shellfish Diet1800; Instant algae, Reed Mariculture Inc. USA) was added to prevent starvation before the start of feeding experiments. The density of settled larvae was assessed under a stereomicroscope before the culture dishes were transferred to experimental tanks, marking the experimental time point “0.”

Egg hatching and settlement rates

Eighteen hours after fertilization, Petri dishes were analysed to assess the hatching rates. To this end, the contents of each dish was examined under a stereomicroscope (Leica M165 C), with a total of five microscopic fields observed. Each field contained, on average, 100 to 150 eggs and larvae (both hatched and unhatched). Post-larval growth experiment

Post-larvae settled on plates were transferred to individual culture systems. For each experimental treatment (C + vs. C-), 10 plates were equally distributed across two replicate tanks. All post-larvae were reared using a diet based on dried cyanobacteria (Arthrospira sp.) and milk powder, according to previous authors34. Post-larval growth was carried out using a modified version of a patented automated rearing system (E.U. Patent 102015000012043)35 specifically designed for culturing Ciona. The system consisted of 35 L glass tanks filled with natural seawater. A computer-controlled system managed water exchanges and feeding. Seawater was collected in the Bay of Naples at 15 m depth and underwent a multi-step filtration process (mechanical, adsorbent, skimming) before being temperature-adjusted and stored in a large sump. The system automatically flushed out the tank water every 3 h, and refilled it with fresh seawater pumped from the sump. A central control unit operated centrifugal pumps to transfer water in and out of the tanks. Feed was stored in 5 L glass flasks and was distributed into individual tanks using peristaltic pumps. Recirculating pumps within each tank ensured consistent feed suspension. The automated system followed a cyclical sequence: (a) Water discharge for 10 min; (b) Water refill for 10 min; (c) Feed addition for 15 min; (d) Rearing with recirculation pumps for 3 h. To prevent sedimentation and burial of juveniles, Petri dishes were positioned vertically within each tank using PVC racks. Dissolved oxygen, pH, redox potential, temperature, were monitored daily, while nutrient concentrations (NH4+, PO43−, NO3−, NO2−) were recorded weekly. Post-larvae were examined under a stereomicroscope using a 10x ocular micrometer to measure average length, width, and density within each plate. The experiment lasted 30 days, until the completion of larval development.

Results

Diagnostic characters of the examined P. gibber specimens

The specimens analyzed in this study conform to all the diagnostic features of P. gibber as described by Kim & Boxshall (2020)36. Specifically, the material exhibits: (1) a terminal claw on the caudal ramus that is distinctly larger (approximately three times) than the other three claws, with the two caudal setae positioned at about 60% and 80% of the ramus length; (2) an 8-segmented antennule bearing three setae on the first segment; (3) an antennal allobasis armed with a single small seta on the outer margin and a free endopod that is one-segmented; (4) a mandibular endopod with a setal formula of 4 and 10; (5) a maxillulary endopod armed with four and three setae on the first and second segments, respectively; (6) a third exopodal segment of legs 2 to 4 that is approximately 2.5 times longer than wide and bears reduced inner setae on leg 2, but lacks inner setae on legs 3 and 4; and (7) a free exopodal segment of the female leg 5 measuring about 2.8 times longer than wide (Fig. 1). All these morphological traits were consistently observed in the specimens examined in the present study.

Parental line parameters and reproductive output

The body length of parental Ciona robusta individuals differed significantly between the infested group (C+) and the non-infested group (C-). The mean body length of the C + group was 11.87 ± 4.34 cm, whereas the C- group had a mean length of 7.09 ± 2.03 cm (Fig. 2A).

Panel A (Fig. 2) shows that infested individuals were significantly larger than non-infested ones, suggesting potential size-selective parasitism. Panel B highlights a slight reduction in egg production per unit body length in the infested group, though with overlapping distributions. Panels C and D demonstrate pronounced negative impacts of infestation on early developmental success: hatching rates (Panel C) and settlement rates (Panel D) were consistently lower in the infested group, indicating that P. gibber exerts deleterious effects not only on reproductive output but also on larval viability and recruitment. The maximum and minimum lengths recorded for the C + group were 18.2 cm and 6.9 cm, respectively, while for the C- group they were 10.8 cm and 5.0 cm, respectively. The distribution of body lengths in the C + group (shown in blue) exhibited a wider range and generally higher values compared to the C- group, with significant differences (Wilcoxon rank sum test, W = 13.5, p = 0.006).

The differences in egg production between the C + and C- groups, normalized to body length, were not significant (t-test, t = 1.37, df = 18, p = 0.189). The mean egg production for the C + group was 844 ± 169 eggs/cm, while the C- group exhibited a mean of 944 ± 157 eggs/cm (Fig. 2B). The maximum and minimum values observed were 1139 eggs/cm and 505 eggs/cm in the C + group, and 1140 eggs/cm and 690 eggs/cm in the C- group, respectively.

Hatching rates of fertilized eggs were significantly higher in the non-infested group compared to the infested group (Welch two-sample t-test, t = 5.58, df = 13.78, p-value < 0.0001). The mean hatching rate was 42.02 ± 2.62% for the C- group, while the C + group exhibited a mean hatching rate of 36.78 ± 1.40% (Fig. 2C).

The mean rates of settlement for the C + group were 12.2 ± 7.05%, whereas the C- group exhibited a mean rate of 35.2 ± 17.51% (Fig. 2D). The settlement rates for the C + group ranged from a minimum of 1.9% to a maximum of 24.1%, whereas the C- group showed higher variability, ranging from 10.6 to 77%. The difference between the two groups were consistently significant (t-test, t = 3.85, df = 18, p = 0.001).

Post-larval fitness: growth rates

The body size of post-larvae differed significantly between progeny of infested (C+) and non-infested (C−) C. robusta across all time points, with differences evident from the onset, which intensified throughout the experiment. At the initial measurement (week 0), C − individuals exhibited significantly larger mean body length (0.77 ± 0.20 mm) and width (0.48 ± 0.13 mm) as compared to C + individuals (0.46 ± 0.12 mm and 0.33 ± 0.10 mm, respectively; p < 0.0001 for both; Fig. 3A and B).

Infested juveniles (C+; Fig. 3) exhibited a significant reduction of growth compared to non-infested juveniles (C−) across both metrics, indicating a strong negative impact of Pachypygus gibber infestation on host development. These differences progressively widened over time, culminating at week 4 in a mean length of 3.93 ± 0.64 mm and width of 2.71 ± 0.29 mm in the C − group, versus 1.24 ± 0.37 mm and 0.79 ± 0.27 mm in the C + group. Sidak-adjusted post hoc tests confirmed that these differences were statistically significant at every time point for both traits (p < 0.0001).

Statistical modelling supported these findings, revealing a significant effect of parental infestation status, both on length (Estimate = − 0.41, p < 0.0001) and width (Estimate = − 0.24, p < 0.0001), with strong time-by-group interactions (Estimate = 0.14 for length, 0.24 for width; p < 0.0001 in both cases). Random effects indicated that variation at the plate level (nested within tank) accounted for a modest proportion of total variance (σ² = 0.0028 for length; σ² = 0.0030 for width), while tank-level variance was negligible (σ² = 0), confirming the reproducibility of results across biological replicates.

Growth dynamics were further examined using Malthusian models (Fig. 3A and B). For length, the initial size (Y₀) was 0.57 mm in the C + group and 0.75 mm in the C − group, with corresponding growth rates (k) of 0.21 and 0.41, respectively. For width, Y₀ was 0.34 mm in the C + group and 0.38 mm in the C − group, and k values were 0.20 and 0.49, respectively. The doubling times derived from these rates were consistent across traits, with C − offspring doubling in size in 11.8 days (length) and 9.9 days (width), compared to 23 and 23.9 days in the C + group, respectively.

Post-larval density and mortality rates

The density of settled post-larvae differed significantly between individuals originating from infested (C+) and non-infested (C-) parental lines, across the experimental period. From the initial time point, C- juveniles exhibited markedly higher densities compared to C+, with mean densities at time 0 of 705 (± 350) and 244 (± 141) individuals per plate, respectively. This difference progressively widened over time (Fig. 4A and B).

While the C − group maintained relatively stable density and low mortality throughout the experiment, the C + group showed a marked decline in density and a progressive increase in mortality over time, indicating significant negative impacts of Pachypygus gibber infestation on juvenile survival.

Mixed effects modelling confirmed a significant main effect of infestation status (group: Estimate = −1.096, p < 0.0001) and a significant interaction between time and infestation (time: group: Estimate = −0.223, p = 0.0019), indicating a steeper decline in density in the infested group over time. Sidak’s post-hoc contrasts revealed that C- densities were significantly higher than C + at all experimental time points (p < 0.0001 in all cases), with the magnitude of difference increasing from a 2.99-fold difference at the beginning of the experiment to a 7.30-fold difference at the end of the fourth week.

In parallel, mortality rates were significantly elevated in the progeny of infested individuals (C+) relative to their non-infested counterparts (C-; Fig. 4C and D). Initial mortality levels across both groups were modest; however, by the week 4, the C + group reached a mean mortality of 93% (± 15), while the C- group maintained a substantially lower mortality of 27% (± 15). The mixed effect model detected a significant effect of infestation status (group: Estimate = 1.014, p = 0.00032) and time (Estimate = 0.332, p < 0.0001), while their interaction was not statistically significant (p = 0.424). Sidak’s adjusted post-hoc tests confirmed significantly higher mortality in the C + group at all assessed time points (p < 0.0001 in all cases), with differences amplifying progressively throughout the experiment.

Discussion

Impacts on host reproduction and development

Critical insights into the impact of Pachypygus gibber on the reproductive and developmental parameters of Ciona robusta emerged from this study, improving our knowledge on previously unexplored host-parasite dynamics. Historically, P. gibber was considered a “safe” commensal of Ciona, causing no harm to its host. In fact, previous studies classified P. gibber as a commensal species, with earlier research reporting no visible damage to host tissues, no reduction in survival, and no apparent impact on host energy reserves22,39.

Such findings suggested that P. gibber would not impose significant energetic costs on its host and it was thus regarded as an innocuous symbiont rather than a parasitic organism.

Our findings contradict this view, demonstrating that P. gibber imposes significant reproductive and developmental costs. Infested hosts showed substantial declines in hatching rates and larval settlement compared to uninfested controls (e.g., hatching: ~36.8% vs. 42.0%). These reductions suggest interference with gamete viability or early embryogenesis. Beyond reproduction, we observed severe carry-over effects: offspring from infested parents were markedly smaller and exhibited dramatically reduced survival (~ 7% vs. ~ 73% at 4 weeks). These results confirm that P. gibber, though lacking direct feeding behavior, functions as a parasite sensu lato, by impairing host reproductive success and juvenile viability. This conclusion aligns with earlier suggestions that some ascidicolous copepods can exploit host resources as parasites, and it necessitates a re-evaluation of the ecological role of P. gibber from commensal to parasitic or kleptoparasitic.

Comparison with baseline growth and survival

The severity of the developmental deficits observed can be appreciated by comparing them with the known baseline performance of C. robusta in the absence of parasites. Under favourable laboratory conditions with food available ad libitum, C. robusta juveniles exhibit rapid exponential growth, reaching several mm in length within a month​34. For example, juveniles reared on a high-quality diet can attain ~ 5–12 mm length in 4 weeks, depending on food concentration, with Malthusian growth doubling times of ca. 9–10 days ​34. Likewise, survival rates of Ciona larvae and juveniles in culture are normally moderate; even under suboptimal feeding, roughly one-third of larvae survive to an age of one month ​34. In striking contrast, the progeny of P. gibber-infested adults in our study achieved only ~ 1.2 mm in length by 4 weeks and suffered ~ 93% mortality in the same time. Such mortality rates by far exceed typical attrition under laboratory conditions​, highlighting that the impact of copepods is more detrimental than poor nutrition or other ordinary stressors. In effect, P. gibber infestation mimics an extreme resource deprivation or pathological condition for the host’s offspring. The presence of parasites likely diverts or depletes resources that the parent would otherwise invest in gametes and embryos, resulting in larvae being energetically compromised since the outset. A parallel case involved a parasitic copepod in a nudibranch snail, which reduced host embryo viability by ~ 34–54% without fully sterilising the host—a phenomenon termed partial parasitic castration​40. Similarly, P. gibber does not halt reproduction entirely but severely hampers it, with transgenerational impacts on offspring growth and survival. Thus, our findings place P. gibber in line with other parasitic copepods known to impose reproductive issues on their hosts (i.e., lower hatching rates in infested groups), with an important novel observation, demonstrating that the effects extend into the growth and survival of future generations. This trans-generational impact, where parental infestation leads to less fit offspring, is notable and signifies a compounded fitness cost to the host population.

Host size selection by the parasite

Another important finding of this study is that infested C. robusta adults were significantly larger than non-infested ones. This size difference may suggest a relationship between host size and susceptibility to P. gibber infestation. This pattern strongly suggests that P. gibber preferentially targets larger hosts, or alternatively, its presence influences the size of adults. Size-biased infestation is a common strategy among parasites and hosts, as larger hosts can offer richer resources24,41,42. In our system, a larger ascidian likely filters more water and thus provides more food particles for the copepod, and also possesses a larger pharyngeal basket that can accommodate the parasite with less spatial constraint24,43. In other host–parasite associations, similar preferences have been observed; for instance, ectoparasitic mites prefer larger individuals of their fly hosts, resulting in disproportionate parasite loads on large hosts in nature​44. By choosing the most sizable (and typically older, more fecund) hosts, P. gibber maximizes its own nutritional intake and reproductive opportunities. However, this strategy comes at a cost to the host population. The removal or impairment of large, highly fecund individuals, can have an outsized effect on population dynamics, since those individuals contribute disproportionately to the next generation. In our case, heavily infested large C. robusta produced fewer viable offspring. If P. gibber commonly infests the largest fraction of the population, it could effectively truncate the upper end of the host size distribution in terms of reproductive output. Over time, this pressure might even drive evolutionary shifts45. There could be selection for C. robusta individuals that reach maturity at smaller sizes or for life-history strategies that reduce exposure to the parasite. Hosts that remain relatively small or grow slowly might escape infestation and thus have higher relative fitness, potentially skewing the population towards smaller body sizes over many generations​. Such parasite-mediated size selection is a form of coevolutionary dynamics that has been theorized and observed in other systems46and our findings indicate it may be occurring in the Ciona–Pachypygus interaction. Interestingly, P. gibber has two male morphotypes, typical and atypical29. The mobile atypical males may serve as dispersal agents, facilitating infestation of large hosts, a hypothesis boosting further study30. While atypical males were rare in our samples, their existence suggests a possible adaptive mechanism for host selection that is worthy of further investigation. In any case, the size preference of P. gibber means that its impact is not random across the host population: it is concentrated on the most reproductively valuable host individuals, thereby amplifying the overall fitness of parasites and the effects on the host populations.

Ecological and evolutionary implications for host populations

The cascading consequences of P. gibber infestation at a population level are potentially severe. By sharply reducing the number of juveniles that survive and grow to maturity, infestations can lead to a net reproductive rate below replacement for local C. robusta populations. In affected areas, one would expect lower recruitment rates, skewed age/size structures, and possibly population decline if infestation rates are high and sustained. Our results indicate that infested lineages contribute only a small fraction of viable offspring as compared to uninfested ones. This may translate into substantially smaller population sizes over multiple breeding seasons and reduced genetic diversity. Indeed, chronic parasitism can act as a population-regulating force. In the case of the nudibranch host infested by Ismaila copepods, it was inferred that the parasite could limit the natural population size of the host by suppressing its reproduction​40. When a significant portion of a population of Ciona robusta is infested with P. gibber, effective fecundity plummets: fewer juveniles reach adulthood, the breeder pool shrinks in subsequent generations, and the overall density declines. This demographic shift towards younger, smaller individuals reduces the resilience of the population and carries long-term evolutionary consequences, as discussed above. A population under parasite stress may be less able to recover from additional disturbances such as pollution events or climate-induced stressors, because its reserves of reproductive capacity are eroded47–50. By depressing the number of effective breeders, the parasite lowers the effective population size, intensifying genetic drift and accelerating the stochastic loss of rare alleles and heterozygosity51,52. Strong selection for resistance alleles which arise may trigger selective sweeps, further purging linked neutral variation. Although a selection mediated by parasites and frequency-dependent can theoretically maintain polymorphism (by periodically favouring rare host genotypes53, such balancing forces may operate efficiently only when the population size remains above critical thresholds. In heavily infested C. robusta populations, drift could outpace balancing selection within a few generations, causing a measurable contraction of genetic diversity. This genetic impoverishment is added to the ecological costs described above, by diminishing the host’s capacity to adapt to additional stressors54.

Beyond the host population itself, there are broader ecological considerations. Ciona robusta is an important filter-feeder in coastal communities, known to contribute to water clarity and nutrient cycling by siphoning substantial quantities of suspended organic particles55. It also serves as a sensitive bioindicator of environmental conditions55 and, as a dominant member of fouling assemblages, supplies habitat structure for small invertebrates that settle on or within its tunic56,57. A significant reduction of C. robusta abundance, due to parasitism, could evidently reverberate through the ecosystem58. Locally, a decline of Ciona populations leads to increased phytoplankton and detrital loads in the water column due to loss of filtration service59. Species that prey on, or organisms associated with Ciona, could likewise be affected, and parasite-driven declines can propagate through the food webs, mediated by trophic cascades60. By suppressing C. robusta, parasites such as P. gibber alter energy flows and nutrient processing that the ascidian normally influences, and outbreaks concentrated in hotspots could shift local community composition—an outcome consistent with broader syntheses, showing that parasites may structure the food-web topology and the ecosystem functioning58,61.

Implications for experimental and aquaculture contexts

Beyond natural ecosystems, our findings have practical implications for any context where Ciona robusta is cultured or studied. Ciona robusta is a model organism globally important for developmental biology, genomics, immunology, and evolution, due to its rapid life cycle and tractable laboratory maintenance62,63. The presence of P. gibber could confound experimental results if not properly controlled. For example, an experiment testing the effects of a pollutant or a genetic modification on Ciona reproduction would be severely compromised if the broodstock are infested with P. gibber. The randomly distributed infested individuals would show reduced spawning success and slower offspring growth, independently of any experimental treatment, potentially leading to false negatives or misattribution of effects. Indeed, reductions in reproductive output or larval growth due solely to parasitism could be misinterpreted as treatment effects if the parasite factor is unrecognized. Likewise, any studies on metabolism, feeding, or stress responses could be skewed – an infested ascidian might have altered physiology (e.g., energy reserves diverted to coping with the parasite, or chronic inflammation) that makes it an outlier, as compared to healthy specimens. Therefore, it is crucial for researchers using C. robusta to screen colonies for P. gibber and other potential symbionts and parasites. Even though P. gibber resides internally (often in the pharyngeal basket), careful examination of adults (dissection or endoscopic observation of the branchial sac) can reveal infestations. Quarantining or culling infested individuals may be necessary to maintain parasite-free cultures.

This study advocates for heightened awareness of this issue in the ascidian research community. Similar recommendations pertain to aquaculture. If C. robusta or related ascidians are to be cultivated (for instance, for biofilter purposes or to supply research needs), heavy infestation by P. gibber could undermine production by slashing larval yield and juvenile growth rates. Mitigation strategies, such as periodic cleaning of culture systems, might be required to ensure sustainable captive populations.

On the positive side, the interaction between C. robusta and P. gibber offers a valuable model to study host–parasite dynamics in a controlled setting. The typical transparency and ease of breeding characterizing Ciona robusta, combined with the measurable effects of the copepod on its life history, provide an opportunity to delve into questions of energy allocation, immune response, and coevolution in real time. As our work demonstrates, what was once considered a harmless commensal can exert powerful effects on host fitness, highlighting an underappreciated influence of cryptic symbionts. This system thus exemplifies the complexity of symbiotic relationships and their capacity to influence both individual physiology and population-level outcomes. Going forward, it would be illuminating to investigate the mechanisms through which P. gibber causes the observed reproductive suppression; for instance, does it damage gonadal tissues, compete with the host filter-feeding for nutrients, or perhaps secrete bioactive compounds that disrupt host reproduction? Additionally, surveying different Ciona populations (from native ranges to invasive ranges, if applicable) for the prevalence of P. gibber would reveal widespread and general ecological impacts. Such studies could also examine whether other ascidian species may harbor similar associated copepods and if they have comparable effects on host fitness.

Conclusion

In summary, this study unveils the hidden physiological and ecological impacts of Pachypygus gibber on Ciona robusta. Although it was once deemed an innocuous symbiont, it acts as a parasite sensu lato. Pachypygus gibber infestation leads to pronounced reductions in C. robusta reproductive success and offspring viability, thereby posing a potential threat to host population maintenance and altering host life-history trajectories. These findings prompt a redefinition of the role of P. gibber in the Ciona system and serve to raise awareness of its broader ecological and practical significance. Far from being merely a commensal, P. gibber emerges as an agent capable of influencing host population dynamics and evolutionary pathways, as well as a factor to be reckoned with in experiments and cultures involving C. robusta. By illuminating this host–parasite relationship, our work contributes to a deeper understanding of how, even subtle interspecies interactions, can shape the biology and ecology of marine organisms. It also opens avenues for future research to further explore the mechanisms and consequences of such associations, ensuring that the possible impacts of P. gibber are recognized both in ecological and experimental contexts.
