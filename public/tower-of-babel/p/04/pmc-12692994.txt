Characterization of Maternal and Fetal Immunity Following in Utero Spina Bifida Repair and Surgery‐Induced Preterm Birth
Authors: Katherine Martinez Carmona, Petra K. Lothert, Bohdana Fedyshyn, Jin Sung Jang, Aparna Govindan, Percy N. Pacora Portella, Maureen A. Lemens, Rana Chakraborty, Rodrigo Ruano, Jose L. Peiro, Eric P. Bergh, Mauro H. Schenone, Elizabeth Ann L. Enninga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveFetal surgery (FS) for spina bifida (SB) repair improves infant outcomes; however, spontaneous preterm birth (sPTB) is common. We aimed to evaluate maternal and fetal immune responses following FS for SB repair.MethodPregnant participants undergoing fetoscopic SB repair at 24–26 weeks of gestational age (GA) were prospectively recruited and compared to GA matched low‐risk controls. Blood samples were collected at baseline, 2‐week post‐surgery (POD14), and delivery. Cytokine analysis, flow cytometry, and T cell receptor (TCR) sequencing were performed.ResultssPTB occurred in 5/6 of the FS group (0/6 of controls). Maternal blood in the FS group demonstrated increased alpha‐fetoprotein (AFP) and anti‐inflammatory IL‐1RA at baseline and POD14, increased pro‐inflammatory IL‐8 only post‐operatively, and elevated growth factors throughout. T cell phenotypes following FS revealed reduced TH17 frequency but increased activation receptors on cytotoxic T cells. At delivery, the FS cohort had reduced Th2 and Th17 helper T cells and CD4+ memory T cells, but CD8 T cells still showed increased activation. No TCR expansion was detected post‐surgery, suggesting antigen‐independent activation. Cord blood in FS repaired neonates had increased AFP, IL‐8, IL‐1RA.ConclusionSystemic immune changes following FS, particularly T cell maturation and activation, were observed. Further characterization of these mechanisms may help predict and prevent surgery‐induced PTB, improving outcomes.

Introduction

In utero interventions have demonstrated improved outcomes for fetuses diagnosed with spina bifida (SB), a common non‐fatal neural tube defect associated with significant lifelong morbidity [1]. Advancements in surgical techniques have demonstrated that antenatal surgery for SB repair improves early neurologic outcomes and provides significant benefits in quality of life compared with postnatal repair [2, 3, 4]. Described benefits include a decreased need for ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement, lower rates of hindbrain herniation on MRI evaluation, and higher rates of independent mobility in the child [4].

While FS has demonstrated enhanced survival and reduced morbidity, it is not without risk to both mother and child. Fetoscopic SB repair offers advantages over open repair, such as smaller incisions, reduced uterine scarring, and the opportunity of having a vaginal delivery [5]. However, the therapeutic benefit of intervention is limited by the increased risk of preterm labor [4, 6]. Preterm birth (PTB) rates after fetal intervention vary widely in the literature (36%–80% reported across series) [4, 5, 6, 7]. Complications associated with PTB represent the leading cause of mortality in children under 5 years of age [8], underscoring the critical need for improving our understanding of surgery‐induced PTB.

Both innate and adaptive maternal immunity are altered in pregnancy to accept the semi‐allogeneic fetus, with a pool of T cells recognizing fetal antigens that are generally protective versus pathogenic in normal pregnancies [9, 10, 11]. Spontaneous preterm birth (sPTB) is associated with significant alterations in maternal immunity, including T cell activation and an inflammatory proteome [12, 13]. Cord blood from sPTB neonates has increased proinflammatory cytokines with an expansion of activated T cells compared to infants born at term, which induces uterine contractility [14]. Previous animal studies have shown fetal intervention increases the trafficking of maternal immune cells to the placenta with increased maternal T cell activation against fetal antigens [15]. However, human placentas from pregnancies that underwent FS did not exhibit an increased incidence of inflammatory pathologies [16]. How FS impacts systemic immunity is unknown. Therefore, this study explores whether FS changes systemic adaptative immunity in the mother and fetus with SB and explores their association with sPTB.

Materials and Methods

Patient Selection

This study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (19‐011382) in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki principles. Twenty‐one pregnant participants were enrolled across three medical centers (Mayo Clinic, UTHealth Houston, and Cincinnati Children's Hospital) between 2022 and 2023 (NCT04484441). Ten subjects were enrolled in the FS group and underwent fetoscopic SB repair between 23 and 26 weeks, while eleven subjects having a low‐risk pregnancy were recruited as controls. Inclusion criteria consisted of pregnant patients ≥ 18 years of age having singleton pregnancies with a normal fetal karyotype. Following written informed consent, participants provided a pre‐operative baseline blood draw before FS and an additional blood sample 2 days post‐operatively (POD2) prior to hospital discharge. Control participants were matched to surgical cases by parity and their baseline blood collection occurred at the same gestational age as the pre‐operative blood sample in the FS group. Two weeks after the baseline blood collection, participants in both groups had an additional blood draw (POD14). Upon delivery admission, maternal blood was drawn, and fetal cord blood was collected after delivery. Blood samples from Texas and Cincinnati were shipped overnight to the Mayo Clinic and processed uniformly for mononuclear cells and plasma to minimize site‐related differences. Plasma was stored at −80 degrees C until use. Peripheral and cord blood cells were isolated by density gradient centrifugation and viably cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen. Clinical variables were collected in a REDCap database [17, 18].

Fetoscopic Spina Bifida Repair

The surgical procedure for both institutions was similar to minor differences; the Cincinnati protocol has been previously published here [19]. Both institutions performed prenatal repair for spina bifida via midline laparotomy‐assisted fetoscopy via three surgical trocars (2 for laparoscopic surgical instruments and 1 laparoscopic scope). The surgery is performed under general anesthesia with the possible addition of epidural anesthesia. The mother was given prophylactic maternal antibiotics (Cefazolin) and tocolytics indomethacin (pre‐operation) and magnesium sulfate (during the surgery and possibly for 1–2 days post‐operation). Texas cases received 1 course of betamethasone within the week before surgery, whereas Cincinnati cases were not given pre‐operative steroids. Following exposure of the pregnant uterus, the fetus is repositioned for optimal neural defect exposure, the placenta is mapped, and the 3 entry‐points are determined. The membranes were anchored with suture material prior to placement of the 3 entry‐points. Warmed humidified CO2 gas was used to insufflate the uterus. The fetus is given an intramuscular injection of vecuronium, fentanyl, and atropine for anesthesia. The defect is then closed and intraamniotic antibiotics (Nafcillin at Cincinnati and Vancomycin at Texas) are administered at the end of the fetal procedure. Post‐operative, the mother is given indomethacin for 24–72 h. At delivery, antenatal corticosteroids for fetal lung maturation were considered for all pregnant patients presenting to the hospital with symptoms of preterm labor.

Protein Analysis

Protein quantification from maternal (baseline, POD2, POD14, and delivery) and cord blood plasma was determined using MagPlex cytokine/chemokine multiplex assays (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA). Antibody‐conjugated beads specific to 38‐targets were incubated with samples and ran on a Luminex xPONENT system (Luminex Corporation). Concentrations were calculated using a standard curve and analyzed on the MILLIPLEX Analyst 5.1 software (MilliporeSigma). ELISAs were utilized to measure alpha‐fetoprotein (AFP) and C‐reactive protein (CRP), and concentrations were determined using a 7‐point standard curve of absorbances at 450 nm. All samples were run in duplicate and batched to minimize variability. If samples were re‐run, the plate would include a previously measured sample for correcting batch variability.

Flow Cytometry

Maternal mononuclear cells from baseline, POD14, and delivery were blocked with Fc blocking antibody (Biolegend, San Diego, CA) before being stained with viability dye and 14 monoclonal antibodies specific for T cell subtypes (Supporting Information S1: Table 1). Flow cytometry data were acquired on a LSRFortessa X‐20 (BD Biosciences) using a standardized protocol [20, 21, 22]. Analysis of cellular populations was performed in FlowJo (version 10.10.0, Tree Star).

T Cell Receptor (TCR) Sequencing

Maternal peripheral blood cells from baseline and POD14 were thawed and CD3+ T cells were isolated by magnetic negative selection (Miltenyi Biotec, Waltham, MA). Genomic DNA was then isolated using the QIAamp Blood Isolation protocol (Qiagen, Germantown, MD). Nucleic acids were sent to Adaptive Biotechnologies (Seattle, WA) for TCR beta sequencing using their established multiplex PCR method [23]. Samples were sequenced on the same day to minimize run variability. TCR data were analyzed using the immunoSEQ Analyzer (Adaptive Biotechnologies) and R (version 4.4.2).

Data Analysis

Continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percent. Mann‐Whitney tests and repeated measures ANOVAs were used to compare between timepoints and groups, with the Benjamini, Krieger, and Yekutieli method used for multiple test correction. Grubbs tests were used for outlier analysis. Statistical analysis and data visualization was performed with GraphPad Prism software version 10.4 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) or R (version 4.2.2), with two‐sided p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Increased Inflammation in Maternal Plasma of the FS Cohort

Maternal plasma from the FS and control cohorts was measured for 40 proteins (Supporting Information S1: Table 3). The analysis of proteins on POD2 is highlighted in Supporting Information S1: Figure 1. As expected due to SB diagnosis, AFP was significantly elevated in the FS group at baseline and POD14 compared to controls (Figure 2A). At delivery, AFP levels in maternal blood of the FS group were no longer significant. Interleukin 1 receptor antagonist (IL‐1RA) concentrations were significantly higher in the FS group at baseline and POD14 compared with controls (Figure 2B). Additionally, interleukin (IL)‐10 was significantly elevated at baseline in the FS group compared with controls but not at POD14 or at delivery. At POD14, IL‐8 was elevated in the FS group versus controls (Figure 2C). Epidermal growth factor (EGF) and platelet‐derived growth factor (PDGF)‐AA/BB were significantly increased in FS compared with controls across all time points (Figure 2D). Evaluating chemokines (Figure 2E), granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF) was decreased at baseline in FS compared with the control group. Conversely, macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (M‐CSF) increased in the FS group at delivery. Lastly, macrophage inhibitory proteins (MIP) were significantly increased in the FS group (Figure 2F). MIP‐1α was elevated at POD14 and delivery in FS, whereas MIP‐1β was elevated only at POD14. These data suggest that pro‐inflammatory cytokines in maternal blood increase after surgery, while anti‐inflammatory cytokines were elevated from baseline, suggesting that certain effects could be important in the response post‐surgery while others may be due to SB pathophysiology. Several growth factors were also elevated throughout pregnancy, implying that SB alters circulating concentrations of proteins involved in tissue repair.

FS Increases Activation and Induces Phenotypic Changes in Maternal T Cells

Phenotypic changes in median expression and frequency of maternal T cell populations were determined by flow cytometry. Gating and phenotype definitions are provided in Supporting Information S1: Figure 2. The frequencies of CD4+ Th1 populations increased and Th2 populations decreased at delivery in the FS group (Figure 3A–B). Th17 frequencies were significantly lower in the FS group at POD14 and delivery (Figure 3C). Expression of CD25 was significantly increased on naïve CD8+ cells in FS cases at delivery (Figure 3E), but effector, effector memory (EM) and central memory (CM) CD8+ CD25+ T cells from FS cases were increased at both POD14 and delivery (Figure 3F–H). None of the CD25 increases were associated with an increased frequency of regulatory (Treg) populations (CD25 + CD127lo; Figure 3D). Median expression of co‐inhibitory receptor program death ligand (PD1) on CD8+ EM cells was lower in the FS group at baseline and POD14 but higher at delivery (Figure 3I). Lastly, increased HLA‐DR expression on EM CD4+ T cells was also observed at delivery in the FS group compared with controls. Together, these data highlight unique changes in T cell activation, CD4 bias, and differentiation across gestation corresponding with SB, response to surgery, and surgery‐induced PTB.

No Clonotype Expansion in T Cells Post‐FS

TCR spectratyping was completed to identify whether clonal expansion occurs following FS. An average of 2.3 × 106 CD3+ cells were isolated from each maternal blood sample at baseline and POD14, resulting in an average gDNA concentration of 72.4 ng/μL. The total number of nucleated cells and total T cells were increased at POD14 in the FS group only (Figure 4A–B); however, the ratio of T cells to nucleated cells was not significantly different between timepoints or groups (Figure 4C). Diversity, defined using iChao1, demonstrated similar T cell richness between the FS and control groups at baseline and POD14 (Figure 4D). There were more functional T cell rearrangements following fetal surgery (Figure 4E), but this increase did not shift clonality (Figure 4F). Comparing the numbers of clones directly, there were no differences in the number of clones expanded, contracted, or shared between the FS and control groups 2 weeks after the baseline sample (Figure 4G–I). Lastly, the composition of the maternal TCR‐B V and J segments was profiled to identify areas of high variability that are disproportionately represented between timepoints or groups. Comparing gene usage between baseline and POD14 in the FS group, there were no differences in V/J genes following surgery (Supporting Information S1: Figure 3A). When comparing the differences between baseline and POD14 by group, there were also no differences in gene usage observed between FS and controls (Supporting Information S1: Figure 3B). Overall, the data suggest that FS does not increase the clonal expansion of maternal T cells, making it unlikely that enhanced antigen presentation has a role in preterm birth triggered by FS.

Altered Cord Blood Proteins at Delivery

Fetal cord blood was evaluated to identify changes in plasma proteins in those neonates who underwent fetoscopic SB repair (Supporting Information S1: Table 4). There was a significant increase in various factors, including AFP, anti‐inflammatory IL‐1RA, and proinflammatory IL‐8, in cord blood from the FS group compared with controls (Figure 5A–C). Eotaxin was significantly lower in the FS group versus controls (Figure 5D). Lastly, the FS group exhibited increases in several growth factors and chemokines, including EGF, PDGF‐AA, and M‐CSF, but decreased G‐CSF (Figure 5E–F). Together, these results suggest that neonates in the FS cohort have an altered plasma protein milieu at delivery.

Discussion

Principal Findings

This pilot study aimed to explore the immune system changes after FS and the increased risk of sPTB post FS [24]. The data presented here demonstrates novel associations between maternal‐fetal immunity and fetoscopic SB repair, specifically as it relates to increases in cellular activation and inflammation compared with controls. Measurements of these changes throughout gestation can help with interpretation of their association with SB, FS and sPTB pathophysiology.

Features different prior to FS are likely driven by mechanisms related to SB pathophysiology. AFP, released from cerebrospinal fluid spillage out of the defect, is used for first‐trimester screening for neural‐tube defects [25]. Here, AFP was increased in the maternal circulation in the FS group at baseline and POD14; however, when measured at delivery, maternal AFP levels were not significantly different but were still elevated in fetal cord blood. Growth factors were also increased in the FS group across all time points in maternal and fetal blood. EGF exerts a wide variety of actions, including tissue repair and neural stem cell migration [26]. Murine studies revealed decreased EGF in spinal tissue from fetuses with SB [27]. PDGF has a role in neural crest function and alignment of the neural arch [28, 29], which could explain its increase in maternal and fetal plasma in the FS group.

Changes observed at POD14 suggest mechanisms of adaptive immunity promoting healing post‐FS. Anti‐inflammatory IL‐1RA was increased at baseline and POD14 in the FS group and is known to rise in response to surgery [30], indicating its important role in immune homeostasis and healing. IL‐1RA elevation at baseline in the FS group could be a mechanism to contain the stress response reported in pregnant patients following a congenital diagnosis [31]. Elevated IL‐8 and MIP‐1α/β in the maternal plasma of FS cases were also noted at POD14. IL‐8 plays a direct role in neutrophil chemotaxis and angiogenesis [32, 33]. In the FS cohort, IL‐8 was significantly increased at POD14 but not at delivery, suggesting involvement in tissue repair. FS increased chemokines MIP‐1α and ‐1β, commonly produced by cytotoxic CD8 T cells [34, 35, 36], for recruitment of immune cells to sites of inflammation [37]. Maternal CD8 populations are more activated in FS, which could be a source of MIP increase. Although an infectious process after surgery could potentially elevate IL‐8 and MIP‐1α/β, no surgical complications or infections were observed in the participants. Moreover, TCR analysis did not reveal significant expansion due to antigens. An increase in the expression of CD25 on CD8 T cells at POD14, independent of clonal antigen expansion, indicates indirect activation following FS, and is consistent with a proinflammatory post‐surgical state.

Spontaneous preterm birth following in utero intervention occurred in 83% of participants in the FS group and none of the controls; thus, factors altered at delivery are likely to be driven by sPTB pathogenesis and FS. Unfortunately, there is limited data on this population. As PTB is heterogeneous, surgery‐induced PTB may have unique, unexplored mechanisms that contribute to its much higher rate than general sPTB (∼10%). The data presented here highlights that MCSF increased in the FS group at delivery; however, other investigators report that low levels of MCSF correlate with sPTB [38]. MIP‐1α was increased in maternal blood at delivery in the FS group, but others found no differences in MIP‐1α in women who did not undergo FS but delivered preterm [38]. T cell phenotypes at delivery showed decreased frequency of counter‐inflammatory Th2 and Th17 cells but increased expression of activation markers CD25 and HLA‐DR, which shares similar findings with sPTB phenotypes [12, 13]. Labor is a highly inflammatory process [39], and these results suggest that activation of T cells could have a role in FS‐induced PTB, which may be unique from sPTB.

There is limited data on cord blood from neonates who undergo FS; however, profiling of cord blood from preterm and term infants has been widely reported. IL‐8 was significantly increased in the cord blood of the FS group, but published data are conflicting [40, 41]. IL‐1RA and M‐CSF were also significantly elevated in the cord blood of the FS group but neither was altered in preterm versus term neonates [40]. Growth factors PDGF and EGF were significantly elevated in the neonates who underwent FS, which was contrary to other PTB results [40]. Therefore, these increases could be attributed to healing post‐surgery or reflect SB pathophysiology. These results highlight the heterogeneous nature of PTB and the need to study these mechanisms further in the context of FS.

These findings reveal a dynamic and complex interplay of adaptive immune responses in both mother and fetus following FS. The observed immune patterns differ from those reported in sPTB, suggesting potential distinctions between spontaneous and FS‐induced PTB. These results highlight the need to carefully explore post‐surgical management strategies aimed at modulating maternal inflammatory responses to reduce the risk of sPTB. Additionally, investigating potential biomarkers for the prediction of sPTB post‐FS could provide valuable insights into early diagnosis and risk stratification for patient management. Given the limited research in patients undergoing FS, these data also highlight that T cell activation occurs not through direct antigen recognition but rather indirectly via cytokine release. This presents an opportunity to target this process to prevent surgery‐induced PTB. Larger, multicenter studies focusing on validating these findings, studying underlying mechanisms of inflammation, and identifying potential interventions are necessary to mitigate this outcome.

Strengths and Limitations

This prospective study focused on a single type of FS, which enabled us to minimize procedural bias and disease heterogeneity. As these immunologic changes are likely also driven by SB pathophysiology, these results may not be generalizable to other conditions for which fetal interventions are warranted. The inclusion of participants from multiple medical centers enhances the generalizability of the findings. However, there are notable limitations. Due to the rare character of FS in SB, the requirements for follow‐up and delivery at the same center, and the challenges with samples lost during shipping, the sample size of this cohort is small, which decreases study power and conclusions. While the baseline and POD14 samples are matched by gestational age between groups, the delivery samples are not. Additionally, 5/6 FS cases received steroids due to preterm labor (0 in the control group); thus, the immune changes seen in the delivery samples could be further confounded by this exposure. Comparing additional groups, including those with SB who did not undergo FS and cases of PTB without SB, would provide valuable context for these findings. Lastly, the associations highlighted in the manuscript cannot address causation but are consistent with a possible role of the immune system in PTB. Still, as the impact of FS on maternal and fetal immunity remains poorly understood, and surgery‐induced PTB remains a significant clinical challenge, these results provide foundational evidence on immune mechanisms altered in SB, FS, and sPTB, which should be validated in an expanded cohort.

Conclusion

Prenatal repair of SB has improved clinical outcomes, but the high incidence of sPTB constrains its positive impact. Our findings suggest that maternal and fetal immune responses are different pre‐ and post‐FS compared with normal pregnancies at the same gestational age, specifically through increased activation of numerous inflammatory T cell phenotypes and elevated levels of proinflammatory factors in the FS group. While no evidence of T cell expansion following FS was observed, the activation of these T cells, marked by the release of soluble proteins, sheds light on possible associations with PTB pathophysiology. These biological insights can contribute to our overall understanding of biology and be useful for improving neonatal outcomes post‐FS in the future.
