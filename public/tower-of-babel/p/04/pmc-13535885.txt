Current State of Physical Assessment Education for Newborn and Infant Visits in Public Health Nursing Schools in Japan: A Nationwide Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Mana Shirouchi, Hiroshige Matsumoto, Chikako Honda, Misa Shiomi, Noriko Hosoya, Kyoko Yoshioka‐Maeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivePhysical assessment by public health nurses is vital for childhood health, but its education remains unclear. This study examines the structure, process, and outcomes of physical assessment education for newborn and infant home visits in public health nursing schools.DesignNationwide cross‐sectional study.SampleOf the 280 public health nursing schools in Japan, 85 were analyzed.MeasurementsSurvey based on Donabedian's model: structure (e.g., program type), process (e.g., practice‐oriented education score), and outcome (e.g., proportion of graduates who achieved the graduation goal). Greater practice‐oriented education was positively associated with higher proportions of graduates achieving graduation goals (B = 0.029, p = 0.042).ConclusionsSubstantial variability exists in the structure and delivery of physical assessment education for newborn and infant home visits. Strengthening practice‐oriented, competency‐based education may support more consistent competency development in foundational education.Trial RegistrationClinical trial registration was not required for this study.Keywords: community, foundational education, home visit, physical examination, public health nurse

Introduction

Early detection of health risks in newborns and infants is crucial for ensuring healthy growth and development; nurses play a pivotal role in this process (Giltenane et al. 2021; Wightman et al. 2022). During home visits, nurses are expected to conduct comprehensive physical assessments—palpation, auscultation, measurement, observation, interviews, and screening—to identify potential risks and provide targeted support (Giltenane et al. 2021; Hagan et al. 2017; Yoshioka‐Maeda et al. 2023). Globally, home visit programs demonstrate that nurse‐led assessments improve child health outcomes and highlight the importance of nursing competencies and public health nursing in promoting health during infancy and early childhood (Giltenane et al. 2021; Milojevich et al. 2025; Stevens et al. 2024).

In Japan, newborn and infant home visits are universally implemented under national legislation and conducted primarily by public health nurses (PHNs) employed or commissioned by municipal governments. Under the Child Welfare Act (1947) and the Maternal and Child Health Act (1949), municipalities are responsible for providing maternal and child health services, including early postnatal home visits (Child Welfare Act 1947; Maternal and Child Health Act 1949). PHNs, licensed as one of Japan's three national nursing professionals—nurse, PHN, or midwife—are employed by municipal governments and provide maternal and child health services, including universal home visits (Japanese Nursing Association 2023). Unlike clinical nurses, municipal PHNs are required to integrate individual‐level assessment and care with administrative coordination, policy planning, and community‐ and system‐based interventions (Yokobori et al. 2022; Yoshioka‐Maeda 2020). This multi‐layered role structure increases the diversity and complexity of educational content in public health nursing education and requires that PHNs acquire advanced competencies.

Despite this critical responsibility, a previous study revealed that around 80% of novice PHNs lack the ability to independently perform physical assessments, and gradually acquire the skill over time (Miyata et al. 2022). From early in their careers, PHNs are expected to accurately assess newborns and infants and link these assessments to appropriate support and systemic actions (Miyata et al. 2022). This gap between expected professional competence and early‐career performance raises concerns regarding the adequacy of foundational education. One limitation of basic nursing training curricula is that learning often focuses on broad biomedical understanding rather than practical application (Tan et al. 2021). Nursing students have limited opportunities to bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and hands‐on practice, leaving them underprepared for real‐world assessments (Kishi et al. 2018; Maniago et al. 2021). However, no nationwide data have clarified how educational structures, teaching processes, and student outcomes are aligned in public health nursing programs in Japan.

Although general governmental guidelines for public health nursing education exist in Japan, programs are offered at the undergraduate level and in supplementary programs (master's and one‐year junior colleges), and each school independently designs its curriculum (Japanese Nursing Association 2023; Kishi et al. 2018). Such structural diversity may influence whether educational goals emphasize knowledge acquisition or independent performance and how practice opportunities are provided. Professional practice requires progression from “knows” to “does,” emphasizing that competence cannot rely on knowledge alone but also requires practice in real‐world contexts (Miller 1990). Consistent with these circumstances, international nursing education has employed advanced simulation‐based and mobile‐assisted training to enhance students’ competence and confidence, demonstrating the value of practice‐oriented approaches (Egilsdottir et al. 2023; Hwang et al. 2022). In contrast, physical assessment education for newborns and infants in Japan remains underdeveloped, and the current educational status has not been systematically investigated (Yoshioka‐Maeda et al. 2024). In particular, whether schools emphasizing competency‐based graduation goals provide more practice‐oriented education and whether such processes are associated with improved student achievement remain unclear.

To address this gap, this study integrates two complementary theoretical perspectives. Miller's pyramid conceptualizes clinical competence as progression from “knows” to “does” (Miller 1990). Donabedian's model posits that outcomes are shaped by educational structures and processes, providing a framework to examine how institutional characteristics and teaching practices influence learning results (Donabedian 1988; Ghofrani et al. 2024). By combining these frameworks, it becomes possible to examine whether structural emphasis on competency is reflected in teaching processes and ultimately in educational outcomes. This nationwide cross‐sectional study aimed to examine the structure, process, and outcomes of physical assessment education for newborn and infant home visits in public health nursing schools in Japan. Specifically, we tested the following hypotheses regarding physical assessment education: (1) Independent curriculum design by schools may result in variation across schools in the emphasis placed on educational goals as an educational structure, (2) educational structures that emphasize independent performance, representing higher levels of Miller's pyramid, are expected to be associated with more practice‐oriented teaching processes, and (3) Practice‐oriented teaching processes are positively associated with higher proportions of students achieving the schools’ graduation goals. Clarifying these relationships will contribute to strengthening competency‐based education and improving the quality and standardization of PHN preparation nationwide.

Methods

Definitions

Physical assessment education was defined as teaching activities designed to develop the knowledge and practical competencies necessary to assess the health status, growth, and development of newborns and infants during home visits, including knowledge and practical competencies in anthropometric measurements and caregiver interviews used also in public health checkups (Giltenane et al. 2021; Hagan et al. 2017; Yoshioka‐Maeda et al. 2023).

Study Design

This study adopted a cross‐sectional study design.

Participants and Procedure

In this study, we targeted 292 schools of higher education in public health nursing (including universities, master's programs, and one‐year junior colleges) designated by the Japanese Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science, and Technology as of May 1, 2024. We excluded schools that the authors are affiliated with (n = 3), those that stopped recruiting students before April 2019 (n = 2), and those that were newly established after April 2023 (n = 7). Consequently, request letters and a web‐based survey link were sent in December 2024 to 280 eligible schools, inviting them to participate (Figure 1).

Participant selection process for this study. A flowchart shows the number of public health nursing schools in Japan (292), the number of schools excluded (12), the number of schools included in the study (280), and the number of schools that responded to the web survey and were analyzed (85).

One faculty member responsible for public health nursing education at each school was asked to complete a structured, self‐administered questionnaire, including items on the school's educational status during the 2023 academic year. To enhance the response rate, reminders were sent to all schools 3 weeks after the initial request. Data collection closed in January 2025.

Measurements

Educational status was assessed using the Avedis Donabedian structure–process–outcome framework, which conceptualizes outcomes as arising from structural characteristics through educational processes (Donabedian 1988; Ghofrani et al. 2024) (Figure 2). This model was developed to assess the quality of medical care. Recently, it has also been applied to evaluate the quality of education in nursing schools (Donabedian 1988; Ghofrani et al. 2024).

The conceptual structure of this study. The conceptual framework of this study. This figure shows the survey items included in the Structure, Process, and Outcomes of physical assessment education.

Structure

We collected data on the program type, number of full‐time faculty members in public health nursing education, years of experience of the most experienced faculty members in municipal maternal and child health projects and newborn and infant home visits, departments involved in teaching physical assessment during newborn and infant home visits, the degree of interdepartmental information sharing in teaching physical assessment for newborn and infant home visits (Kishi et al. 2018), the number of graduates in the 2023 academic year, and the number of graduates per full‐time faculty member of public health nursing education.

Additionally, graduation goals for physical assessment were categorized into four ordinal levels according to Miller's pyramid and the education guidelines for nursing schools in Japan: 1. Knows (knowledge only), 2. Shows (demonstration in campus training), 3. Does under supervision, and 4. Does independently with minimal guidance (Miller 1990; Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare 2019). The perceived importance of physical assessment education was rated on a 4‐point Likert scale (1: Very important, 4: Not important at all).

Process

The process was evaluated by assessing the score of practice‐oriented education regarding physical assessment. Twenty items covering physical assessment points (e.g., body symmetry) were adapted from previous studies and guidelines (Hagan et al. 2017; National Center for Child Health and Development 2018; Silva et al. 2018). Response categories reflected the score of practice‐orientation: unknown and not covered (0 points), faculty explains (1 point), faculty demonstrates (2 points), students practice in a simulation (3 points), and students perform on newborns/infants under supervision (4 points) (The Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science, and Technology 1956). For each physical assessment item, we calculated the number and percentage of schools selecting each response category. We also calculated the mean score, with a possible range of 0–4, and standard deviation for each item as indicators of the degree of practice‐oriented education. The mean score was calculated by summing the products of each response‐category score and the number of schools selecting that category, and then dividing the total by the 85 responding schools. In addition, a total process score, with a possible range of 0–80, was calculated for each school by summing the scores across the 20 items; higher scores indicated a stronger practice orientation. Additionally, the proportion of graduates who participated in newborn and infant home visits during practical training was collected (Kishi et al. 2018).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of graduates in 2023 who achieved the school's stated graduation goal for physical assessment competence (Kishi et al. 2018).

Statistical Analysis

First, descriptive statistics were conducted for each structure, process, and outcome variable, including the graduation goal based on hypothesis (1). Second, to test hypothesis (2), multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the association between structural indicators and process indicator. The dependent variable was the total score of practice‐oriented education as a process indicator (continuous). The primary independent variable was the graduation goal of physical assessment during newborn and infant home visits as a structure indicator (ordinal; knows = 1, shows = 2, does under supervision = 3, does independently with minimal guidance = 4), which was treated as a continuous variable in the analysis. Covariates included following structural indicators: program type (dichotomous; university = 1, supplementary education = 2), departments involved in teaching physical assessment for newborn and infant home visits (dichotomous; other departments = 1, community/home/public health nursing = 2), the degree of interdepartmental information sharing (dichotomous; no sharing of educational content = 1, partial sharing, complete sharing, and no need to share because education is offered in only one department = 2), and the number of graduates per full‐time faculty member involved in public health nursing education (continuous). These variables showed significant correlations with the total score of practice‐oriented education regarding physical assessment in Spearman's correlation analysis. Unstandardized coefficients (B), standard errors (SE), standardized coefficients (β), t‐values, and p‐values were estimated and reported. Model fit was evaluated using R2 and adjusted R2. Third, to test hypothesis (3), a simple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the association between practice‐oriented education and the achievement of graduation goals. The dependent variable was the proportion of graduates achieving graduation goals (continuous), and the independent variable was the total score for practice‐oriented education of physical assessment (continuous). Since no significant association was found between the proportion of graduates who achieved their graduation goals and those who participated in newborn and infant home visits during practical training, this process variable was not included as a covariate. The significance level was set at two‐sided p < 0.05, and all analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS 30.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons, given the exploratory nature of this study.

Ethical Considerations

The Institutional Review Board at the University of the First Author [Approval No. 2023101NI‐(3)] approved this study protocol, which followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. During the study request phase, we communicated with the faculty members at each school who were primarily responsible for public health nursing education. This communication included an explanation of the study, an emphasis on ethical considerations, a request for letters to accompany a survey, a web‐based survey link, and a document outlining the questions. Participation in the survey was voluntary and anonymous. Additionally, a consent checkbox for study participation was included in the web‐based survey to obtain consent from all participants.

Discussion

This study is the first nationwide comprehensive analysis to clarify how public health nursing schools in Japan teach physical assessment for newborn and infant home visits. By systematically analyzing the structures, processes, and outcomes of education, we addressed the gap identified in the introduction—the lack of empirical data on how curricula translate policy expectations into actual teaching practice. Our findings suggest a potential structural gap between national policy goals and educational implementation, providing new insights into the design and evaluation of competency‐based public health nursing education.

Regarding hypothesis (1), most schools recognized the importance of physical assessment education, although some set graduation goals below the national standards. The guideline lists 77 items, one of which is to “assess through integrated collection of objective and subjective data, considering developmental, physical, psychological, and sociocultural dimensions” (Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare 2019). This item is intended for students to perform independently with minimal guidance (Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare 2019). Our findings suggest that these policy‐level expectations are not uniformly reflected in school‐level graduation targets. This pattern is consistent with prior concerns about limited instructional time and educator capacity in public health nursing education (Hakim 2023; Kishi et al. 2018; Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare 2019).

From a workforce perspective, PHNs are frontline professionals responsible for early identification of health risks and provision of timely support for the maternal–child population. If foundational education provides limited opportunities to develop performance‐level assessment competence, early‐career PHNs may rely heavily on on‐the‐job learning, which may delay readiness in time‐sensitive contexts (Delaplain et al. 2022; Giltenane et al. 2021). Simulation‐based education can partially replace clinical training without reducing competency (Hayden et al. 2014). Accordingly, strengthening performance‐oriented graduation goals and integrating simulation strategically may be a feasible approach to enhance competence under constrained training resources.

Regarding hypothesis (2), schools with higher graduation goals were more likely to implement practice‐oriented education, supporting the hypothesized association between structure and process. This result aligns with Miller's pyramid, which emphasizes progression from “knows” to “does,” and with Kolb's experiential learning theory, which highlights learning through action and reflection (Kolb 2014; Miller 1990). Physical assessment requires the practical integration of multiple skills—inspection, palpation, and measurement—through guided practice (Maniago et al. 2021). Our findings indicate that establishing practical competency goals is crucial for delivering courses that help novice PHNs confidently perform assessments early in their careers (Daneshfar and Moonaghi 2025; Mani 2025). Aligning educational goals with instructional content is the responsibility of faculty members (Mani 2025) and may play an important role in enhancing the quality and standardization of maternal and child health nationwide.

In this study, schools where multiple departments collaborated on physical assessment education and shared information across departments tended to provide more practice‐oriented instruction. This suggests that curriculum integration can influence the practicality of education. Physical assessment is an interdisciplinary competency integrating knowledge of anatomy, pathophysiology, development, and clinical skills (Morrell et al. 2021). Collaboration among nursing educators in sharing educational goals and instructional content enables interdisciplinary education that links knowledge to practice and ensures educational quality (Ito and Lim 2025). Therefore, positioning physical assessment as a core, cross‐departmental competency and designing an interdisciplinary curriculum may be important for fostering practical skills.

In addition, this study newly revealed that practice‐oriented education varies by assessment item. Fundamental items, such as height, weight, and head measurements, were consistently emphasized, while areas like the back, nose, and oral cavity received less attention. These results align with a previous report, which showed that the proportion of nursing schools teaching physical assessment varies by body region (Ota et al. 2025). Such selective emphasis suggests that schools may prioritize essential items for maternal and child health surveillance at their discretion (Giltenane et al. 2021; Kishi et al. 2018). However, PHNs are expected to possess high‐quality and equitable assessment and support for children's growth and development as a public resource, especially in regions with limited medical resources (Dahl et al. 2022; Nam et al. 2025; Ohta and Sano 2024; Yoshioka‐Maeda et al. 2023). Therefore, strengthening comprehensive physical assessment education is crucial for developing PHNs as key community health resources and contributing to the reduction of health disparities through upstream approaches.

Concerning hypothesis (3), there was a positive correlation between practice‐oriented education and the proportion of graduates who achieved their learning goals. This reinforces Kolb's experimental learning theory, which emphasizes the value of feedback‐rich, practice‐based environments in consolidating knowledge and skills (Kolb 2014). For students, having opportunities to perform physical assessments under supervision and reflect on them afterward is likely to promote deeper learning and better skill retention (Byermoen et al. 2022; Egilsdottir et al. 2021; Kolb 2014). However, similar to the results of this study, nursing universities in Japan are still in the developmental stage of introducing physical assessment education for patients of all ages (Ota et al. 2025). Expanding realistic training opportunities and strengthening the integration of theoretical knowledge with practical skills are necessary (Kindi et al. 2025).

This study has some limitations. First, although reminders were sent to each school, the response rate was 30.4%, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Second, only one representative per school responded, and their views may not fully reflect the collective stance of the school. Schools that are interested in physical assessment education may have been more likely to respond, potentially leading to overestimation of the actual implementation of physical assessment practices. Third, since physical assessment is regarded as a core nursing skill, respondents may have overreported its actual implementation.

Despite these limitations, this study provides new evidence that not all public health nursing schools in Japan deliver practical education on newborn and infant physical assessment, even though such skills are essential for PHNs and widely recognized as important. By clarifying structural, process‐related, and outcome‐related aspects of education, our findings offer novel insights into how competency‐based curricula can be strengthened. Future research should develop standardized and practice‐oriented programs, evaluating their effectiveness in improving students’ confidence and readiness to conduct physical assessments in community maternal and child health settings with long‐term perspectives.

Conclusion

This study is the first nationwide comprehensive investigation of how Japanese public health nursing schools teach physical assessment for newborn and infant home visits, examining their educational structure, process, and outcomes. The findings revealed substantial variability across schools in graduation goals and in the degree of practice‐oriented instruction. Schools that set higher performance‐based graduation goals tended to provide more practice‐oriented education, and greater practice orientation was associated with higher reported achievement of graduation goals.

These findings suggest that alignment between educational structure and teaching processes may play an important role in supporting competency attainment in foundational public health nursing education. Although causal relationships cannot be inferred from this cross‐sectional design, the results provide empirical support for strengthening performance‐oriented goals and expanding supervised practice opportunities within public health nursing curricula.

Enhancing coherence between graduation goals and instructional strategies may contribute to improving the consistency and quality of preparation for newborn and infant home visits nationwide. Future research should evaluate standardized, competency‐based educational models and examine their impact on graduate readiness and early‐career performance in community maternal and child health settings.

The Institutional Review Board at the University of the First Author [Approval No. 2023101NI‐(3)] approved this study protocol, which followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. During the study request phase, we communicated with the faculty members at each school who were primarily responsible for public health nursing education. This communication included an explanation of the study, an emphasis on ethical considerations, a request for letters to accompany a survey, a web‐based survey link, and a document outlining the questions. Participation in the survey was voluntary and anonymous. Additionally, a consent checkbox for study participation was included in the web‐based survey to obtain consent from all participants.
