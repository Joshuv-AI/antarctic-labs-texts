Augmented CENH3 loading is accompanied by transcriptional and epigenetic reprogramming at rice centromeres during meiosis
Authors: Lei Cao, Yangzi Zhao, Hanli You, Huixin Liu, Bingxin Wang, Yonghang Run, Han Yang, Minsi Wen, Jiawei Chen, Pengtao Zhao, Yi Shen, Yafei Li, Wenli Zhang, Zhukuan Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCentromere identity in eukaryotes is defined epigenetically by CENH3 (CENPA), a specialized histone H3 variant essential for kinetochore establishment and faithful chromosome segregation. However, the regulatory mechanisms governing CENH3 loading during meiosis and how they differ from mitotic patterns remain insufficiently elucidated.ResultsHere we characterize the dynamics of CENH3 deposition across meiosis and compare them with mitotic loading in rice. Quantitative fluorescence imaging reveals a pronounced increase of CENH3 signal during meiotic prophase I, coinciding with increased accumulation of multiple kinetochore components. Super-resolution stimulated emission depletion microscopy further confirms a distinct peak of CENH3 loading at zygotene. Through low-input ChIP-seq integrated with multi-omics profiling of purified meiocytes, we find that this meiosis-specific enrichment reflects both expanded and intensified CENH3 deposition, predominantly at Ty3-Gypsy retrotransposons. This remodeling is accompanied by reduced transcription of mRNAs and small RNAs, along with a reduction in CHG methylation and H3K9me2 heterochromatin marks.ConclusionOur findings uncover a previously unrecognized, meiotic-specific pattern of CENH3 loading in rice and highlight a coordinated regulatory network linking centromeric chromatin reorganization, transcriptional repression, and epigenetic modification during early meiotic progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-026-04020-2.Keywords: CENH3, Centromere, Rice, Meiocyte, DNA methylation, Histone modification

Background

Centromeres are regions on eukaryotic chromosomes where microtubules attach during mitosis and meiosis to ensure the proper segregation of sister chromatids or homologous chromosomes to daughter cells. The histone variant of the H3 family, CENH3 (known as CENP-A in mammals and CID in Drosophila melanogaster), specifically replaces the canonical H3 at centromeres and marks these regions [1, 2]. The resulting chromatin environment has distinct properties, differing from those of both canonical heterochromatin and euchromatin. The CENH3 nucleosome is recognized and bound by CENP-C and CENP-N [3–5], two components of the constitutive centromere-associated network (CCAN), whose primary roles are to recruit and interact with the outer components of the kinetochore [6]. Outer kinetochore proteins support and form a clamp that connects microtubules during mitosis and meiosis to the underlying centromeric chromatin. The kinetochore complex consists of more than 100 proteins that are highly conserved across different species.

In most plants and animals, regional centromeres typically consist of a central core (where CENH3 nucleosomes reside) composed of centromeric DNA comprising megabases of tandemly repeated satellite sequences [7, 8], and an outer heterochromatic domain, termed the pericentromere, which typically contains fewer ordered repeats. Monomers of satellite repeats range in length from 5 bp in Drosophila to 1,419 bp in cattle, although most described monomers range between 100 and 200 bp, often considered approximately the length of nucleosomal DNA [9, 10]. These monomers are arranged head-to-tail to form higher-order repeats across the centromere and serve as a platform for kinetochore binding, which is regulated by epigenetic mechanisms [1, 7]. The CENH3 nucleosomes typically occupy only a portion of the satellite repeats, often in discontinuous blocks. Ty3-Gypsy class retrotransposons are reported to be associated with unique centromere repeat junctions in several model plants, including Athila repeats in Arabidopsis thaliana, centromeric repeats in maize (CRM), centromeric repeats in rice (CRR), centromeric repeats in wheat (CRW) in wild einkorn wheat (Triticum boeoticum) and centromeric repeats in sugarcane (Saccharum officinarum) (CRS) [11–17], and they potentially target functional centromeres as insertion sites [18]. Interestingly, while the DNA underlying kinetochores is typically associated with large arrays of tandem repeats, centromeres can assemble on various sequence types, including unique genic sequences [19, 20].

Despite the specificity of DNA sequences and centromere structures, the centromere is identified more by its epigenetic characteristics in most eukaryotes. Early studies linked centromeres with heterochromatin [21], and subsequent work showed that the pericentromere, in particular, is heterochromatic, and contains hypermethylated H3K9me2/3, histone H4 trimethylated at lysine 20 (H4K20me3), and hypoacetylation [22, 23]. However, nonrepetitive centromeres and neocentromeres frequently lack surrounding heterochromatin [24, 25]. In contrast to heterochromatic pericentromeres, the core centromere features CENP-A-containing nucleosomes interspersed with canonical H3-containing nucleosomes that carry transcriptionally permissive marks, particularly H3K4me1/3 [26–28], H3K36me2/3, and hyperacetylated marks such as H4K5ac and H4K12ac [29, 30]. In addition to the markedly different histone environment, DNA methylation displays a similar distinction between the centromere and pericentromere. In A. thaliana and maize, 5mC methylation was found to be absent at centromeres [31]. More specifically, CHG and CHH context methylation, which uniquely exist in plants, shows relatively reduced levels in centromeric regions compared to pericentromeric regions in A. thaliana, which is consistent with the centromeric depletion of H3K9me2, which maintains DNA methylation in non-CG contexts [32, 33]. DNA methylation at rice and maize centromeres is reported to vary among chromosomes and gametic stages [34–36], with a subtle decrease in CHG methylation at the centromere core. Besides these epigenetic marks, active transcription has been detected from both regions (centromere and pericentromere) in many organisms, originating from the active genes at the centromere core, as well as from centromeric satellites and retroelements [9, 30, 36–41]. A clear balance in transcriptional output from each region is required to maintain chromosome stability, such as by facilitating the binding of CENP-C to DNA and the assembly of centromeric chromatin and the kinetochore. Additionally, meiotic crossover suppression, which spans distances from kilobases to megabases [42–45] and extends into centromere-flanking regions, is linked to epigenetic organization and CENH3 distribution in Arabidopsis [46].

Although the essential functions of CENH3 loading onto the centromere and kinetochore assembly in mitosis are well studied, their functions in meiosis are less understood. Investigations into the cell cycle timing of centromere assembly during mitosis have proven critical for understanding the mechanisms of centromere specification and function. Unlike the diverse timing of mitotic CENP-A assembly, which occurs during late telophase and early G1 phase in humans, anaphase in syncytial divisions in Drosophila embryos, or at the G2 phase in plants and fission yeast, meiotic CENH3 assembly in most organisms occurs during early prophase I. Furthermore, the centromere, indicated by CENH3 fluorescence intensity, clearly expands during this period, which makes it markedly different from mitosis [47–50]. Our previous research also revealed that the expansion of the centromere signal in early prophase I is indispensable evidence of normal meiotic initiation [51–54]. However, the mechanisms underlying meiotic CENH3 loading and its effects on centromeric epigenetic environment, especially in plants, have not yet been reported primarily due to the difficulty in obtaining pure plant reproductive cells and the technical limitations in detecting epigenetic characteristics using small quantities of cells.

In this study, we tracked the timing of CENH3 loading and quantified centromere expansion in rice meiocytes (pollen mother cells, PMCs). By integrating low-input chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) and multiomics analyses, we revealed that compared with mitosis, meiosis-specific CENH3 deposition is accompanied by transcriptional and epigenetic reprogramming at centromeres. We propose that the coordinated regulation of centromeric chromatin facilitates proper meiotic centromere assembly.

Results

CENH3 signals are enhanced in rice early meiosis compared with mitosis

To better understand the distinct centromere behaviors between meiotic and mitotic cells, we performed dual fluorescence immunolocalization in rice to explore their dynamic distribution during cell division, thus providing insights into the developmental divergence between these two processes. An antibody against CENH3 (the centromere-identity histone) was used to label active centromeres, while an antibody against Recombinant Protein 8 (REC8), a meiosis-specific cohesin component, was employed as an axial signal to indicate the progression of prophase I (Fig. 1a). REC8 signals were first detected as dotted foci in the nucleus at leptotene, whereas CENH3 signals were distributed as round dotted foci along the chromosomal axis. At early zygotene, REC8 signals extended into filamentous structures, coinciding with the onset of homologous synapsis. Simultaneously, the centromere signals coalesced into larger and irregular foci. As homologous synapsis progressed from chromosome arms to the centromeres at late zygotene, REC8 signal became more continuous, whereas CENH3 signals further increased in volume. At pachytene, complete synapsis was observed along both chromosome arms and centromeric regions, with twelve compact and regularly arranged CENH3 foci corresponding to all paired centromeres. At diakinesis, the chromosomal axis signals diffused, whereas centromeric signals approximately doubled in intensity, preparing for chromosome segregation at metaphase I.

Accordingly, a fluorescence immunolocalization assay of CENH3 signals was also performed in rice seedling root tip cells to investigate differences in centromere dynamics between mitosis and meiosis (Fig. 1b). At mitotic prophase, 24 chromosomes became clearly visible; meanwhile, CENH3 foci remained spherical without significant deformation, and only a slight increase in signal volume was observed compared with interphase. At metaphase, chromosomes aligned tightly on the metaphase plate, with centromere signals paired on both sides. During anaphase, centromere signals gradually separated toward the poles as chromosomes disjoined, forming irregular spots—this process continued until telophase, when chromosomes and centromeres fully segregated at the poles. Unlike the ongoing morphological and volume changes of centromere signals in meiotic prophase, CENH3 signal intensity remained essentially consistent throughout mitosis, with changes in shape.

To characterize the expansion–contraction dynamics of chromatin and centromeres during meiotic prophase I, we quantified the fluorescence intensities of both CENH3 and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), following the methodology adopted in previous studies (Fig. 1c, Additional file 2: Table S1). The nuclear volume increased upon entry into prophase I and the initiation of homologous synapsis, reaching its maximum at pachytene, followed by a gradual reduction associated with chromatin condensation. Centromeres exhibited a parallel but distinct pattern: they expanded concomitantly with chromatin during the early stages, peaked at zygotene, and then contracted from pachytene to diplotene. Interestingly, despite global chromatin condensation, centromeres exhibited a secondary expansion at diakinesis, a phenomenon also observed in hexaploid wheat [47].

A meiotic nucleus and a mitotic nucleus within the same microscopic field were selected to visualize the expanded CENH3 signal during early meiosis. As indicated by the arrow and arrowhead, meiotic nuclei exhibited significantly stronger CENH3 fluorescence than smaller mitotic nuclei (Fig. 1d). To quantitatively illustrate this difference, the fluorescence intensities of total nuclear and CENH3 signals were measured in approximately 35 meiotic nuclei and 35 mitotic nuclei, respectively (Additional file 2: Table S2). The results revealed that the proportion of centromeric signals relative to total nuclear fluorescence averaged 8.67% in meiotic nuclei—more than twice that of the average of 3.21% in mitotic nuclei (Fig. 1e).

Moreover, the aggregation and morphological transformation of CENH3 signals were observed exclusively in nuclei that entered the meiotic process normally. In contrast, meiocyte-like nuclei from initiation-defective mutants, such as retinoblastoma related 1 (rbr1), retained CENH3 signals resembling those in mitotic cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S1).

Kinetochore protein signals enhance synchronously with those of CENH3 in early rice meiosis

Centromeres are essential for chromosome segregation, serving as attachment sites for kinetochores that anchor chromosomes to spindle microtubules. The assembly of the kinetochore complex is a critical prerequisite for chromosome separation during both meiosis and mitosis. Given the close association between centromere and kinetochore assembly, the dynamics of kinetochore protein signals during prophase I may parallel the expansion of centromeric signals. To test this hypothesis and rule out potential specificity issues with the CENH3 antibody, dual fluorescence localization experiments were performed in meiotic nuclei using antibodies against CENH3 and four representative kinetochore proteins. These proteins included Centromere Protein C (CENP-C), a component of the inner kinetochore CCAN complex, and BUB1-Related Kinase 1 (BRK1), Minichromosome Instability 12 (MIS12), and Nuclear Division Cycle 80 (NDC80), which are constituents of the outer kinetochore Knl1-Mis12-Ndc80 (KMN) network [55, 56]. As expected, all four kinetochore proteins colocalized with CENH3 at pachytene (Fig. 2a) and presented enhanced signals consistent with those of CENH3 throughout prophase I (Additional file 1: Fig. S2a, b).

Furthermore, CENP-C, which directly interacts with CENH3 [50, 57], was used to compare centromeric signals between meiotic and mitotic nuclei in a similar assay. Consistent with the CENH3 localization results, meiotic nuclei presented significantly stronger CENP-C signals than mitotic nuclei (Fig. 2b). Quantitative analysis revealed that the proportion of CENP-C signal area relative to the nuclear area averaged 8.74% in meiotic nuclei, more than twice the average of 3.30% observed in mitotic nuclei (Fig. 2c, Additional file 2: Table S3). This finding further supports the enhanced centromeric signal intensity during early meiosis in rice.

Early meiotic CENH3 loading peaks at zygotene in rice

To further investigate centromere dynamics during meiotic prophase I, stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy was employed to resolve individual CENH3 foci, using the same immunolocalization approach for CENH3 and REC8 (Fig. 3a). Meiotic chromosomes and their corresponding centromeres could be clearly identified and tracked from leptotene to diakinesis. During this process, CENH3 signals progressively clustered into fewer but larger foci and reached their maximum volume at zygotene. To quantify these changes, we measured the number of CENH3 foci per nucleus and the area per focus throughout prophase I (Additional file 2: Tables S4, S5). Compared with an average of 21.7 ± 5.7 and 20.6 ± 6.6 CENH3 foci per nucleus at pre-leptotene and leptotene stages, respectively, the number of foci markedly decreased to 14.5 ± 5.5 at zygotene and further to 10.6 ± 3.4 at late zygotene, with many nuclei exhibiting fewer than 12 foci (Fig. 3b). Furthermore, from pre-leptotene to late zygotene, the average area per CENH3 focus more than doubled, concomitant with an approximately 50% reduction in focus number (Fig. 3c). These results demonstrate that CENH3 loading reaches its maximum at the zygotene stage in rice.

Notably, the peak expansion and dot-like meiotic CENH3 signals at zygotene prompted us to reassess the reported interspersed distribution of canonical H3 and CENH3 at centromeres. CENH3 binding is extended and intensified during meiosis

The observed expansion of meiotic CENH3 signals during early prophase I is likely attributable to additional CENH3 deposition in centromere-flanking regions. To verify this hypothesis, ChIP-seq was conducted to profile CENH3 binding dynamics and assess the potential meiotic-specific distribution of CENH3 at rice centromeres. We first collected clusters of PMCs at leptotene to zygotene stages from squashed anthers of ZhongXian3037 (an indica rice variety) using capillary pipettes under a microscope (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). Three independent biological replicates of CENH3 ChIP-seq were performed using PMCs, and the resulting data were compared with ChIP-seq data from somatic cells of 14-day-old indica rice seedlings (Additional file 2: Table S6). Clean reads from each group were mapped to the MH63RS3 genome, and the normalized IP/Input ratios (with 10-kb sliding windows) were plotted along the centromere-proximal regions of the 12 chromosomes (Fig. 4a, b). Broad CENH3 binding peaks identified by MACS2 are plotted as bars underneath the profiles, along with the satellite repeats in rice centromere (CentO) (Additional file 2: Tables S7, S8).

As illustrated in the two plots (Fig. 4a, b), CENH3 binding patterns in PMCs and seedlings were somewhat similar. In both cell types, CENH3 loading boundaries-defined by peak distributions -extended beyond the CentO satellite repeat boundaries, potentially reflecting centromere evolution through the accumulation of centromere-specific repeat sequences. Additionally, alternating CENH3 binding peaks among centromeric regions of chromosomes 3, 4, 8, and 10 were consistently observed in both PMCs and seedlings, which aligns with previous studies that attributed these gaps to active genes distributed in these regions [30, 58].

Despite these similarities, notable differences were observed in meiotic cells. Compared with those in seedlings, CENH3-binding regions in PMCs expanded across a larger portion of the chromosomes, as reflected by the peak boundaries. Moreover, increased IP/Input ratio suggests a greater overall binding intensity of meiotic CENH3. In several centromeric subregions, such as those in centromeres 3 and 4 (indicated by dashed boxes), CENH3 binding levels were markedly higher in PMCs than in the corresponding regions of seedlings. Collectively, these results demonstrate that CENH3 loading is both extended and intensified in its binding regions during meiosis, which explains the observed expansion of CENH3 fluorescence signals at zygotene.

Additional CENH3 primarily loads onto Ty3-Gypsy class retrotransposons

To better compare differential loading of CENH3 between the two cell types and identify specific regions where additional CENH3 is deposited, a Venn diagram was generated to compare the lengths of shared and cell type-specific CENH3 peaks between PMCs and seedlings (Fig. 4c). In seedlings, the shared CENH3 peaks accounted for nearly the entire total peak length, whereas an additional 3,104 kb of unique peaks were identified in PMCs, comprising 31.0% of the total peak length. Peak annotation of different DNA components was conducted to determine the distribution of the extended CENH3-binding regions (Additional file 2: Table S9). As shown in the bar plot, Ty3-Gypsy class long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons constituted the majority of CENH3-binding peaks in both PMCs and seedlings, followed by CentO satellites (Fig. 4d (i)). Notably, whereas Gypsy retrotransposons constituted 54.5% of the CENH3-binding peaks in shared regions, their proportion of PMC-specific CENH3 peaks further increased to 71.5%, highlighting their preferential enrichment in these regions in rice (Fig. 4d (ii)). Annotation of CENH3 peaks bound to gene-body regions revealed that most of the peaks were located in intergenic regions, both in PMCs and seedlings (Fig. 4e).

Previous studies have suggested that total centromere size is largely determined by genome size in eukaryotes [59, 60]. However, the reduced proportion of CentO satellites in PMC-specific CENH3 peaks prompts a reassessment of the relationship between meiosis-specific CENH3 distribution and the underlying DNA sequences. CENH3 binding regions in PMCs were then classified into three classes based on their changes relative to those in seedlings, assisted by the ratio of PMC IP to seedling IP: (i) extended regions, located in centromeric flanking-regions with expanded CENH3 binding boundaries (ratio > 3, Additional file 1: Fig. S5a, blue boxes); (ii) increased regions, where CENH3 was already present but exhibited significantly higher binding levels in PMCs than in seedlings (ratio > 3, Additional file 1: Fig. S5b, yellow boxes); and (iii) unchanged regions, where CENH3 loading showed no significant differences between PMCs and seedlings (Additional file 1: Fig. S5, green boxes; Additional file 2: Table S10). These three classes were then visualized in the proximal regions of chromosome 3 as an example, with control regions devoid of CENH3 loading in either PMCs or seedlings on chromosome arms (Fig. 5a).

The lengths of the three classes of CENH3-related regions (Fig. 5b, green, yellow, and blue blocks) were subsequently compared with the lengths of CentO satellites (Fig. 5b, orange blocks) across the 12 individual chromosomes (Fig. 5b). The observed variations in the lengths of extended, increased, and unchanged CENH3 binding regions across different chromosomes, as well as the discrepancy between CENH3 expansion and CentO satellite repeat lengths, suggested that the extension and intensification of CENH3 deposition in PMCs were independent of both pre-existing CENH3 binding patterns and centromeric satellite repeat distributions in rice. Additional annotation of the DNA elements in the extended regions revealed that Ty3-Gypsy class retrotransposons were the predominant component (71.39%) of early meiotic CENH3 deposition, consistent with previous peak annotations (Additional file 1: Fig. S6a). In summary, our findings indicate that additional CENH3 is deposited primarily onto Ty3-Gypsy class retrotransposons during meiosis in rice.

Gene expression is more restrained in meiotic-specific CENH3 loading regions

To assess the influence of additional CENH3 deposition on transcription, gene expression data were plotted beneath the ChIP-seq profiles and peaks (Fig. 5a). As expected, transcription levels were lower in centromeric regions in both PMCs and seedlings (Additional file 2: Tables S11, S12). While the sparse distribution of active genes in centromeres partly accounts for the reduced transcription, additional CENH3 loading may further contribute to this reduction. To quantitatively analyze their correlation, putative genes located within CENH3‐loading regions were classified into three groups based on the proportion of their gene bodies overlapping with CENH3 peaks (Fig. 5c, Additional file 1: Fig. S6b, Additional file 2: Tables S13, S14). The average expression levels of these groups decreased with increasing CENH3 occupancy, reaching the lowest when the gene bodies were completely occupied by CENH3, suggesting a potential repressive role of CENH3 nucleosomes.

Furthermore, gene expression levels between PMCs and seedlings for both non-TE and TE genes across the three CENH3 peak-related regions and chromosome arm regions were compared (Fig. 5d, Additional file 2: Table S15). Overall, all three CENH3 peak-related regions exhibited consistently lower expression in both PMCs and seedlings than CENH3-free chromosome arms, though differences remained among the three regions. Although the gene expression levels in seedlings and PMCs were higher in the extended regions than in the unchanged regions, no significant differences in gene expression were observed between these two tissue types within each of the regions. This result is likely attributed to the restrictive effect imposed by the surrounding CENH3-enriched environment. However, in regions with increased CENH3 binding—areas that better capture meiosis-induced effects—the magnitude of gene expression upregulation was less prominent in PMCs than in seedlings. This difference points to the inhibitory impact of increased CENH3 loading in PMCs. TE genes presented lower expression levels in the increased and extended regions than in the unchanged regions in both sample types, with a more marked reduction in PMCs. Both non-TE and TE genes located on chromosome arms, where CENH3 binding was undetectable, presented relatively high expression levels.

Functional characterization of CENH3-associated genes in meiotic-specific CENH3 loading regions

To further elucidate the functional relevance of genes affected by CENH3 loading, we first identified 16 centromeric genes in PMCs that concurrently exhibited increased CENH3 occupancy and reduced transcriptional output relative to seedling cells via intersection analysis (Additional file 1: Fig. S7a), providing a refined set for subsequent functional probing. Two representatives annotated with floral development-related functions were selected for IGV visualization, including OsCO3, a flowering-time regulatory gene, and CYP75B4, a cytochrome P450 gene encoding a zinc-finger transcription factor, which revealed enhanced CENH3 enrichment but diminished mRNA accumulation in PMCs (Additional file 1: Fig. S7b)—direct gene-specific evidence of this inverse correlation in reproductive development-implicated loci.

We then extended transcriptional profiling to 11 out of the 16 genes (with available transcriptomic data) across representative vegetative (root, stem, leaf, seedling) and reproductive (PMCs, anther) organs (Additional file 2: Table S16), which consistently displayed higher transcriptional activity in vegetative tissues compared to reproductive organs—congruent with the floral development annotations of our initial candidates and suggesting tissue-specific regulatory modulation (Additional file 1: Fig. S7c). To delineate core biological functions, two complementary Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were performed: one utilizing downregulated genes alone as a control cohort, and another integrating the 11 target genes with their top transcriptionally correlated partners to enhance statistical power (Additional file 2: Table S17). Concordant results highlighted four key biological processes: floral organ and reproductive development, zinc-finger/bHLH transcription factor-mediated regulation, glycolipid metabolism, and phytohormone signal transduction (Additional file 1: Fig. S7d–e), establishing a functional connection between the CENH3-associated gene set and critical reproductive developmental events.

Small RNA transcription is more restrained in meiotic-specific CENH3 loading regions

Beyond mRNA transcription, small RNA transcription in PMCs was also evaluated to further assess the impact of CENH3 loading. The normalized read counts mapped to the three CENH3 binding regions exhibited a distribution pattern similar to that of non-TE genes, for both the 21 nt and 24 nt small RNAs, the two major size categories in rice male gametes (Additional file 1: Fig. S8a), as well as for total 18–30 nt small RNAs (Fig. 5e; Additional file 1: Fig. S8b, c) in seedling and PMCs, while 21 nt small RNAs were consistently absent in CENH3 binding regions consistently in PMCs. The representative centromere 3 and centromere 8 were selected to visualize the local transcription profiles of small RNAs because of the transcription activity in these regions reported previously (Additional file 1: Fig. S9a). The profiles revealed a marked reduction in small RNA transcription within CENH3-bound regions, especially in CentO satellite repeats, which are known to be enriched with CENH3 binding.

To further explore the relationship between CENH3 binding and small RNA transcription, a sliding-window analysis (3-kb windows) was conducted across CentO satellite repeats (primarily unchanged regions, indicated as orange blocks in Fig. 5b), CRRs (CENH3 peak-related regions lacking CentO satellites), and chromosome arms (Additional file 1: Fig. S9b). The results consistently revealed lower small RNA levels in CENH3-bound regions than in chromosome arms, suggesting a possible negative association between CENH3 and the transcription of small RNAs.

Additionally, although the overall sRNA expression in centromeric regions is consistently lower compared to that in chromosome arms, centromeric small RNAs presented distinct expression patterns between PMCs and seedlings: 21 nt small RNAs were consistently more frequently lacking in CentO satellites and CRR regions, whereas 24 nt small RNAs were relatively more abundant in PMCs, particularly in CRRs (Additional file 1: Fig. S8d). The enrichment of 24-nt small RNAs in PMCs suggests a potential association between their accumulation and meiotic progression.

CHG methylation is more reduced in extended CENH3 binding regions

Centromeres are defined by epigenetic features, with DNA methylation serving as a key regulatory component. Previous studies have reported varying degrees of DNA methylation depletion at centromeres across different species [31, 32, 46]. To investigate potential DNA methylation changes in PMCs, we performed whole‐genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) for two independent biological replicates for both PMCs and seedlings (Additional file 1: Fig. S10; Additional file 2: Table S6). To quantify methylation changes in CENH3-loading regions across all chromosomes, we compared DNA methylation levels in each individual (C) context (CG, CHG and CHH) between unchanged and extended CENH3-binding regions (Fig. 6a). Compared with unchanged regions, all three types of DNA methylation were significantly elevated in extended regions in seedlings. However, in PMCs, the differences in CG and CHG methylation levels among these regions were less pronounced or statistically insignificant, suggesting that the additional CENH3 binding may interfere with the establishment or maintenance of DNA methylation in these regions.

To further explore the relationship between DNA methylation and CENH3 deposition, scatterplots were generated to illustrate the distribution of each DNA methylation type alongside the corresponding CENH3 enrichment ratios in 10-kb sliding windows. Spearman's correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the associations between DNA methylation and CENH3 loading (Fig. 6b; Additional file 1: Fig. S11a). Although the overall correlations were weak, CHG methylation showed a less positive correlation with CENH3 enrichment in the unchanged CENH3 binding regions (ρ = −0.069) than in the increased regions (ρ = 0.187). This result suggests that the positive relationship between CHG methylation and CENH3 binding is weaker in the CENH3 highly enriched regions and that the association between these two epigenetic features is more complex.

To provide a broader perspective on the epigenetic landscape surrounding centromeres in PMCs, we selected four representative chromosomes and their centromeric regions for detailed analysis: chromosome 1, with a large centromere and long CentO satellites; chromosome 3, featuring both extended and increased CENH3-binding regions; chromosome 8, characterized by few CentO satellites, discontinuous CENH3 loading with active genes spreading in the intervals and no detectable 5mC methylation, as reported previously; and chromosome 11, which contains two separate CENH3-binding regions (Fig. 6c, d). Given that centromeres are recombination-inhibited regions and that previous studies have linked the meiotic crossover landscape to centromeric DNA methylation [47], historical recombination data calculated from 75 indica group varieties (Additional file 2: Table S18) was incorporated to investigate their relationships. Recombination events were generally more enriched on chromosome arms, with inconsistent distributions in middle arm regions and telomeric regions, while being absent in centromeric regions (Additional file 1: Fig. S12). At the chromosome-wide level, DNA methylation levels, especially in the CG and CHG contexts, were relatively low on chromosome arms and increased toward centromeric regions. This pattern correlated positively with GC content and transposon distribution, while displaying an inverse correlation with historical recombination rates and gene density (Fig. 6c). In centromere-proximal regions, CHG context methylation decreased with intensified CENH3 loading, particularly in areas overlapping with CentO satellite repeats (Fig. 6d).

CENH3-binding regions display lower H3K9me2 enrichment in PMCs

Histone modification is a key component of the centromeric epigenetic landscape, with CENH3 serving as its most prominent marker. Previous studies have focused on various euchromatic and heterochromatic histone marks in centromeric regions, with histone H3 Lys 9 (H3K9) dimethylation receiving particular attention for its pericentromeric accumulation and depletion at the centromere [32, 46, 61]. To assess the impact of CENH3 loading on histone modifications in rice meiotic centromeres, we incorporated ChIP-seq data for four modifications (H3K9me2, H4K12ac, H3K4me3, and H3K27me3) from both our previous research and newly generated studies (Additional file 2: Table S6, [62]). Based on their roles in the regulation of gene expression (Additional file 1: Fig. S13a, b), H3K9me2 was selected as a heterochromatin marker, and H3K4me3 was selected as an euchromatin marker. The enrichment profiles of these two markers were plotted for four representative centromere-proximal regions (Fig. 6d). Despite the presence of active genes in centromeres (e.g., centromere 3), H3K4me3 displayed low enrichment levels, whereas H3K9me2 showed significant variations with lower enrichment levels in CENH3-bound regions and higher enrichment levels in CENH3-free areas (Fig. 6d, Additional file 1: Fig. S14).

A comparison of H3K9me2 enrichment levels between CENH3-bound and flanking regions in both PMCs and seedlings revealed no significant differences in seedlings, but a remarkable decline in PMCs (Fig. 6e). Specifically, in PMCs, H3K9me2 enrichment in unchanged regions was significantly lower than that in flanking regions, whereas increased and extended CENH3-binding regions showed a gradual decrease (Fig. 6f). Correlation analysis further confirmed an inverse relationship between H3K9me2 modification and CENH3 binding, with a significant negative correlation in the unchanged regions (Fig. 6g; Additional file 1: Fig. S11b). Notably, the H3K9me2 modification pattern paralleled that of CHG methylation within CENH3-binding regions, which is consistent with its reported role in maintaining CHG methylation (Additional file 1: Fig. S15a-c [32, 63];).

To visually confirm the distribution of these two histone markers in centromeric regions of meiotic cells, we performed fluorescence immunolocalization using antibodies specific to H3K9me2, H3K4me3, and CENH3 (Fig. 6h). Both H3K9me2 and H3K4me3 signals were clearly detected in pachytene-stage meiocytes but exhibited distinct localization in centromere-proximal regions: H3K4me3 was absent from both centromeric and pericentromeric regions, whereas H3K9me2 was absent only from centromeres but strongly enriched in pericentromeric regions. These results suggest that CENH3, a centromere-specific histone, may suppress both euchromatic and heterochromatic histone modifications within its binding domains, leaving transcription-permissive marks only in the intervening regions.

Sequence and epigenetic features of PMC-specific hypomethylated regions linked to CENH3 loading

To explore the sequence features of methylation-altered regions linked to CENH3 loading, we refined our analytical strategy—given that conventional tools (DMRfinder and DMRseeker) identified only a limited number of differentially methylated regions (DMRs). We instead used a targeted window-based method: we divided the genome into non-overlapping 200-bp windows, calculated CG/CHG methylation level differences between PMCs and seedling cells, and defined hypo-Differentially Methylated Windows (hypo-DMWs) within extended or increased CENH3 loading regions.

We next characterized the genomic distribution of these hypo-DMWs by annotating the genetic localization and component traits (Additional file 1: Fig. S16a, b, Additional file 2: Table S19). Notably, CHG-hypo regions were less frequently located within gene bodies and more predominantly distributed in intergenic regions—hinting at distinct regulatory roles for CG and CHG methylation in meiotic-specific CENH3 loading.

Building on these localization patterns, we performed motif enrichment analysis for both hypo-DMW types using HOMER. The top enriched motifs (Additional file 1: Fig. S16c) included those associated with the bHLH/bZIP and zinc-finger protein families; this aligns closely with the transcription factor-related functional categories of CENH3-associated genes we identified earlier, implying potential molecular crosstalk between methylation changes and transcriptional regulation.

To connect these sequence and distribution features to epigenetic regulation, we compared the enrichment levels of three key epigenetic marks—CENH3, H3K9me2, and H3K4me3—between hypo-DMWs and random regions (sampled from CENH3-extended/increased regions) using ChIP-seq data. As shown in the box plots (Additional file 1: Fig. S16d-f), CHG-hypo DMWs exhibited significantly higher CENH3 enrichment alongside slightly elevated H3K9me2 levels, which aligns with our prior observation of a coordinated correlation between CENH3 and H3K9me2 in PMCs. Additionally, CHG-hypo DMWs showed reduced H3K4me3 enrichment; this likely stems from the combined effects of fewer promoter components in these regions and increased CENH3 loading.

A proposed model for meiotic and mitotic centromere assembly

On the basis of previous findings, we propose a working model for meiotic centromere and kinetochore assembly that highlights key differences from their mitotic counterparts (Fig. 7). Centromere, together with the kinetochore, constitutes a multilayered structure that ensures the accurate segregation of homologous chromosomes during meiosis and sister chromatids during mitosis (Fig. 7a). In this study, we demonstrated that CENH3 loading is both extended and intensified across its binding regions during rice meiosis, resulting in an enhanced signal during prophase I compared with that during mitosis, in synchrony with four representative kinetochore proteins (Fig. 7b). In contrast to somatic cells, where CENH3 predominantly localizes to CentO satellite repeats and the Gypsy LTRs, the major constituents of CRRs, the additional meiotic CENH3 is primarily deposited onto Gypsy-class LTRs (as shown in Fig. 4d). This meiosis-specific CENH3 enrichment is accompanied by transcriptional repression of both mRNAs and small RNAs in the extended binding regions. Furthermore, the surrounding epigenetic landscape, including DNA methylation and histone modifications such as H3K4me3 and H3K9me2, undergoes coordinated remodeling.

Discussion

Previous studies have demonstrated that meiotic CENH3 is predominantly loaded during early prophase I, accompanied by an expansion of centromeric signals in plants [47, 48, 52]. In this study, we provide evidence that in rice meiocytes, CENH3 extends its binding regions and intensifies its enrichment in specific genomic regions compared with that in somatic cells. The additional meiotic loading of CENH3 results in a marked expansion of centromeric signals at zygotene accompanied by clustering and deformation of CENH3 signals exclusively in cells initiating meiosis, which differs from that in wheat—where centromere clustering occurs primarily at leptotene and resolves with the initiation of homologous synapsis at zygotene [47]— and from that in Drosophila, where CID identity intensifies continuously until diplotene [49].

The enhanced meiotic deposition of CENH3 may facilitate the assembly of larger and more robust centromeres, thereby promoting the synchronous recruitment of kinetochore proteins. This process likely improves microtubule attachment, enhancing the fidelity of chromosome segregation [64]. Our experimental results align with this mechanism: dual immunolocalization of α-tubulin and CENH3 in root tip cells and PMCs revealed that tubulin in PMCs exhibits a tighter and clustered state, providing direct cytological evidence for the aforementioned regulatory pathway (Additional file 1: Fig. S17). Moreover, larger centromeres may confer a selective advantage, particularly during female gametogenesis, where only one oocyte survives [65, 66]. Here, the increased enrichment of CENH3 and other centromeric proteins, including CENP-C, BRK1, MIS12, and NDC80—key players in kinetochore assembly and microtubule attachment during both mitosis and meiosis—along with the increased signal intensity observed, provide evidence that increased centromeric protein loading facilitates accurate chromosome segregation during meiosis.

The additional meiotic loading of CENH3 is primarily observed at Ty3-Gypsy class LTRs, the major components of rice CRRs, regardless of their original binding regions in mitosis or the abundance of CentO satellite repeats. These findings support the hypothesis that young centromeric retrotransposon repeats have evolved to contribute to the accumulation of satellite arrays over generations [10]. Although the presence of neocentromeres and the absence of centromeric satellites in various species suggest that centromere function is not strictly sequence-dependent, the underlying DNA structure remains critical for centromere activity [67, 68]. Various non-B-form DNA structures have been shown to influence gene expression, DNA replication, and genome organization [64, 69–71]. In rice, our predictions using nBMST [72] indicated a subtle positive correlation between the density of direct repeats (DR) and A-phased repeats (APR) motifs and the enrichment of CENH3 loading, whereas mirror repeats (MR) and short tandem repeats (STR) showed more negative correlations and other motifs (inverted repeats (IR), Z-DNA, and G-quadruplex (GQ)) did not display a consistent distribution (Additional file 1: Fig. S18). These results imply that non-B-form DNA structures alone do not necessarily recruit CENH3 and that the dense distribution of TE repeats in pericentromeric regions may also influence its deposition.

As a histone H3 variant, CENH3 epigenetically defines centromere identity. The centromere-specific epigenetic landscape, including heterochromatic and euchromatic histone modifications, DNA methylation, and small RNA transcription, further distinguishes centromeres from other genomic regions. For example, centromeric RNA transcription, which may trigger downstream regulation via the RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM) or RNA interference (RNAi) pathway, has been implicated in maintaining chromosomal stability and ensuring proper kinetochore function [37, 61, 65, 73, 74]. In addition, H3K9me2 modifications often cooperate with non-CG methylation to establish a repressive chromatin environment [32]. Together, these epigenetic factors modulate the balance between repressive and accessible chromatin states.

Given that alterations in small RNA levels and H3K9me2 enrichment can significantly affect chromatin compaction, regions with reduced repressive marks adopt a more open conformation. This relaxed chromatin state may facilitate the preferential loading of additional CENH3 during meiosis, thereby enhancing the recruitment of additional centromeric proteins and supporting efficient meiotic centromere and kinetochore assembly.

This model is further supported by observations on the epigenetic mutants affecting centromeric DNA methylation. For example, rice DECREASE IN DNA METHYLATION 1a/1b (ddm1a/1b) and CHROMOMETHYLASE 3a (cmt3a) mutants exhibit dwarfism, short roots, and sterility [75–78]. Notably, the ddm1a/ddm1b double mutant shows reduced CG and CHG methylation in centromeric regions, but paradoxically enhances small RNA transcription in these areas (Additional file 1: Fig. S19; [74, 79]). ChIP-qPCR analysis of ddm1a/1b panicles further revealed a marked reduction in both CENH3 deposition and H3K9me2 enrichment in these regions, mirroring the epigenetic alterations observed in Arabidopsis, but associated with more severe reproductive defects in rice. Such disruptions in the centromeric epigenetic landscape also impact meiotic recombination, which is typically suppressed by high CENH3 enrichment, thereby contributing to chromosome segregation errors [78, 80].

In summary, these findings highlight the critical role of a stable epigenetic environment for both mitotic and meiotic CENH3 loading, as aberrant modifications can compromise both vegetative and reproductive development. The meiosis-specific deposition of CENH3 suggests a sophisticated interplay among histone architecture, centromeric RNA transcription, and epigenetic remodeling, shaping a centromeric epigenetic landscape optimized for meiosis and the corresponding cytogenetic phenotype, thereby ensuring proper meiotic progression. Future studies on the mechanisms underlying this interplay are expected to provide deeper insights into centromere and kinetochore assembly during meiosis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study reveals a dynamic, meiosis-specific pattern of CENH3 deposition in rice, marked by a pronounced enrichment during prophase I compared with mitosis. This additional loading, predominantly targeting Gypsy-class retrotransposons, is coupled with transcriptional downregulation, reduced small RNA abundance, and extensive epigenetic reprogramming. These findings illuminate the intricate interplay among centromeric chromatin dynamics, gene expression, and epigenetic modifications, offering new insights into their coordinated roles in safeguarding proper meiotic progression.

Methods

Plant material

Wild type and rbr1 mutant plants of the ZhongXian3037 cultivar (Oryza sativa ssp. indica) were used in this study. Germinated seeds were grown in the paddy fields of Changping District, Beijing, China (116.42°, 40.10°).

Collection of rice male meiocytes at the early prophase I stage and seedlings

The germ cells at early meiotic prophase I were manually collected using the capillary collection method as previously described [62]. Briefly, anthers containing meiocytes at early prophase I were collected and dissected from approximately 3–4 mm long spikelets by dissecting needles. Worm-like meiotic cell clusters were gently squeezed from the anther wall and released into one drop of 1 × phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Pure meiocytes were manually collected into 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes using capillary glass pipettes under a microscope and stored at –80 °C for later use. Aboveground parts of two-week-old rice plants were collected as seedlings and stored at –80 °C for later use. Additional steps were performed for ChIP-seq as follows. Approximately 10,000 cells or 1 g of freeze-ground seedling powder was cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde (v/v) in 1 × PBS at 4 °C for 10 min. Excess formaldehyde was quenched by adding glycine to a final concentration of 0.125 M glycine for an additional 5 min. The crosslinked cells or powder were washed twice with 1 × PBS and stored at –80 °C for later use.

Antibodies

To generate an antibody against CENP-C, a multiple antigenic peptide (MAP) consisting of the C-terminal 20 amino acids (KVKSFV-PEQYSDLVAKSARY) of the rice CENP-C coupled with keyhole limpet hemocyanin (KLH) was prepared. Two guinea pigs were inoculated with the CENP-C MAP to generate the antibody. Antibodies against rice CENH3, REC8, BRK1, MIS12 and NDC80 were generated previously [56, 81, 82]. Anti-H3 (Cat# E-AB-22003, Ela Science), anti-H4K12ac (Cat# ab320815, Abcam) anti-H3K4me3 (Cat# ab8580, Abcam) and anti-H3K9me2 (Cat# ab1220, Abcam; Cat# 39,420, Active motif) were purchased from commercial products.

Immunofluorescence assay

Fresh young panicles were fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. Different meiotic stages of anthers were squashed in a drop of 1 × PBS solution on glass slides. After freezing in liquid nitrogen and ethanol dehydration, the slides were incubated with different combinations of diluted antibodies (1:100) in a humid chamber at 37 °C for 2 h. Thereafter, the slides were washed with 1 × PBS three times and further incubated for 1 h with the appropriate fluorochrome-coupled secondary antibodies, including fluorescein-isothiocyanate-conjugated goat anti-mouse antibodies (Southern Biotech, Birmingham, AL, USA), rhodamine-conjugated goat antirabbit antibodies (Southern Biotech), and AMCA-conjugated goat anti-guinea pig antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA).The slides were washed with 1 × PBS three times and subsequently stained with DAPI. The immunofluorescence signals were analyzed and captured using a Carl Zeiss A2 microscope imaging system.

STED microscopy and image analysis

Immunofluorescence assays were conducted via the procedures described above. STED images were acquired using an Abberior STEDYCON (Abberior Instruments GmbH, Göttingen, Germany) fluorescence microscope built on an upright microscope BX53 (Olympus UPlanXAPO 100 ×, NA1.45, Tokyo, Japan). The microscope is equipped with pulsed STED lasers with 775 nm and 640 nm excitation lasers. The appropriate fluorochrome-coupled secondary antibodies used in this study, included the following: the abberior STAR ORANGE goat anti-mouse IgG (STORANGE-1001–500 UG) and the abberior STAR RED goat antirabbit lgG (STRED-1002–500 UG). Imaging and image processing were performed with ImageJ software.

ChIP-seq and data analysis

ChIP for PMCs was performed as described previously [62]. In brief, the PMC pellet was trimmed by MNase (Cat# M0247S, New England Biolabs) first for 10 min at 37 °C, and then sonicated for 10 cycles (20 s on, 40 s off) to obtain the chromatin. The supernatant collected after centrifuged twice at 12,000 g and 4 °C for 5 min each, was incubated with anti-CENH3, anti-H3K9me2 or anti-H4K12ac with constant rotation at 4 °C overnight. A total of 10,000 cross-linked PMC pellets were used for each replicate. Each antibody was biologically replicated. ChIP for seedlings was performed as described previously [83]. Biologically replicated ChIP-seq and input libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, E7645S). All prepared libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. The raw reads of all the sequencing data, together with public data downloaded from NCBI’s Sequence Read Archive (SRA), were trimmed using Fastp [84]. Reads with a quality value of Q < 20 and a read length < 50 bp were discarded. The clean reads were mapped to the rice MH63RS3 reference genome with the Bowtie2 aligner [85]. Sambamba-0.8.0 [86] was used to remove any PCR duplicates. Samtools [87] was used to select reads with mapping quality (MapQ) greater than 10 for further analyses. Deeptools [88] was used to convert the bam files to the bigwig files with the bam files normalized by RPKM (Reads Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads) values and profile the data in certain regions. Read distributions across the whole genome were visualized by using IGV [89]. MACS2 software [90] was used for peak calling with the following parameter: -f BAM -g 3.9e8 -nomodel -q 0.01 –broad –broad-cutoff 0.1. Only peaks present in both replicates were retained for downstream analyses.

RNA extraction and sequencing

TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for the extraction of total RNA following the protocol provided. The same total RNA isolated from WT seedlings was used for both mRNA and sRNA. Library construction and sequencing were performed by Beijing Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

mRNA-seq data analysis

The mRNA-seq library datasets generated in this study or downloaded from NCBI’s Sequence Read Archive (SRA) were subjected to the same mRNA analysis pipeline. The raw mRNA-seq data were cleaned by removing adaptor contamination and low-quality reads via Fastp software. Clean reads were then aligned to the rice MH63RS3 reference genome via HISAT2 [91]. StringTie [92] was used to calculate the expression level (TPM, transcripts per million) of each gene. All genes were divided into three subgroups according to their lengths overlapping with CENH3 peaks (0%−50%, 50%−100%, 100%). Deeptools [88] was used to convert the bam files to bigwig files with the bam files normalized by RPKM.

Gene ontology annotation of co-expressed genes of downregulated genes

Expression profile data were retrieved from the Rice Genome Annotation Project (RGAP) website (https://rice.uga.edu/). A custom R script was used to calculate the Spearman correlation coefficients of expression levels between the target genes and other genes. The top 10 genes with the highest absolute correlation coefficients were selected for subsequent GO enrichment analysis, which was performed using OmicShare Tools.

sRNA-seq data analysis

The sRNA-seq library datasets generated in this study or downloaded from NCBI’s Sequence Read Archive (SRA) were subjected to a similar small RNA analysis pipeline. The raw data were first cleaned by removing adapters via Trim Galore (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/), and reads with lengths of 18–30 nt were retained. The clean reads were uniquely mapped to the MH63RS3 reference genome using Bowtie [93], allowing no mismatches. Two types of sRNA abundance were calculated by 21- or 24-nucleotide read numbers falling into 1- or 10-kb windows throughout the whole genome.

Bisulfite-seq for PMCs and seedlings and data analysis

Micro DNA-WGBS was performed for PMC with 3,000 cells as a repeat each by Shenzhen E-GENE Tech Co., Ltd. WGBS was performed for seedlings by Shenzhen BGI Genomics Co., Ltd. Fastp software was used to remove connectors and filter low-quality reads from the acquired raw BS-seq data. Clean reads were mapped to the MH63RS3 rice genome. Bismark [94] was used to match, deduplicate and extract methylation sites with default settings. To obtain more loci for analysis, we combined two biological replicates. Duplicates were removed, uniquely mapped reads were retained, and only cytosines covered by at least three reads were included for subsequent analysis.

Hypo-methylated windows and motifs identification in PMCs

Three types of methylation data from PMCs and seedlings were divided into 200-bp non-overlapping windows, and the mean values were calculated. Differentially methylated windows (DMWs) were filtered using thresholds of CG > 0.3 and CHG > 0.2. Subsequently, genomic component annotation was performed for the hypomethylated windows, and HOMER software was used to annotate the sequence motifs of these hypomethylated windows. Among these annotated motifs, the top eight 12-bp motifs with the highest enrichment ratios were plotted as sequence logos.

Calculation of the rice historical recombination rate

The SNP/variants dataset of 3000 Rice Genomes Project data aligned to IRGSP-1.0 was downloaded from SNP-Seek (https://snp-seek.irri.org/_download.zul [95]). The same 75 accessions from the indica rice population with positive SNPs as reported previously [96] were selected to calculate the historical recombination rate. Minimap2 [97] and Transanno (https://github.com/informationsea/transanno) were used to create the mapping chain between the IRGSP-1.0 and MH63RS3 genomes. CrossMap was then applied to convert the genome coordinates of the SNPs between the two assemblies. The historical recombination rate was estimated by treating phased haplotypes as haploid individuals within subsets of 2000 consecutive SNP windows, overlapping by 250 SNPs via the program Interval from the software package LDhat v2.2 [98]. The results are presented as ρ/kb in 10-kb windows.
