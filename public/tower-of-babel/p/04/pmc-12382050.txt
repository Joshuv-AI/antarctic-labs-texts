JC Stand-by-U– development and evaluation of an in-home respite service model using mixed-method quasi-experimental design: study protocol
Authors: Nga-Yan Hui, Linda Dong-Ling Wang, Jeff Yiu, Matthew Guocheng Feng, Peiyi Lu, Vivian W.Q. Lou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn an ageing society, most older adults who require long-term care are still living in their homes and communities. Their families are burdened by caregiving duties, but their unique needs are often overlooked. In response to the increasing needs of respite care, the Jockey Club Stand-by-U (JC Stand-by-U) Caregivers Community Support Project aims to study the family caregivers’ needs and how to best support them in the context of Hong Kong.MethodsThe caregivers and their care recipients are evaluated by a comprehensive assessment before and after the intervention and followed up in 3, 6, 12 months, separately. Caregivers are divided into four needs levels and provided by different types of services, delivered by either trained volunteers or substitute caregivers. A mixed-method quasi-experimental study with a comparison group of non-users of in-home respite will be conducted to evaluate the in-home respite model designed in this project. Questionnaire data will be used to evaluate the effect of the service model quantitatively, while the service records will be used to analyse the quality of service and the mechanism of service effect qualitatively.DiscussionCaregivers are expected to show improvement in their mental wellbeing after the respite. The intervention follows a screening-guided approach which balances the resource supply and the needs of the caregiver, optimising the effectiveness and sustainability of the in-home respite programme. In the long-run, policymakers can ultilise the result from this study when designing long-term care plan in this ageing society.Trial registrationClinicaltrials.gov Identifier NCT06981117, 20 May 2025, retrospectively registered.

Background

As the global population ages, the number of older adults requiring assistance in daily life is increasing every year, and the care and support of older adults have become increasingly important. With the world’s longest life expectancy [1], Hong Kong faces a serious ageing situation with rapidly rising caregiving needs. According to the Census and Statistic Department of Hong Kong in 2021 [2], among all older adults living at home, 16.6% of them required long-term care, and this percentage increased dramatically to over 50% for those aged 80+. Meanwhile, only 18.6% of the interviewed older adults have an intention of moving into a residential care home even if their health condition deteriorated [3], showing that older adults preferred to live at home even when they have high caregiving needs. Moreover, in Hong Kong context, where the long-care care service is publicly funded but faces crowding issue, individual who intended to move into nursing facilities needed to wait for an average of 18 months for a subvented institution and 9 months for a private institution [4]. Most of the caregiving duties are therefore taken up by the family members.

While continuous efforts from the government and the general public are being made to address the needs of older adults, the burden placed on caregivers is still heavy. It was reported that the caregivers spent a median of 60 h per week caring for their family [2], which is much more than the normal working hours of a full-time job. Besides the physical challenges and the financial burden brought by caregiving duties [5], the lack of “me-time” is frequently experienced by caregivers but receives less attention. Some caregivers feel that their time is fully occupied by their care recipients, which one of the caregivers described the experience as “I cannot leave him for a minute” [6]. Caregiving restricted the caregivers from participating in activities they normally would be able to, for instance, working or socialization. The chronic stress from caregiving could leads to emotional exhaustion and eventually affects the physical and mental health of the caregivers [7].

The situation is more severe if the care recipient is homebound. This study targeted caregivers who care for homebound older adults. “Homebound” is defined as “people who cannot leave their home independently without assistance [8–10], for example, person who required someone to push their wheelchair. It also includes people who are physically capable of leaving their homes on their own but in doing so could create dangerous situations, for instance, dementia patients who have the risk of wandering. For caregivers of homebound older adults, the caregiving hours can easily be round-the-clock. Besides the physical effort involved in providing activities of daily living (ADL) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) assistance, the caregiver’s personal time is sacrificed in order to provide full-time caregiving to their care recipient, which often leads to the feeling of isolation, loneliness and even depression in a long-run [5]. Nevertheless, caregivers usually prioritise the needs of care recipients over their own. Often, they neglect their mental wellbeing issues until it reaches a breaking point.

Respite service aiming at relieving the caregiver is, therefore, provided. Respite refers to the temporary relieve from caregiving duties with the aim of improving the overall wellbeing of the caregivers [11]. It is believed that respite helps both the caregivers and care recipients in many positive ways, including reducing hospitalisation of the care recipient and relieving caregivers’ stress [12]. There are various forms of respite, including care support, social activities, support groups, carer skill training, and practical help in daily activities [13]. While the scope of respite service is relatively broad, this paper focuses on services that temporarily take care of the care recipients so the caregivers can have some free time to pursue activities of their choice. In Hong Kong, centre-based adult day-care service is the most commonly adopted type of respite, where the caregivers temporarily send their care recipients to a day-care centre during working hours and take them home afterwards, allowing the caregiver to work or have some personal time at daytime with peace in mind that their care recipient is in safe hands. Nevertheless, the utilisation rate of day-care services in Hong Kong is relatively low, with a record of only 46% of centre-based day respite services was occupied in 2019–2020 (pre-COVID time) and as low as 18% during Covid time [14]. It is suggested that despite the wide availability of centre-based respite care, older adults are unable to access these services as they cannot leave their home easily, leaving their caregivers’ needs in respite service unmet.

Respite service has to be tailored to different populations to fulfil their needs culturally, socially, and demographically [15]. In Hong Kong, almost one-third of primary caregivers are aged 60 or above [2], showing that “the elderly taking care of the elderly” is a prevalent situation. This potentially brings more difficulties in taking the care recipients to the day-care centres, as the journey to the centre could be physically challenging. Besides the troubles of taking the care recipients to and from the day-care centres [16, 17], some caregivers prefer in-home service compared to centre-based service as the care recipients, especially for those with cognitive impairment, could stay in a trusted and familiar environment [18, 19]. Moreover, sending the care recipients to a centre might induce the sense of guilt for some caregivers, as they feel that they are avoiding their responsibility and abandoning their loved ones. In previous literature, a caregiver decided to cancel a two-week respite programme the next day after booking the service, and explained that she felt “Guilt. I didn’t sleep that night, hardly any at all, and in the morning I got up and… cancel[led] [20]”. With all these concerns surrounding using existing centre-based services, in-home respite service is a promising solution to supplement the gaps in service.

Caregivers often put priority on CR’s needs over their own. A caregiver interviewed in a previous study described her experience in receiving a alternative care arrangement by saying “I will still be thinking about what he will be doing and whether it is done properly [21]. ”, while the other caregiver describe her view on respite that “It is not about what the caregiver wants. What is important is that the patient is well and safe [21]”. This shows that even if the caregiver is using the respite service, it is likely that they concentrate on the benefits for the older adults rather than themselves. It is common that some might confuse the aim of respite with other types of home care services for the older adults, for example, meal delivery or community nurse services. The goal of respite is to relieve the caregiving stress of the caregiver, while home care services is to provide continuous support in daily living and health needs to the older adults. Frankly, it is impossible not to provide any kinds of service to the CR in a respite session, that the respite itself essentially functions as a supervision service. When considering the effectiveness of respite, one should emphasise the fulfilment of caregivers’ needs and the consequences on the wellbeing of the caregivers.

However, the effectiveness of respite service is still under debate. One of the reasons might be due to the lack of standardised definition of “respite” [11], therefore the comparison between studies is almost impossible. For instance, some studies only provide information on whether the caregiver had respite in the past year [22], without specifications on the frequency nor the types of respite they had. In this case, some caregivers might be receiving professional services regularly, while others could have received only one day-off with the help from a friend. This lack of specificity hinders our ability to evaluate the dosage and effectiveness of respite for relieving caregivers’ stress. In addition, the activities of the caregivers and care recipients in respite session might contribute to how satisfied they are with the time [21]. Some believe that resting is the best for the caregivers, some argued that the time should be used to catch up with chores to allow the sense of normalcy for the caregivers, and some suggested that the key lies in whether what they did in respite was the same as what they wanted to do [23]. The detailed record of each respite session is therefore needed.

Moreover, respite service is more likely to be provided on an on-demand basis without considering the actual needs of the caregiver. For example, it is possible that some caregivers with relatively lower needs of respite but well-resourced might request frequent services, whereas those with higher needs but less well-resourced could hardly receive any services. This creates a mismatch between the needs of the caregivers and the actual consumption of the service, leading to a possible ceiling effect when evaluating for the intervention’s effectiveness [18]. In addition, previous studies often recruit participants based on care recipient’s status (e.g., caregivers of dementia patients [24] and frail older adults [25]) rather than based on caregivers’ needs [26]. Although it is likely that those caring for someone with higher dependence in daily life would have greater stress, it is not necessarily always the case. For instance, some might see caregiving as a bounding with the care recipients and perceived caregiving as a positive experience [27, 28]. In view of this, the Jockey Club Stand-by-U Caregivers Community Support Project triages caregivers of homebound older adults into four levels of needs, provides different types of respite services accordingly, and evaluates the effectiveness of the service by using both quantitative and qualitative measurements. This project aims to study the caregivers’ needs and evaluate the effectiveness of respite in the context of Hong Kong.

The primary aim of this study is to develop a screening-guided in-home respite care service model and evaluate its effectiveness. This is achieved by the quasi-experimental design, in which we compare respite users with a non-user comparison group and evaluate their well-being through a series of standard measurements.

Methods

Study design

This study adopts a screening-guided intervention approach, that caregivers are divided into four needs levels (i.e., low, mild, moderate and high) using the Carer Need Screening Tool (CNST-11), and provided with different services tailored to their needs. Caregivers (CGs) in high- and low-need levels are referred to professional services (e.g., professional counselling and psychotherapy) or community-based service (e.g., support groups offered by local organisations) outside of this project, respectively. CGs in mild- and moderate-need levels will enter this project and will receive in-home respite service provided by either trained volunteers or the more intensively trained and experienced substitute caregivers. Both the caregivers and their care recipients will be evaluated using a comprehensive assessment before and immediately after the intervention, and followed up in 3, 6, and 12 months after the completion of the intervention. A comparison group of respite non-users will be recruited and assessed at the same interval as the intervention group.

Figure 1 shows the service flow of the intervention group, and Fig. 2 shows the screening and assessment flow of the comparison group. This mixed-method quasi-experimental study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of the respite service using both qualitative and quantitative data.

The study will be conducted in Hong Kong, with the support of five NGO community partners. Each NGO partner is responsible for providing services in their service region. Although the current study does not provide services to all 18 districts in Hong Kong, it covers a vast spectrum of the population from districts with the highest household income (e.g., Central and Western District, Wan Chai) to the lowest (e.g., Kwai Ching, Sham Shui Po) [29], providing a representative sample that reflects different socio-economic statuses in Hong Kong.

Data collection is on-going. The project officially started on 1 April 2024. The first year was considered as pilot data collection to refine the overall project protocol. In the pilot stage, we have collected comments from frontline social workers and caregivers who participated in the program in both assessments and service provision, to refine the service protocol to be both scientifically meaningful and smooth in real world operation.

Participants

There are four groups of participants in this study. It includes two types of service users: the caregivers (CG) and care recipients (CR), and two types of service providers: volunteers and substitute caregivers. The four groups of participants are all recruited by the partnered NGOs in their service regions. In addition, a comparison group of CG and CR dyads will be recruited.

This study targeted caregivers who care for homebound older adults. “Homebound” is defined as “people who cannot leave their homes independently without assistance (8, 9, c.f., 10)”, for example, those with physical disability or bedbound. It also includes people who are physically capable of leaving their homes on their own, but for whom doing so could create dangerous situations, for instance, dementia patients who have the risk of wandering.

In-home respite service is provided by either a volunteer or a substitute caregiver, based on the needs level of the CG. Both volunteers and substitute caregivers are trained by the Hong Kong Association of Gerontology on skills in taking care of older adults. As they are providing services to CGs and CRs of different need levels, they are trained differently. Volunteers will complete a 24-hour course on basic caregiving skills (e.g., lifting, transferring from wheelchair to chair) and the communication with older adults and caregivers. The substitute caregivers will complete a 32-hour course, with the extra hours on the assistance in activity of daily living (ADL) and instrumental ADL (IADL). The details of the training and assessment are reported in another paper (under preparation). Hereafter they are referred to as “trained caregivers (TC).

The number of participants is determined by pwr package in R [30] with k = 2, f = 0.25, α = 0.05, and power = 0.80. To allow for a 10% attrition rate, it is estimated that 71 pairs of CG/CR dyads is required in the study. A non-equivalent comparison group design is adopted with a ratio of 1:2 [31]. Therefore, this study will recruit a minimum of 142 CG/CR pairs as the comparison group.

Assessments for CG

Demographic information

Demographic information will be collected from both CGs and CRs through a survey. This includes age, gender, highest education level, financial status and housing condition.

Health information

Information on their physical health will be collected through survey. For example, whether they are suffering from any chronic diseases or pain, and their mobility indoor and outdoor.

Five-level EuroQoL five-dimensional questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L) [32]

This is a 6-item scale measuring quality of life. Participants are asked to rate their mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression on a 5-level scale: no problems, slight problems, moderate problems, severe problems, and extreme problems. The responses are converted into a single index value representing overall health status using Hong Kong’s health state value [33]. The sixth item (EQ-VAS) is a vertical visual analogue scale on which participants indicate their self-perceived health condition, with 0 representing “the worst health you can imagine” and 100 representing “the best health you can imagine”.

Depression Anxiety Stress Scales—Hong Kong version (DASS-21) [34, 35]

Participants are asked to rate 21 items on a 4-point scale ranging from “never” to “almost always.” It divides participants into five levels each for depression, anxiety, and stress subscales: “normal,” “mild,” “moderate,” “severe,” and “very severe”.

Carer Need Screening Tool (CNST-11) [36]

This is an 11-item scale that measures problems faced by caregivers, rated in a 4-point scale from “very disagree” to “very agree”. CG will be considered as High-need if the CG exhibits at least one of the following problems: [1] aggression [2], family conflicts, and [3] self-harm or harming others. Otherwise, CGs are triaged into Low-, Mild-, and Moderate-need levels according to their scores.

Caregiving duties

Information on what caregiving duties the CG has to perform will be recorded. For instance, their relationship with the CR, the number of CRs they are caring for, the number of hours of caregiving provided per week, and the types of caregiving tasks (assistance with ADL/IADL).

Eight-item Caregiver Self-efficacy Scale (CSES-8) [37]

Participants rate themselves on 8 items in a 10-point scale on how confident they are in handling caregiving in different scenarios, e.g., alone or by seeking resources to support themselves.

The World Health Organisation Quality of Life (WHOQOL-BREF) [38]

The 28-item survey measures the quality of life in five domains: overall quality of life, physical health, psychological, social relationship and environment. Compared to EQ-5D-5L which also measures quality of life, this scale focuses more on the influences from external factors.

Role Captivity using Pearlin’s model [39]

This scale measures the internal strain of involuntarily taking on the caregiver role. It includes three items, all rated using a 4-point Likert scale.

Cantonese short version of the Zarit Burden Interview (CZBI-short) [40]

The Cantonese version of the 12-item Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI) examines the burden of carers using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (very frequently). Although originally developed for dementia patients’ carers, we adopted it to assess the caregiving of older adults in general.

Assessments for CR

CR will be asked to provide the same demographic and health information as the CG. See the above sub-section for a detailed description.

EQ-5D-5L [32]

See the above sub-section for a detailed description.

PHQ-4 [41]

PHQ-4 is a 4-item scale for screening the presence and severity of depression and anxiety. Participants are asked to rate their feelings over the last two weeks from on a 4-point scale from “not at all” to “nearly every day”.

Modified Barthel Index– Chinese version [42]

CR will be asked to provide information on the dependency of 10 activities of daily living (ADL) in 5 levels, from fully dependent on others to fully independent. The weighted final score indicates how dependent the CR is.

Lawton Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Scale– Hong Kong version [43]

CR are asked to rate their dependency of 8 activities of instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) in 3 levels: fully dependent on others, partly dependent, and fully independent.

Triaged intervention

This project uses the Carer Need Screening Tool (CNST-11) [36] to triage caregivers and provide different services. The caregivers will answer 11 items related to their views on caregiving duties across different dimensions, including physical health, mental health, caregiving needs, caregiving capability, and social support. CGs will be considered High-need only if they exhibit at least one of the following problems: (1) aggression (2), family conflicts, and/or (3) self-harm or harming others. Otherwise, CGs will be triaged into Low-, Mild-, and Moderate-need levels according to their scores.

CGs will receive different services based on their level of need. Those in the High-need level group will be referred to professional services including the mental health clinics and psychotherapy. Those in the Low-need level will be referred to community-based respite services (e.g., centre-based respite “JC Carer Space”) and day-care centres. CGs in the Mild- and Moderate-need levels will enter this project’s intervention and receive in-home respite services provided by volunteers and substitute caregivers, respectively.

Respite session

The respite service is free-of-charge for all CGs/CRs. A total of 12 or 36 hours of respite service within a 6-month period is provided to the Mild- and Moderate-need CGs, respectively. They are encouraged to use the service regularly (e.g., 2 h every week) to cultivate the habit of taking breaks from caregiving duties. However, it is also acceptable for CGs to use more service hours per session or week, given that there are remaining service hours and the service provider (i.e., the TCs) is available.

CGs are asked to plan what to do during the service hours when making the reservation for the respite service. Previous studies have shown that CGs feel more relieved if they are able to complete what they planned to do during respite, regardless of the activity [23, 44]. Therefore, we place greater emphasis on the planning of what to do in the free time and the execution of the plans, instead of simply “resting”. Although it is not compulsory, CGs are strongly encouraged to put down their caregiving duties during the respite period, instead of staying home to supervise the TC or remaining involved in caregiving tasks.

Figure 3 describes the flow of each service session. As the respite is aimed at giving the CGs a peace of mind that their loved one is in safe hand but not as any kind of training session for the older adults, the TCs are only required to keep an eye on the CRs to ensure their safety at home. However, if the CR is physically and/or cognitively capable, the TC may lead simple exercises (e.g., stretching, seated exercises) or cognitive training during the service session. The TC can also accompany the CR for a short walk outside the home if there are no foreseeable risks. The TC may also do some hobby-related activities with the CR, for instance, crafting, singing, or playing chess. For the CGs, TC may share their caregiving experience and teach basic caregiving skills if needed. However, TCs will not provide any personal hygiene services (e.g., changing diapers, bathing) or handle food or medication, due to liability and safety concerns.

Flow of service provision

After each service, the TC will submit a service record detailing the activities the CG and CR do during the service. This is to cross-reference to what the CG had planned during the service booking stage to evaluate the effectiveness of the time spent during respite. The CGs are also invited to rate their satisfaction level and indicate whether they are able to improve their time use after each service [44]. The social workers will read the service record to see if there is any incident reported and to follow it up if necessary. Because the social workers will not accompany the TC in each service, such record provided by the TC is beneficial in two ways: (1), social workers can duly follow up any incidents happened in each service, and (2) it provides an observation of the changes of the status in a more frequent manner than only the pre- and post-assessments, as physical and mental health might have a relatively rapid change for older adults. As for research purpose, this provides qualitative information on the respite service from the perspective of the service providers.

Comparison group

Figure 3 shows the flow of screening and assessment for the comparison group. As a randomised controlled trial is not a realistic approach in a community setting, this study implements a quasi-experimental method with a non-equivalent comparison group design. The Stand-by-U project adopts a natural control approach. We recruit CGs/CRs who meet the inclusion criteria but either refuse the service or live outside the service coverage areas. They will be screened and assessed again at 3 months (T1), 6 months (T2), 9 months (T3) and 15 months (T4) after the baseline assessment.

The comparison group will be treated as usual. However, because many caregivers see caregiving as their personal responsibility, they are often unwilling to delegate caregiving duties to others [21]. It is common that caregivers do not request any service for themselves. Instead, they are more likely to use services that the primary goal is to help their care recipients, such as meal delivery or community nursing. This also applies to the intervention group. Therefore, the potential confounding effects from receiving other types of services are minimal. Information on the types of service used during the project period for both intervention and comparison groups will be collected.

The intervention and comparison group will be matched by caregiving burden level using Carer Needs Screening Tool [36] and the baseline levels on outcome measurements (see Methods for details), as well as their care recipients’ ADL and IADL scores.

Ethical consideration

Ethics approval from The University of Hong Kong Human Research Ethics Committee was obtained on 26 July 2024 (Reference number: EA240373). All participants in this study will join the project on a voluntary basis and written informed consent will be collected. For care recipients who cannot provide consent by themselves (e.g., due to cognitive impairment), their legal guardians will be asked to provide consent by proxy instead.

All study participant-related data will be pseudonymised for the investigators to analyse. The NGO community partners will have access to the names and the information of the study participants of their organisation only. All information collected will remain strictly confidential and stored on a password-protected hard drive that only the investigators can access. The data containing personal identifiers and the anonymised data will be kept for 5 years and 10 years, respectively, after the publication of the first paper arising from the research project.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis includes two parts. Quantitatively, a standardised score will be computed for each scale. We will assess the effect of the intervention on the mental wellbeing and quality of life of CGs by comparing pre- and post-intervention scores, as well as longitudinal differences using follow-up assessments (T2, T3, and T4). Also, we will compare the intervention group and the comparison group to evaluate the effectiveness of respite in terms of stress relief and improvement in mental wellbeing. In addition, we will identify the subgroups of caregivers who benefit most from the respite service using linear regression models, with predictive variables including CGs’ demographic information, caregiving duties, and CRs’ health condition changes (i.e., improved, no change, declined). Qualitatively, we will analyse the data obtained from service records to provide insight into the quality control of the service provided and, potentially, how differences in caregivers' perceptions of respite might influence its effectiveness. After the intervention, we expect the CGs to show improvements in self-care and quality of life, and reductions in caregiver role strain, anxiety, depression, and stress, as measured by differences between pre- and post-intervention assessments. Moreover, we anticipate the CGs will cultivate a habit of taking regular rest time after the service period.

Stakeholders involvement

The CRs were not involved in the study design. The TCs provided comments on making the service record more user-friendly, especially for older TCs. The social workers from different NGO partners provided feedback on the design of the service flow to balance both scientific research requirement and real-life scenarios in the front line. Some of the CGs were interviewed and provided comments from the service user angle. Their comments will be used to continuously modify the service flow when data collection continues to create an optimal design of the respite service.

Discussion

This project aims to evaluate the effectiveness of the in-home respite programme. In this ageing world, it is expected that everyone will become a caregiver at some point. It is, therefore, especially important to develop a respite service model that effectively alleviates caregiver stress and is sustainable in the long term. To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the few that investigate the effects of in-home respite programmes specifically for caregivers of homebound older adults, and it is the first to do so in Hong Kong.

An important strength of this study is the screening-guided intervention design. Caregivers are triaged into four levels of need and are supported by different types of services accordingly. Unlike in previous literature where caregivers request respite service by themselves, in our project, only caregivers with the actual needs will receive in-home respite service. Our approach not only ensures that caregivers receive service that they need, but also optimizes resource allocation and avoids ceiling effects. Moreover, the screening assessment allows social workers to recommend future care plans based on concrete evidence, which enhance caregiver supports beyond the current project. Overall, the screening-guided intervention strike a balance between resources utilisation and meeting caregiver’s needs, which is important in the development of an effective and sustainable service model.

In addition, the design allows a thorough evaluation of the programme based on both quantitative and qualitative evidence. For quantitative measurements, all caregivers and care recipients in the intervention and comparison group are assessed by a comprehensive battery of standard measurements on their physical and mental wellbeing before and after the respite programme (or 3 months after the baseline for the comparison group), allowing researchers to compare the effect of the intervention. Qualitatively, TCs who provided service are required to submit a record on their observation in each service session, allowing the researchers to keep track on the changes of the caregivers in a more frequent manner and from the point of view of the service providers. Moreover, they are required to provide information on what kind of activities were done by the caregivers and care recipients in each session, allowing the researcher to further explore how the nature of session is related to the effectiveness of respite [21].

Currently, the respite service relies heavily on volunteerism. In the long run, we anticipate that substitute caregiving could become an occupation providing standardised, evidence-based and effective respite service. There will be two potential benefits to society: (1) CG and CR will receive a more professional service, and (2) ex-caregivers (i.e., whose CR no longer requires caregiving) with abundant caregiving experience and skills can contribute their talent and develop a career path with it.

Despite the potentials of this project, there are several limitations. First, due to the limitation of resources, each family can receive only a set number of service hours (12/36 h). This might not be sufficient for some CGs, especially those with higher needs. The effectiveness of respite might therefore be hindered. Second, the study does not employ random sampling method due to feasibility of the service provision, which might not represent a full picture of the population of Hong Kong caregivers who are caring for homebound older adults. Thirdly, although highly recommended, it is not compulsory to join any long-term service as support after our intervention. We expect a relapse in the follow-up assessments if the CGs do not receive other services after this intervention.

Although there are some limitations, we believe that the in-home respite service model in this study will be able to supplement the currently available centre-based respite services in supporting caregivers with different needs.
