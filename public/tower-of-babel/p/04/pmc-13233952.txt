Cardiovascular risk assessment characterized by proteomics in cancer survivors
Authors: Peng Wang, Yu Peng, Fubin Liu, Chao Sheng, Kexin Chen, Fangfang Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe classic cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk scores perform poorly in predicting CVD in cancer survivors. This study aimed to identify proteins associated with major CVDs risk and explore their roles in risk prediction for major CVDs in cancer survivors.MethodsWe included 4225 cancer survivors from the UK Biobank with available plasma proteomic data and no major CVDs at recruitment. Associations between proteins and risks of major CVDs (heart failure, atrial fibrillation, myocardial infarction, angina, peripheral vascular disease, and stroke) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models, followed by enrichment analysis. Candidate protein biomarkers were further identified through random forest model, and classification performance was evaluated using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), net reclassification index (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement index (IDI).ResultsThis study identified 182 proteins, the majority of which were positively associated with major CVDs risk in cancer survivors (HRs:1.11-1.93), especially NTproBNP, NPC2, TNFRSF12A, LMNB2, and EDA2R, mainly involved in biological processes of immune, inflammatory, and angiogenesis. Further, a panel of 23 proteins derived from a random forest model demonstrated moderate predictive performance for major, 5-year, and 10-year CVDs in the test set (AUCs: 0.646–0.665), outperforming several classic CVD risk scores. Incorporating the protein panel into CVD risk scores significantly improved discrimination (AUCs: 0.647–0.705) and 5-year risk reclassification (NRI: 0.245–0.327; IDI: 0.055–0.060).ConclusionsCertain plasma proteins were associated with major CVD risk and may serve as promising biomarkers for risk prediction in cancer survivors, but these require further investigation.Subject terms: Cardiovascular biology, Biomarkers, Cancer

Introduction

Cancer and cardiovascular disease (CVD) remain the major causes of mortality worldwide, contributing to huge disease burdens1. Notably, these two conditions are not isolated entities. Evidence suggested that cancer survivors suffered increased risks of incident CVD compared with the healthy population2, and CVD had served as the leading non-cancer cause of death in cancer survivors3,4. Several possible reasons exist for this phenomenon, including shared risk factors (obesity, smoking, lack of physical activity, and unhealthy diet)5, the potential cardiotoxic effects of cancer therapies6, and shared disease mechanisms underlying CVD and cancer, including inflammation, oxidative stress, clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential, microbial dysbiosis, and circulating soluble factors.7 However, the understanding of the pathophysiological mechanism linking cancer and CVD is still incomplete, which had aroused a growing concern and catalyzed the emerging field of cardio-oncology.

Given the adverse effects of CVD on the prognosis of cancer survivors, early identification of people at high risk of CVD among cancer survivors is necessary. Despite conventional CVD risk assessment models, such as the Framingham Risk Score (FRS)8 and Systematic Coronary Risk Estimation 2 (SCORE2)/Systematic Coronary Risk Estimation 2-Older Persons (SCORE2-OP)9,10 and QRISK3 based on traditional cardiovascular risk factors (sex, age, blood pressure, lipids, and other risk factors), have been developed and validated for the general population, they have shown limited accuracy and utility in predicting CVD events in cancer survivors11. Proteins mediate the phenotypic effects of genetic and environmental factors12, and may specifically reflect genetic variability between individuals and responses to environmental factors, lifestyle, and drug treatments, providing an integrated assessment of human health status and diseases. Identifying circulating protein characteristics associated with the occurrence of CVD in cancer survivors can provide insights into the mechanisms underlying the relationship between cancer and CVD. Studies have indicated that plasma proteins can act as novel biomarkers for CVD and improve risk prediction in the general population12–15. However, it remains uncertain whether plasma proteome alone and integrating the proteome into conventional CVD risk prediction models can improve risk stratification for cancer survivors. Therefore, this population-based cohort study aimed to identify proteomic signatures associated with major CVDs and then explore the role of plasma proteomics in risk prediction of major CVDs in cancer survivors.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study was based on the UK Biobank, a population-based prospective study, which recruited over half a million residents (aged 37–73 years) between 2006 and 2010 across 22 recruitment centers. Baseline characteristics, lifestyle, and blood biochemical indicators were obtained using self-completed questionnaires, brief interviews, physical and functional measurements, and blood assays at recruitment.

We included 4225 participants with a history of cancer at recruitment (except for nonmelanoma skin cancer), without a diagnosis of major CVDs, and with available plasma proteomic data at baseline. Further, we randomly divided the study population into training set and test set in a ratio of 7 to 3 in order to identify protein markers associated with major CVDs in individuals with cancer and to assess their predictive performance (Fig. 1).

Ethics statements

The UK Biobank study was approved by the National Health Service North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent at baseline assessment.

Proteomics assay

Proteomic analysis of plasma samples collected from 54,219 UK Biobank participants was performed by the antibody-based Olink Explore 3072 PEA, measuring 2941 protein analytes and capturing 2923 unique proteins. Detailed information on proteomic assays, data processing, and quality control has been published16. In brief, sample- and assay-level quality control were based on Olink internal controls (incubation, extension, and amplification) and external controls (negative, plate, and sample). Data points with sample or assay warning, outliers, and potential sample swaps were excluded. The protein expression was expressed as normalized protein expression (NPX), which was an arbitrary unit of the log2 scale. In the present study, ten proteins with more than 20% missing NPX values were further excluded, and a total of 2913 proteins were retained for subsequent analyses. Missing NPX values within the retained proteins were then imputed using the K-nearest neighbor algorithm (KNN), which estimates missing values based on the most similar samples17.

CVD risk scores

We considered four classic CVD risk scores currently used in standard clinical practice, including SCORE2/SCORE2-OP, FRS, the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Pooled Cohort Equation (PCE) and QRISK3. SCORE2, SCORE2-OP, FRS, PCE, and QRISK3 (Supplementary Table 1) estimate 10-year CVD risk for populations aged 40–69, ≥70, 30–74, 40–79, and 25–84 years, respectively, which aligned with the age distribution of our study cohort. These traditional CVD risk scores employed some basic parameters, such as sex, age, smoking status, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), systolic blood pressure, hypertension treatment, and diabetes history. The specific algorithms for these risk scores have been reported in previous studies8–10,18,19.

Ascertainment of cancer and major CVDs

The study outcomes were major CVDs, including six specific types of CVDs as heart failure (HF), atrial fibrillation (AF), myocardial infarction (MI), angina, peripheral vascular disease (PVD) and stroke (Supplementary Table 2). Among these, coronary heart disease (CHD) and stroke have been shared by the four risk scores as their predicted endpoints (Supplementary Table 1). Ascertainments of the cancer and major CVDs were based on self-reported, hospital inpatient, death data, as well as the cancer registries. The self-reported data fields, International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) and Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes were used to identify cancer and major CVDs. All participants were followed from baseline assessment until the date of major CVDs, death, or end of follow-up (admission and mortality data as of October 31, 2022, and November 30, 2022, respectively), whichever occurred first.

Covariates

Demographic characteristics, such as age at recruitment, sex, ethnicity (self-reported by participants), Townsend deprivation index, and education level, were collected through touch-screen questionnaires at the baseline assessment center visit. Smoking status was classified as never smoker, previous smoker, and current smoker. Physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire and categorized into low, moderate, or vigorous levels (Supplementary Table 3). Alcohol frequency was classified as never, less than 3 or 4 times a week, and greater than or equal to 3 or 4 times a week. A healthy diet was defined by fruits, vegetables, fish, processed meats, unprocessed red meats, whole grains, and refined grains (Supplementary Table 3) based on the previous literature20. Clinical variables included cancer type, time since cancer diagnosis, medical history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, and the use of lipid-lowering and antihypertensive medications. The family history of CVD was also considered.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of this study population were presented as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables or number (percentage) for categorical variables. Missing values for covariates and CVD risk scores variables were imputed using the KNN17.

In the training set, Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models were performed to estimate associations of plasma proteins with risks of major CVDs and specific types of CVDs in cancer survivors, and the association between proteins and major CVDs risk in site-specific cancers, treating non-CVD death as a competing event. Time since baseline assessment was used as the underlying time metric. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, with no violation observed. Enrichment analysis of plasma proteins for major CVDs and specific CVD subtypes was performed using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.12.6). Fisher’s exact test was used for pathway enrichment, with P-values corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR). The protein-protein interaction network analysis constructed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/)21, where the interaction score integrates evidence from curated databases, experiments, computational predictions, and additional information, such as text mining, co-expression, and homology. Furthermore, the main analysis was stratified by sex, and a sensitivity analysis was conducted using non-imputed data to assess the stability of the results.

Furthermore, we identified protein biomarkers for major CVDs through a random forest model (401 trees, two variables randomly sampled at each split, and a minimum terminal node size of 20) in the training set. Then, the association between protein biomarkers for major CVDs and cardiometabolic indexes, including blood pressure, triglycerides, HDL, total cholesterol, apolipoprotein A and B (ApoA and ApoB), low-density lipoprotein (LDL) and C-reactive protein (CRP) was assessed by Spearman correlation analysis. In addition, we constructed predictive models of protein biomarkers, traditional CVD risk scores, and a combination of both for predicting risks of major CVDs and specific types of CVDs onset in training and testing sets. Further, the predictive performance of these predictive models for major CVDs was assessed in survivors with specific types of cancer, including breast cancer, prostate cancer, cutaneous melanoma and colorectal cancer, which were the four most prevalent cancers within the UK Biobank cohort. The classification performance was assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). At the same time, the net reclassification index (NRI) and the integrated discrimination improvement index (IDI) were used to compare the model incorporating protein biomarkers plus traditional risk scores with the model including traditional risk scores alone in training and testing sets. NRI was calculated using four predefined risk categories (<5%, 5–10%, 10–20%, >20%). The AUCs for these three predictive models were compared using DeLong’s test22.

Analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.3), and a two-sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses. Multiple testing correction was conducted using the FDR method.

Results

Baseline characteristics

During a median follow-up of 13 years, there were 804 (19.03%) major CVDs cases among participants with cancer, including 195 (4.62%) cases of HF, 375 (8.88%) cases of AF, 176 (4.17%) cases of MI, 176 (4.17%) cases of angina, 94 (2.22%) cases of PVD, and 137 (3.24%) cases of stroke (Supplementary Table 4). The characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1. Those with major CVDs among cancer survivors were more likely to be older, male, obese, current smokers, physically inactive, and more socioeconomically deprived, as well as have an unhealthy diet, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and diabetes.

Associations between plasma proteins and major CVDs in cancer survivors

In multivariable analysis adjusting for demographics and risk factors, associations with higher incidence of major CVDs were observed for increased plasma levels of 181 proteins at FDR < 0.05 (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Table 5), especially for NTproBNP, NPC2, TNFRSF12A, LMNB2, EDA2R, ASGR1, LTBP2, and WFDC2. Only BCAN was negatively correlated with the major CVDs risk [HR (95%CI) = 0.68 (0.53, 0.85)]. In detail, there were 4 proteins (including TNFRSF12A, MYBPC1, NTproBNP, and FGF23) and 67 proteins (such as NTproBNP, IGFBP4, NPC2, and ASGR1) positively related to increased major CVDs risk in women and men, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1). In addition, we found 5 proteins, such as TNF and TNFRSF12A, were associated with higher risk of major CVDs in participants with breast cancer (Supplementary Table 6). There were positive associations of 24 and 21 proteins with major CVDs in prostate cancer and cutaneous melanoma, respectively. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis of data without covariate imputation was conducted, which identified 174 proteins associated with major CVDs risk, and the majority of these protein associations remained consistent (Supplementary Fig. 2).

With respect to the specific types of CVDs, 337, 28, 26, and 1 proteins were positively associated with increased incidences of HF, AF, MI, and PVD, respectively, and 4 proteins were negatively associated with HF (Fig. 2B–E and Supplementary Table 5). However, there was no statistical association of plasma proteins with angina and stroke risks in cancer survivors. Moreover, higher levels of 19 proteins, such as NTproBNP, LTBP2, EDA2R, and CLEC14A, were associated with increased risks of both HF and AF (Fig. 2B, C, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Table 5). Meanwhile, there were 189 plasma proteins associated solely with HF, such as FABP4, MANSC1, NOS3, COL18A1, and CD300E, while EDA2R and VSIG4 were specifically linked to AF and MI, respectively.

Enrichment analysis of 182 plasma proteins related to major CVDs showed that most were enriched in biological processes involving the adhesion, activation, and proliferation of immune cells, angiogenesis regulation, and inflammation-related functions (Fig. 3A), as well as pathways like cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction (Fig. 3B), indicating that immune, inflammatory, and angiogenic pathways in cancer survivors may be involved in the occurrence and development of major CVDs. Protein signaling pathways of HF resembled those of major CVDs (Supplementary Fig. 4). Proteins related to AF mainly were enriched in negative regulation of immune cells, which may help limit overactive immune responses (Supplementary Fig. 5). The protein pathways related to MI mainly involve angiogenesis and vasculature development (Supplementary Fig. 6). Next, we found 99 proteins were significantly associated with each other with an interaction score of 0.7 or higher based on 182 proteins associated with major CVDs, especially strong interactions among 83 proteins, such as TNF, IL6, TNFRSF1A, CXCL8, CD80, CXCL10, and IL2RA, which were involved in the cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Screening for protein markers for predicting the risk of major CVDs in cancer survivors

In the training set, 182 plasma proteins related to major CVDs were incorporated into the random forest model, and 23 optimal protein biomarkers of major CVDs (such as NTproBNP, EDA2R, FABP3, LGALS9, RNF149, CHCHD10, IFI30, and CD302, ranked by the relative importance) were screened out considering lower 10-fold cross-validation error and higher AUC (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 8 and Table 7). Among them, the majority of proteins were positively correlated with systolic blood pressure, triglyceride, and CRP (r: 0.07–0.43; FDR < 0.05), but negatively correlated with HDL and ApoA (r: −0.08 to −0.32; FDR < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 9).

In the training set, the 23 protein biomarkers demonstrated improved discrimination compared with conventional CVD risk scores, with AUCs ranging from 0.680 to 0.690 (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 4B–D). In the test set, consistent findings were observed. The protein markers (AUCs: 0.646–0.665) showed superior predictive performance over some CVD risk scores, outperforming FRS and PCE for major CVDs, SCORE2/SCORE2-OP, QISK3 and FRS for 5-year CVDs and only FRS for 10-year CVDs (AUCs: 0.518–0.605; FDR < 0.05; Fig. 4E–G).

In the training set, incorporating the protein biomarkers into conventional CVD risk scores significantly improved predictive performance for major CVDs, 5-year CVDs, and 10-year CVDs, reflected by higher AUCs (0.688–0.711; FDR < 0.05) and significant increases in NRI (0.087–0.272) and IDI (0.012–0.075) (Table 2). In the test set, after adding the protein biomarkers to conventional CVD risk scores, the AUCs for predicting major CVDs, 5-year CVDs and 10-year CVDs also improved significantly (AUCs: 0.647–0.705; FDR < 0.05; Table 2). For 5-year CVDs, the inclusion of protein biomarkers significantly improved risk reclassification (NRI: 0.245–0.327) and discrimination (IDI: 0.055–0.060) for all CVD risk scores. However, for major CVDs and 10-year CVDs, significant improvement was only observed for FRS.

For specific types of CVDs, the protein biomarkers achieved moderate discrimination for specific CVD subtypes (AUCs: 0.557–0.706) and generally outperformed conventional risk scores, except for angina and PVD in the training set (Supplementary Fig. 10). When incorporated into conventional risk scores, they primarily improved the prediction of AF and HF (Supplementary Tables 8 and 9). In the test set (Supplementary Fig. 11), the protein biomarkers merely had moderate discriminative power for AF, PVD, and stroke (AUCs: 0.604–0.669), outperforming the four CVD risk scores (FDR < 0.05). Supportively, the addition of protein biomarkers to SCORE2/SCORE2-OP, QRISK3, FRS, or PCE scores, mainly improved the risk prediction of AF (AUCs: 0.675–0.698; FDR < 0.05) and stroke (AUCs: 0.551–0.624; FDR < 0.05; Supplementary Tables 8 and 9).

Performance assessment of protein biomarkers for major CVDs in survivors with site-specific cancers

Among breast cancer survivors, protein biomarkers achieved the AUC of 0.630 and 0.680 for predicting major CVDs in the training and test sets, respectively, but showing comparable performance to CVD risk scores (FDR > 0.05; Supplementary Figs. 12, 13, and Table 10). The addition of protein biomarkers only improved the predictive performance of FRS for major CVDs in the training set (NRI: 0.126; IDI: 0.022) and test set (NRI: 0.173; IDI: 0.100; Supplementary Table 10). Among prostate cancer survivors, protein biomarkers showed moderate performance for major CVDs (AUC: 0.696; Supplementary Fig. 12) and further improved predictive performance of conventional risk scores when combined in the training set (Supplementary Table 10). However, in the test set, their predictive performance was poor (AUCs: 0.503; Supplementary Fig. 13), with no significant improvement observed when combined with conventional risk scores (Supplementary Table 10). For cutaneous melanoma survivors, protein biomarkers showed limited discrimination for major CVDs (AUC = 0.580; Supplementary Fig. 13) in the test set and did not improve prediction when combined with conventional risk scores. Discussion

This prospective cohort study based on the UK Biobank identified 182 plasma proteins associated with the risk of major CVDs in cancer survivors, especially NTproBNP, NPC2, TNFRSF12A, LMNB2, EDA2R, ASGR1, LTBP2, and WFDC2, mainly involved in biological processes of immune, inflammatory, and angiogenesis. Further, a panel of 23 protein biomarkers was developed and demonstrated moderate predictive performance for major, 5-year, and 10-year CVDs in the test set, outperforming several classic CVD risk scores. Incorporating the protein panel into classic CVD risk scores significantly improved discrimination for major, 5-year, and 10-year CVDs, and mainly improved reclassification for 5-year CVDs. Simultaneously, the predictive ability varied across different CVD subtypes and among site-specific cancer survivors.

Previous studies have mostly explored and identified plasma protein markers associated with CVD risk based on the general population12–15. It remains unknown whether these CVD-related protein markers are also applicable to cancer survivors, considering the particularity of cancer survivors as a high-risk population for CVD. A cohort study explored the association between several proteins and 2-year major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) risk in cancer survivors and found that ICAM-1, P-/E-/L-selection, and NT-proBNP were independently associated with 2-year MACE risk23. However, there are still gaps in understanding the relationship between proteins identified through high-throughput proteomics and CVD risk in cancer survivors and whether these circulating proteins can enhance the prediction of CVD risk.

Our prospective cohort study aimed to fill this gap and identified 182 plasma proteins related to major CVDs based on high-throughput proteomics data in 4425 cancer survivors with a median follow-up of 13 years, especially NTproBNP, NPC2, TNFRSF12A, LMNB2, EDA2R, ASGR1, LTBP2, and WFDC2, which have also been reported to be linked with cardiovascular risk in general population cohorts from the UK Biobank13,15. As a traditional cardiac biomarker, NT-proBNP had been reported to have great potential to identify the development of adverse CVD events during cancer treatment to guide clinical diagnostic and treatment decisions24, which further supported our findings. NPC2 plays a role in cholesterol trafficking within lysosomes25,26, however, how this further influence the incidence of CVD is unknown13,15. LTBP2 has been reported to be related to cancer27,28 and CVD15,29. Also, they were involved in multiple biological processes, such as inflammatory response and oxidative stress30–33, which may help understand the pathophysiology behind the link between cancer and CVD.

The enrichment analysis suggested that cancer survivors were frequently exposed to immune activation and chronic inflammation, which may promote endothelial dysfunction, accelerate atherosclerosis, and induce myocardial stress and injury. Furthermore, the significant enrichment of cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction pathways and the inflammatory network formed by 83 key proteins, such as TNF, IL6, CXCL8, and CD80, highlighted the central role of immune-inflammatory mechanisms. For CVD subtypes, proteins related to HF, AF, and MI further involved pathways of myocardial injury and structural remodeling, negative control of T-cell and leukocyte proliferation, and pathways of inflammation, endothelial injury, and thrombosis, respectively.

It is necessary to identify high-risk groups for CVD in cancer survivors early based on risk prediction tools and guide clinical treatment and care. However, existing classic CVD risk scores perform poorly in cancer survivors, resulting in underestimation of CVD risk11, possibly because these scores did not capture factors other than traditional CVD risk factors, such as the effects of the tumor itself, related factors on the cardiovascular system and the cardiovascular toxicity of cancer treatment7. Considering that the proteome can reflect the phenotypic effects of genetic and environmental factors, we evaluated its predictive value for major CVDs in cancer populations. The prediction model constructed based on 23 protein biomarkers showed moderate predictive performance for major CVDs, outperforming FRS and PCE in the test set. This suggested that the proteome may capture biological effects of exposures other than traditional risk factors on CVD34, but further validation is needed. In addition, considering that cancer survivors may continue to suffer an elevated CVD mortality risk compared with the general population for many years after diagnosis35, we evaluated the prediction performance of this protein prediction model for 5-year and 10-year major CVDs risk and found that it could still achieve better predictive performance for 5-year major CVDs compared with classic CVD risk scores including SCORE2/SCORE2-OP, QISK3 and FRS, while for 10-year major CVDs, the protein panel outperformed FRS. However, the protein model in our cancer survivor cohort (10-year CVDs AUC 0.646) showed lower predictive performance than those in general population studies, with reported AUCs of 0.75813 and 0.79515. This likely reflected reflects the more complex cardiometabolic and treatment-related perturbations in cancer survivors, such as chemotherapy-induced cardiotoxicity, chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation. Additionally, incorporating the 23 protein biomarkers into classic CVD risk scores improved discrimination for major, 5-year, and 10-year CVDs, with improvements in 5-year risk reclassification also observed for all CVD risk score among cancer survivors in the test set. Most protein biomarkers were positively correlated with systolic blood pressure, triglycerides, and CRP, but negatively correlated with HDL and ApoA, suggesting that they reflected both traditional cardiovascular risk factors and additional biological processes, such as inflammation and immune dysregulation. These associations supported the biological plausibility of the protein prediction model and explain its improved predictive performance over conventional risk scores.

However, the predictive value of protein biomarkers for different CVD subtypes and in survivors with different site-specific cancer, as well as their improvement effects on classical scores were inconsistent, possibly due to different treatment approaches and pathological mechanisms, etc. Therefore, although protein biomarkers had the potential to predict major CVDs risk and make up for the deficiencies of traditional CVD risk scores, their efficacy varied for different CVD subtypes and among cancer types. At the same time, given the potential biases, further research is needed to validate these findings in larger scales population in the future.

The study has several strengths. This is a systematic study to explore CVD-related proteins and evaluate the potential application of proteomics in CVD risk prediction in cancer survivors based on a large-scale prospective cohort with long-term follow-up. In the context of cardio-oncology, this study provides new insights into CVD risk prediction and management in cancer survivors by high-throughput proteome analysis based on blood specimens. However, some limitations exist. Firstly, the study population was predominantly white in the UK Biobank, and the predictive model was evaluated only by internal validation. In the future, this study needs to be verified in more populations. Secondly, only the levels of plasma proteins measured at baseline and was used in this study, given the limited data from multiple measurements, although plasma proteins may change over time. Thirdly, detailed information on cancer treatment regimens and cancer stage was not available for cancer survivors in the UK Biobank. Given that several widely used cancer treatments, such as anthracyclines, have been reported to show severe cardiotoxicity6, the lack of treatment-specific data may have introduced unmeasured or residual confounding, potentially affecting the observed associations. Although we adjusted for multiple covariates, including cancer type and time since cancer diagnosis, caution is warranted when interpreting the results. Fourthly, we cannot exclude the possibility of reverse causation, as elevated baseline protein levels may reflect early or subclinical CVD. Although the prospective design reduces this concern, further validation in longitudinal studies is needed. Finally, individuals with cancer prior to study recruitment may introduce survivor bias and volunteer bias, potentially underestimating true associations, though the long follow-up and competing risk models likely mitigate this effect.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study identified plasma proteins prospectively associated with the risk of major CVDs in cancer survivors, mainly involved in biological processes, such as immunity, inflammation, and angiogenesis, providing clues for understanding the underlying mechanisms of increased major CVDs risk in cancer survivors. Further, we constructed a 23-protein panel as promising biomarkers for risk prediction of major CVDs in cancer survivors, showing favorable predictive performance compared with classic CVD scores, but further validation is required.
