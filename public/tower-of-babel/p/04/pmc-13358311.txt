Knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding immediate breast reconstruction after mastectomy: a cross-sectional study in Jordan
Authors: Aseel O Rataan, Mariam Alameri, Ghaith M Al-Taani, Hadeel K Shreim, Leen H Al-Shalakhti, Bara’ah A AlRefai, Lian Shqeer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) of Jordanian women regarding post-mastectomy breast reconstruction (PMBR), and to recognise key sociodemographic factors influencing these outcomes.DesignA descriptive cross-sectional study.SettingCommunity-based settings across Jordan, representing a general population sample.ParticipantsA total of 752 adult women were enrolled in the study. Eligible participants were women aged 18 years or older who were living in Jordan. Recruitment took place across a variety of community settings. Data were collected on sociodemographic variables such as age, level of education, insurance coverage and any personal history of breast disease.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcomes were KAP related to PMBR, measured using a standardised, pretested Arabic questionnaire. Secondary outcomes included identifying sociodemographic factors associated with KAP.ResultsParticipants demonstrated moderate knowledge (56.2%), a generally positive attitude (48.6%) and moderate practice levels (42.0%) toward PMBR. Regression analysis indicated that age, level of education, insurance status and personal history of breast disease were significant predictors of KAP outcomes.ConclusionsAwareness and use of PMBR among Jordanian women remain inadequate. These results highlight the need for culturally tailored educational initiatives, improved communication between patients and healthcare providers, and the establishment of national guidelines to facilitate informed decision-making. Further studies are warranted to identify barriers to PMBR uptake and to evaluate the impact of targeted awareness interventions.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most commonly diagnosed malignant tumor in women worldwide. The increasing rates of mastectomy highlight the growing importance of breast reconstruction in post-treatment care.13 Recent data also indicate that the incidence of BC is increasing, with an estimated annual growth rate of around 1% in recent years.4 In addition, despite the role of prevention in many cases, global estimates indicate that this burden will keep growing substantially, with projections pointing to substantial rises in BC incidence and mortality by 2050, particularly in low-income and middle-income countries.5 American recommendations do not entirely reflect current clinical practice; mastectomy remains common in the management of BC and can significantly affect a woman’s psychological well-being, physical appearance and body image as well as sense of identity.6 The study by Lee et al7 further demonstrated that women’s sociodemographic characteristics influenced perceptions of breast reconstruction. Increased awareness was observed in women with higher education, while greater access to reconstructive procedures correlated with higher income and insurance coverage. Additionally, urban residence was associated with improved information and healthcare services accessibility, positively influencing patient perceptions. Such factors account for the diversity of perspectives on reconstruction observed among various patient populations.7 On the other hand, breast reconstruction is still not widely adopted in the Middle East and several low and middle-income countries.8 This is primarily due to poor awareness, existing sociocultural beliefs regarding body image and cultural perceptions of cancer, along with differences in healthcare systems and referral pathways. Additionally, the role of physicians and the lack of adequate patient counselling may further influence decision-making regarding breast reconstruction.9

Post-mastectomy breast reconstruction (PMBR) has been an important choice for many women. After surgery, it has been shown to improve the quality of life (QoL) and body image, as well as the psychosocial well-being of patients.10 11 Therefore, breast reconstruction is integral to BC overall management, with its increasingly recognised contribution to higher QoL and better psychosocial outcomes in mastectomy patients.1214

BC accounts for approximately 20% of the total burden of cancers diagnosed in Jordan; it remains one of the most common causes of cancer-related morbidity and mortality among women.15 Interestingly, the disease tends to affect younger women in Jordan, with a significant proportion of cases occurring in those under 50 years of age.16 BC care in Jordan is mainly provided by major public and private hospitals, but compliance with international guidelines varies from one to the other. Screening practices were mostly in line with the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) recommendations, but treatment protocols were more varied. Multidisciplinary team approaches to guiding treatment decisions were present at some centres but were not consistently used across the board. PMBR usually took place with plastic surgeons working alongside general or breast surgeons, depending on what each hospital can provide. Such differences highlight the need for unified national guidelines and better integration of the reconstructive services within the standard BC care.16 Although PMBR is safe and comes with physical and psychological benefits to PMBR patients, limited awareness among patients and low referral rates have been suggested to explain the underutilisation of the procedure. It has also been noted how influential surgeons are in patients’ choices to have reconstruction.17 The context surrounding breast reconstruction practices for women in Jordan entails several factors; cultural views related to body image and femininity, and the stigma attached to cancer, among others, may have an impact on women’s opinions towards pursuing breast reconstruction surgeries.18 Despite the availability of specialised breast cancer care, not all hospitals offer reconstructive services, and referral practices are often poorly defined. Furthermore, a lack of awareness and patient education, and discrepancies in insurance coverage influence the decision-making process and also the adoption of reconstruction.19 These contextual factors underscore the need to evaluate women’s knowledge, perceptions and practices in the Jordanian population to inform culturally sensitive interventions and healthcare policies.

Factors such as information gaps, cultural traditions, religious beliefs and unequal access to specialised care cause women in Jordan and elsewhere to be hesitant in undergoing PMBR despite its benefits.20 Knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) surveys are often done to gain a better understanding of women’s perceptions of such an option, as they provide insight into awareness, beliefs and behaviours related to health decisions.21 22

Assessing the KAP of Jordanian women towards PMBR can help to identify knowledge gaps, misconceptions and behavioural factors that may delay making an informed decision at the time of diagnosis or preclude access to timely reconstructive care. With an increased focus on survivorship and reconstructive options for BC patients, a gap remains in the knowledge related to these outcomes in Jordan and the surrounding region. Most of the existing research pertains to BC in general, focusing on awareness of the disease, treatment choices or investigating cosmetic outcomes, but little attention is given to women’s knowledge, attitudes and actual practices regarding the subject of PMBR post-mastectomy.23 To our knowledge, no local cross-sectional study has addressed these aspects through the lens of a KAP framework, highlighting the need and significance of this study.

Our study aims to determine the KAP of adult women in Jordan with or without BC towards PMBR and identify the factors associated with differences in KAP, such as sociodemographic factors and prior exposure to BC. Results are anticipated to provide useful recommendations that could: (1) assist in the creation of culturally tailored, data-driven delivery interventions, (2) guide healthcare policies, and (3) enhance the availability of BC and reconstructive services in Jordan.

Methods

Research design

A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted to assess women’s knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding PMBR. The study was carried out in Jordan and included women who lived across the country, whether they were diagnosed with breast cancer or not.

Research tool

Data were collected using a standardised questionnaire adapted from previously published studies.23 24 To ensure linguistic accuracy and cultural authenticity of the tool, the original English version of the instrument was translated into Arabic using a forward-backward translation approach. Minor changes were made to align the tool with the Jordanian situation.

The pretested questionnaire was divided into four sections: (1) sociodemographic characteristics such as age, education, place of residence, occupation, marital status, income and insurance status; (2) knowledge: nine items measuring the awareness of breast cancer, surgical treatment options, eligibility for primary reconstruction and procedural understanding; (3) attitudes: 10 Likert-scale questions evaluating cultural and individual beliefs, feelings and perceptions regarding breast reconstruction; and (4) practices: 12 items examining health-related behaviours, sources of decision-making support, participation in awareness campaigns and the factors influencing reconstruction decisions.

Study population

Eligible participants were women aged 18 years or older, living in Jordan and willing to participate irrespective of a prior breast cancer diagnosis. Women who refused to participate, were unable to give informed consent or submitted incomplete responses were excluded.

Patient and public involvement

Participants were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews carried out by trained fourth and fifth-year pharmacy students with cancer clinic patients and members of the public at King Abdullah University Hospital, Al-Bashir Hospital and Princess Basma Teaching Hospital during the period from June 2025 to September 2025. Before data collection, interviewers received standardised training to ensure consistent and ethical questionnaire administration. The interviewers’ academic background and training in patient interaction supported effective communication and accurate data collection.

To reduce interviewer bias, a structured protocol was applied. Interviewers were instructed to use standardised wording, avoid leading questions and maintain neutrality. Pilot testing and mock interviews were also carried out to guarantee consistency and inter-interviewer reliability. A convenience sampling method was used, whereby they were recruited within diverse community settings to improve diversity and representation. These include medical and non-medical centres, social places and various parts of Jordan.

Ethical considerations

Informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection, and participation was voluntary. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Yarmouk University (Reference No. IRB/2024/615).

Reliability and validity of the instrument

Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding values of 0.861 for the overall scale, 0.855 for the knowledge domain, 0.666 for the attitude domain and 0.845 for the practice domain. Internal consistency of the attitude domain produced a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.666, meaning that it had an acceptable reliability. The value of 0.70 is accepted as a usual threshold, though a range of 0.60 to 0.70 could be taken as a point of acceptable evidence in exploratory studies and KAP research. Other domains were more reliable, which confirms the overall strength of the instrument.

A panel of 8 experts from the School of Pharmacy was used to evaluate the content validity of the scale. All of whom had postgraduate degrees and relevant research experience. A pilot study involving 10 participants was conducted to assess the clarity, readability and feasibility of the questionnaire. Feedback from this phase led to minor changes in wording and presentation. Data from the pilot study were excluded from the final analysis.

Construct validity was supported by the predefined domain structure of the questionnaire (knowledge, attitudes and practices), which was based on previously published research and showed conceptual consistency within every single domain.

Statistical analysis

The data were analysed using SPSS (version 26). Descriptive statistics, such as means, percentages and frequencies, were used to summarise the data. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the predictors’ associations between summated KAP scores and sociodemographic characteristics. Initially, an univariable linear regression analysis was performed to identify candidate variables, defined by a p value <0.05. Candidate variables were then entered into a backward linear regression model, retaining variables with an independent statistically significant contribution to the outcome variable. Statistical significance was set at p<0.050.

Results

The total number of participants in the study was 752 adult participants, after excluding those who did not agree to participate. The sample included 175 (23.3%) participants who had relatives or friends diagnosed with BC, whereas a significant proportion (39.9%; n=300) of participants were BC patients. The participants had an average age of 42.5 years. The residence place was mainly in urban areas (60.1%). Data on education levels revealed that 29.8% of participants had secondary school education or less, 61% had a BSc degree or diploma, and only 9.2% had a postgraduate degree. Participants’ occupations were as follows: 35.8% were housewives, 29.7% were healthcare professionals, 19.8% were government employees and 14.8% were business owners. The monthly income per capita was below 500 Jordanian Dinar (JD) in the majority of respondents (64.5%), whereas 27.3% had an income between 500 and 1000 JD, followed by 8.2% of respondents earning above 1000 JD. Married respondents comprised the most (50.8%) of respondents, followed by singles (36.4%), widowed (8%) and divorced individuals (4.8%). Medical insurance covered 70.5% of the participants, rendering the remainder (29.5%) not insured. Importantly, regarding the sources of information about breast reconstruction surgery, the majority relied on healthcare professionals as the main source of information (40.6%); other credible sources of information cited were hospital campaigns and education (15.4%). A significant proportion of respondents cited social media and news outlets (28.5%). Lastly, 15.6% of the respondents mentioned that they had not heard about it. A total of 330 (43.9%) respondents disclosed that they had a history of breast disease or previous benign breast surgery, and 56.1% of respondents had not. Knowledge levels of Jordanian women regarding breast cancer and breast reconstruction

Table 2 summarises the respondents’ knowledge about BC and breast reconstruction surgery. The results revealed a varying degree of knowledge of various aspects of the topic. It was reported that the majority (78.3%) of respondents accurately identified that ‘BC is the most prevalent malignancy in Jordanian women’. A high percentage (71.4%) of respondents highlighted that breast surgery is a critical intervention for early BC linked with breast loss/disfigurement. In a similar percentage, most respondents (77.1%) accurately identified that ‘breast reconstruction is a surgical procedure to reconstruct the breast loss into a natural-looking breast’. Regarding the knowledge of the impact of breast reconstruction surgery on disease progression, 45.5% of participants stated that ‘breast reconstruction does not affect the evolution of the tumor and does not increase the risk of recurrence or metastasis’. A limited knowledge of the PMBR was noted; only 37.4% of the respondents were aware that ‘PMBR means that radical BC surgery and breast reconstruction are performed in a single operation, without the patient experiencing the pain of breast loss’. Approximately half (52.7%) of the respondents indicated the suitability of the primary reconstruction for early-stage BC patients. Almost half (55.9%) of the respondents indicated that they were aware of the reconstruction methods, specifically the use of autologous tissue or prosthetic materials, in order to limit the trauma caused by the surgery. Knowledge about postoperative radiotherapy was limited; only 55.9% of the respondents were aware that the use of prosthetic materials would be best avoided if postoperative radiation was used. 60% of respondents stated that they were aware that reconstruction surgery can include procedures directed at the contralateral breast to achieve symmetry. For the whole knowledge scale, the overall mean score was 56.2% with moderate variability, that is, an SD of 2.43. Other knowledge gaps identified in table 2 related to the fact that 41.6% of respondents don’t know about BC recurrence/metastasis risk, 35.8% are confused about the definition of breast reconstruction, 34.7% are confused about the role in early BC, 35.9% and 30.1% are confused about the technique, and 35.1% don’t know about radiotherapy considerations.

Perceptions and attitudes of Jordanian women toward breast cancer and post-mastectomy reconstruction

Table 3 illustrates the attitude towards BC and breast reconstruction surgery, which was noted to be a positive attitude in general. The majority (83.3%) agreed or strongly agreed that PMBR is a very advanced and important technique that can help BC patients get rid of their breast loss and regain their confidence and beauty. In a similar percentage, 82.5% of the respondents agreed or strongly agreed that breast reconstruction can reduce the psychological stress of patients and make them feel that they are still whole. A significant majority (80.4%) agreed or strongly agreed that breast reconstruction requires patients to be psychologically prepared and be courageous enough to face the challenges of the surgery and recovery period. Encouraging BC patients to undergo breast reconstruction surgery was high, with 76.0% agreeing or strongly agreeing. The agreement was high (76.8%) that the appearance restoration of the breasts can improve the QoL. However, mixed results were noted concerning the potential risks and concerns, with 24% agreeing that it is unnecessary or increases surgical trauma. Divided results were noted for their effect on the subsequent treatment of the patient, that is, 28.9% agreed, 28.8% disagreed, and 42.4% were neutral. In relation to cultural impact, undergoing breast reconstruction after mastectomy might negatively impact a woman’s femininity or traditional roles in Jordanian society. The results revealed that 24.5% agreed, 52.9% disagreed and 22.6% were neutral. Religious impact, that is, religious considerations, may influence decisions about breast reconstruction. In terms of access in Jordan for breast reconstruction surgery, 34.5% agreed that the access was adequate, 29.9% disagreed and 35.6% were neutral. The mean summated attitude score equaled 17.01 (48.6% of the total possible score) with an SD of 3.55, which reflects a moderate attitude with some variability.

Behavioral patterns and practices toward breast cancer and reconstructive options

Table 4 summarises participants’ practices related to BC and breast reconstruction surgery. The results indicated that the participants had moderate engagement levels with generally supportive practices. A high percentage (75.4%) of participants highlighted that they always or often fully support their relatives/friends who need to undergo a total mastectomy to choose primary breast reconstruction. Regarding the advocacy at the public health level, 72.9% of the respondents indicated that they will always or often publicise the risks and prevention of BC to their family, friends and community to raise their awareness and knowledge. A significant proportion (60.5%) of respondents would always or often participate in charity events or awareness campaigns to support BC research and related charities. An interest in keeping up with and studying the information on primary reconstruction surgery was always or often expressed by 67.4% of participants. For the summated score of participants’ practice, the mean score was 8.4 out of 20 (42.0% of the total possible score) with an SD of 3.01, which indicates a moderate level of proactivity with some variability of engagement. When the participants were asked to rate the determinants and factors that can influence one’s decision to undergo PMBR, the major determinants were patient’s health condition (76.6% indicated that it always or often impact the decision), age (74.3%), economic situation (76%), breast appearance (78.9%), psychological condition (79.5%) and family or social support (79.1%).

Discussion

PMBR plays an important role in breast reconstruction following mastectomy in patients with BC; reconstructive surgery not only restores the physical appearance of the breast but also improves QoL and body image of the BC patients. There are multiple different reconstructive options available, from implant-based reconstruction to autologous tissue flaps, and their outcome can vary significantly based on patient factors, surgical procedure and psychological outcomes; thus, it is vital to choose a personalised reconstructive procedure.24 25 Early reconstruction is often the desired choice due to its advantages in the quick re-establishment of breast volume and shape, which can improve psychological outcomes by diminishing the period of body image distress following mastectomy.2628 Studies have shown that patients who undergo immediate reconstruction experience lower levels of psychosocial distress compared with those who opt for delayed reconstruction.26 28

Decision on reconstruction incorporates multiple interacting factors, including medical factors (eg comorbidity), emotional readiness, cultural expectations and service availability. Although strong evidence suggests that reconstruction does not worsen oncologic outcomes,29 30 complications—such as infection, dissatisfaction with implant feel, or less than ideal cosmetic results—can be associated with psychosocial distress and regret, highlighting the need for thorough preoperative counselling.3134 These findings identify significant knowledge, attitude and practice gaps about PMBR among Jordanian women that are influenced by contextual factors. A moderate level of knowledge (56.2%) was demonstrated, with considerable uncertainty regarding basic concepts about reconstruction (ie, definition, eligibility, and the implications for cancer recurrence and adjuvant therapies). These gaps suggest that, despite relatively high levels of formal education among participants, awareness of PMBR remains insufficient in the Jordanian context. Results that are consistent with those of Al-Mousa et al.35

The results also showed that most of the participants (48.6%) held positive attitudes towards breast reconstruction, particularly in the context of perceived psychosocial benefits in terms of body image and QoL. However, this positive view was accompanied by a lot of ambivalence, particularly around cultural norms, religious beliefs and fears related to surgical risk. Nonetheless, practice levels were 42.0%, suggesting that while many women could get behind reconstruction in principle, this did not always translate into participation in awareness activities and informed decision-making. Collectively, these findings suggest a gap between awareness and perception vs health practices that are likely shaped by country-specific contextual, cultural and structural aspects.

A significant finding in our study was the positive correlation between obtaining information from healthcare providers and higher knowledge scores. This highlights the significance of education provided by the providers, as in evidence from international studies such as that by Alderman et al,36 which demonstrated that discussions regarding reconstruction significantly influenced women’s decisions to undergo it. However, access to such counselling remains inconsistent, and insurance or structural barriers often restrict exposure to the options available, concerns that have been similarly documented in the literature.37

This study also examined the attitudes of women towards breast reconstruction surgery, with mixed responses in favour of breast reconstruction. The majority of participants acknowledged the psychological advantages of such a procedure, as there will be more confidence, better well-being and improved QoL. This is also consistent with both international as well as local research, such as a study by Bani-Ahmed et al.38 In this study, they concluded that Jordanian women who had reconstruction experienced lower rates of depression and anxiety, particularly when they received family support and participated in physical rehabilitation. However, our study exposed culture and religion issues as well, with respondents who were either unsure or worried about the process and how it would align with their religious beliefs, as well as their traditional gender roles and behaviours. This was also evident in the qualitative study done by Faria et al,39 where mastectomy and reconstruction were found to a great extent to influence body-image, femininity, and roles within the family and society. Family ties may also heavily influence decision-making, as well as in relation to surgery, having family discussions, particularly with spouses or the wider extended family. In Jordan, it is not a direct choice for women to have breast reconstruction, with family ties staying strong and influencing the women’s choice. Further, religious clarification and opinions on body integrity as well as surgical alteration may also influence women’s perception of reconstruction and have varying influences in different population groups; hence, the mixed feelings with the high percentage of women selecting neutral responses, not outright rejecting but indicating their uncertain feelings. This highlights the important role for culturally relevant counselling that include religious support when relevant.40

Financial factors also significantly impact women’s choices regarding breast reconstruction. For instance, while 70.5% of the participants reported having medical insurance, coverage for reconstructive procedures in Jordan can vary and might not completely cover all options, making it more challenging financially. Likewise, cultural expectations and family pressure also deter women from seeking out reconstruction, particularly if they distinguish its potential benefits. Our findings highlighted that gaps in information and social and economic obstacles must also be addressed to offer women fair access to breast reconstruction.41

As illustrated earlier, Jordanian women have a moderate level of knowledge about PMBR (56.2%) with generally positive views (48.6%), although practice is suboptimal (42.0%). Knowledge levels in Jordan seem slightly higher than in Saudi Arabia (46.4%) and comparable to India (~51%), but awareness remains far from complete in all of these studies. Nuances may not be directly comparable, but attitudes in Jordan were relatively favourable and similar to those in Saudi Arabia, where about half of women were willing to consider reconstruction. This is in contrast to women in India who had less favourable attitudes, with many women unwilling to consider reconstructions, as they do not feel the need for reconstruction or have adapted after mastectomy. Psychological benefits were the key motivators across all studies. Fear and misconception were common barriers, as was age.

Despite those differences, all studies show a gap between awareness, attitude and actual practice. For example, only 27.9% of women in India underwent reconstruction, and practice in Jordan is limited despite moderate knowledge and positive attitudes. These findings suggest that simply increasing awareness is not enough and that there should be a greater emphasis on counselling/supportive interventions to facilitate the translation of knowledge into actual uptake.42 43

Our research also identified some key predictors of KAP outcomes. Older women and those without a personal history of BC were linked with lower knowledge, while being uninsured paradoxically was associated with higher knowledge, possibly as a result of more self-learning in the absence of access to professional care. In contrast to this, however, practice was higher in older women but lower in those with higher educational achievement or without insurance, reflecting a disconnect between knowledge and what is actually practiced, which warrants further investigation.

Women with higher education in our study were less likely to have positive scores in attitudes and practices, which is counterintuitive and against the majority of literature showing an association between better education and health behaviour. Women with more education may be better informed about surgical risks, leading to lower practice scores. Alternatively, women with more education may face greater professional and social demands, which could detract from their ability to invest energy and time in health-related behaviours. Additionally, higher education does not necessarily mitigate deeply rooted cultural or religious beliefs, which may continue to influence decision-making despite improved knowledge levels. This suggests that, in this population, better knowledge may not equate to behavioural change, and that sociocultural factors influence even the more educated among us.

A potential explanation may be the coexistence of professional or academic knowledge with persistent cultural and religious beliefs that shape personal decisions. Practice levels were moderate, with the majority of women demonstrating support for family members who underwent reconstruction or willingness to participate in awareness campaigns, yet fewer reported active participation in health education or charity events. These findings support previous studies noting that while Jordanian women may demonstrate support for awareness activities, behavioural engagement is more commonly restricted by logistical, cultural or informational barriers.44 45 From a broader perspective, decision-making about mastectomy and reconstruction also appears to be strongly influenced by fear of recurrence, desire for peace of mind and minimal exposure to models of shared decision-making. Obeidat et al44 reported that up to 67% of women with early-stage BC in Jordan chose mastectomy, with decisions more strongly shaped by anxiety and the stage of the disease than by full awareness of available surgical options. This highlights the necessity for extensive counselling, including timely information about reconstruction.

In the context of Jordan, it is important to interpret the findings of Obeidat et al44 within the broader sociodemographic and cultural framework. Jordanian women are generally well educated; national statistics indicate that approximately 92%–93% of women are literate, and females constitute over half of university students (⁓54%–56%), reflecting a relatively high level of educational attainment.46 However, despite this, decision-making regarding breast reconstruction is not solely knowledge-driven. Cultural and religious values, while not always explicitly reported in studies, may indirectly influence choices. In Jordan, a predominantly Muslim society, religious beliefs generally do not prohibit reconstructive surgery, particularly when it is considered part of medical treatment and restoration rather than cosmetic enhancement. Nevertheless, modesty considerations and perceptions of body image may affect attitudes toward reconstruction.

Finally, the findings of this study indicate that there is an urgent need for culturally sensitive, directive educational interventions addressing informational deficits and psychosocial distress. Oncologists and other healthcare providers must be adequately trained to clearly describe alternatives with empathy, focusing on myths and misconceptions regarding recurrence, religion and gender identity. Additionally, psychological counselling and family involvement in the process of reconstruction can improve acceptance of reconstruction and overall well-being. A multidisciplinary approach—involving surgeons, nurses, psychologists and community advocates—is essential to provide women with the knowledge, support and confidence to make informed decisions regarding BC treatment and reconstructive care.

From a policy standpoint, incorporating breast reconstruction counselling into routine oncology care is crucial to support informed decision-making among women diagnosed with breast cancer.47 National clinical guidelines should require that reconstruction options be discussed at the time of diagnosis, rather than depending on either patient initiative or individual provider practices. Concurrently, healthcare professional training should prioritise clear, effective communication that is sensitive to cultural and religious contexts, particularly in addressing the misconceptions and uncertainties highlighted by this study. In addition, context-specific public health initiatives are needed to enhance awareness and facilitate more open discussions about breast reconstruction, especially in relation to societal and religious considerations. Expanding and standardising insurance coverage for reconstructive procedures is also essential to minimise financial barriers and ensure equitable access to care. Together, these strategies have the potential to enhance knowledge, foster more positive attitudes and ultimately increase the utilisation of breast reconstruction services in Jordan.

It is also noteworthy to mention that this study has several limitations. First, the use of face-to-face interviews may introduce interviewer bias, despite standardised training. Second, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference between predictors and outcomes. Third, reliance on self-reported data may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. Additionally, although efforts were made to recruit a diverse sample, the findings may not be fully generalisable to all Jordanian women, particularly those in remote or underserved areas. Future studies employing longitudinal or qualitative designs may provide deeper insights into decision-making processes.

Conclusion

This study highlights persistent knowledge gaps, moderate attitudes and limited engagement in practices related to PMBR among Jordanian women. Cultural norms, religious factors and structural obstacles, such as differences in insurance coverage and access to specialised care, continue to affect decision-making. To tackle these issues, we need to include reconstruction counselling in routine oncology care, improve culturally sensitive communication and broaden public health initiatives that meet local needs. Future research should explore personal viewpoints and assess targeted interventions to improve informed decision-making and increase the use of breast reconstruction services. Together, these steps can empower women, improve access and reduce the psychosocial effects of mastectomy in Jordan. These findings highlight the importance of adding reconstruction counselling to national breast cancer care guidelines, enhancing communication between providers and patients, and addressing financial and structural challenges.
