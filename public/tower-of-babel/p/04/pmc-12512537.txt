Proteins driving liquid-liquid phase separation and histone modifications cooperatively associate with chromatin looping and transcriptional regulation
Authors: Xue Ji, Yumin Nie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) proteins are known to participate in genome organization and transcriptional regulation through the formation of biomolecular condensates, their functional interplay with other regulatory proteins and histone modifications in chromatin loop formation remains poorly characterized. By combining Hi-C chromatin interaction data with ChIP-seq profiles of 12, 27, and 24 LLPS proteins in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cell lines, respectively, we identified chromatin loops associated with LLPS proteins and systematically analysed patterns of cooperative protein binding and histone modification enrichment within these loop-associated peaks.ResultsWe identified 162, 313, and 431 chromatin loops associated with LLPS proteins in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cell lines, respectively. These loops were relatively small in size and predominantly anchored at enhancer regions. Examination of cooperative binding of proteins within loop-associated peaks revealed that transcriptional repressor IKZF1, HDAC1, and SAP130 most frequently co-localized with LLPS proteins in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cells, respectively. Further analysis of histone modification enrichment patterns revealed that active histone modifications, such as H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, and H3K27ac, co-localized at loop-associated peaks, with H3K4me1 exhibiting additional specific co-localization with these four histone modifications at enhancer-localized loop-associated peaks. Notably, bivalent chromatin domains where H3K27me3 co-localized with active histone modifications were identified at promoter-localized loop-associated peaks in HepG2 cells, and elevated H3K27me3 occupancy at these peaks was associated with transcriptional repression of target genes. Moreover, quantitative RNA-seq analysis revealed that the expression of target genes associated with enhancer-promoter loops was correlated with both the binding of LLPS proteins and the enrichment patterns of histone modifications within their ChIP-seq peaks at loop anchors.ConclusionsOur study suggests that LLPS proteins may cooperate with transcriptional repressors to facilitate chromatin looping. Furthermore, local enrichment of histone modifications at loop-associated peaks provides additional regulatory control over chromatin architecture and gene transcription.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13072-025-00632-3.

Background

Eukaryotic genomes are organized into A and B compartmental domains which correspond to transcriptionally active and inactive chromatin, respectively [1]. The kilobase (kb)-sized compartmental domains [2] preferentially interact with other compartmental domains in the same transcriptional or chromatin state, and Hi-C contact maps thus display a plaid-like pattern within and between chromosomes. Phase separation is emerging as the key driver of genomic compartmentalization and plays important roles in controlling higher-order chromatin organization and gene transcription [3, 4]. Liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), a phenomenon in which liquid-like condensates are formed through multivalent interactions between proteins and/or nucleic acids, has been confirmed to influence multiple levels of genome organization including enhancer-promoter (E-P) interactions and heterochromatin formation.

Multiple proteins, including transcriptional coactivators, transcription factors, and RNA-binding proteins, have been experimentally validated to undergo LLPS and subsequently regulate gene transcription via the formation of transcriptional condensates. Two transcriptional coactivators BRD4 and MED1 which contain large intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) form phase-separated condensates at super-enhancers to compartmentalize and concentrate the transcription apparatus [5]. Myeloid factor CEBPA also undergoes IDR-mediated phase separation, which correlates with chromatin compartment switching and the formation of long-range chromatin hubs during induced B cell-to-macrophage transdifferentiation [6]. Transcription factors with IDRs in their activation domains, including the pluripotency factor OCT4, the yeast transcriptional activator GCN4, and the multifunctional protein yin yang 1 (YY1), recruit transcriptional coactivators into biomolecular condensates to drive target gene expression [7, 8]. Multi-colour 3D imaging further reveals that transcriptional condensates dynamically interact with both the enhancer and Sox2 gene locus, and there is a direct anti-correlation between gene burst intensity and condensate-to-gene distance [9]. Besides, the FET family of proteins FUS and TAF15 drive phase separation through interactions between the prion-like domain and RNA-binding domain [10], and interact with the C-terminal domain of RNA polymerase II to regulate gene transcription [11].

Post-translational modifications in histone proteins, mediated by specific enzymes including histone acetyltransferases and deacetylases, also contribute to the dynamic regulation of chromatin structure and function. Both active and repressive histone modifications have been demonstrated to interact with their readers, writers or erasers to regulate chromatin compartmentalization via LLPS. Histone acetylation can cause decompaction of chromatin and highly acetylated chromatin can be induced by multi-bromodomain proteins, such as BRD4, to form distinct droplets which are immiscible with unmodified chromatin droplets [12]. Multivalent interactions between H3K9me3 and chromodomain-containing protein complexes, such as a complex composed of H3K9me3 reader heterochromatin protein 1 and H3K9me3 writer SUV39H1, drive LLPS to regulate chromatin compartmentalization and the formation of heterochromatin [13].

Proteins capable of driving LLPS, hereafter referred to as LLPS proteins, cooperate with histone modifications to shape chromatin structure and modulate gene expression through the formation of biomolecular condensates. Here we specifically focused on the small-scale chromatin organization, investigating how LLPS proteins were associated with the formation of chromatin loops and gene expression in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cell lines. Based on Hi-C data and ChIP-seq data for 12, 27, and 24 LLPS proteins in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cell lines (Additional file 1), respectively, we first identified chromatin loops associated with LLPS proteins and their corresponding ChIP-seq peaks, and analysed co-localization patterns of LLPS and non-LLPS proteins within these loop-associated peaks. Furthermore, we examined the patterns of histone modification enrichment at these peaks to uncover the epigenomic features associated with the formation of chromatin loops. Finally, we assessed the functional impact of LLPS protein-associated loops on gene transcription.

Results

Identification of chromatin loops associated with LLPS proteins

11,457, 17,626, and 19,686 chromatin loops were identified using the HOMER program findTADsAndLoops.pl at 5 kb resolution [14], and the median size was 775 kb, 1 Mb, and 845 kb in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cell lines, respectively. Only 7.1%, 4.9%, and 8.0% of the identified chromatin loops contained convergent CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF) binding sites at both anchors in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cell lines, respectively, demonstrating that a small proportion of chromatin loops were dependent on the CTCF-mediated loop extrusion. However, both anchors of 87.0%, 88.8%, and 90.9% of identified loops in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cell lines, respectively, were located within the same compartment type, suggesting that the great majority of chromatin loops might relate to phase separation.

In order to investigate how LLPS proteins are associated with the loop formation, chromatin loops with both anchors lacking convergent CTCF binding sites, located within the same compartment type (A or B), and overlapping ChIP-seq peaks of LLPS proteins, were considered as loops associated with LLPS proteins. 162, 313, and 431 LLPS protein-associated loops were identified and the median size was 387, 325, and 305 kb in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cell lines, respectively, which was significantly smaller than those loops that were independent of both LLPS proteins and CTCF (Fig. 1A; Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p < 2.2 × 10− 16). Compared with CTCF-associated loops, chromatin loops associated with LLPS proteins were significantly smaller in size in K562 and HepG2 cells (Fig. 1A; Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p < 1.9 × 10− 7).

As LLPS proteins, such as BRD4, have been demonstrated to form phase-separated condensates at super-enhancers [5], we further investigated overlaps between loop anchors and enhancers, and found that 87.7%, 72.8%, and 93.5% of LLPS protein-associated loops in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cell lines, respectively, contained at least one anchor overlapping with enhancers. Specially, the proportions of E-P loops in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cell lines were 52.5%, 29.1%, and 54.8%, respectively, while the proportions of enhancer-enhancer (E-E) loops were 48.8%, 27.2%, and 55.0% in the same cell lines (Fig. 1B and C). Compared with CTCF-associated loops and loops independent of both LLPS proteins and CTCF, LLPS protein-associated loops exhibited higher proportions of E-P and E-E interactions, suggesting that LLPS proteins in our study may play crucial roles in modulating enhancer activity.

Identification of ChIP-seq peaks associated with chromatin looping

For each of the LLPS proteins, ChIP-seq peaks associated with chromatin looping were identified by overlapping peaks with the anchors of LLPS protein-associated loops, and only a small proportion (0.5% to 2.4%) of peaks were found to be associated with loop formation. Histone acetyltransferase p300 (EP300), myc proto-oncogene protein (MYC), and protein argonaute-2 (AGO2) were identified as the LLPS proteins with the highest number of loop-associated peaks in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cell lines, with 217, 409, and 458 peaks, respectively (Fig. 2A). We further investigated whether the loop-associated peaks of each LLPS protein overlapped with promoter or enhancer regions, and found that the loop-associated peaks for most LLPS proteins predominantly overlapped with enhancer regions, particularly for polycomb complex protein BMI1, transcription activator STAT3, and EP300 in GM12878 cells; EP300 and transcriptional enhancer factor TEAD4 in K562 cells; and EP300, TEAD4, and TEAD1 in HepG2 cells (Fig. 2B and C). For interferon regulatory factor IRF3 in GM12878 cells, SP1 in K562 cells, and TATA-binding protein (TBP), BRD4 and FUS in HepG2 cells, higher proportions of loop-associated peaks overlapped with promoter regions. These results suggest that distinct LLPS proteins may preferentially bind to specific genomic regions to facilitate the formation of chromatin loops.

Cooperative binding of proteins within loop-associated peaks of LLPS proteins

In order to characterize the cooperative binding patterns between LLPS proteins and their interaction partners within loop-associated peaks, the degree of peak overlap between loop-associated peaks of each LLPS protein and those of other proteins was measured. We extracted and visualized the top five proteins that most frequently co-localized with each LLPS protein, and found that non-LLPS proteins IKZF1, HDAC1, and SAP130 most frequently co-localized with LLPS proteins in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cells, respectively (Fig. 3). HDAC1 is a histone deacetylase that interacts with multiple proteins to regulate transcription. Previous studies have shown that YY1 interacts with HDAC1/3 to maintain a moderate LLPS state required for METTL3 expression control in acute myeloid leukemia, and disruption of the YY1-HDAC1/3 interaction induces excessive LLPS, leading to aberrant condensate formation and subsequent suppression of METTL3 transcription [15]. Similar to YY1, interactions between LLPS proteins and HDAC1 in K562 cells may facilitate moderate phase separation. IKZF1 is a critical regulator of lymphoid differentiation and mainly regulates gene expression through interactions with the nucleosome remodelling and deacetylase complex including histone deacetylases HDAC1 [16]. SAP130 is a subunit of the histone deacetylase-dependent corepressor complex mSin3A that acts as a transcriptional repressor to regulate the diverse signalling pathways and biological processes [17].

Besides, we observed that proteins MYC and MYC-associated factor X (MAX) frequently co-localized with LLPS proteins in K562 cells (Fig. 3B). MYC undergoes LLPS through its IDRs and a conserved negatively charged region [18], and interacts with MAX to activate the transcription of target genes. The frequent co-localization events between the two proteins and LLPS proteins suggest that the formation of MYC-MAX heterodimer and interactions with other LLPS proteins might play key roles in the formation of chromatin loops in K562 cells.

Histone modifications within loop-associated peaks of LLPS proteins

Histone modifications are closely related to phase separation. We investigated the enrichment of individual histone modifications within loop-associated peaks of LLPS proteins, and found that over 63.1% of peaks were enriched with H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, and H3K27ac, while more than 49.9% were enriched with H3K4me1 in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cells, suggesting that loop-associated peaks of LLPS proteins are generally enriched with active histone modifications. Besides, proteins whose peaks preferentially overlapped promoter regions showed higher enrichment of H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, and H3K27ac, while those with peaks preferentially overlapping enhancer regions displayed greater enrichment of H3K4me1 (Fig. 4). For example, TBP and EP300 are known to bind preferentially to promoter and enhancer regions, respectively. Consistently, our analysis revealed that TBP was more enriched with H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, and H3K27ac, while EP300 exhibited greater enrichment of H3K4me1 in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cells. Finally, repressive histone modification H3K27me3 was markedly enriched within loop-associated peaks in HepG2 cells. Specially, more than 47.6% of peaks for TBP, ring finger protein 2 (RNF2), lysine-specific demethylase 5 A (KDM5A), and TAF15 were enriched with H3K27me3, suggesting that these LLPS proteins may be associated with Polycomb-mediated chromatin looping.

We subsequently classified loop-associated peaks into three groups according to their presence in promoter, enhancer, or other genomic regions, and found that loop-associated peaks within promoter regions were preferentially enriched with H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, H3K27ac, and H3K79me2, and those within enhancer regions were more enriched with H3K4me1 and H3K4me2 in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cells (Fig. 5). These results, consistent with previous studies [19], demonstrate that the enrichment patterns of histone modifications within loop-associated peaks are strongly associated with their genomic localization. Besides, compared with the other two cell types, HepG2 cells exhibited higher H3K27me3 enrichment within loop-associated peaks across distinct genomic regions. Notably, 50.1% of peaks within promoter regions were H3K27me3-enriched, suggesting that these promoter-associated loops likely form through Polycomb-mediated chromatin interactions and may potentially regulate gene expression in HepG2 cells (Fig. 5C).

In order to characterize the combinatorial patterns of histone modifications within loop-associated peaks, pairwise cosine similarities among ten histone modifications were computed for loop-associated peaks located within promoter and enhancer regions. H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, and H3K27ac showed strong pairwise correlations (r >0.63) in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cells, with particularly stronger correlations (r >0.87) at promoter-localized loop-associated peaks (Fig. 6), suggesting that the four histone modifications often co-occur within loop-associated peaks. Compared with promoter-localized loop-associated peaks, H3K4me1 showed stronger correlations with H3K4me2, H3K9ac, and H3K27ac (r >0.67) at enhancer-localized loop-associated peaks in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cells, recapitulating previous observations of H3K4me1 enrichment at enhancers [19]. Notably, H3K27me3 exhibited distinct cell-type-specific correlation patterns: while demonstrating only weak correlations (r < 0.10) with other histone modifications in GM12878 and K562 cells, it showed strong correlations (r >0.69) with H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, and H3K27ac at promoter-localized loop-associated peaks in HepG2 cells (Fig. 6C), suggesting the existence of cell-type-specific bivalent chromatin domains co-marked by both active and repressive histone modifications.

Expression of target genes associated with E-P loops

Quantitative RNA-seq analysis revealed significantly lower expression of E-P loop-associated target genes in HepG2 compared with GM12878 cells (Fig. 7A; Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p < 0.05). Promoter regions participating in E-P loop formation contained 358 LLPS protein peaks, with 58.4% showing H3K27me3 enrichment in HepG2 cells (Fig. 7B). Promoter-localized loop-associated peaks were further classified into two groups based on H3K27me3 enrichment status, to investigate whether elevated H3K27me3 occupancy at these peaks was associated with transcriptional repression of target genes. Although both peak groups displayed comparable enrichment of active histone modifications H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, and H3K27ac (Fig. 7C), target genes associated with H3K27me3-enriched peaks showed significantly lower expression levels than those linked to H3K27me3-depleted peaks (Fig. 7D; Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p < 3.0 × 10− 6), consistent with the known repressive function of bivalent chromatin domains [20].

In HepG2 cells, H3K27me3 enrichment at promoter-localized loop-associated peaks primarily marked repressor activity, with 72.6% of these modified peaks showing repressor binding. Among these, AGO2, TAF15, EZH2, and RNF2 exhibited the highest binding frequency, collectively accounting for 63.1% of all repressor-associated peaks. In order to systematically evaluate the regulatory impact of LLPS proteins on gene expression, we categorized them as activators or repressors and identified active activator and repressor binding sites based on characteristic histone modification patterns within ChIP-seq peaks across distinct cellular contexts. Active activator binding sites were characterized by enrichment of H3K4me1, H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, or H3K27ac, whereas active repressor sites were marked by H3K9me3 or H3K27me3 within ChIP-seq peaks. We further classified E-P loops into three distinct categories based on the relative abundance of active activator versus repressor binding sites at their anchors: (1) active-active (both anchors with predominant activator binding sites), (2) acitve-repressive (one anchor predominantly bound by activators and the other by repressors), and (3) repressive-repressive (both anchors with predominant repressor binding sites). We found that target genes associated with active-active E-P loops exhibited cell-type-specific frequencies (GM12878: 48.7%; K562: 41.9%; HepG2: 19.7%) and significantly higher expression levels than those linked to active-repressive loops across all three cell lines (Fig. 8A; Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p < 0.05), suggesting that dual-activator E-P loops promote transcriptional activation. Besides, target genes associated with active-repressive loops showed significantly higher expression levels compared with repressive-repressive loop-associated genes in HepG2 cells (Fig. 8A; Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p < 0.05), suggesting that dual-repressor E-P loops suppress transcriptional activation. In GM12878 and K562 cells, zero and two repressive-repressive loop-associated genes were identified, respectively. Given the insufficient sample size for statistically meaningful evaluation, both gene sets were excluded from our analysis. The combinatorial effect of increased active-active and decreased repressive-repressive E-P loops likely contributes to the significantly elevated expression levels in GM12878 cells relative to K562 and HepG2 cells (Fig. 7A; Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p < 0.05).

To determine whether the observed effects were specific to LLPS proteins, we computationally excluded this protein category and applied the identical analytical framework to non-LLPS proteins to assess their associations with gene expression. E-P loops were categorized as active-active, active-repressive, or repressive-repressive based on functional annotations of non-LLPS proteins and histone modification enrichment patterns within their ChIP-seq peaks, and the expression levels of target genes associated with active-active and active-repressive E-P loops did not differ significantly in either K562 or HepG2 cells (Fig. 8B). These results suggest that the binding of LLPS proteins and the presence of specific histone modifications at loop anchors critically regulate the expression of genes associated with E-P loops.

Discussion

Through self-association or heterotypic interactions, LLPS proteins facilitate the formation of chromatin loops and modulate transcriptional regulation. Our analysis reveals that LLPS protein-associated chromatin loops preferentially form at shorter genomic distances and are strikingly enriched for enhancer-associated interactions, as evidenced by the higher abundance of E-P and E-E loops, implying that these loops may functionally contribute to enhancer activity. The functional relevance of these loops is further supported by the coordinated enrichment of active histone modifications at loop-associated peaks. Active histone modifications, such as H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, and H3K27ac, co-localize at loop-associated peaks, while H3K4me1 additionally co-localizes with these marks specifically at enhancer-localized loop-associated peaks. Notably, cell-type-specific bivalent chromatin domains where H3K27me3 co-localizes with active histone modifications were identified at promoter-localized loop-associated peaks in HepG2 cells. The enrichment of H3K27me3 at these loci correlates with transcriptional repression of target genes, suggesting that these promoter-associated loops may represent poised regulatory states, and the dynamic balance between repressive and active histone modifications fine-tunes transcriptional outcomes [20]. More generally, our results indicate that the expression of genes associated with E-P loops correlates with the binding of LLPS proteins and the enrichment patterns of histone modifications within their ChIP-seq peaks at loop anchors. Specifically, active-active E-P loops are associated with transcriptional activation, whereas repressive-repressive loops are linked to transcriptional repression. However, the role of LLPS proteins in chromatin loop formation and transcriptional regulation, as indicated by our analysis, remains correlative rather than causative. Direct experimental approaches are required to establish a causal relationship linking LLPS protein binding to chromatin looping and transcriptional regulation. To systematically elucidate the formation, material properties, and functional roles of biomolecular condensates associated with LLPS proteins, we propose a multi-tiered experimental strategy for future investigation. First, live-cell fluorescence microscopy and fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) will be utilized to characterize the spatiotemporal dynamics and internal mobility of these condensates. Subsequently, immunofluorescence combined with DNA and nascent RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization will be applied to correlate condensate localization with chromatin loop formation and transcription of target genes [5]. Finally, to establish functional necessity of LLPS proteins, one or more targeted perturbation approaches will be employed to disrupt condensate integrity and dynamics. These may include structure-guided mutations in IDRs of LLPS proteins, CRISPR-based knockout or degradation of LLPS proteins, optogenetic control of condensate disassembly using CasDrop [21], and incorporation of a killswitch peptide sequence for condensate arrest [22]. The functional impact of these perturbations will be evaluated using Hi-C to assess changes in chromatin interactions and RNA-seq to quantify alternations in transcriptional programs.

Although loop-associated peaks of LLPS proteins are predominantly marked by active histone modifications, we unexpectedly found that transcriptional repressors, including IKZF1 in GM12878 cells, HDAC1 in K562 cells, and SAP130 in HepG2 cells, exhibit frequent co-localization with LLPS proteins at chromatin loop anchors. These spatial associations suggest that repressors may dynamically engage with phase-separated condensates to modulate both genome architecture and transcriptional regulation. The cell-type-specific recruitment of distinct repressors implies lineage-dependent strategies for balancing transcriptional activation and repression within phase-separated compartments, though the observed differences may be partially attributable to variations in ChIP-seq data availability across cell types. Existing evidence indicates that the YY1-HDAC1 interaction establishes moderate LLPS, which is necessary for proper METTL3 expression in acute myeloid leukemia [15]. Our results further suggest that the recruitment of transcriptional repressors into condensates may represent a broader mechanism operating across diverse cellular contexts. However, additional experimental validation is required to determine whether repressors are selectively recruited into condensates formed by LLPS proteins, and to evaluate the functional consequences of such recruitment on transcriptional output. Live-cell fluorescence microscopy and immunofluorescence represent essential experimental approaches for the direct visualization and validation of repressor recruitment into condensates within dynamic live-cell and fixed-cell environments, respectively.

Our analysis reveals that anchors of ~ 90% of chromatin loops are located within the same compartment type and lack convergent CTCF binding sites, strongly suggesting that phase separation mechanisms play a fundamental role in loop formation. However, only a small fraction (< 2.2%) of these loops are directly associated with known LLPS proteins. This apparent paradox may arise from two primary considerations. First, chromatin loops were identified as LLPS protein-associated when both anchors satisfied three criteria: (1) lacking convergent CTCF binding sites, (2) located within the same compartment type, and (3) overlapping ChIP-seq peaks of LLPS proteins. The lack of comprehensive ChIP-seq data for all known LLPS proteins significantly constrains our ability to fully evaluate their contributions to chromatin loop formation. Due to current limitations in ChIP-seq data availability, our analysis was restricted to 12, 27, and 24 LLPS proteins in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cells, respectively. We anticipate that the inclusion of additional LLPS proteins in future studies will enable the identification of more chromatin loops linked to LLPS mechanisms. Second, we identified a distinct subset of chromatin loops (~ 9%) that satisfied the first two criteria but displayed LLPS protein binding at only one anchor. These loops may form through asymmetric LLPS protein recruitment mechanisms, but were excluded by our stringent classification criteria.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that chromatin loops associated with LLPS proteins are predominantly anchored at enhancer regions. Although transcriptional repressors frequently co-localize with LLPS proteins at the loop-associated peaks, these peaks are markedly enriched with active histone modifications (H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, and H3K27ac), with H3K4me1 exhibiting specific co-occupancy with the four active modifications at enhancer-localized loop-associated peaks. Moreover, our study reveals that the expression of target genes associated with E-P loops is correlated with both the binding of LLPS proteins and the enrichment patterns of histone modifications within their ChIP-seq peaks at loop anchors. Notably, cell-type-specific bivalent chromatin domains at promoter-localized loop-associated peaks are associated with transcriptional repression of their target genes in HepG2 cells. Together, these findings suggest that LLPS protein binding, transcriptional repressor co-localization, and histone modifications may coordinately facilitate chromatin loop formation and regulate cell-type-specific transcriptional programs.

Materials and methods

Data source

ChIP-seq peak data for 133, 316, and 265 proteins in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cell lines, respectively, were retrieved from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus under accession number GSE104247 [23] and the UCSC Genome Browser database (https://hgdownload.soe.ucsc.edu/goldenPath/hg19/database/). All ChIP-seq peak coordinates were aligned to the hg19 human genome assembly. For each protein in a cell line, peaks identified across multiple experimental replicates from independent laboratories were merged.

Enhancer coordinates for three cell lines (49,672 in GM12878, 43,148 in K562, and 50,160 in HepG2), based on the hg19 genome assembly, were obtained from the EnhancerAtlas2.0 database (http://www.enhanceratlas.org/) [24]. A total of 51,384 promoter regions (5 kb upstream of transcription start sites) corresponding to UCSC-annotated genes were retrieved from the UCSC Genome Table Browser (http://genome.ucsc.edu/cgi-bin/hgTables).

Raw paired-end sequencing data from in situ Hi-C experiments performed in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cell lines were obtained from the ENCODE portal [25] with the assay accession number ENCSR410MDC, ENCSR545YBD, and ENCSR194SRI [26, 27], respectively. Raw reads were initially processed by trimming the 3′ end to the GATC restriction site motif, followed by alignment to the hg19 human genome assembly using Bowtie2 [28]. Uniquely mapped reads with mapping quality scores larger than 10 were kept, and read pairs with identical 5′ and 3′ mapping coordinates were only considered once to eliminate PCR amplification artefacts. The HOMER program [14] makeTagDirectory was further implemented in two sequential runs: first to generate tag directories from filtered reads, and second to remove small fragments and self-ligations with the parameters -removePEbg -removeSpikes 10,000 5. Chromatin loops were finally identified using the HOMER program findTADsAndLoops.pl at 5 kb resolution, while A/B compartmental domains were called using runHiCpca.pl at 25 kb resolution.

Aligned ChIP-seq BAM files for ten histone modifications (H3K4me1, H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, H3K27ac, H3K9me3, H3K27me3, H3K36me3, H3K79me2, and H4K20me1) in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cell lines were downloaded from the UCSC Genome Browser database (https://hgdownload.soe.ucsc.edu/goldenPath/hg19/encodeDCC/wgEncodeBroadHistone/). For each histone modification, modification levels within protein ChIP-seq peaks were measured by counting overlapping reads using bedtools coverage [29].

Aligned paired-end RNA-seq data for GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cell lines [30], generated by the Wold Lab, were obtained from the UCSC Genome Browser database (http://hgdownload.soe.ucsc.edu/goldenPath/hg19/encodeDCC/wgEncodeCaltechRnaSeq/). Expression levels of all UCSC-annotated genes were quantified using Cufflinks [31] with default parameters.

Classification of LLPS and non-LLPS proteins

Proteins were classified as LLPS-associated based on their inclusion in PhaSepDB [32], a manually curated database that compiles experimentally verified LLPS proteins from published literature. Each of the reviewed LLPS proteins in PhaSepDB has been experimentally validated through in vitro reconstitution of LLPS condensates from purified components, in vitro or in vivo FRAP, and/or in vivo immunofluorescence imaging. By intersecting the 410 human LLPS proteins from PhaSepDB with ChIP-seq-annotated proteins, we identified 12, 27, and 24 overlapping proteins (Additional file 1) in GM12878, K562, and HepG2 cell lines, respectively, which were classified as LLPS proteins in our study. All other proteins with available ChIP-seq data were designated as non-LLPS proteins.

Identification of chromatin loops associated with LLPS proteins

Phase separation might contribute to the formation of chromatin loops whose anchors were located within the same compartment type and lacked convergently oriented CTCF binding sites. In order to identify chromatin loops associated with LLPS proteins, we first excluded CTCF-associated loops, characterized by convergent CTCF binding sites at both loop anchors. The orientation of CTCF binding sites was determined by scanning DNA sequences corresponding to CTCF ChIP-seq peak regions using FIMO with a p-value threshold of 0.001 [33]. The remaining chromatin loops whose both anchors were located within the same compartment type and overlapped with ChIP-seq peaks of LLPS proteins were subsequently identified as LLPS protein-associated. Classification of E-P and E-E loops

The classification of E-P and E-E loops was based on the genomic overlap between loop anchors and known promoter/enhancer annotations. An E-P loop was classified as a chromatin loop with one anchor overlapping an enhancer and the other overlapping a promoter, while an E-E loop was defined as a loop whose both anchors overlapped enhancers. Since a single loop anchor could simultaneously overlap with both promoters and enhancers, some loops were classified as both E-P and E-E loops.

Co-binding patterns of proteins within loop-associated peaks of LLPS proteins

The percentage of overlapping ChIP-seq peaks between each LLPS protein and other proteins was calculated using bedtools intersect [29], and the five proteins exhibiting the highest co-localization frequency with each LLPS protein were identified and visualized using Cytoscape [34].

Classification of activator and repressors

A total of 2,164 human activators and 1,144 repressors were first retrieved from the Uniprot Knowledgebase [36] using the search terms “activator AND (organism_id:9606) AND (reviewed: true)” and “repressor AND (organism_id:9606) AND (reviewed: true)”, respectively. Both LLPS and non-LLPS proteins were classified as activators or repressors by intersecting with the curated activators or repressors from Uniprot Knowledgebase. For multifunctional proteins annotated as both activators and repressors, histone modification enrichment patterns within ChIP-seq peaks were further incorporated to resolve functional ambiguity. Multifunctional proteins exhibiting enrichment of H3K4me1, H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, or H3K27ac within their ChIP-seq peaks were classified as activators, while those associated with H3K9me3 or H3K27me3-enriched regions were designated as repressors.
