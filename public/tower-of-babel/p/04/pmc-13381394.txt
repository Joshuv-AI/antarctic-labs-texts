Mindfulness-based cognitive-behavioral therapy improves sexual quality of life and reduces sexual distress in pregnant women
Authors: Mina Iravani, Shahla Molavi, Saeed Ghanbari, Hedye Ahmadiyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivePregnancy is accompanied by numerous physical and psychological changes that can affect sexual quality of life and increase sexual distress in women. Identifying effective interventions to address these issues is essential. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of mindfulness-based cognitive-behavioral therapy on sexual distress and sexual quality of life in pregnant women.MethodsIn this single-blind randomized clinical trial, 84 pregnant women (20–35 weeks of gestation) were randomly assigned to an intervention group (n = 40) or a control group (n = 41) using a block design. The intervention group participated in seven weekly sessions of mindfulness-based cognitive therapy. Data were collected using a demographic questionnaire, the Sexual Quality of Life–Female (SQOL-F) questionnaire, and the Female Sexual Distress Scale (FSDS).ResultAt baseline, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in demographic characteristics, sexual distress, or sexual quality of life. Repeated measures analysis showed significant improvements in the intervention group compared with the control group in overall sexual quality of life and its dimensions (psychosexual feelings, sexual and relationship satisfaction, self-worthlessness, and sexual repression). Sexual distress scores were also significantly reduced in the intervention group after the intervention and at follow-up.DiscussionMindfulness-based cognitive-behavioral therapy appears to be an effective approach for enhancing sexual quality of life and reducing sexual distress in pregnant women. Integrating this intervention into prenatal care services may help promote the overall well-being of expectant mothers.Trial registrationThis study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences (IR.AJUMS.REC.1399.135) and registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20200901048581N1) on September 21, 2020.Keywords: Pregnant women, Quality of sexual life, Sexual distress, Mindfulness-based cognitive-behavioral therapy

Introduction

Pregnancy and childbirth can be a profound and empowering experience in a woman’s life, fostering emotional well-being, maternal confidence, and a positive transition into motherhood when supported by respectful care and informed preparation [1]. At the same time, however, it is considered a stressful experience, leading to major psychological and physical changes in women [2, 3]. During this period, many factors influenced the change in sexual relations; Among them, we can mention the anatomical and physiological changes in pregnant women, the change in a woman’s self-perception of her body, the feeling of decreasing attractiveness for her husband, the sense of guilt for having sex during this period, and the fear of the consequences of sexual relations on the consequences of pregnancy. Pregnancy is accompanied by a range of physiological, emotional, and relational changes that may negatively affect sexual response and intimacy between partners. Factors such as altered body image, fatigue, and fear of harming the fetus can disrupt sexual desire and satisfaction [4, 5]. One of the most prevalent psychological challenges during pregnancy is sexual distress, which encompasses negative emotions related to one’s sexual life, including guilt, frustration, anxiety, shame, and ange. Recent studies show that sexual distress is significantly associated with reduced sexual satisfaction, avoidance of sexual activity, and impaired relationship quality. These findings highlight the importance of addressing sexual health and emotional well-being during pregnancy through supportive care and open communication [6, 7].

Recent evidence indicates that sexual activity tends to decline progressively during pregnancy, with the lowest frequency observed in the third trimester. In a 2025 cross-sectional study of 171 healthy pregnant women, dyadic sexual desire markedly declined in the first trimester, partially recovered in the second trimester, and decreased again in the third trimester, with no cases reporting an increase compared to pre-pregnancy levels [8]. Unfavorable sexual performance may negatively affect a woman’s quality of life, emotional well-being, mental health, self-confidence, interpersonal relationships, and social relationships [9, 10]. One of the important physical and psychological dimensions of women’s quality of life is the quality of people’s sexual life and it plays a decisive role in the life and health of women [11]. The sexual quality of life is one of the key issues in the field of sexual health and fertility [12] and similar to the concept of quality of life, which is defined as people’s understanding of their position in terms of culture, standards, value system, priorities, goals and expectations [13]; It is based on people’s understanding of their sexual affairs [14]. Physiological changes during pregnancy, including hormonal fluctuations, alterations in body image, and physical discomfort, may influence a pregnant woman’s perception and understanding of her sexual life. Such changes can affect her awareness of sexual needs, comfort levels, and expectations within intimate relationships [15].

Pregnant women suffer from sexual dysfunction due to physical factors such as nausea and vomiting in the first trimester, increased belly size in the third trimester, psychological factors, and physical discomfort related to intimacy. Reducing the frequency or lack of sexual activity during pregnancy may have negative effects on the emotional relationships of couples and lead to a decrease in affection and a decrease in the quality of women’s sexual life and make them irritable [15, 16]. Therefore, compared to men, women’s sexual quality of life is more sensitive during pregnancy because they directly experience the physiological and hormonal changes, such as fluctuations in estrogen and progesterone levels, physical discomfort, and altered body image, that can influence sexual desire, arousal, and comfort. In addition, pregnancy-related psychological factors, including anxiety about fetal health and changes in self-esteem, may further affect women’s sexual experiences more profoundly than their male partners [17].

Therefore, the sexual quality of life and sexual distress have a unique contribution to couples’ relationships; thus, appropriate counseling is essential for better mental and physical adaptation to the changes during pregnancy [18, 19]. Today, various therapeutic approaches have been used to address sexual dysfunction and improve sexual quality of life in pregnant women, including sexual rehabilitation [20], correcting ineffective sexual beliefs, sexual education [21], correcting ineffective sexual beliefs, sexual education [22]. and sexual counseling [19]. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) has also been shown to be effective in modifying maladaptive thoughts and behaviors related to sexuality. In addition, group counseling [23] particularly mindfulness-based interventions, is another treatment method that can reduce sexual distress and enhance sexual well-being [24]. CBT is a structured, goal-oriented psychotherapy that focuses on identifying and modifying dysfunctional thoughts, emotions, and behaviors. In the context of sexual health, CBT helps individuals challenge maladaptive beliefs about sexuality, reduce anxiety, and develop healthier coping strategies. Mindfulness-based cognitive-behavioral therapy (MBCT) integrates the principles of CBT with mindfulness practices, aiming to increase present-moment awareness and reduce automatic negative thinking patterns. This combination is particularly relevant for pregnant women, as it addresses both the cognitive distortions and the emotional distress that may arise from physical and psychological changes during pregnancy, thereby potentially improving sexual quality of life and reducing sexual distress [23].

Practicing mindfulness leads to the development of people’s psychological well-being, which has immediate effects on increasing mood and has long-term effects on the quality of life [25]. Among the consequences of mindfulness exercises, it can be mentioned that people realize that most thoughts, feelings, and emotions are volatile and fleeting and pass like the waves of the sea [26]. Mindfulness-based interventions are increasingly applied in both physical and mental health contexts to enhance quality of life and alleviate psychological distress [26]. Evidence suggests that such approaches can improve coping skills, emotional regulation, and overall well-being [27]. Pregnancy is a critical period in a woman’s life, during which significant physical, hormonal, and psychological changes can influence sexual quality of life. Limited knowledge among couples about sexual relationships during pregnancy may contribute to misunderstandings, reduced intimacy, and increased distress [28].

In this regard, Hosseini et al. [29] reported that mindfulness training significantly improved women’s quality of life. Similarly, Najiabhary et al. [30] found that mindfulness-based programs reduced psychological symptoms and enhanced relationship satisfaction in pregnant women. Li et al. [31] also reported that reproductive quality of life increases in pregnant women. However most studies focused on the overall quality of life and reproductive quality of life, and the studies conducted on the sexual quality of life and sexual distress were few, which is a knowledge gap in this field [29–31]. Also, the conducted studies mainly investigated the entire population of pregnant women and did not investigate the population at risk of sexual disorders [32]. Therefore, there is a need to educate and evaluate the sexual quality of life and sexual distress. Finally, given the importance of this issue and the limited research on the impact of mindfulness on the sexual quality of life and sexual distress, this research was conducted to determine the effect of CBTbased on mindfulness on the distress and sexual quality of life of pregnant women.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study used a randomized clinical trial design with a control group conducted from July 2020 to February 2021. The statistical population of this study includes all pregnant women from 20 to 35 weeks who receive prenatal care at health and treatment centers of Gharb of 11th and 17th of Shahrivar city of Ahvaz, Iran.

Participants

In this study, participants were pregnant women who were purposefully selected from the target population based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to recruit individuals who had both a low sexual quality of life and a high level of sexual distress. The gestational age range of 20 to 35 weeks was chosen to avoid the early stages of pregnancy, when symptoms such as nausea and fatigue are more prevalent, as well as the final weeks before delivery, when physical discomfort and medical restrictions may interfere with participation. The age range of 20 to 35 years was selected to focus on women in the most common reproductive age group and to minimize the influence of age-related fertility or health issues. Only nulliparous and primiparous women were included to reduce variability related to previous childbirth experiences.

All participants were legally married and living with their spouses to ensure the presence of an active marital relationship, as the study variables were directly related to sexual interactions within marriage. Additional inclusion criteria were the ability to read and write, the absence of medical restrictions on sexual intercourse, a score of 11 or higher on the Female Sexual Distress Scale (FSDS), indicating clinically relevant sexual distress, and a score below the normative mean for the Iranian population on the Sexual Quality of Life–Female (SQOL-F) questionnaire, indicating reduced sexual quality of life.

Women were excluded if they had experienced a major adverse life event in the past six months, such as the death of a close relative or a severe accident resulting in disability, as such events could independently affect psychological well-being and sexual functioning. Other exclusion criteria included having a diagnosed physical or mental illness, experiencing obstetric complications during the current pregnancy, addiction to alcohol, narcotics, or other psychoactive substances, and the use of medications other than prenatal supplements or contraceptives during pregnancy. A history of sexual dysfunction in either the participant or her spouse before pregnancy was also an exclusion criterion; This information was obtained through self-report during the initial screening interview.

The sample size was calculated using med_calc statistical software based on the findings of the study by Abdi Malikabadi et al. [33] with 95% power, 5% error, and 70 cases (35 cases in each group). Including 20% ​​dropout, the number of samples was 40 people for each group.

Initially, 300 pregnant women attending routine prenatal visits and meeting the basic eligibility criteria (gestational age 20–35 weeks, age 20–35 years, legally married, and living with their spouse) were invited to take part in the screening phase. All 300 women completed the two screening instruments, FSDS and SQOL-F questionnaire, to determine their eligibility for the intervention phase.

Based on the predefined criteria, women scoring below 11 on the FSDS (indicating no clinically significant sexual distress) and/or scoring above the societal average on the SQOL-F (indicating normal or high sexual quality of life) were excluded. Of the 300 women screened, 112 were excluded for these reasons: 68 scored below 11 on the FSDS, 31 scored above the societal average on the SQOL-F, and 13 met both exclusion conditions. In addition, 4 eligible women declined to participate in the intervention phase.

This process resulted in 84 eligible participants, who were randomly assigned to either the intervention group (n = 42) or the control group (n = 42) using a four-block (2 × 2) randomization design. During the study, 2 participants from the intervention group and 1 from the control group were excluded from the final analysis due to incomplete questionnaire responses. Consequently, the final dataset comprised 40 participants in the intervention group and 41 in the control group (Fig. 1).

During the intervention phase, the experimental group attended seven structured group training sessions based on cognitive-behavioral therapy with a mindfulness approach, delivered by the primary researcher under the supervision of a qualified consultant. The control group did not receive any counseling during the study period. The trial followed a single-blind design: the facilitator delivering the intervention was unaware of the participants’ group assignments, and the statistical analysis was conducted by an independent researcher blinded to group allocation.

Data collection

Data were collected using three instruments: a demographic information form, the Female Sexual Quality of Life Questionnaire (SQOL-F), and the Female Sexual Distress Scale (FSDS). The demographic form included questions on the woman’s and her husband’s ages, length of marriage, ethnic background, educational attainment of both partners, occupations, economic status, living arrangements, bedroom conditions, number of pregnancies, whether the current pregnancy was desired or undesired, and the frequency of sexual intercourse per week.

The SQOL-F was developed by Symonds et al. [34] to evaluate the connection between sexual dysfunction and the sexual quality of life in women. This self-administered instrument was validated for use within the Iranian population by Masoumi et al. in 2013. The questionnaire, which consists of 18 items, emphasizes dimensions such as women’s sexual confidence and emotional and relational beliefs. It is organized into four primary sections: Psychosexual Feelings, Sexual and Relationship Satisfaction, Self-Worthlessness, and Sexual Repression. In the context of this study, the total score can range from 18 to 108 [15]. Each item is rated on a Likert scale from 1 to 6, where 1 indicates strong agreement and 6 indicates strong disagreement. A higher total score reflects a better quality of sexual life. The validity of this questionnaire in Iran was further corroborated by Pakpour [35], who also established its reliability using the internal consistency method, yielding Cronbach’s alpha coefficients between 0.84 and 0.98. Both Persian versions of the SQOL-F, validated in Iran, demonstrated similar psychometric properties. In the present study, we used the version validated by Masoumi et al. (2013).

The FSDS was developed by Derogatis et al. to measure sexual distress in women. It consists of 13 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = never to 4 = always), producing a total score between 0 and 52; higher scores indicate greater sexual distress [36]. A score of 11 or above is considered indicative of clinically significant sexual distress. In the present study, we used the Persian version validated by Shairi et al. [37], which showed excellent internal consistency (α = 0.94) and high test-retest reliability (r = 0.89). This version has also demonstrated satisfactory differential validity in distinguishing women with normal sexual function from those with sexual dysfunction, and appropriate divergent validity when compared with the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI-6).

Intervention

Participants who met the inclusion criteria were randomly allocated into two groups, intervention (n = 42) and control (n = 42), using a four-block (2 × 2) randomization design. Baseline data collection was performed before the start of the intervention, with all participants completing the demographic questionnaire, the SQOL-F, and the FSDS.

The intervention group attended seven weekly group sessions of mindfulness-based cognitive-behavioral therapy(MBCT), each lasting 90 min, conducted by the primary researcher under the supervision of a qualified consultant. Sessions were held in the therapy classrooms of selected medical centers on fixed days each week. Each session included a review of homework from the previous week (such as recording pleasant and unpleasant events), delivery of MBCT-related concepts, and practical mindfulness exercises. The content was compiled from evidence-based sources, and participants were instructed to practice the techniques daily for six days a week using the provided booklet.

The control group did not receive any counseling during the intervention period. Outcome measures (SQOL-F and FSDS) were administered at three time points: T1, before the intervention (baseline), T2, immediately after the seventh session (week 7), and T3, at follow-up, one month after the intervention.

After completion of all data collection and following ethical considerations, the control group was offered two intensive educational sessions covering the same MBCT content, and each participant received the educational booklet as a token of appreciation for their participation. The content and structure of the MBCT sessions (Table 1) were adapted from the standard protocol developed by Segal, Williams, and Teasdale (2013) and have been validated in previous clinical and educational studies [38, 39].

Ethical considerations

This research received approval from the Ethics Committee of Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences in Ahvaz (IR.AJUMS.REC.1399.135) and was registered with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20200901048581N1; September 21, 2020). All participants provided written informed consent. The study adhered to ethical standards throughout its duration. Confidentiality of participants’ information was ensured, and they retained the right to withdraw at any time. Counseling sessions were conducted in a private setting, and all COVID‑19 health protocols, including appropriate social distancing, were strictly followed.

Following ethical considerations, after completion of the second follow-up assessment and closure of data collection, the control group was offered the same MBCT program as the intervention group. This consisted of seven weekly 90-minute group sessions delivered by the same instructor, following the identical session structure and content described in Table 1. Each control group participant also received the same educational booklet used in the intervention group, to support daily practice between sessions. This ensured that all participants had equal access to the potential benefits of the intervention, without influencing the study outcomes.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted utilizing SPSS version 22. The Shapiro-Wilk test indicated that both sexual quality of life and sexual distress exhibited a normal distribution. The Independent t-test was employed to compare the quantitative demographic variables, including age, husband’s age, duration of marriage, and frequency of intercourse per week, between the pregnant women in the experimental group and those in the control group. For the qualitative demographic variables such as ethnicity, education, occupation, husband’s occupation, economic status, independent residence, independent bedroom, and type of pregnancy—the Chi-square test was applied for comparison between the two groups. Furthermore, repeated measures analysis was utilized to assess the trends in sexual quality of life and sexual distress variables across both the control and intervention groups. A significance level of P < 0.05 was established for statistical relevance.

Discussion

The repeated measures analysis revealed a statistically significant improvement in psychosexual feelings in the intervention group compared to the control group, suggesting that MBCT had a beneficial effect on this specific dimension of sexual quality of life. Supporting this conclusion, the research conducted by Basharpoor et al. [26] indicated that mindfulness-based stress reduction training effectively enhanced the psychological well-being and quality of life among pregnant women, aligning with the results of the present study. Additionally, Mirzaee et al. [40] found that a multi-faceted educational program, which included mindfulness as a component, positively influenced the psychosexual feelings of pregnant women [41]. Given that psychosexual feelings pertain to women’s perceptions of their sexual experiences and acknowledging that mindfulness practices can assist individuals in recognizing the transient nature of thoughts, feelings, and emotions, it is crucial to address this matter specifically in pregnant women [26]. In light of the favorable outcomes associated with MBCT, this approach may serve as an effective means to enhance these feelings. By cultivating a non-judgmental awareness of their thoughts, feelings, and emotions, this may have contributed to a more positive perception of psychosexual feelings, although further qualitative research is needed to explore this mechanism.

The findings of the current study indicated a notable statistical difference between the two groups regarding satisfaction and the relationship stemming from sexual activity among pregnant women. This suggests that MBCT effectively influences this aspect. In a study by Sarbandi Farahani et al. [42], it was demonstrated that a nine-session intervention focused on mindfulness-based childbirth and parenting education resulted in a significant change in marital satisfaction from pre-test to post-test, aligning with the outcomes of the present research. Furthermore, Kumar et al. [43] found that the enhancement of mindfulness skills contributes to increased satisfaction and improved relationships related to sexual activity, despite employing a different type of intervention. Satisfaction in relationships and sexual activity encompasses both the quantity and quality of sexual interactions, as well as positive self-regard and interpersonal connections such as intimacy. Additionally, social support, which includes cognitive-behavioral therapies, is linked to elevated well-being and marital satisfaction [44]. In conclusion, MBCT improved sexual quality of life and reduced sexual distress in pregnant women. While these benefits were observed up to one month after the intervention, it is possible that they may persist beyond pregnancy; however, confirming such long-term effects requires further longitudinal studies.

The results of the present study showed a significant statistical difference between the two groups in terms of the self-worthlessness of pregnant women. These results indicate that the MBCT intervention was effective in improving overall sexual quality of life in this sample of pregnant women. The study by Mohammadi et al. [28] showed that training had a positive effect on reducing the score of dysfunctional sexual beliefs and increasing the score of sexual quality of life. Consistent with the present study, Barez [45] found that mindfulness was effective in reducing the self-worthlessness of pregnant women. Self-worthlessness assesses the creation of negative feelings such as loss of self-confidence and guilt [15]. Self-worthlessness can be considered a dysfunctional belief and a worrying and anxiety-provoking feeling. Since MBCT can reduce the tendency to respond to worrying thoughts, using this therapeutic method for pregnant women can reduce self-worthlessness, although further studies are needed. Given the significant negative impact that self-worthlessness can have on pregnant women’s mental health, addressing this issue with MBCT could have important implications for improving their overall psychological well-being during a critical time of their lives.

The results of the present study showed a significant statistical difference between the two groups in terms of the sexual repression of pregnant women. Therefore, MBCT was effective on this variable. Alexakos et al. [46] also reported that mindfulness skills training can reduce sexual repression in pregnant women. The study by Salehzadeh et al. [47] showed that using cognitive-behavioral therapy intervention significantly improved sexual dysfunctions (anorgasmia, sexual avoidance, low frequency of sexual relations, lack of sexual relations, lack of sexual arousal, and lack of sexual satisfaction) in the experimental group compared to the control group, which is consistent with the present study.

In explaining the findings, it can be said that human sexual desires are considered one of the most important aspects of reproductive health and quality of life [41]. Sexual repression measures the lack of pleasure, embarrassment, and avoidance of sexual activities [45]. Sexual repression is often accompanied by feelings of guilt and shame arising from sexual impulses [48]. Therefore, using an effective therapeutic method to address sexual repression in pregnant women is essential. Negative thinking about sexual activity exacerbates and perpetuates symptoms. Therefore, MBCT can help these individuals learn skills to identify more useful ways and let go of shame and avoidance of sexual activities, thereby reducing sexual repression.

The results of the present study showed a significant statistical difference between the two groups in terms of the sexual quality of life of pregnant women. Therefore, MBCT was effective on this variable. Consistent with the present study, Li et al. [49] reported that mindfulness training improves the sexual quality of life of pregnant women, although it did not specifically address sexual quality of life. The study by Mohammadi et al. [28] also showed that correcting dysfunctional sexual beliefs during pregnancy increases the score of sexual quality of life. Mindfulness practice is associated with improvements in psychological well-being and mood; some studies suggest potential long-term benefits, although such effects were not assessed in the present study [26]. Therefore, MBCT shows promise as a potential approach to improve sexual quality of life in pregnant women, although further research is needed to confirm its effectiveness across broader populations. Mindfulness-based cognitive-behavioral therapy likely enhances sexual quality of life by promoting non-judgmental awareness of sexual thoughts and emotions, reducing anxiety and negative cognitive patterns associated with sexual distress. By integrating mindfulness practices with cognitive restructuring, MBCT may help pregnant women reframe maladaptive beliefs about sexuality, increase emotional regulation, and improve intimacy with their partners [50].

The findings of the current research revealed a notable statistical difference between the two groups regarding the sexual distress experienced by pregnant women. This indicates that the intervention group experienced a more substantial and significant reduction in sexual distress. Consequently, MBCT was associated with a statistically significant reduction in sexual distress in this sample. Supporting this, Rashedi et al. [51] found that the intervention significantly enhanced desire, distress, self-disclosure, and sexual function at immediate, 4-week, and 12-week follow-ups post-training. Additionally, Braeken et al. [52] noted that mindfulness practices alleviate sexual distress among pregnant women. Furthermore, Brotto et al. reported that cognitive-behavioral and mindfulness interventions effectively reduced sexual distress in women with a history of childhood sexual abuse, aligning with the results of the present study [25]. Given the more pronounced decrease in sexual distress within the intervention group compared to the control group, the MBCT intervention exhibited a significant statistical impact [44]. Considering that inadequate sexual function can diminish the quality of life and emotional well-being, adversely affecting women’s mental health, self-esteem, and social interactions, it is crucial to implement targeted therapeutic programs aimed at alleviating sexual distress in women. Additional research is warranted to investigate this matter further.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the reliance on self-report measures (SQOL-F and FSDS) introduces the potential for response biases, including social desirability and recall bias. Given the sensitive nature of sexual health, participants may have underreported distress or overreported improvements, which could affect the accuracy of the observed effect sizes.

Second, the single-blind design, in which participants were aware of their group allocation, may have introduced performance bias. Participants in the intervention group might have been influenced by heightened expectations for benefit (the placebo effect), while those in the control group may have experienced disappointment, potentially amplifying the observed between-group differences.

Third, the generalizability of the results is constrained by the study’s context. The sample consisted of a relatively small number of Iranian pregnant women from a single geographic region, all of whom were married, nulliparous/primiparous, and presented with clinically significant sexual distress at baseline. Consequently, the findings may not extend to pregnant women in different cultural settings, with different parity histories, or those without pre-existing sexual distress.

Fourth, the lack of an active control group (e.g., receiving standard prenatal counseling or a non-specific support group) limits the ability to definitively attribute the improvements to the specific components of mindfulness-based cognitive-behavioral therapy. The observed effects could be partly due to the non-specific factors of group therapy, such as therapist attention, peer support, or simply dedicating time to focus on well-being.

Finally, while the intervention demonstrated significant short-term effects, the follow-up period was limited to one month post-intervention. The sustainability of the improvements in sexual quality of life and distress beyond this point, particularly into the postpartum period, remains unknown and warrants investigation in studies with longer-term follow-ups.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that a seven-session MBCT program significantly improved sexual quality of life and reduced sexual distress in a sample of pregnant women experiencing baseline sexual difficulties. These findings suggest that integrating such mindfulness-based interventions into prenatal care could be beneficial. However, further research with larger, more diverse populations and active control groups is necessary to confirm these results and establish the long-term efficacy and generalizability of the approach before clinical implementation can be recommended.
