Phase-dependent iron dysmetabolism in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury: From mechanisms to therapies
Authors: Yilin Huang, Shuang Li, Yuanlin Zeng, Zeyu Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI) remains a critical clinical challenge, often worsening myocardial damage after reperfusion therapy for acute myocardial infarction (AMI). Phase-dependent dysregulation of iron homeostasis is critically involved in the pathogenesis of MIRI. Ischemia leads to depletion of the labile iron pool (LIP) and iron accumulation in cardiomyocytes, whereas reperfusion leads to iron overload and oxidative stress. These changes destroy cellular homeostasis, triggering ferroptosis and other programmed cell death pathways. Potential therapeutic targets have been identified through molecular mechanisms regulating ferritinophagy, hepcidin modulation, and transferrin receptor 1-mediated iron transport. This review summarizes research progress in these phase-specific changes in iron metabolism during myocardial ischemia and reperfusion, emphasizing their roles in oxidative stress and cell damage. Potential therapeutic strategies integrate the correction of dysregulated homeostasis into clinical trials using drugs that target AMI and MIRI, offering perspectives for the development of novel treatments.

Introduction

Ischemic heart disease remains the leading cause of death, accounting for 13 % of global mortality. The largest absolute increase in deaths has been recorded in 2000, with an additional 2.7 million deaths, reaching 9.1 million deaths in 2021 [1]. This considerable global burden is starkly illustrated by the situation in China. According to the China Cardiovascular Health and Disease Report 2023, an estimated 330 million people have cardiovascular diseases, including 11.39 million with coronary heart disease. Moreover, the number of cardiovascular disease-related deaths rose from 3.09 million in 2005 to 4.58 million in 2020. In both rural and urban China, cardiovascular diseases account for approximately two of every five deaths, with ischemic heart disease being a primary contributor [2,3].

Myocardial infarction (MI) is a specific type of ischemic heart disease that is one of the most common causes of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, with timely reperfusion serving as the cornerstone treatment modality. Commonly used reperfusion strategies, such as thrombolytic therapy, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), and primary PCI, largely limit the infarct size and reduce cellular death in MI. However, these interventions paradoxically introduce the risk of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI), a severe and often irreversible condition [4]. MIRI exacerbates myocardial injury, as well as increases infarct size, programmed cell death of cardiomyocytes, and mortality [5,6]. Several methods are available to reduce MIRI, including antioxidant therapy, anti-apoptotic agents, anti-inflammatory drugs, calcium channel blockers, ischemic preconditioning (IPC), ischemic postconditioning, and innovative approaches, such as cytotherapy and gene therapy [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. However, their clinical results did not meet expectations, despite the encouraging findings in animal studies. This is likely attributed to the complex molecular pathways involved in MIRI and the compensatory interactions among these pathways. Therefore, researchers should further explore related molecular pathways.

Iron is an essential trace mineral in the human body, working as a cofactor for numerous enzymes and proteins involved in energy metabolism and mitochondrial respiration, both essential cellular processes [11,12]. Its function is particularly relevant in the cardiovascular system, which has high metabolic demands. Indeed, a potential association exists between iron dysregulation in cardiomyocytes and the pathophysiology of MIRI [5,13,14]. Oxidative stress and cell death are the key features of MIRI, and the role of iron in these processes has gained noteworthy attention [[15], [16], [17]]. Therefore, this review focuses on iron metabolism in the human body and the alterations in iron metabolism during the ischemic and reperfusion phases of MIRI, as well as the impact of these changes and potential protective measures.

Overview of iron metabolism in the human body

In the human body, iron is primarily absorbed, transported, and stored with minimal excretion, and this process is primarily regulated by hepcidin [18]. Cells absorb the iron required for metabolic functions from the bloodstream, and excess iron is stored or excreted by the iron transport protein ferroportin (FPN). Several pathways regulate intracellular iron homeostasis, including the iron regulatory proteins (IRPs) [19], hepcidin [18], ferritinophagy [20], the prolyl hydroxylase domain - hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) pathway [21], and the NRF2 factor [22].

Abnormalities in iron metabolism during MIRI

Abnormal iron metabolism in MIRI persists throughout the pathophysiology, showing distinct phase-specific characteristics. Transient depletion of the labile iron pool (LIP) and compensatory iron accumulation are predominant during the ischemic phase, whereas Fe2+ re-accumulation is observed during the reperfusion phase, leading to cellular iron overload and oxidative stress. These dynamic changes are interconnected during both phases, contributing to cardiomyocyte damage.

Iron metabolism abnormalities during the ischemic phase

Cardiomyocyte iron metabolism during hypoxia shows a complex dynamic balance, characterized by transient LIP depletion (predominantly Fe2+) and subsequent compensatory iron accumulation, that shapes the extent of damage in the subsequent reperfusion phase.

Transient depletion of LIP (Fe2+)

Insufficient Local Iron Supply: The interruption of myocardial blood flow during ischemia severely limits the delivery of iron to cardiomyocytes. Transferrin is the primary iron carrier in the bloodstream [23]; however, its function becomes restricted during ischemia, exacerbating the deficiency of local iron supply [24]. Additionally, the downregulation of TfR promotes the up regulation of ferritin expression [25]. The transferrin and TfR together exacerbate the deficiency of local iron supply, leading to the depletion of LIP ultimately [[23], [24], [25], [26], [27]].

Iron accumulation

Following a transient shortage of Fe2+, cells initiate compensatory responses that enlarge the iron through the mechanisms outlined below:

Heme Degradation: High heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) expression under ischemic conditions accelerates heme degradation, releasing the iron bound to heme and increasing LIP [27]. Although HO-1 expression during ischemia is partially considered as a protective stress response to the hypoxic environment, its excessive activation leads to iron overload during the subsequent reperfusion phase, thus triggering oxidative stress.

Ferritin Degradation: A key characteristic of myocardial ischemic phase is the suppression of ferritin degradation. Under ischemic conditions, the downregulation of NCOA4 inhibits ferritin degradation, resulting in the accumulation of intracellular iron. Importantly, the degree of NCOA4 downregulation is positively associated with the duration of ischemic exposure [25].

Iron regulatory mechanisms during the ischemic phase

HO-1: Under ischemic conditions, HO-1 catalyzes the breakdown of heme that induces the release of ferrous iron, while inducing the expression of ferritin. Ferritin effectively stores free iron and prevents further oxidative damage caused by iron overload. Yao et al. [28] found that pre-treatment with panaxatriol saponin (PTS) for 7 consecutive days before left anterior descending artery ligation in rats significantly promoted HO-1 expression by activating the Nrf2 signaling pathway. This intervention reduced reperfusion-induced excessive ROS production, inhibited the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, stabilized the mitochondrial membrane potential, and mitigated cardiomyocyte apoptosis. Additionally, ferritin heavy chain knockout experiments suggested the upregulation of both HO-1 and Slc7a11. HO-1 enhances ferroptosis tolerance by upregulating Slc7a11, thereby alleviating MIRI [29]. These findings underline the critical role of HO-1 in the dynamic regulation of iron metabolism, providing antioxidant protection during ischemia. Despite its protective role during ischemia, HO-1 exhibits detrimental properties during the reperfusion phase. From 6 h to 1 day after myocardial ischemia-reperfusion, Hmox1 expression increases markedly; it accelerates heme degradation and causes iron overload and ferroptosis in the endoplasmic reticulum [30].

IRP-1 and 2: IRP-1 promotes iron uptake by upregulating TfR1 expression while reducing iron efflux and downregulating storage genes, including FPN1 and ferritin heavy chain [31,32]. The dynamic regulation of iron homeostasis during the ischemic phase is an essential protective mechanism to preserve cellular homeostasis. In comparison to non-mutant mice, MID mice lacking IRP-1 and IRP-2 showed greater myocardial hypertrophy and contractile dysfunction 4 weeks after left anterior descending coronary artery ligation, as well as increased mortality after heart failure [31]. However, IRPs have a dual role, potentially because of their phase-specific effects. IRPs are primarily associated with damage during the reperfusion phase, and they regulate ferroptosis. Ferroptosis inducers, such as erastin and RSL3, upregulate IRP-1 and IRP-2 expression, whereas IRP-1 knockdown remarkably inhibits ferroptosis induced by these agents [33,34]. Although IRP-2 has a weaker effect on ferroptosis, it catalyzes IRP-1, exacerbating the iron metabolism imbalance [34].

Protective effect of iron supplementation during the ischemic phase

Transient Fe2+ deficiency during early ischemia triggers compensatory iron accumulation, contributing to worsened myocardial injury after reperfusion, whereas iron supplementation during this phase significantly reduces these pathological changes. This approach is substantially protective against myocardial damage during the reperfusion phase. Inserte et al. [35] demonstrated that cardiac iron-deficient mice present a larger infarct size because of the left anterior descending coronary artery ligation for 45 min and 24 h of reperfusion than the control group. Intravenous iron hydroxide in sucrose restores systemic and cardiac iron levels and prevents deleterious effects on infarct size. Similarly, Paterek et al. [36] showed that weekly intravenous injections of ferric carboxymaltose (10 mg/kg, 0.5 mL) during the first 4 weeks after MI in a heart failure rat model partially improve the left ventricular ejection fraction, slow the progression of left ventricular dilation, and protect calcium regulatory proteins, including SERCA, in left ventricular cardiomyocytes. Additionally, this treatment reduces levels of the inflammatory marker C-reactive protein (CRP) and increases the activity of the antioxidant enzyme superoxide dismutase (SOD), thereby alleviating oxidative stress.

The protective mechanism of iron supplementation likely involves maintaining myocardial iron homeostasis, thereby improving energy metabolism, reducing oxidative stress, and mitigating ischemia/reperfusion injury [36].

Iron metabolism dysregulation in MIRI during the reperfusion phase

The reperfusion phase is characterized by the restoration of oxygen and blood flow, during which the dynamic change in iron amount leads to more severe oxidative stress and cellular damage.

Cellular iron overload

During the early stage of reperfusion, multiple mechanisms jointly contributed to the increase of LIP. In LIP, iron primarily exists as ferrous iron [37], which initially binds to reduced glutathione (GSH). In contrast, iron chaperone proteins, such as poly(C)-binding proteins (PCBPs), facilitate iron delivery by interacting with Fe2+/GSH complexes [33]. Cells are under a rich oxygen environment during the reperfusion phase, where oxidative stress depletes GSH. Thus, the binding capacity of PCBPs becomes saturated, combined with iron overload. Consequently, unbound free iron, lacking PCBPs, becomes highly reactive and induces oxidative stress, which finally triggers various forms of cell death, specifically ferroptosis [38].

Restoration of iron uptake

The restoration of blood flow after reperfusion triggers a substantial imbalance in cellular iron homeostasis [39]. Cells absorb excessive iron because of the increased expression of TfR1, leading to cytoplasmic iron accumulation and subsequent expansion of LIP [40].

Tang et al. [41] demonstrated that TfR1 is upregulated in both ischemia/reperfusion-treated rat cardiomyocytes and hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R)-treated H9c2 cells. This upregulation is facilitated by USP7 and p53, where increased USP7 expression promotes p53 expression through post-translational modification, thus leading to TfR1 upregulation by p53 in a transcription-dependent manner. In addition, Park and Chung [42] demonstrated that autophagy is involved in the upregulation of TfR1 expression. This leads to an overload of total and ferrous intracellular iron.

TfR1 is also regulated by inflammatory factors during the reperfusion phase. This period is characterized by a surge in pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-6, which activate NF-κB and MAPK signaling cascades. These pathways indirectly modulate the expression of HIF-1α and IRPs. For example, NF-κB can enhance the stability of HIF-1α and amplify the activity of its downstream targets, thereby promoting TfR1 upregulation [43]. Meanwhile, oxidative stress triggered by inflammation activates the IRP system, further driving iron accumulation via TfR1 [44]. This interconnected axis—from inflammation to signaling pathways to TfR1—mediates the pathophysiology of reperfusion injury and warrants more attention.

In MIRI, TfR1 regulation is shaped by its position at the crossroads of multiple signaling pathways. It is influenced by factors, such as hypoxia, iron levels, and inflammation, while also exerting feedback effects on iron homeostasis, redox balance, and cell fate. Notably, this regulation appears to shift gradually and varies between ischemic and reperfusion phases. Therapeutically, intervening in the TfR1 axis may help prevent iron overload and blunt ferroptosis during early reperfusion. Although this approach remains under investigation, it provides a direction for targeted intervention.

Dysregulation of iron storage

Ferritin undergoes degradation during the reperfusion phase through a selective autophagy process termed ferritinophagy, leading to an increase in LIP [45]. NCOA4 regulates ferroptosis in diabetic MIRI by mediating ferritinophagy, which promotes ferritin degradation and subsequent iron release [46,47]. This event exacerbates ferroptosis [46,47].

Two signaling pathways are involved in the regulation of NCOA4 expression: the USP14/NCOA4 pathway and the cGAS-STING-NCOA4 pathway. USP14 upregulates NCOA4 through deubiquitination, thereby activating ferritinophagy [48]. The inhibition of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway reduces NCOA4 expression, alleviating the pathological damage associated with ferroptosis and autophagy [49].

Although the synthesized ferritin started to sequester the excess iron, the synthesis rate during reperfusion often fails to match the increased iron accumulation [50]. Concurrently, ROS-mediated damage further compromises ferritin's ability to bind iron [51], resulting in increased free iron levels in both the cytosol and mitochondria [42,52,53].

Hepcidin also contributes to dysregulating iron metabolism. Ischemia/reperfusion injury triggers systemic inflammation (e.g., IL-6 and TNF-α release), thereby activating the hepatic STAT3/BMP/SMAD signaling cascade and increasing circulating hepcidin levels [[54], [55], [56]]. These increased hepcidin levels exacerbate myocardial iron overload during reperfusion by suppressing FPN-mediated iron efflux, finally leading to Iron-dependent cell death, such as ferroptosis [57,58].

Mitochondrial iron overload

Iron is absorbed by mitochondria, the energy center of cardiomyocytes, for the production of heme and Fe—S clusters, which are necessary for oxidative phosphorylation [59]. However, iron overload causes excess iron to move across the mitochondrial membrane, triggering the Fenton reaction with hydrogen peroxide and generating ROS [13]. Moreover, ROS production further increases during mitochondrial respiratory chain turnover [60]. These dual mechanisms lead to excessive ROS levels that overwhelm the detoxification capacity of mitochondria, causing compensatory mechanisms to fail under pathological conditions.

The rise of ROS levels induces phospholipid peroxidation, which directly damages the mitochondrial membrane structure, such as its integrity, increasing oxidative stress and inducing functional impairment [61]. These changes finally result in an insufficient energy supply and myocardial injury [33].

The inhibition of mitochondrial iron overload through genetic approaches (cardiac-specific overexpression of mitochondrial iron exporter protein) or pharmacological methods (using mitochondria-permeable iron chelators) protects cardiomyocytes from ischemia/reperfusion injury [62].

Iron-catalyzed oxidative damage

Iron overload, specifically in the ferrous state, catalyzes the production of ROS through the Fenton and Haber-Weiss reactions. Furthermore, the acidic environment caused by the rupture of lysosomes during reperfusion increases the rate of the reactions [63]. The iron-catalyzed excessive ROS production triggers a cascade of cellular damage, including lipid peroxidation, protein denaturation, and DNA damage, leading to cardiomyocyte death [64]. Targeting iron metabolism (e.g., enhancing ferritin iron sequestration and/or chelating labile iron) inhibits the reaction, offering critical therapeutic strategies to mitigate oxidative injury.

Therapeutic strategies targeting iron metabolism during reperfusion

Ferritin-mediated protection

Ferritin exerts critical protective effects during reperfusion by sequestering labile iron. IPC increases myocardial ferritin expression, which mitigates cellular damage [[65], [66], [67]].

Ismail et al. [65] demonstrated that IPC and ischemic postconditioning preserve mitochondrial network integrity by upregulating Mfn1 (1.59-fold in H9c2 cells) and suppressing Drp1 (0.5-fold in H9c2 cells), effectively restoring elongated mitochondria and reducing IRI-induced cardiac damage. Boovarahan and Kurian [68] showed that IPC in a Langendorff rat heart model reduces global DNA hypermethylation by 32 % and DNMT activity by 36 %, but it upregulates PI3K/AKT signaling (2.0-fold) and suppresses caspase-3 expression to reduce ischemia-reperfusion injury. IPC reduces iron mobilization during early reperfusion, compared with ischemia-reperfusion alone [69]. Collectively, newly synthesized ferritin in preconditioned hearts prevents excessive labile iron release. By binding redox-active iron, ferritin prevents the production of iron-catalyzed hydroxyl radicals (•OH) through Fenton and Haber-Weiss reactions, thereby reducing oxidative stress and subsequent reperfusion injury [70]. Notably, pretreatment with iron chelators completely abolishes both the cardioprotective effects of IPC and its associated ferritin upregulation, confirming the essential role of iron-dependent signaling pathways in IPC-mediated protection [71]. Thus, IPC-induced ferritin overexpression buffers iron surges upon reperfusion, representing an effective strategy to prevent myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury.

Iron chelation therapy

Iron chelators, including ciclopirox olamine and dexrazoxane, reduce myocardial infarct size in ischemia/reperfusion injury by reducing intracellular iron levels [15,72,73]. Their cardioprotective effects involve the suppression of ferroptosis by interfering with ROS-driven Fenton reactions [74]. However, the effect of chelators depends on their subcellular iron targeting. Chang et al. [62] proposed that the different efficacy of chelators reported in various studies derives from differences in their mitochondrial penetrance. Deferoxamine (DFO) binds extracellular and endosomal iron but does not modulate mitochondrial iron pools. It fails to reduce infarct size post-ischemia/reperfusion, as reported in several studies [75,76]. In contrast, myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury can be prevented by pharmacologically modulating mitochondrial iron with the mitochondrially permeable chelator 2,2′-bipyridyl effectively [62]. These findings highlight the potential role of mitochondrial iron as a critical therapeutic target, suggesting that chelators with enhanced mitochondrial permeability may represent a promising direction for future research [77].

Targeted mitochondrial iron therapy

Mitochondria-targeted iron chelators: During ischemia-reperfusion, cardiomyocyte mitochondria are the major sites of both ROS production and iron accumulation. Traditional iron chelators, such as DFO, fail to enter the mitochondria, making it difficult to target iron overload. To this end, researchers have developed mitochondria-targeted iron chelators or prodrugs that can effectively reach and act within the mitochondria. Alta et al. [78] synthesized a mitochondria-targeted iron chelator, TPP-DFO, by conjugating DFO with triphenylphosphonium (TPP). In their study, TPP-DFO effectively reduced mitochondrial labile iron levels in A2780 cells while maintaining low cytotoxicity. Similarly, Sandoval-Acuña et al. [79] generated “Mito-DFO” by linking DFO to TPP+, which reduced mitochondrial labile iron levels. Notably, the conjugate preserved iron‑sulfur cluster function and reduced ROS levels, suggesting a multi-level protective effect against mitochondrial injury. Targeting mitochondrial iron serves as a novel intervention for interrupting iron-driven ROS production during cardiac reperfusion.

Nanoparticle-based mitochondrial delivery systems: Nanoparticle-based platforms have been explored to deliver therapeutic agents directly to mitochondria. Zhu et al. [80] developed a dual-targeted system (D/Ce@mPDA-C/P) combining cerium dioxide nanozymes and dexrazoxane, which accumulated in myocardial mitochondria. It reduced ROS and non-heme iron levels and suppressed ferroptosis via GPX4 upregulation and ACSL4 downregulation. In MIRI mouse models, this system improved cardiac function and reduced oxidative stress, apoptosis, and fibrosis. Similarly, Hong et al. [81] utilized PDA nanoparticles loaded with Fer-1 to inhibit NOX4, enhancing GPX4 and FTH1 expression while lowering intracellular Fe2+. Finally, it attenuated injury. Zheng et al. [82] designed cysteine-loaded, mitochondria-targeted mesoporous polydopamine nanoparticles (Cys-mPDA@TPP) to eliminate ROS at its source. The system stabilized cysteine activity and improved mitochondrial ROS clearance through TPP-mediated targeting. In myocardial cells, Cys-mPDA@TPP not only prevented mitochondrial dysfunction but also reduced macrophage-mediated inflammation, providing a dual-action antioxidant and anti-inflammatory strategy for cardiovascular protection.

Although in early stages, these delivery strategies present a compelling framework for the precise control of mitochondrial iron metabolism. Conclusions

This review comprehensively describes the critical, phase-dependent dysregulation of iron metabolism that characterizes MIRI, progressing from Fe2+ deficiency during ischemia to severe iron overload and oxidative damage during reperfusion. The intricate molecular mechanisms underlying this process, including ferritinophagy, TfR1 regulation, and mitochondrial iron handling, alongside potential therapeutic interventions, have been systematically examined. However, the distinct pathophysiological processes in each phase necessitate a re-evaluation of the term “reperfusion injury.” The term MIRI may lack conceptual precision. Liu and Nordbeck [103] argue that most damage attributed to reperfusion is a consequence of irreversible injury caused by prolonged ischemia. The delay in restoring blood flow leads to substantial cellular dysfunction, making “ischemia/delayed reperfusion injury (IdRI)” a more accurate descriptor. Within this framework, the iron overload during reperfusion and the resulting oxidative and inflammatory cascades emerge as central features [6,63]. Most investigations, however, either emphasize iron-metabolic shifts confined to the ischemic phase or treat the ischemia–reperfusion sequence as a single continuum, overlooking the dynamic, phase-to-phase fluctuations in iron metabolism. Unraveling these temporal patterns may help identify critical windows during which iron chelators deliver maximum benefit, offering new avenues for therapeutic innovation in AMI and MIRI.

Despite the critical need for novel AMI and IdRI treatments, drugs in clinical trials, particularly those targeting iron metabolism, are scarce. Several therapeutic candidates remain in the preclinical phase, necessitating bridging the translational gap from laboratory investigation to clinical application. The preclinical studies summarized herein indicate two primary strategies for mitigating iron-mediated cardiac injury: (i) an upstream approach targeting the TfR1 to limit cellular iron uptake, exemplified by EGCG; (ii) more downstream strategy, focuses on mitochondrion protection—the primary site of iron-induced damage—which underlies the action of drugs, such as Tanshinone IIA and Salidroside. While both avenues show considerable promise, this preclinical evidence raises a crucial question of prioritization for future drug development. Because excessive iron influx is the initiating event, developing targeted TfR1 inhibitors appears to be a feasible strategy. However, the mitochondrion is the nexus of ferroptotic cell death; thus, the parallel development of therapies that specifically chelate mitochondrial iron or protect it from toxic effects might be an important and more precise goal to address this therapeutic need.
