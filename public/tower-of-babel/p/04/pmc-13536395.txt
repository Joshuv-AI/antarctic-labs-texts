Pathogenic oral microbiota abundance is associated with reduced bite force in patients with periodontitis
Authors: Chih-Chieh Wen, Thu Ya Linn, Ting-Yi Renn, Hui Yin Nam, Chia-Chen Hsu, Yi-Jen Lin, Yi-Fan Wu, Wei-Jen Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: /purposePeriodontitis leads to tissue destruction and functional impairment, but the interplay between microbiota, clinical parameters, and biomechanics remains unclear. This study aimed to quantitatively correlate specific oral microbial signatures and clinical inflammatory indicators with bite force in patients with periodontitis.Materials and methodsFifteen patients with stage III-IV periodontitis and 15 periodontally healthy controls were recruited. Clinical parameters (Probing Depth, Clinical Attachment Loss, Bleeding on Probing, Plaque Index) and MBF (pressure-sensitive film) were recorded. Supragingival plaque was analyzed through 16S rRNA gene sequencing (PacBio SMRT) to determine microbial composition. Correlation analyses were performed.ResultsThe periodontitis group had significantly higher CAL (4.97 ± 1.18 vs. 2.49 ± 0.21 mm) and BOP (49.16 ± 17.97% vs. 14.52 ± 5.29%), and significantly lower MBF (561.17 vs. 891.46 N) (all P-values <0.05). Across all subjects, Plaque Index and BOP were significantly negatively correlated with bite force. In patients, Porphyromonas abundance showed a significant positive correlation with BOP (ρ = 0.425), whereas Streptococcus showed a negative correlation (ρ = −0.381). Furthermore, higher abundances of pathogenic genera (e.g., Porphyromonas, Capnocytophaga) were significantly associated with reduced MBF.ConclusionBite force decline in periodontitis is directly associated with both clinical inflammation (BOP) and specific pathogenic bacterial loads (e.g., Porphyromonas). Microbial signatures combined with MBF may serve as a novel, integrative biomarker for assessing periodontitis severity beyond traditional clinical indices.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by the progressive loss of periodontal tissues, resulting from an inflammatory response triggered by a disruption in the symbiotic balance between the host’s immune response and the oral plaque biofilm.1 When the host’s immune system is compromised or overstimulated, the oral biofilm shifts to a dysbiotic state, jeopardizing local and systemic health.2 This disruption of equilibrium not only negatively impacts oral health but may also contribute to systemic conditions, including gastrointestinal tract and nervous system disorders.3 Clinically, periodontitis leads to the deterioration of periodontal supporting structures.4,5 It is classified based on disease severity, with potential masticatory dysfunction typically observed in the most advanced stages.6

The consequences of periodontitis are two aspects, manifesting in both microbial and functional dimensions. First, from a microbial perspective, the disease progression is highly associated with a shift in the oral microbiome. The diversity of the oral microbiome is reported to be greatest in patients with periodontitis compared to healthy individuals,7,8 marked by a proliferation of specific pathogenic genera. Second, from a biomechanical perspective, the destruction of periodontal supporting structures directly impairs masticatory function. Maximum bite force (MBF) is a key functional indicator of the masticatory system, and its intensity is heavily influenced by the support of the periodontal ligament.9 The ability of bite force is influenced by multiple factors such as age, gender, number of present teeth, prosthesis, and oral conditions.10 A study by Okiyama et al. reported a significant correlation between MBF and masticatory performance, indicating that MBF is a functional indicator of oral health.11 Thus, previous studies have demonstrated that chronic periodontitis is highly correlated with a significant reduction in overall bite force.10

However, a critical gap still remains regarding the direct relationship between oral microbiota composition and bite force parameters in patients with periodontitis. Although several studies have explored the oral microbial shifts associated with periodontal disease, and others have independently investigated the decline in biomechanical function, the potential interplay between microbial signatures and masticatory function has still not been fully investigated.

Therefore, this study utilized 16S rRNA-based third-generation sequencing for bacterial profiling and pressure-sensitive film for recording bite force. Unlike traditional short-read sequencing, this full-length sequencing approach provides enhanced taxonomic resolution down to the species level, allowing for a more precise identification of the specific pathogenic taxa driving functional impairment. The objective was to compare the oral microbiota composition and occlusal characteristics, including bite force and bite force distribution area, between healthy individuals and patients with periodontitis in order to elucidate the interaction between periodontal health and masticatory function.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This cross-sectional, observational study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Taipei Medical University (TMU-JIRB N202111050). The study design and reporting adhere to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. All participants were recruited from Shuang-Ho Hospital (New Taipei City, Taiwan) between 2022/03/23 and 2024/12/31. Informed consent was thoroughly explained to all participants, who then signed the forms prior to any procedures.

Subject recruitment and clinical examination

Patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were enrolled in the study. The strict inclusion criteria required patients to be diagnosed with stage III-IV periodontitis, while the exclusion criteria strictly prohibited current smokers and individuals with relevant systemic conditions to minimize confounding factors. Demographic data, including gender, age, systemic conditions, smoking status, and alcohol consumption habits, were documented using a standardized questionnaire. All oral examinations, plaque collections, and bite force registrations were performed by a single calibrated operator (Department of Periodontology, TMU, Taipei, Taiwan). Under proper lighting, the operator recorded clinical parameters, including plaque index (PI), probing depth (PD), gingival recession, Clinical Attachment Loss (CAL), furcation involvement, tooth mobility, and bleeding on probing (BOP).

Bite force registration

The subject’s dental arch dimensions and occlusion were examined to determine the appropriate size of the GC Dental Prescale II (Fujifilm Co., Tokyo, Japan) film. The subject was then instructed to occlude with maximum bite force for 3 s. After the test, the film was carefully removed and analyzed on the same day using a calibrated scanning device (Perfection V600; Seiko Epson Corp., Nagano, Japan) and a locator sheet to determine Maximum Bite Force (MBF) and bite force distribution area.

Supragingival plaque collection and sequencing

Supragingival plaque was collected from all tooth surfaces using sterile Gracey curettes without subgingival activation. The pooled samples were transferred into sterilized 0.5 mL Eppendorf tubes, weighed, sealed (Bemis), and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Total genomic DNA was extracted from plaque samples using the Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The quality and quantity of extracted DNA were assessed. Full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed using the PacBio SMRT platform (SMRT platform; Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA).

Bioinformatic analysis

Raw sequencing reads were processed using the Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm 2 (DADA2) pipeline (v1.28) for quality filtering, error correction, and merging to generate Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs). Taxonomic classification was performed by aligning ASVs against the Human Oral Microbiome Database (HOMD) (v15.22). Alpha-diversity (within-sample richness and evenness) was assessed using the Shannon index, Chao1, and Observed ASVs. Beta-diversity (between-sample community dissimilarity) was calculated using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and visualized via Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics, v22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All data were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Demographic and clinical parameters between the control and periodontitis groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous or ordinal variables (e.g., Age, PD, CAL, MBF) and the Chi-squared (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Differences in alpha-diversity indices between groups were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Analysis of Similarities (ANOSIM) was performed on the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix to assess significant differences in overall community structure (beta diversity). Correlations between clinical parameters, microbial abundance (at the genus level), and bite force were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation (ρ), as the data were predominantly non-normally distributed.

For all analyses involving multiple comparisons (e.g., comparisons of numerous microbial taxa), the resulting P-values were adjusted to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR) using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method. An adjusted P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic data and clinical parameters

The demographic data were collected for the 15 control subjects (median age, 43) and the 15 periodontitis subjects (median age, 54). Clinical parameters, including probing depth, clinical attachment loss, BOP, GI, plaque weight, plaque control record, and PI are shown in Table 1. The periodontitis group demonstrated significantly greater mean probing depth and clinical attachment loss than the control group (both P-values<0.001). Similarly, mean BOP and GI were also significantly elevated in the periodontitis group (both P-values < 0.001). In terms of plaque accumulation, the periodontitis group exhibited significantly higher total plaque weight and average plaque weight per tooth (both P-values < 0.05). Although mean values for plaque control record and plaque index were higher in the periodontitis group, these differences were not statistically significant (P> 0.05).

Maximum bite force

Significant differences in masticatory function were observed between the groups, as shown in Fig. 1. The control group exhibited a significantly higher average maximum bite force display area (22.85 vs.14.25 mm2, P< 0.05) and a significantly greater average total bite force (891.46 vs. 561.17 N, P< 0.05) compared to the periodontitis group. No significant difference was found in mean bite pressure (P> 0.05).

Supragingival microbiota assessment

The microbial composition differed significantly between the two groups (ANOSIM R = 0.15, P< 0.01). The periodontitis group also exhibited significantly higher alpha-diversity, as measured by both the Shannon and Chao1 indices (both P-values <0.05). Briefly, the periodontitis group exhibited a dysbiotic microbiome characterized by a significantly higher relative abundance of periodontal pathogens, including Porphyromonas, Prevotella, and Treponema, compared with healthy controls, consistent with established disease patterns (Fig. 2).

Correlations between clinical, masticatory function, and microbial parameters

The correlation between the key differing parameters was evaluated. First, average occlusal contact pressure showed a moderate negative correlation with plaque weight (r = −0.55) and Plaque Control Record (r = −0.60) when analyzing all subjects (Figs. 3A–1 and 3B-1). These correlations were notably stronger within the periodontitis group alone (r = −0.70 and r = −0.78, respectively) (Figs. 3A–2 and 3B-2).

Second, clinical attachment loss (CAL) and bleeding on probing (BOP) both demonstrated moderate negative correlations with masticatory performance. CAL was inversely associated with bite force display area (r = −0.42), average bite pressure (r = −0.38), and total bite force (r = −0.44) (Fig. 4A–C). Similarly, BOP showed moderate negative correlations with bite force display area (r = −0.38), average bite pressure (r = −0.35), and total bite force (r = −0.40) (Fig. 5A–C).

Finally, the abundance of key pathogenic genera was negatively correlated with masticatory function. Bite force display area showed moderate negative correlations with the absolute counts of Prevotella, Fusobacterium, and Porphyromonas (r ≈ −0.3 for all, P< 0.01) (Fig. 6). Total bite force showed similar negative correlations with the absolute counts of these same genera (r ≈ −0.3 for all, P< 0.01) (Fig. 7).

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between oral microbiota composition in dental plaque and maximum bite force in patients with periodontitis. The results confirm that the microbial composition of periodontitis patients is significantly different from that of healthy individuals, and that clinical indicators of periodontitis, such as clinical attachment loss and bleeding on probing, are closely related to changes in masticatory function. Most importantly, through correlation analysis, this study directly links the abundance of specific pathogenic microbial communities to the decline in bite force, providing microbial-level evidence for the functional loss caused by periodontal tissue destruction.

Our results confirm established biomechanical principles, demonstrating that the periodontitis group had significantly lower MBF and a smaller bite force display area (Fig. 1), which aligns with previous findings.10,12 This is a direct consequence of the progressive destruction of periodontal supporting tissues.13,14 This was reflected in the strong negative correlation we observed between CAL and bite force parameters (Fig. 4). However, our findings also reveal a more specialized physiological mechanism. We found a strong negative correlation between BOP and all bite force measures (Fig. 5). BOP reflects active inflammation,15 which may influence the mechanoreceptors of the periodontal ligament (PDL) and alter the neuromuscular regulation of occlusal forces.14,16 This suggests that the functional decline is not only due to chronic tissue loss (CAL) but is also exacerbated by acute inflammation (BOP), supporting the idea that bite function is a sensitive indicator of the health of the periodontal neurosensory unit.

Consistent with previous studies, we found that periodontitis subjects exhibited greater species richness, evenness, and alpha diversity, suggesting a notable increase in supragingival bacterial diversity accompanies the dysbiosis shift. While Streptococcus remained the predominant genus in both groups, the average abundance of the “orange” and “red” complex genera, specifically Prevotella, Fusobacterium, and Porphyromonas, was significantly higher in the periodontitis group (Fig. 2). This aligns with findings from Ximénez et al. and confirms that supragingival plaque may serve as a critical reservoir for these pathogenic species.17

The most critical finding of this study is the direct, moderate negative correlation between biting capacity (bite force and display area) and the absolute counts of key periodontal pathogens, including Prevotella, Fusobacterium, and Porphyromonas (Figs. 6 and 7). This provides a mechanistic link uniting our other findings. These genera are known to produce virulence factors that inhibit leukocytes and trigger a host inflammatory response, leading to tissue destruction (CAL) and inflammation (BOP) as observed. Specifically, the localized inflammatory response directed by these virulence factors accelerates alveolar bone resorption and compromises the structural integrity of the periodontal ligament.5,13,14 This microbially driven tissue destruction logically leads to a reduced bite force and impaired masticatory function.18–20 Therefore, our results provide evidence that the decline in biting capacity is directly associated with the specific pathogenic bacterial load. Our findings suggest that the traditional clinical parameters (e.g., PD, CAL) may only capture a single dimension of periodontal health. The strong correlation between microbial signatures (pathogen load), inflammatory status (BOP), and key masticatory functional outcomes (MBF) supports the adoption of a more integrated diagnostic approach. We propose that biting function (MBF) may serve as a vital, non-invasive supplementary parameter for evaluating periodontal disease severity. A multi-modal biomarker panel, integrating microbial and functional indicators, could provide a more comprehensive assessment of the disease and treatment outcomes.

A potential limitation of this study was patient selection, as all participants were recruited from Taiwan, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Additionally, the sample size was relatively small due to strict inclusion criteria. While the relationship between clinical parameters, oral microbiota, and maximum bite force was thoroughly investigated, the association between these oral health conditions and systemic diseases remains to be explored. Nevertheless, the current findings provide a foundation for future studies examining the interrelationship among oral microbiota, bite force, and systemic health.

This study provides direct evidence linking the abundance of specific supragingival pathogenic bacteria (Porphyromonas, Prevotella, Fusobacterium) to the decline in masticatory function (MBF) in patients with periodontitis. These findings highlight the potential of utilizing MBF and microbial signatures as part of an integrated assessment for evaluating periodontal disease severity. In clinical practice, routine monitoring of MBF could provide dentists with a rapid indicator to detect disease progression or to evaluate the efficacy of periodontal interventions. Future research, using longitudinal designs and larger cohorts, is warranted to validate these markers and further explore the interplay between microbial dysbiosis, functional impairment, and systemic health outcomes. Restoring the symbiotic balance of the oral microbiome may be a strategy not only for controlling localized inflammation but also for preserving masticatory function and improving patients’ overall quality of life.
