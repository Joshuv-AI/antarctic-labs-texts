The effect of B4C addition on the shielding properties of polydimethylsiloxane-novolac composite
Authors: Morteza Sarvari, Hamzeh Foratirad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This research aims to make a polymer composite of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)/Novolac base reinforced by B4C particles. The hot press method was used to produce a polymer composite with an optimum ratio of 85/15 PDMS/Novolac and 7, 12, 15, 20, and 30% B4C. The impact of variation in the set values of B4C concentration on the physical, mechanical, and shielding properties of polymer composite was examined through a variety of analytical techniques, including thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), hardness testing, tensile bending strength testing, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), neutron radiography, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The mechanical properties findings revealed that the B15 sample with a concentration of 15% wt. B4C had the highest hardness, tensile and bending strength, which were 109 shore A, 203 MPa, and 74 MPa, respectively. Additionally, the SEM images exposed the equilibrium between porous structure and agglomeration of B4C particles in B15 sample. Besides, adding 15–20% B4C increased shielding properties by 9–10%. Moreover, the results of TGA demonstrated better thermal stability of polymer composites, with weight loss of less than 30% at 500 °C after adding up to 30% B4C.

Introduction

Radioactive technology has revolutionized industries such as medical diagnostics and power plants1–3, but it also endangers human health by emitting hazardous radioactive radiation. Exposure to γ-rays and X-rays can cause ionization, permanent damage, and serious illnesses4. Exposure time, distance, and shielding can all lower scattered radiation doses, necessitating appropriate radiation protection5,6. Metallic shielding, such as lead, barium, aluminum, copper, iron, and concrete, is widely utilized in the nuclear industry to protect humans from ionizing radiation7,8. However, its high weight density, low flexibility, corrosion resistance, and high processing requirements limit its usage. To circumvent these constraints, new materials such as polymers and polymer composites have been developed with unique features9–14.

Polymer-based composites are viable choices for neutron attenuation for the following reasons. They with high hydrogen content have the smallest atomic diameter and effectively convert fast neutrons into thermal neutrons15. They are also lighter than metal matrix composites and can serve as structural materials when combined with micro- or nanoparticles. Polymer composites offer good structural, mechanical, and radiation-shielding qualities13,16–20. Therefore, the radiation protection industry is increasingly focusing on polymer-based composites due to their lightweight, processable, flexible, and low manufacturing costs21–27.

Typically, organic polymers such as high-density polyethylene (HDPE)10,25,28, epoxy resin (EP)29, phenolic resin30, polyimide resin (PI)31 and EPDM32,33 are commonly used in radiation shielding engineering. To improve neutron shielding efficacy, boron-containing such as B2O3, BN, B4C, and colemanite are typically added to organic polymers34,35. Boron carbide (B4C) is a hard material with excellent mechanical properties, including high hardness, strength, high melting point, low specific gravity, and high neutron absorption cross-section, making it a better alternative36–38.

High-density polyethylene (HDPE), one of the most frequently used polymers, has been developed for use in neutron shielding engineering applications39. On the other hand, low glass transfer temperature40 and degradation the mechanical and physical properties of polymer composite by γ-rays produced by reaction of the thermal neutrons capturing1H (n, γ)2H). Moreover, lack of interfacial adhesion between the HDPE matrix and BN or B4C particle, leading to a limited improvement or even decay of physical and radiation shielding properties of polymer composite. General polymers utilized in nuclear processes, such as polyethylene (PE) and polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA), have poor thermal and mechanical stability when exposed to long-term radiation. High-energy radiation exposure produces free radicals, which cause polymers to undergo chain scission. Tensile strength and other characteristics were consequently found to have decreased41,42.

Pharmaceuticals, coatings, power network systems, electronics, adhesives, automobiles, and medical applications are just a few of the many industrial uses for silicone rubber. In a variety of harsh conditions, it can withstand heat, ozone, and UV radiation. Furthermore, silicone rubber possesses unique qualities such as stability, softness, nontoxicity, nonflammability, and resistance to radiation. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) is the most widely used kind of silicone rubber43. Polydimethylsiloxane (PMDS) is an inorganic polymer that possesses many attractive properties such as good thermal stability, oxidation resistance, flexibility, toughness, and hydrophobic character, which render it suitable for many applications44. Some reports explain the improvement of the almost undesirable properties of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) by combining it with novolac45,46. A rigid polar oligomeric novolac resin can enhance the mechanical properties of PDMS.

In this project, a PDMS/Novolac-based polymer composite reinforced by B4C particles with neutron shielding capacity was aimed to be developed. The impact of B4C variation on the mechanical and physical properties was investigated in detail.

Fabrication methods

Initially, the molar fraction of Novolac to PDMS was optimized; to this end, the molar fraction of PDMS/Novolac was investigated between 100/0 and 85/15, with a constant amount of B4C of 5% by weight. The PDMS/Novolac/B4C composite was prepared via a liquid-based process by mixing and dispersing the material in the basic solution. Briefly, a defined amount of B4C (5 wt% based on the PDMS + Novolac weights) dissolved in 5 ml. toluene followed by the addition of PDMS. The mixture containing PDMS and B4C was homogenized by mechanical stirring at a stirring rate of 200 rpm for 20 min. Then the mixture of Novoloc with hexamethylenetetramine (HMTA) in a 95:5 ratio was slowly added via syringe to the basic solution and ultrasonicated for 30 min using a probe sonicator (Leewen Ultrasound). Before curing in hot press device in stainless steel molds at a pressure of 20 kN at 200 °C for 10 min, tetraethylorthosilicate (TEOS) as a cross-linking agent, ZnO as a corrosion inhibitor and CNTs as a flame retarder were added to the basic solution in approximately 1 ml., 1 wt% and 0.5 wt% based of PDMS + Novoloc weights, respectively. Experimental analysis

X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD) was performed for the phase determination of the composite using a Philips diffractometer model PW3020 with radiation Cu-Kα (1.5418 A) at a scanning speed of 0.3 s in the region 2θ = 10–90°. The structures and morphologies of a cross-section of samples were imaged using an EVO 15 scanning electron microscope (SEM, ZEISS) at 20 kV. FTIR was performed for the identification of compounds and bonds using a Perkin-Elmer Model 1600 Series FTIR Spectrophotometer. Thermal characteristics of the PDMS + Novolac composites were conducted on a Perkin-Elmer Pyris-1 DSC. The samples were heated from ambient temperature to 800 °C with a heating rate 10 °C/min. Tensile tests were performed on a universal mechanical testing machine (SANTAM 5T) following ASTM D638. Hardness measurement was carried out using a portable hardness meter NOVOTEST TSh-A. Furthermore, the physical parameters of the samples, such as water absorption, bulk density, and bulk porosity, were measured using the Archimedes method as specified in ASTM C 373. Neutron attenuation experiments were performed in the Isfahan miniature neutron source reactor (MNSR) at 300 W power using a BF3 detector. The Miniature Neutron Source Reactor of Isfahan is a tank-in-pool research reactor that uses highly enriched uranium as fuel, light water as coolant, and beryllium as reflector. The typical setup for neutron radiography is shown in Fig. 2. Image detectors convert the transmitted neutrons through the thickness of the sample. For neutron radiography, a special area detector detects neutrons that provide digital images with high sensitivity. The image histogram was used to examine the appearance frequency of the various gray levels in the image. In this case, the grey levels of the image’s histogram decrease with increased transmitted neutrons. The neutron attenuation values of polymer composites are based on the mean gray level of the captured digital images were compared to the standard sample, borated polyethylene with 7 wt% boron.

Results and discussion

Table 2 displays the hardness and density values of polymer composite samples with varying weight ratios of PDMS/Novolac. An increase of up to 15% of Novolac in polymer composites increases density and hardness. As a consequence, the sample with the 85:15 PDMS/Novolac ratio has the highest level of hardness and density. It’s worth noting that Novolac was utilized as a curing agent in the composites. The hardest sample is 85:15 PDMS/Novolac, while the softest is 0:100 PDMS/Novolac. By increasing the weight fraction of Novolac to 15 wt%, the composite’s average hardness reached 78 Shore A, but when the weight fraction of Novolac was increased to a higher value, the average hardness value declined.

Additionally, the FTIR spectra of PDMS/Novoloc polymer composite with different ratio are reported in Fig. 3. The spectra of PDMS showed two main groups including Si–C stretching at 813 cm- 1, symmetric C–H bending at 1265 cm- 1, asymmetric C–H bending at 1414 cm- 1, C–H stretching at 2965 cm- 1, symmetric C–H stretching at 2905 cm- 1, asymmetric –Si–O–Si stretching 1027 and 1085 cm–14748. By adding Novoloc resin the significant peaks appears at 3345 cm- 1 due to –OH stretching vibration, 2919 cm- 1 for CH2 stretching vibration. C-C aromatic double bond in the region 1642 and 1596, absorption beak in the region 1439 due to CH2 bending while C-C double bond bending vibration at 1361 cm- 1, while at 818, 756 cm- 1 was due to asymmetric stretching of aromatic C-C-OH and CH out of plane. According to the FTIR results (Fig. 3), increasing the amount of Novolac reduces the intensity of the Si-O and Si-C bond peaks. As a result, the relative value of the aforementioned bonds has decreased in comparison to other atomic bonds. As a result, the drop in hardness of polymer composites with the highest Novolac percentage (80:20) is due to a decrease in Si-O and Si-C bonds caused by reducing the PDMS content. Consequently, the low hardness of the samples with low Novolac is due to imperfect curing. On the other side, low hardness with increased Novolac is associated with a reduction in the number of silicate functional groups in PDMS.

The porous structure is transformed into a dense structure by adding more Novolac. Raising the PDMS/Novolac ratio increases the density of the polymer composite. As a result, the sample with an 85:15 PDMS/Novolac ratio has the highest density and hardness level. If the optimal conditions are violated, these are drastically decreased. This optimized sample was utilized to generate a reinforced polymer composite composed of B4C, ZnO, and CNTs in an 85:15 PDMS/Novolac matrix.

The concentration of B4C has an optimum value; increasing B4C by more than 15% reduces hardness. It is caused by the agglomeration of B4C powder, which increases by over 15%. The microstructures of B7, B12, B15, B20, and B30 samples are compared using SE-SEM images from Fig. 5. It was shown that increasing B4C causes agglomerated particles to develop, resulting in increased porosity (Fig. 5d), which reduces resistance to water penetration as well as hardness, tensile and bending strength. Figure 5c depicts the equilibrium between porous structure and agglomeration of B4C particles. As a result, it has the best mechanical qualities while also having the lowest porosity. Figure 6 depicts the results of water absorption and porosity measurements performed in accordance with the C 373 − 88 standard. Considering SEM images Fig. 5e, f and the graph in Fig. 6, the sample of B30 has the highest porosity and water absorption. Due to the high porosity in samples of B20 and B30, are shown in Fig. 5d, e and f, respectively, the water absorption of these samples is at a high level. It is also expected that these phenomena will destroy the mechanical properties. Table 4 summarizes the mechanical test results, including bending and tensile strength. Adding hard B4C particles improves the mechanical properties. Concurrently, specific elongation diminishes. Such behavior of mechanical properties of polymeric composites is conventional; reinforcing of polymer matrix with hard particles results in a rise of elasticity modules and yield stress accompanied by a simultaneous decrease in elongation. The mechanical properties are improved by adding hard particles of B4C49. In addition, the tensile-strain curves of polymer composite with different weight percentages of B4C are shown in Fig. 7.

The addition of ZnO and CNTs led to proper mechanical properties in all sample that is also reported by Sankar Rajan et al.50. Moreover, The results of the mechanical examination show an optimum value of adding B4C particles. The sample of B15, includes 15% wt. B4C, is found to have the most favorable mechanical properties. It has high strength and ductility values. Tensile strength, bending strength, and fracture energy are 203 MPa, 74 MPa, and 7.3 kJ/m2, respectively (Table 4). The two components have an unfavorable effect on the mechanical properties of composite polymers. The first is reduced adhesion between the matrix and B4C particles, this is probably associated with rather large dimensions of B4C particles compared to the matrix and poor adhesion of B4C to the polymeric matrix. So, B4C particles act as the defects in the polymeric matrix, which is also reported by Shao-Yun Fu et al.51. The last is agglomeration, which causes greater porosity. Therefore, these factors adversely affect the mechanical properties by increasing B4C particles by more than 15% wt.

In addition to mechanical properties, thermal properties are important for determining proper polymer composite. The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry tests were performed to assess the decomposition temperature, weight loss, and phase transition of the composite polymer. TGA analysis was used to investigate the thermal stability of a polymer composite with varying B4C sample concentrations as a function of temperature. Figure 8 depicts TGA graphs of polymer composites used to determine the effect of adding boron carbide particles on thermal stability. This graphic shows the sample’s weight decrease as a result of degradation. At 600 °C, B7 (7% wt. B4C) loses more than 50% of its weight. However, adding boron carbide particles to the polymer composite reduces weight loss to less than 50%. In addition, B30 has a weight loss of less than 30%. As a result, the findings demonstrate that adding B4C as a neutron absorber causes the graph to shift upward, indicating that the thermal stability of the polymer composite rises in the presence of boron carbide52,53.

The DSC analysis curves show two exothermic peaks; the first peak occurs between 450 and 650 °C and corresponds to carbon oxidation. The intensity of the initial peaks decreases as the concentration of B4C in the polymer composite increases. As the weight fraction of the matrix decreases, so does the carbon content, resulting in less carbon oxidation. Also, TGA analysis results show that it is compatible with a decrease in weight loss in the temperature range of 450–650 °C. Research indicates that B4C oxidizes at around 700 °C, creating a B2O3 oxygen barrier54,55. As a result, the second peak relates to the oxidation of B4C at temperatures ranging from 700 to 800 °C. The oxidation of B4C produces B2O3. It is an oxide ceramic phase with a heavy weight. Furthermore, due to the creation of B2O3 at high temperatures, the graph of TGA analysis is raised by increasing the temperature above roughly 700 °C. As a result = of high-temperature oxidation, a high amount of B4C produces a significant amount of heavy oxide. It is discovered that the B15 and B20 samples had the best combination of total weight loss and B2O3 oxide phase content.

The results of neutron shielding property examination is shown in Fig. 9. The neutron transmission value of polyethylene-7wt% B standard sample and PDMS/Novolac composites with different values of B4C were drawn in Fig. 9a.The thickness of the samples in the neutron transmission coefficient experiments was equal to 5 mm. As observed in Fig. 9a, a polyethylene sample transmitted about 70% of the neutron beam, while the PDMS/Novolac composites with 30 wt% B₄C in the same thickness transmitted about 48% of the neutron beam. The PDMS/Novolac composites with 7, 12, 15 and 20 wt% B4C transmitted about 68, 60, 53 and 51%, respectively. Figure 9b displays the results of neutron absorption. The findings are compared to a polyethylene-7wt% B standard sample. Several investigations found that adding B4C to the matrix composite improved the polymer’s thermal neutron radiation shielding properties56. In addition, as noted by Gwaily et al.57, raising B4C improves sideling properties up to a certain threshold. As a result, raising the B4C content up to 15%wt. improves neutron absorption, but no significant difference is observed after that. As a result, the neutron absorption intensities of the B15, B20, and B30 samples are practically identical.
