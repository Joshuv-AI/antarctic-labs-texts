Structure-preserved integration of scRNA-seq data using heterogeneous graph neural network
Authors: Xun Zhang, Kun Qian, Hongwei Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe integration of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from multiple experimental batches enables more comprehensive characterizations of cell states. Given that existing methods disregard the structural information between cells and genes, we proposed a structure-preserved scRNA-seq data integration approach using heterogeneous graph neural network (scHetG). By establishing a heterogeneous graph that represents the interactions between multiple batches of cells and genes, and combining a heterogeneous graph neural network with contrastive learning, scHetG concurrently obtained cell and gene embeddings with structural information. A comprehensive assessment covering different species, tissues and scales indicated that scHetG is an efficacious method for eliminating batch effects while preserving the structural information of cells and genes, including batch-specific cell types and cell-type specific gene co-expression patterns.Keywords: scRNA-seq, integration, structural information, heterogeneous graph, contrastive learning

Introduction

With the rapid advancement of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technologies, it has become feasible to investigate cellular homogeneity and heterogeneity at the single-cell level. However, due to the limited number of cells that can be sequenced simultaneously, actual datasets often comprise multiple experimental batches, which may originate from various platforms, techniques, and sequencing times. This situation leads to batch effects that can obscure genuine biological variation. Consequently, the removal of batch effects is essential for subsequent analyses [1–4].

In recent years, numerous number of integration approaches have been developed. MNNs [5], Seurat V3 [6], and Scanorama [7] are integration methods that employ matching correction. These methods identify MNNs between distinct batches and construct batch correction vectors to obtain the embedding without batch effects. Harmony [8] and BBKNN [9] integrate datasets through clustering. In particular, Harmony adopts an iterative procedure to update the cell embeddings in a manner that aligns them with the clustering center. BBKNN links cells of the same type in different batches by constructing a KNN graph, where the edge weights are correlated with the similarity of the nodes. More recently, deep learning-based methods have been developed, including variational autoencoders (VAE) [10] like scVI [11], generative adversarial networks (GAN) [12] like Cell BLAST [13], contrastive learning networks like INSCT [14] and scDML [15], Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence-based neural network like DeepBID [16], and graph embedding model like SIMBA [17]. By integrating zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB) distributions with VAEs, scVI represents each cell in a common low-dimensional latent space. In contrast, Cell BLAST employs a neural network-based generative model that utilizes adversarial strategies to align cells across different batches. INSCT employs a batch-aware triplet neural network to achieve cellular visualization without batch effects. Given the known number of cell types, scDML utilizes hierarchical clustering to merge clusters and update cell embeddings based on deep metric learning. DeepBID [16] employs an autoencoder with dual KL divergence loss functions to align cells from different batches within a consistent low-dimensional latent space, progressively mitigating batch effects through iterative clustering. SIMBA constructs a cell and feature graph to jointly embed cells of all batches and various features into a shared latent space. These embeddings are subsequently utilized for downstream analyses, including marker detection and gene regulation inference. Although the performance of the existing integration methods has been demonstrated, they often fail to remove batch effects while capturing cell and gene embeddings with structural information, which relates to the similarity among the biological neighbours of cells and genes.

Interaction graphs and graph convolutional networks (GCN) [18] have been demonstrated to constitute a valuable instrument in numerous fields. CFSSynergy [19] utlizes a protein–protein interaction graph to capture complex synergistic interactions between drugs and cell lines, thus achieving drug synergy prediction. To address the challenges associated with drug repurposing and predsiction of drug-protein and disease-protein interrelationships, HGTDR [20] contructs a heterogeneous knowledge graph with drug and disease nodes and indication edges, and utilizes a heterogeneous graph transformer network to solve the issues. More recently, GCNs have commenced to be effectively applied to the analysis of single-cell data [21, 22]. In particular, scMoGNN integrates single-cell multimodal data through heterogeneous graph, assuming that partial cell type information is available. Inspired by this, we propose the scHetG, which leverages higher-order structure information in the multi-batch cell–gene heterogeneous graph to simultaneously acquire cell and gene embeddings. scHetG’s key innovations lie in modeling cell–gene relationships within batches and cell–cell relationships between batches by converting multi-batch gene expression matrices into a heterogeneous graph. A graph reconstruction strategy combing ZINB is utilized to minimize the loss of structural information between cells and genes captured from the aforementioned graph. Furthermore, contrastive learning is incorporated to augment the efficacy of integration and preserve the structural information between cells.

We propose an innovative approach that leverages heterogeneous graph neural networks to simultaneously integrate single-cell data and acquire gene neighbourhood structure information, which most existing methods cannot achieve simultaneously and efficiently. Although SIMBA [17] also construct a heterogeneous graph, it employs graph embedding techniques that are less robust with complex data [23]. This limitation can be overcome by the superior node representation learning capabilities of heterogeneous graph neural networks.

In this work, we compared scHetG with existing state-of-the-art integration methods using both simulated and real datasets derived from diverse species, tissues, and scales. The results demonstrated that, from a cellular perspective, the method effectively removes batch effects while preserving genuine biological variations, including batch-specific cell types. From a gene perspective, the method captures cell-type-specific gene co-expression patterns and facilitating the identification of marker genes. Additionally, scHetG is extended to integrate datasets that encompass distinct genes across batches, which is beneficial for investigating more comprehensive genotype-phenotype relationships.

Materials and methods

In this section, we will explore the scHetG method in seven components, encompassing data preprocessing, graph construction, graph encoder, graph decoder, contrastive learning, training process, and softmax transformation. Additionally, we will introduce the datasets utilized in this work.

An overview of the scHetG method

As exhibited in the workflow (Fig. 1), through the construction of a heterogeneous graph that represents the inter-batch cell–cell relationships and intra-batch cell–gene relationships derived from multi-batch gene expression matrices, scHetG converts the integration problem to a node embedding problem given the heterogeneous graph. This facilitates the simultaneous attainment of low-dimensional embeddings of cells and genes. A autoencoder is utilized to address this problem due to its outstanding capability in capturing the latent embeddings of the data (Fig. 1A, B). And a contrastive learning module is used to constrain the cell embeddings obtained by the autoencoder, thus ensuring that the method has more robust integration performance (Fig. 1C, D).

In particular, scHetG employs a graph encoder, which is a multilayer heterogeneous graph neural network (HetGNN) leveraging LightGCN [24], to encode cell and gene embeddings with higher-order structural information derived from the heterogeneous graph (Fig. 1B). Each layer of the HetGNN accepts a multi-batch cell–gene heterogeneous graph of interactions and node embeddings from the previous layer (randomly initialized node embeddings for the first layer of HetGNN). The node embeddings are updated through the mutual transfer of information among the first-order cell-to-gene and gene -to-cell neighbouring nodes. The final cell and gene embeddings are attained by the weighted summation of the cross-layer node embeddings, with the employment of learnable weights.

Subsequently, for each pair of final cell and gene embeddings obtained from the graph encoder, we first spliced the cell embeddings onto the batch one-hot vectors. Then, along with the gene embeddings and the heterogeneous graphs, we estimated the ZINB parameters for each cell–gene pair using the generalized matrix factorization (GMF) [25] algorithm. The negative log-likelihood function of the ZINB distribution is treated as the reconstruction loss function, thereby facilitating the reconstruction of the gene expression matrix for each batch, which also represents the complete interaction information between cells and genes in the graph.

Given the potential incompleteness of biological information retained through exclusive reliance on graph autoencoder, such as neighbourhood information between cells across batches, which is not directly captured in the graph, the constrastive learning module is employed to preserve this information. In particular, the MNNs identified in the canonical correlation analysis (CCA) space and the KNNs identified in principal component analysis (PCA) space are serverd as anchors and positives, and negatives are randomly selected in accordance with the Louvain preclustering clusters (Fig. 1C). The constrastive learning loss function is employed to facilitate the attraction of anchor-positive and the repulsion of anchor-negative (Fig. 1D), thereby ensuring the preservation of cellular neighbourhood structures and the elimination of batch effects in the final cell embeddings, resulting in superior cell clustering results.

Softmax transformation used to caputure gene co-expression patterns

Due to the tendency of graph neural networks to over-smooth, gene embeddings obtained from the graph encoder may exhibit insufficient differentiation, even among genes from distinct biological types.

After model training, considering the distinct clustering of cell embeddings and the retained structural information between cells and genes, a softmax transformation was employed to map the gene embeddings obtained from the graph encoder into the same latent space as the cell embeddings. This mapping ensures that gene embeddings, particularly those of marker genes, are closely associated with cell types. Specifically, marker genes within the same cell type exhibit attraction, while those from different cell types exhibit repulsion. This association facilitates downstream analyses, such as gene co-expression studies. Implementation

The scHetG package is implemented in Python 3.9. The required input of the scHetG software is an scRNA-seq read count matrix with batch labels. In the encoder, the embedding dimension is set to 64, and the number of convolution layers is set to 2. A two-layer graph convolution captures second-order neighbourhood information, while a multi-layer graph convolution may lead to over-smoothing and increased computational complexity. In contrastive learning, the CCA reduction dimension is set at 10. The numbers of nearest neighbours of and are set to 10 and 5, respectively, due to the greater probability of closer neighbouring cells belonging to the same cell type. The k-m-ratio hyperparameter, which denotes the ratio of the number of kNN pairs sampled to the number of MNN pairs, is set to 0.1 by default. The preclustering resolution is set to 0.2, considering that a smaller resolution leads to the identification of larger clusters of cells, increasing the likelihood that cells not in the same cluster belong to different cell types. The margin is set to 1.5. For datasets with common genes, the training epochs amounts to 200, and the early stop training epochs is no less than 50. For datasets containing distinct genes, PCA (with a reduction dimension of 50) is employed to identify MNNs, and the training epochs is compelled to be set at 250. To compare the final cell embedding, clustering was performed using Louvain algorithm [30] with a resolution of 0.5. In the calculation of the total loss, and are set to 1 and 0.0001 by default. A comparison for different values of is presented in Supplementary Fig. S1. For clustering metric NMI, the median and maximum lines for are the highest. For integration metric GC, the median and maximum values for are nearly equal to the highest median and maximum lines observed for other levels, respectively. Considering the excellent performance of scHetG on existing datasets with default hyperparameters, we recommend utilizing these default settings, particularly for datasets containing up to 40 000 cells. For cell counts exceeding 40 000, if the user wishes to adjust the relative weight of the contrastive learning loss, we suggest modifying the value of alpha within the range of 0.1–10, using a step size of 0.05 for each adjustment.

Results

In order to evalute the effect and scalability of scHetG, experiments were conducted on multiple datasets derived from various species and tissues.

The results were visualized by employing UMAP [42]. The clustering performance was evaluated through Accuracy (ACC) [43], Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) [44] and Adjustment Rand Index (ARI) [45], and the integration performance was evaluated through Batch Effect Removal (BER) [46] and Graph Connectivity (GC) [47, 48]. Local Inverse Simpson’s Index (LISI) [8] serves as a comprehensive metric for quantifying the effectiveness of batch effect correction and the preservation of biological variation. Specifically, integration LISI (iLISI) is used to assess the effectiveness of batch effect removal, and cell-type LISI (cLISI) is employed to evaluate the preservation of biological variations. Combined observation of iLISI and cLISI to assess the balance of methods in terms of integration and clustering. The details of the metrics are provided in Supplementary Note 1.

The performance of scHetG was appraised in contrast to eight methods, including Seurat [6], Scanorama [7], Harmony [8], BBKNN [9], scVI [11], Cell BLAST [13], INSCT [14], and DeepBID [16] and SIMBA [17]. Prior to performing preprocessing for all comparison methods, genes expressed in fewer than 3 cells and cells with fewer than 200 expressed genes in the raw dataset were filtered out. Subsequently, all comparison methods are implemented using the default parameters and the default preprocessing steps provided in the tutorial (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

The results indicated that scHetG manifested outstanding performance in eliminating batch effects, maintaining cellular biological variations, capturing gene embeddings associated with cell types and identifying novel marker gene.

scHetG effectively removes batch effcts and identifies batch–specific cell type in simulated dataset

To validate the integration capability of scHetG, we conducted experiments using a simulated dataset. This dataset comprised 3500 cells and 5000 genes, organized into three batches across four groups. Specifically, batches 1, 2, and 3 encompassed groups 1, 2, and 3, respectively, while group 4 was exclusive to batch 3. Figure 2, which was obtained through visualizing cell embeddings derived from diverse methods and calculating metrics, represented the integrated comparison of the simulated dataset. As illustrated in Fig. 2(A) and 2(B), scHetG thoroughly sparated cells of diverse types, including group 4 and mixed cells from different batches. Harmony, Scanorama, INSCT, and DeepBID didn’t integrate the different batches of datasets effectively. BBKNN, scVI, Cell BLAST, and SIMBA appeared overcorrected, as they mixed group 4 with the the remainder of the cell types to varying extents. Regarding performance metrics, scHetG achieved values approximating the theoretical best for ACC (1.00), NMI (1.00), ARI (1.00), BER (0.02), GC (1.00). Although the iLISI metric of scHetG was ranked in the middle among all methods, the cLISI metric of scHetG achieved the theoretical best value of 1.00 (Supplementary Fig. S2). These experimental results illustrated that our method functions as a soft integration approach. While it doesn’t completely eliminate the batch effects, it is less prone to overcorrection, thereby facilitating the clustering perfomance of cells.

To evaluate the performance of scHetG on datasets with varying species and tissues, we conducted experiments using five real datasets, including two small-scale datasets called mouse pancreas and human lung, three mid-scale datasets called mouse atlas, human pancreas, and human pancreas 2.

Comparing the UMAP visualization of the different methods (Fig. 3A, B) on the mouse atlas dataset, scHetG effectively integrated the two batches and aggregated epithelial cells, neutrophil cells and T cells, respectively, an achievement not achieved effectively by other comparison methods. Cell BLAST’s UMAP visualization of cell types demonstrated that it separated stromal cells into two groups, one of which had a distribution that closely resembles the neutrophil cells. Besides, the distribution of epithelial cells obtained by each of comparison methods other than Cell BLAST and DeepBID was discrete. T-cell embeddings obtained by DeepBID were clustered in two distinct locations.

scHetG mines for consistency of the same type cells in large-scale datasets

As the scale of scRNA-seq expands, the significance of integration methods that are scalable to large datasets becomes increasingly significant. To evaluate the scalability of scHetG, experiments were conducted on two large-scale datasets. The mouse brain dataset, consisting of two batches and 40,000 cells, was sampled by Yu et al. [15] in a manner that maintained the proportionality of the number of cell types, as observed in a larger dataset of 833,206 cells. Similar to the original dataset, the sampled dataset was predominantly composed of neurons, while other cell types were rare. The human heart dataset, consisting of 39,187 cells, was composed of 10 batches randomly sampled from a normal human heart atlas of 485,193 cells. Experiments were conducted on these datasets using the eight aforementioned methods, except for Seurat [6], which was excluded due to its high memory consumption and extended running time.

Comparing the UMAP visualization results of the diverse methods on the mouse brain dataset, it was observed that the neuron cells were distributed in diverse locations for scVI, Harmony, Scanorama, Cell BLAST, DeepBID, and SIMBA, whereas scHetG, INSCT, and BBKNN clustered the majority of these cells together. INSCT did not distinguish between mural and polydendrocyte cells, BBKNN mingled ependymal cells with other cell types, but scHetG achieved these differentiations (Fig. 4A, 4B). Regarding clustering metrics, scHetG achieved the highest values for ACC, NMI and ARI (Fig. 4C). Concerning integration metrics, scHetG presented the highest BER and GC (Fig. 4D). The GC indicated that scHetG effectively extracted consistency among cells of the same type. With regard to comprehensive integration metrics, scHetG achieved the fifth highest cLISI, which was only about 0.01 higher than the best value. Besides, scHetG has the second highest iLISI (Supplementary Fig. S12). These results suggest that scHetG is effective not only in maintaining cellular heterogeneity but also in facilitating the extraction of consistency among cells of the same type when integrating multiple batches of scRNA-seq datasets.

Gene embeddings obtained by scHetG using the softmax transformation are associated with cell types and facilitate the identification of marker genes

The embeddings obtained through scHetG hold significant biological relevance. Gene embeddings are commonly employed in gene co-expression analysis, a systems biology approach used to describe correlation patterns among genes [49]. Additionally, identification of marker genes is also a common downstream analysis for gene embeddings [17].

Previous studies have demonstrated that eigengenes, which are genes captured by PCA loading, possess the capability to represent network modules in gene co-expression networks [50]. It is assumed that the gene embeddings obtained by scHetG are specific to the cell type and are aggregated in the embedding space.

In this experiment, a comparison was conducted between scHetG and SIMBA [17], which was also capable of obtaining significant gene embeddings. Furthermore, PCA for normalized gene expression data was utilized as a benchmarking technique. Gene co-expression experiments were carried out on two actual datasets: the human lung and the human pancreas. For each dataset, the marker genes specific to a given cell type were retained. It is anticipated that the embeddings of marker genes will be similar for the same cell type and dissimilar for different cell types. The marker genes for the human pancreas dataset encompassed five cell types, derived from the CellMarker database [51]. The marker genes for the human lung dataset encompassed four cell types, which were derived from the SCEA database [52]. Subsequently, the ratio between inter-cell-type similarity and intra-cell-type similarity [53] was computed, with the details of the ratio provided in Supplementary Note 4.

Figure 5, which was acquired by visualizing gene embeddings derived from diverse methods and computing the inter/intra ratio, represented the comparison of marker gene embeddings on the human lung dataset. The marker gene embeddings of the lung endothelial cell obtained by scHetG was distributed centrally and separated from the marker gene embeddings of the lung secretory cell. The distribution of marker gene embeddings corresponding to lung endothelial cells from SIMBA was discrete, and the gene embeddings corresponding to lung endothelial cells and lung secretory cells obtained from normalized data were mixed. We then calculated the similarities between the marker gene embeddings and scHetG led to the best ratio between inter- and intra-cell-type similarities. The marker gene embeddings obtained from scHetG on the human pancreas dataset also had the best visualization and ratio (Supplementary Note 5 and Supplementary Fig. S15).

Moreover, we identify new marker genes by calculating the similarity between the embeddings of each gene and those of known marker genes. Specifically, we first calculate the sum of similarities between the embeddings of each gene and the embeddings of five known marker genes of the same type to derive a similarity score, where the details of the calculation of pairwise similarity are as in equation (3) in Supplementary Note 4. The top five genes with the highest scores are then selected as potential marker genes of the known type.

For the human lung dataset, the known marker genes associated with the lung macrophage cell type are ENSG00000165140, ENSG00000170323, ENSG00000173369, ENSG00000173372, and ENSG00000213145. It was found that all five genes (ENSG000000191 69, ENSG00000117984, ENSG00000130208, ENSG00000135047, and ENSG00000260314) with the highest similarity scores are all marker genes associated with the lung macrophage cell type.

For the human pancreas dataset, the known marker genes associated with the beta cell type are G6PC2, GPSM1, GREM1, IAPP, and IGF2. It was found that three (ATP2A3, CDKN1C, and DLK1) of the five genes with the highest similarity scores were identified as marker genes associated with beta cell type.

The results indicated that scHetG, employing softmax transformation, was able to capture marker gene embeddings associated with cell types and facilitate the identification of novel marker genes when a few known marker genes of the same type are available.

scHetG is extended to integrate datasets encompassing distinct genes between batches.

Genes within scRNA-seq datasets, originating from diverse sources, are not uniformly prevalent, and previous integration approaches typically are performed only when genes are shared across batches. However, scHetG is capable of overcoming this constraint.

In this experiment, the mouse pancreas dataset and the simulation dataset were used. Specifically, 3000 highly variable genes were simultaneously and independently selected from both batches of the mouse pancreas dataset, and were, respectively, used as the dataset encompassing common genes and the dataset encompassing distinct genes. In a manner consistent with the abovementioned dataset construction strategy, with respect to the simulated dataset generated by Xu et al. [31], 2500 highly variable genes were selected for batch 1 and batch 2 simultaneously and independently to constitute the datasets for comparison.

With respect to the mouse pancreas dataset, scHetG achieved highly comparable metrics in the context of common genes and distinct genes (Fig. 6). On the simulated dataset, scHetG attained the theoretically optimal metrics for both common and distinct genes (Supplementary Fig. S16).

The integrated comparison of scHetG on the mouse pancreas dataset under conditions of common and distinct genes between batches. The bars are colored in accordance with whether the genes are common between batches or distinct.

The results indicate that scHetG also integrates effectively datasets that possess distinct genes in different batches.

Ablation study

For the purpose of investigating the efficacy of HetGNN, contrastive learning module, and the ZINB decoder, the performance of diverse variants of scHetG was assessed. Specifically, scHetG (multi-layer perceptron, MLP) indicates the use of a MLP instead of a HetGNN in the autoencoder. The number of layers and dimensions in this MLP match those used in the complete scHetG. scHetG (DOT+MSE) implies the application of a dot product decoder accompanied by MSE loss, and scHetG (w/o cl) indicates the absence of contrastive learning loss. As illustrated in Fig. 7, which depicted the boxplot of the ACC, NMI, ARI, BER and GC metrics for the various variants, the complete scHetG possessed the highest midlines and maximum lines across all metrics, with the exception of BER, where lower values indicates superior performance and scHetG achieved the lowest values. Regarding cLISI metrics, the complete scHetG exhibited the lowest median and the lowest line, indicating the highest capability for preserving biological information. And the midline of cLISI for scHetG (MLP) was much higher than that of the other methods, which implied that HetGNN is significant for the preservation of biological information. Regarding the iLISI metrics, although the complete scHetG did not achieve as high a median line as the other three variants, it had the highest minimum line and the second-highest maximum line. Although scHetG (w/o cl) attained the highest maximum line, it scaled poorly across different datasets (Supplementary Fig. S17).

Boxplots of the performance of scHetG and its variants on seven real datasets. The boxes are colored by scHetG variants.

The results indicated that the HetGNN, contrastive learning and the ZINB decoder contribute to the effectiveness of this method.

Computational time and memory usage

We measured the running time and maximum memory usage of scHetG and the other nine methods on the seven real datasets (Supplementary Fig. S18). In the experiments, Seurat, Scanorama, Harmony, and BBKNN used only CPUs, and the other six methods needed GPUs.

In terms of running time, scHetG demonstrated mid-range performance compared to other methods for both small-scale and mid-scale datasets. Although scHetG has a longer running time than most algorithms on large-scale datasets, it is significantly shorter than the longest computation time (Supplementary Fig. S18A). In terms of memory usage, scHetG’s maximum memory consumption was higher than that of most alternative methods but was of the same order of magnitude as that of other deep learning-based approaches (Supplementary Figure. S18B).

To address the potential problem of increased training time with fine-tuning, especially for users with limited computational resources, it is recommended to use a smaller subgraph during the graph encoding and decoding phases of each training epoch. This approach may slightly impact method performance but helps mitigate the computational burden.

Discussion

In this work, we put forward the scHetG, an innovative scRNA-seq integration approach that is devised to offer structure-preserved cell and gene embeddings. scHetG reacts directly to cell–gene relationships and indirectly to cell–cell relationships by establishing a heterogeneous graph and employing a graph autoencoder to extract higher-order structural information. While the risk of overcorrection cannot be entirely eliminated, we can mitigate its impact. We gently eliminated the batch effect by indirectly converting batch information among different batches of cells through their second-order neighbours in the heterogeneous graph. This method is less susceptible to overcorrection than approaches that directly adjust cell embeddings across different batches by constructing batch correction vectors or clustering centers. Furthermore, the structural information of cells and genes is preserved through graph reconstruction, thereby maintaining the biological signals of both cell and gene embeddings. Additionally, the contrastive learning loss constrains the cell embeddings through neighbourhood relationships between cells, produced by the encoder, thereby enhancing integration performance.

The results of both simulated and real data investigations have demonstrated that scHetG is competent in capturing cell and gene embeddings that preserve the structure information. Regarding cell embeddings, the experimental results suggested that scHetG effectively eliminates batch effects while preserving cellular heterogeneity, encompassing batch-specific cell types. Regarding gene embeddings, scHetG preserves the correlation among marker genes and identifies patterns of gene co-expression, which may reflect true biological signal (e.g. pathway). Moreover, scHetG’s ability to capture gene embeddings associated with specific cell types facilitates the discovery of novel marker genes, which offer valuable reference information for annotating cell types in single-cell expression data [54]. Additionally, scHetG eliminates the constraint that genes must be common among different batches of datasets during integration.

In many existing methods, including our own, the processes of obtaining cell embeddings and performing cell clustering are conducted separately, potentially leading to the accumulation of computational bias. To mitigate this issue, we will consider reconstructing a weighted neighbourhood graph of cells through the graph decoder. Clustering algorithms such as Louvain [30] can directly utilize this graph, which may be more appropriate than the one obtained from latent cell embeddings for clustering purposes.

Our work substantiates the feasibility of extracting cell and gene information through the utilization of graphs. In the future, we have the intention to expand this method to the domain of single cell multi-omics [55, 56] and spatially resolved transcriptomics [57]. This will facilitate the discovery of the regulatory mechanisms among the features of diverse omics and the examination of the influence of spatial context on biological regulatory activities.
