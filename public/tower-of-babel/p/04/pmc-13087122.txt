Chronic high-altitude exposure and cognitive health in Chinese college students: a 4-year longitudinal neuroimaging study
Authors: Hong Li, Qian Zhang, Siyao Zeng, Yang Zhou, Xinqin Liu, Wenbin Zhang, Wenjing Luo, Yazhuo Kong, Xiaoming Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-altitude environments pose significant challenges to human cognition due to hypoxic conditions. The long-term effects of chronic high-altitude exposure on cognitive functions remain poorly understood. Cognitive decline in this age group may compromise academic performance, occupational safety and future productivity. In this 4-year longitudinal study, we examined 69 college freshmen who relocated from sea level to the high-altitude region of Tibet. Over four years, we conducted comprehensive cognitive assessments and brain MRI scans at baseline, year two, and year four. Behavioral data revealed persistent impairments in cognitive functions such as working memory and psychomotor function among those exposed to high altitudes for extended periods. Neuroimaging analyses demonstrated a notable decrease in gray matter volume and resting-state brain activities, specifically in the left putamen. These neural alterations were significantly correlated with the observed cognitive deficits, and importantly, the effect of exposure time on working memory was mediated by the left putamen volume. Chronic high-altitude exposure in otherwise healthy young adults is associated with enduring cognitive deficits and convergent structural and functional alterations in the left putamen. These findings highlight cognitive health as an important but under-recognized public-health concern in populations who study or work at high altitude, and suggest that monitoring and preventive strategies may be warranted for long-term high-altitude migrants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42645-2.

Introduction

High-altitude regions are home to hundreds of millions of people worldwide and host substantial numbers of temporary migrants, including college students, military personnel and workers involved in construction, mining and transportation. For these groups, chronic exposure to hypobaric hypoxia represents a pervasive environmental risk factor that may compromise neurocognitive functioning, academic achievement and occupational safety. However, most evidence on high-altitude health effects comes from cross-sectional comparisons between indigenous high-altitude residents and low-altitude populations, making it difficult to disentangle the specific impact of long-term hypoxic exposure from cultural, socioeconomic and developmental confounders.

High-altitude hypoxic environments can lead to physiological and cognitive changes, often due to the body’s adaptive responses to low oxygen availability. Most human, even those who have lived on the plateau for generations, are not totally adapted to high-altitude exposure, as indicated by headache, sleep disruptions1, cognitive impairment2, polycythemia, and reduced fertility3. Crucially, cognitive impairment is of clear hypoxia relevance. Although controversial findings have been reported, Yan et al. and Niermeyer et al. compared low- and high-altitude residents and identified that high-altitude residents had lower accuracy in a verbal-working memory task, longer reaction times in spatial- and verbal-working memory tasks4,5. In theory, deficits of cognitive function from high altitude could occur mainly through two very different speculations. Some studies speculated that this deficit could be due to hypoxia-ischemia, nutritional deficits, and/or socioeconomic considerations6,7. There have also been speculations that educational and cultural practices as well as nutritional deficiencies such as iron-deficiency anemia might also contribute to cognitive deficits in high-altitude residents8,9. Despite numerous studies documented of mechanisms underlying high-altitude-induced health challenges, the cognitive-related mechanism underlying longitudinal high-altitude-induced alteration remains largely unclear10.

In parallel, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies have shown that hypoxic exposure can induce persistent neurobiological changes. Structural MRI has reported cortical abnormalities that may persist for months after high-altitude exposure, particularly in sensorimotor and temporo-occipital regions11,12. Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) provides complementary insights into intrinsic brain function, which are closely linked to cognition. Specifically, the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) reflects the intensity of spontaneous low-frequency BOLD activity within a region, while resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) captures temporal synchronization between regions, offering a measure of large-scale network integrity that supports cognitive control13,14. Previous studies have shown that alterations in ALFF and rsFC, especially within fronto-striatal and cingulo-opercular circuits, are associated with working memory and attentional performance15,16. However, most existing studies rely on cross-sectional comparisons between high-altitude immigrants and sea-level controls, making it difficult to distinguish the longitudinal effects of chronic hypoxia from demographic and sociocultural confounding factors.

As the effects of the hypoxic environment on an individual’s brain are chronic and persistent, the current study conducted an ambitious four years’ longitudinal panel study on a young healthy high-altitude immigrant population, allowing us to clarify the cognitive changes as well as the brain structural and functional alterations associated with chronic high-altitude exposure. Previous neuroimaging studies have demonstrated that extensively brain areas (e.g., prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, parietal gyrus, thalamus, hippocampus, putamen) were associated with cognition (e.g., working memory and executive functions) and chronic high-altitude exposure11,17,18. In addition, our previous work found the reduction of gray matter volumes in left putamen and caudate, which was correlated with decreased cognitive performance after two years’ high-altitude exposure12. Based on previous literatures, we hypothesized that brain structural and functional alternations would be identified continuously after several year’s chronic high-altitude exposure, and these changes may be associated with decreased cognitive function. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first panel study over the four years to address potential cognitive impairments and associated neuroimaging changes in high-altitude immigrants.

Methods

Study design

In July 2014, 69 healthy right-handed high-school graduates (48 males) from Shaanxi Province were recruited as the experimental cohort. All planned to enroll in a four-year undergraduate program at Tibet University in Lhasa (average altitude: 3,658 m). Participants underwent baseline neuropsychological testing and MRI before relocation, and were followed up in June 2015, May 2016, and May 2018; MRI scans were acquired again in 2016 and 2018 in Lhasa. During follow-up, high-altitude exposure duration and major medical events were recorded.

A control group of healthy college students was also part of the study, with data sourced from the publicly available Southwest University Longitudinal Imaging Multimodal (SLIM) Brain Data Repository: A Long-term Test-Retest Sample of Young Healthy Adults in Southwest China. We selected and downloaded longitudinal MRI data that were matched with the experimental group in terms of both gender and age12.

Participants

A total of 69 healthy right-handed high school graduates (48 males) were recruited from high-altitude and 20 participants were excluded because of incomplete MRI or questionnaires. Finally, 49 participants (32 males) were included in the current study. Participants were screened according to the following criteria: (1) 17 ~ 20 years old; (2) altitude of permanent residence is less than 900 m; (3) no high-altitude exposure (≥ 2500 m) in the past; (4) no history of smoking and drug abuse; (5) no history of medications that may affect cognitive function or carbonic anhydrase activity; (6) no history of chronic or genetic diseases, defined as the absence of clinically diagnosed neurological, psychiatric, cardiovascular, metabolic, or hereditary disorders, as assessed through medical history review and structured health screening prior to enrollment. All enrolled participants in this study signed the informed consent prior to the experiment on the premise of fully understanding the content of the experiment. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty of Fourth Military Medical University (Registry no. KY20143344-1) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles for medical research involving human individual as defined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

A control group consisting of 49 healthy college students (32 males) was also included in the study. Young adults were eligible for the SLIM if they were university freshmen or sophomores and proficient in Chinese. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) MRI-related exclusions, including claustrophobia, metallic implants, Meniere’s syndrome, or a history of fainting in the past 6 months; (2) aged between 17 and 20 years; (3) current psychiatric or neurological disorders; (4) use of psychiatric medications within the three months prior to scanning; (5) pregnancy; or (6) a history of head injury. The SLIM database was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Brain Imaging Centre at Southwest University. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and for the two youngest participants (aged 17), written consent was also obtained from their college instructors, who acted as their guardians.

Neuropsychological tests (NST)

The neuropsychological test battery consisted of the following four tests: (1) visual memory tests, providing immediate (IVIM) and delayed (DVIM) visual memory indices for figures and shapes; (2) verbal memory tests, providing immediate (IVBM) and delayed (DVBM) verbal memory indices for word lists; (3) simple reaction time tests, yielding visual (VSRT) and auditory (ASRT) simple reaction times as measures of psychomotor speed; and (4) recognition reaction time tests, yielding visual (VCRT) and auditory (ACRT) recognition reaction times in response to target versus distractor stimuli. Each test was scored according to standardized procedures, and the reliability and validity of the CNS Vital Signs battery (http://www.cnsvs.com/) have been well established in previous research19.

Brain imaging

For the experimental group, MRI data were acquired on a 3.0-T GE Discovery MR750 scanner (GE Healthcare, CT) at Xijing Hospital. High-resolution T1-weighted anatomical images were obtained using a spoiled gradient-recalled echo (SPGR) sequence (TR = 2530 ms, TE = 3.5 ms, flip angle = 7°, matrix = 256 × 256, 192 slices, slice thickness = 1.0 mm, voxel size = 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm³). Resting-state fMRI data were collected using an echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 90°, field of view = 220 × 220 mm², matrix = 64 × 64, 30 slices, slice thickness = 4 mm, voxel size = 3.5 × 3.5 × 4.0 mm³, 180 volumes). For each participant, T1-weighted structural images were acquired first, followed by resting-state fMRI. The T1-weighted scan lasted approximately 7 ~ 8 min, and the resting-state fMRI scan lasted approximately 6 min. During the resting-state scan, participants were instructed to keep their eyes closed, remain still, stay awake, and not engage in any specific cognitive task.

For the control group, MRI data were acquired on a 3.0-T Siemens Trio scanner (Siemens Medical, Erlangen, Germany) at the Southwest University Center for Brain Imaging. T1-weighted images were obtained using a magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient-echo (MPRAGE) sequence (TR = 1900 ms, TE = 2.52 ms, TI = 900 ms, flip angle = 9°, matrix = 256 × 256, 176 slices, slice thickness = 1.0 mm, voxel size = 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm³). Resting-state fMRI data were collected using an EPI sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 90°, field of view = 220 × 220 mm², 30 slices, slice thickness = 3 mm, gap = 1 mm, voxel size = 3.4 × 3.4 × 3.0 mm³).

Although different MRI scanners were used across groups, scanner-related effects were mitigated by emphasizing within-subject longitudinal analyses, applying identical preprocessing/analysis pipelines, and interpreting group differences primarily in terms of relative longitudinal changes rather than absolute signal values.

Imaging data processing

Structural MRI data analysis

Structural MRI data were processed using FreeSurfer v7.0.0 (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu). Standard preprocessing steps included motion correction, skull stripping, Talairach transformation, hemispheric separation, and gray/white matter segmentation20,21. Each participant’s cortex was parcellated into standard gyral and sulcal regions on the reconstructed surface, aligned to the FreeSurfer average template based on cortical folding patterns, and smoothed with a 10-mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel. All segmentations were visually inspected and manually corrected when necessary.

Cortical thickness at each vertex was defined as the shortest distance between the white and pial surfaces. Subcortical volumes were derived using FreeSurfer’s automated segmentation. Volumes of subcortical structures (e.g., caudate nucleus, putamen, thalamus, amygdala, hippocampus) were extracted bilaterally. Group differences and longitudinal changes were examined using one-way repeated-measures ANOVA, controlling for age, sex, and total brain volume22.

Resting-state fMRI data pre-processing

Resting-state fMRI preprocessing and seed-based connectivity analyses were performed using the FMRI Expert Analysis Tool (FEAT) within FSL. Preprocessing included motion correction (MCFLIRT)23, distortion correction with field maps (FUGUE), brain extraction (BET)24, spatial smoothing with a 5-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel, and high-pass temporal filtering (cutoff=100s). Temporal autocorrelation was modeled using FMRIB’s Improved Linear Model. Functional images were co-registered to individual T1-weighted images using boundary-based registration25, and then normalized to the MNI-152 2-mm template using initial linear registration (FLIRT23, followed by nonlinear registration (FNIRT23.

Functional MRI: amplitude of low frequency fluctuations (ALFF) analysis

Amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) was computed using DPABI26. Time series for each voxel were transformed into the frequency domain (0–0.25 Hz) using fast Fourier transform, and the square root of the power spectrum was averaged within the 0.01–0.08 Hz band to obtain ALFF values27. Voxel-wise ALFF values were normalized by the global mean ALFF. Group-level ALFF analyses within the putamen were performed using FSL Randomise with 5000 permutations and threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE; p < 0.05, corrected). In the experimental group, Pearson correlation analyses were conducted between putamen ALFF and neuropsychological measures.

Functional MRI: resting-state seed-based functional connectivity

Given the observed reduction in left putamen volume after high-altitude exposure, the left putamen was used as a seed for seed-based functional connectivity analysis. Resting-state seed-based functional connectivity analysis was conducted through a general linear model (GLM) framework following the standard procedures28. Mean time series from the seed and nuisance regions (cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and white matter) were extracted for each participant. At the first level, the seed time series was entered as a regressor of interest in a general linear model, with CSF and white matter signals as nuisance regressors. Group-level analyses were performed using a mixed-effects FLAME approach. Statistical maps were thresholded at Z > 3.1 with a cluster-corrected significance level of p < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

For neuropsychological outcomes in the experimental group, one-way repeated-measures ANOVA (within-subject factor: time), controlling for age and sex, was used. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test, and Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied when sphericity was violated. Significant main effects (p < 0.05) were followed by Bonferroni-corrected post hoc paired t-tests, and partial eta-squared (ηp²) was reported as the effect size.

Subcortical volumes and regional ALFF values were also analyzed using one-way repeated-measures ANOVA, with age, sex, total brain volume, and head motion (mean frame-wise displacement) as covariates. In the experimental group, Pearson correlations were computed between imaging metrics (ALFF and functional connectivity) and neuropsychological performance.

Finally, bootstrapped mediation analyses (PROCESS macro in SPSS, 5000 bootstrap samples) were conducted to test whether structural and functional characteristics of the left putamen mediated the association between high-altitude exposure duration and neuropsychological outcomes, with exposure time as the independent variable, neuropsychological scores as dependent variables, and putamen measures as mediators (Fig. 5). Mediation was considered significant when the 95% bias-corrected confidence interval for the indirect effect did not include zero.

Results

Behavioral results

Demographic information

Flow chart of participant recruitment and follow-up in this study were presented in Fig. 1. The average age of the participants (M: 48; F: 21) was 18.2 ± 0.3 (range: 17.5–19.1) in Year 2014 (Time point 1). All participants had just completed the 3-year high school curriculum, and their academic performance in the entrance examination was comparable (513.3 ± 13.6, range: 483 ~ 544), therefore they were admitted to the same university cohort. In 2015 (Time point 2), cumulative high-altitude residence time was 270.0 ± 7.4 days (range: 257 ~ 299) and increased to 280.8 ± 7.3 days in 2016 (Time point 3; range: 268 ~ 314), reflecting academic-year exposure (excluding vacations). By 2018 (Time point 4), after two additional years of residence, cumulative exposure reached nearly 680 days.

The average age of the control group (M:27, F:22) was 19.16 ± 0.86 at the baseline (Time point 1). The average number of days between the first scan and second scan were 515 days (Time point 2), and the days between the second scan and third scan were 742 days (Time point 3). Notably, the control group only have the MRI data both at baseline and follow-up.

Cognitive performance declined

Participants in the experimental group completed the neuropsychological tests both at baseline and follow-up studies. The neuropsychological features are shown in Table 1.

Accuracy in the verbal/visual memory test

One-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed significant evidence for a main effect of accuracy for both immediate verbal memory (IVBM, F (3, 144) = 4.762, p = 0.011, ηp2 = 0.090, Table 1) and immediate visual memory (IVIM, F (3, 144) = 5.574, p = 0.011, ηp2 = 0.090, Table 1). Post-hoc paired-sample t-tests showed that the accuracy of IVBM in the experimental group were significantly higher in Year 2014 than that in the Year 2016 and 2018 (both p < 0.001), and the accuracy of IVIM in the experimental group were significantly higher in Year 2014 than that in the Year 2018 (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between Year 2015, Year 2016, and Year 2018 for either IVBM or IVIM (all p > 0.9). The trend lines of IVBM and IVIM over time are shown in Fig. 2(A).

One-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant evidence for a main effect of the number of correct responses for both delayed verbal memory (DVBM, F (3, 144) = 6.447, p = 0.002, ηp2 = 0.118, Table 1) and delayed visual memory test (DVIM, F (3, 144) = 4.953, p = 0.003, ηp2 = 0.094, Table 1). Post-hoc paired-sample t-tests showed that the accuracy of DVBM in the experimental group were significantly higher in Year 2014 than that in the Year 2015, 2016, and 2018 (both p < 0.05), and the accuracy of DVIM in the experimental group were significantly higher in Year 2014 than that in the Year 2016 and 2018 (p < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between Year 2015, Year 2016, and Year 2018 for either DVBM or DVIM (all p > 0.9). The trend lines of DVBM and DVIM over time are shown in Fig. 2(A).

Reaction time in the visual/auditory test

One-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant evidence for a main effect of the reaction times for both visual simple reaction times (VSRT, F (3, 144) = 41.597, p < 0.000, ηp2 = 0.464, Table 1) and auditory simple reaction times (ASRT, F (3, 144) = 159.0, p < 0.000, ηp2 = 0.768, Table 1). Post-hoc paired-sample t-tests showed that the reaction time of VSRT (ms)/ASRT(ms) in the experimental group were significantly longer in Year 2015, 2016, and 2018 than those in the Year 2014 (all p < 0.001). In addition, the reaction time of VSRT (ms)/ASRT (ms) were significantly longer in 2016 and 2018 than that in the Year 2015 (both p < 0.001). And the reaction time of VSRT (ms) were significantly longer in 2018 than that in the Year 2016 (p < 0.015). The trend lines of VSRT (ms) and ASRT (ms) over time are shown in Fig. 2(B).

One-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant evidence for a main effect of the reaction times for both visual recognition reaction times (VRRT, F (3, 144) = 17.687, p = 0.000, ηp2 = 0.269, Table 1) and auditory recognition reaction times (ARRT, F (3, 144) = 40.234, p = 0.000, ηp2 = 0.456, Table 1). Post-hoc paired-sample t-tests showed that the reaction time of VRRT (ms) in the experimental group were significantly longer in Year 2015, 2016, and 2018 than those in the Year 2014 (all p < 0.05). And the reaction time of VRRT (ms) were significantly longer in 2018 than that in the Year 2015 (p < 0.001). In addition, the reaction time of ARRT (ms) in the experimental group were significantly longer in Year 2015, 2016 and 2018 than that in the Year 2014 (all p < 0.001); the reaction time of ARRT (ms) were significantly longer in 2016 and 2018 than that in the Year 2015 (both p < 0.001); the reaction time of ARRT (ms) were significantly longer in 2018 than that in the Year 2016 (p < 0.015). The trend lines of VRRT (ms) and ARRT (ms) over time are shown in Fig. 2(B).

MRI results

Comparisons in baseline brain characteristics between groups

Imbalance between groups in baseline variables that may influence outcome (e.g., brain characteristics) can bias statistical tests. To avoid these effects, we first compared brain structural and functional characteristics between the experimental and control groups at baseline. For both cortical thickness and subcortical volumes, we identified no significant differences (all p > 0.05) between experimental and control groups at the baseline.

Decreased gray matter volume in the putamen

For cortical thickness, no significant differences were observed in both experimental and control groups after four years’ high-altitude exposure and sea-level living while controlling the effect of gender. For subcortical volumes, one-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant evidence for a main effect of the putamen after four years’ high-altitude exposure while controlling for ICV and gender (F (2, 96) = 5.844, p = 0.004, ηp2 = 0.111, Table 2; Fig. 3(A)). Post-hoc paired-sample t-tests showed that the volume of putamen in the experimental group were significantly decreased in Year 2016 and 2018 than that in the Year 2014 (both p < 0.001). No significant main effects were observed in other subcortical volumes (i.e., the thalamus, caudate nucleus, hippocampus, and amygdala, all p > 0.05, Table 2) for the experimental group. Additionally, there were no significant differences in subcortical volumes (i.e., the thalamus, caudate, putamen, hippocampus, and amygdala, all p > 0.05, Table 3) after four years’ sea-level living while controlling for ICV and gender for the control group.

Given that previous studies have demonstrated that only the left putamen exists structural morphological changes after high-altitude exposure, in order to explore whether there was a lateralization in our study, we next compared the alterations in the volume of the left and right putamen in the experimental group after four years’ high-altitude exposure, respectively. One-way repeated-measures ANOVA showed strong evidence for a main effect of the left putamen volume after four years’ high-altitude exposure while controlling for ICV and gender (F (2, 96) = 9.661, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.165, Fig. 3(A)), while the main effect of the right putamen volume was marginally significant (F (2, 96) = 3.332, p = 0.058, ηp2 = 0.065, Fig. 3(A)). Post-hoc paired-sample t-tests showed that the left putamen volume in the experimental group were significantly decreased in Year 2016 and 2018 than that in the Year 2014 (both p < 0.001).

Correlations between the gray matter volume changes in the putamen and cognitive impairments

To further understand the effects of the chronic high-altitude exposure on brain structural plasticity, we conducted correlation analyses between before-after exposure cognitive impairments and putamen volume changes. Subcortical volume alterations in the left putamen volume were identified to be positively correlated with the accuracy changes of IVBM (r = 0.381, p = 0.008, Fig. 3(B) and IVIM (r = 0.335, p = 0.022, Fig. 3(B)), and marginal correlated with the accuracy changes of DVIM (r = 0.256, p = 0.081, Fig. 3(B)); Right putamen volume changes were positively correlated with the accuracy changes of IVIM (r = 0.309, p = 0.032, Fig. 3(B)).

Decreased ALFF value in the putamen

Seed-based functional connectivity

Given that the structure and function of left putamen were altered over the high-altitude exposure, it was chosen as a seed for the resting-state functional connectivity analyses. For the experimental group, one-way repeated-measures ANOVA analysis revealed that the resting-state functional connectivity of left putamen with the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and insula varied over the exposure time. Post hoc analyses showed that the left putamen exhibited weaker functional connectivity with the ACC and insula in the Year 2016 than Year 2014, and showed stronger functional connectivity with the ACC, insula, and frontal gyrus in the Year 2018 than Year 2016 (Z > 3.1, p < 0.05 cluster-wise corrected, Fig. 5(A)). For the control group, one-way repeated-measures ANOVA analysis revealed that the resting-state functional connectivity of left putamen with the insula, cerebellum, frontal and occipital cortex varied over the sea-level living. Post hoc analyses showed that the left putamen exhibited stronger functional connectivity with the ACC, insula, frontal gyrus and occipital cortex in time 3 than time 1, and showed stronger functional connectivity with the insula, and frontal gyrus and occipital cortex in time 3 than time 2 (Z > 3.1, p < 0.05 cluster-wise corrected, Fig. 5(B)).

Discussion

As social, economic, and military activities thrive in China’s plateau region, an influx of individuals from less developed rural areas has emigrated to Tibet. Researchers have begun to pay increasing attention to the impact of high altitudes and low oxygen environments on the human brain. However, longitudinal studies exploring the effects of hypoxia on the brain remain scarce, and those that do exist often suffer from limited follow-up durations due to time and economic constraints. In our study, we devoted special attention to participants aged below 20, recognizing this as a pivotal stage of neurodevelopment. During this phase, the nervous system is acutely vulnerable to the profound impacts of the hypoxic conditions unique to high altitudes. We focused on a representative cohort of Chinese high-altitude immigrants, comprehensively collecting their cognitive performance alongside MRI data. Furthermore, we conducted a longitudinal study spanning four years, ensuring a robust understanding of the long-term cognitive implications of hypoxic exposure among this younger generation. This undertaking holds immense significance in assessing the cognitive sequelae and formulating targeted prevention and intervention strategies. The cognitive and neuroimaging assessments conducted in this study are representative and carry profound research value. This study serves as an important reference for designing tailored prevention and intervention strategies for Chinese immigrants’ cognitive health, laying a basic foundation for future research and applications.

Our results suggest that the favorable association between altitude hypoxia and cognitive performance, found here as in many previous studies, could be associated with preservation of specific brain features in the putamen region. Behavioral data indicates that prolonged exposure to high-altitude impairs cognitive function, including working memory and psychomotor function. In line with these observation, neuroimaging analysis revealed a notable reduction in gray matter volume and regional functional connectivity within the cognitive-related region, specifically the left putamen. Whole-brain functional connectivity analysis showed multiple functional coupling alterations between the insula, ACC, and left putamen. In particular, structural and functional differences in the left putamen prior to and following high-altitude exposure were significantly correlated with individual’s cognitive alterations. The mechanism underlying hypoxia-induced cognitive impairment in this younger generation may be reflected in the structural and functional deficits of the left putamen, or alternatively, it could be attributed to a converging neurodestructive process.

Modulation of high-altitude exposure on cognitive function performance

Consistent with previous observations, our study demonstrated that the participants’ neuropsychological performances were significantly impaired over the duration of altitude exposure, both in terms of accuracy in verbal/visual memory tests and reaction time in visual/ auditory tests. This was evident in their impaired performance on spatial and linguistic working memory tasks compared to controls16,29. As depicted in Fig. 2, there was a year-on-year decline in memory performance, whereas the audio-visual response time increased, indicating the persistent nature of cognitive damage caused by altitude exposure. Notably, these cognitive impairments emerged early during exposure and did not show improvement over the subsequent follow-up period, suggesting persistent cognitive changes associated with chronic high-altitude exposure. This may be partly attributed to alterations in physiological mechanism, encompassing stress30,31, cellular, and molecular processes32. Unfortunately, disentangling these factors remains challenging, as long-term high-altitude exposure results in hyperplasia of red blood cells, leading to cumulative changes in brain structure, fine tissue structure, and function, ultimately having a negative impact on cognitive function.

Brain structural and functional modulations

Previous studies on hypoxia-related brain morphology have reported inconsistent gray matter volume changes. A decrease in gray matter has been attributed to hypoxia-related metabolic stress, whereas an increase may reflect neurogenesis/gliogenesis, synaptogenesis, and/or vascular remodeling33. In our longitudinal cohort, putamen volume was significantly reduced in 2016 and 2018 compared to 2014 after prolonged high-altitude exposure. This regional vulnerability may involve hypoxia-induced disruptions in energy metabolism, neuroinflammatory responses that affect synaptic plasticity, and oxidative stress-related cellular damage. Supporting this framework, animal studies under chronic hypoxia have shown increased levels of lipid peroxidation products, free radicals, and oxidized glutathione34,35. Future studies incorporating metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers in humans will be essential to more directly test these proposed pathways.

Further analyses showed that reduced left putamen volume was significantly associated with cognitive impairment under chronic hypoxic exposure. Beyond its role in motor regulation36, the putamen is critically involved in reinforcement learning, category learning, and cognitive control, suggesting that structural alterations in this region may contribute to altitude-related cognitive decline37–39. In addition, significant ALFF changes were observed in the left putamen, following a non-monotonic trajectory with an increase in 2016 and a decrease in 2018, indicating a potential mid-term inflection point. This pattern may reflect an initial adaptive or compensatory upregulation of spontaneous neural activity under hypoxic stress, followed by functional downregulation as compensatory capacity diminishes with prolonged exposure. Consistently, changes in left putamen ALFF were correlated with alterations in reaction time and verbal memory40, in line with previous high-altitude studies. Given the biennial assessment intervals, shorter-term functional dynamics during early acclimatization may not have been captured41,42. Although the putamen contributes bilaterally to cognition, the left putamen may be more susceptible to hypoxic stress due to hemispheric specialization of fronto-striatal circuits supporting language and cognitive control, consistent with the lateralized associations observed here.

Resting-state functional connectivity analyses further characterized network-level changes associated with chronic high-altitude exposure. Using the left putamen as a seed, we observed altered connectivity with the insula, ACC, frontal gyrus, and occipital cortex across exposure time. Compared with 2014, functional connectivity between the left putamen and the ACC and insula was reduced in 2016, whereas connectivity with the ACC, insula, and frontal gyrus was relatively stronger in 2018 compared with 2016. This temporal pattern suggests dynamic reorganization of putamen-centered networks during prolonged hypoxic exposure. One possible interpretation is that these changes reflect compensatory network-level adjustments rather than restoration, consistent with the observed associations between putamen-ACC connectivity and cognitive performance. Given the ACC’s established role in cognitive control and memory-related processes, altered functional coupling between the putamen and ACC may index ongoing adaptations in cognitive network functioning under chronic hypoxia.

Mediation analyses further indicated that the association between high-altitude exposure duration and verbal memory performance was statistically mediated by left putamen volume. This finding suggests that prolonged hypoxic exposure may influence memory performance partly through structural alterations of the putamen, a region involved in learning and cognitive control and functionally connected with other memory-related networks. Importantly, these results reflect statistical mediation rather than a definitive causal mechanism. Additional longitudinal studies incorporating post-exposure follow-up and multimodal biomarkers will be necessary to clarify whether putamen changes represent reversible adaptations or longer-lasting alterations in memory-related neural circuits.

Limitation and future directions

We acknowledge several limitations. First, although an age- and sex-matched control group with longitudinal MRI data was included, longitudinal neuropsychological assessments were unavailable for sea-level controls, precluding direct comparisons of cognitive trajectories; thus, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. Second, the modest sample size may have limited power to detect subtle effects. In addition, participants were full-time university students with relatively uniform campus-based routines, but lifestyle factors (e.g., diet and physical activity) were not systematically characterized and may have contributed to inter-individual variability. Moreover, some neuropsychological measures may have been subject to ceiling effects in this young, high-functioning cohort, potentially reducing sensitivity to subtle longitudinal changes. Finally, while the selected MRI assessment intervals allowed characterization of long-term trajectories under chronic high-altitude exposure, they may not fully capture shorter-term or transient neural dynamics. Altitude-related symptoms and individual coping strategies were also not systematically assessed, which may contribute to variability in cognitive and neuroimaging outcomes, particularly during early acclimatization. Future studies with larger samples, longitudinally assessed control cohorts, denser sampling schedules, and more sensitive cognitive tasks as well as standardized assessments of altitude-related symptoms/acclimatization responses are needed to strengthen causal inference and test recovery after return to sea level.

In summary, putamen alterations were statistically associated with neuropsychological impairments during prolonged high-altitude exposure, highlighting the left putamen as a key region linking longitudinal brain changes to cognitive performance. These findings support the value of monitoring cognitive outcomes in populations studying or working at high altitude and incorporating neuropsychological assessment into routine health surveillance to identify vulnerable individuals and inform targeted support.
