YY1-induced upregulation of DLGAP5 promotes tumor progression in lung adenocarcinoma via Wnt/β-catenin/EMT pathway
Authors: Jiahe Li, Hong Kuang, Jiayu Lu, Xiaokang Shen, Qiang Lu, Chunbao Zang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDLGAP5 is frequently upregulated in human cancers and is associated with tumor progression. However, its biological role, regulatory mechanism, and downstream signaling in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) remain unclear.MethodsPublic databases were used to analyze DLGAP5 expression, diagnostic value, and prognostic significance across cancers, especially in LUAD. Functional assays in LUAD cells and xenograft models were performed to evaluate the effects of DLGAP5 on proliferation, migration, invasion, and tumor growth. Mechanistic studies were conducted to examine cell cycle- and EMT-related proteins, as well as the transcriptional regulation of DLGAP5 by YY1.ResultsDLGAP5 was significantly upregulated in multiple cancers and showed strong diagnostic value. High DLGAP5 expression predicted poor overall survival and disease-specific survival, and was an independent prognostic factor in LUAD. DLGAP5 knockdown inhibited LUAD cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and tumor growth. Mechanistically, DLGAP5 silencing reduced CDK4 and CDK6 expression and suppressed the Wnt/β-catenin/EMT pathway, as shown by decreased N-cadherin, Slug, Snail, Twist1, and β-catenin, with increased E-cadherin. In addition, YY1 directly bound to the DLGAP5 promoter and positively regulated its expression. YY1 knockdown reduced DLGAP5 expression and impaired LUAD progression.ConclusionsDLGAP5 promotes LUAD progression by enhancing cell proliferation, metastasis, and EMT, while YY1 acts as its upstream transcriptional activator. The YY1/DLGAP5 axis may serve as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in LUAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08274-z.Keywords: DLGAP5, Lung adenocarcinoma, YY1 transcription factor, Prognostic biomarker, Wnt/β-catenin/EMT signaling pathway, Metastasis

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 1.3 million deaths annually, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) representing the most common histological subtype [1, 2]. Although traditionally associated with smoking, the incidence of LUAD has continued to rise in certain regions, particularly among Asian non-smokers. This emerging trend has been attributed to multiple factors, including genetic susceptibility (e.g., a high prevalence of EGFR mutations), environmental exposures such as air pollution and indoor cooking fumes, as well as hormonal and metabolic influences [3, 4]. LUAD is characterized by marked molecular heterogeneity, with frequent driver alterations involving EGFR, KRAS, ALK rearrangements, and BRAF mutations, which critically influence therapeutic strategies and clinical outcomes [5–7]. Despite advances in targeted therapy, immunotherapy, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, the five-year survival rate of LUAD patients remains below 20%. This highlights the urgent need to identify robust biomarkers that enable accurate prognostic stratification and support the development of personalized therapeutic approaches.

Human discs large-associated proteins (DLGAPs) are a family of proteins that are associated with various cellular processes, including cytoskeletal dynamics, cell adhesion and cell signaling [8]. Named DLGAP1, DLGAP2, DLGAP3, DLGAP4, and DLGAP5, these proteins are found in humans [9, 10]. In particular, these proteins regulate neuronal maturation, cancer progression, and synaptic transmission. Among the members of the DLGAP protein family is DLGAP5, a gene on chromosome 14 (14q22.3) that is mostly expressed in the cell nucleus. Supporting microtubule growth and stability in the spindle, it not only regulates the cell cycle in an oncogenic way but also plays a key role in tumor development and metastasis [11–13]. Additionally, DLGAP5 has been recognized as an underlying prognostic factor in colorectal, clear cell renal cell carcinoma, breast, and lung malignancies, and overexpression of this gene is linked to a poor prognosis in these cancers [14–18]. Nevertheless, the function of DLGAP5 in LUAD remained unclear.

We discovered that DLGAP5 levels were considerably increased in LUAD tissues in this investigation. High DLGAP5 expression was significantly associated with a poor prognosis, including decreased overall survival, according to bioinformatics research and clinical sample validation. The results of the functional tests showed that DLGAP5 enhanced the migration, proliferation, and resistance to apoptosis of LUAD cells. Additionally, we found that DLGAP5 may control important signaling pathways that contribute to tumor growth, including EMT and the cell cycle. It has been suggested that DLGAP5 may be a diagnostic marker and therapeutic target for LUAD because targeting it decreased LUAD growth and metastasis in both vitro and in vivo.

Methods

Data collection

For the LUAD Project, which included 539 patients with LUAD and 59 healthy controls, we used the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) to get RNAseq data in the FPKM format along with the relevant clinicopathological information (TCGA-LUAD). An FPKM-to-TPM (Transcripts Per Million Reads) format conversion was performed on the RNA sequencing data.

Pan-cancer analysis of DLGAP5 expression and prognostic analysis

The UCSC Xena database, which contains RNA-Seq data from TCGA across different types of cancer, was used to collect the pan-cancer expression data of DLGAP5. We used Wilcoxon rank sum tests to assess DLGAP5 expression levels in tumor and normal tissues. To account for multiple comparisons, we used the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction. After accounting for demographic factors such age, gender, tumor stage, and treatment status, Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed to evaluate DLGAP5’s predictive significance in terms of overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS). In order to create Kaplan-Meier survival curves for LUAD, patients were categorized into two groups based on their median DLGAP5 expression levels: high and low. We used the log-rank test to compare OS between the groups (p < 0.05), and we used Cox models to produce hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). To assess DLGAP5’s prediction power for five-year OS, researchers used receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis; the area under the curve (AUC) shows the specificity and sensitivity of the model. To confirm that DLGAP5 is an independent prognostic factor, both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed.

Cell culture and transfection

The American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) was consulted for the acquisition of five distinct human LUAD (lung adenocarcinoma) cell lines: A549, SPCA-1, H1975, H1299, and PC-9, as well as a normal bronchial epithelial cell line, BEAS-2B. We cultivated these cell lines in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA; Cat. No. 10099141 C), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco; Cat. No. 15140122), and 1% L-glutamine (Gibco; Cat. No. 25030081) in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco; Cat. No. 11965092). Cells were maintained under standard culture conditions in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. When the cells reached approximately 80–90% confluence, they were passaged, and the culture medium was replaced every two to three days. Geneseed Biotech (Guangzhou, China) designed and synthesized two lentivector-mediated short-hairpin RNAs specifically targeting DLGAP5 (sh-DLGAP5-1 and sh-DLGAP5-2) and YY1 (sh-YY1-1 and sh-YY1-2), along with non-targeting control plasmids (sh-control). Lentiviral vectors and packaging plasmids were co-transfected into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen; Cat. No. 11668019) according to the manufacturer’s instructions to produce lentiviral particles. The lentivirus-containing supernatants were collected and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter (Millipore; Cat. No. SLHV033RS) before being used to infect LUAD cells in the presence of Polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; Cat. No. TR-1003-G; 8 µg/mL). To establish stable knockdown cell lines, infected cells were selected using puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat. No. P8833; 2 µg/mL) for one week. For overexpression experiments, full-length YY1 cDNA was amplified by PCR using Thermo Scientific Phusion Flash High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat. No. F548S). The amplified fragments were subcloned into the pcDNA3.1(+) expression vector (Invitrogen; Cat. No. V79020) under the control of the CMV promoter. An empty pcDNA3.1(+) vector served as the negative control. Recombinant pcDNA3.1(+)-YY1 constructs were transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells (Takara Bio, Japan; Cat. No. 9057) for plasmid propagation according to standard bacterial culture procedures. Plasmids were isolated using a commercial plasmid extraction kit (Qiagen Plasmid Mini Kit; Cat. No. 12123) following the manufacturer’s instructions, and DNA sequencing was performed to confirm successful cloning. For transient transfection, LUAD cells were transfected with either the empty vector or pcDNA3.1-DLGAP5 using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen; Cat. No. 11668019) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well and transfected when they reached approximately 60–70% confluence. For each well, 4 µg of plasmid DNA and Lipofectamine 2000 were diluted in Opti-MEM Reduced Serum Medium (Gibco; Cat. No. 31985070). The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 20 min before being added to the cells. After 48 h of transfection, transfection efficiency was evaluated by Western blot analysis and fluorescence microscopy (for GFP-tagged plasmids).

Cell proliferation assay

Transfected A549 and H1299 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3,000 cells per well in 100 µL of complete culture medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA; Cat. No. 10099141 C). Cells were incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ to allow cell attachment. Cell proliferation was assessed at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h after seeding. At each time point, 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 reagent (CCK-8; Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Kumamoto, Japan; Cat. No. CK04) was added to each well and the cells were further incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy HTX Microplate Reader, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA; Cat. No. SYNERGYHTX). The optical density (OD) values were used as an indirect indicator of cell proliferation. All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates, and each sample was analyzed in duplicate technical wells to minimize experimental variability. Cell proliferation rates were calculated based on the change in OD values over time. The OD values were normalized and plotted to generate growth curves representing proliferation dynamics. Statistical significance among groups was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).

RNA isolation and real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells and tissue samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA; Cat. No. 15596026) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, samples were lysed in TRIzol reagent, followed by chloroform extraction and centrifugation to separate the aqueous phase. RNA was precipitated using isopropanol and washed twice with 75% ethanol. The RNA pellet was air-dried and dissolved in RNase-free DEPC-treated water (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. AM9915). RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA; Cat. No. ND-2000). Samples with A260/280 ratios of 1.8–2.0 and A260/230 ratios > 2.0 were considered suitable for downstream experiments. To eliminate potential genomic DNA contamination, RNA samples were treated with DNase I (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA; Cat. No. 04716728001) at 37°C for 30 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 65°C. For reverse transcription, 2 µg of total RNA from each sample was converted into complementary DNA (cDNA) using an M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA; Cat. No. 28025013) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The reverse transcription reaction (20 µL total volume) contained total RNA, random hexamer primers (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. N8080127), dNTPs (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. R0192), and M-MLV reverse transcriptase. The reaction conditions were as follows: 25°C for 10 min, 42°C for 60 min, and 70°C for 10 min. The synthesized cDNA was stored at − 20°C until further use. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using an ABI 7300 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA; Cat. No. 4351108) with SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. A25742). Each reaction mixture contained 2 µL cDNA template, 10 µL SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.4 µL forward primer (10 µM), 0.4 µL reverse primer (10 µM), and nuclease-free water (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. AM9937) to a final volume of 20 µL. The qPCR cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 s, annealing at 60°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 30 s. A melting curve analysis was performed at the end of each run to verify the specificity of the amplification products. To ensure the PCR products were specific, a melting curve analysis was carried out. The qRT-PCR results for DLGAP5 mRNA levels were adjusted to align with the mRNA levels of GAPDH, an internal control gene, in order to assess these levels. The 2^−ΔΔCt method was used to determine the relative expression levels, where ΔCt is equal to Ct (DLGAP5) minus Ct (GAPDH), and ΔΔCt is equal to ΔCt (experimental group) minus ΔCt (control group). The PCR primers were as follows: DLGAP5 forward, 5’-AAGTGGGTCGTTATAGACCTGA-3’; DLGAP5 reverse, 5’-TGCTCGAACATCACTCTCGTTAT-3’; GAPDH forward, 5’-CACCCACTCCTCCACCTTTG-3’; GAPDH reverse, 5’- CCACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAG − 3’. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates, and each sample was analyzed in technical triplicate.

Colony formation

Colony formation assays were performed using six-well plates. Briefly, transfected cells were seeded at a density of 500 cells per well in 2 mL of complete culture medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA; Cat. No. 10099141 C). Cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ to allow cell attachment. After adhesion, cells were treated according to the experimental design. During the incubation period, fresh culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days. After 10–14 days, when visible colonies had formed, the experiment was terminated. Colonies were gently washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Gibco, USA; Cat. No. 10010023) to remove residual medium and non-adherent cells. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat. No. P1110) for 30 min at room temperature. After fixation, wells were washed with PBS and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat. No. G1064) for 20 min at room temperature. Excess dye was removed by rinsing the wells with running water, and the plates were air-dried. Colonies were photographed and counted under an inverted microscope (Olympus IX73, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Cell clusters containing ≥ 50 cells were defined as colonies. All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates, and each condition was analyzed in technical triplicate.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from treated LUAD cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; Cat. No. P0013B) supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche Diagnostics; Cat. No. 04693159001) and 1% phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche Diagnostics; Cat. No. 04906837001). Cell lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min with intermittent vortexing and then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. The supernatant containing total protein was collected for subsequent analysis. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA; Cat. No. 23225) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 562 nm using a microplate reader, and bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. 23209) was used to generate the standard curve. Equal amounts of protein (30–50 µg) were mixed with 2× SDS loading buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; Cat. No. P0015) containing β-mercaptoethanol and denatured at 95 °C for 5 min. Proteins were separated by 10% SDS–PAGE at 100 V for approximately 1.5 h and then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA; Cat. No. IPVH00010) using a wet transfer system at 100 V for 1.5 h at 4 °C. After transfer, membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. 23209) or 5% non-fat dry milk (Bio-Rad, USA; Cat. No. 1706404) prepared in TBST buffer (Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20; Beyotime; Cat. No. ST673) for 1 h at room temperature to prevent nonspecific binding. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-YY1 (Abcam; Cat. No. ab109228; 1:1000), anti-DLGAP5 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; Cat. No. sc-515703; 1:1000), anti-CDK4 (Cell Signaling Technology; Cat. No. 12790; 1:1000), anti-CDK6 (Cell Signaling Technology; Cat. No. 13331; 1:1000), anti-Cyclin D1 (Cell Signaling Technology; Cat. No. 2978; 1:1000), anti-β-catenin (Cell Signaling Technology; Cat. No. 8480; 1:1000), anti-E-cadherin (Cell Signaling Technology; Cat. No. 3195; 1:1000), anti-N-cadherin (Cell Signaling Technology; Cat. No. 13116; 1:1000), anti-Slug (Abcam; Cat. No. ab27568; 1:1000), anti-Snail (Abcam; Cat. No. ab216347; 1:1000), anti-Twist1 (Abcam; Cat. No. ab175430; 1:1000), anti-Twist2 (Abcam; Cat. No. ab66031; 1:1000), and anti-β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat. No. A5441; 1:5000) as a loading control. After incubation with primary antibodies, membranes were washed three times with TBST for 10 min each. Membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, including goat anti-rabbit IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch; Cat. No. 111-035-003; 1:5000) or goat anti-mouse IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch; Cat. No. 115-035-003; 1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; Cat. No. P0018S). Chemiluminescent signals were visualized using an imaging system (Bio-Rad ChemiDoc XRS+, Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), and protein expression levels were normalized to β-actin. All Western blot experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates.

Transwell assay

Cell invasion ability was evaluated using a Transwell chamber system (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA; Cat. No. PICM01250) with 8 μm pore size polycarbonate membranes. For invasion assays, the upper chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel Basement Membrane Matrix (Corning, NY, USA; Cat. No. 354234). Briefly, 100 µL of Matrigel diluted in serum-free medium was added to the upper chamber and incubated at 37 °C for 2–3 h to allow polymerization and formation of a reconstituted basement membrane layer. For the invasion assay, 600 µL of complete culture medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA; Cat. No. 10099141 C) was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. Cells from the experimental and control groups were resuspended in serum-free medium and seeded into the upper chamber at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells in 100 µL. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ for 24 h to allow invasion through the Matrigel-coated membrane. After incubation, non-invading cells on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed using a sterile cotton swab. The membranes were then washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Gibco, USA; Cat. No. 10010023). Cells that had migrated to the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat. No. P1110) for 30 min at room temperature, followed by washing with PBS. The invaded cells were stained with Giemsa staining solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat. No. G1010) for 15 min and gently rinsed with running water to remove excess dye. The membranes were air-dried before imaging. Stained cells were photographed and counted under an inverted microscope (Nikon Eclipse TS100, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) at ×200 magnification. Five randomly selected, non-overlapping fields per insert were counted, and the average number of invaded cells was calculated. All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates, and each condition was analyzed in technical triplicate.

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Tangdu Hospital, Air Force Medical University. Male BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old, 18–22 g) were obtained from Guangdong Yaokang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China (License No.: SCXK (Yue) 2020-XXXX) and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle, controlled temperature (22–24 °C), and relative humidity (50–60%). Mice had free access to sterilized food and water. For xenograft tumor experiments, mice were randomly assigned to two groups (control group and DLGAP5- or YY1-knockdown group), with 4 mice per group, which is consistent with sample sizes commonly used in similar xenograft studies. Subcutaneous injections were performed into the dorsal flank using a 27-gauge needle, delivering 5 × 10⁶ tumor cells suspended in 0.1 ml of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Tumor growth was monitored every 2–3 days, and tumor length and width were measured using a caliper. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: volume = 1/2 × length × width². After three weeks, mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. Tumors were excised, weighed, and collected for subsequent Western blotting. The number of animals used in each experiment was minimized to reduce animal suffering while ensuring sufficient statistical power to detect biologically meaningful differences, based on prior experience and published studies using comparable xenograft models. All surgical procedures, including injections and tumor excision, were performed under anesthesia (isoflurane or ketamine/xylazine) to ensure humane treatment.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay (ChIP)

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were performed using the Pierce Magnetic ChIP Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat. No. 26157) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, LUAD cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well and cultured until reaching 80–90% confluence. Cells were then subjected to gene manipulation or treatment as required by the experimental design. Protein–DNA complexes were crosslinked by incubating the cells with 1% formaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. 28908) for 10 min at room temperature. Crosslinking was quenched by adding glycine (final concentration 0.125 M; Sigma-Aldrich; Cat. No. G8898) and incubating for 5 min. Cells were subsequently washed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Gibco, USA; Cat. No. 10010023) and harvested for further processing. Cells were lysed using the lysis buffer provided in the kit supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche Diagnostics; Cat. No. 04693159001). Chromatin was fragmented by sonication to generate DNA fragments ranging from 200 to 500 bp. The sheared chromatin was then subjected to immunoprecipitation using an anti-YY1 antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; Cat. No. ab109228) or normal rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. 10500 C) as a negative control. The chromatin–antibody mixtures were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rotation. Following immunoprecipitation, magnetic protein A/G beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. 88802) were added to capture antibody–protein–DNA complexes and incubated for 1 h at 4 °C. The beads were then washed five times with the wash buffers provided in the kit to remove nonspecific binding. Protein–DNA crosslinks were reversed by incubating the samples at 65 °C for 4 h, followed by treatment with RNase A (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. EN0531) and Proteinase K (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. EO0491) to remove RNA and proteins. DNA was subsequently purified using the DNA purification reagents included in the kit. Enriched DNA fragments were analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) using an ABI 7300 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA; Cat. No. 4351108) with SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. A25742). Primers were designed to amplify the DLGAP5 promoter region containing the predicted YY1 binding sites. Relative enrichment of DNA fragments was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with input DNA serving as the reference control. All ChIP experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

To investigate the transcriptional activity of the DLGAP5 promoter, a 908-bp fragment of the DLGAP5 promoter region containing the predicted YY1 binding site was amplified by PCR and cloned into the pGL3-Basic luciferase reporter vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA; Cat. No. E1751) to generate the wild-type reporter construct (DLGAP5-WT). A mutant reporter construct (DLGAP5-MUT), in which the predicted YY1 binding site was disrupted, was generated using a site-directed mutagenesis kit (Agilent Technologies; Cat. No. 200523). For the luciferase reporter assay, LUAD cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured overnight to allow cell attachment. Cells were then co-transfected with 500 ng of either the WT or MUT DLGAP5 promoter luciferase plasmid together with 20 ng of pRL-SV40 Renilla luciferase plasmid (Promega, USA; Cat. No. E2231) as an internal control for transfection efficiency. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA; Cat. No. 11668019) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 24 h of transfection, cells were harvested and luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Glo® Luciferase Assay System (Promega, USA; Cat. No. E2920) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were first lysed with Dual-Glo® Lysis Buffer and incubated at room temperature for 15 min to ensure complete cell lysis. Luciferase activity was measured using a GloMax® 96 Microplate Luminometer (Promega, USA; Cat. No. E6501). Firefly luciferase activity was measured first, followed by Renilla luciferase activity from the same sample. Relative luciferase activity was calculated as the ratio of firefly luciferase activity to Renilla luciferase activity, which was used to normalize transfection efficiency. All luciferase reporter assays were performed with three independent biological replicates, and each condition was analyzed in technical triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Software from GraphPad Software, Inc. (La Jolla, CA, USA) and IBM’s SPSS 19.0 (Armonk, NY, USA) were used to conduct the statistical analyses. A paired, two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to assess any significant differences between the groups. A significance level of P < 0.05 was used.

Results

DLGAP5 is broadly upregulated across cancers and predicts unfavorable clinical outcomes

To explore the potential role of DLGAP5 in tumor progression, we conducted a pan-cancer analysis and found that DLGAP5 exhibited dysregulated expression in various tumor types (Fig. 1A). The expression of DLGAP5 is clearly illustrated in the human tissue map, showing that its levels are significantly higher in the organs of tumor patients compared to normal individuals (Fig. 1B). ROC analysis across multiple cancers demonstrated that DLGAP5 has strong diagnostic potential in distinguishing tumor tissue from normal tissue (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, analysis of the HPA dataset revealed that DLGAP5 is highly expressed in several tissues, including the thymus, bone marrow, tonsils, lymph nodes, and appendix (Fig. 1D). Moreover, based on the HPA dataset, we showed the expression trend of DLGAP5 in tumor tissues. Overall, DLGAP5 tends to be highly expressed in tumor tissues. We also found that high DLGAP5 expression is associated with overall survival (OS) in several cancer types, including ACC, KIRP, LGG, LIHC, and LUAD (Fig. 1F). Additionally, high DLGAP5 expression was linked to disease-specific survival (DSS) in cancer patients, including ACC, KIRP, LGG, LUAD, MESO, and PAAD (Fig. 1G).

Elevated DLGAP5 expression independently predicts poor prognosis in LUAD

We further investigated DLGAP5 expression in LUAD and observed that its expression was significantly elevated in LUAD tissues compared to normal, non-tumor tissues, as demonstrated in Fig. 2A. To understand the clinical relevance of DLGAP5 in LUAD, we next explored its association with various clinical characteristics. As shown in Fig. 2B, we found that high expression of DLGAP5 was positively correlated with factors such as age, gender, and clinical stage, suggesting that DLGAP5 may be involved in the progression of LUAD and could serve as a potential indicator of disease severity. Next, we sought to assess the prognostic value of DLGAP5 expression in LUAD. Our analysis revealed that patients with elevated DLGAP5 expression had significantly shorter overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) compared to those with lower expression levels, as depicted in Fig. 2C. These findings underscore the potential of DLGAP5 as a prognostic biomarker for LUAD, potentially helping to identify patients at higher risk for poor clinical outcomes. To further validate the robustness of DLGAP5 as a prognostic marker, we performed a time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (time-ROC) curve analysis. The results indicated that DLGAP5 expression exhibited excellent predictive ability, demonstrating strong performance in forecasting both OS and DSS over time (Fig. 2D). Crucially, we conducted multivariate Cox regression analysis to evaluate whether DLGAP5 expression could independently predict patient survival outcomes. The results confirmed that DLGAP5 expression was an independent prognostic factor for both OS and DSS in LUAD patients, as shown in Fig. 2E and F. These findings solidify the potential of DLGAP5 as a valuable prognostic biomarker in LUAD, providing clinicians with a tool to better assess patient prognosis and guide therapeutic decision-making.

DLGAP5 is required for LUAD cell proliferation and tumor growth in vitro and in vivo

The investigation of DLGAP5’s potential role in LUAD cells began with a comprehensive analysis of its expression across a range of LUAD cell lines. Strikingly, both DLGAP5 mRNA and protein levels were significantly elevated in six distinct LUAD cell lines, as illustrated in Fig. 3A. Among these, the A549 and H1299 cells exhibited the highest expression of DLGAP5, prompting us to select these two cell lines for further experimentation. To explore the functional consequences of DLGAP5 overexpression, we utilized shRNA plasmids to effectively knock down DLGAP5 expression in both A549 and H1299 cells, as shown in Fig. 3B. Subsequent functional assays, including CCK-8 and clonogenic assays, demonstrated that DLGAP5 knockdown substantially hindered the proliferative capacity of both A549 and H1299 cells, as seen in Fig. 3C and D. To further probe the relationship between DLGAP5 and key cell cycle regulators, we conducted an analysis using TCGA datasets, revealing a positive correlation between DLGAP5 expression and the expression levels of CDK4 and CDK6, as depicted in Fig. 3E. Expanding on this, we assessed the protein levels of CDK4, CDK6, and Cyclin D1 in DLGAP5-silenced cells. The results showed a significant downregulation of both CDK4 and CDK6 following DLGAP5 knockdown (Fig. 3F), suggesting that DLGAP5 may regulate the cell cycle via modulation of these critical cyclins and kinases. Flow cytometric analysis showed that knockdown of DLGAP5 significantly altered cell-cycle distribution in both cell models. Compared with the sh-NC group, cells transfected with sh-DLGAP5-1 or sh-DLGAP5-2 exhibited an increased proportion in the G0/G1 phase and a reduced proportion in the S and G2/M phases. These findings suggest that DLGAP5 depletion induces G0/G1 phase arrest and suppresses cell-cycle progression, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation(Fig. 3G). In order to evaluate the potential in vivo effects of DLGAP5 silencing, we established subcutaneous xenograft models using A549 cells with stable DLGAP5 knockdown. Tumor growth was monitored over a period of time, and the results revealed a marked reduction in tumor growth rate in the DLGAP5 knockdown group, leading to smaller tumor sizes and significantly lower final tumor weights compared to the control groups (Fig. 3H and I). Western blot analysis demonstrated that knockdown of DLGAP5 markedly reduced the protein levels of CDK4 and CDK6 compared with the sh-NC control group (Fig. 3J). Consistently, DLGAP5 protein expression was efficiently suppressed in the sh-DLGAP5-1 group, confirming the effectiveness of the knockdown strategy. To further evaluate the impact of DLGAP5 depletion on tumor proliferation in vivo, immunohistochemical staining for Ki-67 was performed in xenograft tumor tissues. The sh-NC group displayed abundant Ki-67–positive nuclei, indicating strong proliferative activity. In contrast, tumors derived from sh-DLGAP5-1 cells exhibited a markedly reduced number of Ki-67–positive cells, reflecting significantly decreased proliferative capacity (Fig. 3K). These findings collectively support the notion that DLGAP5 plays a crucial role in LUAD cell proliferation and may serve as a potential therapeutic target for intervention in LUAD.

DLGAP5 promotes LUAD cell migration and invasion through activation of the Wnt/β-catenin/EMT pathway

To further investigate the impact of DLGAP5 inhibition on the metastatic behavior of LUAD cells, we conducted transwell migration and invasion assays using A549 and H1299 cells with stable DLGAP5 knockdown. The results showed a significant reduction in both migratory and invasive capabilities of these cells, indicating that DLGAP5 plays an important role in promoting metastasis (Fig. 4A). We next analyzed the relationship between DLGAP5 expression and key signaling pathways involved in cancer metastasis. Using TCGA datasets, we observed a positive correlation between DLGAP5 expression and the expression of genes associated with the Wnt/β-catenin and EMT (epithelial-mesenchymal transition) pathways, which are critical regulators of cell migration and invasion (Fig. 4B). To further explore this connection, we examined the expression levels of key proteins involved in the Wnt/β-catenin/EMT signaling axis, including N-cadherin, E-cadherin, Slug, Snail, Twist1, ZEB1, ZEB2, and β-catenin. Our findings revealed that silencing DLGAP5 led to a significant downregulation of mesenchymal markers, including N-cadherin, Slug, Snail, Twist1, ZEB1, ZEB2, and β-catenin, while simultaneously promoting the expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin (Fig. 4C). These results suggest that DLGAP5 regulates the EMT process and modulates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which are crucial for the metastatic potential of LUAD cells.

YY1 directly transcriptionally activates DLGAP5 expression in LUAD

First, we retrieved the 2000-bp upstream sequence of the human DLGAP5 gene promoter from PubMed. We then downloaded the transcription factors targeting DLGAP5 from six databases: HumanTFDB (criteria: Source: database, Strand: +, Score ≥ 10), ENCODE, GTRD (criteria: SiteCount ≥ 4), hTFtarget, TPLink, and ChIP Atlas. After taking the intersection of these databases using a Venn diagram, we identified five potential transcription factors that may interact with the DLGAP5 promoter: CTCF, ETS1, FOXA2, TBP, and YY1 (Fig. 5A). Next, we downloaded and processed RNA-Seq data from the TCGA-LUAD project(https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov) using the STAR pipeline, extracting the data in TPM format. Data processing involved log2 transformation of the values (log2(value + 1)). We then performed a Pearson correlation analysis to explore the relationship between four transcription factors and DLGAP5 expression. Our analysis revealed that YY1 showed the strongest correlation with DLGAP5 (Fig. 5B), prompting us to investigate the specific regulatory role of YY1 in LUAD. Moreover, gene expression and clinical data for 497 LUAD patients were obtained from the TCGA database. Patients were divided into high and low YY1 expression groups based on the median YY1 level and into high (Hexp) and low (Lexp) DLGAP5 expression groups according to DLGAP5 expression. Patients were then stratified into four subgroups: YY1 high/DLGAP5 high (n = 156), YY1 high/DLGAP5 low (n = 92), YY1 low/DLGAP5 high (n = 93), and YY1 low/DLGAP5 low (n = 156). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with the log-rank test showed a significant difference in overall survival among the four groups (P < 0.001, Fig. 5C). Notably, patients with YY1 low/DLGAP5 high expression had the worst prognosis, suggesting that this combined expression pattern may serve as a potential biomarker for poor prognosis in LUAD.

YY1 is aberrantly expressed across cancers and correlates with poor prognosis

To investigate the potential role of YY1 in tumor progression, we performed a pan-cancer analysis of YY1 expression across various cancer types. Our analysis revealed that YY1 expression is dysregulated in many types of tumors, with its expression levels being significantly altered compared to normal tissues (Fig. 6A). The human tissue map further illustrates the expression pattern of YY1, showing that its levels are markedly higher in the organs of tumor patients than in normal individuals (Fig. 6B). To assess YY1’s diagnostic potential, we conducted pan-cancer ROC (receiver operating characteristic) analysis, which demonstrated that YY1 exhibited strong diagnostic capabilities in distinguishing tumor tissues from normal tissues (Fig. 6C). This suggests that YY1 could serve as a reliable biomarker for cancer diagnosis. Further exploration using the HPA (Human Protein Atlas) dataset revealed that YY1 is generally highly expressed in various tumor tissues, supporting the idea that YY1 may play a significant role in tumorigenesis. Interestingly, specific tissues, including the thymus, tonsils, and bone marrow, showed particularly high expression of YY1, further emphasizing its potential relevance in these tissues (Fig. 6D and E). Additionally, we analyzed the correlation between YY1 expression and patient survival outcomes. We observed that high YY1 expression was significantly associated with poorer overall survival (OS) in several cancer types, including adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), and LUAD (Fig. 6F). Similarly, high YY1 expression correlated with reduced disease-specific survival (DSS) in patients with tumors such as ACC, KIRC, and LUAD (Fig. 6G), suggesting that YY1 expression could serve as a prognostic indicator in these cancers.

YY1 drives LUAD cell proliferation, metastasis, and EMT programs

Clonogenic experiments showed that YY1 knockdown considerably decreased the proliferation of both A549 and H1299 cells (Fig. 7A), so we knew YY1 had a function in LUAD progression. The results indicate that YY1 is essential for LUAD cell proliferation. Figure 7B shows that following our analysis of TCGA datasets, we found that DLGAP5 expression was positively correlated with those of two important cell cycle regulators, CDK4 and CDK6. This discovery suggested that DLGAP5 may have a role in controlling the advancement of the cell cycle via CDK4 and CDK6. The effects of YY1 silencing on CDK4 and CDK6 expression were subsequently examined. Figure 7C shows that protein levels of CDK4 and CDK6 were significantly downregulated when YY1 was knocked down, indicating that YY1 may control the expression of these important cyclins in LUAD cells. We created subcutaneous xenograft models using A549 cells that had a stable YY1 knockdown in order to assess the in vivo impact of YY1 on tumor progression. After tracking tumor growth over time, researchers found that tumors formed from YY1 knockdown cells developed more slowly than control tumors. This suggests that YY1 is crucial for tumor development in vivo, since the YY1 knockdown group showed substantially smaller tumor volumes and lower final tumor weights (Fig. 7D and E). To further investigate the role of YY1 in regulating cell cycle progression, Western blot analysis was performed using xenograft tumor tissues. As shown in Fig. 7F, YY1 expression was markedly decreased in tumors derived from the sh-YY1-1 group compared with the sh-NC group, confirming the effective knockdown of YY1 in vivo. Importantly, the protein levels of the cell cycle regulators CDK4 and CDK6 were also significantly reduced following YY1 silencing, whereas β-actin served as a loading control. These results suggest that YY1 positively regulates CDK4/CDK6 expression in tumor tissues. To assess tumor proliferative activity, Ki-67 immunohistochemical staining was performed on xenograft tumor sections (Fig. 7G). The sh-NC group exhibited abundant Ki-67–positive nuclei, indicating high proliferative capacity. In contrast, tumors from the sh-YY1-1 group showed a markedly reduced number of Ki-67–positive cells. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that YY1 depletion suppresses tumor cell proliferation in vivo, likely through downregulation of CDK4/CDK6-mediated cell cycle signaling. We also used transwell migration and invasion experiments to investigate how inhibiting YY1 affected the metastatic potential of A549 and H1299 cells. Figure 7H shows that YY1 knockdown significantly reduced the invasive and migratory properties of these cells, indicating that YY1 is involved in LUAD cells’ ability to metastasize. In addition, we used TCGA datasets to study the correlation between DLGAP5 expression and EMT markers. Figure 7I shows a strong correlation between YY1 expression and the expression of important EMT-associated genes. These genes are known to be involved in cancer cell migration and invasion. We looked at the levels of expression of many important proteins in the Wnt/β-catenin/EMT pathway, such as N-cadherin, E-cadherin, Slug, Snail, Twist1, and ZEB1, to confirm this. Figure 7J shows that E-cadherin, a hallmark of the epithelial phenotype, was upregulated in response to YY1 silencing, whereas the expressions of N-cadherin, Slug, Snail, Twist1, and ZEB1 were significantly downregulated. The results indicate that YY1 controls the evolution of EMT in LUAD cells through regulating the expression of important proteins linked to EMT.

Discussion

Treatment options for LUAD might involve a variety of methods, including surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and combinations of these [19, 20]. To enhance survival odds, tumors can be removed during surgery for early-stage LUAD [21, 22]. When surgery isn’t an option, patients who need local control typically turn to radiation therapy. Although chemotherapy is linked with significant adverse effects, it is still an important treatment for advanced lung cancer, particularly small cell lung cancer (SCLC) [23, 24]. More precisely and with fewer adverse effects, targeted therapy targets particular genetic mutations like EGFR and ALK. Some patients get long-term benefits from immunotherapy, which stimulates the immune system to fight cancer cells; however, not all patients respond positively to this treatment [25, 26]. Factors including illness stage and treatment response impact the prognosis of lung cancer; nonetheless, survival rates are greatly improved with early detection and care. Important prognostic indicators that aid in assessing the efficacy of treatment and making predictions include CEA, CYFRA21-1, EGFR mutations, ALK rearrangements, and PD-L1 expression [27, 28]. Unfortunately, lung cancer still has a bad prognosis generally, especially in its advanced stages when a combination of treatments is typically required for the best chance of survival. This is true despite improvements in treatment.

Multiple studies have shown that DLGAP5 contributes to the development of many cancers in the past few years. In the case of gallbladder cancer (GBC), for example, Huang et al. showed that DLGAP5 levels are much higher and strongly linked to a bad prognosis for GBC patients. In GBC cell lines, overexpressing DLGAP5 increases cell proliferation, migration, and macrophage M2 polarization, whereas knocking it down has the reverse impact. In order to drive the advancement of GBC and the polarization of M2 macrophages, DLGAP5 activates the cAMP pathway. Based on these results, DLGAP5 may represent a good therapeutic target for the treatment of GBC [16]. The bioinformatic investigations performed by Li et al., which included weighted gene co-expression network analysis and differential analysis, demonstrated DLGAP5’s potential as a breast cancer biomarker. According to the research, DLGAP5 was highly expressed in BC; lowering its expression prevented cell migration, invasion, and proliferation; increasing its expression had the reverse effect. In addition, the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway affects BC formation, and DLGAP5 is known to regulate this pathway. These results point to DLGAP5 as a possible treatment target and useful biomarker for BC [17]. Endometrial cancer (EC) patients with high DLGAP5 expression had a worse prognosis, according to research by Chen et al. The inhibition of EC cell growth, invasion, and caspase-3-induced apoptosis was seen with knockdown of DLGAP5. In addition, the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway was rendered inactive by DLGAP5 silencing, and the effects of DLGAP5 knockdown on EC cell malignancy were counteracted by overexpression of β-catenin. According to these results, DLGAP5 enhances EC progression via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and may be a possible target for therapy [18]. Dysregulation of DLGAP5 has been shown in lung cancer in previous research [14, 29]. Nevertheless, its mechanism and function were mostly unknown. Our results showed that individuals with poor prognoses, especially those with LUAD, were more likely to have tumors with high expression of DLGAP5. The results of the study showed that DLGAP5 was highly overexpressed in tumor tissues when compared to normal tissues. ROC analysis further confirmed that DLGAP5 had a great diagnostic ability to differentiate between tumor and normal tissues, applicable to various forms of cancer. These results provided more evidence that DLGAP5 may be useful as a biomarker for the diagnosis of cancer. A more in-depth examination of LUAD revealed that high DLGAP5 expression was linked to clinical variables such age, gender, and disease stage; it was also substantially linked to shorter OS and DSS, indicating that DLGAP5 could potentially enhance tumor malignancy. The validity of DLGAP5 expression as a diagnostic for LUAD patient prognosis was validated by time-dependent ROC curve analysis. The significance of DLGAP5 expression as a separate prognostic factor for OS and DSS in LUAD patients was further validated by multivariate COX regression analysis. Our findings point to DLGAP5 as an important player in tumor development and indicate it as a potential cancer prognostic biomarker; this could lead to exciting new possibilities in clinical trials and individualised medicine.

Embryonic development, tissue healing, cell proliferation, differentiation, and migration are all impacted by the Wnt/β-catenin/epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) system, a significant cellular signaling network [30, 31]. Wnt protein family members provide the backbone of this pathway; they attach to Frizzled receptors on cell surfaces, trigger subsequent signaling cascades, and eventually cause β-catenin to be stabilized and accumulated [32, 33]. In lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), the Wnt/β-catenin/EMT pathway is very important for carcinogenesis, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. When the Wnt protein binds to its receptor, this route activates β-catenin, which subsequently stimulates the proliferation, migration, and invasion of tumor cells [34–36]. The formation and proliferation of tumors are intimately associated with the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in LUAD. It is worth mentioning that the buildup of β-catenin makes the EMT process easier, which in turn promotes metastasis by making tumor cells migrate and invade more effectively. In LUAD, immune evasion mechanisms assist tumor cells in escaping immune monitoring, leading to an increase in tumor invasiveness, and activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is related with this [37, 38]. Treatment difficulties are exacerbated by the fact that this route is associated with resistance to targeted treatments and chemotherapy. Thus, as prospective targets for therapeutic intervention, the Wnt/β-catenin/EMT pathway is crucial for LUAD tumor development, metastasis, and resistance to therapy. Researching this pathway thoroughly could lead to the creation of novel treatment approaches, especially those that target the Wnt/β-catenin/EMT pathway. The function of DLGAP5 in LUAD was examined in this research. A number of LUAD cell lines, including A549 and H1299, showed markedly increased levels of DLGAP5, according to the study’s authors. Reducing the expression of important cell cycle regulators CDK4 and CDK6 and inhibiting LUAD cell proliferation by knocking down DLGAP5 suggests that DLGAP5 may regulate the cell cycle in a way that promotes tumor cell growth. Subsequent in vivo investigations demonstrated that A549 cell tumors were considerably less in size and weighed less when DLGAP5 was knocked down. Furthermore, the research examined how DLGAP5 influenced the migration and invasion of LUAD cells. A significant reduction in the migratory and invasion capacities of LUAD cells was observed with DLGAP5 knockdown in transwell migration and invasion tests, suggesting that DLGAP5 is involved in tumor metastasis. Key regulators of cell migration and invasion, the Wnt/β-catenin and EMT (epithelial-mesenchymal transition) pathways, were found to be positively correlated with DLGAP5 expression in the TCGA datasets. This suggests that DLGAP5 controls the Wnt/β-catenin/EMT pathway to increase the metastatic potential of LUAD cells, as further experiments showed that repressing DLGAP5 decreased expression of various mesenchymal markers (like N-cadherin, Slug, Twist1, ZEB1, etc.) and increased expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin. In conclusion, this research shown that DLGAP5 is essential for LUAD cell growth, migration, invasion, and metastasis, most likely by influencing the Wnt/β-catenin/EMT pathway. Thus, DLGAP5 may provide useful therapeutic information for future LUAD treatments.

Influencing carcinogenesis, progression, and metastasis, transcription factors are vital in LUAD [39]. Transcription factors affect gene expression by binding to certain DNA sequences; this involvement drives tumor cell malignancy through mechanisms such cell proliferation, cell cycle control, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and immune evasion [40–42]. Important roles are played by transcription factors in LUAD, including EGFR, p53, NF-κB, Twist1, Slug, Snail, and β-catenin [43, 44]. Overactivation of the receptor leads to cell proliferation promotion by downstream transcription factors including STAT3, which is facilitated by EGFR mutations, which are prevalent in LUAD. Cells can evade typical cell cycle checkpoints when p53 is mutated or inactivated, which aids in tumor growth. Cancer metastasis, chemoresistance, and tumor invasiveness are all correlated with the NF-κB pathway activity [45–47]. The EMT process is controlled by transcription factors like Twist1, Slug, and Snail, which facilitate tumor cell migration and invasion. On the other hand, tumor growth is driven by the aberrant activation of β-catenin, which promotes cell proliferation and the EMT process. Thus, transcription factors are both essential for LUAD and a possible target for targeted therapy due to their role in controlling the expression of tumor suppressor and oncogene genes. By shedding light on YY1’s critical function in controlling DLGAP5 expression and tumor growth, our findings contributed significantly to our understanding of LUAD. By increasing DLGAP5, YY1 facilitates LUAD cell proliferation and metastasis. The proliferation, migration, and invasion of LUAD cells were markedly reduced, and tumor growth in vivo was halted, with YY1 knockdown. It is suggested that YY1 is essential for tumor metastasis because it controls EMT, which impacts motility and invasion of tumor cells. A pan-cancer study found that YY1 is dysregulated in many different kinds of tumors and is linked to a bad prognosis. The potential of YY1 as a predictive biomarker is highlighted by the fact that high expression of the gene is associated with a worse outcome in LUAD patients. Targeting YY1 could offer a novel approach to treating LUAD because it controls LUAD progression, metastasis, and the EMT process.

The study might have been limited in a few ways. The in vivo effects of DLGAP5 knockdown are firstly assessed in the study using subcutaneous xenograft models. The intricacy of tumor microenvironments and metastasis in human LUAD may be beyond the capabilities of these models. Secondly, the exact molecular specifics of how DLGAP5 regulates these pathways are yet unknown, even though the work associates DLGAP5 with the Wnt/β-Catenin/EMT pathway. Additional molecular processes and downstream signaling cascades could be elucidated by future proteomics or phosphoproteomics research.

Conclusion

Finally, our study presents strong evidence that DLGAP5 is critical for LUAD progression. Our findings show that DLGAP5 is dysregulated in various tumor types and could be used for diagnostic and prognostic purposes, especially in LUAD, according to our pan-cancer analysis. One possible independent prognostic marker is the high expression of DLGAP5, which was linked to poor overall survival and disease-specific survival in LUAD patients. Further functional studies demonstrated that DLGAP5 enhances LUAD cell proliferation, metastasis, and EMT in both laboratory and living organism settings. The mechanism by which DLGAP5 contributes to the progression of LUAD is that it controls the expression of important cell cycle proteins and alters the Wnt/β-Catenin/EMT signaling pathway. A key transcription factor that upregulates DLGAP5 expression by directly binding to its promoter region was identified as YY1. Deletion of YY1 impaired LUAD cell motility, EMT, and proliferation in addition to repressing DLGAP5 expression. These results point to the possibility of new therapeutic approaches for LUAD that target DLGAP5 and its regulatory pathway, which includes YY1. Further clinical inquiry and therapeutic development are warranted by our results, which highlight the potential of DLGAP5 as a diagnostic biomarker and a therapeutic target in LUAD.
