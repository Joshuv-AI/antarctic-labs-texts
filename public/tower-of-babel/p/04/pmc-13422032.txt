Distress among primary caregivers of patients with cancer: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ram Mohan Jha, Suman Supratik, Surendra Uranw, Rajesh Kumar, Soniya Dulal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe primary objective of this study was to determine the prevalence, severity and associated factors of distress among primary caregivers of patients with cancer using the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) distress thermometer (DT). The secondary objective was to examine the demographic characteristics and cancer types associated with clinically significant distress.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted at the oncology unit of B.P. Koirala Institute of Health Sciences, Dharan, Nepal, surveying 173 primary caregivers of patients with cancer between 22 September 2023 and 20 June 2024. An interview-based questionnaire, including the NCCN DT, assessed the prevalence and severity of distress. Distress levels were categorised as mild (DT 0–3), moderate (DT 4–6) and severe (DT 7–10); a score of ≥4 denoted clinically significant distress which encompasses moderate and severe distress. Secondary outcomes included domain-specific distress, sociodemographic and clinical factors.ResultsDistress was reported by all caregivers, with 91.9% experiencing clinically significant (DT≥4) and 49.7% severe distress (DT≥7); median DT score was 6 (SD=1.755). Clinically significant distress was more common in caregivers aged ≥30 years, married individuals (p=0.03) and those with lower education levels (p=0.01). Caregivers of patients with gastrointestinal/hepatobiliary (p=0.029) and breast cancers reported higher distress. Statistically insignificant (p=0.208) higher distress was observed among caregivers of patients undergoing multimodal/palliative treatment. Domain-specific analysis revealed distress across physical, emotional, practical, social and spiritual domains.ConclusionDistress among caregivers of patients with cancer in Nepal is significantly high. Routine screening and interventions reduce adverse outcomes and improve both caregiver well-being and patient care.Keywords: Cancer, Quality of life, Spiritual care, Supportive care, Depression

Introduction and objectives

Cancer is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality across the globe. According to estimates from WHO in 2019, cancer is the first or second leading cause of death before the age of 70 years in 112 of 183 countries and ranks third or fourth in a further 23 countries.1 The diagnosis of cancer evokes fear, anxiety, hopelessness and many other distressing consequences both in patient and primary caregivers. Distress is defined by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) as a “multi-factorial unpleasant emotional experience of a psychological (cognitive, behavioral, emotional), social, and/or spiritual nature that may interfere with the ability to cope effectively with cancer, its physical symptoms, and its treatment.” Distress extends along a continuum, ranging from common normal feelings of vulnerability, sadness and fears to problems that can become disabling, such as depression, anxiety, panic, social isolation, and existential and spiritual crisis.2 The distress associated with cancer impacts not only patient but also caregivers to a great extent. A primary caregiver is someone who provides continued support and care to patients with cancer without taking any payment in return. Many caregivers, particularly family members, often act in an unpaid capacity to fulfil what they perceive to be an obligation to their family member who is diagnosed with cancer.3 The role of a caregiver can take many different forms, and, for some, it can even be a full-time job, with many caregivers having to do more things than they could handle.4 Over the past few decades, cancer care has shifted from an inpatient setting to an outpatient setting, placing greater responsibility on caregivers. The role played by caregivers is often context dependent, but typically ranges from providing emotional and practical support to assisting with medical care.5 However, providing support comes with its own set of challenges for caregivers, such as maintaining their own self‐care and needs.6 In taking care of all the cancer-related issues, caregivers’ quality of life gets affected to a great extent. Patients’ primary caregiver experiences a wide range of distress in terms of physical, emotional, social, spiritual or religious concerns. It drives the caregivers to the brink of physical and emotional breakdowns. Distress, which is seen among primary caregivers, is just the tip of the iceberg. The proportion of caregivers of patients with cancer is likely to increase based on the growing prevalence of cancer. The need for timely identification of a spectrum of distress among primary caregivers and addressing it is of paramount importance.

Thus, the diagnosis of cancer sets not only patients but also patients’ primary caregivers in distress. It takes a heavy toll on caregivers’ mental and physical health and other aspects of life. Adequate screening and necessary intervention for distress in the caregivers have been an overlooked issue. Distress among primary caregivers directly or indirectly impacts patients’ overall care and treatment. Distress should be recognised, monitored, documented and treated promptly at all stages of the disease and at all settings. A score of ≥4 on the distress thermometer (DT) should trigger further evaluation by the oncologist or nurse and referral to an appropriate resource, if needed.2 Distress is the fifth vital sign in oncology. Thus, timely detection and management of distress can significantly prevent undesirable and unfortunate events. The data from the studies done in a South Asian country and other parts of the world show varying results. The data regarding the prevalence and severity of distress among caregivers in Nepal are limited. This study aims at providing insights on the subject, which would perhaps strengthen the need for timely and adequate intervention in caregivers of patients with cancer.

Objectives

The primary objective of this study was to determine the prevalence, severity and associated factors of distress among primary caregivers of patients with cancer using NCCN DT (online supplemental figure 2). The secondary objective was to characterise the demographic characteristics and the type of cancer associated with significant distress.

Methods

Study design and study area

This cross-sectional study was conducted in ward, outpatient department and daycare centres of the Oncology Unit of Internal Medicine Department of B.P. Koirala Institute of Health Sciences, Dharan, Sunsari, Nepal, from 22 September 2023 to 20 June 2024.

Hence, a total of 173 participants were enrolled in this study.

Identification of the primary caregiver was done based on inclusion criteria. Data collection was done using the NCCN DT Nepali V.2.2022. Consent was taken before screening the patient for distress. The NCCN-DT and problem checklist were first given to the primary caregivers. The tool was self-administered, and participants took approximately 10 min to complete the screening. Those who were illiterate were assisted in documenting by reading out problems under each domain of NCCN DT. Demographic profile of study subjects (age, sex, region, mailing address, socioeconomic status, occupation, level of education, marital status, etc) was noted. History regarding the past known medical condition was taken. Caregivers diagnosed with chronic medical illness after the diagnosis of cancer in the patient were excluded in view of the creation of potential bias, as those medical illnesses could be the stressor. Chronic medical illnesses that were excluded were systemic hypertension, diabetes mellitus, thyroid dysfunction, physical deformity due to any aetiology and infections like pulmonary tuberculosis and leprosy.

Current medications of patients, including doses, were documented, if any. General physical examination and systemic examination were done to find out relevant abnormalities.

The NCCN DT Nepali V.2.2022 and problem checklist were used to measure caregivers’ level of distress. It is a validated tool to assess the levels of distress and the problem categories affecting their distress. The screening tool has two parts: the first part comprises the DT, a graphical representation of thermometer ranging from 0 to 10 representing the intensity of distress (0 = no distress and 10 = extreme/severe distress), and the second part comprises the problem checklist, which consists of 39 items.

It is divided into five different categories (physical concerns, emotional concerns, social concerns, practical concerns and spiritual/religious concerns). The DT asks the caregivers to rate their level of distress in the past 7 days on a scale from 0 to 10 (no distress to extreme distress). These values are categorised as low distress (0–3), moderate distress (4–6) and severe distress (7–10) for the purpose of analysis. A score ≥4 denotes a clinically significant marker for distress, alerting the screener that the person may require referral to a service to address their identified source(s) of distress. A score ≥7 denotes ‘severe’ distress requiring urgent intervention.8 9 A recent meta-analysis reported that the DT demonstrated 77.1% sensitivity and 66.1% specificity to detect cancer-related distress, and 80.9% sensitivity and 60.2% specificity to detect depression.10 The NCCN DT Nepali V.2.2022 screening tool was used to ease the process of comprehension (online supplemental figure 1).

Primary and secondary outcome measures

The primary outcomes included prevalence and severity of clinically significant distress, which was a score of ≥4 according to the NCCN DT. The secondary outcomes included domain-specific distress and associated sociodemographic and clinical factors.

Study period

The duration of the study period was 1 year.

Data analysis

Data were entered in MS Excel 2007. The NCCN DT Nepali V.2.2022 was added with a unique code. Primary caregivers of patients with cancer were instructed to rate their distress under each domain on a scale of 0–10.

Descriptive statistics such as frequency and percentage were presented for categorical data; mean and SD for normally distributed data and median or IQR for skewed data were calculated for quantitative variables. Qualitative data regarding the baseline characteristics were described using bar graphs and pie charts wherever necessary.

Data analysis was performed with SPSS 11.5.

Patient and public involvement

The components of the questionnaire were explained to the participants. The participants were not involved during data analysis or reporting of the study.

Our study shows all caregivers (100%) were clinically distressed, with the majority (91.9%) identified as having a clinically significant distress (score of ≥7 on NCCN DT). Only a small percentage (8.1%) of primary caregivers were found to have a mild distress. The median NCCN DT score for caregivers was 6 (SD 1.755).

Likewise, primary caregivers having a clinically significant distress were between the ages of 30–60 years and >60 years.

Among primary caregivers, age had no statistically significant association with the clinically significant distress (p=0.755).

Similarly, 69 caregivers (41.1%) were females, with more than half (58.9%) being males. Clinically significant distress was more prevalent in female primary caregivers. However, gender had no statistically significant association with the clinically significant distress (p=0.323).

More than half (51.5%) of the primary caregivers had received formal education up to the secondary level. However, primary caregivers who were illiterate and those who had received formal education up to primary and secondary levels reported a clinically significant level of distress. Education had a statistically significant association with the clinically significant distress (p=0.01).

A huge proportion (43.3%) of primary caregivers were unemployed. However, a clinically significant level of distress was more prevalent in those who were labours, government job holders and unemployed. Occupation had no statistically significant association with the clinically significant distress (p=0.228).

A majority of primary caregivers (71.09 %) were residents of Koshi zone of Nepal. Of those recorded, gastrointestinal and hepatobiliary cancers were the most prevalent, representing 33.5% of the total cancer cases. However, a clinically significant level of distress was more prevalent in those primary caregivers who were catering to bone cancer, skin cancer, lung cancer and malignancy of unknown primary. Type of cancer had a statistically significant association with the clinically significant distress (p=0.008). Breast cancer (p=0.000) and gastrointestinal and hepatobiliary cancer (p=0.029) had a statistically significant association with the clinically significant distress (online supplemental figure 3).

Stage of cancer was poorly recorded due to paucity of data. Hence, the severity of distress in relation to the stage of cancer could not be evaluated.

Most of the primary caregivers were sons in relation to the patient, representing 27.7% of the total subjects. The relation of the primary caregiver to the patient had no statistically significant association with the clinically significant distress (p=0.396).

The majority of patients were receiving chemotherapy (45.1%) and an equal proportion (45.1%) were being treated with surgery and chemotherapy. However, clinically significant distress was more prevalent in those primary caregivers who were catering to patients being treated with all three modalities of treatment (chemotherapy, radiotherapy and surgery) and those receiving supportive and palliative care. The modality of treatment had no statistically significant association with the clinically significant distress (p=0.208) (online supplemental figure 4).

The majority (60.1%) of primary caregivers fell under the upper lower socioeconomic group as per the modified Kuppuswamy score. All primary caregivers (100%) belonging to the lower socioeconomic group as per the modified Kuppuswamy scale had a clinically significant distress in our study. Socioeconomic status had no statistically significant association with the clinically significant distress (p=0.489).

Distress of primary caregivers was assessed in five different domains: physical concerns, emotional concerns, practical concerns, social concerns and spiritual or religious concerns. Among these, emotional concerns and practical concerns were the common distresses among primary caregivers of patients with cancer, occurring in 99.4% and 98.2%, respectively. Financial problem, worry or anxiety and grief or loss accounted for the three most frequently reported problems across all domains, with each reported by 83.2%, 81.5% and 75.2% of the samples, respectively.

The three most frequently reported physical concerns by caregivers were fatigue, changes in eating pattern and pain, reported by 74%, 66.5% and 65.9% of the caregivers, respectively. Sleep disturbance was identified as a problem causing distress by 50.9% of the subjects. Likewise, disturbance in sexual health and tobacco use was reported by 18.5% and 17.3% of the caregivers, respectively (figure 1).

In the emotional concerns category, sadness or depression, loneliness and fear were reported by 74%, 28.9% and 24.3% of the caregivers, respectively. Nearly a quarter of the primary caregivers (26%) reported an increase in anger after the diagnosis of cancer in the patient (figure 2).

In the social problem category, problems relating to relationship with family members and relationship with friends and coworkers were reported by 37.4% and 24.9% of the coworkers. Likewise, difficulty in communication with the healthcare team was reported by 9.8% of the primary caregivers during the course of treatment (figure 3).

Social concerns of primary caregivers of patients with cancer.

Practical concerns in terms of taking care of oneself, taking care of others, interruption of the regular work and transportation were reported by 36.4%, 40.5–57.2% and 32.4% of the primary caregivers, respectively. About a quarter (25.4%) of all primary caregivers had no insurance coverage and slightly less than two-quarters (63.6%) had difficulty in taking decisions regarding treatment of the patient (figure 4).

Discussion

Our study results showed that 91.9% of the primary caregivers reported clinically significant distress assessed using the NCCN DT. It depicted higher prevalence of clinically significant distress among primary caregivers compared with other studies using the same DT screening tool; for example, Rajeshwari et al7 reported 22.2% done in 102 primary caregivers, Hawkes et al11 reported 90.4% done in 341 subjects screened via community-based telephone helpline, Heckel et al12 reported 42% done in 108 primary caregivers screened by a telephone outcall programme and De Laurentis et al13 reported 63% done in 38 caregivers.

There exist multiple factors responsible for the higher prevalence of distress among primary caregivers in our study. Patients and, thus, primary caregivers encounter challenges in different forms during the treatment of cancer, financial problem being the prime stressor.

Most of the primary caregivers fall in the upper lower and lower middle socioeconomic groups. The unemployment rate is much higher among primary caregivers in Nepal, contributing to financial troubles. Moreover, access to healthcare facilities and healthcare team, poor insurance coverage, transportation problems due to difficult landscape, all contribute to higher distress among primary caregivers.

Clinically significant distress was more prevalent in those primary caregivers who were residents of zones other than Mechi and Koshi of Nepal, highlighting the relevance of difficult landscape and transportation facility in creating clinically significant distress among primary caregivers. Kirk et al14 reported 96.24% of all primary caregivers encountering clinically significant distress, showing resemblance to our study in terms of burden of clinically significant distress.

In our study, females reported higher prevalence of clinically significant distress, sharing common findings across most studies. The reason could be that male caregivers are less likely to seek support and/or potentially under-report their distress during screening, due to historic yet perpetual male stereotypes of needing to appear strong in the face of adversity.15

Socioeconomic group was found to be one of the strong determinants of level of distress among primary caregivers. Primary caregivers belonging to the lower socioeconomic group as per the modified Kuppuswamy scale were more likely to have a clinically significant distress compared with another group. The reason for this boils down to financial constraints in a developing country like Nepal.

Emotional concerns were among the commonly reported issues by the primary caregivers in our study, with worry or anxiety being the commonly encountered problem. Kirk et al14 showed that emotional problems accounted for more than half of the most frequently reported problems in caregivers with a clinically significant distress. In our study, sadness or depression was reported by almost three-quarters of the subjects. This finding seems to be an eye-opener for the treating physician as to why screening of distress and timely intervention in primary caregiver is of paramount importance.

Apart from common physical concerns like pain and fatigue, problems in sexual health were encountered by a significant proportion of spousal primary caregivers. Likewise, use of tobacco was also reported by 17.3% of the caregivers, putting them at a higher risk of cancer.

Distortion in relationship with a family member was reported by a significant proportion of the caregivers. In Nepal, people residing in a joint family is quite common, which creates misunderstanding among family members when it comes to catering to the patient.

Practical concerns were among the commonly reported problems. Financial problem, poor insurance coverage and dilemma in taking treatment decisions, all stem from caregivers’ high dependence on the patient before the diagnosis of cancer. Kirk et al14 reported the proportion of primary caregivers with difficulty in taking treatment decisions to be 28.2% as opposed to as high as 63.6% in our study. This difference could be attributed to the limited resources and poor financial status of caregivers.

Many caregivers still resort to faith healing and traditional home-based remedies for treating cancer due to the conflict between beliefs and cancer treatment, causing delay in the initiation of proper cancer treatment. In our study, children (son and daughter) were more commonly involved in caregiving. A study conducted by Rajeshwari et al7 showed that children were more commonly involved in caregiving and reported higher levels of distress. Further, spouses (husbands of patients with breast cancer) and adult children of dependent parents during the patients’ treatment exhibited significant distress.

Conclusion

The diagnosis of cancer is not just related to the patient in terms of distress it evokes. It equally affects primary caregivers by affecting their physical, mental, social and spiritual health. Distress among primary caregivers directly or indirectly impacts patients’ overall care and treatment. The prevalence of clinically significant distress has been found to be as high as 91.9% in our study, which is an alarming finding. Timely screening and, thus, necessary intervention of distress among primary caregivers of patients with cancer not only prevent adverse outcomes in caregivers but also improve the standard of care of patients. Hence, it is important for all treating physicians and healthcare teams to step up on screening and addressing primary caregivers’ distress.
