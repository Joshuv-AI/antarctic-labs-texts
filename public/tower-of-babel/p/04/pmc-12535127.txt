Efforts to increase uptake of cervical cancer screening in the absence of a national programme in Ghana: a qualitative study of stakeholders’ experience
Authors: Priscilla Naana Efua Wurudua Vandyck-Sey, Helen Elizabeth Denise Burchett
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical cancer is the fourth most frequently diagnosed cancer in women globally. Ghana, West of Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), is reported to have a high crude cervical cancer incidence of 18.3 per 100,000 women. Cervical cancer screening (CCS) uptake in Ghana has been reported to be between 0.8% and 12%. Ghana, like many developing countries worldwide, are yet to experience the benefits of organised screening. This study sought to understand the direct and indirect actions of key stakeholders in Ghana that contributed to the promotion of CCS in a country without a population-based screening programme.MethodologyPrimary qualitative data was obtained from 16 stakeholders using in-depth interviews via ZOOM virtual platform. Stakeholders were those involved in the delivery of CCS activities and were identified through purposive and snowball sampling. Transcripts were manually created from verbatim accounts of interviews. Data was managed in NVivo software and analysed using an inductive approach to qualitative data evaluation. Emerging themes were drawn out by thematic analysis.ResultsKey findings identified were intra-health facility-based factors that promoted CCS including cost control measures, screening methods, opportunistic CCS, promotion linked activities and innovative health practices. Additionally, sponsored mass CCS, staff training, policy advocacy and proactive health promotion and education were extra-health facility-based factors that stakeholders felt led to CCS uptake at the facility and community levels. Stakeholders played an active role in promoting CCS using health education through health promotional measures such as mass media advertisements, electronic fliers, creating social media content especially during the Cervical Cancer Awareness Month (CCAM) in January. CCS costs were often reduced during in January because of negotiations with non-governmental organisations and corporate bodies, ultimately resulting in sponsored mass cervical cancer screening which improved cervical cancer screening uptake.ConclusionIn the absence of a national cervical cancer screening programme, the study found out that it is the contributions of committed stakeholders that may be ensuring the continued existence of CCS services in Ghana. The goodwill that is shown by society during the CCAM in January should be harnessed to broaden the scope and reach of cervical screening activities beyond January.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24817-3.

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC) is the fourth most frequently diagnosed cancer globally and the fourth leading cause of death in women [1]. Over half a million new cases are diagnosed and 342,000 deaths occur yearly, with the highest regional incidence and mortality occurring in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [1, 2] Approximately 85% of women diagnosed with CC and 88% of deaths from CC worldwide are in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [3] CC is preventable and no woman should die from it. A third of all CC deaths worldwide occur in SSA [3] This contrasts the situation in High Income Countries (HIC) who have seen a decline in morbidity and mortality from CC due to state sanctioned and sponsored preventative CCS, uptake of HPV vaccines and early treatment of CC [4–6].

Interestingly, even in some HIC without population-based cervical cancer programmes, due to the incorporation of standardised clinical guidelines into their routine care this has helped to reduce the incidence and mortality from cervical cancer [7].

In 2018 the World Health Organisation initiated strategies to provide a comprehensive, population-based approach to assist all countries towards the goal of eliminating cervical cancer and this was subsequently adapted in 2020 [8]. Whereas some gains are being made globally in this regard, LMICs are still lagging behind due to challenges which persisted even before the pandemic and have worsened after the pandemic [8]. These challenges include lack of sustained prevention programmes, poor health infrastructure and the worsening brain drain of health workers [8]. Despite these problems CCS as a health service exists in most LMICS but the availability and accessibility of the service remains a challenge.

The Ghanaian situation

Ghana is reported to have a high crude CC incidence of 18.3 per 100,000 women, and a CC incidence rate about three times the global incidence rate [9].

A major contributing factor to the high morbidity and mortality experienced within LMICs can be attributed to a lack of an organised and subsidised screening programme for women [10, 11]. Gakidou et al. report a CCS coverage rate of 36.9% globally but only 18.5% in the least developed countries [12]. CCS uptake rates in Ghana have been reported to be between 0.8% and 12% [13–16] These rates are comparable to neighbouring countries, Cote d’Ivoire, Burkina Faso, and in SSA more widely; 7.5%, 11.07% and 12.8% respectively [17–19]. Olson et al. reported that Ghana and India were the 2 SAGE countries with the least effective CCS coverage of 4% and 5.3% respectively in their comparison with other SAGE countries, China, Russia, Mexico and South Africa to HIC like Australia and United Kingdom [11]. This is not unexpected in the case of Ghana since no CCS guidelines nor CCS programmes exist.

Screening policies and guidelines

After a Johns Hopkins Program for International Education in Gynecology and Obstetrics (JHPIEGO) project from 2001 to 2004 introduced Visual Inspection with Acetic acid (VIA) for CCS and cryotherapy for treatment of precancerous lesions, the expected nationally coordinated, government-led roll out of the initiative never took off in Ghana [20, 21]. Low political will and poor health infrastructure were cited as reasons for this [20, 21]. The first national policy on CC prevention in Ghana was developed as part of the National Reproductive Health Policy in 2005 and recommendations were made on screening and treatment [22]. The National Strategy for Cancer Control in Ghana: 2012–2016, recommended various screening methods endorsed by the WHO but it was not rolled out nationally [22]. In the absence of a national cervical cancer screening programme in Ghana, unlike other countries without programmes, Ghana additionally has no standardised CCS national guidelines to help improve uptake [11].

It may be assumed that most women in Ghana undergo opportunistic CCS [23, 24], i.e. is when women who present to health facilities for various complaints and are offered an opportunity to screen for cervical cancer. The women usually get screened by making out-of-pocket payments (OOP) then. CCS services are available at varying degrees from the primary to tertiary levels of care in Ghana and some individuals, facilities and organisations are undertaking activities aimed at increasing uptake of CCS, despite a lack of coordination at the national level. Most CCS occur in the urban areas and at secondary and tertiary levels which reduces access of CCS services to most women. CCS services are available in all regions of Ghana in the urban areas. Stakeholders like those at the Cervical Cancer Prevention and Training Centre (CCPTC) have taken initiatives to formalise training of health care workers in cervical cancer screening in Ghana [25]. However, if there are other established activities or interventions at the institutional or community level, leading to improved uptake of CCS it needs to be documented.

Measures to increase CCS uptake

Targeted education using a video-based educational intervention and sustained educational sessions have been found to increase uptake of CCS in Ghana and Malawi respectively [26, 27]. Reminder letters and/or cards and mass media work is noted to increase CCS uptake in other jurisdictions [28] Patient navigation approaches have also been used to increase CCS elsewhere [29, 30] Social marketing strategies were used in a study in Lagos, Nigeria, however these failed to demonstrate an effect on CCS uptake rates [31]. It is not clear if any of these, or other, strategies are being used in Ghana to promote uptake of CCS.

Previous studies in Ghana have reported barriers to uptake of cervical cancer screening, mostly from women’s perspectives [14, 21–24]. Both qualitative and quantitative studies in Ghana have outlined nurses’ views about their knowledge and the barriers to CCS [32, 33].

Barriers identified in some of these studies in Ghana and other LMIC include personal barriers such as fear of abnormal results [17, 34–36], perceived invulnerability [36, 37], misconceptions about CCS [11, 34, 37], costs [11, 36, 38], lack of information of where to screen [36, 39], low awareness [11, 17, 36, 39], low educational and socioeconomic status. Some common social barriers identified include lack of spousal or social support [35], taboos on gender of provider [34, 35], and religious beliefs. Some institutional barriers such as distance from screening centres, unavailable screening centres, perceived provider attitude [35, 40], lack of privacy [37], lack of equipment and absence of trained service personnel have also been described [37].

Yet in Ghana like many other LMICs that lack a population-based screening programme for cervical cancer some form of CCS still occurs.

However there has been little exploration of the efforts deployed by stakeholders such as clinicians, health managers, professional groups, and others in Ghana on how their actions lead to improvement of CCS uptake. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, fewer studies have been conducted focusing on promoters of CCS. To address this gap in knowledge we aimed to gather experiences of clinical and non-clinical stakeholders about their activities and efforts that were geared towards increasing uptake of CCS in Ghana.

Methods

This qualitative study used semi-structured interviews of professional CCS stakeholders to explore what measures are being utilised to increase the uptake of CCS in Ghana.

A sample of stakeholders known in Ghana to be engaged in CCS activities based on their profession and position were purposively invited to participate; others were recruited by snowball sampling based on recommendations from initial participants. A total of 24 potential participants were identified to be potentially interviewed at the commencement of a 6-week period of data collection. Participants were recruited from different regions in both the private and public sector across Ghana, from a range of professional roles, to ensure varied experiences and perspectives. Eligible stakeholders included health managers, community health workers, nurses, doctors, policy makers, representatives of professional groups and journalists. Journalists were included as non-clinical stakeholders because their occupational roles include publication of health information and the creation of an enabling media environment that propagates messages that may potentially improve CC and CCS knowledge and awareness.

Semi-structured interviews included questions about what stakeholders were doing to promote CCS as individuals and or what institutions they may be representing also do to encourage CCS uptake. Interviews were recorded remotely via ZOOM software. Participants chose their own pseudonyms and were identified by their pseudonyms in the recordings and transcripts. Respondents were offered a token of 100 Ghana cedis for internet data. Signed consent forms were electronically obtained from respondents prior to their scheduled Zoom sessions and verbal consent was additionally captured as part of the zoom video and audio recordings.

All the interviews were transcribed verbatim. Subsequently the transcripts were uploaded into NVivo software and codes were generated line by line. More codes were added as more transcripts were coded. Subsequently codes that could be merged were merged. All documents were systematically coded using an inductive approach to coding analysis, inspired by Braun and Clarke’s six phase approach of data familiarisation, initial code generation, theme recognition, themes review, theme definition and report write-up [41]. NVivo qualitative data analysis software assisted with organising and managing the dataset. As this primary research was conducted as a dissertation PVS coded the document and HB reviewed it as the supervisor and gave feedback on the emergent themes. Due to time limitations other means of gathering qualitative information such as Focus Group Discussions and observational approaches could not be utilised.

Of the eight that were not recruited five did not engage despite at least two reminders, two indicated their busy schedules would not permit them to be interviewed and one person had internet challenges.

The respondents reported that during the CCAM they actively sought support from corporate institutions and NGOS to facilitate free or affordable screening for unscreened women in Ghana. Accounts from some stakeholders suggested that these subsidised or free CCS during the CCAM or sporadically throughout the year caused an acute rise in screening activities.“So, we’ve been organising free screenings which over the past couple of months we’ve been able to screen over 100 women for free.”(Health manager, public, Central Region)"And then finally, every year as part of the cervical cancer awareness week we try to do some highly subsidized screening in the facility.” (Health manager, public, Greater Accra region)cProactive Health Promotion and Health EducationRespondents mentioned that during the CCAM, they engaged in more rigorous health education, mostly through radio, TV and in-person health talks, at varied settings such as social gatherings, churches, mosques, schools etc. Some also leveraged social media to target a different audience. Discussion

This study found a range of factors and activities which respondents believed affected CCS uptake in Ghana, despite a lack of coordination at the national level.

Opportunistic Cervical cancer screening

Some respondents felt that the most common way women are offered CCS all year round is when they present to health facilities for other reasons i.e. opportunistic CCS. However, opportunistic CCS is a passive way of promoting CCS uptake and is likely to widen inequalities, as women who do not visit health facilities may never be offered screening. Opportunistic CCS has been found to be ineffective in reducing CC morbidity and mortality, hence it may be a contributing factor to the high CC mortality rates in countries without national screening programmes [9, 42, 43]. OOP opportunistic CCS found in this study may be contributing to the low uptake of CCS observed in Ghana [13, 14, 16, 44]. However, a consideration can be made for more opportunities to be created for screening more women in established health services such as during family planning services, antenatal and post-natal visits [45]. Some studies in India have shown that when CCS is conducted during the antenatal period it is important to repeat these tests in the post-natal period as abnormal outcomes may persist beyond delivery [46]. Additionally, a number of studies have found screening amongst antenatal and post-natal women acceptable and one that can be considered in Ghana [45–48]. Effah et al. reported on a study of the use of HPV DNA screening in antenatal and post-natal women in Battor in Ghana [49]. Measures like these are likely to increase uptake in areas with the absence of organised screening.

Cervical cancer awareness month

Respondents agreed that the month of January saw an increased uptake of CCS compared to other months of the year mostly due to the CCAM occurring annually in January. It may be because women were likely to have been educated about CCS during CCAM activities and received subsidised or free CCS during this period. Tin et al. also corroborate this finding that free or subsidised cost of CCS may increase uptake of CCS than a costed procedure [50]. Some respondents explained that, in the absence of sponsored screening and/or outreach, some facilities did not provide regular screening services outside of this month, despite having skilled staff.

Reducing cost as a barrier

Participants believed that sponsored CCS was an important driver of CCS uptake in Ghana. Overtime, the Ghanaian CCAM in January has become the pivot around which CCS activities revolve and demonstrates an active role played by respondents on CCS uptake. Interestingly, studies in Nigeria reported low CCS uptake despite subsidised CCS cost [51, 52] It was postulated in one of these studies that some psychosocial barriers were affecting uptake apart from cost [51]. This suggests more research is needed into the impact of sponsored mass opportunistic CCS in Ghana. It is a well-known factor that cost is a major barrier to CCS [11, 36, 38] The idea of reducing cost by having many women undergoing CCS was suggested by Quentin et al., when they were looking at cost projections using VIA for screening women nationally [53]. This suggests that ultimately, cost effectiveness may be linked to demand or uptake of CCS. A high demand for CCS services is likely to make investments in screening set-ups more cost effective, providing an argument for a population-based national cervical cancer screening programme. However, this is still unavailable in Ghana [43].

Some participants described various initiatives to encourage CCS which the authors described as innovative health practices. Conducting a pelvic examination without the ‘stirrup’ was identified in literature to reduce vulnerability and physical discomfort in women during gynaecological examination [54]. This technique of cervical cancer sampling was considered by the authors as an innovative health practice however none of the respondents described utilising this approach in CCS in Ghana. Purposefully delaying pelvic assessments to combine with CCS where possible, is likely to result in decreased frequency of pelvic examination, which could improve the acceptability and uptake of CCS. Research suggests that HPV DNA self-sampling for CCS increases participation rates irrespective of jurisdiction [55, 56]. Additionally a systematic review of interventions to increase CCS found that self-sampling with HPV testing may increase uptake of CCS relative to VIA [50]. A study in Burkina Faso, a neighbouring country of Ghana, observed that Care HPV a form of HPV DNA screening could be considered cost-effective depending on the willingness to pay threshold because it had a higher sensitivity compared to VIA and Visual Inspection with Lugol’s Iodine [57] Interestingly, HPV DNA was mentioned by few respondents as a screening test known for its convenience rather than its cost-effectiveness. It may also be the case that in the Burkina Faso study was under consideration as a CCS in the context of a population-based screening whilst population-based screening is currently unavailable in Ghana. interviewees stated that the CCS methods currently being utilised are selected based on affordability, availability and convenience. Almost all respondents reported doing some ad hoc promotional activities to encourage uptake through individual and or institutional efforts. Respondents described measures such as repeated public announcements in places of worship, hospitals and communities, mass media advertisements, audiovisuals and print media as a means of CCS awareness creation. The video-based educational sessions used by some participants has already been evaluated in Ghana, with positive results [26]. Studies elsewhere have reported that sustained, structured health education leads to increased uptake of CCS [26, 27, 58]. These measures have similarly been utilised in other studies with positive outcomes in Australia and Bangladesh [59–61] More robust studies are however, needed to determine the direct effect of these measures on CCS uptake in Ghana. Some studies described the use of reminder letters, cards, barrier specific telephone counselling as a cost-effective way to promote CCS [28, 62]. Studies that used some of these methods did so in the context of population-based CCS, hence it was not surprising that no respondent in this study mentioned reminder letters, cards and telephone counselling as methods of promoting CCS in Ghana. However, a systematic review by Tin et al. reported that reminding women to screen via phone calls may increase uptake of CCS compared to letters [50].

In a study where the health system readiness of six countries in SSA were evaluated to determine their readiness to adapt the WHO 2030 goals as regards CC most had important policies and guidelines which is still lacking in Ghana [63]. For instance all the countries reviewed i.e. Eswatini, Rwanda, Guinea, Malawi, Uganda and Zambia had dedicated cervical cancer units at their respective Ministries of health which is initiative lacking in Ghana. Additionally, with the exception of Uganda, just like in Ghana, the other 5 countries all have guidelines on CCS [63]. In this study none of the respondents mentioned the adherence of any guidelines or specific recommendations as a way to promote CCS uptake.

Overall, it appeared stakeholders were motivated to engage in various activities to encourage CCS uptake. The number and variety of promotional activities that took place suggests a motivated and proactive approach to increasing uptake. However, these activities peaked during the CCAM. Also due to the uncoordinated nature of these individual and institutional efforts there is the risk of over screening in some women who may not be due for screening whilst other women may miss out on opportunities to screen. Although the efforts of these stakeholders are remarkable the distribution of these benefits may be skewed and limited to only certain regions or urban areas. Hence, a nationally coordinated population based cervical cancer screening programme is still needed to ensure that more women receive access to this preventative service. If there were a nationally coordinated CCS programme, activities could be more consistent between regions and facilities, and their impact potentially greater.

Strengths and limitations

This primary qualitative data study was able to gather views of CCS stakeholders from diverse backgrounds. The first author’s position as a physician facilitated access to medical colleagues. Nevertheless, the sample was small and may not represent all views held by stakeholders in Ghana. In particular, there were few interviews with nurses and other health staff, as were views from private health sector providers. Additionally, the use of only in-depth interviews was a limitation as other qualitative methods of data collection could have enriched the research. Observational studies could help to verify or support what respondents said may be increasing CCS. This was not feasible due to time and resource constraints.

Conclusion

OOP Opportunistic CCS and sponsored Opportunistic CCS were the most described screening strategies in Ghana. There were several ways that CCS stakeholders played an active role in promoting CCS, especially during the CCAM in January. However, without a National CCS programme in Ghana, such activities are ad hoc, time-limited and vary between facilities. A nationally coordinated approach could enable the consistent and universal use of some health promotional measures known to improve CCS uptake.

In the absence of a national cervical cancer screening programme, the study found out that it is the contributions of stakeholders (such as health care workers, health managers of some health institutions, professional health associations and non-clinical professionals like health journalists) in both the private and public sectors that may be ensuring the continued existence of CCS services in Ghana.
