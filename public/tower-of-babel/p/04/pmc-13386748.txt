Digital addiction comorbidity patterns and their differential links to internalizing symptoms, suicidal behaviors and non-suicidal self-injury in vocational high school students
Authors: Liping Wen, Lu Xu, Li Bai, Lingyan Zhu, Liangxue Liao, Ruini He, Kezhi Liu, Yilin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDigital addiction is a growing public health concern among adolescents, yet evidence on co-occurring addiction patterns and their differential associations with mental health outcomes remains limited, particularly in vocational students.MethodsA cross-sectional survey of 15,311 vocational high school students was conducted in Zigong, China. Smartphone, gaming, and social media addiction were assessed using validated scales. Participants were classified into no addiction, single addiction, and multiple addictions groups. Outcomes included internalizing symptoms (clinically significant depression or anxiety), suicidal behaviors (ideation, planning, or attempts), and non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI). Hierarchical regression models with progressive adjustment were supplemented by sensitivity analyses using continuous scores and operating characteristic (ROC)-derived alternative cut-offs.ResultsA total of 20.9% of participants met the criteria for one form of digital addiction, with smartphone-only addiction being the most prevalent pattern (19.4%). A graded association was observed, with the prevalence of all adverse outcomes increasing progressively with the number of addictions (all p < 0.001). In fully adjusted models, single addiction was associated with internalizing symptoms (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.54–2.04), suicidal behaviors (OR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.24–1.52), and NSSI (OR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.24–1.63). Multiple addictions were associated with internalizing symptoms (OR = 5.58, 95% CI: 3.71–8.41) and suicidal behaviors (OR = 2.56, 95% CI: 1.74–3.77), but the association with NSSI was attenuated (OR = 1.17, 95% CI: 0.81–1.70, p = 0.404). When the three continuous addiction scores were mutually adjusted, smartphone addiction severity remained independently associated only with internalizing symptoms (OR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.04–1.06), whereas gaming and social media addiction severities were independently associated with both suicidal behaviors and NSSI. Triple addiction was associated with the highest risk of suicidal behaviors (OR = 3.42, 95% CI: 1.62–7.21). Sensitivity analyses using data-driven ROC-optimal cut-offs preserved the core graded pattern.ConclusionCo-occurring digital addictions are common among vocational students and exhibit graded associations with adverse mental health outcomes. Background

The rapid growth of digital technologies has significantly impacted adolescent development, a critical developmental period spanning approximately ages 10–19 years as defined by the World Health Organization, expanding opportunities for social interaction, entertainment, and access to information. However, the line between healthy digital engagement and excessive, harmful use has become increasingly blurred, contributing to the emergence of digital addiction as a growing global public health issue [1, 2]. Digital addiction is a broad term that includes problematic behaviour, i.e., excessive use of smartphones, social networking, and online gaming. These behaviours are characterized by reduced control over usage, increased importance, tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, and impairment in daily life [3, 4].

The prevalence of digital addiction among adolescents and young adults has reached concerning levels. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported pooled prevalence rates of 26.99% for smartphone addiction, 17.42% for social media addiction, and 14.22% for internet addiction, although these rates vary across different regions [1]. Increasing evidence indicates that compulsive digital engagement is strongly associated with severe mental health consequences among young people. For example, a large systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Gillespie et al., which synthesized data from 61 studies involving more than 338,000 participants aged 18 years or younger, found that internet addiction was significantly associated with both suicidal behaviour (SB, i.e., suicidal ideation, suicidal planning, and suicide attempts; pooled OR = 3.08) and non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI; deliberate self-harm without suicidal intent, such as cutting or burning). These relationships were often mediated by psychological and behavioural factors such as insomnia and depression [5]. Similarly, a systematic review by Losaberidze et al. reported strong associations between NSSI and problematic internet use and problematic gaming, identifying shared underlying mechanisms, including impulsivity and difficulties in emotional regulation [6].

Besides these serious outcomes, digital addiction has consistently been linked to various negative health issues in adolescents, i.e., depression (OR = 1.76), anxiety (OR = 2.14), sleep problems (OR = 1.50), and problematic substance use (OR = 1.94), with a dose-response relationship observed between smartphone use and poor sleep quality [7, 8]. Excessive digital use may replace crucial health-related behaviors such as sleep and physical activity. Moreover, online environments can expose adolescents to social comparison, harmful content, and cyber-victimization, all of which may increase psychological distress [9, 10].

Despite the existing studies, significant gaps remain in the literature. First, most existing studies have examined individual forms of digital addiction separately, which does not adequately capture the reality that multiple addictive digital behaviors can occur simultaneously within the same individual [6, 11]. The concept of “multiple digital addictions,” where adolescents experience dependencies on smartphones, gaming, and social media simultaneously, has not been sufficiently explored. This gap is especially important, as emerging research indicates that these behaviors may share core psychological mechanisms, such as impulsivity, emotional dysregulation, and maladaptive coping strategies, which could increase risk when they co-occur [6, 12]. For example, Losaberidze et al. suggested that problematic internet use, gaming, and gambling might reinforce each other over time, potentially forming a “maladaptive coping cycle” that increases psychological distress and vulnerability to severe outcomes like NSSI [6]. Similarly, Gillespie et al. found that internet addiction, gaming disorder, and mobile phone addiction were each linked to SB, though the strength of these associations varied, suggesting that different types of digital engagement might carry different risk levels [5]. Few studies have examined how risks may accumulate when multiple digital dependencies exist together. It is therefore necessary that such comorbidity produces a synergistic, dose–response effect on mental health outcomes, possibly exceeding the risks associated with any single addiction. This idea aligns with broader addiction research, which shows that co-occurring addictive behaviour, such as polysubstance use, often leads to compounded harms [13].

Second, vulnerable subgroups, including vocational high school students, remain underrepresented in research on digital addiction. This population often faces unique psychosocial challenges, such as academic pressures associated with vocational training, societal stigma surrounding vocational education, and an earlier transition into adult roles and employment responsibilities. In China, vocational high school students have been increasingly recognized as a high-risk group for mental health problems, with studies reporting elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and internet addiction compared to their peers in academic high schools [14]. Moreover, these students often face social stigma and discrimination due to public perceptions of vocational education as a marker of academic failure, which may further compromise their psychological well-being and increase their vulnerability to maladaptive coping behaviors such as excessive digital engagement [15, 16]. These factors may increase vulnerability to maladaptive coping strategies, including excessive digital engagement [17, 18]. Third, recent discussions highlight the need to move beyond broad measures of digital use to better understand how different types and contexts of digital engagement (e.g., gaming versus social media) may relate differentially to outcomes such as self-harm. However, empirical evidence exploring these detailed relationships remains limited [7, 11].

To address these gaps, this study uses data from a large-scale survey of 15,311 vocational high school students in Zigong, China. The study aims to: (1) describe the distribution of specific digital addiction patterns related to smartphones, gaming, and social media; (2) explore the relationship between the number of digital addictions (none, single, or multiple) and the prevalence of internalizing symptoms, SBs, and NSSI; (3) examine the independent links between particular combinations of digital addictions and these outcomes; and (4) evaluate the relationship between continuous addiction severity scores and mental health outcomes. Based on the existing literature, we hypothesized that a greater number of co-occurring digital addictions would be associated with higher rates of internalizing symptoms, SBs, and NSSI, and that distinct addiction patterns would show differential associations with these outcomes. As this is a cross-sectional investigation, the findings are exploratory in nature and intended to generate hypotheses for future longitudinal research, which may ultimately inform the development of targeted prevention strategies for this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted as part of the Zigong Adolescent Mental Health Screening Project in 2025. A census-based cluster sampling approach was adopted to maximize representativeness. Specifically, all seven vocational high schools located in Zigong City, Sichuan Province, China, were invited to participate, and all agreed to take part in the survey. All students enrolled in these schools during the 2024–2025 academic year were eligible for inclusion. Because the sampling frame comprised the entire population of vocational high school students in the city, the sample can be considered highly representative of this target population, and no weighting procedures were applied.

Eligible participants were required to: (1) be aged between 12 and 20 years and (2) provide informed consent, along with written informed consent from a parent or legal guardian. Students were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) severe cognitive impairment; (2) significant communication difficulties; (3) incomplete answers in the questionnaire; or (4) responses with a total completion time shorter than the pre-established minimum valid duration for any of the standardized instruments. These minimum time thresholds were determined by having four experienced psychotherapists (each with ≥ 3 years of psychological assessment experience) complete each questionnaire as rapidly as possible without engaging with the item content; the shortest time recorded across the four raters was adopted as the validity cut-off. The specific thresholds were as follows: Smartphone Application-Based Addiction Scale (SABAS; < 6 s), Internet Gaming Disorder Scale–Short Form (IGDS9-SF; < 5 s), Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS; < 6 s), 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9; < 10 s), 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7; < 7 s), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI; < 20 s), Multidimensional Peer Victimization Scale (MPVS; < 5 s) and the 26-item Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC; <20 s ). Any standardized questionnaire completed faster than its respective threshold was considered invalid and excluded from analyses.

Measures

Digital addictions

Digital addiction was evaluated across three areas: smartphone use, gaming, and social media activity.

Smartphone addiction was measured using the SABAS. This tool includes six items that measure addictive behaviour related to smartphone applications over the past 12-month recall period. Each item is rated on a 6-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree), resulting in total scores between 6 and 36. In line with validation research among Chinese adolescents, a score of ≥ 23 was used to indicate problematic smartphone use [19].

Internet gaming disorder was measured using the IGDS9-SF. This 9-item tool was based on DSM-5 diagnostic criteria and evaluates the severity of gaming disorder over the past 12-month recall period. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (never) to 5 (very often). Participants were classified as “no problematic use” (≤ 20), “at-risk users” (21–31), or “probable cases” of gaming disorder (≥ 32) according to established cut-offs validated in Chinese adolescents [20].

Social media addiction was measured using the BSMAS. This six-item instrument measures key addiction features, such as salience, mood modification, tolerance, withdrawal, conflict, and relapse, related to social media use over the past 12-month recall period. Responses are given on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (very rarely) to 5 (very often). Consistent with previous studies in Chinese populations, participants were categorized as “no problematic use” (≤ 18), “at-risk users” (19–23), or “probable cases” of social media addiction (≥ 24) [21].

For the primary analyses, participants were grouped based on the number of digital addictions identified. Individuals exceeding the cut-off for problematic use in any area were considered to have an addiction in that area. Participants were classified as: “no addiction” (no problematic use in any area), “single addiction” (problematic use in only one area), and “multiple addictions” (problematic use in two or three areas).

Internalizing symptoms, suicidal behavior and non-suicidal self-injury

Based on conceptual distinctions in developmental psychopathology [22], we categorized mental health outcomes into three domains: internalizing symptoms, SB, and NSSI. Internalizing symptoms refer to emotional distress directed inward, including depression and anxiety [22]. SBs encompass actions involving an intent to die, including suicidal ideation (SI), suicidal planning (SP), and suicidal attempts (SA) [23]. NSSI was defined as deliberate self-harm without suicidal intent, which has partially distinct psychosocial correlates and risk factors from those of SBs [23].

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the PHQ-9 over a two-week recall period. According to established guidelines, a score of 10 or higher was used to indicate clinically relevant depression [24]. Anxiety levels were assessed with the GAD-7 over a two-week recall period, with a score of ≥ 10 indicating clinically significant anxiety [24].

Suicidal and self-injurious behaviors were assessed using four self-report items, each referring to the past year. SI and SP were evaluated with the items: “In the past year, have you ever had thoughts of ending your life?” and “In the past year, have you ever made a plan to end your life?”, respectively. Responses were recorded on a 5-point frequency scale ranging from “never” to “very often (at least once a week or more).” SA were assessed with the item: “In the past year, have you ever taken action to try to end your life?”, rated on a 4-point frequency scale from “never” to “more than five times a year.” NSSI was evaluated with the item: “In the past year, how many times have you intentionally hurt yourself without the intention to die (e.g., cutting, burning, bruising yourself on purpose)?” For all analyses, responses to each item were dichotomized as “yes” (any occurrence) or “no” (never).”

Covariates

A wide range of covariates was included in the analyses to control for potential confounding factors. These variables covered demographic details, health-related behavioral and sleep variables, and psychosocial aspects.

Demographic variables included age, sex, type of residence (rural area, township, or urban city), only-child status (yes/no), left-behind status (yes/no), single-parent family status (yes/no), and perceived relationship quality with the primary caregiver, which was categorized as very good/good, average, or poor/very poor.

Health-related behaviour included participation in weekly physical activity (yes/no) and lifetime histories of e-cigarette use (yes/no), cigarette smoking (yes/no), and alcohol consumption (yes/no). Sleep quality was assessed using the PSQI over a one-month recall period. Based on established scoring standards, a global PSQI score ≥ 6 was used to indicate poor sleep quality or sleep disturbance [25, 26].

Psychosocial characteristics included stressful life events, peer victimization, and aggressive behaviour among peers. Experiences of peer victimization and aggression were measured using instruments derived from the Multidimensional Peer Victimization Scale [27], with indicators related to cyberbullying. The measurement tool contained two separate five-item subscales: one assessing experiences of being bullied and the other evaluating perpetration of bullying. These subscales measured both offline and online types of peer aggression commonly observed among vocational school adolescents across five areas: relational (e.g., social exclusion), physical (e.g., hitting or pushing), cyber (e.g., sending harmful messages or sharing private information online without permission), property-related (e.g., damaging or stealing belongings), and verbal (e.g., insults or public humiliation). All items were rated on a five-point frequency scale from 0 (never) to 4 (frequently). Separate total scores were calculated for peer victimization and peer aggression, with higher scores indicating high frequency or severity. Exposure to stressful life events (SLEs) was evaluated using the Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC) [28, 29]. This validated 26-item tool assesses the occurrence and perceived impact of common stressors experienced over the past 12 months. The scale includes five domains, and scores from all domains were used as covariates in the fully adjusted models: interpersonal stress, academic stress, adaptation-related stress, loss-related stress, and punishment-related stress [29]. For each item, participants first indicated whether the event had occurred. If it did, they then rated the perceived impact on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (no impact) to 5 (extremely severe impact). Subscale scores were calculated by summing the relevant items, with higher totals indicating greater exposure to stressful experiences.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted utilizing SPSS 25.0. Descriptive statistics characterized the total sample and delineated findings across the three addiction classifications: ‘no addiction’, ‘single addiction’, and ‘multiple addictions’. Continuous variables exhibiting non-normal distributions appeared as medians (25th and 75th percentiles), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages). Group disparities were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous measures and the chi-square test for categorical ones.

Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the relationships among the three digital addiction measures: (SABAS, IGDS9-SF, and BSMAS). To investigate the associations between the number of digital addictions (none, single, multiple) and the three primary outcomes, we conducted a series of binary logistic regression analyses with progressive, hierarchical adjustment. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for demographic variables ( age, sex, residence, only-child status, left-behind status, single-parent family, and relationship with primary caregiver). Model 3 additionally adjusted for health-related behavioral and sleep variables ( weekly exercise, e-cigarette use, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, and sleep disturbance). Model 4 (the fully adjusted model) further included psychosocial variables (the five subscales of stressful life events, peer victimization score, and peer aggression score).

We acknowledge that some covariates included in Model 4 (e.g., stressful life events, peer victimization) may lie on the causal pathway between digital addiction and mental health outcomes, potentially acting as mediators rather than pure confounders. Adjusting for such variables may yield attenuated effect estimates. To provide transparency regarding the impact of adjustment, we present results from all four models so that the progressive attenuation of odds ratios across models can be evaluated.

To explore the effects of specific addiction combinations, additional fully adjusted logistic regression models (based on Model 4 specifications) were performed to assess associations between each unique addiction pattern (e.g., smartphone addiction only, smartphone plus gaming addiction) and the three outcomes, with the “no addiction” group serving as the reference category.

As an additional sensitivity analysis to address the potential influence of categorical cut-offs and to isolate the independent contributions of each digital addiction type, we conducted two complementary sets of analyses using continuous addiction severity scores. First, each addiction scale (SABAS, IGDS9-SF, BSMAS) was entered separately into fully adjusted models to assess its overall association with outcomes. Second, all three scale scores were entered simultaneously into the same fully adjusted model to identify the unique, independent effect of each addiction type while controlling for the others.

To further validate the cut-off scores used in our primary categorical analyses, we conducted Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analyses using both individual and a composite adverse mental health outcome (the presence of internalizing symptoms, SBs, or NSSI) as reference standards. The area under the curve (AUC) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated to assess the discriminative ability of each scale. The optimal cut-off was determined by maximizing the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). As an additional robustness check, we repeated the primary logistic regression analyses using these data-driven alternative cut-offs to assess whether our core findings were sensitive to the choice of classification thresholds.

Results from all regression analyses are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with respective 95% CIs. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Missing data were minimal (< 2%) and were handled using listwise deletion.

Results

Participant characteristics and distribution of digital addiction patterns

The analytic sample included 15,311 vocational high school students, with a median age of 16 years (IQR: 15–17), approximately 61.5% of whom were male. Overall, 20.9% of students (n = 3,201) met the criteria for at least one type of digital addiction. As summarized in Table 1, the most common addiction profile was smartphone addiction alone, reported by 2,968 students (19.4%). Addiction involving only gaming or only social media was very rare, occurring in 0.085% and 0.098% of participants, respectively. Co-occurring addictions were found in 144 students (0.94%), with smartphone plus gaming addiction being the most frequent dual pattern (n = 86, 0.56%). A combination of all three addictive behaviours (smartphone, gaming, and social media) was observed among 61 students (0.40%). Correlation analyses showed moderate positive links among the three addiction scales, with Spearman’s coefficients ranging from 0.419 to 0.547 (all p < 0.001) (Table S1). These results indicate that although the scales measure related types of problematic digital engagement, they represent different behavioral constructs.

Participant characteristics based on the number of digital addictions are shown in Table 2. Significant differences were observed among the no addiction, single addiction, and multiple addictions groups across nearly all measured variables. In fully adjusted models (Model 4), single addiction remained associated with internalizing symptoms (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.54–2.04, p < 0.001), SBs (OR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.24–1.52, p < 0.001), and NSSI (OR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.24–1.63, p < 0.001). Multiple addictions were associated with internalizing symptoms (OR = 5.58, 95% CI: 3.71–8.41, p < 0.001) and SBs (OR = 2.56, 95% CI: 1.74–3.77, p < 0.001); the association with NSSI was attenuated and no longer significant (OR = 1.17, 95% CI: 0.81–1.70, p = 0.404).

Sensitivity analyses

Several sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings. First, analyses with individual symptoms as separate outcomes (depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation, plan, and attempt) yielded a consistent graded pattern across all four hierarchical models (Table S2): the odds of each symptom increased progressively from no addiction to single addiction to multiple addictions, mirroring the primary composite outcome analyses.

Second, ROC analyses using individual and composite adverse outcomes as reference standards (Table S3) showed acceptable discriminative ability (SABAS AUC: 0.660–0.769; IGDS9-SF AUC: 0.711–0.772; BSMAS AUC: 0.712–0.783). Data-driven cut-offs maximizing the Youden index against internalizing symptoms were SABAS ≥ 21, IGDS9-SF ≥ 14, and BSMAS ≥ 10. The addiction pattern distribution under these alternative thresholds is presented in Table S4.

Repeating the primary analyses with these data-driven cut-offs (Table S5) preserved the core finding of a stepwise increase in risk across addiction groups. For internalizing symptoms, both single addiction (OR = 1.83, 95% CI: 1.48–2.26, p <0.001) and multiple addictions (OR = 3.16, 95% CI: 2.51–3.92, p <0.001) remained significantly associated with elevated odds. Comparable patterns were observed for SBs and NSSI. These findings confirm that our core conclusions are robust to the choice of classification thresholds.

Discussion

This large cross-sectional study provides new evidence on the co-occurrence of multiple digital addictions and their associations with mental health outcomes among vocational high school students in China. Several important findings emerged. First, while smartphone-only addiction was the most prevalent pattern (19.4%), a meaningful minority of students exhibited co-occurring addictions, including dual (0.94%) and triple (0.40%) patterns. Second, a clear graded association was observed between the number of digital addictions and all three outcome domains—internalizing symptoms, SBs, and NSSI—with the risk of adverse outcomes increasing progressively from no addiction to single addiction to multiple addictions. These associations remained significant after adjustment for demographic, behavioral, and psychosocial covariates, although the multiple addictions–NSSI association was attenuated to non-significance in the fully adjusted model, suggesting that psychosocial factors such as stressful life events and peer victimization may partially account for this relationship. Third, distinct addiction patterns showed differential associations with outcomes, with combinations involving gaming and social media, particularly triple addiction, showing the strongest associations with SBs. Fourth, and notably, when the three addiction scales were mutually adjusted as continuous scores, smartphone addiction severity remained independently associated only with internalizing symptoms, whereas gaming and social media addiction severities were independently associated with both SBs and NSSI. This differential pattern indicates that specific digital engagements, rather than device usage per se, may precipitate distinct psychopathological trajectories.

The prevalence of digital addiction in our sample (20.9%) is broadly consistent with global estimates, although direct comparisons are complicated by heterogeneity in measurement instruments and cut-off thresholds across studies [1]. A robust, stepwise increase in risk was observed for internalizing symptoms, SBs, and NSSI across the spectrum of no addiction, single addiction, and multiple addictions. This pattern aligns with a cumulative risk framework, wherein the co-occurrence of multiple behavioral addictions may exert a synergistic effect on psychological distress, paralleling findings in polysubstance use research [13]. The attenuation of the multiple addictions–NSSI association in the fully adjusted model suggests that psychosocial stressors, particularly stressful life events and peer victimization, may partially account for this relationship, possibly as shared risk factors or as mediators. This observation warrants further investigation through longitudinal mediation designs.

The most instructive finding of this study lies in the juxtaposition of the categorical and continuous analyses. In categorical models, smartphone-only addiction was associated with elevated odds of all three outcomes. However, when the three addiction scales were mutually adjusted as continuous scores, a clear differentiation emerged: smartphone addiction severity retained an independent association only with internalizing symptoms, whereas gaming and social media addiction severities were independently associated with SBs and NSSI.

This divergence is not contradictory but rather illuminates the nature of the categorical associations. The "smartphone-only" category likely contains a substantial proportion of adolescents with subthreshold yet elevated gaming and social media use, which drives the categorical association with self-harm. Once these co-occurring behaviors are statistically isolated, the relationship between smartphone use and self-harm largely disappears, suggesting it was confounded rather than causal. This finding carries a critical clinical implication: the risk of self-harm linked to digital technology appears to be driven by specific online activities: notably gaming and social media engagement rather than by device use per se. This aligns with the growing consensus that screen time is an insufficient metric and that research and clinical assessments must account for the context and content of digital engagement [11, 30]. Possible mechanisms for the gaming- and social-media-specific pathways include exposure to online harassment and competitive frustration in gaming environments, and social comparison and self-harm content exposure on social media platforms [6, 9, 10, 12].

Among the specific patterns, triple addiction was associated with the most pronounced risk, especially for suicidal behaviors. This underscores the particularly detrimental nature of a generalized, cross-platform pattern of digital overuse, which may reflect pervasive deficits in behavioral regulation. Estimates for single-gaming and single-social-media subgroups were extremely unreliable due to very small sample sizes and should be interpreted only as preliminary observations [5].

Sleep disturbance was treated as a covariate in the present analyses, consistent with emerging evidence that it may function as a transdiagnostic mediator linking digital engagement to both internalizing and self-harm outcomes [31]. The substantial attenuation of effect sizes upon its entry into the model (alongside other health behaviors in Model 3) supports this conceptualization. Similarly, the further attenuation in the fully adjusted model (Model 4) after the inclusion of stressful life events and peer victimization suggests that these psychosocial adversities may act as shared antecedents or as pathway mediators [32]. Future longitudinal work employing formal mediation analyses is essential to disentangle these pathways.

This study benefits from a large, census-based sample of an underrepresented population, extensive covariate control, and a rigorous multi-pronged sensitivity analysis strategy. The finding that the literature-based, high-specificity cut-offs for gaming disorder (≥32) and social media addiction (≥24) yielded much lower sensitivity for detecting clinical outcomes highlights the distinction between screening and case-identification goals. The data-driven cut-offs derived from ROC analyses maximize the balance of sensitivity and specificity, and may be more appropriate for screening in clinical settings. Reassuringly, the core stepwise pattern was preserved across both classification approaches.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; the observed associations may be bidirectional, with pre-existing mental health difficulties also predisposing adolescents to problematic digital engagement [33]. Second, although our census-based sampling within Zigong City supports the representativeness of findings for vocational high school students in this region, generalizability to vocational students in other provinces or to general high school populations remains to be established. All prevalence estimates should be understood as specific to this population rather than as national estimates. Third, the sample sizes for certain addiction patterns (particularly gaming-only and social-media-only) were very small, limiting the precision of effect estimates for these subgroups. Fourth, the choice of cut-off thresholds inevitably involves a trade-off between sensitivity and specificity; our primary analyses used high-specificity literature-based cut-offs suitable for case identification, whereas the ROC-optimal cut-offs prioritized balanced sensitivity and specificity. As demonstrated in our sensitivity analyses, the core graded pattern was robust to this choice, but future research employing clinical interviews as the gold standard is needed to definitively validate optimal thresholds for this population. Fifth, the recall periods differed across the instruments used (one month for SABAS and PSQI; two weeks for PHQ-9 and GAD-7; 12 months for IGDS9-SF, BSMAS, and suicidality/NSSI items). This discrepancy limits the precision of temporal inferences. Sixth, all measures were self-reported and may be subject to recall and social desirability biases. Seventh, although we adjusted for a comprehensive set of covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured variables (e.g., personality traits, family psychiatric history, academic performance) cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

Among Chinese vocational high school students, the number of co-occurring digital addictions is associated with internalizing symptoms, SBs, and NSSI in a clear, stepwise fashion. Critically, the association of smartphone addiction with self-harm appears largely confounded by co-occurring gaming and social media problems, suggesting that prevention and intervention efforts should target specific online behaviors rather than screen time in its entirety. Longitudinal research is required to clarify the directionality of these associations and to evaluate the potential mediating role of sleep disturbance and psychosocial adversity.
