Circulating Tumor DNA Profiling Identifies Actionable Mutations as Prognostic Markers in Advanced Neuroendocrine Tumors
Authors: Na Zhou, Guofeng Zhao, Yang Gao, Jiazhang Xing, Mingying Wu, Hongyan Ying, Mei Guan, Huanwen Wu, Lin Zhao, Chunmei Bai, Yuejuan Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNeuroendocrine tumors (NETs) are a rare and heterogeneous group of neoplasms with both clinical and genetic diversity. The clinical applicability of molecular profiling using liquid biopsy for identifying actionable drug targets and prognostic indicators in patients with advanced NETs remains unclear.MethodsIn this study, we utilized a custom-made 37 genes panel of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) based on next-generation sequencing in 47 patients with advanced NETs.ResultsA total of 223 non-synonymous mutations were identified, with the highest frequencies found in TSC2, JAG2, NOTCH3, KMT2C, and SETD2. NETs originating from the stomach exhibited the highest average mutation frequency, while paragangliomas had the lowest. TERT mutations were associated with significantly higher hazard ratios for both progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS). Higher blood tumor mutational burden (bTMB) was linked to poor prognosis in octreotide LAR-treated patients and correlated with higher Ki-67 levels and alterations in the TM, mTOR, and Notch pathways. Treatment responders had higher bTMB, chromatin remodeling signaling mutation burden, and maximum somatic allele frequency (MSAF). Patients with high MSAF demonstrated better PFS, whereas those with low MSAF had better OS. A strong positive correlation was observed between MSAF levels and mutational burden. Co-occurring mutations in the TM, mTOR, and NOTCH pathways suggested future therapeutic strategies.ConclusionsWe constructed an overview of mutations in 37 genes, linking ctDNA to survival in advanced NETs. TERT mutations, bTMB, and MSAF predict PFS or OS. Co-occurring TM, mTOR, and NOTCH pathway alterations highlight ctDNA’s potential in guiding precision medicine.

Introduction

Most patients with advanced NETs do not benefit from genome-based precision medicine because of common challenges: (i) genotyping through tissue biopsy is difficult and sometimes unsuccessful due to relatively inaccessible tumor locations; (ii) the genetic landscape of advanced NETs is heterogeneous, with the frequency of putative actionable mutations less than 50% [1]; (iii) single-lesion biopsies usually miss intratumoral heterogeneity, leading to treatment failure; and (iv) tissue biopsies upon every progression are impractical, causing messages about tumor evolution and drug-resistant mutations to be largely neglected. Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis may overcome the barriers of tumor accessibility, heterogeneity, and sampling frequency [2]. High accuracy of ctDNA assays has led to their use in clinical practice for genotyping and guiding targeted therapy in advanced cancers, including lung, breast, and gastrointestinal cancers [2]. Moreover, using serial ctDNA assays to predict treatment response, monitor molecular progression, and detect emergent resistant mutations is of great interest and under extensive investigation in some cancers [2]. Although there is preliminary evidence to support the feasibility of ctDNA detection in neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) [3, 4], few studies have assessed its value as a diagnostic or prognostic biomarker in the management of advanced diseases.

Previous profiling has indicated that four principal pathways are frequently altered in pancreatic NETs (pNETs), including DNA damage repair, chromatin remodeling, telomere maintenance, as well as mTOR activation, and particularly mutations in genes such as TERT, PTEN, TSC2, TSC1, DEPDC5, etc., in these signalings are associated with a poor prognosis [5]. The tumorigenesis of NETs from other locations also implicated the alterations in some if not all of these pathways [6–10]. In addition, the Notch pathway, by modulating neuroendocrine differentiation, emerges as a central player in the development of NENs as well as a potential therapeutic target [11, 12]. Mutations of tumor suppressors such as TP53 and RB1, though seldom noticed in low-grade NETs, are enriched in neuroendocrine carcinomas, suggesting these genes may drive phenotype transformation toward higher-grade tumors during disease progression [6, 13, 14]. Detecting and monitoring these genes or pathways are of great prognostic and therapeutic value in clinical management of advanced NETs.

Recently, next-generation sequencing (NGS) has been approved as a novel method for ctDNA analysis. It has advantages in consolidating a wider range of genes and offering sensitivity needed to detect low-abundance variants [15]. However, commercially available targeted ctDNA assays based on NGS technology are almost entirely designed for identifying essential alterations in common solid tumors. Here, we developed a custom ctDNA assay with high sequencing depth by integrating 37 genes, which involve mutations and pathways that are commonly seen in NETs and are pharmacogenomic related.

Methods

Patient Cohort and Clinical Data Collection

From August 2017 to December 2018, 57 blood samples were collected from 47 patients with advanced NETs to detect tumor genomic alterations. Only patients with well-differentiated NETs were recruited, including gastroenteropancreatic NETs (GEP-NETs) G1–G3, thymic and pulmonary carcinoids, and paragangliomas. These patients were either treatment naive or at the point of progression from their last antitumor systemic therapies and needed adjustments in treatment. Clinical characteristics of the patients were collected from electronic medical records and a prospectively maintained institutional database (shown in online suppl. Table S1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000548256). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Peking Union Medical College Hospital, and all patients were provided with informed consent. The blood samples were sent to the Beijing Genomics Institute and were sequenced using targeted gene panel sequencing with an average of 109 Mb total reads and an average of 3,784 targeted sequencing depth across samples. The information of our sequencing data was in online supplementary Table S2.

Somatic Variant Detection

After removing terminal adaptor sequences and low-quality reads, the remaining reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner [16, 17]. PCR duplicated sequence reads were removed using Picard. Realignment was performed using the Genome Analysis Toolkit [18]. Somatic mutations were then detected using MuTect2 of Genome Analysis Toolkit and annotated using SnpEff software [19]. Finally, candidate variants were filtered against public databases including the 1000 Genomes Project [20], the Single Nucleotide Polymorphism Database [21], and the NHLBI exome sequencing project (ESP)6500 [17].

Blood Tumor Mutation Burden, Pathway Burden, and Pathway Score

Our panel design used blood tumor mutational burden (bTMB) to represent the total number of non-synonymous single-nucleotide variants, multi-nucleotide variants, and insertion-deletion mutations (indels) and pathway burden to represent the number of such mutations in each pathway. Pathway score, on the other hand, indicates the number of pathways with altered mutations (range: 0–6).

Survival Analysis

The overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) of the patients were analyzed. OS was defined as the time from the collection of blood samples to death from any cause, while PFS defined as the time from the collection of blood samples to disease progression or death from any cause.

We used the Survival R package to create survival curves and analyze differences in survival for all genes, bTMB, and MSAF with the log-rank test and used the SurvMiner R package to plot survival curves and determine the best cutpoint for bTMB and MSAF. For the cox proportional hazards regression analysis, we used coxph function in Survival R package to analyze genes that were significantly different in OS and PFS and finally used the ggforest function in SurvMiner R package to plot their forest figures. We considered p values <0.05 to be statistically significant.

Maftools Analysis for Somatic Mutations

To manage and visualize the panel data in MAF files, we used the maftools [22] package in the R environment. With maftools, we generated various visualizations, including MAF summaries, transition and transversion mutations, co-occurring or exclusive somatic interactions, drug-gene interactions, and lollipop plots for amino acid changes.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.0.2. Log-rank test was used to identify prognostic factors. Then, prognostic factors with a p value of <0.05 were further screened by multivariate Cox regression in survival package to identify independent prognostic factors. The Mann-Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) of ggpubr (version: 0.6.0) R package was used to calculate bTMB, pathway burden, and MSAF, whether there were differences in groups of Ki-67 and treatment response. Pearson correlation was used to evaluate MASF with bTMB, pathway score, and pathway burden. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the distribution of altered patient of seven pathways in groups of bTMB and MSAF. p value <0.05 was the threshold for significance.

Results

Mutational Landscape of NETs from Different Primaries

A comprehensive analysis focusing on the non-synonymous mutations of 37 genes (shown in online suppl. Table S3) in 47 patients was conducted. We finally found a total of 223 non-synonymous mutations (shown in online suppl. Table S4), with missense mutations accounting for 80.72% (n = 181) and SNPs accounting for 90.58% (n = 202). Among the single nucleotide variants, 74.75% of mutations were C to T transitions (n = 151) (shown in online suppl. Fig. S1A–C). We created a heat map diagram using the “ComplexHeatmap” package to display the non-synonymous alterations in 37 genes among 47 patients in detail. We found that the frequencies of non-synonymous mutations varied across NETs from different primary locations, with gNETs exhibiting the highest frequency (Freq = 0.176, bTMB = 7.00) and paragangliomas exhibiting the lowest frequency (Freq = 0.027, bTMB = 1). Besides, the mutation frequencies and bTMB were 0.125 and 5.92 in pNETs, 0.084 and 3.56 in enteric NETs, as well as 0.078 and 3.00 in lung and thymic carcinoids (shown in Fig. 1).

Non-synonymous mutation landscape of advanced NETs. The top panel shows seven types of mutations and SNV types. The bottom panel shows patient clinical information.

Our study also found that mTOR pathway displayed the lowest AMF of 6.9%, with TSC2 having the highest mutation frequency of 21%. No non-synonymous mutations of AKT1 were detected. The Notch pathway had the highest AMF among molecular pathways at 13%, with JAG2, NOTCH2, and NOTCH3 having the highest mutation frequencies of 21%, 21%, and 19%, respectively. In the cycle regulation (CR) pathway, KMT2C exhibited the highest mutation frequency of 23%, with the non-synonymous mutations in KMT2C and SETD2 solely identified in paragangliomas. In the DDR pathway, BRCA2 (17%) and BRCA1 (15%) were the most commonly mutated genes, predominantly in GEP-NETs, while low mutation frequencies were observed in CHEK2 (2%) and MUTYH (0%). In the TM pathway, ATRX and TERT had the highest mutation frequencies of 19%, while no mutations in DAXX were found, contradicting previous observations that the inactivating mutations of DAXX or ATRX were present in one-third of pNETs and were mutually exclusive [5, 23]. In the TSG pathway, mutations were mainly detected in GEP-NETs, with TP53 having the highest mutation frequency of 17%, and no mutations of VHL were detected.

Dynamic Mutation Patterns in Serial ctDNA-NGS Analysis

To evaluate the utility of serial ctDNA-NGS, patients were included if they had at least 2 serial tests between 30 days prior to the present therapies and therapy discontinuation. Specific pathogenic mutations were found repeatedly in individual patients (shown in online suppl. Fig. S1D; Table S5). For example, mutations in TSC2 (p. I496fs), MEN1 (p. PP71fs), and TP53 (p. P191fs) were identified across multiple time points in patient P-P05. Similarly, mutations in TSC2 (p.N1651S), ALK2 (p.R467W), MEN1 (p. I147del), KMT2C (p.S3660L), ATRX (p.Q2146E), and RB1 (p.R661Q) were consistently detected in patient P-P06.

Importantly, mutations with time-specific patterns were observed in these patients, indicating dynamic changes in their mutational profiles during treatment and disease progression. For instance, both patients P-P06 and P-P22 had missense mutations in MTOR, DLL4, and TERT before treatment, which disappeared after treatment when the disease status became stable. Additionally, patient-specific mutations were observed only before treatment, such as PIK3CA, DLL1, JAG2, ARID1A, BCOR, BARCA1, and TERT in P-P06 and DEPDC5, JAG1, NOTCH3, MEN1, and ATRX in P-P22.

Interestingly, some mutations emerged later in the treatment course. In P-P06, mutations in AKT2, KMT2C, and RB1 appeared, while in P-P22, mutations in BRCA1 and BRCA2 were observed. Additionally, patient P-P23 developed mutations in DLL1 and NOTCH1 later in the treatment process. These time-specific mutation patterns highlight the dynamic nature of tumor evolution and underscore the importance of serial ctDNA-NGS in capturing these changes. The co-occurrence of variants in the cell CR pathway and mTOR regulators echoes the phenomenon observed in a previous study, suggesting that these pathways may play a critical role in the adaptive mechanisms of tumors under therapeutic pressure [5].

Genomic Alterations in ctDNA as a Prognostic Biomarker

To assess the prognostic of ctDNA in advanced NETs, we stratified patients into “wildtype” and “mutated” according to the baseline ctDNA-NGS gene mutation status. In survival analysis, we found that the presence of BRCA2 and NOTCH3 mutations was significantly associated with longer PFS (shown in Fig. 2a, b), while tumor harboring NOTCH1 and TERT mutations were significantly associated with poorer PFS (shown in Fig. 2c, d). Meanwhile, no significant differences in OS were observed between “wildtype” and “mutated” genes across pathways (shown in Fig. 2e–h; online suppl. Table S6). To identify the independent prognostic biomarkers, we performed a multivariate regression analysis using these four genes, and TERT was found to have a significantly higher hazard ratio of “mutated/wildtype” on both PFS and OS (shown in Fig. 2i, j), indicating that it could be an independent prognostic biomarker for poor survival in patients with advanced NETs.

Role of Mutation Burden as a Prognostic Biomarker and Their Correlation with Ki-67 and Treatment Responses

To investigate the prognostic role of mutation burden in NETs, we divided patients into high and low subgroups based on the optimal threshold by using SurvMiner R package and found that only mutational burden of DDR pathway was significantly correlated with PFS (p = 0.04) and was not significantly correlated with OS of all patients (shown in Fig. 3a, b; online suppl. Fig. S2A–L). Of particular note, bTMB was also not significantly associated with PFS and OS in all patients (shown in online suppl. Fig. S2A, B). However, among patients who were then treated with octreotide long-acting release, those with higher bTMB had a worse prognosis (shown in Fig. 3c, p = 0.27; Fig. 3d, p = 0.049). We also compared the mutation burden of all patients in the treatment response group and the Ki-67 high-low group (grouped by 20%). Patients with a treatment status of PR had significantly higher bTMB and mutational burden of CR pathway levels in baseline ctDNA than those with PD/SD (shown in Fig. 3e, p = 0.024; Fig. 3f, p = 0.011) but no significant differences of mutation burdens between PD/SD and PR groups in the Notch, mTOR, TSG, DDR, and TM pathways (shown in online suppl. Fig. S3A–E). Besides, patients with Ki-67 high had significantly higher bTMB as well as mutational burdens of mTOR, Notch, and TM pathway than those with Ki-67 low (shown in Fig. 3g, p = 0.031; Fig. 3h, p = 0.018; Fig. 3i, p = 0.016; Fig. 3j, p = 0.031). However, there were no significant differences in mutational burden levels among CR, TSG, and DDR pathways (shown in online suppl. Fig. S3F–H). Specifically, we detected the PFS and OS outcomes in low and high groups of Ki-67, and the results show that the PFS and OS of low Ki-67 were better than that of high Ki-67 group (shown in online suppl. Fig. S3I, J). We then used Fisher’s exact test to compare the number of patients with or without genomic alterations of 6 pre-set pathways between high and low bTMB groups (shown in online suppl. Table S7). Significantly more patients with genomic alterations in CR, DDR, mTOR, and Notch pathways were observed in high bTMB group than low bTMB group (shown in Fig. 3k).

Role of MSAF as a Predictive Biomarker and Its Correlation with Mutational Burden, Ki-67, and Treatment Responses

Since the maximum somatic allele frequency (MSAF) can reflect the ctDNA amount in the blood [24], we explored the prognostic role of MSAF in patients with advanced NETs and found that patients with high MSAF in PFS had better PFS, while patients with low MSAF in OS had better OS, which indicated that MSAF can potentially be a prognosis biomarker in patients with advanced NETs (shown in Fig. 4a, b [PFS, p = 0.027] [OS, p = 0.027, but n = 2 for high MSAF]). Moreover, we observed a high positive correlation between the levels of MSAF and mutational burden (shown in Fig. 4c–j). In particular, bTMB (shown in Fig. 4c, r = 0.35, p = 0.02), pathway score (shown in Fig. 4d, r = 0.37, p = 0.012), and the mutational burden of CR pathway were significant correlated with MSAF. Notably, we found that MSAF was significantly higher in patients who achieved PR under current treatments than those who achieved SD/PD (shown in Fig. 4k, p = 0.023). These results implied that patients with higher MSAF were likely to be positively correlated with higher overall tumor burden and these could further guide clinicians to treat these patients with intensive regimens, which are more likely to produce objective responses. Indeed, out of 5 PR patients, 3 patients received doublet chemotherapies and 2 patients received surufatinib. Additionally, there was no significant differences of MSAF in the two groups of Ki-67 (shown in Fig. 4l, p = 0.74).

Comprehensive analysis of MSAF. a, b Survival analyses of MSAF (PFS) and MSAF (OS); patients were divided into MSAF-high and -low groups. c–j Correlation analysis between MSAF and bTMB, pathway score, mutation burdens of CR, TSG, mTOR, Notch, DDR, and TM pathway, respectively. k–l MSAF of patients with different treatment responses and Ki-67 levels. m Stacked histogram of the number of pathway mutations in patients divided into high and low MSAF groups according to the median value of MSAF.

We then compared the number of patients with or without genomic alterations of 6 pre-set pathways between high and low MSAF groups classifying based on the median value (shown in online suppl. Table S8). As expected, more patients with genomic alterations as a whole were observed in high MSAF group than in low MSAF group. However, less patients with TSG pathway alterations were found in high MSAF group (shown in Fig. 4m).

Mutual Exclusion and Co-Occurrence among Genes and Drug Target Mining

We then investigated whether genes were mutually exclusive or co-occurring by using maftools R package. TERT, BRCA2, NOTCH3, and NOTCH1, whose mutations were prognostic for PFS, all had co-occurring genetic alterations. TERT mutations were found to co-occur with mutations in mTOR, TSC2, DLL4, and JAG2 (shown in Fig. 5a; online suppl. Table S9), primarily in patients with pNETs (shown in online suppl. Fig. S4). A lollipop plot showed the distribution of mutations, with the majority being missense mutations (shown in Fig. 5b) and only one nonsense mutation in the TERT gene.

Gene correlation analysis and drug target mining. a Mutual exclusion and co-occurrence of genes. Green in the color scale represents co-occurrence and yellow represents mutual exclusion. b Mutant lollipop map of TERT. c Network of significant co-occurrence genes. d Histogram of gene-enriched druggable categories for significant co-occurrence genes.

A co-occurrence network, created using events with a p value <0.05, highlighted that genetic alterations in mTOR, NOTCH, and TM pathways frequently appeared together in ctDNA of NET patients (shown in Fig. 5c). This suggested these pathways collaborate in tumorigenesis and could have combinatorial therapeutic implications in the future. Five genes with top co-occurring frequencies also appeared in patients with pNETs. The dominant type of mutations in these five genes was missense mutation, with no hotspot mutations detected (online suppl. Fig. S5). Notably, 4 patients with gastrointestinal NETs harbored ATRX (p.Q929E) mutation. No mutually exclusive events were observed.

Finally, we conducted target-based screening for drug discovery focusing on significant co-occurring genetic alterations. We identified Clinically Actionable, Druggable Genome, Tumor Suppressor, DNA Repair, and Histone Modification as the top five enriched druggable categories (shown in Fig. 5d; online suppl. Table S9). Of particular interest was the importance of those pathways particularly essential to DNA integrity and their genetic interactions with other pathways, such as mTOR signaling. This may provide therapeutic tactics of synthetic lethality or combinations to indirectly target tumor suppressors such as ATRX and BRCA1/2 [25].

Discussion

Only a handful of studies have investigated the feasibility of ctDNA analysis in NENs before our study. A pilot study using droplet digital PCR technique offered the first evidence for the existence of tumor-specific mutations in plasma of metastatic pNETs [3]. Later, Zakka and colleagues detected molecular alterations of pNETs with a commercial plasma-based NGS panel [4]. However, the panel included genes whose alterations are rarely seen in well-differentiated NETs. Our panel is composed of critical genes from several signaling pathways including mTOR and NOTCH signaling, as well as DDR, chromatin remodeling, and telomere maintenance. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore custom ctDNA panel analysis for potential clinical utility in this rare disease.

In this study, we found that the frequency of gene mutations in the pathway is highly heterogeneous. The mutation frequencies of TSC2 in the mTOR pathway, JAG2 and NOTCH3 in the Notch pathway, and KMT2C and SETD2 in the CR pathway all exceeded 20% in advanced NETs. When comparing ctDNA portrays of certain genes with those of the NET cohort in Zakka’s study, we observed similar mutation frequencies with mTOR, BRCA1, and BRCA2 genes, but there were also some differences including a lower incidence of TP53 and PIK3CA mutations in our study, which was consistent with genomic profiling findings from tumor tissue samples [5]. By modulating cell growth and apoptosis, the mTOR pathway plays a predominant role in the tumorigenesis of NETs. Frequent alterations in TSC2 and other main components of mTOR signaling may be targetable by everolimus, an mTOR inhibitor already proved to be effective in treating advanced pNETs. However, evidence regarding the association between mTOR signaling alterations and response to everolimus in solid tumors is weak [26].

We have known that ctDNA could be used to estimate prognosis and treatment response in advanced solid tumors [27]. A high level of pretreatment ctDNA is presumably linked to worse survival. In our study, although bTMB could not predict PFS and OS outcomes in all patients, there were significant differences in the level of bTMB among patients with octreotide-long-acting release treatment. This result showed that if we exclude confounding factors as much as possible, bTMB can also be a marker of prediction factor in PFS and OS. Due to the highly significant correlation between MSAF and bTMB (R = 0.35, p = 0.02), we explored whether MSAF could be a predictor. OS survival with high MSAF is significantly worse, which is consistent with OS survival with high bTMB being worse.

In a pilot study, mutations of particular genes detected in plasma were associated with shorter PFS under certain chemotherapies in neuroendocrine carcinomas [28]. We found mutated TERT significantly linked to shorter PFS and OS in advanced NETs. The telomerase activation induced by TERT promoter mutations and alternative lengthening of telomere related to ATRX mutations permit cell immortality, thereby facilitating invasion and metastases [29, 30], and were regarded as risk factors of poor prognosis in NETs [5, 31]. A ctDNA TERT mutation was also reported to be an independent predictor of poor survival in unresectable hepatocellular treated with atezolizumab and bevacizumab combinations [32]. Further validation of TERT ctDNA mutations in prognosis prediction is needed using larger prospective cohorts.

Our study of innovative identification of co-occurring altered genes and pathways in NETs not only emphasizes their cooperative tumor promoting characters but may also lead to develop combination therapies that increase the efficacy of anti-tumor treatment and limit drug resistance. Such strategy has been brought to attention recently. For instance, mTOR inhibitors are capable of suppressing homologous recombination repair in BRCA-proficient triple-negative breast cancer and synergizing with PARP inhibitor [33]. In addition, prevalence of genetic alterations in pathways implicated in DNA replication and repair processes including CR, TM, DDR signaling provides synthetic lethal vulnerabilities [25] and holds promise for novel treatment designing in NETs. For instance, the combination of PARP inhibitor with cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 (CDK4/6) inhibitor or 177Lu-DOTA-octreotate radionuclide were demonstrated resulting in synergistic anti-tumor efficacy in several preclinical NET models [34, 35].

Overall, our custom panel only recruited a limited number of clinically valuable gene targets based on commonly altered pathways shared by NETs from different locations. It may miss out actionable targets beyond these pathways or mutations of other genes within these pathways but infrequently mutated. For example, a certain frequency of alterations of KRAS, EGFR, BRAF, MYC, and CCNE1 were found in blood-based sequencing in patients with pNETs. Nevertheless, the panel allowed us to focus on those genes mostly pertinent to the tumorigenesis of NETs and might be a rapid and cost-effective molecular analytic tool for actionable biomarkers as well as predictors for patient outcomes. Meanwhile, measurement of certain molecular alterations such as fusion variants, copy number variations, and epigenetic changes using ctDNA remains difficult. These alterations could also be targeted and capable of predicting clinical outcome. Finally, the analytical process is based on bioinformatics analysis and needs to be validated in clinical trials with large sample sizes.

In conclusion, we constructed an overview mutation map in 37 genes and identified high-frequency mutation genes in advanced NETs. We established a connection between ctDNA and survival data in this rare disease and found that TERT mutations, bTMB, and MSAF can serve as markers to predict PFS or OS. Particularly, we observed co-occurring altered genes in pathways involving TM, mTOR, and NOTCH signalings and identified druggable categories according to these genetic alterations, which highlights the potential of pretreatment ctDNA genotyping in guiding precision medicine.
