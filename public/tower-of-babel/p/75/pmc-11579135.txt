“This Is What You Get When You Lead with the Arts”: Making the Case for Social Wellness
Authors: Andrea Charise, Nicole Dufoe, Dirk J. Rodricks
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Like other key terms in the medical and health humanities—empathy, creativity, and reflection, to name just a few—wellness has become a weasel word, rife the language of optimization, duty, and self-perception. While alternative vocabularies exist—well-being and quality of life among them—these options usually privilege the objectives of academic (often psychological) research, health institutions, and the economic state apparatus, rather than people themselves. In mind of these concerns, why attempt to make a case for wellness at all? We present a historically informed, theoretically driven, praxis-guided framework for a renewed vision of social wellness (a concept first defined in the late 1950s). While definitions since Bill Hettler’s “hexagonal” model (1980) have included mutual respect for others and the assumption of cooperative behaviors, conspicuously absent from contemporary definitions and usage is any mention of the aesthetic realm, which we—alongside philosophers like Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum—take as a central human capability. How can the relational possibilities of arts engagement be understood as not just a means of promoting individual wellness, but also as a method and outcome of social wellness? We propose that social wellness is ultimately premised on the interplay between wellness of the collective and the strength of the relational encounters it engenders. We turn to a key practice paradigm—community arts engagement—as both a vehicle for and site of social wellness. With brief reference to a Canadian exemplar, we conclude with concrete recommendations for addressing critical opportunities for advancing arts-led social wellness initiatives involving academic and community partners.

Despite widespread critique by academic and cultural critics alike, wellness—as a concept, practice, state of being, or generalized goal—does not seem to be going anywhere. A broad capture of data, tracking the word’s use across published books in English, shows a steady increase since the mid-twentieth century (Google Books Ngram 2023). At the same time, a backlash has been brewing, as “[r]ecent scholarly and public commentaries on wellness products and services offer entertaining exposés of the excesses of wellness culture and provide important insight into the potential problems of its global ubiquity” (Derkatch 2022, 3). The academic distaste for wellness often focuses on the commercialization of the term—its seemingly limitless commodity market of supplements, tinctures, and often orientalized tools and gadgetry—as well as its neoliberal policies of self governance, characterized equally by spending (be it money on organic, “clean” foods or time on mindfulness apps) and withholding (detoxifying diets and restrictive practices), most often an amalgam of the two. Medical rhetorician Colleen Derkatch describes the “self-generating” nature of wellness, which becomes a perpetually “moving target” (2022, 5): one can never achieve the ever-changing endpoint of complete wellness, so there is always more wellness to buy and to sell. From the whiteness of wellness (Khúc, 2021) to its function as the highly commodified polish of aspirational lifestyles (Conor 2021), one may ask if there remains any utility in critically minded people salvaging the term “wellness” at all.

Like other key terms in the medical and health humanities—empathy, creativity, and reflection, to name just a few (Bleakley 2015; Charise and Krecsy 2022; Garden 2009)—wellness often serves as evasive, euphemistic jargon unwilling to acknowledge its ideological inheritances. Yet this also suggests an opportunity for renewed engagement with the term. Troubling language is a perennial source of debate in this field: even the competing taxonomy of “medical humanities” versus “health humanities” suggests a key pattern of semantic critique in the broader field of arts- and humanities-based engagements with health (Crawford et al. 2010; Crawford, 2020). In fact, what current critiques of wellness share with critical views of medical/health humanities more broadly is a focus, fixation perhaps, on the individual. While individual health and wellbeing do of course matter, this focus occludes a sense of social responsibility or relationality beyond the clinical (or commercial) encounter (Charise and Krecsy 2022). Perhaps we might even admit how subversive approaches to wellness—practices that downplay or even counteract its largely consumerist connotations in broader western culture, as we will discuss here—may be keys to nurturing decent human qualities like agency, autonomy, and self-regulation. Together, these issues signal a vital need for further consideration of what it means to be healthy or sick—well or unwell—and why renewed definitions of these terms, including wellness, must extend far beyond the individual: perhaps even taking community and the social as their starting points.

This paper asks how we might de-emphasize the individual as unit of analysis by reorienting toward the connective tissue implied by a revitalized definition of social wellness. Rather than ask what the social can do to better individual health (a fact that, of course, remains essential to enhancing health and social care systems around the globe [Fancourt 2017]), here we advocate for a revision of this dynamic by foregrounding the relational emphases of the collective, specifically through an engagement with the arts. Conspicuously absent from contemporary wellness discourse and definitions of wellbeing is any mention of the aesthetic realm, which we—alongside philosophers like Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum—take as a central human capability. An early-pandemic editorial in the Globe and Mail makes the case succinctly: “From the microscopic to the macro-economic, the pandemic is chewing its way through the arts. Yet, trapped inside our houses, it is to the arts that we turn . . . [i]t is how we find escape but also how we seek meaning” (Taylor 2020). As we continue to make meaning of life beyond the pandemic conditions of COVID-19, we ask: how can arts engagement be understood as not just a means of promoting individual wellness, but also as a method and an outcome of social wellness? What could an arts-infused reframing of social determinants of health look like, both in theory and in practice? Understanding arts engagement as a fundamental pillar of social wellness occasions a conceptual framework distinct from predominant, economically driven, productivity-centered models of wellness and of wellbeing. Building on the existing successes of one Canadian initiative as a case study for such possibility, we turn to a key practice paradigm—arts engagement in (micro) community—as both a vehicle for and site of social wellness.

Defining the limits of wellness and wellbeing

We began by acknowledging that wellness—as a culture, an industry, or simply a state of being—has become, at best, devoid of meaning, and at worst a convenient vehicle for expanding capitalist consumption. Health and sociolegal scholar Anna Kirkland describes the hegemony of wellness in various influential domains of Western culture, tracing the contemporary Western origins of wellness to the 1950s (an etymological lineage supported by the OED [2023]). Kirkland reminds readers that “the roots of the concept extend far back” beyond this date and are embedded “into the history of American ideas about health, morality, and responsibility” (Kirkland 2014b, 959): values which, in 2024, have been thoroughly entangled within a globalized mash of commodified and often appropriated cultural practices.

Echoing the assorted dimensions of “health,” broadly defined, wellness likewise encompasses a diverse range of human activities, including physical, spiritual, and environmental facets (Derkatch 2022; Ciziceno 2022). Scholarly and popular definitions tend to include recurring descriptors. The concept of optimization, for example, becomes a motif, “emphasizing function over dysfunction, agency over passivity”—even if it is not always clear what exactly is being optimized or acted upon (Derkatch 2022, 3). There is also a frequent focus on attitude and self-perception, as much as on action: “wellness is viewed as a dynamic process through which peopl[e are] aware and assume positive health attitudes in the physical, social, intellectual, and spiritual dimensions of their life” (Ciziceno 2022; Adams et al. 1997). Derkatch connects wellness to “larger shifts in cultural understandings of bodies and health over the last several decades that download responsibility for health from the state and onto individuals” (2022, 16–17). Both in onus and in outcome, then, wellness undoubtedly privileges the individual.

With its focus on the individual, this uni-directionality of wellness may potentially empower personal growth, agency, and even betterment, but ultimately it negates communal bonds and relational entanglements. It may be the individual’s duty to strive toward and display markers of wellness, but that is not to say that it is that individual—or individuals—who reaps the benefits, or the profits, of wellness regimens.1 In mind of these concerns, why attempt to rehabilitate wellness at all—to say nothing of adding to this already flawed and unstable taxonomy the coinage social wellness? There are, after all, other choices. Terms like wellness, well-being, and quality of life are often clumped together or used interchangeably (Ciziceno 2022, 12). As many have noted, the idea that health is more than just the absence of disease draws directly from the World Health Organization’s 1948 constitution (Fancourt 2017, 23). Seventy-five years later, the WHO remains “firmly committed” to promoting health as “a state of complete physical, mental and social wellbeing and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity” (WHO 2024). Not unlike the concept of wellness, however, a functional definition of wellbeing remains elusive, as it is “often split into different dimensions such as hedonic wellbeing, which includes happiness and positive affect; eudaimonic wellbeing, which includes feeling a sense of purpose and meaning in life; and evaluative wellbeing, which includes general satisfaction with life” (Fancourt 2017, 32). Like wellness, wellbeing is an intrinsically prismatic term, comprising physical, emotional, spiritual, financial, vocational, environmental, and intellectual dimensions (Miller 2005).

While the general-sense language of wellbeing remains amorphous, an analysis of its applications suggests that wellbeing lends itself more readily to institutional guidelines and outcomes than does wellness. For example, the US-based Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) defines wellbeing as a “relative state where one maximizes his or her physical, mental, and social functioning in the context of supportive environments to live a full, satisfying, and productive life” (Kobau et al. 2010). Organizations including the CDC and WHO propose the use of various existing scales of measurement, often based on self-assessments of “subjective” wellbeing (see, for example, the WHO-5 Well-Being Index, The Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scales [WEMWBS], Porter Novelli Healthstyles Survey, and others). Yet, in the context of wellbeing research, any such community appears to remain defined by quantifiable health outcomes, contributions to gross domestic product (GDP), or biomarkers (epigenetics, or the hypothesis that behavior impacts health at the genetic level)—all inextricably tethered to ideas of growth, progress, and productivity.2

Given these emphases, it is perhaps inevitable that the arts and arts engagement are largely absent from conventional wellbeing definitions and assessment scales. Like wellness’s focus on optimization, the ethos of productivity often stands out in such institutional endorsements of wellbeing. Productivity—whether at the level of the individual, the corporation, or the nation—conjures practices of tracking and measurement. Of course, such scales can and do offer meaningful findings, particularly by acknowledging and attempting to capture the often intangible benefits of positive social relationships. However, their assessments of subjective, psychological, or mental wellbeing still privilege individual health outcomes that are tied in some way to corporate or national interests. Moreover, beyond their telling focus on “psychological” or “mental” wellbeing, measurements of subjective wellbeing pay little attention to factors of gender or cultural difference (Lindert et al. 2015; VanderWeele 2017).3 As a result of these combining factors, current conceptualizations of wellbeing and related scales seem more relevant to the objectives and mandates of academic research (psychology especially), health institutions, and the economic state apparatus, than to people themselves.

Despite gestures to the social realm implied in such working definitions of wellbeing, this is not quite the collective we hope to describe, and to foster, through a renewed definition of social wellness. We are after something different, something that nurtures and reflects a collectivity that is both a reality and an ideal. “Health,” writes Julia Zielke, “is thus not ‘located’ in any single body that is cordoned off from their wider systems . . . it traverses through multiple complex interdependent systems in a non-linear way” (Zielke 2022, 265). We share the sense that there is something deeply relational, entangling, and rhizomatic in the reality of not only human health but what we are envisioning as social wellness. Central to our emerging use of the term social wellness, and conspicuously absent from contemporary wellness discourse and the definitions of wellbeing described above, is any mention of the aesthetic realm, which we—alongside philosophers like Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum—take as a central human capability. A capabilities approach emphasizes what a species (human or otherwise) is able to do—i.e., is capable of—in both a more inclusive and expansive way than standard, deficit-driven definitions of wellbeing. Nussbaum’s articulation of ten central human capabilities (2006, 2011) that should be supported in all democratic contexts has compelling applicability to our discussion. Of the ten central capabilities, namely, life; bodily health; bodily integrity; senses, imagination and thought; emotions; practical reason; affiliation; other species; play; and control over one’s environment, senses, imagination and thought (the capacity to develop sense-attention, imagination, and creative thought as a generative, valuable, and often pleasurable activity) has been recently invoked in the context of health-related educational and practice settings (Costa et al. 2020).

As currently theorized, neither wellness nor wellbeing makes sufficient space for the contributions and practices of arts engagement. We take this opportunity to introduce the aesthetic realm as a key, as-yet undeveloped, dimension of wellness and wellbeing, which in turn invites an arts- and humanities-led reframing of both theory and praxis relevant to critical health studies. Understanding arts engagement as a fundamental pillar of social wellness provides a conceptual framework distinct from dominant economically driven, productivity-centered models of wellness and of wellbeing. Based on our analysis of the etymology, historical employment, and theoretical background we assemble here, it is clear: arts engagement is a community-driven summons to center aesthetic sensibility as social wellness.

Situating social wellness: Making space for arts in community

We are not the first to argue for, or attempt to rehabilitate, a renewed place for social wellness. Gamby et al. (2021) argue that wellness connotes a meaning much older, and more global, than the definitions and criticism above imply. Among his original six dimensions of wellness—intellectual, physical, emotional, social, spiritual, and occupational—Bill Hettler (1980), the founder of the American-based National Wellness Institute, specifically defines social wellness in terms of accountability toward the external environment. Hettler’s multi-dimensional approach introduced nuance to the earlier High-Level Wellness model, defined by Halbert Dunn as “an integrated method of functioning which is oriented toward maximizing the potential of which the individual is capable” (1959). Hettler’s “hexagonal” model defined the social dimension as “the degree to which one contributes to the common welfare of one’s community. This emphasizes the interdependence with others and with nature” (Hettler 1980, 78). While a comprehensive overview of wellness models after Hettler is beyond the scope of this article (see instead Amaya et al. 2019; Brubaker and Sweeney 2022; Oliver et al. 2018), social wellness is consistently included as a key domain of contemporary wellness models, including The National Institutes of Health’s “Social Wellness Toolkit” (Adams et al. 1997; Renger et al. 2000; Moore and Leafgren 1990; Crose et al. 1992; Durlak 2000).

Social wellness has been summarized thusly: “the quality and extent of interaction with others and the interdependence between the individual, others, the community, and nature. Furthermore, social wellness comprises the skills and comfort level one is able to express in the context of interpersonal interactions, as well as the motivation, action, intent, and perception of those interactions. Social wellness also includes the extent to which one gives support and how one receives support and the respect of others” (Roscoe 2009, 218). In other words, definitions of social wellness have long included mutual respect for others and the assumption of cooperative behaviors. What we propose now is the amplification of a key premise: social wellness is ultimately dependent upon the interplay between wellness of the collective and the strength of the relational encounters contained therein. However, the role of arts engagement in facilitating these very phenomena is rarely if ever considered.

Revisiting the concept of social wellness today—in a profoundly altered global context of multiple pandemics, COVID-19 but one among them—necessitates a revived and renewed approach. We propose that social wellness and, more specifically, a capabilities-led vision of arts engagement as a method for social wellness, as a framework and vocabulary. To develop these theoretical foundations of social wellness, we turn to a longstanding aspect of ethical thought: technē, a philosophical concept that refers to making, doing, and the process of developing craft-like knowledge.4 Commonly associated with Aristotelian ethical thought, technē is distinguished from theoretical knowledge because of its rootedness in material application: what is learned by experiential practice rather than general explanation (the latter is contrasted as episteme). Technē, translated as craft or art, is not quite captured by a “means” versus “ends” distinction; instead, technē defines outcomes as both an activity and the product of that activity. For example, as Richard Parry notes, in Plato’s Dialogues (Euthydemus and Charmides), health is cited as an example of an end that is produced from the technē of medicine: “if medicine is an epistêmê which studies health, it is also a technē which produces health” (Parry 2020). In a similar manner, Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics asserts that it is the values-driven praxis of technē, the sustained engagement in the development of this craft-like knowledge, that constitutes the process of weaving as well as the fabric outcome. The two, in fact, cannot be disentangled.

In taking this position, we do not intend to elevate classical values within our theorization of arts engagement as a method for social wellness (and our current work within the space). But the concept of technē allows us to traverse the ideological contours of wellness and wellbeing—including their persistent emphasis on optimization, deficit-driven striving, and the material realization of individual health outcomes—and the forms of praxis that, we argue, distinguish arts engagement as a method of social wellness. The making, doing, and development of craft-like knowledge thus reframe social wellness as a practice more consistent with the values and activist potential of the arts and humanities. We admit that, by talking about values, we are verging on something that smacks of the unfashionable, and even the oppressive. But our renewed call for social wellness is, undeniably, a promotion of values: an incantation of common affinities that exist beyond the bonds of monetary exchange. The praxis—the technē—that comprises social wellness thrives in the creating and experiencing of art-making together: a practical expression of more contemporary, humanities-centric values of interdependence, relationality, and reciprocity.

This theoretical background is reiterated in the emergent findings of contemporary research, particularly connections between arts engagement and belonging that have been laid bare during the ongoing conditions of the COVID-19 pandemic. Early Canadian research indicates that arts participation is linked with enhanced community belonging; at the same time, precarity remains the norm for many artists and community partners in the arts, a sector whose survival is increasingly at risk (Hill Strategies 2010, 2019). In place of monadic visions of individual optimization, the arts become a means of engaging as both process and outcome. This is what community arts advocates have long known: arts engagement is asset-driven rather than deficit focused, and creative capabilities are directed toward work that de-escalates the primacy of progress, outputs, or even accomplishment. For these reasons, a comprehensive vision of social wellness requires arts engagement as both a means and an end.

The model of arts engagement we present here is participatory, collective, and accountable. Engagement in the arts is an active means of giving form to ineffable moments of relationality, community, and thus social wellness. It is this focus on the ensemble, the collective engagement with arts, that distinguishes our emphasis from apparently cognate movements (such as art therapy, which is primarily an individual prescription within a clinical paradigm of health). This is not an act of invention, or even innovation: there are many sites where arts-led social wellness already exists and thrives. As will be demonstrated in our discussion below, this has—for various reasons—historically been the case in Black, Indigenous, and other communities of color in the Canadian context and beyond. Access to arts engagement therefore marks a fundamentally distinct vision of what culturally sustaining social wellness involves, not as (or simply as) a solution to overlapping pandemics—epidemiological, racial and Indigenous injustice, socioeconomic hardship, to name only a few. Social wellness, we propose, is both an outcome of and a process for mobilizing sense, imagination, and thought, catalyzed by participation in the aesthetic.

A window into social wellness in practice: “We keep us safe, right?”

The window as metaphor offers one way to illustrate our arts-led vision of social wellness in practice. A view from a window offers a temporal standpoint: what is seen, who is doing the seeing, and when? But also, as importantly, it allows for a recognition of conditions that impact one’s ability to see clearly. Typically, a view from a window is a quick brief assessment of conditions and impacts. We offer one such window into social wellness—a brief summary because it is taken up in greater detail elsewhere—based on an initiative that emerged through the work of the FLOURISH Collective (2023) at the University of Toronto Scarborough. Established in 2020, FLOURISH: Community-Engaged Arts as a Method for Social Wellness was a designated Cluster of Scholarly Prominence, now an externally funded research initiative, that brought together researchers, community partners, artists, and student-trainees. Weaving together the radically interdisciplinary (but often institutionally siloed) expertise of arts practitioners, community partners, policy makers, health professionals, and implementation scientists, the Collective examined through micro and macro interventions how community-based arts practices may foster social wellness. Guided by community-engaged, participatory action research methodologies, research and research-creation initiatives emphasized local definitions of wellness, and scaffolded practices of arts-engagement already underway in the historically underserved community of Scarborough, Ontario, Canada (Charise 2017).

One of the key anchoring projects of the FLOURISH program was a commitment to emerging and early career scholars in the form of a Learning Academy, which brought together advanced undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral students working alongside faculty members on FLOURISH-funded projects to engage in a cohort-like experience that centered story, community, and creative practice. The cohort of trainees would meet monthly, and the curriculum would feature guest speakers who would share their journeys with arts, community, and wellness; offer both conceptual and methodological provocations; and invite the academy members into creative practice. From these early constructions of the Learning Academy emerged an arts-based community practice where members of the research lab, all of whom were multiply minoritized, engaged in four 3-hour workshops over 5 months with creative practice as an anchoring device. Each member of the research team would facilitate one workshop that drew on their interests, familiarity with, and passions for art and artmaking. Workshops included “found poetry,” “collaging,” “oral history performance,” and “digital artmaking,” where arts as metho-pedagogy moved away from product and instead prioritized relational process; in their doing, it eschewed any adherence to a rhetoric of effects and rather sought to advance a rhetoric of cultural production (see Gaztambide-Fernández 2013). The workshops occurred in-person and online (based on the facilitator’s workshop design) and participants worked together in the same space for the duration of the workshop, sharing creative work and responses with each other, exploring ways in which the work both resonated and diverged from each other, and drawing connections to quotidian experiences: past, present, and future imaginings. This kind of pedagogical project took place alongside multiple research project meetings: the business of academia, if you will. In doing so, the Learning Academy emerged as a critical space for reflection and connection that facilitated agency despite the usual trappings of academia.

As one of the doctoral students involved in the workshops offered:I had a tough year academically, and so, for me, I left feeling a lot more supported. I felt it was a space where it was a space to vent, but also feeling a lot more empowered and emboldened that my trajectory in this [academic] program is mine: it’s my choice to make if I continue, if I pause, or power through or whatever . . . I think that up until then, I was really feeling flattened and I didn’t know if I could make it through. And now, it’s more so that I am thinking: do I want to do this because I want to do this? It’s less about the effect that the university has had, and more about looking internally at what I want for myself.

Another participant echoed James Thompson’s (2015) idea of participatory artistic practice as a form of care: practice itself as imbued with a caring dimension. He had built up for himself “multiple informal communities of care” with creative and relational work as an orientation to doing community, honoring those who opened doors for him by creating opportunities for others. In essence, the workshops became a “caring with.” Joan Tronto (2013, 38) offers “caring with” as a necessary personal and political practice for social justice and involves an ongoing recognition of power and vulnerability in the caring relationship and the context in which that relationship occurs. For this participant, and shared by others as well, the curated workshop space offered a micro-community, mobilized arts practices, and allowed for a normalizing of individual and collective wayfinding for emerging and early career scholars: a cultivation of a critical form of community cultural wealth (Yosso 2005) often lacking for minoritized bodies in academia. As another doctoral student connected care to their own sense of wellness, they offered the following:As someone that used to define themselves as being in wellness and really not finding healing there, I found our work really refreshing. In those wellness spaces, I was always seeing that you had to perform wellness, which is strange ‘cause people go to these places to get well or to facilitate wellness. And so, just to in these workshop spaces where we were not having to perform wellness, but it was naturally facilitated – going through it and navigating it together. I see it as akin to that slogan they use around abolition work, which is “we keep us safe, right?” We will be the ones to actually support one another to the finish line, on our terms, on our timeline.

Drawing on Sylvia Wynter’s notion of “being human as praxis” (McKittrick 2015), we see the technē of social wellness as a fundamentally creative force that converges the embodied past, present, and future with notions of border-crossing, care, and collectivity: where the collective creation represents, as Saidiya Hartman suggests, “a refusal of the imposition of a certain kind of sense-making project” (2020). For these participants, this refusal-in-relation exists alongside the very reason they exist within the institution. When we consider the whiteness of wellness (Khúc, 2021) but also the whiteness of higher education (Cabrera et al. 2017), this curated micro-community functions in as a borderland between formal sites of learning: the classroom and their research experiences. This nonformal site of learning, anchored by arts-based practice, is especially critical to navigating survival in and through academia as multiply minoritized bodies. The creative doing-in-relation echoes the words of Chicana scholar Cindy Cruz who writes: “The body prompts memory and language, builds community and coalition. The body is a pedagogical devise, a location of recentering and recontextualizing the self and the stories that emanate from that self” (2001, 668). In this window into social wellness, participants found that their creative work became a critical release valve to bring those experiential learnings as well as classroom learning into conversation with lived and felt experience. In other words, trainees experienced, first-hand, arts-led social wellness as technē: social wellness realized in large part by its attempted practice.

Creating consistent counterspaces within and between systems that are often experienced as hostile by minoritized bodies allows us to take seriously Khúc’s (2021) offering of normalizing our experiences of chronic unwellness. In our arts-led vision of social wellness, we have been positing arts-engagement as a liberatory praxis in ways big and, especially, small (as the aforementioned window illustrates) thorough craft and art-making, supportive of social connection, healing, and community building. In some ways, this is consistent with what Daisy Fancourt describes as the impact of the arts on “social behavior,” citing evidence of the arts’ capacity to mitigate conflict within and between groups (Fancourt 2017, 40). Just as we foregrounded the critique of wellness and wellbeing as ingrained with, and further ingraining, capitalist ideologies, so might we pause to consider how arts engagement as a method of social wellness might be confused with encouraging or enacting social or even political quietism; the stakes could not be higher. It is imperative to understand that the arts, and the artist, can also disrupt, aggravate, and trouble the status quo: in fact, our vision of arts engagement is that which can tease out sites of congestion, injustice, and stagnancy. As our brief case study above suggests, arts engagement as a method for social wellness must also be invested in the work of exposing conditions that diminish social wellness, realizing the paradoxical kind of belonging that is found in pulling at the threads of social life as much as in weaving them together.

Social wellness: Reframing social determinants through the arts

As of 2024, growing evidence supports arts engagement as a preventive health measure that expands conventional (that is, clinically defined) health outcomes to include enhanced social relationality, fellowship, and community-centered knowledge (Putnam 2000; Sinner and Conrad 2015; Clift and Camic 2016; Waytz 2013). One might also look to the rise of social prescription in multiple national contexts—often including arts practices—as an emergent, but material, example of social wellness as health and social policy. Movements toward “creative health” in the UK context, led by initiatives including The National Centre for Creative Health, the All-Party Parliamentary Group for Arts, Health and Wellbeing’s “Creative Health Inquiry Report” (2017), and the Arts Council of England’s “Creative Health & Wellbeing” strategic vision, have formalized co-production and collaboration as approaches developing culturally specific activities aimed at dismantling inequalities in health and, equally, in arts and culture.

Access to arts engagement therefore marks a fundamentally distinct vision of what culturally sustaining social wellness involves—a rootedness in the lifeways of communities who have been, and often continue to be, damaged and erased by health, social, and, importantly, educational institutions. Beyond what may be quantifiable social returns on investment associated with arts engagement (Fancourt et al. 2020; Whelan 2016), what we are proposing is not just a recognition of collectivity or community in the practice of human flourishing, but solidarity (incidentally, a virtue described in Stoic philosophy as the object of life itself). Such research auditions the possibility of an arts-led reframing of social determinants of health toward community-strengthening practices that underscore the values—and value—of arts and humanities-centered work. In this model, equity, access, belonging, intergenerativity, and justice, particularly in underserved and historically marginalized community settings, become not only considerations, but constituents.

Through this work, we see an opportunity to reframe how academic research might better engage in arts-led social wellness. Community-based scholarship has long critiqued the power imbalances, divergent priorities, and (on the academic side) tendencies toward extractivism and transactional research practices in university-community partnerships. Collaboration in the arts-wellness-community space must acknowledge and actively grapple with these tendencies, taking guidance from methodologies long-championed by participatory action research; in fact, the arts-led vision of social wellness we describe here presents an obvious opportunity to enact these principles. Beginning with the premise that individual and collective wellness depends on factors outside of the hospital walls and beyond biomedical worldviews, our arts-led methods for exploring and assessing social wellness are thoroughly community-engaged, rather than conventionally university-based. To share an anecdote: throughout our extensive interactions with a variety of community partners, the value of arts engagement as a method for social wellness has been taken as entirely self-evident, and required no explanation or theorization.

The fundamental challenge for academia, in our view, is to catch up with a latent community understanding of arts engagement as a method for social wellness. What, then, is the necessary role of academia in this space, if community partners are already working with, and often advancing, the tenet that arts engagement is a wellness-supporting practice? Our arts-led vision of social wellness seems especially suited to confront this fundamental imbalance. The first step requires academic researchers to acknowledge that work in this area is already occurring, and indeed thriving, far outside of institutional parameters. Accordingly, by diminishing the presumption of expertise as the contribution of academics in arts-wellness-community work, we make space for not only authentic collaboration and engagement, but for a critical interruption of the anti-Black and anti-Indigenous discourse that has long-characterized conventional university research (Tuhiwai Smith 1999). In turn, by unseating expertise as the primary motivation for academic engagement in arts-wellness-community work, we can begin to imagine the university as part of—indeed in service to—the wider community, rather than positioning the community as a resource for academic research. A vital tenet of our theoretical and practical work in arts-led social wellness, then, is to support what already occurs in practice and in community.

In other words, we see our role as university researchers as helping scaffold what is already within community and known by community, rather than colonizing existent knowledges of arts-engagement as social wellness for academic discourse.

In conclusion: most published research on wellness (social or otherwise) has, as we review above, been an academic enterprise. By contrast, our revised approach involves offering resources to community partners, rather than (re)establishing the university as a locus for this work. Our renewal of arts engagement as social wellness also works to bolster the connective tissues that have historically bound communities, and to foster mutual exchange between such communities and the academy. Advancing arts-engaged social wellness asks practitioners to develop outcomes that are meaningful, legible, and easily communicated beyond academic audiences, reorienting this work in service to the communities with which it purports to engage. We see our role as integrating craft-like knowledge, both as method and finding, into the university. Understanding such scaffolding work as service, enabled by the resources of academic institutions, presents a very different model of research and innovation—social innovation, even—than is typical to the academic and market-driven contexts. Together, these reoriented tactics add up to far more than merely “collaboration” with community organizations engaged in arts-wellness work, with participants that value those programs, and with the artist facilitators that make these programs possible. What we propose is an ethic and methodology of solidarity in the arts-wellness-community space.5 This definition, and resulting methodology, situates the artist and researcher (indeed, the artist-researcher) as not only a facilitator or promotor of social wellness, but also a figure of its existence, and successes.
