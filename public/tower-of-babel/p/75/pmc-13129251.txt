How Dietary Restrictions Can Lead to Being Caught in a Negative Spiral: A Qualitative Study of Patients' Experiences After Orthognathic Surgery
Authors: Eva Dorby Vest Hansen, Line Lundell Andreasen, Ulrik Sundberg Simonsen, Charlotte Nielsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThe purpose of this qualitative study was to explore patients' experiences following orthognathic surgery with a focus on dietary management.MethodsTo gain in‐depth knowledge of patients' postoperative dietary management, a phenomenological−hermeneutic approach was applied, in which data were collected through individual semi‐structured telephone interviews. Eleven patients (seven men, four women, aged 19–44) were recruited through purposive sampling during two periods in 2023 and 2024. The interviews were conducted 5–6 weeks postoperatively during home recovery and lasted on average 31 min. The data analysis was based on Ricoeur's theory of narrative interpretation.ResultsFour themes emerged describing challenges regarding dietary protocols: (1) patients struggle with dietary restrictions due to a lack of information, (2) to be in a negative spiral, (3) soft non‐chew diet as a period of restrictions, and (4) dietary restrictions cause psychosocial burden.ConclusionsThe dietary challenges were primarily practical and often linked to insufficient information. Consequently, patients reported psychosocial difficulties, often describing a sense of being caught in a negative spiral. These experiences may reflect limited health literacy.ImpactThe findings highlight the need for a more structured approach to nutritional care to enhance patients' health literacy, strengthen their self‐management skills and support better recovery outcomes in the orthognathic trajectory. The study has led to changes in both nutritional protocols and patient education for patients undergoing orthognathic surgery.Keywords: Dietary, Health Literacy Nutrition, Orthognathic surgery, Patient education, Postoperative Care, Qualitative Study

Introduction

Patients undergoing orthognathic surgery are subject to postoperative dietary restrictions designed to minimise mechanical stress on the surgical site and support tissue recovery. Several studies have shown that these dietary restrictions make patients particularly vulnerable to nutritional deficits, often resulting in unintended weight loss [1, 2, 3].

Orthognathic surgery realigns the jaws to improve both functional and aesthetic outcomes and is typically indicated for patients with skeletal discrepancies not correctable through orthodontic treatment alone, such as severe malocclusion, facial asymmetry or obstructive sleep apnoea [4]. The procedure, most often performed in young adults, involves repositioning the maxilla, the mandible, or both for bimaxillary procedures, commonly using Le Fort 1 osteotomy, bilateral sagittal split osteotomy (BSSO) and occasionally genioplasty to reposition the chin. These procedures restore occlusion and improve mastication, speech and airway function, while enhancing facial harmony. Postoperatively, stabilisation of the jaw segments is critical to ensure proper healing and alignment. This is achieved by using rigid internal fixation that secures the repositioned bone segments. Additional support is employed in the form of intermaxillary fixation, which involves temporary immobilisation of the jaws by means of elastics or wires attached to orthodontic brackets or arch bars, which serves to maintain occlusion and minimise movement during the initial healing phase.

During recovery, patients must follow a strict dietary protocol. Initially, patients are prescribed a liquid diet to accommodate their limited jaw mobility and avoid any need for mastication. As recovery progresses, the patients transition to a soft non‐chew diet before a regular diet, including chewable textures, is reintroduced. No international standardised postoperative dietary protocol following orthognathic surgery has been identified, and considerable variation has been reported across clinical departments and national healthcare systems [1].

The practical management of the dietary protocol has been identified as a significant concern by patients undergoing orthognathic surgery and their caregivers [5, 6]. Along with the reported findings of unintended weight loss during the recovery phase [1, 2, 3], this highlights dietary support as a key element of patient care during rehabilitation. Insufficient dietary intake following surgery is associated with increased risks of postoperative complications, including delayed wound healing and increased infection risk [7, 8]. Nevertheless, dietary management remains underemphasised in patient education and postoperative care protocols despite its clinical relevance [2, 5].

This gap highlights the need for an evidence‐based framework to provide structured dietary support following orthognathic surgery. However, to our knowledge, no studies have investigated patients' nutritional education needs and their lived experiences with managing the postoperative dietary regimen. Addressing this gap could strengthen dietetics practice and enhance the quality of patient care. Therefore, the aim of this qualitative study is to explore patients' experiences of managing the dietary restrictions required for recovery following orthognathic surgery.

Methods

This study is a qualitative study of patients' experiences of undergoing orthognathic surgery with a focus on dietary management. The approach applied was phenomenological‐hermeneutic, inspired by Ricoeur's theory of narrative and interpretation. Ricoeur's integration of phenomenology with critical hermeneutics enables a deeper understanding of patients' narrated experiences through interpretive analysis [9, 10]. The data was generated through individual semi‐structured interviews [11].

Setting

The study was conducted at a university hospital where patients underwent elective orthognathic surgery following pre‐surgical orthodontic treatment. Postoperative hospitalisation lasted 1–2 days, followed by 6–8 weeks of home recovery. Patients followed a dietary protocol starting with a 10‐day liquid diet and progressing to a soft, non‐chew diet for a total of 6 weeks. The final post‐surgical orthodontic phase, aimed at finalising occlusion, lasted 6–12 months. This is illustrated in Figure 1.

The orthognathic patient trajectory.

Preoperative information was provided by an oral and maxillofacial surgeon and a nurse at the outpatient clinic. Approximately 6 weeks before surgery, patients attended a consultation covering medical assessment and guidance on pre‐ and postoperative care, including dietary restrictions. Patients were encouraged to use the My Hospital app for supplementary information to support patient education. The app offered a comprehensive overview of the care pathway, including details on the surgical procedure and pre‐ and post‐operative restrictions related to fasting, physical activity and dietary, supplemented with examples of suitable foods and recipes. Furthermore, information and guidance on common postoperative physical challenges such as nausea, pain, swelling, infection and oral care. Admission took place on the day of surgery, and during the brief hospital stay, surgeons conducted rounds while nurses provided instructions and practical support for the liquid diet (e.g., squeeze bottles with a nozzle to ease dispensing of liquids). Planned follow‐ups took place in the outpatient clinic one‐ and 3‐weeks post‐surgery.

The interviews were conducted near the end of home recovery (5–6 weeks postoperatively), when diet restrictions were easing, and most participants were preparing to resume school or work.

Recruitment

A nurse recruited participants during their routine 3‐week postoperative follow‐up visit. Following the information about the study, the participants were given time to consider participation before being contacted by a researcher to schedule an interview. Inclusion criteria were adults (≥ 18 years) who had undergone orthognathic surgery. Purposive sampling was applied to get in‐depth knowledge by including participants with detailed knowledge of the phenomenon and with diverse perspectives (age, gender, occupation). Recruitment took place over two periods due to temporary organisational circumstances unrelated to the study design or methodology. Five participants were included in May 2023 and six in August 2024.

Data Collection

Individual, semi‐structured telephone interviews were conducted to explore the participants' experiences with orthognathic surgery. Interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim, and NVivo12 was used to structure the data. A semi‐structured interview guide was followed [11], which contained open‐ended questions that allowed the participants to elaborate on questions such as ‘Can you tell me about how it went with eating after your surgery?’. This approach enabled the interviewer to follow up on reflections and experiences during the conversation. The research team collaboratively developed the interview guide based on relevant literature [1, 12, 13]. The interview guide focused on postoperative experiences and home recovery, particularly dietary management. Data was collected between June 2023 and August 2024. Telephone interviews were chosen to accommodate participants living far from the hospital and to minimise disruption of their daily lives. Previous studies have shown that a telephone interview is a valid alternative to a face‐to‐face interview [14, 15].

Data Analysis

The analysis draws on Ricoeur's theory of narrative and interpretation, following a dialectical movement across three levels: naïve reading, structural analysis and critical interpretation and discussion [9, 10]. The naïve reading provided an overall impression of meaning, while the structural analysis examined the text in depth by moving from the participants' quotations (‘what is said’) to the underlying significance (‘what the text is talking about’), generating key themes through iterative interpretation between the parts and the whole. This validated the interpretations both against the complete dataset and the first impressions from the naïve reading. This process is illustrated in Figure 2.

Ethics Statement

The study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [16]. The participants received both oral and written information about the study and provided informed consent prior to the interviews. After inclusion, the participants were given time to consider their participation before being contacted by a researcher to arrange a time for the interview. Participation was voluntary, and there were no consequences for patients who declined to participate. The study was approved by the Danish Data Protection Agency (journal number: 23/19488). Approval from the Danish National Committee on Biomedical Research Ethics was not required according to Danish law.

Rigour

The study followed the COREQ checklist (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) to ensure transparency and trustworthiness [17]. The multidisciplinary research team consisted of a nurse with an MSc in Nursing, a lecturer with an MSc in Human Nutrition, a nurse with a PhD—all experienced in qualitative research—and a dentist who is a trainee in oral and maxillofacial surgery, who was the only member with in‐depth knowledge of the research field. Data was collected by two members of the research team, who collaboratively developed the interview guide, and subsequently, the interviews were divided between them and conducted individually. None of the researchers were familiar with the participants. Data collection and analysis involved researcher triangulation and continuous reflexive discussions to strengthen the study's credibility [18]. All three levels of the analysis were conducted by three authors and finally discussed with the dentist.

Themes that emerged from the structural analysis.

Patients Struggle With Dietary Restrictions Due to Lack of Information

The patients emphasised that preparation is important to them, and that information was crucial for being able to prepare for the postoperative period and the associated dietary restrictions. One patient described the type of dietary information that was given by the healthcare professionals in this way:

It was mostly about the first 10 days; that it had to be liquid…and that I wasn't allowed to chew and all that. I had to eat soft food and after the 6 weeks it was kind of normal again. As for actual recipes and things like that, I didn't get much information.

(P5)

Patients recalled receiving information about general dietary restrictions and their rationale; however, lacked information and felt unprepared to manage their own dietary. They needed specific guidance on suitable liquid and soft foods, including preparation methods and portion sizes. Instead, healthcare professionals referred to the app for this information, yet it was described as inadequate for preparing them for the period with dietary restrictions. Patients suggested that the app should include step‐by‐step recipes with dietary information, as the lack of such guidance made it difficult to know what to eat. Some sought advice independently through Facebook or other websites. Those who prepared in advance by shopping and cooking before surgery found meal management easier, as food was readily available.

To Be in a Negative Spiral

The period with liquid food was described as the most exhausting and hardest part of the postoperative period. The liquid diet caused a change in the patients' feelings of hunger and satiety because such food was filling the stomach differently and did not provide the same sense of fullness as regular food, as described in the following quote:

When protein powder is added, it's like that's what gives a feeling of fullness. Even though it's a bit strange when you're only drinking. You feel full. But at the same time, not completely full.

(P5)

The feeling of satiety was diffuse: on the one hand, you felt full; on the other hand, you did not. Adding protein powder to meals or drinking hospital‐prescribed nutritional supplements sometimes helped. One common problem was unintentional weight loss of 4–10 kg during the period with a liquid diet, and the body felt drained of energy. The diet consisted mainly of nutritional supplement drinks, milk‐based beverages and juice, which was monotonous and lacked texture or taste variation. Some patients blended regular meals with water to make them drinkable, simply to be able to consume something. Preparing and consuming these meals was exceptionally time‐consuming and exhausting. Eating became an act of necessity rather than enjoyment. Fatigue, sensory disturbances, swallowing difficulties, and the repetitive nature of the diet reinforced each other, creating a negative spiral that worsened the patients' overall experience.

Soft Non‐Chew Diet as a Period of Restrictions

The patients described the transition from a liquid to a soft non‐chew diet as relieving and reintroduced varying taste nuances. However, the soft diet also felt repetitive and was accompanied by physical complications, including facial swelling, altered taste, dry mucosa oris, fatigue and constipation.

One patient explained it as follows:

I mean, I found it relieving that I didn't have to eat soup all the time, but it was also pretty difficult, you know, learning to control your mouth.

(P2)

The soft diet was limited to the same few dishes, leading to monotony, loss of appetite and motivation to cook, which ultimately resulted in patients skipping meals. Limited knowledge and education about dietary, suitable food items, specific recipes and daily energy and protein requirements further hindered adequate intake. Some patients managed better by preparing meals in advance, blending family dishes and experimenting creatively to ease their adjustment to a soft diet and make eating easier. Patients described an adaptation period, during which they learned to eat without chewing, often struggling with paraesthesia around the mouth that impaired control and sensation, causing unintentional biting into their cheeks or lips, drooling or spilling food without being aware of it.

Patients tried to find a solution on their own to adjust to the challenge. This is described in the following quote:

I just couldn't hit my mouth (laughs). I tried, but I kept hitting my cheek, and it was just such a surreal experience…then I figured to get a small mirror, so I could figure out where my mouth actually was.

(P11)

Patients could use a mirror while eating to compensate for reduced mouth control. Following a soft non‐chew diet was time‐consuming, requiring effort in preparation, consumption, and frequent small meals due to the limited portion sizes tolerated. Some patients continued the liquid diet due to physical limitations.

Dietary Restrictions Cause Psychosocial Burden

The postoperative period was also implied significant psychosocial consequences, as one patient expressed:

Something I had read about beforehand was regarding how your mood could be affected, and you could actually become a bit depressed… I was really, really glad I had read about it beforehand, because that's exactly what I experienced, when you're just sitting there unable to eat or drink anything, and you can't do a damn thing, really.

(P11)

Patients experienced both physical and psychological changes; their mood was lowered by dietary restrictions, fatigue and lack of drive in daily life. This led to frustration and vulnerability, creating a feeling of powerlessness. Information about the emotional challenges that may arise during the postoperative period was important, as preparation could generate mental readiness and help patients navigate these emotional reactions. Social aspects of meals were particularly difficult, with physical limitations and inability to eat like others caused feelings of exclusion and avoidance of shared meals. Concerns about recovery and future progress sometimes led to fear, which affected nutritional intake. Mental strategies, such as taking 1 day at a time and keeping meals simple, helped some patients cope. Support from relatives was highly valued and considered essential by many. Preparing for the emotional challenges of the postoperative period appeared important for fostering mental readiness and managing one's reactions.

Discussion

This study has identified four themes that describe patients' experiences of eating and managing life with dietary restrictions during the postoperative period following orthognathic surgery. Overall, the findings indicate that the postoperative recovery at home was challenging due to diet restrictions and recovery. The findings also raise issues for discussion, which require a critical interpretation to achieve a deeper understanding of their meaning.

Health Literacy Among Patients Undergoing Orthognathic Surgery

This section discusses health literacy in relation to three identified themes: challenges in adhering to dietary restrictions; limited knowledge of dietary management in daily life; and the need for sufficient education to address dietary limitations and psychosocial burdens.

Previous studies have highlighted that dietary restrictions and adherence to a liquid diet are among the most difficult aspects of the rehabilitation process following orthognathic surgery—for the patients and for their relatives [5, 19]. For parents, it is challenging to watch their child lose weight and to be the one who must encourage eating [5]. Similarly, the patients described feeling restricted in their food choices due to complications such as difficulty swallowing, swelling, and the inability to eat the same food as others. These limitations were often accompanied by fatigue and fear of eating related to concerns about potential complications, which further discouraged food intake [5, 13]. This is also reflected in the present study, in which patients reported skipping meals due to uncertainty about what to eat and described a psychosocial burden characterised by fear and social exclusion. The lack of knowledge regarding dietary restrictions and how to manage them in daily life suggests a potential gap in the information provided by healthcare professionals. This issue, which has also been discussed by Watts et al. [19], highlights the importance of health literacy in postoperative recovery.

Health literacy denotes the individual knowledge and skills required to access, comprehend and apply health‐related information in ways that support personal health and well‐being [20]. Don Nutbeam describes health literacy as a multidimensional construct that goes beyond basic reading abilities. He outlines three progressive levels: functional, interactive and critical health literacy [21]. The functional and interactive levels involve the ability to understand and engage with health information, whereas critical health literacy encompasses the capacity to evaluate information and make autonomous, informed decisions about one's health based on that understanding [22]. Inadequate health literacy can hinder patients' ability to cope effectively with postoperative side effects such as dietary restrictions. Our study shows that the information provided fails to address patients' needs regarding the management of postoperative dietary restrictions and that patients also request guidance in how to handle psychosocial challenges they may encounter. Focusing on patents' health literacy can improve clinical outcomes and patient experiences by enhancing their ability to understand, interpret and act on health information throughout the postoperative period [23]. Improving health literacy can have the potential to reduce the overall burden of care, enhance care management and empower patients to better navigate the recovery process [21, 23]. Preparing the patients for possible emotional consequences supports their ability to recognise and manage emotions following the operation, which enables a more informed and well‐prepared approach to recovery. However, research also shows that patients often find information from healthcare professionals insufficient and prefer to seek out others who have undergone similar procedures, valuing firsthand accounts more highly [13]. Wade et al. found that although healthcare professionals were the preferred source of information, the internet and peer experiences served as important supplementary resources [24]. Our findings reflect limitations in interactive and critical literacy in particular, as the lack of understanding appeared to affect the patients' ability to adhere to treatment and manage dietary restrictions. This points to the need for an increased focus on the ways in which patient education can be improved, which in turn places demands on healthcare professionals and the organisational structure of the healthcare system [25, 26].

Optimising Postoperative Dietary Protocols

Our findings revealed that the period of liquid diet was the most challenging one and that patients were caught in a negative spiral of fatigue and physical difficulties. This raises questions about its duration, considering the discussion about health literacy. Moreover, several studies have reported weight loss following orthognathic surgery in an international context, particularly within the first 2 weeks [12, 27, 28, 29]. Research shows that weight loss was highest among patients living alone [28]. This indicates the importance of support from relatives, which is also highlighted in our findings: some patients expressed that they could not imagine going through the process without the support of their relatives.

Orthognathic surgery induces a metabolic stress response, which leads to increased energy and protein demands in patients. Inadequate nutrition causes delay in wound healing and increased risks of infections, since malnutrition decreases anabolism and depresses immunity [27, 29]. Tailoring the postoperative diet protocols is crucial due to the heightened nutritional needs and the risk associated with inadequate nutrition. Nevertheless, considerable variation in these protocols is reported internationally. The most frequently reported regimen consists of a liquid diet for 2–4 weeks followed by a soft diet for 4–6 weeks, although some protocols recommend starting with a soft non‐chew diet that transitions directly into a soft diet in which patients are allowed to chew carefully [1]. However, evidence supporting one regimen over another remains limited and specific protocols for orthognathic surgery patients require further validation [22]. In a national context, dietary protocols differ across the five clinical departments where the procedure is performed; some centres apply a soft non‐chew diet for the entire 6 weeks of the postoperative period, whereas others allow patients to begin chewing as early as in the fourth week.

Based on the results of this study and in light of the absence of evidence‐based guidelines, it may be worth considering whether the current requirement of a 10‐day liquid diet is necessary. A shorter period might improve patient comfort and reduce unintended weight loss.

Strengths and Limitations

This study was conducted at a single centre with 11 patients, a relatively small sample; however, qualitative research aims to deepen understanding rather than achieve numerical representability [30], and data saturation was achieved, as the final interviews did not generate new perspectives, and rich descriptions were obtained throughout the study. The participants reflected the patient group in terms of variation in gender, age and type of performed surgery, and despite data collection took place over two periods, the data material was suitable for collective analysis.

Telephone interviews are not commonly used because they limit access to contextual data, such as nonverbal communication [31]; however, studies have shown that telephone interviews can generate data of comparable quality to face‐to‐face interviews [14, 15]. In this study, we did not collect sensitive information, and the participants shared their experiences freely and openly. Therefore, telephone interviews were appropriate in relation to the scope of the study [32]. This approach caused minimal disruption to participants' daily lives and enabled the inclusion of patients living at a distance. Additionally, conducting interviews while participants were in their own homes appeared to help them feel relaxed, allowing them to share their experiences openly and freely.

Researcher triangulation strengthened validity [18]. Two researchers conducted the interviews separately and collaborated on developing the interview guide and data collection procedures. Having two interviewers can enhance reflexivity, as discussions allow comparison and refinement of the interview approach, improving data depth and richness [11]. However, differences in interviewing style may influence data generation; to mitigate this, regular debriefings and alignment discussions were held, and the first interviews were reviewed before conducting the remaining ones to ensure consistency. The data analysis was carried out collaboratively by the authors in an iterative dialogue from which four themes emerged. Awareness of researchers' preconceptions, combined with diverse backgrounds and a systematic method, ensured analysis remained guided by the research question and framework rather than assumptions [33].

The study's findings can inform improvements in the orthognathic patient pathway and may also be relevant for other patients with dietary restrictions. Additionally, insights into health literacy can guide patient education practices more broadly.

Conclusion

The aim of this study was to explore patients' experiences following orthognathic surgery with an emphasis on dietary management. Four themes emerged, which highlighted the challenges that patients face in managing dietary restrictions after surgery. These challenges were primarily practical and often linked to insufficient information provided by healthcare professionals. As a consequence, the patients reported psychosocial difficulties and often described a sense of being caught in a negative spiral. These experiences may reflect limited health literacy, both on an individual level and within the healthcare system responsible for patient education and support.

In response to these findings, clinical practice was revised to shorten the liquid diet period, enhance patient education, incorporate peer‐supported guidance, and provide specific dietary resources aimed at improving dietary management and recovery following orthognathic surgery.
