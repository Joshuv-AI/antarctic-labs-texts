Comparative Performance of Gemini 3 Pro and GPT-5 Family Models on Ophthalmology Board-Style Questions
Authors: Ryan S Shean, Jayanth Kumar Mallapu, Tathya Shah, Haroon Adam Rasheed, David N Younessi, Yih Chung Tham, Van Nguyen, Kyle Bolo, Benjamin Y Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare the performance of state-of-the-art Gemini and GPT models on ophthalmology board–style questions and examine variation by subspecialty, cognitive complexity, and question type.DesignA cross-sectional evaluation of 12 distinct large language model (LLM) configurations using a standardized ophthalmology question set.SubjectsFive hundred multiple-choice questions (250 from the American Academy of Ophthalmology’s Basic and Clinical Science Course [BCSC]; 250 StatPearls).MethodsTwelve configurations of the following LLMs: Gemini 3 Pro, Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-5.1 Pro, GPT-5 Pro, GPT-5.2, GPT-5.1, and GPT-5, interpreted the questions using standardized prompting procedures. Questions were categorized by subspecialty, multimodal content (image vs. text-only), and cognitive complexity (first, second, or third order). Accuracy, paired discordance (McNemar tests), and one-way analysis of variance with Tukey correction were used to compare performance. Human benchmarking used BCSC percent-correct data.Main Outcome MeasuresOverall accuracy, subspecialty accuracy, image vs. nonimage accuracy, cognitive-complexity accuracy, and paired model-level discordance.ResultsModel accuracy ranged from 81.4% to 94.0%. Gemini 3 Pro High Reasoning achieved the highest accuracy (94.0%), followed by Gemini 3 Pro Low Reasoning (92.4%). GPT-5.1 Pro led the GPT family (90.4%), whereas GPT-5.2 Base Model performed lowest (81.4%). Analysis of variance showed significant heterogeneity (P < 0.001), but most Tukey-corrected pairwise differences were nonsignificant. McNemar tests demonstrated significantly more correct paired responses for Gemini 3 Pro High Reasoning than for GPT-5.2 and all GPT-5/5.1 variants. Models performed markedly better on BCSC (mean 94.4%) than StatPearls (81.9%); human BCSC mean accuracy was 64.5%. Image-based items produced a 10- to 22-point accuracy decrement across all systems. Accuracy declined with increasing cognitive complexity, with the clearest separation on third-order management questions.ConclusionsGemini 3 Pro had the best general-purpose LLM performance on ophthalmology board–style questions, providing near-perfect accuracy, while outperforming all GPT-5 family variants across domains and complexity levels. Significant deficits on image-based and third-order questions highlight persistent multimodal limitations and the need for ongoing benchmarking using challenging, clinically grounded datasets.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.Keywords: Gemini 3 Pro, GPT-5, Large language models, Ophthalmology board-style questions, Ophthalmology education

Large language models (LLMs) have emerged as a transformative class of generative artificial intelligence (AI) with rapidly expanding applications across medicine, medical education, and clinical decision support.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 In ophthalmology, these systems are being explored for tasks ranging from simulated patient consultations to interpreting board-style questions and generating patient-facing eye-care guidance.6, 7, 8 Large language models hold the potential to augment clinical reasoning, enhance trainee education, and broaden access to high-quality ophthalmic information. As LLMs become more common in training environments and point-of-care workflows, rigorous assessment of their accuracy, reliability, and domain-specific limitations is essential. Earlier evaluations have shown variable specialty-specific performance, and the rapid pace of model development and breadth of ophthalmic tasks underscores the need for continuous, clinically meaningful benchmarking.9

Board-style questions offer a practical and standardized framework for evaluating LLM performance because they reflect the core knowledge and clinical reasoning competencies required of medical trainees. In the field of ophthalmology, assessments administered by American Academy of Ophthalmology span the major subspecialties and encompass both foundational concepts and higher-order analytical skills. Prior work demonstrated strong performance from both Google Gemini and OpenAI GPT model families on ophthalmology board-style questions, but results have varied substantially by model generation, question domain, and cognitive complexity.10 For example, GPT-4.0 and OpenAI o1 matched or exceeded earlier Gemini models in corneal disease identification and ocular oncology question banks, whereas Gemini Advanced outperformed GPT-4 on multidisciplinary ophthalmology question sets.11, 12, 13, 14 Since these earlier comparisons, both LLM ecosystems have evolved rapidly; Google reported that its newest flagship model, Gemini 3 Pro, surpasses competitor models on advanced academic reasoning tasks such as Humanity’s Last Exam.15 As a result, no head-to-head evaluations of the newest models have been conducted using a comprehensive ophthalmic benchmark, leaving important gaps in understanding their performance and readiness for educational or clinical applications. Although individual model rankings will evolve, benchmarking that interrogates performance across subspecialty domains, cognitive reasoning tiers, multimodal inputs, and human reference points yields durable insight into architectural strengths and persistent limitations. In ophthalmology, where diagnostic reasoning depends heavily on visual interpretation and structured management prioritization, such characterization helps define realistic expectations for how LLMs may augment training and knowledge-driven clinical tasks.

To address this gap, we conducted a systematic comparison of 10 configurations across the newest Gemini 3 Pro, Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-5.1 Pro, GPT-5 Pro, GPT-5.2, GPT-5.1, and GPT-5 models across 500 board-style ophthalmology questions drawn from the American Academy of Ophthalmology’s Basic and Clinical Science Course (BCSC) and StatPearls, an independent question bank. We assessed performance across ophthalmic subspecialties, cognitive complexity levels, and image-versus text-based formats and benchmarked the results against available human trainee data. Our objective was to compare contemporary LLM performance and define capability boundaries across reasoning complexity, imaging dependence, and subspecialty scope, providing a benchmark for tracking performance evolution over time and informing expectations for integration into ophthalmology training and clinical practice.

Methods

Ethical Approval

This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was exempt from review by the University of Southern California Institutional Review Board because it constituted non–human subjects research. The analysis involved only commercial LLMs and authorized educational materials; therefore, informed consent was not required.

Question Sources and Dataset Construction

A total of 500 ophthalmology board–style multiple-choice questions were used, drawn evenly from 2 widely used educational platforms: 250 items from the American Academy of Ophthalmology’s BCSC and 250 items from StatPearls. Permission to use BCSC content was obtained from the American Academy of Ophthalmology, and explicit permission was granted by StatPearls for use of their questions, including associated images. Because permission for image-containing BCSC items was not provided, all image-based questions in the data set originated from the StatPearls set. Questions were classified as image-containing (n = 74) or image-free (n = 426). All questions contained a single correct answer. Percent-correct data for human test takers were available only for BCSC items and were used for benchmarking.

Question Categorization and Complexity Assessment

The 500 questions were categorized into 9 distinct ophthalmology subfields: (1) lens and cataract, (2) cornea, external disease, and anterior segment, (3) glaucoma, (4) neuro-ophthalmology, (5) oculofacial, plastics, and orbit, (6) pediatric ophthalmology and strabismus, (7) refractive management and surgery, (8) retina, vitreous, and intraocular tumors, and (9) uveitis. Each question was stratified into 1 of 3 levels based on Buckwalter taxonomic schema to evaluate cognitive complexity: first-order (recognition/recall), second-order (comprehension/interpretation), and third-order (application/problem-solving).16

LLM Configurations and Experimental Conditions

Twenty total model runs were conducted across 12 model configurations within the Gemini and GPT families. These comprised Gemini 3 Pro High Reasoning, Gemini 3 Pro Low Reasoning, Gemini 3 Pro Base Model (three-run average), Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-5.2 Base Model (three-run average), GPT-5.1 Base Model (three-run average), GPT-5.1 High Reasoning, GPT-5.1 Medium Reasoning, GPT-5.1 Low Reasoning, GPT-5 Base Model (three-run average), GPT-5 Pro, and GPT-5.1 Pro. All models were accessed via the Application Programming Interface (API) except for GPT-5 Pro and GPT-5.1 Pro, which were evaluated manually because API access for these configurations was not available at the time of analysis. GPT-5.1 was evaluated at 3 reasoning levels (low, medium, and high), and Gemini 3 Pro was evaluated both at 2 reasoning levels (high and low) and in 3 separate runs under standard conditions. GPT-5, 5.1, and 5.2 Base Models were also assessed in 3 separate runs under standard conditions. All models were run between November 18, 2025, and November 26, 2025; GPT-5.2 was subsequently run after its release date on December 15, 2025.

Reasoning levels represent distinct settings within each API. Reasoning levels determine the model’s internal allocation of chain-of-thought resources and are defined options at the API level. At the time of analysis, only a subset of evaluated models supported multiple reasoning levels. For base models without reasoning-level variants, 3 independent runs were performed and averaged to generate a single model configuration. All 20 original runs were retained in secondary analyses examining item-level discordance across models and runs. For configurations evaluated across repeated inference runs, performance metrics were calculated by pooling responses across runs rather than averaging per-run accuracy. Each run consisted of the full 500-question data set; therefore, three-run configurations produced 1500 total model–question evaluations. Reported numerators and denominators reflect this pooled framework unless otherwise specified. Single-run configurations retained a denominator of 500.

Subscription-related costs were documented: GPT-5 Pro and GPT-5.1 Pro required a $200/month subscription for access to these models, whereas Gemini 3 Pro was available for limited use with a free account or for greater use through the Gemini Pro student subscription, which was free for 1 year at the time of data collection.

LLM Querying Procedure

All evaluations were conducted using a standardized prompt applied identically across models. The prompt was: “Hi GPT/Gemini, you are instructed to answer the following questions from the BCSC/StatPearls Ophthalmology question bank. I will copy each question stem, with 4 to 6 possible answer choices. Please select the answer choice(s) that you believe is/are the correct answer to the best of your abilities and knowledge.” Each question stem and all answer choices were copied verbatim into the query. Models were not prompted to provide explanations. All 500 questions were presented in the same sequence for each run. For questions with images, the corresponding image was provided directly to the LLM using its native multimodal image-input interface. Images were loaded from file, Base64-encoded, and included alongside the text prompt in a single API query.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was the accuracy of each model, defined as the percentage of correctly answered questions. Secondary outcomes included accuracy differences for image versus nonimage questions, performance stratified by cognitive complexity (first-order, second-order, and third-order reasoning), accuracy across ophthalmic subspecialties, and estimated computational cost. Human performance was compared using percent-correct data available for the BCSC question bank.

Statistical Analysis

Differences in accuracy across the 12 analytic model configurations were assessed using a one-way analysis of variance treating correctness as a binary outcome and model as the only factor. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey honestly significant difference test when the global test demonstrated statistical significance. Because all models answered the same set of items, paired McNemar tests were used to evaluate question-level discordance between models. For the large number of model-pair comparisons, P values from McNemar tests were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Across 500 ophthalmology board–style questions per configuration (250 StatPearls items [Q1–250] and 250 BCSC items [Q251–500]), all models performed at a high level, with accuracies ranging from 81.4% to 94.0% (Fig 1). Gemini 3 Pro High Reasoning achieved the highest overall accuracy, correctly answering 470 of 500 questions (94.0%). Gemini 3 Pro Low Reasoning was second (462/500; 92.4%), followed by the Gemini 3 Pro Base Model (1360/1500; 90.7%), where results reflect pooled performance across 3 independent runs of the 500-question set (total N = 1500 model–question evaluations) rather than a single pass. The 3 GPT-5.1 reasoning variants, GPT-5.1 Pro, GPT-5 Pro, GPT-5 Base Model, and Gemini 2.5 Pro were clustered in the middle (85.6%–90.4%). GPT-5.1 and GPT-5.2 Base Models demonstrated the lowest performance (1224/1500, 81.6% and 1221/1500, 81.4%, respectively).

Bar graph of the overall performance of each model. ∗ Indicates significantly worse than the best model (McNemar test, P < 0.05). SE = standard error.

One-way analysis of variance showed a significant overall difference in accuracy across the 12 configurations (P < 0.001). In Tukey honestly significant difference post hoc testing, only the comparisons involving GPT-5.2 Base Model, GPT-5.1 Base Model, GPT-5.1 Low Reasoning, and the Gemini 3 Pro models remained significant after adjustment: Gemini 3 Pro High Reasoning outperformed GPT-5.2 Base Model, GPT-5.1 Base Model, GPT-5.1 Low Reasoning (adjusted P < 0.001 for all), and Gemini 3 Pro Low Reasoning (adjusted P = 0.02). All other pairwise contrasts were nonsignificant (P ≥ 0.06).

Paired McNemar tests assessing item-level discordance showed strong and consistent advantages for Gemini 3 Pro, particularly the High Reasoning configuration. After correction for multiple comparisons, Gemini 3 Pro High Reasoning had significantly more correct paired responses than GPT-5.2 Base Model, GPT-5.1 Base Model, GPT-5 Base Model, GPT-5.1 High Reasoning, GPT-5.1 Medium Reasoning, GPT-5.1 Low Reasoning, GPT-5.1 Pro, GPT-5 Pro, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and the Gemini 3 Pro Base Model (all P ≤ 0.02). Gemini 3 Pro Low Reasoning also outperformed GPT-5.1 Low, GPT-5.1 High, GPT-5.1 Medium, GPT-5 Pro, GPT-5.2 Base Model, GPT-5.1 Base Model, and Gemini 2.5 Pro on question-by-question comparisons (all P ≤ 0.02).

Across the 2 question sources, models consistently performed better on BCSC items than on StatPearls items (Fig 2). Averaged across all 12 model configurations, mean accuracy on BCSC questions was 94.4%, compared with 81.9% on StatPearls questions. When restricted to nonimage questions only, mean accuracy across models was 94.4% for BCSC and 84.6% for StatPearls. On BCSC questions, all model configurations operated near ceiling: Gemini 3 Pro High Reasoning was nearly perfect (249/250; 99.6%), followed by Gemini 3 Pro Low Reasoning (245/250; 98.0%) and GPT-5.1 Pro (242/250; 96.8%), with the remaining models scoring between 89.2% and 96.5%. On StatPearls questions, accuracies were lower across all models but maintained the same relative ranking. Gemini 3 Pro High Reasoning again led (221/250; 88.4%), followed by Gemini 3 Pro Low Reasoning (86.8%) and the Gemini 3 Pro Base Model (84.8%). Most GPT and Gemini configurations clustered closely between 79.6% and 84.0%, while GPT-5.1 Base Model and GPT-5.2 Base Model were lowest (73.6% for both). Human accuracy on BCSC questions averaged 64.5%, substantially below all LLMs. Human accuracy underperformed all LLMs across all 3 question orders and all 8 clinical categories (Supplementary Table S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Bar graph of performance of each model on BCSC and StatPearls question banks, compared with human performance on the BCSC question bank. BCSC = Basic and Clinical Science Course; SE = standard error.

Across the 74 image-based questions, accuracies were consistently lower than on non-image questions for every model (Fig 3). This pattern persisted across GPT and Gemini models. Gemini 3 Pro High Reasoning was the strongest model on image questions (62/74; 83.8%), followed by Gemini 3 Pro Low Reasoning (79.7%) and GPT-5.1 Medium Reasoning and GPT-5.1 Pro variants (78.4% for both). Most models clustered between 75% and 78% on image-based items. In contrast, performance on nonimage questions remained markedly higher: Gemini 3 Pro High Reasoning achieved 95.8%, Gemini 3 Pro Low Reasoning 94.6%, and GPT-5.1 Pro 92.5%.

Bar graph of the performance of each model, divided into image-based versus text-only questions. SE = standard error.

Accuracy was high across all 9 clinical subcategories (Supplementary Fig S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Models performed best on glaucoma and uveitis questions overall: many configurations, including Gemini 3 Pro High and Low Reasoning, achieved near-perfect performance, with nearly all other models still ≥ 90% in these domains. Retina/vitreous/tumors, neuro-ophthalmology, pediatrics/strabismus, plastics/orbit, lens/cataract, and refractive management also showed uniformly strong performance, with typical accuracies in the mid-80s to high-90s range. Cornea/external/anterior segment was relatively more challenging, with accuracies spanning approximately 69% to 88% across models. Within this high-performance landscape, Gemini 3 Pro High and Low Reasoning were at or near the top of the ranking in most subspecialties, while GPT-5.1 and GPT-5.2 Base Models were frequently among the lowest performers in each category, albeit still maintaining accuracies in the 70% to mid-80% range.

Performance was further stratified by question order or cognitive complexity (1 = first-order/recall, 2 = second-order/interpretive, 3 = third-order/management or multi-step reasoning) (Supplementary Fig S2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Across all 12 models, accuracy declined with increasing cognitive complexity: mean accuracy was the highest for first-order questions (96.9%), decreased for second-order questions (91.3%), and was lowest for third-order questions (83.5%). For first-order/recall questions (n = 99 per model), all configurations performed near ceiling (92.9%–99.0%), with Gemini 3 Pro High Reasoning, Gemini 3 Pro Low Reasoning, and GPT-5.1 Pro each answering 98 of 99 questions correctly (99.0%). For second-order/interpretive questions (n = 129), accuracies ranged from 85.5% to 97.7%; Gemini 3 Pro High and Low Reasoning led (126/129, 97.7% and 124/129, 96.1%, respectively), and GPT-5.2 Base Model scored lowest (331/387, 85.5%). For third-order/management questions (n = 272), separation was greatest: Gemini 3 Pro High Reasoning reached 246 of 272 (90.4%), Gemini 3 Pro Low Reasoning reached 240 of 272 (88.2%), while GPT-5.1 Base Model scored the lowest (609/816, 74.6%).

Discussion

In this comprehensive head-to-head evaluation of the newest generation of LLMs on a large, mixed-source ophthalmology question set, Gemini 3 Pro consistently outperformed all GPT-5 family variants across overall accuracy, subspecialties, and cognitive complexity tiers. All models consistently outperformed human test-takers on BCSC questions, demonstrated declining accuracy with increasing cognitive complexity and exhibited performance degradation on image-based questions. GPT-5.1 and GPT-5.2 base models consistently occupied the lower end of performance, demonstrating that nonreasoning-based GPT models exhibit the lowest performance in standardized multiple-choice formats. Together, these results indicate that substantial variation persists within and across current model families and provide a timely reassessment of model capabilities as LLM ecosystems are beginning to intersect more directly with medical education and decision-support workflows.

Our results demonstrate that Gemini 3 Pro outperforms GPT models overall, as well across most ophthalmic subspecialties and image-based and text-based questions, representing a notable shift from earlier comparative patterns and highlighting how iterative architectural improvements can rapidly alter model hierarchies. By incorporating 12 modern model configurations, paired question-level comparisons, and a combined StatPearls and BCSC data set, our study provides a comprehensive assessment of the current LLM ecosystem. The consistent superiority of Gemini 3 Pro across multiple analytical dimensions suggests that recent Gemini iterations may now surpass GPT models in domains requiring structured medical reasoning within ophthalmology, representing a clear shift from earlier findings in which GPT-4, o1, and related GPT systems outperformed prior versions of Gemini across corneal disease identification, ocular oncology, and general ophthalmology reasoning tasks.11, 12, 13,17,18 Notably, GPT-5.2 base model demonstrated the lowest overall accuracy across most analyses, underperforming even earlier GPT-5 configurations. This finding may represent an inflection point in recent LLM development, suggesting a possible tradeoff in which newer base models prioritize attributes such as computational efficiency, latency, or deployment scalability over raw accuracy on complex medical reasoning tasks. Importantly, this observation demonstrates that performance gains across model generations cannot be assumed and that relative capability hierarchies may shift over time, reinforcing the need for standardized benchmarking of each significant update. Moreover, the tight clustering of mid-tier models, despite architectural differences, indicates emerging convergence in core performance capabilities, with high-reasoning configurations providing the clearest separation among top-tier systems.

Performance varied meaningfully across ophthalmic subspecialties and cognitive complexity levels, offering insight into model strengths and limitations. Gemini 3 Pro consistently led or co-led in glaucoma, neuro-ophthalmology, retina, pediatrics/strabismus, refractive management, and uveitis, suggesting broad generalizability rather than niche superiority. In contrast, accuracies were systematically lower for cornea/external/anterior segment questions, mirroring earlier observations that anterior segment diagnoses may be more challenging due to nuanced pathophysiology and image-dependent differentiation.12,19,20 Cognitive complexity revealed even larger disparities: models were near-ceiling on first-order recall but separated more clearly on third-order management questions, where Gemini 3 Pro High Reasoning maintained a distinct advantage. These results collectively suggest that multistep medical decision-making remains the most discriminative capability among modern LLM architectures and reinforce the importance of reasoning-optimized configurations for tasks requiring diagnostic prioritization, management selection, or synthesis of higher-order clinical information.

Substantial performance gaps emerged between image-based and text-only questions, with all models experiencing 10% to 20% point declines on items requiring image interpretation. This pattern reinforces findings from earlier multimodal studies and highlights the ongoing difficulty generalist LLMs face when integrating visual information into clinical reasoning.21 Gemini 3 Pro High Reasoning showed the smallest decrease, possibly due to Google’s extensive image-training ecosystem and large-scale multimodal data sets. Nonetheless, overall performance on image-based items remained well below text-only accuracy, emphasizing the need for specialized ophthalmic multimodal models or hybrid pipelines that incorporate dedicated vision architectures. As multimodal LLMs continue to mature, future research should examine whether domain-specific fine-tuning can mitigate these reproducible deficits.

Differences between StatPearls and BCSC items further contextualize model performance and future benchmarking needs. All models operated near ceiling on BCSC, whereas StatPearls questions elicited substantially lower accuracies and wider performance stratification. One significant difference between these 2 question sets was that StatPearls questions contained image-based questions, where BCSC questions did not; however, the performance on StatPearls questions remained roughly 10% lower after excluding image-based questions. StatPearls questions’ emphasis on interpretation, management, and higher-order reasoning likely contributes to this difference and underscores their value as a discriminative benchmark for evaluating rapidly improving LLMs. Human performance data on BCSC questions revealed that all models significantly outperformed human test-takers. This pattern was consistent across analytic stratifications, with human accuracy lower than all LLM configurations across question orders and all evaluated clinical categories, reinforcing the robustness of this human–model performance gap. Notably, relative model rankings were stable across both question banks, suggesting that identified performance hierarchies reflect true architectural differences rather than dataset artifacts. As LLMs continue to advance, reliance on more challenging benchmarks like StatPearls, particularly image-based and third-order questions, will be critical to differentiate high-end models.

The competitive positioning of these models also has practical implications for access, affordability, and educational equity.22 Gemini 3 Pro is available for up to 100 queries per day through Gemini Pro, which is currently available free of cost for students for 1 year on the web-based interface. In contrast, access to GPT-5.1 Pro requires a $200 monthly subscription for access to high reasoning tiers. That a low-cost model consistently outperformed premium GPT alternatives represents a marked inversion of earlier AI generations, where higher cost often correlated with superior accuracy. For individual trainees or residency programs using LLMs as supplementary study tools, the availability of a high-performing free model may meaningfully reduce financial barriers to advanced AI-assisted learning. Nonetheless, practical use remains constrained by restrictions from question-bank publishers, many of whom prohibit direct input of proprietary questions into LLMs, a policy that limits the utility of these systems in examination preparation. These considerations underscore the need to balance performance with accessibility as educational institutions evaluate LLM integration.

This study has some limitations. First, our data set included multimodal items, but image-based BCSC questions were unavailable, limiting assessment of clinically realistic imaging complexity. Second, human trainee performance data were available only for BCSC questions and lacked stratification by training level, limiting the depth of human–AI comparison. Third, the evaluation setting does not capture the full breadth of diagnostic reasoning in a clinical setting, including integration of incomplete histories, multimodal imaging, and laboratory data. Fourth, LLM hallucinations, latent bias, and opaque reasoning processes remain important concerns for any clinical application. Fifth, the “black box” nature of LLMs makes it challenging to interpret how conclusions are reached, which could be a barrier to gaining clinician trust and adoption. Finally, the rapid evolution of LLM architectures means that absolute performance estimates may become outdated; however, documenting performance trajectories remains essential for identifying stable capability domains, persistent limitations, and inflection points in model development that influence clinical adoption decisions.

In conclusion, this study provides both a contemporary benchmark and a longitudinal reference point for tracking the evolving capabilities of general-purpose LLMs in ophthalmology reasoning tasks, demonstrating that Gemini 3 Pro currently represents the state-of-the-art among evaluated configurations. These results demonstrate that meaningful performance differences persist among top-tier LLMs, especially on complex management questions and image-based tasks. The observation that a widely accessible, low-cost model on web interface achieved the highest performance has important implications for educational equity and the integration of AI-assisted learning into medical training programs. At the same time, the consistent image-interpretation deficit across all models underscores an unmet need in ophthalmic multimodal AI. Taken together, these insights clarify the current capabilities and limitations of emerging LLM architectures and highlight the areas where further improvements are most needed. Future research should incorporate more challenging data sets, repeated runs, structured prompting, and dedicated multimodal ophthalmic models to better define performance ceilings and ensure safe, effective translation into medical education and clinical decision support.
