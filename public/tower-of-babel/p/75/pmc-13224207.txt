Rare case of intracranial histiocytic sarcoma: A case report and literature review
Authors: Keiichi Shibagaki, Shiro Ohue, Kentaro Murayama, Satoshi Fujiwara, Kouji Furukawa, Shinji Onoue, Haruhisa Ichikawa, Shinji Iwata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Histiocytic sarcoma (HS) is a rare malignant neoplasm derived from mature histiocytes, typically affecting lymph nodes, the gastrointestinal tract, spleen, soft tissues, and skin. Intracranial occurrence is extremely rare and poses significant diagnostic challenges due to its histopathological resemblance to other aggressive tumors such as anaplastic meningioma.Case Description: A 70-year-old man presented with acute right hemiplegia and was found to have two extra-axial lesions with intracerebral hemorrhage in the left frontotemporal region. Initial imaging and immunohistochemistry suggested anaplastic meningioma, and total resection was performed. Six months later, the tumor recurred with brain infiltration and scalp metastasis. However, persistent fever and new neoplastic lesions on whole-body computed tomography (CT), despite negative blood cultures, prompted positron emission tomography-CT, which revealed systemic metastases. This aggressive clinical course led to further immunohistochemical analysis, revealing positivity for cluster of differentiation (CD)68 and CD163, confirming the diagnosis of HS. Due to widespread disease progression, best supportive care was initiated.Conclusion: This case highlights that HS can radiologically mimic meningioma, even presenting with a classic dural tail sign. Clinicians should remain vigilant for “imaging paradoxes” – where an unusually aggressive clinical course contradicts typical radiological features. In such atypical cases, an expanded immunohistochemical panel including CD68 and CD163 is essential to avoid diagnostic delays and ensure accurate identification.

INTRODUCTION

Histiocytic sarcoma (HS) is a rare malignant tumor that originates from histiocytes, based on morphological and immunohistochemical evidence.[1] The sites of occurrence mainly include lymph nodes, the gastrointestinal tract, spleen, soft tissues, and skin.[14] It is extremely rare for HS to occur intracranially. Here, we report a case of HS that developed intracranially and posed significant diagnostic challenges.[2,6] We specifically emphasize the diagnostic pitfall of the “dural tail sign,” which typically suggests meningioma but can appear in HS as a reactive change, necessitating careful differentiation based on clinical progression.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 70-year-old male presented with right hemiplegia upon waking. He was transferred to our hospital after being diagnosed with a brain tumor and intracerebral hemorrhage in the left cranial vault at another hospital. Initial examination revealed consciousness disturbance, motor aphasia, and right hemiplegia. Head computed tomography (CT) showed two extra-axial tumor lesions in the left frontotemporal region, with intracerebral hemorrhage [Figure 1a]. On noncontrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), the lesion exhibited hypointensity on T1-weighted images and isointensity on both T2-weighted images and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery sequences [Figure 1b-d]. MRI after the administration of a gadolinium (Gd)-based contrast agent (0.1 mmol/kg) demonstrated faint enhancement, suggesting internal vessels and hemorrhage, along with a dural tail sign [Figure 1e-g]. Cerebral angiography revealed feeders from the left middle meningeal artery supplying the mass. No obvious primary site was identified on whole-body CT. Preoperative diagnosis was meningioma, solitary fibrous tumor (SFT), or lymphoma based on imaging. He underwent emergent tumor resection. The tumor was minimally adherent to the dura mater but was strongly adherent to the brain surface in parts. The tumor was totally resected.

Pathologically, hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining showed densely interwoven polygonal to spindle-shaped cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm, pseudo-palisading necrosis, numerous mitotic figures with multinucleated and atypical mitotic figures, and a high Ki-67 proliferation index. Immunohistochemical staining was positive for Vimentin and progesterone receptor (PgR) and negative for epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), cluster of differentiation (CD)-34, and signal transducer and activator of transcription 6. Although EMA expression was negative, the diagnosis of anaplastic meningioma was prioritized due to the strong positivity for Vimentin and PgR, combined with the classic radiological presence of a dural tail sign. After surgery, he became alert and his right hemiplegia improved. He was discharged home after rehabilitation. Given that gross total resection (GTR) was achieved and the early postoperative Gd-enhanced MRI confirmed no residual tumor, immediate adjuvant radiotherapy was withheld. Instead, a strict observational follow-up strategy was adopted, scheduling clinical and Gd-enhanced MRI evaluations every 3 months. The therapeutic policy was to initiate salvage radiotherapy only upon evidence of tumor recurrence.

Six months later, right hemiplegia worsened again, and MR images revealed recurrence and brain infiltration of the tumor [Figure 2a and b], along with suspected subcutaneous metastasis in the scalp. He underwent another resection of the tumor, and the pathology was similar to the previous findings, confirming the scalp metastasis. However, the patient developed a persistent fever. While blood tests revealed a minor elevation in inflammatory markers, various culture tests remained negative. Furthermore, whole-body plain CT showed no radiological evidence of infection, instead revealing multiple new neoplastic lesions. To further investigate these findings and explore a neoplastic origin for the fever, positron emission tomography-CT was performed, which demonstrated widespread systemic metastases [Figure 3]. The clinical course deviated from that of a typical meningioma, prompting additional immunohistochemical staining, which was positive for CD68 and CD163 and negative for CD1a, CD21, and CD34 [Figure 4a-f]. The final diagnosis was HS, and the decision was made to pursue best supportive care.

DISCUSSION

HS is defined as a malignant tumor showing morphological and immunohistochemical characteristics of mature histiocytes, excluding acute monocytic leukemia and associated neoplastic proliferation.[5] Furthermore, while HS often arises as a primary neoplasm, it can also occur secondarily. Secondary HS is frequently associated with other hematologic malignancies, such as acute lymphoblastic leukemia or myeloid neoplasms, often sharing a common clonal origin through transdifferentiation.[12] HS is an extremely rare condition, accounting for <0.5% of all lymphomas,[10] with limited case numbers making epidemiological features like incidence unknown. Past reports indicate a median onset age of 46 years, with a slight male predominance. HS can form extranodal lesions beyond the lymph nodes, including reports of the gastrointestinal tract, spleen, soft tissues, and skin[1] although intracranial involvement has been rarely reported. In the literature, primary central nervous system HS has predominantly been described in the brain parenchyma or dura mater, mostly presenting as solitary lesions.[2,6,7,19] Given its histopathological resemblance to anaplastic meningioma, differential diagnosis is critical. The tumor cells exhibit abundant eosinophilic or foamy cytoplasm, accompanied by nuclear atypia and frequent mitotic figures.

Immunohistochemical staining is crucial for diagnosis, with at least one histiocytic marker (CD68, CD163, CD204, or lysozyme) being positive.[8] Negative markers include Langerhans cell markers (CD1a), follicular dendritic cell markers (CD21, CD35), myeloid cell markers (CD34, myeloperoxidase), T-cell markers (CD3), and B-cell markers (CD20). In this case, initial HE staining showed a dense interlacing of polygonal to spindle cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, map-like necrosis with pseudopalisading, and a high Ki-67 proliferation index of 60%, all of which morphologically resembled anaplastic meningioma. Despite this initial diagnosis, the intraoperative findings showed minimal adhesion between the tumor and dura mater, and immunohistochemical staining was negative for EMA. While it is recognized that some high-grade meningiomas exhibit reduced EMA expression, this finding should have prompted greater diagnostic caution. The combination of EMA negativity, a high Ki-67 index, and a lack of dural adhesion constitutes a significant clinical red flag, even when classic radiological features like the dural tail sign strongly suggest a meningioma. However, our diagnostic process was prematurely concluded because this classic radiological presentation and PgR positivity introduced a strong diagnostic bias toward meningioma. In retrospect, these atypical features should have immediately triggered an extended immunohistochemical panel to rule out nonmeningothelial mimics such as HS. The presence of the dural tail sign typically strongly suggests meningioma. However, in our case, despite this radiological feature, intraoperative findings revealed minimal dural adherence. This discrepancy suggests that the dural tail in HS may represent reactive dural changes rather than direct invasion, serving as a potential diagnostic pitfall. This diagnostic complexity is echoed in recent literature. For instance, while primary intracranial HS can present with marked leptomeningeal involvement that strongly mimics meningioma,[20] true anaplastic meningiomas can atypically present entirely within the brain parenchyma without any dural attachment or dural tail sign.[9] These overlapping and sometimes paradoxical imaging features underscore that relying solely on radiological signs, such as the dural tail, can be deceptive, further emphasizing the need for comprehensive immunohistochemical evaluation. In our case, additional staining performed during the surgery for recurrence revealed positive CD68 and CD163, confirming the HS diagnosis.

Therapeutically, GTR has been identified as a key prognostic factor in solitary lesions. Radiotherapy is frequently employed postoperatively, while the efficacy of chemotherapy remains uncertain.[3,7,19] Recent studies have reported programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L)1/PD-L2 expression and actionable mutations such as PDGFRA in select cases, suggesting potential roles for immune checkpoint inhibitors and targeted therapies.[7] In this case, the delayed diagnosis hindered the implementation of postoperative therapy, affecting the prognosis negatively. Given the rarity and diagnostic complexity of intracranial HS, it is imperative to include HS in the differential diagnosis of atypical intracranial tumors. Immunohistochemical staining with histiocytic markers remains a cornerstone for accurate identification and appropriate therapeutic planning.

CONCLUSION

Intracranial HS is an extremely rare entity that can radiologically mimic meningioma, including the presence of the dural tail sign. However, clinicians should be aware that the dural tail in HS may represent reactive changes rather than tumor invasion, as evidenced by the lack of strong dural adhesion intraoperatively in our case. A discrepancy between benign-appearing imaging features and a rapid, aggressive clinical course warrants immediate suspicion of nonmeningothelial malignancies. In such scenarios, a comprehensive immunohistochemical panel including CD68 and CD163 is mandatory to differentiate HS from anaplastic meningioma or SFTs, ensuring accurate diagnosis and timely multimodal treatment.
