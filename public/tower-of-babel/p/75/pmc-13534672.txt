Circulating tumor-associated autoantibody signatures for diagnosis and prognosis in small-cell lung cancer and lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Chaoqi Liu, Eriko Fukuda, Yoji Sagiya, Hiromi Tsuru, Yuka Okamoto, Takayuki Morisaki, Yoichiro Kamatani, Yukinori Okada, Yutaka Suzuki, Akinori Kanai, Naoki Goshima, Chizu Tanikawa, Koichi Matsuda, the BioBank Japan Project
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTumour-associated autoantibodies (TAAbs) are promising biomarkers for cancer detection, but their induction and clinical relevance in lung cancer remain unclear.MethodsSerum samples from 695 individuals were analysed for TAAb profiling by protein-array screening and two-stage ELISA validation. Diagnostic models were constructed with identified TAAbs and compared with conventional tumour markers. Potential mechanisms, clinical and prognostic features of TAAb seropositivity were analysed and its presence in prediagnostic sera was evaluated to assess the potential for early detection.ResultsSix TAAbs for small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and four for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) were identified, demonstrating excellent diagnostic performance (AUC > 0.8) and outperforming ProGRP and CEA. TAAb induction correlated with antigen overexpression, somatic mutations and HLA class II amino acid polymorphisms. TAAb panel seropositivity was associated with older age and advanced stage in both subtypes, and predicted poor survival in SCLC but a favourable outcome in advanced LUAD. In prediagnostic sera, the TAAb concentration increased progressively, with detectability up to 2 years before clinical diagnosis.ConclusionsDistinct TAAb panels were identified for SCLC and LUAD, serving as accurate diagnostic markers that enable early detection and as indicators of prognosis in different clinical contexts.Subject terms: Tumour biomarkers, Lung cancer

Background

Lung cancer has the highest global burden in terms of incidence and cancer-related mortality. In 2022, it caused over 2.48 million new cases and 1.81 million deaths, representing 12.4% of all new cancers and 18.7% of cancer-related deaths [1]. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common subtype, accounting for approximately 39% of newly diagnosed lung cancer cases in males and 57% in females [2]. Small cell lung cancer (SCLC), a highly aggressive neuroendocrine malignancy, accounts for approximately 10–15% of all lung cancers [2, 3]. In LUAD, the 5-year relative survival rate is 73.7% for localised disease but decreases to 13.2% in distant stages [4]. Similarly, in SCLC, the 5-year survival rate is 60% for stage I and 42% for stage II, compared with 19% for stage III and 3% for stage IV [5], underscoring the importance of early diagnosis. Low-dose CT screening has been shown to reduce lung cancer-related mortality by 20% after three rounds of screening [6] and is recommended for high-risk individuals defined by age and smoking history. However, these criteria may exclude many at-risk individuals [6]. Moreover, low-dose CT screening has been associated with substantial healthcare costs, including an additional cost of $1631 per person compared with no screening [7], as well as a high false-positive rate and unnecessary follow-up procedures [8, 9]. Therefore, minimally invasive, cost-effective biomarkers with high diagnostic accuracy remain an unmet clinical need.

Abnormal protein expressions are frequently observed during tumour development. Such proteins may be directly recognised as cell surface antigens or, once released into the circulation or tumour microenvironment, can be recognised by the host immune system and elicit an immune response. These proteins are termed tumour-associated antigens (TAAs) [10]. In response to TAAs, B cells can produce high-affinity tumour-associated autoantibodies (TAAbs), which occurs during the early stage of cancer, even before clinical or radiological evidence appears [6, 9, 10]. Compared with protein biomarkers, TAAbs are relatively stable in circulation [9] and resistant to routine handling, including multiple freeze‒thaw cycles [10] and can be readily detected using standard immunoassays [11], making them promising biomarkers for early and minimally invasive cancer detection.

Many TAAbs, individually or in panels, have been evaluated for their ability to discriminate patients with lung cancer from noncancer populations [8, 9]. TAAb panels consistently outperform single TAAbs largely because of tumour heterogeneity, yet their sensitivity remains only moderate despite high specificity [9]. The most widely studied commercial assay, the EarlyCDT®-Lung test, utilises a seven-TAAb panel (CAGE, GBU4-5, HuD, MAGEA4, NY-ESO-1, p53 and SOX2) to identify high-risk individuals for low-dose CT screening [12]. Owing to the low sensitivity of this test (8.4–33%) [12–14], further exploration of TAAb panels is needed to improve the overall diagnostic accuracy, particularly sensitivity.

Protein arrays immobilise purified recombinant proteins onto a solid surface, followed by incubation with serum samples to detect antigen‒antibody interactions. By simultaneously displaying thousands of proteins, protein arrays enable comprehensive TAAb profiling using small serum volumes (~100 μL per sample). The comprehensive wet protein array (CWPA), which contains 12,946 human proteins [15], preserves proteins in a wet state throughout fabrication, storage and use, thereby maintaining their native conformations [16]. Protein loading is highly uniform, with over 98% of proteins detected above the threshold across multiple fabrication batches. The amount of immobilised protein varies within an approximately 47‒fold range, markedly narrower than the variability in traditional cell-based systems, where protein abundance can differ by ten orders of magnitude or more, improving the sensitivity and coverage of TAAb screening.

In this study, CWPA was employed to screen for TAAbs in serum samples. Candidate TAAbs were subsequently validated in two independent cohorts by ELISA. In addition to diagnostic evaluation, mechanisms of TAAb induction as well as their clinical associations and potential for early detection were examined. This multiphase approach aimed not only to identify TAAb as diagnostic biomarkers but also to clarify their clinical relevance and application.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted in three phases: screening, validation and post-discovery analysis (Fig. 1). In the screening phase, serum samples (60 healthy controls [HC], 50 SCLC and 50 LUAD) were analysed using the CWPA. To further evaluate cancer-type specificity, additional serum samples from patients with pancreatic cancer (PC), biliary tract cancer (BTC), endometrial cancer (EC) and cervical cancer (CC) were also included in the protein array screening. Every ten samples in each group were randomly pooled to generate one serum-mixture set, yielding six pooled sets for HC and five for each cancer type. Candidate TAAbs were selected based on the tumour-to-normal (T/N) index and the number of positive pooled sets. In the validation phase, the first validation cohort included 290 individual samples (70 HC, 45 SCLC and 175 LUAD). To assess the diagnostic performance of TAAbs against nonmalignant conditions, the second validation cohort included 90 pulmonary fibrosis (PF) samples, along with 70 HC, 45 SCLC and 200 LUAD samples. TAAbs were evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. A subset of samples used for CWPA screening was also used in the ELISA validation to ensure consistency. Finally, a combined dataset of 672 samples from both validation cohorts was used for model construction and further analysis.

Serum samples from HC were obtained at the Institute of Medical Science, the University of Tokyo. Patient samples, including PF, SCLC, LUAD and four non-lung cancer types, were obtained from BioBank Japan (BBJ), with cancer cases confirmed histologically. Protein array

Serum mixture sets were diluted 1:100 and applied to the CWPA. Bound autoantibodies were detected with anti-human IgG Alexa647 (1:1000 dilution; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A-21445). The fluorescence signals were scanned using a Typhoon FLA9500 biomolecular imager (Cytiva). Human IgG, expressed from AK097356 cDNA using a wheat germ cell-free system, was serially diluted in PBS (1:2, 1:4, 1:8 and 1:16) and used as a semiquantitative reference. Signal intensities of serum mixtures were calibrated against this dilution series, with semiquantitative values assigned accordingly (Table S1). A set was defined as positive when its semiquantitative value was ≥1. To assess the relative signal intensity, the T/N index was calculated for each cancer group (n = 50 per cancer group; n = 60 for the healthy group) as follows:

To avoid division errors, values of 0 were converted to 0.1, while all other values remained unchanged.

Candidate TAAbs were selected based on the following criteria: (i) ≥2 positive sets in the cancer group and (ii) a T/N index ≥10.

ELISA

For candidates with reproducible signals in the protein array, ELISA validation was performed using individual serum samples. Seven recombinant proteins (CTAG1B [Cat# H00246100-P01], GST [Cat# P0001], IRX2 [Cat# H00153572-P01], p53 [Cat# H00007157-P02], SOX2 [Cat# H00006657-P01], TNNI3 [Cat# H00007137-P01] and XAGE1B [Cat# H00009503-P01]) were purchased from Abnova Corporation, and four proteins (DDX53, NUFIP2, PHF21B and ZIC4) were synthesised in vitro. Briefly, entry clones of target antigens from a human protein expression resource [17] were converted into expression clones via Gateway LR recombination, fused with N-terminal FLAG-GST tags and expressed using WEPRO®7240G wheat germ extract (CellFree Sciences, Cat# CFS-WGE-7240G).

Recombinant proteins were diluted in carbonate–bicarbonate buffer (0.05 μg/mL) and coated onto 96-well plates together with serially diluted human IgG (Sigma‒Aldrich, Cat# I4506) standards to generate calibration curves. After being blocked with 5% skim milk in PBST (0.05% Tween-20 in PBS), the plates were incubated with serum samples (1:50 dilution) for 1.5 h. Bound antibodies were detected using HRP-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (1:2000 dilution; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A18811) and developed with TMB substrate (R&D Systems, Cat# DY999). The reaction was stopped with 2 N sulphuric acid, and the absorbance was measured at 450 and 570 nm. The final optical density (OD) was calculated as OD₄₅₀ – OD₅₇₀ – OD_blank. TAAb concentrations were determined from the IgG standard curve and adjusted for a 50-fold serum dilution.

Anti-GST autoantibody concentrations were also measured. Samples with concentrations exceeding the mean ± 2 standard deviations were excluded from ELISA measurements targeting TAAbs, resulting in the exclusion of six HC, six PF, one SCLC and ten LUAD cases across the two validation cohorts. Because all recombinant proteins carried a GST tag, anti-GST autoantibody concentrations were subtracted from the final TAAb concentrations to minimise background interference.

Candidate TAAbs were selected based on an area under the curve (AUC) > 0.6 and p < 0.05 in the ROC analysis [18], where statistical significance indicates that the AUC is significantly greater than 0.5.

Multivariable-adjusted analysis

Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between individual TAAbs and cancer risk. Cancer status was treated as the dependent variable, and TAAb levels were entered as continuous variables, with sampling age (age at serum collection), sex and smoking status (when available) included as covariates. Prior treatment history was defined as any cancer-directed therapy received before serum collection, including surgery, chemotherapy and radiotherapy.

Diagnostic model construction and evaluation

Diagnostic models were developed using logistic regression. HC and PF controls were combined into a noncancer group and compared with SCLC or LUAD patients to establish a TAAb-panel model. In subsets with available TAA data, the performance of the TAAb-panel model was re-evaluated, and TAA-panel as well as combined models (TAAbs + TAAs) were constructed. Only TAAs that were significantly elevated in cancer compared with benign controls were included in model construction. In subsets with available tumour marker (ProGRP for SCLC and CEA for LUAD) data, comparisons among tumour markers, TAAb-panel and combined models (tumour marker + TAAb-panel) were performed. Diagnostic performance was assessed by ROC analysis, with comparisons performed using the DeLong test.

Bioinformatic analysis

For LUAD, RNA sequencing and mutation data were obtained from TCGA-LUAD, and for SCLC, normalised data were retrieved from the GEO dataset GSE60052.

TAA expression analysis

A subset of serum samples used in the validation stage, including 57 SCLC, 114 LUAD and 83 PF patients, was analysed using the Olink Explore HT panel (Olink Proteomics) to detect TAA expression. All the samples passed the quality control procedures. Protein expression levels are reported as normalised protein expression (NPX) values on a log2 scale. Group differences were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

HLA genotypes

High-resolution imputed HLA genotype data were obtained from the BBJ genome-wide association study data and HLA imputation using the Japanese reference panel [19]. Given their key role in autoantibody production and immune regulation, the analysis focused on HLA class II genes. Logistic regression models were constructed for each polymorphic amino acid position, with TAAb status (positive vs negative) as the outcome and principal components plus relevant clinical variables as covariates. An omnibus likelihood ratio test compared a full model (all residues at a given position) with a covariate-only null model to assess overall associations. Significance was set as p < 2.22 × 10⁻⁴ (Bonferroni correction: 0.05/225 amino acid positions). For positions with significant omnibus results, the effects of individual residues were further examined in separate logistic regression models.

Survival analysis

The prognostic impact of TAAb seropositivity was evaluated using overall survival, defined as the interval from clinical diagnosis to the last follow-up or death. Survival differences were assessed by Kaplan–Meier analysis. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were fitted, adjusting for age at diagnosis, TNM stage and smoking status. Analyses were conducted in R using the survival, survminer and forestmodel packages.

Statistical analysis

To enable within-cohort comparisons, TAAb concentrations were standardised by Z-score transformation. Group differences were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. For each TAAb, the cutoff was defined as the 95th percentile of the noncancer group (HC + PF). A sample was classified as TAAb-panel negative only when all TAAbs were below their cutoff values; otherwise, it was considered TAAb-panel positive. Associations between the TAAb panel status and clinicopathological features were analysed using Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (two-sided).

Results

Screening of candidate TAAbs

Eight candidate TAAbs for SCLC and four for LUAD were selected, with two (anti-p53 and anti-DDX53) shared between both subtypes (Table 2). To evaluate the cancer-type specificity of candidate TAAbs, four non-lung cancer types were included in the screening phase. Although four individual TAAbs (anti-p53, anti-DDX53, anti-CTAG1B and anti-IRX2) showed cross-reactivity in non-lung cancers, the overall TAAb panels remained relatively enriched in SCLC or LUAD compared with the other cancer types. Furthermore, the TAAb panels identified for SCLC and LUAD differed in TAAb compositions, indicating a degree of subtype discrimination within lung cancer.

Two-stage validation of candidate TAAbs

In SCLC, serum levels of seven TAAbs (anti-CTAG1B, anti-DDX53, anti-NUFIP2, anti-p53, anti-SOX2, anti-TNNI3 and anti-ZIC4) were significantly higher than those in HC (all p < 0.05; Fig. S1a), with fold changes (FC) ranging from 1.21 to 1.54 (Table S2). In addition, these TAAbs had an AUC ranging from 0.624 (95% CI: 0.512–0.735) to 0.760 (95% CI: 0.667–0.852), with sensitivities of 18.2–29.5% and specificities of 92.4–97.0% (Fig. S1c, Table 3). Despite an AUC > 0.6, anti-PHF21B did not reach statistical significance in ROC analysis (p = 0.052), indicating no significant discrimination beyond random classification, and was therefore excluded from the second validation. In LUAD, four TAAbs (anti-DDX53, anti-IRX2, anti-p53 and anti-XAGE1B) were significantly elevated compared with HC (all p < 0.01; Fig. S1b), with FCs of 1.22–1.57 (Table S2). AUCs ranged from 0.612 (95% CI: 0.541–0.683) to 0.748 (95% CI: 0.679–0.817) (Fig. S1d, Table 3).

In the second validation, serum levels of six TAAbs (anti-CTAG1B, anti-DDX53, anti-NUFIP2, anti-p53, anti-SOX2 and anti-TNNI3) were significantly elevated in SCLC compared with noncancer samples (all p < 0.05; Fig. S2a). FCs ranged from 1.22 to 1.50 (Table S2), and the corresponding AUCs ranged from 0.623 (95% CI: 0.513–0.733) to 0.733 (95% CI: 0.645–0.821) (Fig. S2c, Table 3). Anti-ZIC4, however, showed no diagnostic value (AUC = 0.519, 95% CI: 0.411–0.627, p = 0.701) and was excluded from the finalised SCLC panel. In LUAD, all four TAAbs (anti-DDX53, anti-IRX2, anti-p53 and anti-XAGE1B) were significantly upregulated (all p < 0.05; Fig. S2b), with FCs of 1.20–1.55 (Table S2). The corresponding AUCs ranged from 0.614 (95% CI: 0.555–0.673) to 0.734 (95% CI: 0.681–0.786) (Fig. S2d, Table 3).

Independent associations of TAAbs with cancer risk

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate whether the associations between TAAbs and cancer risk were independent of epidemiological risk factors. When comparing SCLC patients with HC, all six TAAbs remained significantly associated with increased SCLC risk after adjustment for sampling age and sex, with adjusted ORs of 3.12–6.21 (all p < 0.05) (Table S3). Similar results were observed for LUAD, with adjusted ORs of 2.55–7.69. To further address potential confounding by smoking, the analysis was restricted to cancer patients and benign controls for whom smoking information was available. After multivariable adjustment for sampling age, sex and smoking status, all TAAbs remained significantly associated with cancer risk, with adjusted ORs ranging from 1.94 to 4.45 (all p < 0.01) (Table S4). These findings indicate that the observed relationships between TAAbs and cancer risk were independent of sampling age, sex and smoking status.

To further evaluate whether prior cancer treatment might influence these associations, an exploratory analysis restricted to treatment-naïve cancer patients was conducted. Given the limited number of treatment-naïve SCLC cases (n = 2), analyses were performed only in treatment-naïve LUAD patients (n = 12). When compared with HC (n = 134), three of the four TAAbs remained significantly associated with LUAD risk after adjustment for sampling age and sex, whereas anti-IRX2 showed borderline significance (p = 0.051) (Table S5). When compared with PF (n = 84) with additional adjustment for smoking status, all four TAAbs remained significantly associated with LUAD risk (Table S6), with ORs of 2.69–9.77. These findings suggest that the observed associations between TAAbs and cancer risk are unlikely to be influenced by prior cancer treatment.

Construction of diagnostic models

The TAAb-panel model demonstrated good diagnostic performance in the full dataset, with an AUC of 0.852 (95% CI: 0.802–0.903), sensitivity of 73.0%, and specificity of 85.8% for SCLC, and an AUC of 0.806 (95% CI: 0.770–0.842), sensitivity of 80.8% and specificity of 66.5% for LUAD. These values are comparable to previously reported TAAb-panel diagnostic models, which have shown AUCs of 0.640–0.813, with sensitivities of 40.8–68.9% and specificities of 57.9–90.5% [18, 32–34].

To further evaluate the diagnostic relevance of circulating TAAs, serum levels of these proteins were compared between cancer and PF controls. In SCLC, CTAG1B, DDX53 and TNNI3 were significantly elevated in cancer patients, whereas in LUAD, only DDX53 showed a significant increase compared with PF (Fig. S3). Therefore, these TAAs were selected for subsequent model construction. To further determine whether TAAbs provide diagnostic value beyond circulating TAAs, the diagnostic performance of TAAb-only, TAA-only and combined TAAb + TAA models was compared (Table S7). In SCLC, the TAAb panel (AUC = 0.815) outperformed the TAA panel model (AUC = 0.726), while the combination further improved performance (AUC = 0.873). Similarly, in LUAD, the TAAb panel (AUC = 0.876) showed significantly higher performance than the TAA model (AUC = 0.536), and the combined model achieved a modest improvement (AUC = 0.882).

The diagnostic performance of the TAAb panel was then compared with conventional tumour markers (Table 4, Fig. 2). In SCLC, the TAAb panel (AUC = 0.834) markedly outperformed ProGRP (AUC = 0.621, p < 0.001). The addition of the TAAb panel significantly improved the diagnostic performance of ProGRP (AUC = 0.846, p < 0.001 vs. ProGRP only). Similarly, in LUAD, the TAAb panel (AUC = 0.804) showed markedly superior performance compared with CEA (AUC = 0.493, p < 0.001). The addition of the TAAb panel to CEA significantly enhanced its diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.835, p < 0.001 vs. CEA only). These findings indicate that TAAb-based models outperform conventional tumour markers, and that integrating TAAbs can substantially improve the diagnostic performance of existing biomarkers.

Expression and mutational features of TAAs

Serum expression of TAAs

In SCLC, CTAG1B (log2FC = 1.86, p = 0.012), DDX53 (log2FC = 0.50, p = 0.005), p53 (log2FC = 0.77, p = 0.017), SOX2 (log2FC = 0.48, p = 0.018) and TNNI3 (log2FC = 1.52, p = 0.023) exhibited significantly higher serum expression in TAAb-positive patients compared with TAAb-negative patients, whereas NUFIP2 showed a borderline increase (log2FC = 0.79, p = 0.051) (Fig. 3a, Table S8). In LUAD, the serum p53 level was significantly elevated in TAAb-positive patients (log2FC = 0.70, p = 0.008), whereas the DDX53 level tended to increase (log2FC = 0.27, p = 0.114) (Fig. 3b, Table S8).

Transcriptomic expression and mutation frequency

In SCLC, SOX2 (FC = 1.96, p = 0.015) and TNNI3 (FC = 1.11, p = 0.017) were significantly upregulated, whereas other genes showed nonsignificant trends (Fig. S4a, Table S9). In LUAD, DDX53, TP53 and XAGE1B were markedly overexpressed (all p < 0.001; Fig. S4b). In contrast, cross-subtype analyses revealed the absence of such overexpression in the other subtype: SCLC-specific TAAs (CTAG1B, NUFIP2, SOX2 and TNNI3) were not elevated in LUAD (Fig. S4c).

Somatic mutation analysis confirmed frequent TP53 alterations (~50% of cases) in LUAD, whereas other TAAs (IRX2, DDX53 and XAGE1B) had low mutation rates (~3%) (Fig. S4d).

Associations between TAAb seropositivity and HLA class II amino acid polymorphisms

Omnibus tests revealed significant associations for three SCLC-related TAAbs: anti-DDX53, anti-p53 and anti-TNNI3 (Fig. 4). The representative residues included HLA-DRB1 position 71 (anti-DDX53), HLA-DPB1 position 55 (anti-p53) and multiple sites at HLA-DRB1 positions −25, −16, 11, 13, 26, 28 and 140 (anti-TNNI3) (Table S10).

Associations between TAAb seropositivity and clinical characteristics

The TAAb status of each cancer sample was visualised using a heatmap (Fig. S5). In SCLC, seropositivity for anti-CTAG1B (p = 0.019), anti-p53 (p = 0.032) and anti-SOX2 (p = 0.009) correlated with older sampling age (>60 years), and anti-SOX2 seropositivity was also enriched in advanced-stage disease (p = 0.030) (Table 5). In LUAD, anti-XAGE1B seropositivity was linked to older age (p = 0.013), whereas anti-DDX53 (p = 0.009) and anti-IRX2 (p = 0.014) seropositivity were enriched in advanced-stage patients. Across both subtypes, TAAb panel seropositivity was significantly associated with older sampling age (p = 0.004 for SCLC and p = 0.034 for LUAD) and advanced stage (p = 0.021 for SCLC and p = 0.014 for LUAD). In addition, the number of positive TAAbs (TAAb load) increased with sampling age in both subtypes (Fig. S6a, c), whereas the proportion of advanced-stage patients increased with TAAb load only in LUAD (Fig. S6b, d).

Association between TAAb seropositivity and survival

In SCLC, TAAb panel seropositivity was associated with significantly worse overall survival (log-rank p = 0.015; Fig. 5a) and remained an independent prognostic factor in multivariable Cox analysis (HR = 2.64, p = 0.021; Fig. 5b). Smoking status was independently associated with poorer prognosis (HR = 5.50, p = 0.026). In LUAD, however, TAAb seropositivity showed no significant impact on survival in either Kaplan–Meier analysis (log-rank p = 0.593; Fig. 5c) or multivariable Cox regression (HR = 0.85, p = 0.308; Fig. 5d). Older age (>60 years) (HR = 1.49, p = 0.020), advanced stage (HR = 2.40, p < 0.001) and smoking (HR = 1.81, p < 0.001) were independently associated with poorer prognosis.

Stage-stratified analyses were performed to further evaluate whether the prognostic effect of TAAb seropositivity differed according to stage. Considering the relatively small sample size of SCLC, these analyses were conducted only for LUAD. TAAb seropositivity showed no prognostic impact in early-stage LUAD (Fig. S7a, b), whereas among advanced-stage patients, TAAb seropositivity was independently associated with better overall survival (HR = 0.54, p = 0.037; Fig. S7c, d), highlighting the stage-dependent roles of humoral immune responses in lung cancer progression.

Elevation of TAAb levels in prediagnostic serum samples

Serial serum samples from fifteen TAAb-positive patients (ten SCLC and five LUAD patients), including some collected up to 44 months before diagnosis, were analysed. TAAb levels frequently increased as the time of diagnosis approached, indicating a progressive elevation of humoral immune responses during tumour development. In SCLC, the proportion of patients with TAAb-panel seropositivity gradually increased from 0% at 36 months before diagnosis to 67% within the final 12 months and 80% in the last 6 months before diagnosis (Fig. 6a). In LUAD, a similar pattern was observed, although the increase occurred more abruptly during the final year before diagnosis (Fig. 6b). Overall, 67% of patients were already TAAb-panel positive at least 6 months before clinical diagnosis, and several individuals maintained detectable autoantibody responses for more than 2 years (Fig. S8). These longitudinal findings indicate that TAAb responses can arise months to years before clinical diagnosis, highlighting their potential value for early cancer detection.

Discussion

In this study, we used a high-throughput protein array with two-stage ELISA validation to systematically identify TAAbs, leading to the discovery of six TAAbs for SCLC and four for LUAD. This work provides a comprehensive evaluation of TAAb panels as diagnostic biomarkers, together with analyses of their induction mechanisms, clinical correlates, prognostic significance and longitudinal dynamics.

Among the eight validated TAAbs, anti-IRX2, anti-NUFIP2 and anti-TNNI3 autoantibodies were newly identified. Their corresponding antigens, however, have been investigated in cancer biology and immune regulation. IRX2 is involved in early lung development during mouse embryogenesis [35], and its DNA hypermethylation is associated with aggressiveness and unfavourable clinical outcomes in LUAD [36]. TNNI3, a regulator of myocardial contraction, is overexpressed in lung cancer [37]. NUFIP2 has emerged as an immune regulator [38], with reported roles in immune cell function in autoimmune diseases [39] as well as in tumour-associated immune responses in pancreatic cancer [40].

The induction of TAAbs is likely driven by multiple factors. TAA overexpression may increase immune exposure, as most TAAs exhibited elevated mRNA and protein levels in their respective tumours. In addition, genetic alterations may enhance immunogenicity. TP53 is mutated in approximately 50% of LUAD cases, with nearly half being missense mutations. Mutant p53 proteins are relatively stable and tend to accumulate, increasing the likelihood of immunogenic epitope presentation [41, 42]. Furthermore, specific TAAbs were significantly associated with polymorphic residues in HLA class II molecules, six (DPB1 position 55, DRB1 positions 11, 13, 26, 28 and 71) of which are located within antigen-binding grooves [43–47]. Such variation may alter the peptide-binding repertoire and presentation efficiency, thereby modulating immune recognition and humoral response. Collectively, these findings indicate that antigen expression, somatic alterations and host HLA variation may jointly contribute to TAAb induction.

For both subtypes, the TAAb-based diagnostic models achieved strong performance (AUC > 0.8), with markedly higher sensitivity than the EarlyCDT®-Lung test (>70% vs. ~33%). Several factors may account for this difference. First, four TAAbs identified in this study (anti-IRX2, anti-NUFIP2, anti-TNNI3 and anti-XAGE1B) were not included in the EarlyCDT®-Lung test. Although few studies have investigated these TAAbs in lung cancer, their antigens have been implicated in tumorigenesis and tumour-associated immune responses in other malignancies, as discussed above. Notably, our TAAbs were identified through an unbiased, proteome-wide screening using subtype-specific sera, whereas the EarlyCDT®-Lung panel was constructed primarily based on known TAAs that can also be detected in other malignancies or autoimmune conditions [14, 48]. Second, ethnic differences in the study populations may also have contributed. Our cohorts were the Japanese population, whereas the EarlyCDT®-Lung panel was developed and validated in Western populations. Distinct genetic backgrounds, including tumour mutation spectra and HLA polymorphisms, may influence the induction of specific TAAbs.

The TAAb load increased with sampling age in both subtypes, which is consistent with previous reports showing a higher TAAb prevalence among elderly individuals [49]. This pattern may reflect immunosenescence, a process characterised by altered B-cell repertoire diversity and increased emergence of autoreactive clones [24, 50]. Such immunological shifts may increase the likelihood of autoantibody production in response to various antigens. In parallel, aging is often accompanied by genomic instability and the accumulation of somatic mutations [51], which may increase the TAA burden and enhance immune recognition. In contrast, associations with tumour stage differed by subtype. In LUAD, the TAAb load increased with the advanced stage, whereas no such trend was observed in SCLC. This discrepancy may reflect differences in immunological heterogeneity across subtypes, as TAAb production largely depends on both TAA exposure and a responsive host immune system. SCLC is characterised by rapid progression and profound immune evasion, which may limit humoral responses despite abundant antigen release. Features such as downregulation of major histocompatibility complex class I molecules, infiltration by immunosuppressive cells and suppression of antigen-presenting cells [52] further hinder B-cell activation and TAAb production, potentially explaining the absence of stage-dependent increases. In contrast, LUAD is considered highly immunogenic [53], and patients with LUAD show durable responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors [54, 55], suggesting that the immune system remains responsive to support TAAb induction.

In SCLC, TAAb panel seropositivity emerged as an independent predictor of poor survival, whereas age and stage were not significant in the multivariable Cox model. These findings suggest that TAAb panel seropositivity may capture prognostic information that overlaps with traditional clinical factors. On the contrary, in LUAD, TAAb seropositivity was not associated with prognosis in the unstratified analysis, while older age, advanced stage and smoking were independently associated with worse outcomes, consistent with previous reports. After stratification by stage, TAAb seropositivity emerged as an independent predictor of improved survival only in the advanced stage. Although TAAb seropositivity was more frequently observed in advanced patients, this association likely reflects immune activation during LUAD progression [56, 57] rather than a direct causal link to poor outcomes. In the advanced stage, patients capable of generating strong autoantibody responses may preserve more responsive anti-tumour immunity [58]. Accordingly, TAAb seropositivity may define an ‘immune-reactive’ subset of advanced LUAD characterised by better prognosis, contrasting with the ‘immune-suppressed’ subset.

Our longitudinal analysis revealed that TAAb seropositivity increased progressively before clinical diagnosis, with detectable responses observed up to several years before diagnosis in some patients. This temporal pattern suggests that humoral immune responses against TAAs may emerge and accumulate during tumour development. Previous studies have similarly reported that circulating TAAbs can be detected in prediagnostic sera up to 2–4 years before lung cancer diagnosis [59, 60], supporting the concept that tumour-associated immune responses may precede clinical detection. However, research investment in cancer prevention and early detection remains relatively limited compared with that devoted to treatment [61], despite their substantial potential for improving survival [4, 5]. In this context, circulating TAAbs may represent promising minimally invasive biomarkers for early detection and risk stratification of lung cancer, providing an additional strategy that may complement imaging-based screening.

This study has several limitations. First, the range of diseases included for evaluating cancer specificity was limited. Although several non-lung cancer types were examined, additional cancer types, other lung cancer subtypes and benign pulmonary diseases were not included. Second, the absence of an independent external validation may cause the possibility of selection bias or population-specific effects. Third, although serum samples were collected through a standardised biobank workflow and uniform laboratory procedures, the potential influence of collection-site variability on TAAb measurements cannot be entirely excluded. Fourth, IRX2 and XAGE1B were not included in the Olink platform, limiting assessment of their circulating levels and related analyses. Future studies incorporating a broader spectrum of cancers and pulmonary conditions, independent multi-centre validation and more comprehensive proteomic platforms are needed to confirm the specificity and reproducibility of these TAAb panels.

In summary, identified TAAbs are promising biomarkers for lung cancer that not only have excellent diagnostic performance but also correlate with age, stage, prognosis and prediagnostic detectability. These findings highlight the potential of TAAbs to connect tumour biology with clinical outcomes and to advance strategies for lung cancer diagnosis and prognosis.
