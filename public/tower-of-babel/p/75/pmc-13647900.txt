Stakeholders’ perceptions on guidelines to prevent abuse of older people in Norway: a qualitative analysis
Authors: Ashley Rebecca Bell-Mizori, Miroslava Tokovska, Anja Botngård, Wenche Karin Malmedal, Vanessa Nolasco Ferreira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbuse of older people is a public health and human rights issue globally, with approximately 1 in 6 older adults experiencing abuse annually. This prevalence highlights the need for robust prevention strategies, even in Norway, despite its comprehensive healthcare systems.ObjectivesThis study explored stakeholders’ perceptions of existing legislation and guidelines, as well as the shortcomings that needs to be addressed to prevent abuse of older people in Norway.MethodsThe exploratory qualitative design employed in-depth and formal natural group interviews with stakeholders and experts in the abuse of older people field.ResultsThrough reflexive thematic analysis, three themes were identified: (1) Stakeholders experience with, and perspective on, abuse of older people; (2) System-level challenges in preventing abuse of older people; and (3) Pathways for systematic improvement and cultural change in preventing abuse of older people.ConclusionStakeholders identified key challenges including ambiguous definitions, complexity of the older adult population, and vulnerability to abuse. Introduction

Abuse and violence against older people are increasingly recognized as a significant global public health and human rights issue, affecting individuals in both community-based and institutional settings [45]. The consequences of abuse of older people extend beyond immediate harm, affecting the quality of life, dignity, and overall well-being of older adults [19, 23, 45]. In Norway, despite having one of the world’s most comprehensive healthcare systems [38], the prevention of abuse of older people remains a complex challenge requiring systemic attention and a coordinated, cross-sectoral response.

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines “elder abuse”, or “abuse of older people”, as “a single or repeated act, or lack of appropriate action, occurring within any relationship where there is an expectation of trust, which causes harm or distress to an older person”; an older person is defined as 60 years and over [44]. abuse of older people may take many forms, including physical, sexual, psychological, financial or material abuse, as well as neglect [44, 45].

Prevalence estimates vary substantially across regions and nations. The most recent and comprehensive global systematic review and meta-analysis of abuse of older people prevalence, conducted by Kitaw et al. [21], synthesized evidence from 94 studies in 35 countries and estimated a pooled global prevalence of 27.6% (95% CI: 23.5–31.6). Psychological and emotional abuse was found to be most prevalent (20.9%), followed by neglect (19.3%), financial/material abuse (11.7%), verbal abuse (11.0%), physical abuse (7.9%), and sexual abuse (1.5%) [21]. Prevalence rates were higher in institutional settings (29.0%, 95% CI: 20.0–38.0) than in community settings (27.0%, 95% CI: 23.0–32.0) [21]. In Norway, Sandmoe et al. [34] conducted a national cross-sectional survey of approximately 2,500 community-dwelling older adults aged 65–90 years, estimating an overall prevalence of abuse of older people ranging from 6.8 to 9.2%. Most perpetrators were relatives or other individuals known to the victims [34]. In institutional settings, Botngård et al. [7] surveyed 3,693 health personnel in 100 Norwegian nursing homes and found that 60.3% of staff self-reported having committed at least one abusive or neglectful act toward a resident during the preceding year.

abuse of older people is a multifactorial phenomenon arising from the interaction of individual, relational, institutional, and societal factors, requiring multilevel prevention strategies that address risk and protective factors across multiple levels of influence [22, 30, 44]. Ageism, defined as discrimination, prejudice, and stereotypes based on age [43], has been identified as a key contextual factor shaping both the occurrence of abuse of older people and societal responses to it [30, 44]. The WHO identifies ageism as a driver of abuse of older people and frames combating ageist attitudes as a central prevention goal [44]. Research by Burnes et al. [10] demonstrated that interventions targeting ageism can produce small to moderate effects, particularly when combining educational components with meaningful intergenerational contact. Ageing populations and growing dependence on formal and informal caregivers may increase older people’s vulnerability to abuse, particularly within care relationships shaped by power imbalance, dependency, and reduced autonomy [45].

Norway, like many developed nations, faces the challenge of an ageing population—the ageing crisis—with projections indicating that by 2050, approximately 20% of the population will be aged 70 years or older [36]. This demographic shift emphasizes the necessity of establishing robust preventive measures against abuse of older people. While several countries have established specific legislation and guidelines to address abuse of older people, the Norwegian approach to this issue has largely been embedded within broader healthcare and social service frameworks rather than governed by stand-alone national policies [24]. Although existing legal frameworks provide general protections, significant gaps remain in practical implementation, including the absence of systematic risk assessment protocols, clear reporting guidelines, and standardized procedures for responding to suspected cases of abuse of older people [7, 24]. This raises critical questions regarding the adequacy and effectiveness of existing measures, as well as the potential need for more explicit legislation and standardized guidelines. International research highlights the importance of multi-sectoral responses to abuse of older people. Recent studies from Australia demonstrate the critical role of frontline emergency services, with paramedics identified as key actors in detecting and responding to abuse of older people in community settings [14, 28].

Despite the recognition of abuse of older people as a serious issue, there is limited research examining stakeholders’ perceptions on existing legislation and guidelines concerning abuse of older people, including the needs for establishing specific guidelines in Norway [6, 15]. Understanding stakeholders’ perspectives is crucial, as they play vital roles in both identifying cases of abuse and implementing preventive measures. Their insights can provide information about current challenges, systemic gaps, and potential solutions. To analyze these stakeholder dynamics, this study drawn on the Advocacy Coalition Framework (ACF), which examines how policy actors with shared beliefs form coalitions and influence policy outcomes [29, 31]. Accordingly, this study aimed to explore and understand stakeholders’ perceptions of existing legislation and guidelines concerning abuse of older people in Norway, as well as the shortcomings that needs to be addressed to prevent abuse of older people, through the perspectives of relevant stakeholders and experts in the field of abuse of older people. By examining the views of authorities, public bodies, and user representatives, this research sought to contribute to the development of more effective approaches to prevent abuse of older people in Norway. The study was guided by the following research question:

What are stakeholders’ perceptions of existing legislation and guidelines concerning abuse of older people in Norway, and what shortcomings needs to be addressed to prevent the matter?

Advocacy coalition framework

This study employed the ACF as a theoretical lens to analyze the influence of different stakeholders on the development of abuse of older people prevention policies in Norway. The ACF, developed by Sabatier and Jenkins-Smith [31] and revisited by Pierce and Osei-Kojo [29] provides a political science lens for understanding how policy actors form coalitions based on shared beliefs and coordinate their actions to influence policy outcomes. The framework is particularly pertinent for the examination of intricate policy matters such as abuse of older people prevention, where a diverse range of stakeholders—encompassing public authorities, healthcare providers, and user representatives—must navigate competing priorities and beliefs. The ACF analyzes how policy actors operate within three hierarchical belief systems. These are I) deep core beliefs (fundamental values about older people rights and dignity), II) core policy beliefs (positions on prevention approaches and intervention strategies), and III) secondary beliefs (views on specific implementation methods) [31]. These belief systems influence how stakeholders interpret evidence, form coalitions, and advocate for policy solutions.

In the context of abuse of older people prevention in Norway, the ACF framework provides a framework analysis model of how different stakeholders’ beliefs and experiences shape their understanding of the problem and their preferred solutions. Specifically, we analyze why certain prevention approaches gain support while others face resistance and how coalitions of stakeholders might shape policy proposals.

Method

This qualitative exploratory study, which uses a hermeneutic phenomenological approach, conducted interviews via both in-depth individual interviews and formal natural group interviews with relevant stakeholders [18]. The aim was to understand the phenomenon of abuse of older people in relation to existing legislation and guidelines in Norway including identifying shortcomings as a preventative measure.

Sample and recruitment

The research group aimed to recruit stakeholders (from public authorities and bodies or user representatives) who had experience in the field of abuse of older people through purposive sampling [11]. Relevant stakeholders were recruited by contacting the research team either through phone or e-mail and were informed verbally about the objectives of the research and the content and requirement of consent. Before the start of each interview, the consent form was presented to each participant. Upon acceptance, written consent (physically or digitally) was conducted. Some participants were later recruited through snowball sampling after recommendation from other participants. While purposive and snowball sampling enabled recruitment of stakeholders with relevant expertise and experience, these strategies have limitations [18]. Purposive sampling may introduce selection bias as participants are selected based on specific criteria, potentially excluding diverse perspectives. Snowball sampling may compound this by recruiting participants from similar networks, potentially overrepresenting stakeholders with stronger opinions or greater availability. To mitigate these limitations, recruitment was continued across different stakeholder groups until data saturation was achieved, ensuring no new themes emerged from additional interviews. The time and place were scheduled for those who agreed to participate, making the interview format formal [18].

All participants were above the age of 18 and were currently, or had previously been, working on the topic of abuse of older people. Owing to the goal of interviewing relevant stakeholders and experts in the field, and ensuring that participants’ identities are not compromised, participants’ gender and age will not be reported. The participants were instead classified as either individuals representing public authorities or bodies (n = 7 groups, 10 participants) or user representatives (n = 4 groups, 5 participants). Through recruitment, participants who were willing to join the study were invited to bring relevant colleagues. In the end, four participants choose to bring one colleague to their interview, making the total number of participants N = 15 divided across 11 interviews (n = 7 in-depth interviews and n = 4 formal natural group interviews). The advantage of using natural groups is that knowledge about a certain topic is easier to obtain by witnessing social interactions between participants [18]. In addition, most of the participants who brought a colleague argued that having a peer with shared knowledge present was helpful, as they could help fill in gaps that they themselves might have forgotten.

Data collection and materials

An open-ended semi-structured interview guide, developed exclusively for this research, was used and designed by the entire research team. All interviews were conducted by researchers experienced with qualitative research and performed between May 2023 and January 2024. The majority (n = 9) of the interviews were held by ARB, one held by AB, and one held by AB and WKM together. Four interviews were held physically, and the rest were held digitally over Zoom. The interviews lasted between 20 and 85 min. The average length of the audio recordings for the in-depth interviews was 45 min and 57 s (SD = 22 min and 10 s), whereas the average length for the group interviews was 65 min and 11 s (SD = 15 min and 30 s). The difference in length reflects the variety of participants and their levels of knowledge applicable to the topic; longer interviews usually hold more relevant insights that can be used in the data analysis than shorter interviews. The interviews were recorded on a secure online service for recording and storing research data called Nettskjema – Dictaphone.

The interviews conducted before July 2023 were transcribed verbatim manually by a research assistant. In July, Nettskjema implemented an automatic transcription service within their system, which resulted in most of our interviews being automatically transcribed, followed by a manual review. All transcripts were read thoroughly (all research members) and controlled (ARB, MT & WKM). Statements were later translated to English from Norwegian and slightly modified to improve readability (ARB). English translation has been verified by all the researchers.

Data analysis

Reflexive thematic analysis, as developed by Braun and Clarke [9] was employed to identify and examine themes that captured stakeholders’ perspectives on preventing abuse of older people in Norway. Thematic analysis is a data analysis method for identifying and reporting patterns within data, and reflexive thematic analysis is further suitable for research questions concerning views, beliefs and opinions, such as those in our study. Hermeneutics is a research design that emphasizes understanding the entire text before breaking it down into parts. When combined, reflexive thematic analysis and hermeneutic design provide the opportunity to understand the meanings, perspectives and perceptions that emerged from the data [8, 9, 35, 46]. Owing to the increased flexibility with reflexive thematic analysis, compared with regular thematic analysis, the steps do not have to be rigorously followed, and it can be combined with other approaches, such as hermeneutic designs, as we did. Even so, the methodological framework facilitated the in-depth analysis of the qualitative data through the following steps: (1) data familiarization—reading transcripts (ARB, MT, VNF & WKM); (2) data coding (ARB and MT); (3) generating initial themes (ARB and MT); (4) reviewing and developing themes (ARB, MT, VNF & WKM); (5) combining, defining and naming themes (VNF & WKM); and (5) writing the original draft—this step included developing Table 11 (ARB, MT & VNF).

Overview of themes

The research team’s expertise, comprising backgrounds in health psychology sciences (ARB), clinical psychologists specializing in health psychology and public health (VNF), nursing sciences (AB & WKM), and social sciences and public health (MT), facilitated a comprehensive analytical approach to examining the complexities of abuse of older people prevention and policy development. The researchers remained consciously aware of their positionality and preconceptions throughout the analytical processes, particularly regarding their prior involvement with abuse of older people cases and policy work. The adoption of a reflexive perspective acknowledges that stakeholders’ experiences and views can be understood through their narratives while recognizing researchers’ role in knowledge construction. This consideration proved particularly significant given the sensitive nature of abuse of older people and the diverse professional and organizational contexts represented in the sample. Maintaining reflexivity throughout the analytical process with thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke [9] guidelines was crucial, especially when various stakeholder perceptions on guidelines and several identified shortcomings were interpreted to prevent abuse of older people in the Norwegian context.

Ethics statement

The participants were informed both in writing and verbally about their voluntary rights, how their personal data would be stored, and how to request the deletion of their personal data. All the data were deidentified, and the participants were allocated pseudonyms during the transcription stage. Voice recordings and signed informed consent forms were stored in Nettskjema, which adheres to the general data protection regulations (GDPR). The Norwegian Agency for Shared Services (SIKT) in Education and Research (reference 910786) approved the study.

Results

The participants, further classified as stakeholders, in the study worked in various institutions within the field of abuse of older people, and since the focus was on their own experiences and observations from working in the field, different elements related to legislation and policy guidelines were reported. Using Sabatier and Jenkins-Smith [31] ACF, the findings were organized into three main aspects: deep core beliefs, policy core beliefs, and secondary beliefs. These categories provided a structure for interpreting shared values, perceived systemic challenges, and proposed practical solutions. From this foundation, three main themes emerged (see Table 1): (1) Stakeholders experience with, and perspective on, abuse of older people; (2) System-level challenges in preventing abuse of older people; and (3) Pathways for systematic improvement and cultural change in preventing abuse of older people.

Theme 1: Stakeholders experience with, and perspective on, abuse of older people

Sabatier and Jenkins-Smith [31] emphasized the role of deep core beliefs in public policy development. These beliefs are described as fundamental, normative values that guide policy actors’ perceptions and actions, providing stability and consistency in their advocacy efforts. Theme 1 therefore focuses on stakeholders’ experiences with, and perspectives on, abuse of older people. Three subthemes were identified: (1.1) Conceptualizing abuse of older people, (1.2) Understanding older people as a multifaceted group, and (1.3) Vulnerabilities for being subjected to abuse as an older person.

1.1 Conceptualizing abuse of older people

Many of the stakeholders expressed concern about the lack of clarity and consensus on the definition of abuse of older people, noting that abuse encompasses not only physical acts but also psychological, economic, and medical neglect. Participant 1A emphasized, “We have defined it as physical abuse, psychological abuse, financial abuse… but the definitions are very broad and often more political than practical”. This ambiguity was identified as a barrier to effective legislation and intervention. According to all the stakeholder interviews, physical, psychological, and sexual abuse is broadly recognized and understood by healthcare workers involved in elder care and, to a certain extent, the overall population, despite their broad definitions. In contrast, financial abuse was not as commonly associated with the term “abuse of older people” as, for example, physical violence. Nonetheless, participant 5 emphasized their belief that “it’s quite a big problem that older people are being exploited financially”. Furthermore, the concept of neglect was particularly highlighted as underrecognized, yet its consequences severe (noted by participant 3B, see Table 1). Another participant (3A) categorized such conditions as “medical abuse”. Stakeholders stressed that neglect and psychological abuse could be just as harmful as physical abuse, especially in institutional settings.

1.2 Understanding older people as a multifaceted group

Subtheme 1.2 revealed that stakeholders view older adults as a heterogeneous group with unique life histories and intersecting identities; for example, one of the quotes reflects the following: “We think of the older adults as a single group, but there are older people with different sexual orientations, disabilities, or substance use issues” (participant 1B). Stakeholders also emphasized that some older individuals may have histories as perpetrators of violence or abuse, a trait that does not disappear with increasing age. Hence, in an institutional setting, staff, guests and patients themselves may act as perpetrators. For example, participant 1A recounted an incident at a nursing home where a male patient with dementia repeatedly sexually assaulted a female patient before the abuse was discovered. This case was particularly challenging to detect and address due to the complexity of the perpetrator being a patient themselves with dementia.

1.3 Vulnerabilities for being subjected to abuse of older people

As highlighted in theme 1.2, older people are portrayed as a diverse group, which suggests that their vulnerability to abuse of older people is varied and widespread. Beyond the factor of old age, vulnerabilities inherent to the individual who increase the risk of being subjected to abuse of older people include cognitive impairment, physical disabilities, social isolation, and dependence on caregivers. Additionally, being part of another minority group, such as immigrants or those who do not speak Norwegian fluently, was identified as a risk factor for increased susceptibility to abuse of older people. Some older individuals may also have histories of being victims of abuse, as noted by participant 4B (see Table 1), which can further increase their vulnerability. External factors, such as lack of competence and inadequate staffing among health professionals, were also reported to exacerbate abuse of older people (will be further elaborated in section “Perceived insufficiencies in competence among personnel and staffing levels”). A stakeholder explained: “A risk factor is those who are around the older people who pose as risk… due to their own challenges financial and psychological” (participant 1A).

Theme 2: System-level challenges in preventing abuse of older people

Throughout all the interviews, it became apparent that a lack of systematic work and policy guidelines was experienced by many of the experts in the field. Therefore, theme two addresses the system-level challenges associated with core policy beliefs in Sabatier and Jenkins-Smith’s [31] theory, which emphasizes stakeholders’ views on effective strategies to prevent abuse of older people. Two subthemes were identified: (2.1) Perceived insufficiencies in competence among personnel and staffing levels, and (2.2) Perceived insufficient resources and reporting guidelines.

2.1 Perceived insufficiencies in competence among personnel and staffing levels

As presented in subtheme 1.3, external factors such as staffing and competence were also reported as risk factors for older people being subjected to abuse of older people. Staffing refers to insufficient personnel on duty, whereas competence concerns the lack of formal qualifications, as noted by participant 2A (see Table 1). Half of the stakeholder interviews (n = 6) pointed to the absence of formal education among health workers as a potential risk factor for abuse of older people. Moreover, all interviews (N = 11) emphasized the need for improved competence and training on abuse of older people for health professionals. Participant 2B stated, “We are aware that there is a significant presence of unskilled workers in care for older adults, and to a large extent, the lack of professional competence constitutes a risk factor.” Participant 2A added: “Lack of staff and competence are major risk areas. When few are on duty, the stress level increases, which leads to neglect.”

2.2 Perceived insufficient resources and reporting guidelines

Stakeholders reported several other issues related to the prevalence of abuse of older people, including a lack of resources, gaps in legal protection, and the absence of standardized reporting mechanisms. One of the interviewed authorities and representatives of public bodies (participant 5) noted that approximately 20% of their inspection cases, estimated at approximately 500 annually, involved older people. However, other interviews revealed that most instances of abuse of older people are unreported, largely due to a lack of awareness, insufficient knowledge and resources, and systemic barriers. Stakeholders directly involved in protective measures against abuse of older people expressed concerns about inadequate funding, which perpetuates these challenges. One participant (10) even argued that the “lack of resources is a form of discrimination by society itself”.

Norway’s legal frameworks addressing abuse of older people were generally deemed sufficient by most participants; stakeholders, on average, agreed that “…it’s not the legislation that is the biggest problem” (participant 2B). The more pressing issue identified was the lack of effective practical implementation. Participant 1A noted, “The current legal framework does not always help us when abuse against someone with dementia is uncovered.” Additionally, other stakeholders, such as participant 6 (see Table 1), underscored the absence of systematic risk assessments and weak leadership structures, indicating a disconnect between policy provisions and on-the-ground realities.

Theme 3: Pathways for systematic improvement and cultural change in preventing abuse of older people

After addressing system-level challenges with establishing preventative measures for abuse of older people, participants expressed what shortcomings they believed needs to be addressed to prevent abuse of older people in Norway. Hence, theme 3 reflects the secondary beliefs in Sabatier and Jenkins-Smith’s [31] theory and identifies pathways for systemic improvement and cultural change. Two subthemes were identified: (3.1): Promoting awareness and education to prevent abuse of older people, and (3.2): Concrete measures for better interdisciplinary efforts.

3.1 Promoting awareness and education to prevent abuse of older people

Stakeholders highlighted education and societal awareness and attitudes as essential elements to consider when implementing preventative measures against abuse of older people. Participant 1B noted, “Older people themselves also need to gain more knowledge about violence and abuse… they need to know that there is help available.” Participant 4B added, “A change in attitude is needed, not only among professionals but throughout society—children, youth, everyone.” Stakeholders further suggested integrating abuse of older people topics into educational curricula for healthcare workers and onboarding procedures for new employees, with mandatory courses recommended by participant 10 (see Table 1). This meaning was shared by participant 1A, who stated, “When they sign the confidentiality agreement, they should also disclose that they are aware of their duty to prevent harm”. Additional educational measures suggested by stakeholders included creating informational brochures on abuse of older people and older people’ rights (interview 1) and launching national or regional awareness campaigns targeting ageism and abuse of older people (interviews 3, 4 and 6).

3.2 Concrete measures for better interdisciplinary efforts

In addition to educating health professionals and the older people and fostering societal attitude changes, stakeholders have suggested concrete measures to prevent abuse of older people by enhancing interdisciplinary collaboration and structural improvements. The key recommendations included establishing clear reporting systems and leadership structures to monitor abuse cases effectively. A system for uncovering, reporting, and following up on abuse reports was seen as crucial. Participant 1B emphasized the need for a straightforward system with defined responsibilities to ensure that cases are handled after being reported: “There must be a simple system one can refer to, where someone has a defined responsibility to find out the nature of the issue and to ensure it’s followed up”. Suggestions included interdisciplinary efforts across sectors and roles to unify efforts (noted by participant 3A, see Table 1) and developing a Norwegian equivalent of the PLISSIT model [Permission, Limited Information, Specific Suggestions, and Intensive Therapy] used in the United Kingdom, assigning specific staff responsibilities related to abuse prevention. At the institutional level, leadership and management were identified as those responsible for creating guidelines, enhancing the competence of their employees—health professionals—integrating debriefing as a measure to reduce stress and demonstrate support and fostering interdisciplinary collaboration (participants 1B and 6, see Table 1).

Finally, stakeholders advocated for a formalized adult protection system similar to child welfare services. As participant 1B noted, “We have a legally mandated child welfare system… but no specific rules or deadlines for the adult sector.” This call was grounded in the observation that older people facing violence and abuse often do not receive the same follow-up care as the overall population, especially in comparison to victims of other vulnerable groups such as children. In addition, it reflects that to balance protection with respect for people, proposals for adult-protection systems should explicitly embed respect for autonomy and limit constraints on decision-making only when there is clear, decision-specific risk of significant harm. Participant 1A therefore highlighted the lack of legal precedence and a need for a clear adult protection system, stating, “It sends a signal that violence and abuse against at-risk adults are somehow not as serious [like abuse against other populations, e.g. children].”

Discussion

The discussion of this study’s findings is structured through the lens of Sabatier and Jenkins-Smith [31] ACF, which helps explain how beliefs and values influence public policy change and implementation. In line with the empirical scope of the study, this session interprets the findings as stakeholder-informed priorities for abuse of older people prevention policy and practice in Norway. By organizing the findings into deep core beliefs, policy core beliefs, and secondary beliefs, we identified shared values, perceived systemic challenges, and proposed solutions for strengthening abuse of older people prevention. In that sense, stakeholders demonstrated broad consensus that abuse of older people constitutes a fundamental violation of human rights requiring coordinated, multi-leveled interventions.

Deep core beliefs are anchored in the shared normative values regarding the dignity and rights of older adults. The interviews indicated that stakeholders view abuse of older people not only as a social and health concern but also as a moral issue that reflects poorly on societal values. As noted by stakeholders in all interviews except one (interview 10), abuse of older people manifests in various forms including physical, psychological, financial, neglect, sexual, and institutional abuse, aligning with global public health discourses that frame abuse of older people as a human rights issue [3, 40, 43, 45]. Neglect was particularly emphasized as an often-overlooked form of abuse. Stakeholders noted that being repeatedly overlooked throughout the day can profoundly affect individuals, underscoring the potential lack of respect and dignity experienced by older adults, especially in institutional settings [2, 3].

The call to combat ageism was a recurrent topic, aligning with the WHO’s recommendations for the Decade of Healthy Aging [42, 44]. In Norway, 14% of the population, predominantly older adults, are low or non-digital users, with 44% citing lack of digital competence [4], potentially heightening vulnerability through social exclusion [13, 26]. Furthermore, stakeholders reported that older abuse victims receive less care and attention than younger victims, reflecting global patterns where abuse of older people remains a neglected public health priority [49]. One stakeholder described a nursing home case where a female patient was forced to interact daily with her assailant for months. As participant 1B noted, ageism can deprive individuals of rights to treatment and help following abuse [1, 22].

Stakeholders emphasized that older individuals retain core identity aspects while carrying accumulated life experiences. Research confirms that individuals who experience abuse earlier in life face increased susceptibility to abuse of older people [16, 41]. This trend is evident in Norway’s neighboring country Sweden, where 58% of abuse of older people victims report having experienced prior victimization [46]. Recognizing older adults as multifaceted individuals challenges monolithic assumptions and underscores the need for context-sensitive approaches. Therefore, healthcare professionals should engage in conversations about histories of violence and abuse as preventative measures [34, 41]. Stakeholders further emphasized the need for unified terminology, noting that varying interpretations among healthcare institutions, municipalities, and protective services in Norway complicate efforts to develop coherent prevention policy, practice guidelines, and, where relevant, future legislative clarification. These definitional inconsistencies create policy gaps in identifying and responding to abuse cases, highlighting opportunities for coalition-building across agencies to establish common standards [31].

The core policy beliefs center on stakeholders’ perspectives on effective strategies for preventing abuse of older people. Stakeholders identified key vulnerabilities including cognitive impairment, social isolation, caregiver dependence, minority group membership, and abuse history, consistent with existing literature [17, 20, 34]. While current laws were considered robust, stakeholders emphasized the need for systematic approaches incorporating clear practice and reporting. Formal training for health workers emerged as essential. These recommendations align with international prevention guidelines [1, 44]. Education as a preventive measure extended beyond professional training to broader societal campaigns targeting attitude change throughout society. Stakeholders advocated for integrating abuse of older people education into professional curricula and national awareness campaigns to combat ageism, strongly aligning with WHO strategic priorities [43].

Interdisciplinary collaboration across healthcare, legal, and social service sectors emerged as critical, with concrete proposals offered including adult protection systems modeled after child welfare services as one possible organizational response to strengthen prevention and referral pathways [19]. Stakeholders stressed that prevention requires leadership accountability within healthcare institutions. High stress levels among health workers can inadvertently lead to psychological abuse or neglect [6, 20]. Recognition from leadership and debriefing sessions following challenging shifts were further proposed as crucial stress mitigation elements, underscoring the importance of institutional structures in fostering sustainable change [27, 32].

Secondary beliefs focused on practical implementation across three key areas. First, reporting systems emerged as a recurring concern, with stakeholders advocating accessible mechanisms including follow-up procedures and designated responsibilities. Such systems are critical for improving detection and response where underreporting remains significant [34, 48]. Second, mandatory education emerged as another key implementation method, with nearly all stakeholders supporting incorporation of abuse of older people topics into professional curricula. Participant 10 argued that specialized courses should be mandatory for all health professionals working with older adults. These proposals are grounded in evidence that lack of professional training contributes to under-recognition of abuse of older people [1]. Furthermore, stakeholders emphasized the importance of educating older adults themselves and involving them in the public conversations about abuse of older people. Participant 9 noted that involving older adults is essential for understanding how abuse is experienced. This aligns with evidence supporting user representative involvement in healthcare planning, delivery, and evaluation [12, 33].

Third, resource allocation represented a critical secondary belief. Stakeholders perceived insufficient funding and inadequate staffing as key barriers to implementing abuse of older people preventative interventions, highlighting structural vulnerabilities also reported in previous research [6, 19, 39]. The demographic shift toward more older adults and fewer potential caregivers [5, 37] will intensify staffing challenges. As participant 2A noted, if staffing is a risk factor, the need for concrete protection measures will only increase. Participant 1A suggested creating larger, coordinated projects rather than numerous small initiatives, potentially streamlining resource allocation and enhancing intervention effectiveness [6]. The gap between broad policy intentions and practical realities suggests that successful prevention requires not only belief alignment but also infrastructure investment, consistent with findings that funding disparities compromise prevention policy effectiveness [40].

The importance of multi-sectoral collaboration is further supported by international evidence. Research from Australia demonstrates that paramedics and emergency medical services play crucial roles in frontline detection and initial response to abuse of older people [14, 28], highlighting that comprehensive prevention systems must extend beyond traditional healthcare and social services to include all professionals who encounter older adults in crisis situations. Building on these international perspectives on multi-sectoral collaborations, these stakeholder recommendations align well with established intervention frameworks for addressing abuse of older people, such as the Abuse Intervention Model (AIM) developed by Mosqueda et al. [25]. The AIM framework emphasizes multi-disciplinary, coordinated responses involving screening and detection, comprehensive assessment, intervention planning, and monitoring—all elements emphasized by stakeholders in this study. Specifically, stakeholders call for formal training, clear reporting systems, and interdisciplinary collaboration mirror this model’s emphasis on coordinated multi-sectoral responses involving healthcare, social services, and legal systems working together to address abuse of older people comprehensively. This pathway synthesizes stakeholder recommendations across the three ACF belief systems into a coordinated prevention framework. Strengths and limitations

The interview guide was deliberately generic to accommodate diverse participants’ backgrounds. While this ensured comfort in addressing relevant topics, it introduced variability and the potential for insight to be omitted. Data collection continued until saturation was achieved. Another limitation involves potential bias from group interview dynamics. The absence of lawmakers may have resulted in an incomplete understanding of policy formulation perspectives. Another limitation involves potential bias from group interview dynamics, where participants knowing each other may have avoided critical statements about their institutions or colleagues. The absence of lawmakers, as distinct from autonomous bureaucrats, may have resulted in an incomplete understanding of legislative and formal policy formulation processes. For this reason, the present findings should not be interpreted as sufficient evidence for specifying national legislation, but rather as identifying stakeholder-informed priorities that may inform future policy, guideline, and practice development. Future research should include policymakers to capture legislative perspectives on abuse of older people prevention if the aim is to examine the development of national legislation or binding national guidelines more directly.

Conclusion and further research

This study identified shared beliefs among Norwegian stakeholders regarding abuse of older people prevention. Findings align with international evidence emphasizing the importance of addressing ageism, strengthening legal frameworks, and investing in education and resources. Stakeholders advocated for multifaceted approaches including improved awareness, interdisciplinary collaboration, and systematic reporting mechanisms. For Norway specifically, these recommendations suggest establishing national standardized definitions and reporting protocols, implementing mandatory abuse of older people training across all healthcare sectors, and creating adult protection systems comparable to existing child welfare services. By leveraging these shared beliefs, stakeholders can build unified coalitions to drive effective national prevention efforts within Norway’s existing healthcare and social service frameworks.

This research contributes to the global discourse on abuse of older people prevention by offering foundations for policy development that prioritize the well-being of older individuals. Future research should employ longitudinal studies to track changes in stakeholder beliefs, conduct comparative analyses to reveal how cultural context influences these factors, and use interdisciplinary approaches to evaluate policy impact and identify best practices.
