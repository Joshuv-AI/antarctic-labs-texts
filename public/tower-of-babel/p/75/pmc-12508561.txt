Workplace-related environmental exposure and its potential influence on hair mineral and gut microbiota in cloned working dogs
Authors: Seunghoon LEE, Mi-Hwa OH, Jong-Hui KIM, Inchul CHOI, Jingu NO, Dong-Hoon KIM, Jae Gyu YOO
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Four genetically identical cloned dogs were produced from a single donor using the somatic cell nuclear transfer technique. They stayed in the same facility during youth period, and were later assigned to different locations according to their roles as working dogs. In this study, we report observations from four genetically identical cloned working dogs exposed to different environments, including an airport and a police station, for two years. Hair and fecal samples were collected to assess potential environmental influences on mineral content and intestinal microbiota composition. The concentrations of toxic minerals, such as Pb and Ni, were significantly higher in the hair mineral analysis of the dog exposed to the airport compared to those of the three police station-working dogs. Fecal microbiota analysis showed that the intestinal microbiota of the airport-exposed dog was simplified, with an increased proportion of Actinobacteria and a decreased proportion of Lachnospiraceae and Fusobacteria. Our observations suggest that environmental conditions may influence the accumulation of harmful substances in the body and affect intestinal microbiota community. This finding also highlights the potential of non-invasive methods for evaluating workplace-related environmental influences on working dogs. These results suggest that different environments may contribute to physiological differences even among genetically identical individuals. Future studies should investigate the mechanisms underlying these variations and assess their long-term implications for canine health monitoring with larger sample sizes.

INTRODUCTION

Companion animals like dogs are also mainly affected by their home environment and food. However, working dogs that perform missions in the military, police stations, customs, airports, and other locations are exposed to various environments depending on their specialized tasks, such as explosive detection, drug detection, and agricultural and livestock product detection [4]. Since most of these tasks utilize the dogs’ excellent sense of smell, there is a high likelihood that various substances will accumulate in their bodies due to contact and inhalation in different environments [7, 13]. Similar to people working in hazardous environments receive regular health checkups, it is necessary to regularly assess the accumulation of harmful substances in working dogs to maintain their health and work efficiency [16].

A representative invasive method, blood analysis, allows for the measurement of mineral concentration in the blood and the assessment of an animal’s health status through complete blood count (CBC) and serum biochemistry test [1]. However, it is not appropriate for detecting long-term mineral exposure. Most minerals are excreted from body fluids and tissues through metabolism in the form of feces, urine, and sweat within a few weeks [8]. Furthermore, regular blood collection causes stress in dogs [40], potentially interfering with their work efficiency.

Hair mineral analysis has been explored as a non-invasive method to assess body mineral status and heavy metal levels in various animal studies [6, 25, 43]. It may provide insights into long-term accumulation trends of trace and toxic elements [8, 27]. Toxic and trace element levels are influenced by environmental factors such as energy intake, living, and working environments [5, 44]. Therefore, hair mineral analysis can be used for predicting physical health changes associated with environmental condition [22, 45]. In a study on dogs, several detection dogs exhibited chronic stress, inflammatory issues such as arthritis and chronic diarrhea, and imbalanced hair mineral patterns compared to non-service companion dogs [39], suggesting that handling and housing conditions influence hair mineral patterns and may also induce changes in health condition.

Fecal microbiome analysis is a noninvasive method used to examine the diverse community of microorganisms in the gastrointestinal tract, which function as a complex ecosystem [36]. These gastrointestinal microbes reflect various host animal’s nutritional responses, metabolism, and innate and adaptive immune systems [12, 24, 49]. Additionally, chronic stress adversely impact health by disrupting the immune, cardiovascular, and central nervous systems, as well as altering the gut microbiota composition [9, 29, 42]. In this context, fecal microbiome analysis is a valuable approach for assessing the health status of animals that are exposed to harmful environments over extended periods.

However, assessing the influence of environmental exposure on working dogs can be challenging due to genetic variability among individuals. Therefore, minimizing genetic variability is critical in animal studies, as individual genetic difference can influence physiological response and it is particularly useful when studying disease mechanisms and therapies [2]. In this study, we utilized cloned dogs generated via somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) to minimize genetic variability [20]. We employed non-invasive approaches, including hair mineral and fecal microbiota analysis, to assess mineral content and gut microbiota composition among dogs exposed to different working place. In addition, hair mineral analysis and fecal microbiome analysis are expected to be effective in detecting these effects. To our knowledge, limited study has explored the hair mineral characteristics and intestinal microbiota of genetically identical animals in different environments. This study highlights the potential of non-invasive methods for assessing environmental influences on working dogs and their long-term health.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics statement

The dog care facilities and protocols including vaccination met or exceeded the standards established by the Committee for Accreditation of the Laboratory Animal Care at the National Institute of Animal Science, Rural Development Administration (IACUC No. NIAS2013-045).

Animals and sample collection

We previously produced four somatic cell cloned Labrador retriever dogs [19]. Ear fibroblast cells of a 7-year-old elite sniffing male Labrador retriever dog were used as donor cells. Ovulated in vivo matured oocytes were recovered from female dogs, and a donor fibroblast cell was transferred to an enucleated recipient oocyte as described previously [15]. The resulting cloned puppies nursed from the same surrogate mother until 6 weeks after birth. Weaned puppies were fed a standard dog diet (Royal Canin Labrador Retriever 33 Junior) for 6 weeks. As described in Fig. 1Fig. 1.Schematic overview of the experiment design, types of samples, sampling times, dogs’ duties, and feeding regimes., after weaning, the four 3-month-old cloned dogs were moved to and cared for at an airport police station as sniffing dogs (cloned dog A) and at two different regular police stations as guard dogs (cloned dogs B, C, and D). All dogs were housed in indoor kennels in accordance with the Korean National Police Agency’s manual. Each kennel had a minimum floor area of 3.8 m2 and a height of at least 120 cm. The kennels were constructed with metal frames and waterproof materials and were equipped with heating and cooling systems. Cloned dogs A and B were fed daily with a commercial diet (PROPLAN Sport 30/20; Purina Korea, Seoul, Korea), and cloned dogs C and D were also fed with a commercial diet (PROPLAN Focus; Purina Korea). The feeding regime is outlined in Fig. 1. Drinking water was available at all times. All dogs were managed by experienced handlers under the Korean National Police Agency’s manual, including basic socialization training. The dogs were exercised daily in outdoor dirt-floored areas, with cloned dog A patrolling airport zones twice daily for one hour each, and other police station dogs exercising in the station’s outdoor exercise areas. Two years after the dogs were moved to their respective police stations, blood samples were collected via cephalic venipuncture for complete blood counts. Hairs were collected from the shoulders of the four cloned dogs. Fresh feces were collected only from cloned dog A (a trained sniffing dog) and cloned dog B (a guard dog), which were fed with the same commercial diet.

Schematic overview of the experiment design, types of samples, sampling times, dogs’ duties, and feeding regimes.

Blood collection and cell blood counts

Whole blood was collected into an evacuated tube containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid to determine red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin concentration (Hb), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), red blood cell distribution width (RDW), platelet count (PLT), mean plasma volume (MPV), white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophils (NE), lymphocytes (LY), monocytes (MO), eosinophils (EO), basophils (BA). For the determination of complete blood counts, Celltac a hematology analyzer (Nihon Kohden, Tokyo, Japan) was used.

Hair mineral analysis

For hair mineral assay, an ICP-MS (7700X; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used with the assistance of GunSei Biotechnology, Inc. (Gwacheon, Republic of Korea; http://www.gunseibio.com). In brief, dog hair samples were placed in a 50 mL polypropylene tube (DigiTube, SCP Science, Baie-d’Urfé, Canada), and mixed well with 5 mL of 65–70% nitric acid (SCP Science). Tubes were incubated at 80°C for 1 hr and cooled down to room temperature. Calibration standards (SCP Science) were prepared with 5% nitric acid. Element concentrations (in ppm) were calculated using the standard curves prepared with certified ICP-MS elemental standards (SCP Science). Validation of the methodology was conducted using two certified reference materials, both for human hair (GBW07601a from China and ERMDB001 from Sigma). We could not find certified reference materials (CRM). However, measurement accuracy was generally within 90–110% of certified values, and spike recovery ranged from 90–105% depending on the element. Precision (%RSD, relative standard deviation) was maintained below 10% (within-run) and 15% (between-run) for 95% of elements. Matrix effects were negligible at hair concentrations below 0.2%.

Microbial analysis of feces

Feces samples were transferred to sterile stomacher bags with 90 mL of a sterile 0.85% NaCl solution and then mixed for 5 min using a stomacher (Seward Laboratory Systems Inc., Bohemia, NY, USA). PCR amplification was performed using primers targeting the V1 to V3 regions of the 16S rRNA gene with extracted DNA. For bacterial amplification, barcoded primers (27F, 5′-CCTATCCCCTGTGTGCCTTGGCAGTC-TCAG-AC-GAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3′ and 518R, 5′-CCATCTCATCCCTGC GTGTCTCCGAC-TCAG-X-AC-WTTACCGCGGCTGCTGG-3′, where X indicates the unique barcode for each target) (http://oklbb.ezbiocloud.net/content/1001) were used, and the amplification was carried out under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 min; 30 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 sec, primer annealing at 55°C for 30 sec, and extension at 72°C for 30 sec; and a final elongation at 72°C for 5 min. The PCR product was confirmed by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis and visualized using a Gel Docsystem (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The amplified products were purified with a QIA Quick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA). Equal concentrations of purified products were pooled together, and short fragments (nontarget products) were removed using an AMPure beads kit (Agencourt Bioscience, Beverly, MA, USA). Product quality and size were assessed on a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, Palo Alto, CA, USA) using a DNA 7500 chip. Mixed amplicons were subjected to emulsion PCR and then deposited on Picotiter plates. Sequencing was carried out at Chunlab, Inc. (Seoul, Korea), with a GS Junior Sequencing system (Roche, Branford, CT, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

The sequence reads from the fecal samples of cloned dogs were sorted by sample-specific barcodes, and linker and primer sequences were trimmed from the raw sequencing reads. Potential chimeric sequences were also filtered by PCR, and the taxonomic classification of each read was assigned according to the EzTaxon-e database (http://eztaxon-e.ezbiocloud.net) [21]. The database includes 16S rRNA gene sequences from type strains with validly published names, as well as representative species-level phylotypes from both cultured and uncultured entries in the GenBank database, all of which are annotated with a complete hierarchical taxonomic classification from the phylum to the species level. Pyrosequencing data of the intestinal microbiota were analyzed using the CLcommunity software (ChunLab Inc., Seoul, Korea). Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were defined as clusters of sequences sharing ≥97% similarity, and this clustering was performed using the CD-HIT algorithm [23]. The richness and diversities of samples were determined by Chao1 estimation and the Shannon diversity index at the 3% distance. Random subsampling was conducted to equalize the read size of samples for comparing different read sizes among samples.

DISCUSSION

Dogs working in diverse environments are particularly susceptible to environmental pollutants due to their occupational exposure and highly sensitive olfactory systems. Unlike companion animals, public working dogs in specialized roles might have a higher likelihood of encountering workplace-related contaminants. However, the extent to which these exposures lead to physiological changes remains uncertain and requires further investigation. For instance, studies have shown that dogs guarding crude oil drilling and natural gas production sites may be exposed to elevated levels of toxic metals like Ni, Cr, and Zn, which are linked to oxidative stress, respiratory issues, and neurological disorders [10]. However, the health implications of such exposures in different working environments remain unclear. In this study, we evaluated the effectiveness of non-invasive methods, specifically hair mineral analysis and fecal microbiota analysis, in assessing the accumulation of harmful substances in genetically identical cloned dogs exposed to different environments.

In this study, we evaluated the effects of different environments using four cloned dogs born around the same time. Our previous study reported that four genetically identical somatic cell-cloned Labrador Retrievers showed no significant differences in their hematologic profiles at eight weeks of age, when they had been raised in the same facility [19]. In addition to non-invasive methods, we conducted CBC after two years of environmental exposure. All CBC values, including white and red blood cells, remained within the reference range, underscoring the importance of non-invasive techniques in detecting subtle or long-term environment effects on the health status of working dogs.

Hair analysis reflects the accumulation of trace and heavy metals over extended periods [3], making it an effective complement to blood and urine tests, which may not always capture the full extent of the body’s burden of harmful substances [28]. Although blood analysis is often considered a standard, hair mineral analysis has been widely recognized for environmental monitoring by both the US Environmental Protection Agency and the International Atomic Energy Agency [18, 34]. Furthermore, job activities and exposure duration significantly influence the elemental composition of hair, with longer exposure times leading to higher elemental concentrations [38] as seen indifferent hair mineral patterns across various human health conditions [45].

In this study, we observed variations in hair mineral content among genetically identical cloned dogs after exposure to different environments for two years. The concentrations of the macro-elements Ca and Mg were 3.19 to 5.71 times and 3.26 to 4.05 times higher, respectively, in cloned dog A compared to the other dogs. The concentrations of micro-elements Fe and Mn were 7.37 to 10 times and 3.54 to 5.69 times higher, respectively, in cloned dog A compared to the other dogs. Additionally, toxic elements Pb, Al, As, and Ni were more elevated, with levels ranging from 3.78 to 14.33 times, 3.52 to 5.89 times, 1.74 to 2.16 times, and 19.02 to 49.88 times higher, respectively. Even trace toxic elements Cd and Cr showed increased concentrations, ranging from 2.13 to 9.91 times and 2.67 to 10.5 times higher, respectively. Notably, the cloned dog working as a sniffer at an airport (cloned dog A) exhibited relatively higher levels of certain macro-elements (Ca and Mg), micro-elements (Fe, Mn, and Cr), and toxic elements (Pb, Al, As, Cd, and Ni) compared to the other dogs. This supports previous findings in humans and working dogs, where individuals working in industrial settings showed higher metal accumulation such as Pb, Ca, Mg, Mn, and Ni in hair or blood [26, 31, 35]. These observations suggest that workplace conditions may contribute to differences in hair mineral content among genetically identical cloned dogs. The elevated levels observed in cloned dog A likely due to direct uptake via mouth, skin absorption, and olfactory exposure to industrial substances and metal particles prevalent in the airport environment [17], highlighting the utility of hair mineral analysis in capturing environmental influences. In particular, Pb and Ni, which showed more than tenfold difference in our study, are commonly detected at high levels in various industrial environments, including airports [14, 48]. Since heavy metals such as Pb and Ni are linked to neurodegenerative diseases, and Al is known to promote oxidative stress, it’s crucial to monitor working dogs like cloned dog A to reduce their exposure and mitigate potential health risks [47]. This study confirmed that non-invasive hair analysis can provide critical information about harmful substance accumulation that traditional blood analysis might miss, demonstrating the value of this method for health assessment. However, further controlled studies are needed to confirm these findings in working dogs because it remains unclear whether these differences are directly attributable to environmental exposure or other unmeasured variables.

Fecal microbiota analysis offers another valuable non-invasive approach for monitoring health. Gastrointestinal microbes play a crucial role in stimulating the immune system and maintaining overall health [30]. Previous studies have shown that environmental pollutants, such as Pb, Cd, and Ni, have been shown to alter gut microbiota composition significantly, leading to changes in microbial diversity [37]. The observed disruptions in fecal microbiota highlight how environmental effects can influence multiple physiological systems. Our previous study showed that cloned dogs raised in the same environment exhibited similar gut microbial communities [46]. However, in this study, cloned dog A displayed higher levels of Actinobacteria and lower proportions of Fusobacteria and Lachnospiraceae compared to cloned dog B after being raised in different environments, despite feeding the same diet. These results indicate that environmental exposure likely impacts in shaping the microbiota differences between the cloned dogs. Evidence from studies of genetically identical twins indicates that environmental influences predominantly determine the composition of gut microbiota [11, 50]. These microbial difference observed in this study resemble profiles observed in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) in both dogs and humans [33, 41]. It has been also reported that heavy metals from air and soil compromise the immune response and increase susceptibility to gastrointestinal infections [32]. While this study did not directly assess such effects, our findings suggest that environmental exposures could contribute to gut microbiota alterations among cloned working dogs. These findings highlight the potential role of fecal microbiota analysis in monitoring gut microbiota responses to environmental factors among cloned working dogs. Further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the clinical significance of these microbiota changes and their implications for working dog health.

This study demonstrates the potential of non-invasive hair mineral and fecal microbiota analyses to assess potential environmental influences on genetically identical cloned working dogs. While variations were observed in mineral content and microbiota composition, the clinical significance of these differences remains to be determined. Our observations suggest that working environments could influence variations in mineral accumulation and gut microbiota in genetically identical cloned working dogs. Moreover, this study supports the potential of non-invasive methods for evaluating workplace-related environmental influences on working dogs. Future studies should identify which environmental factors such as atmospheric condition, soil conditions, and stress factors are primarily underlie these differences, and develop strategies for long-term health monitoring in occupational settings with larger sample sizes.
