Cryptogenic Stroke and PFO Closure: Does Sex Matter?
Authors: Sharon N. Poisson, Michelle H. Leppert, Karen D. Orjuela
Published: 2023

Stroke is a common disease, affecting 750 000 people in the United States each year, the fifth leading cause of death and the leading cause of disability. 1 Many ischemic strokes are related to traditional cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, and large‐artery atherosclerosis, along with cardioembolism caused by atrial fibrillation. One‐third of ischemic strokes, however, remain cryptogenic, with no clear cause, and many of these occur in younger adults. In recent years, the association of patent foramen ovale (PFO) and embolic strokes of unclear source has been of more interest, and now 4 trials 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 have found benefit in percutaneous PFO closure in patients aged <60 years with cryptogenic stroke and few risk factors. In aggregate, these studies have found about a 3.3% absolute risk reduction in recurrent stroke with closure compared with medical therapy alone, and determining which strokes are related to PFO and therefore may benefit from closure is key to the success of the procedure. 6 Several measures for predicting benefit of closure, such as Risk of Paradoxical Embolism (RoPE) score of >7, containing patient risk factors (ie, age; history of hypertension, diabetes, or strokes; cortical infarction), as well as the PFO‐Associated Stroke Causal Likelihood classification of possible or probable, adding higher‐risk PFO features (ie, shunt size and atrial septal aneurysm). 7 , 8 Guidelines currently recommend that PFO closure should be considered in addition to antiplatelet therapy in patients aged 18 to 60 years with nonlacunar ischemic stroke of undetermined cause and PFO with high‐risk anatomic features after a thorough evaluation by a multidisciplinary team (cardiology and neurology) in a shared decision‐making model. 9

While stroke overall is decreasing in incidence over time, there is evidence that stroke in young adults may be increasing. 10 Furthermore, there are also reasons to believe that the causes of stroke in young women may differ from that in young men. 11 While the incidence of myocardial infarction is higher in men than women for every age group, it appears that in adults aged <45 years, women have more strokes than men. 12 , 13 This finding suggests that factors other than the traditional cardiovascular risk factors driving acute myocardial infarction are at play in the development of ischemic strokes among young adults. Some of the nontraditional causes of ischemic strokes in young adults, in addition to PFO, include arterial dissection, migraine, substance use disorder, and, specifically in women, hormone‐related risk factors such as exogenous estrogen use, pregnancy, and puerperium.

In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Farjat‐Pasos et al 14 describe a retrospective cohort of consecutive patients presenting to 2 university hospitals with cryptogenic strokes or transient ischemic attacks (TIAs) who had transcatheter PFO closure performed after a thorough diagnostic workup for other stroke causes. The 1076 patients were then followed for a median of 3 years for follow‐up cerebrovascular and bleeding events. Women made up 44.6% of the cohort, making the ratio of women to men 0.77. On average, women were younger, had more migraines, and had higher Risk of Paradoxical Embolism scores (6.91 versus 6.59), while men had more dyslipidemia and coronary artery disease. There were no differences by sex in the PFO characteristics (size of shunt, presence of atrial septal aneurysm), procedural details, complications, postprocedural medications, or residual shunt rate. In follow‐up, there was no difference in the rate of recurrent strokes or TIAs between men and women, but when including both ischemic stroke and TIA in a combined end point, women had more events than men (incidence rate ratio, 2.58), remaining significant after adjusting for age, risk factors, and medications. Postclosure atrial fibrillation did not differ by sex. However, women had more bleeding events than men in the 3‐year follow‐up (incidence rate ratio, 2.82), again remaining significant in an adjusted model.

This study adds an interesting perspective that has not previously been directly explored: Does the benefit of PFO closure apply equally to women and men? A prior meta‐analysis by Kent et al 7 did a subgroup analysis by sex and did not find a significant difference, although the point estimate for absolute risk reduction was higher in men than in women. There are plenty of prior examples of differences in management and outcomes of women and men with stroke, such as in the management of carotid stenosis. 15 However, it is not clear whether the difference in outcomes between women and men seen in this study reflects differing stroke causes, variable risks of the procedure or any thrombotic therapy, or perhaps differences in how outcomes present or are measured by sex.

The difference in combined cerebrovascular end points by sex is an interesting one, though the inclusion of TIA as an end point event is problematic. TIA, by definition, is a clinical diagnosis and is not supported by any radiographic findings. For this reason, diagnosis of TIA is variable, leading to potential false‐positive diagnoses. In addition, given its transient nature, we suspect that many patients do not present to medical attention with their TIA symptoms and may not get the diagnosis. Therefore, diagnosis of TIA relies on the patient presenting to medical care and a high enough suspicion by a provider. There is evidence that more women than men present to the emergency department with neurologic complaints, 16 and in a population with prior stroke such as this one, diagnosis of TIA may be frequent with any transient episode of neurologic symptoms. Similarly, as an outcome measure, it is not clear what the clinical significance of TIA is if it does not lead to stroke in the longer term, an outcome that affects functional status. The lack of benefit of PFO closure in the CLOSURE I (evaluation of the STARFlex septal closure system in patients with a stroke and/or transient ischemic attack due to presumed paradoxical embolism through a patent foramen ovale) 17 was likely multifactorial, but the inclusion of TIA in the composite primary end point was speculated to have potentially contributed.

The finding of increased bleeding events in women following PFO closure is a curious one. Medical management after PFO closure generally includes dual antiplatelet therapy for up to 6 months, while guideline‐based care in patients after stroke includes single antiplatelet therapy in most patients after a potentially brief period of dual antiplatelet therapy following minor stroke or TIA. 9 The early time period after PFO closure, therefore, may be associated with a higher risk of bleeding. Beyond this early time period, it is not clear why PFO closure may increase bleeding risk. The lack of a control group in this study brings up the question of whether the difference in bleeding events could be related to a baseline difference between women and men as opposed to a relationship with the procedure. There is recent evidence that female patients treated with direct oral anticoagulants have a higher risk of gastrointestinal bleeding compared with male patients, which may support a baseline difference in risk. 18 In addition, bleeding events have been found to be more common in women compared with men for at least a year following endovascular treatment of peripheral artery disease. 19 While the population in the aforementioned studies were older, the current study by Farjat‐Pasos et al involves a younger population, with a good portion of the women likely in a pre‐ or perimenopausal stage, leading to more complicating factors in interpreting the risk of bleeding events.

The outcome of this study is unexpected. Given the higher risk of venous clotting in women of childbearing ages with exogenous estrogen use and pregnancy/puerperium, the contribution of PFO to the development of ischemic strokes in young women may be theoretically greater than their male counterparts. In this study, scores in women were higher, which would suggest that they should benefit more from closure. 7 If the higher rate of the combined ischemic end point is a true statistical finding, this could be related to one of several explanations. One possibility is that the study did not capture a population of young women in whom hormonal risk factors were a major contributor. There could be a mechanism for hormonal risk factors leading to arterial ischemic stroke directly without the PFO as a conduit for a venous thromboembolism. Alternatively, there may be an unexplored mechanism by which men have a higher risk of venous thromboembolism for which we are not accounting. Regardless, the paradoxical finding in this study suggests the need to better understand the differences in the cause of stroke between women and men with PFO to predict the benefit of closure more accurately.

The findings of this study certainly raise the suspicion that the interplay between sex differences in the role of PFO in stroke cause (and therefore the benefit of closure), the complications of percutaneous closure of PFO, and the medication management afterward are complex. Further work to better understand how to identify patients who will benefit from closure will advance the field, and sex may play a role in that identification. Moving forward, phase IV studies and registries addressing differences in outcomes of PFO closure by sex will be crucial to understand PFO‐associated stroke, as well as alternative causes of stroke in young women. This includes the need to adjust our current models to consider differences by sex assigned at birth and populations in which data on stroke and PFO‐associated stroke are scarce, such as transgender individuals. Carefully considering baseline differences in outcomes for women and men in areas such as hemorrhage and choosing appropriate outcomes to more definitively understand the benefit of closure is vital to unambiguously answer the persistent questions about this treatment. Revisions of current guidelines in secondary stroke prevention should contemplate accurate risk–benefit conversations that allow clinicians and patients to make the best shared decisions about PFO closure in multidisciplinary settings.
