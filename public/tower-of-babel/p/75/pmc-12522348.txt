Empowering nurses in the AI era: investigating the interplay between professionalism, AI readiness, and self-efficacy
Authors: Nariman Ahmed Mohamed El-Bassal, Ahmed Abdelwahab Ibrahim El-Sayed, Heba Gamal Elgamal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe rapid integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into healthcare demands that nurses not only develop technical competencies but also uphold strong professional values to maintain effective and ethical practice. While AI readiness is gaining attention, its relationship with nursing professionalism and self-efficacy remains underexplored.ObjectiveTo examine the relationship between nurses’ AI readiness and their self-efficacy, with a specific focus on the moderating role of professionalism within clinical practice settings.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional design was employed involving 278 staff nurses from three tertiary healthcare hospitals: one hospital in Kafr Elsheikh Governorate and two hospitals in Alexandria Governorate, Egypt. Data were collected using three validated instruments: the Nursing Profession Self-Efficacy Scale, the Artificial Intelligence Readiness Scale, and the Nurse Professionalism Scale. Pearson correlation, multiple regression, and path analysis were performed using SPSS v25 to examine the relationships among the study variables and to test the proposed hypotheses.ResultsThe findings revealed that both AI readiness (β = 0.143, p = 0.017) and professionalism (β = 0.147, p = 0.014) were significant predictors of nurses’ self-efficacy. Path analysis confirmed direct effects of both variables on self-efficacy and supported the moderating role of professionalism. AI readiness and professionalism collectively explained 4.7% of the variance in self-efficacy. Nurses reported high levels of self-efficacy and professionalism, but moderate AI readiness, especially in ethical and visionary subdomains.ConclusionNurses’ self-efficacy is shaped by both their preparedness to use AI technologies and their professional values. Professionalism enhances the psychological impact of AI readiness, enabling nurses to adapt confidently in evolving healthcare environments.Implications of the studyNursing leadership and education should integrate AI literacy with professional development to build a confident, ethically grounded workforce capable of leveraging AI for improved care outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Self-efficacy, Artificial intelligence readiness, Professionalism, Nursing, Digital health, Psychological empowerment

Introduction

The digital transformation of healthcare systems is reshaping the future of nursing practice [1]. Among the key drivers of this transformation is Artificial Intelligence (AI) [2]. As AI continues to evolve, it offers nurses unprecedented opportunities to improve care delivery, clinical decision-making, documentation, and patient outcomes [3, 4]. In contemporary nursing environments, AI is not only a technological tool but also an enabler of new models of care, ranging from predictive analytics for early detection of patient deterioration to AI-supported triage systems, risk assessment platforms, and even decision-support software that enhances diagnostic accuracy and workflow efficiency [5]. Nurses increasingly rely on AI-driven tools for scheduling, staffing optimization, and the reduction of administrative workload, thereby allowing more time for patient-centered care [6]. These uses demonstrate that AI has moved beyond theoretical potential into practical application in daily nursing tasks, shaping how nurses deliver, monitor, and evaluate care [7].

However, the success of integrating AI into nursing practice depends not only on technological advancements but also on nurses’ readiness to embrace and apply these innovations effectively [8]. This readiness, combined with professionalism and a strong sense of self-efficacy, plays a pivotal role in shaping nursing performance in the AI-driven era [9]. In this respect, AI readiness reflects more than a willingness to adopt technology; it signals a broader cultural and professional transformation where nurses must adapt their practice philosophies to integrate human compassion with machine intelligence [10].

Moreover, the growing complexity of contemporary healthcare demands more than technical proficiency from nurses—it requires emotional intelligence, ethical sensitivity, and adaptability [11]. In this evolving landscape, professionalism and self-efficacy have become vital components of nursing competence, especially as emerging technologies reshape care delivery [12]. While AI readiness ensures nurses can effectively engage with smart systems, professionalism guides ethical behavior, collaboration, and accountability. Meanwhile, self-efficacy—a nurse’s confidence in their ability to perform tasks—provides the psychological foundation for embracing innovation [13]. Together, these three elements form a triad essential for the safe, ethical, and sustainable integration of AI into nursing practice [14].

This dynamic is particularly salient in Egypt, where the healthcare system is undergoing rapid digitalization [15]. Egyptian nurses are increasingly exposed to AI-enabled systems in tertiary hospitals, yet local studies report that readiness remains moderate, with uneven training and ethical uncertainties influencing adoption [11, 16]. While nurses demonstrate cautious optimism about AI, they also identify barriers such as limited digital competence and fear of job redundancy, underscoring the need for both structural support and professional guidance [17]. Thus, examining AI readiness in combination with professionalism and self-efficacy offers critical insights for strengthening Egypt’s nursing workforce and aligning it with global digital transformation.

Background

AI readiness refers to an individual’s or organization’s preparedness to adopt and utilize AI technologies effectively [18]. In nursing, AI readiness includes knowledge of AI systems, openness to change, and confidence in using digital tools [19]. Given the increasing reliance on AI for monitoring, diagnostics, and decision support, it is critical that nurses are equipped with the competencies needed to function in technology-rich environments [20]. AI readiness in this sense is not merely a matter of technical skills but also encompasses cognitive openness, cultural acceptance, and professional responsibility toward technology adoption [21]. As such, readiness becomes an enabling condition for innovation, ensuring that nurses can move from awareness of AI to meaningful application in their clinical routines [22].

The successful implementation of AI tools in healthcare depends on nurses’ perceptions and attitudes toward these technologies [23]. Studies indicate that nurses who perceive AI positively are more likely to use it effectively, improve clinical accuracy, and enhance patient safety [11, 24]. However, barriers such as high system costs, lack of training, and fear of role redundancy hinder AI adoption [25]. These barriers are particularly prominent in low- and middle-income countries like Egypt, where resource constraints can exacerbate gaps in training and limit the institutional investment required for smooth AI integration [16]. Addressing these contextual challenges requires a combination of policy support, continuous education, and cultural change within nursing practice [26].

Given the increasing importance of AI literacy in healthcare, readiness among nurses is no longer optional—it is foundational to ensuring safe, effective, and innovative care [20]. Nonetheless, AI readiness alone does not guarantee successful integration. The way nurses translate their readiness into practice is influenced by both individual and contextual factors, including professionalism and self-efficacy [21].

Professionalism in nursing encompasses a set of values, behaviors, and commitments that reflect ethical practice, accountability, and dedication to quality care [26]. It is reflected in how nurses communicate, solve problems, make ethical decisions, and collaborate with others [27]. Professionalism is also essential for fostering trust with patients, promoting teamwork, and improving organizational culture. When linked with AI integration, professionalism ensures that technology adoption is guided by ethical principles, safeguarding patient rights, data privacy, and equity of care [28]. Nurses’ professional values become crucial in balancing technological efficiency with human compassion, a balance that remains at the heart of nursing identity [29].

Professionalism supports nurses in adapting to changing clinical environments and technologies [30]. It promotes reflective practice, critical thinking, and lifelong learning—all of which are essential for effective AI integration [31]. Moreover, professional behavior has been linked to increased job satisfaction, role clarity, and improved patient outcomes [8]. Importantly, professionalism also contributes to building self-efficacy. Nurses with strong professional identity and commitment often perceive themselves as competent and capable of handling complex tasks, including those involving AI [32]. Thus, professionalism not only enhances work performance but also reinforces confidence in embracing technological change [10].

Self-efficacy, as defined by Bandura (2023), is the belief in one’s capacity to organize and execute actions required to achieve specific outcomes [33]. In nursing, high self-efficacy is associated with improved clinical judgment, patient advocacy, resilience, and willingness to adopt new practices [34]. It also supports critical competencies such as patient communication, team collaboration, and decision-making under pressure [35]. Self-efficacy in the AI era becomes even more critical, as nurses must learn to trust their own competence while integrating new tools that can sometimes challenge traditional workflows. A confident nurse is more likely to embrace innovation, solve problems effectively, and mentor peers in adapting to technological change [36].

Several studies affirm that self-efficacy influences nurses’ readiness to use AI technologies and contributes to successful practice innovation [37, 38]. Furthermore, types of self-efficacy—including academic, social, and career-related—are crucial for maintaining competency and adapting to evolving professional roles [39]. However, self-efficacy can be hindered by internal and external barriers such as lack of problem-solving strategies, unsupportive work environments, and negative past experiences [40]. Therefore, building self-efficacy requires more than training—it demands a supportive, professional culture and readiness for innovation [38].

Significance of study

Despite the growing body of literature on AI in healthcare, few studies have examined how nurses’ professionalism and AI readiness interact to influence self-efficacy, particularly within the Egyptian context. Much of the current research either isolates these constructs or focuses broadly on technology acceptance, neglecting the specific interplay of professionalism, readiness, and confidence that defines effective nursing performance [16, 17]. In Egypt, where nurses constitute a central pillar of healthcare delivery, the integration of AI poses both opportunities and challenges [15].

Recent evidence suggests moderate AI readiness among Egyptian nurses, coupled with ethical concerns and training limitations [11, 16, 25]. These realities underscore the urgent need to understand how professionalism can amplify AI readiness and translate it into heightened self-efficacy. By situating this study in Egypt, we highlight a context where rapid technological adoption coincides with pressing demands for quality and patient safety, making the findings highly relevant for policy, education, and leadership. This study therefore provides not only an academic contribution but also actionable insights to guide capacity-building programs, ensuring that Egyptian nurses are well-prepared, ethically grounded, and confident in the AI-driven future of healthcare.

Conceptual framework and theoretical underpinning

This study is grounded in Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), which posits that behavior is shaped by the reciprocal interaction of personal, environmental, and behavioral factors [33]. Self-efficacy lies at the core of this theory, serving as a key determinant of how people think, behave, and feel [39]. According to SCT, individuals develop self-efficacy through mastery experiences, vicarious learning, verbal persuasion, and emotional arousal [41].

In the context of nursing, professionalism provides frequent mastery experiences through role performance, ethical challenges, and collaborative problem-solving. These experiences reinforce self-efficacy by confirming competence and fostering confidence [42, 43]. Similarly, AI readiness—through exposure to technology, training, and innovation—can enhance vicarious learning and perceived capability [44]. Within this theoretical lens, our conceptual framework explicitly models AI readiness and professionalism as direct predictors of self-efficacy, with professionalism further hypothesized to moderate the AI readiness–self-efficacy relationship. This means that nurses with strong professionalism are expected to derive greater self-efficacy from the same level of AI readiness compared to those with weaker professionalism. By capturing both direct and interactive effects, the framework reflects the complex reality of nursing practice in an AI-enhanced environment and aligns with the analytical design of this study. The conceptual model developed for this study (Fig. 1) illustrates these hypothesized relationships, showing professionalism as a direct predictor of self-efficacy and as a moderator in the pathway between AI readiness and self-efficacy. Understanding this dynamic will inform leadership, education, and policy decisions aimed at cultivating a future-ready nursing workforce.

Conceptual model of the study

Aim of the study

This study aims to examine the relationship between nurses’ artificial intelligence readiness and their self-efficacy, with a specific focus on the moderating role of professionalism in shaping this relationship within clinical practice settings.

Research hypotheses

H1

There is a significant positive relationship between nurses’ artificial intelligence readiness and their self-efficacy.

H2

There is a significant positive relationship between nurses’ artificial intelligence readiness and their professionalism.

H3

There is a significant positive relationship between nurses’ professionalism and their self-efficacy.

H4

Professionalism influences the relationship between artificial intelligence readiness and self-efficacy among nurses.

Method

Research design

This study employed a descriptive, cross-sectional, and correlational research design. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Setting

The study was conducted in three tertiary healthcare hospitals: one hospital in Kafr Elsheikh Governorate and two hospitals in Alexandria Governorate. These hospitals were selected for several reasons. First, they represent large, technologically advanced healthcare institutions that have begun integrating digital health applications, including early-stage AI-enabled systems in areas such as electronic documentation, imaging analysis, and patient monitoring. This made them particularly suitable for studying nurses’ readiness to adopt AI and its relationship with professionalism and self-efficacy. Second, the hospitals employ a diverse nursing workforce that provides exposure to different specialties, units, and patient-care contexts, which enriches the representativeness of the data. Third, both hospitals are located in urban and semi urban areas where digital transformation is progressing faster compared to rural institutions, allowing us to capture nurses’ perceptions at the forefront of technological change. Finally, access feasibility and administrative approval facilitated the conduct of the study in these institutions, ensuring ethical compliance and logistical practicality. Thus, these hospitals were chosen as they offered a balance of advanced technological infrastructure, diverse nursing staff, and contextual relevance to the study objectives.

Subjects and sampling

The study sample consisted of 278 staff nurses working in inpatient medical and surgical units in three tertiary hospitals in Egypt. A convenience sampling technique was employed. The sample size was calculated using G*Power statistical software (version 3.1.9.7) to ensure adequate statistical power for detecting medium effect sizes in correlation and regression analyses. Assuming a power of 0.80, an alpha level of 0.05, and a medium effect size (r = 0.3), the minimum required sample was estimated at 278 participants.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) registered nurses with at least one year of clinical experience (2), currently employed in inpatient units, outpatient clinics, or critical care settings, and (3) willingness to participate and provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) nursing interns and students (2), administrative staff not involved in direct patient care, and (3) nurses who were on extended leave during the data collection period. These criteria ensured that participants were practicing nurses with sufficient clinical exposure to interact with or be influenced by emerging AI-enabled systems.

The recruitment process followed a multi-step approach. A total of 780 nurses were approached across the three hospitals. Of these, 430 were excluded either because they did not meet eligibility criteria or declined participation. A total of 350 nurses met the inclusion criteria and consented to participate. After excluding incomplete responses (n = 72), the final sample comprised 278 nurses. This recruitment process is illustrated in a flow chart (Fig. 2).

Data collection tools

Data were collected using three standardized and validated instruments.

The Nursing Profession Self-Efficacy Scale, developed by Caruso et al. (2016) [45], was used to assess nurses’ professional self-efficacy. The tool consists of 19 items measuring two dimensions: Attributes of Caring Situations (12 items) and Professional Situations (7 items). Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The total score reflects the overall self-efficacy level and is categorized as low (< 50%), moderate (50% to < 75%), and high (≥ 75%). In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha for this tool was 0.918, indicating excellent internal consistency.

The Artificial Intelligence Readiness Scale (AIRS), developed by Karaca et al. (2021) [46], was utilized to measure nurses’ readiness to adopt and utilize AI technologies in clinical practice. The scale comprises 22 items distributed across four subscales: cognition (8 items), ability (8 items), vision (3 items), and ethics (3 items). Items are rated using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The total score ranges from 22 to 110, with higher scores reflecting greater readiness for AI integration. Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient for the scale was 0.972.

The Nurse Professionalism Scale, developed by De Braganca and Nirmala (2020) [47], was applied to evaluate professionalism among nurses. This instrument contains 22 items grouped into five dimensions: professional advancement and development (4 items), management (6 items), nursing practice (6 items), professional responsibility and accountability (4 items), and valuing human beings (2 items). Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Higher total scores indicate higher levels of professionalism. Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient for this scale was 0.951.

Sociodemographic data sheet

Alongside these instruments, a structured sociodemographic sheet was developed by the researchers to collect personal and professional background data. This included gender, age, marital status, educational qualification, years of nursing experience, and current unit of practice.

Translation, validity, and reliability

The instruments were translated into Arabic using a forward–backward translation procedure to ensure linguistic and cultural equivalence. Content validity was established through evaluation by a panel of five academic experts in nursing, who assessed each item for clarity, relevance, and cultural appropriateness. The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) for the Nursing Profession Self-Efficacy Scale ranged from 0.85 to 1.00, with a scale-level CVI (S-CVI/Ave) of 0.93. For the Artificial Intelligence Readiness Scale, the I-CVI values ranged from 0.86 to 1.00, and the S-CVI/Ave was 0.95. For the Nurse Professionalism Scale, the I-CVI ranged from 0.87 to 1.00, with an overall S-CVI/Ave of 0.94. These results indicate excellent content validity across all instruments. A pilot study was conducted with 30 nurses to assess the clarity, feasibility, and estimated time required to complete the tools. The results of the pilot study were used only to refine and validate the instruments and were not included in the final dataset to avoid biasing the main analysis.

To further verify construct validity, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted for the three instruments using maximum likelihood estimation. The results confirmed the original factor structures and demonstrated acceptable to good model fit. For the Nursing Profession Self-Efficacy Scale, the fit indices were χ²/df = 2.12, Comparative Fit Index (CFI) = 0.95, Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI) = 0.94, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) = 0.059, and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) = 0.047. For the Artificial Intelligence Readiness Scale, the results were χ²/df = 2.25, CFI = 0.96, TLI = 0.95, RMSEA = 0.061, and SRMR = 0.043. For the Nurse Professionalism Scale, the fit indices were χ²/df = 1.97, CFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.052, and SRMR = 0.045. These values all fall within the acceptable thresholds recommended in the literature (CFI and TLI ≥ 0.90, RMSEA ≤ 0.08, SRMR ≤ 0.08), thereby supporting the factorial validity of the tools.

Data collection procedures

Data collection took place over a four-month period, from November 2023 to February 2024. After obtaining administrative approval, the researchers met with nurse managers in the selected hospitals to coordinate the recruitment process and schedule suitable times for data collection that did not disrupt clinical duties. Eligible nurses were approached individually during their break times or at the end of their shifts. The study purpose, procedures, and voluntary nature of participation were explained clearly, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation.

The questionnaires were administered in paper-based format and completed on-site in a quiet area designated by the nursing administration to ensure privacy. Each nurse was provided with the full set of study tools along with standardized written instructions. On average, completion required 20–25 min. The researchers remained available to clarify questions while ensuring they did not influence participants’ responses. Completed questionnaires were placed in sealed envelopes and collected directly by the researchers to maintain confidentiality and prevent access by hospital staff or supervisors. Anonymity was strictly protected, as no identifying information was requested on the forms.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Nursing at Kafr Elsheikh University, Egypt no: KFSIRB200-723. The study adhered to ethical principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration, protecting participants’ rights and well-being. All participants provided written informed consent following a thorough explanation of the study’s objectives. Participants’ privacy and anonymity were rigorously protected, and all data collected was treated with the utmost confidentiality. Furthermore, participants were explicitly informed that they had the right to withdraw from the study at any time. In addition, formal permission to use the three standardized instruments was obtained from the original authors prior to data collection. This ensured that both ethical and intellectual property requirements were fully observed.

Statistical analysis

Data were reviewed, coded, and analyzed using IBM SPSS version 25. Reliability of the study tools was confirmed using Cronbach’s alpha. Descriptive statistics including means, standard deviations, and frequency distributions were used to summarize demographic data and scale scores. Inferential statistical analyses were conducted to test the study hypotheses. The Pearson correlation test was employed to determine the strength and direction of relationships among continuous variables. Independent samples t-tests were used to compare mean differences between two independent groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to evaluate differences across more than two groups. Path analysis was utilized to examine the direct and indirect relationships among AI readiness, professionalism, and self-efficacy. An alpha level of 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance across all analyses.

Discussion

This study explored the interplay between nurses’ professionalism, AI readiness, and self-efficacy, providing empirical support for a model where these constructs are interlinked in meaningful and mutually reinforcing ways. The results confirmed that both AI readiness and professionalism significantly predict self-efficacy among nurses, with professionalism also moderating the relationship between AI readiness and self-efficacy. These findings are situated within Bandura’s (2023) Social Cognitive Theory [33], which asserts that learning and behavior are shaped through the dynamic interaction of personal, behavioral, and environmental factors—especially self-efficacy, which serves as the motivational cornerstone in adopting new practices.

The positive relationship between professionalism and self-efficacy affirms the notion that nurses who exhibit higher ethical standards, accountability, and commitment to professional growth tend to feel more confident and competent in their clinical roles. Professionalism equips nurses with critical thinking, emotional regulation, and ethical decision-making abilities, which are key in navigating the complexities of modern healthcare environments. This supports earlier findings by Berdida (2024) [37] and Jiang et al. (2024) [28], who reported that professional identity fosters resilience and competence. However, this study goes beyond existing literature by showing that professionalism not only contributes to behavioral standards but also enhances the psychological confidence needed for innovation. Unlike earlier studies that considered professionalism as an outcome or trait [48, 49], our findings present it as an active enabler of adaptability and confidence, particularly in the face of technological advancement.

The significant positive association between AI readiness and self-efficacy highlights that technical knowledge, confidence in using digital tools, and openness to technological change bolster nurses’ belief in their ability to perform effectively. This finding aligns with the work of Eminoğlu and Çelikkanat (2024) [5], who established a similar link among nurse leaders, and with Jia and Tu (2024) [6], who emphasized the importance of integrating AI competencies with broader self-efficacy constructs. Notably, this study reveals that AI readiness is not merely a technical attribute but a psychological and motivational disposition. Nurses who score high in AI readiness are more likely to see technology not as a threat but as a tool that complements their professional functions. This finding diverges from earlier research [7, 9], which suggested that AI adoption may provoke fear and reduce confidence among nurses. Our participants, working in a supportive institutional context with access to training, demonstrated that a well-prepared environment can shift AI from being a source of stress to a confidence-enhancing asset.

The study also found a significant positive correlation between AI readiness and professionalism, which suggests that nurses who demonstrate higher professional standards are more inclined to embrace AI technologies. This supports the perspective of El-Sayed et al. (2025) [50] and Buchanan et al. (2020a, 2020b) [4, 7], who posited that AI has the potential to elevate professional performance when integrated into ethically sound and clinically competent practice. However, our study uniquely shows that this relationship is bidirectional: while professionalism facilitates ethical AI adoption, engagement with AI tools can, in turn, strengthen professional roles by promoting efficiency, clinical accuracy, and informed decision-making. In contrast to earlier studies that viewed these constructs as separate [14, 44], our findings emphasize their mutual reinforcement, thus contributing a novel perspective to the discourse on digital transformation in nursing.

Most notably, the path analysis revealed that both AI readiness and professionalism significantly and directly predict self-efficacy, with professionalism playing a moderating role. This interaction suggests that AI readiness alone does not guarantee increased self-efficacy unless it is grounded in a strong professional identity. Nurses with both high AI readiness and high professionalism reported significantly greater confidence in their abilities than those with only one of these attributes. This triangulated relationship offers a new theoretical insight: professionalism serves as a psychological and ethical scaffold that amplifies the benefits of technological preparedness. This finding supports Bandura’s (2023) assertion that self-efficacy is shaped not only by technical skill (mastery experiences) but also by one’s moral and social context (verbal persuasion and vicarious learning) [33]. It also aligns with Rony et al. (2024) [49], who emphasized the importance of ethical and professional grounding in facilitating AI integration and confidence-building among nurses.

Furthermore, this study challenges reductionist views that treat technology readiness as purely skill-based. By revealing how personal values and professional orientation shape the application of technological competencies, it urges a shift from fragmented training approaches to holistic strategies that blend technical training with ethical reflection and professional development. While previous studies focused primarily on developing AI competency frameworks [51, 52], our research extends the conversation by advocating for the integration of professionalism into such frameworks. This highlights the need for leadership and educational programs that do not treat AI as an isolated innovation but embed it within the ethical and professional responsibilities of nursing practice.

Implication of the study

The findings of this study highlight the need for nursing leadership to move beyond traditional skill-based training and foster an integrated environment that nurtures both technological competence and professional identity. Nurse leaders should champion strategic investments in continuous AI education while embedding professionalism-focused mentorship and reflective practice opportunities into organizational culture. Tailored leadership development programs that emphasize ethical reasoning, digital fluency, and psychological empowerment can enhance nurses’ self-efficacy and preparedness for AI-integrated care. Additionally, healthcare policies should mandate structured AI readiness training as a core component of nursing development frameworks, ensuring equitable access to digital learning resources and safeguarding ethical standards in AI use.

From a practice perspective, integrating AI tools should be accompanied by initiatives that reinforce nurses’ professional values, ensuring that technology enhances—not replaces—human-centered care. Nurse educators and clinical preceptors should incorporate simulation-based AI experiences, ethics case discussions, and interprofessional digital health modules to build both confidence and competence. Broader implications include redefining nursing competencies at national and international levels to include AI literacy as a core domain, while preserving the moral, emotional, and collaborative foundations of the profession. Health systems must view AI adoption not only as a technical transformation but as a cultural shift—where human expertise and machine intelligence co-evolve to strengthen patient outcomes and professional well-being.

Limitations and future directions

Although this study provides meaningful insights, it is important to note that the use of self-reported instruments may introduce bias, as participants could overestimate their levels of professionalism, AI readiness, or self-efficacy due to social desirability or limited self-awareness. Additionally, while the validated tools employed offered strong reliability and comparability, their standardized and structured nature may not fully capture the complex, evolving, and context-dependent dimensions of how nurses engage with AI technologies or express professional behavior in practice. These tools provide a necessary quantitative foundation but may miss subtle variations influenced by organizational culture, role expectations, or real-time clinical challenges. To deepen understanding, future studies should integrate mixed-method approaches—such as qualitative interviews or observational techniques—to explore how nurses internalize, experience, and operationalize AI readiness and professionalism in dynamic care environments.

Conclusion

This study offers compelling evidence that both artificial intelligence readiness and nursing professionalism play significant and interconnected roles in shaping nurses’ self-efficacy. It advances the current understanding by demonstrating that self-efficacy is not merely a function of technical competence but is deeply rooted in professional identity and ethical engagement. The findings underscore the importance of fostering a nursing workforce that is not only digitally competent but also grounded in strong professional values.
