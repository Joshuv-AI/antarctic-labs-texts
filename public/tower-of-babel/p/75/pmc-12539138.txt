Esketamine versus sufentanil as analgesics for sedation during painless gastrointestinal endoscopy: a double-blinded randomized controlled trial
Authors: Ting-Ting Li, Xiao-Lan He, Hai-Ling Tang, Qi-jun Li, Si-Ru Lian, Xiao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectiveMost troubling of painless gastrointestinal endoscopy is hypoxia and hypotension. Esketamine has sympathomimetic and respiratory protection, which combined with midazolam and propofol may reduce these troubles. So, this study aims to explore the efficacy and safety of this regimen.MethodsIn total, 318 patients who received painless gastrointestinal endoscopy from November 2022 to April 2023 were included and randomly divided into two groups at 1:1: 0.1 µg/kg sufentanil (Suf) or 0.15 mg/kg esketamine (Esk) group. Except for analgesics, all patients were given midazolam and propofol. The incidence of hypotension and hypoxia (SPO2 < 93%) during the examination was observed.ResultsCompared with the Suf, the Esk group reduced the incidence of hypotension (12.8% vs 36.2%, 95% confidence interval (CI) 13.20—33.09, P < 0.001), respiratory depression (apnea > 30 s) (2.1% vs 15.4%, 6.47—20.52, P < 0.001), and hypoxia (7.1% vs 16.1%, 1.07—16.92, P = 0.018), but may increase the incidence of coughing (P < 0.001) and increased secretions (P = 0.032). There was no significant difference in the examination and recovery time (P > 0.05).ConclusionEsketamine, midazolam, and propofol for painless gastrointestinal endoscopy can achieve opioid-free sedation and reduce the occurrence of hypotension and hypoxia. However, the dosages of this regimen need to be further explored to avoid coughing and increased secretions.Trial registrationThis study has been registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Center (https://www.chictr.org.cn/index.html, Date: November 3, 2022, No: ChiCTR2200065379).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13063-025-09073-5.Keywords: Esketamine, Painless gastrointestinal tract endoscopy, Hypotension, Hypoxia, Opioid-free sedation

Introduction

Gastroscopy and colonoscopy often require some form of analgesia and sedation to minimize patient discomfort and optimize operator performance [1]. The most commonly used sedatives are propofol [2] and benzodiazepines [3]. The sedative effect of propofol was better than midazolam in terms of patient and endoscopist satisfaction, and postoperative recovery [4]. However, the use of propofol alone has some shortcomings, such as easy to cause respiratory and circulation inhibition [2], which brings insecurity to the outpatient of endoscopy [5, 6]. The combined use of multiple drugs can meet the requirements of relevant diagnosis and treatment under the condition of reducing drug dose [7]. However, a meta-analysis showed that propofol combined with analgesics and/or benzodiazepines did not reduce the incidence of bradycardia, hypotension, and hypoxemia [8]. Continuous exploration of new drug combinations is of great significance to reduce the occurrence of related adverse reactions for painless gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy.

Esketamine, an antagonist of the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor, is the right racemic form of ketamine and has twice the anesthetic effect of racemic mixtures [9, 10]. Studies have shown that when esketamine and propofol were used in painless gastroscopy, the higher the dose of esketamine, the lower the likelihood of hypotension [11]. In addition, esketamine has no respiratory depressant effect compared to other sedatives and can even be used to reverse the respiratory depressant effect of remifentanil [12]. A study combined propofol, remifentanil, and esketamine in patients undergoing gastroenteroscopy and found that the use of small doses of esketamine resulted in less propofol and remifentanil, less respiratory depression [13]. But the program has not moved away from opioid use. Due to the risks of opioid resistance and addiction, there has been an increasing number of studies in favor of less opioid [14] or opioid-free sedation [15]. Whether the combination of esketamine, propofol, and midazolam for GI endoscopy can achieve the same advantages, there are no relevant studies reported.

Therefore, this study hypothesized that esketamine combined with midazolam and propofol for GI endoscopy can not only avoid the use of opioids, but also improve the safety of GI endoscopy, compared with opioids combined with midazolam and propofol.

Methods

This study was a single-center, prospective, randomized, double-blind, positive, parallel controlled clinical trial. All procedures were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of West China Hospital of Sichuan University (Chairman: Shaolin Deng, Date: September 28, 2022, No: 2022–1419) (Supplementary file-Ethics). This study had been registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Center (https://www.chictr.org.cn/index.html, Date: November 3, 2022, No.: ChiCTR2200065379). All authors had access to the study data and reviewed and approved the final manuscript.

Patients

Subjects were patients preparing for a painless GI endoscopy. Inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Adult (aged 18 years or older), regardless of gender; (2) Subjects voluntarily participated and signed informed consent; (3) American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status classification (ASA) I-II. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Patients with anticipated difficulty intubating, respiratory diseases and/or lung diseases such as sleep apnea syndrome; (2) Patients with severe heart diseases such as severe bradycardia and malignant arrhythmias; (3) Patients with mental disorders (such as schizophrenia, depression, etc.) and cognitive disorders; (4) A history of psychotropic and narcotic drug abuse; (5) Patients with migraine, epilepsy and other neurological diseases; (6) Patients with hypertension who are not receiving formal antihypertensive therapy or whose blood pressure (BP) is poorly controlled (Systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 160 mmHg or ≤ 90 mmHg, and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 100 mmHg or ≤ 60 mmHg during screening); (7) Patients with hyperthyroidism; (8) Diabetic subjects with unsatisfactory blood glucose control (randomized blood glucose during screening ≥ 11.1 mmol/L); (9) Identify those who are allergic to the investigational drug; (10) Pregnant woman; (11) Participants who had received general anesthesia within 7 days prior to randomization.

The randomization number form and drug number form (numbered according to the proposed analgesic) were randomized through an interactive network response randomization system, and were blinded by dedicated statisticians. Subjects who did not meet the study inclusion criteria or met any exclusion criteria were not eligible for randomized inclusion in the study.

Comparison

The Sufentanil group (Suf): Patients were treated with 0.1 µg/kg sufentanil combined with 0.01–0.02 mg/kg midazolam and an appropriate amount of propofol for painless GI endoscopy.

The Ketamine group (Esk): 0.15 mg/kg Esketamine was used for analgesia, and other sedation regimens were the same as those in the Suf group.

Intervention and research process

After entering the examination room, patients were given standard vital signs monitoring (including heart rate (HR), non-invasive BP, transcutaneous oxygen saturation (SpO2) and respiratory rate (RR)) and oxygen inhalation via nasal catheter (oxygen flow rate 2–4 L/min). Subsequently, 0.01–0.02 mg/kg midazolam and the corresponding analgesic (0.1 ug/kg sufentanil or 0.15 mg/kg esketamine) were administered. Then, the propofol was slowly administered intravenously until the patient reached grade 3 or below on the Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S). During the examination, propofol was appropriately added at 0.3–0.5 mg/kg to maintain the MOAA/S score of 0–3 points. After examination, the patient was sent to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) for observation until the Aldrete score was greater than 9. Patients were followed up 24 h after leaving the hospital by telephone (Supplementary file-Fig. 1).

Outcomes

Primary outcome: (1) Incidence of hypoxia (SpO2 < 93% under conditions without any treatment) during examination; (2) Incidence of hypotension (SBP < 90 mmHg or SBP decreased by 30% above baseline) during examination.

Secondary outcome: (1) Incidence of respiratory depression (RD, the duration of the respiratory arrest was longer than 30 seconds (s)), low HR (less than 50 bpm), hypertension (SBP > 160 mmHg or rising by more than 30% of the pre-test baseline, or DBP ≧ 100 mmHg), cough (as the endoscope passes through the throat), body movement, hiccups, and oral secretions (more than 20 ml) during the examination; (2) Time of GI endoscopy and dosage of propofol (including induction and maintenance amount); (3) Adverse reactions after anesthesia during the PACU, including the incidence of nausea, vomiting, dizziness, abdominal distension, and abdominal pain, et al.; (4) Adverse reactions that occurred within 24 hours (h) of hospital discharge; (5) Patien’s satisfaction (range of 0 to 10 point. 0 point was not satisfied, while 10 point was very satisfied) after the examination.

Statistical analysis

In the preliminary experiment, the incidence of hypotension was 38.5%, and hypoxia was 19.2% in the Suf group. Incidence of hypotension and hypoxia in the Esk group was 15.8% and 10.5%, respectively. The α = 0.05 (bilateral), (1-β) = 80%. Given a possible 10% lost to follow-up rate, a minimum of 42 patients in each group was calculated as 1:1 based on the incidence of hypotension and 159 patients in each group as 1:1 based on the incidence of hypoxia.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 software (IBM, Inc., Chicago, USA), α = 0.05 (two-sided tests). The t-test, Wilcoxon coincidence rank sum test, and the Fisher exact probability method were used for statistical analysis. Figures were produced by GraphPad Prism 9.00 (San Diego, California, USA).

Results

A total of 480 patients who underwent painless GI endoscopy from November 7, 2022, to April 14, 2023, were recruited. And 318 patients were screened for randomization. A total of 315 patients completed the study, and 25 patients were lost to follow-up. Finally, 290 patients were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of patient based on CONSORT 2010 Checklist. Note: n number(s)

Of the 290 patients, 43.1% were male and 56.9% were female. The age was 50.6 ± 12.8 years, and those aged above 60 years were 23.4%. The mean of the s’ body mass index (BMI) was 22.8 ± 2.9 kg/m2. Of the 290 patients, 92 (31.7%) underwent gastroscopy, 39 (13.4%) underwent colonoscopy, and 159 (54.8%) had both gastrointestinal endoscopies. (Supplementary file-Table 1).

Hypoxia and hypotension during the examination

The incidence of hypoxia in the Esk group was 7.1% (vs 16.1%, mean difference (95% CI) 9.02 (1.07, 16.92), P = 0.018, Fig. 2A), which was lower than that the Suf group. And the incidence of hypotension in the Esk group was 12.8% (vs 36.2%, mean difference (95% CI) 23.48 (13.20, 33.09), P < 0.001, Fig. 2B), that also was lower than the Suf group.

Other adverse reactions during the examination

The incidence of RD in the Esk group was lower than the Suf group (2.1% vs 15.4%, mean difference (95% CI) 13.31 (6.47, 20.52), P < 0.001, Fig. 2C). There was no significant difference in the incidence of hypertension, low HR, body movement, and hiccups (Supplementary file-Fig. 2A-–D). However, patients in the Esk group have a higher incidence of cough response (27.9% vs 9.4%, mean difference (95% CI) 18.26 (8.92, 27.44), P < 0.001,Fig. 2D) and increased secretions (5.0% vs 0.7%, mean difference (95% CI) 4.29 (0.34, 9.25), P = 0.032, Fig. 2E) than those in the Suf group.

Propofol dosage and time of endoscopy

Compared to the Suf group, the Esk group was lower at the induction dosage of propofol (58.1 ± 16.5 vs 62.5 ± 16.6 mg, mean difference (95% CI): 4.50 (0.66, 8.33), P = 0.022, Supplementary file-Fig. 3A). But the total amount dosage of propofol was more in the Esk group throughout the endoscopy period (126.3 ± 58.0 vs 112.8 ± 50.4 mg, mean difference (95% CI) −13.47 (−26.04, −0.91), P = 0.036, Supplementary file-Fig. 3B). The time to complete endoscopy was 14.2 ± 7.9 min (Suf: 13.7 ± 7.5 min vs. Esk: 14.8 ± 8.3 min, P = 0.247).

Adverse reactions during PACU

At the end of the examination, the mean of the MOAA/S was 3.1 ± 1.6 in the Suf group and about 2.7 ± 1.7 in the Esk group (95% CI 0.19 (0.05, 0.81), P = 0.029, Fig. 3A). The difference in time from the end of the examination to recovery to MOAA/S at 5 points (Fig. 3B) and Aldrete over 9 points (Fig. 3C) was not statistically significant in the two groups. A total of 12 patients had adverse reactions during the PACU; all were mild adverse reactions, and all improved after symptomatic treatment. The adverse reactions observed in the PACU were mainly SpO2 reduction, low HR, hypotension, cough, dizziness, abdominal pain, and abdominal distension (Table 1).

Adverse reactions that occurred within 24 h of hospital discharge

All adverse events at 24 h after leaving the hospital were mild to moderate (about 22.8%) and improved after rest or symptomatic treatment (Table 2). Among them, the most common adverse event was abdominal pain (14.1%), followed by dizziness (6.9%) and throat discomfort (2.4%). Compared with the Suf group (3.4%), patients in the Esk group had a higher incidence of dizziness (10.6%, P = 0.020).

Patient satisfaction

Patient’s satisfaction in the two groups was 9.9 ± 0.4 (Suf) and 9.8 ± 0.6 (Esk), respectively (P = 0.136). About 33.1% of the patients experienced fatigue after examination (Suf 36.1%, Esk 30.5%, P = 0.382).

Discussion

Esketamine could reduce the incidence of hypotension, hypoxia, and respiratory depression during GI endoscopy, compared with sufentanil as analgesics for sedation. Hypotension and hypoxia were common problems encountered during painless GI endoscopy [5]. Reducing the occurrence of these events as much as possible was important for maintaining patient safety. The unique pharmacological features of esketamine make it not only both sedative and analgesic, but also sympathomimetic, with little effect of respiratory depression [10]. A prospective randomized controlled study showed that esketamine and propofol can improve the development of hypoxemia in patients compared with sufentanil and propofol [16]. Yuanjing Chen et al. used a similar anesthesia regimen in patients undergoing endoscopic varicose vein ligation and also found that esketamine combined with propofol reduced the occurrence of hypotension and hypoxia [17]. Similar findings were demonstrated in low-dose esketamine use in elderly patients [18]. Esketamine provided a more stable hemodynamic state and respiratory protective function, which was similar to the results of this study. Unlike the previous study, in order to reduce the side effects, this study used the similar solutions combined midazolam for painless GI endoscopy. This study found that the combination of esketamine with midazolam and propofol for painless GI endoscopy can reduce the occurrence of hypotension and hypoxia.

Yankun Feng et al. used a lower dose (0.15 mg/kg) esketamine with propofol in elderly patients and found that the regimen of esketamine can reduce propofol use compared with sufentanil [18], and similar results were shown in other studies [19]. In this study, propofol was used for lower anesthesia induction dosage when combined with esketamine, compared with sufentanil. However, the dosage of propofol for maintaining sedation in the esketamine group was higher than that in the sufentanil group. This may be caused by the incidence of cough reaction and secretion during examination, which makes the user mistakenly think that the patient had insufficient sedation [20]. In addition, good analgesia was also conducive to the suppression of the throat cough reaction [5]. The higher incidence of cough may be due to the lower analgesic effect of esketamine than sufentanil. Furthermore, the combination of esketamine may increase the incidence of postoperative dizziness, compared with sufentanil. This may be related to the psychomimetic effect of esketamine [21]. These were not reported in previous studies [16–18].

In contrast to previous literature, this study was the first to propose an opioid-free sedation, combined with esketamine, midazolam, and propofol, for painless GI endoscopy. We found that this regimen reduced the incidence of hypotension and hypoxia during painless GI endoscopy compared with existing opioid regimens, but may lead to inter-examination cough reaction, increased secretions, and a risk of dizziness after examination. This opioid-free sedation regimen was beneficial to further improve the safety during painless GI endoscopy. However, further exploration may be needed in the dosage of drugs to avoid the occurrence of discomfort such as cough reaction. In addition, this study explored the risk factors for hypoxia and hypotension during painless endoscopy. This part of the results helped us further avoid the occurrence of related adverse reactions in clinical work, which was not mentioned in previous studies. Of course, there are some limitations to this study. Based on the combination of the three drugs, the dose of esketamine was lower than in other studies. This may lead to an increase of adverse reactions such as cough reaction in this study, thereby weakening the advantage of this combination. In the later study, we will try new dose exploration, or the improvement of the target population, to clarify the strengths of this study protocol.

Conclusion

The use of esketamine with midazolam and propofol for painless GI endoscopy can not only achieve opioid-free sedation, but also significantly reduce the occurrence of hypotension and hypoxia. However, the relevant combination dose of this regimen for painless GI endoscopy may require further exploration to avoid the occurrence of discomfort such as cough.
