Heterogeneous origins and functions of lung mesenchymal stromal cells
Authors: Danyi Peng, Ke Cao, Ruili Yang, Yongfeng Luo, Hui Chen, Satish K. Madala, Matthew E. Thornton, Brendan H. Grubbs, Elizabeth P. Henske, Susan M. Majka, Wei Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) and their secretomes have been extensively studied for the treatment of various pulmonary diseases; however, consistent therapeutic benefits have yet to be demonstrated. The fundamental biology of lung-resident MSCs remains poorly understood.MethodsWe used a previously characterized fetal lung mesenchyme-specific reporter mouse model to initially identify lung MSC subpopulations based on their developmental origins. Bulk RNA-seq was then used to confirm mouse lung MSC differences at the transcriptomic level and to separate human lung MSC subsets. A variety of in vitro methods were used to measure cell proliferation, differentiation, adhesion, T cell modulation, alveolar epithelial cell growth.ResultsWe identified two distinct subsets of mouse lung resident MSCs: one originating from embryonic lung mesenchymal progenitors, and another with an as-yet undetermined tissue origin. While both subsets exhibit core MSC characteristics, transcriptomic analyses revealed distinct gene expression profiles, which were conserved in MSCs isolated from human lungs. These subsets also differed in key biological functions, including proliferation, adhesion, immunomodulation, and their capacity to support alveolar epithelial cell growth in both mouse and human systems.ConclusionsTwo different lung MSC subpopulations have been identified and characterized. These findings advance our understanding of the complexity of mesenchymal progenitors in lung injury repair and disease and may inform the development of effective stem cell-based therapies for pulmonary conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04716-5.

Introduction

Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) are unique mesenchymal progenitor cells [1, 2], which were originally defined based on their clonogenicity, selective cell surface marker expression, and multiple differentiation potential in culture [3]. Bone marrow is believed as one of the sources for these precursor cells that can populate in other adult tissues [4, 5]. Other tissue resident MSCs are also found to be important in organogenesis, tissue repair and regeneration, and inflammatory response, and lung resident MSCs have been identified in human and other species [6–10]. Bone marrow and other tissue-derived MSCs have been tested in clinical trials for treating certain pulmonary diseases but compelling evidence of benefit has not yet been consistently observed [11, 12]. A key barrier is the limited understanding of lung MSC origins, heterogeneity, biological properties, and functional roles in pulmonary health and disease, which constrains the development of effective MSC-based therapies.

During development, the lung epithelium arises from the anterior foregut endoderm, which invaginates into the surrounding splanchnic mesoderm to form the lung primordium. Reciprocal epithelial-mesenchymal interactions are essential for lung organogenesis. Like lung epithelia, fetal lung mesenchymal progenitor cells are specified at the beginning of organ formation and give rise to diverse interstitial lineages, including airway chondrocytes, a variety of fibroblasts, airway and vascular smooth muscle cells (SMCs), pericytes, and endothelial cells during development [13]. Post-developmentally, lung MSCs are assumed to be critical in maintaining tissue homeostasis, and their alterations have been implicated in several pulmonary diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, and lung fibrosis [14–16]. To better understand lung MSC biology and its relationship to lung disease, we investigated lung resident MSC origins and heterogeneity, as well as the differences in their biological activities using a previously characterized Tbx4-rtTA/TetO-Cre/mT-mG genetic mouse model [13]. In this system, the Tbx4 lung enhancer drives rtTA expression specifically in fetal lung mesenchymal progenitors. Upon doxycycline induction, rtTA activates Cre expression, which excises the floxed-mTomato (mT) DNA cassette and switches reporter expression from default mT to downstream mGFP (mG), permanently labeling these progenitors and their descendants with GFP. This strategy enables distinction between MSCs derived from fetal lung mesenchymal progenitors (mG+) and MSCs of other origins (mT+).

Using this approach, we identified for the first time two distinct subsets of mouse lung MSCs: one derived from embryonic lung mesenchymal progenitors and another of a previously unrecognized origin. These subsets exhibit distinct transcriptomic profiles, suggesting functional divergence. Moreover, transcriptomic analysis of human lung-resident MSCs revealed two analogous subsets that mirror those found in mice. Functional assays further demonstrated subset-specific differences in proliferation, adhesion, immunosuppressive capacity, and support for alveolar epithelial growth. Together, these studies reveal the developmental origins and heterogeneity of lung MSCs in both mice and humans. This newly recognized lung resident MSC complexity has important implications for understanding lung injury repair and disease mechanisms and may inform innovative MSC-based therapeutic strategies.

Methods

The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Mouse strains, breeding, and genotyping

Lung mesenchyme-specific reporter mice were generated by crossing Tbx4-lung enhancer-rtTA/TetO-Cre mice and mT-mG reporter mice with doxycycline induction (625 mg/kg chow and 0.5 mg/ml drink water) from embryonic day (E) 6.5 to postnatal day (P) 1 [13]. The Tbx4-rtTA mouse line was generated in our lab [13]. The TetO-Cre mouse line was provided by Dr. Jeffrey Whitsett at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center [17]. The mT-mG mice were obtained from Jackson laboratory [18]. Sftpc-GFP mice were obtained from Dr. Saverio Bellusci [19]. All mice have C57BL/6 strain background. Both male and female mice were used for cell isolation and analysis. Mice were housed in pathogen-free conditions, and the procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees at Children’s Hospital Los Angeles and at University of Cincinnati.

Mouse lung MSCs isolation, culture, and characterization

A modified method for isolating bone marrow MSCs has been used [20]. Briefly, the experimental mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation followed by a thoracotomy procedure. The lung was first de-blooded by intra-cardiac perfusion of PBS prior to isolation, and the harvested lung tissue was minced into 1 mm3, followed by enzymatic digestion (2 mg/ml dispase II, 1 mg/ml collagenase and 200 U/ml DNase) at 37 °C for 1 h with intermittent pipetting. The cell suspension was filtered through 70 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 1600 rpm for 6 min at 4°C. The cell pellet was resuspended in α-MEM medium (Corning) supplemented with 20% of FBS (Avantor), 100 U/ml of penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich), 2 mM of glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich), and 55 µM of β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich) and seeded at 5 × 104 cells per 100 mm plastic culture dish. Dexamethasone was included in the culture medium after day 10, which started with 5 nM at the beginning and increased to 10 nM on day 13 and thereafter. About 2 weeks, cells from the expanding colonies were trypsinized and passed to fresh dishes (passage 1). The cells from passage 2 were used for general MSC surface marker analyses or flow sorting to separate membrane GFP (mG)-positive cells from membrane tdTomato (mT)-positive cells. These isolated mG+ and mT+ lung MSCs were expanded and use for other characterization. The cell surface markers were stained with APC-conjugated antibodies (Supplemental Table 1) followed by flow cytometry analysis.

Human lung MSCs isolation, culture, and characterization

Deidentified fresh normal human lung tissue was obtained from the local organ procurement office, LifeCenter Cincinnati, under the University of Cincinnati IRB protocol number 2013–8157. A small piece (0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 cm3) of lung tissue was processed and digested using the same method described for the mouse lung above. The suspended cells were plated at 5 × 104 cells per 100 mm dish and cultured using the same medium described above. At day 14, individual cell colonies were isolated using a cloning ring approach. The cells were expanded and used for a variety of analyses at passage 4–5, which included cell surface marker staining, cell proliferation and differentiation, cell adhesion, and 3-D epithelial cell culture.

RNA isolation and RT-PCR

Cells were lysed with TRIzol™ (Invitrogen) directly in culture dishes and transferred to a 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tube, followed by phase separation with chloroform. After centrifugation, the upper aqueous phase was transferred to a new microcentrifuge tube for subsequent isolation steps using RNeasy Micro Kit (QIAGEN). cDNA synthesis was performed using iScript™ cDNA synthesis kit (BIO-RAD) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time RT-PCR analysis was carried out using the primers listed in Supplemental Tables 2 and relative difference of gene expression was calculated using ΔΔCT [21].

Bulk RNA-seq and analysis of differential expression genes and gene ontology (GO)

Total RNA quality was checked with a bioanalyzer (Agilent) with RIN greater than 7.0 being acceptable. RNA-sequencing was performed at Novogene (Sacramento, CA). Data processing was performed using the University of Southern California high performance-computing cluster (https://carc.usc.edu/). Roughly 20 million 150 bp paired-end sequences were aligned to either the Ensembl 107 annotation based on the Genome Reference Consortium mouse genome (GRCm39) or the Gencode H45 based on the Genome Reference Consortium human genome (GRCh38.p14) using the STAR aligner [22, 23]. Differential gene expression was determined using the R/Bioconductor software ‘edgeR’ [24]. Genes were considered significantly different if their false discovery rate (FDR) corrected P-values were less than 0.05 and the absolute value of the log2-fold change was equal or greater than 1.0. The sequencing data were deposited to the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository with accession number GSE299841 for mouse lung MSCs and GSE299778 for human lung MSCs.

Heatmap and smear plot were used for the visualization of the differentially expressed genes. Heatmaps of reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM) were made using the lists of statistically significant differentially expressed genes using the R software “gplots”. After isolation of the genes which had the same expression trend between the two subsets lung MSCs in mouse and human lungs, GO analysis was performed to facilitate elucidating the biological implications of the differentially expressed genes in the experiment [25]. Fisher’s exact test was applied to identify the significant GO categories (FDR < 0.05).

Cell proliferation, differentiation, and adhesion

Cell proliferation was measured using EdU labeling with Click-iT® EdU kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) [26]. In brief, the same numbers of cells were seeded to 8-well chamber slides and cultured for 24 h. Then, EdU was added to the culture medium with final concentration of 10µM for another 24 h. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5%Triton-X 100, and stained with azide-conjugated fluorophore and DAPI. EdU-positive nuclei and total nuclei were then quantified from the confocal images using Image J software. Mouse lung MSCs from multiple isolations and human lung MSCs from different individual colonies (>3 colonies per subset) were used for this measurement, and technical replicates were included for each sample.

Osteoblast and adipocyte differentiations were performed as described previously [20], and they were detected by Alizarin Red staining and Oil Red O staining, respectively. The positively stained areas were quantified by Image J. For smooth muscle cell differentiation, the lung MSCs were cultured with basal medium 231 plus smooth muscle differentiation supplement (ThermoFisher) for 10 days, and smooth muscle cells were assessed by immunostaining of both Acta2 and Myh11.

Cell adhesion assay was performed for individual colonies of human lung MSCs using CytoSelect™ 48-well cell adhesion assay kit (CBA-070, Cell Biolabs). Briefly, 5 × 104 cells in serum-free media were added to each well. After 1 h culture at 37 °C, the unattached cells in the media were discarded, followed by cell staining and dye extraction. 150 µl lysate from each extracted sample was transferred to a 96 well plate to measure the absorbance at 560 nm by a 96-well plate reader.

T cell suppression activity of MSCs

The effect of MSCs on T cell proliferation was measured following a published method [27]. Briefly, 5 × 103 cells per well of human lung MSCs were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h. 1 × 104 human T cells (Jurkat cells, ATCC) were then added to each well and cultured in RPMI1640 with 10% FBS. After 120-hour co-culture, the suspension of Jurkat cells was transferred to another 96-well plate and the cell numbers were quantified using CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution Reagent (Promega). After 4 hours of incubation at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator, absorbance at 485 nm was determined by a plate reader. Technical triplicates were used for each sample.

Cell tube forming assay

The capabilities of lung MSC subsets to form a vascular-like tube network in culture were compared using a published method [28]. Briefly, each well of 96-well plate was pre-coated by 400 µl diluted Matrigel (Corning 354264, San Jose, CA, USA) at 37 °C for 45 min. The cultured cells were trypsinized and pelleted, followed by resuspension in MSC culture medium at optimal concentration. The cell suspension was then mixed with 4% Matrigel at 1:1 on ice and seeded to the pre-coated well with 1.5 × 104 cells per well. After 4-hour incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2, a significant tube formation was observed and recorded with BZ-X Series All-in-One Fluorescence Microscope (Keyence, Japan). The numbers of branching points in the tube network formation were quantified by Wimasis analyzing the images [29].

Alveolar epithelial sphere formation assay

For mouse alveolar epithelial 3-D culture, primary AEC2 were isolated from Sftpc-GFP mouse lungs in which AEC2 expressed GFP [19, 30]. Briefly, lung tissues were digested as described for the mouse lung MSCs isolation [30], and AEC2 were isolated through FACS sorting of GFP-positive cells. 5 × 103 primary AEC2 and 1 × 105 mouse lung MSCs were then mixed and resuspended in 90 µl of 1:1 Matrigel: MTEC-Plus [31], which was then placed into a 24-well 0.4 μm Transwell insert. 500 µl of MTEC-Plus medium was added to the lower chamber and changed every other day. Alveolar epithelial spheres with strong GFP signal were live-recorded and analyzed after 2-week culture. The number and size of alveolar balls were measured using ImageJ.

For the co-culture of human lung cells, type 2 alveolar epithelial cells with Nkx2.1-GFP/Ager-tdTomato reporters were derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iAEC2) at Dr. Darrell Kotton lab at Boston University [32]. 2.5 × 103 iAEC2 were mixed with 1.5 × 104 human lung MSCs of individual colonies in 50 µl growth factor-reduced Matrigel (Corning) and plated in the well of a 12-well plate as droplets. After Matrigel was solidified, 1 ml CK + DCI culture medium supplemented with 10 µM Y-27,632 (ROCK inhibitor, Tocris) was added to the well. After 3-day culture, the CK + DCI culture medium without Y-27,632 was used and changed every other day. The bubble-like alveolar spheres were lively recorded and analyzed after 10-day culture, and the number and size of alveolar spheres were quantified using ImageJ. iAEC2 alone was used as a baseline control as iAEC2 was able to grow in spheres under the defined culture medium above. At the end, the droplets of Matrigel were detached by pipetting and collected by centrifugation. The samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, washed with PBS, dehydrated in series of ethanol and xylene, and embedded in paraffin [33]. 5 μm sections were then used for immunofluorescence staining GFP and proSPC following our published method [34].

Data analysis

Independent experiments were repeated at least three times with technical replicates for each experiment. Data was presented as mean ± SD. Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used for two-group and multiple-group comparison, respectively, using Prism 10 software. p ≤ 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Identification of mouse lung MSC subpopulations by lineage tracing

Using Tbx4-rtTA/TetO-Cre/mT-mG reporter mice with doxycycline induction at gestation (E6.5 to P1), fetal lung mesenchymal progenitor cells, which give rise to a variety of mesenchymal cell types including smooth muscle cells, fibroblasts, pericytes, and some endothelial cells during development [13], were all permanently labeled with membrane GFP (mG) expression via Cre/loxP recombination (Fig. 1A and B, Supplemental Fig. 1). In contrast, lung epithelial cells and extrapulmonary cells including those cells that are recruited to lung postnatally expressed membrane tdTomato (mT) in this system. The lung tissues were harvested from postnatal day 1 to 6 months, enzymatically dissociated, and plated at low density in MSC culture medium. Individual cell colonies emerged expressing either mG+ or mT+ reporters (Fig. 1C, passage 0). Upon expansion, cultured cells remained heterogeneous, containing both mG+ and mT+ cells as shown in Fig. 1D. Notably, the proportion of mG+ cells decreased with age as assessed by FACS at passage 2 of the culture (Fig. 1E and F).

The MSC features of the cultured cells were validated by their surface marker expression and multipotent differentiation capability. Immunofluorescence staining and FACS analysis of the cultured cells at passage 2 confirmed that both mG+ and mT+ subpopulations expressed canonical MSC surface markers (Sca1, CD90, CD105) and lacked markers of hematopoietic (CD45, CD34), endothelial (CD31), and epithelial (CD326) lineages (Supplemental Fig. 2A), which is recommended by the International Society for Cell Therapy [3]. Furthermore, both subsets demonstrated multipotency, differentiating into osteoblasts, adipocytes, and smooth muscle cells under appropriate culture conditions (Supplemental Fig. 2B-D). These findings confirm the MSC identity of both subsets and their distinct developmental origins, as determined by differential reporter expression as well as the relative decrease in fetal lung-derived MSCs over aging.

Mouse lung MSC subsets exhibit unique transcriptomic profiles

To further characterize the MSC heterogeneity, we conducted Bulk RNA-seq on mG+ vs. mT+ sorted lung MSCs isolated from the mice at 3 months of age. Both principal component analysis and hierarchical clustering revealed distinct transcriptomic profiles between mG+ and mT+ MSCs, which was further demonstrated by the heatmap of the overall differential expression genes (DEGs, Fig. 2A and Supplemental Table 3). Several DEGs selected from the smear plot (Fig. 2B) were validated by real-time PCR (Fig. 2C). Genes with differential expression were identified, such as Cxcl12 in mG+ cells and Meox2 in mT+ cells. These data support true biological divergence at the transcriptomic level for the lung MSC subpopulations and exclude the possibility that MSCs with different reporter expression is caused by low efficacy of Cre-mediated genetic labeling of the cells, such as varied Cre expression and incomplete Cre-mediated loxP recombination in the Tbx4-rtTA/TetO-Cre/mT-mG mouse reporter line.

Lung resident MSC subpopulations were conserved in human

To determine whether the different subpopulations of MSCs seen in mouse lungs also exist in human lungs, we isolated MSCs from fresh human donor lung tissue received from the Life Center. Lung tissue dissociation and cell culture followed the same procedure used for mouse lung MSC isolation. More than 2 dozen individual colonies at passage 0 were isolated and expanded. Since we had identified mouse lung MSC subpopulations with distinct transcriptional profiles, we analyzed the transcriptomic profile for these human lung cell colonies by performing bulk RNA-seq. Among the randomly selected human cell colonies, two groups of cell colonies were identified based on an RNA-seq hierarchical clustering analysis (Fig. 2D) and further demonstrated by an overall DEG heatmap (Fig. 2E and Supplemental Table 4). Expression of the MSC surface markers but not the markers for other cell types was validated by FACS analysis (Supplemental Fig. 3A-B). Differentiation to osteoblasts and adipocytes in culture under the specified conditions was also confirmed for both groups of human lung MSCs (Supplemental Fig. 3C-D). The selected DEGs tested in mouse lung MSCs were similar to changes between these two subsets of human lung MSCs by RNA-seq smear plot and were validated by real-time PCR (Supplemental Figure.4).

Functional divergences in cell behavior and interactions were observed between lung MSC subpopulations

To understand the potential functional differences between lung MSC subpopulations, integral analysis of RNA-seq datasets from mouse and human MSCs was performed, identifying over 600 conserved DEGs between the two MSC subsets (Fig. 3A). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that the group that include mouse mG+ and human mG+-like MSCs was enriched for genes involved in cell division, cell cycle, and DNA repair, while the other group (mouse mT+ and human mT+-like subset) showed enrichment for genes related to cell adhesion, migration, extracellular matrix organization, and angiogenesis (Fig. 3B). In culture, mouse mG+/human mG+-like MSCs proliferated faster than mouse mT+/human mT+-like subset as measured by EdU labeling (Fig. 3C). In a cell adhesion assay, more human mT+-like MSCs were attached to the plate coated with collagen I or fibrinogen than human mG+-like MSCs (Fig. 3D). Moreover, in a culture on Matrigel, while both groups of MSCs formed a network of tubes, the mG+ MSCs had more branches with shorter loops while mT+ MSCs had less branching but longer loops (Fig. 3E).

MSCs have immune modulating potential in vivo and in vitro [35]. Thus, we compare these lung MSC subsets for their ability to regulate T cell proliferation in an MSC-Jurkat cell co-culture system [27]. Addition of either human mG+-like or mT+-like MSCs significantly reduced the numbers of viable Jurkat cells after 120 h (Fig. 3F), but the inhibitory activity of mT+-like subset is more pronounced (4.2-fold inhibition by mT+-like subset vs. 1.9-fold inhibition by mG+-like subset, p = 0.0029). This suggests that human lung mT+-like MSCs have stronger immune suppression potential than the human mG+-like MSCs.

Lung MSC subpopulations exhibit different activity to support type 2 alveolar epithelial cell growth in 3-D culture

Given their proposed role as niche cells, we tested each MSC subset’s capacity to support alveolar epithelial cell progenitors using in a 3-D alveolosphere assay [31]. Primary mouse type 2 alveolar epithelial cells (AEC2) were isolated from Sftpc-GFP+ transgenic mouse lungs by sorting GFP+ cells after tissue dissociation. Either mG+ or mT+ mouse lung MSCs were mixed with AEC2 and co-cultured in Matrigel. Two weeks later, alveolospheres were formed and measured for their numbers and sizes (Fig. 4A and B). The mG+ MSC-AEC2 co-culture had more and larger alveolospheres compared to the mT+ MSC-AEC2 co-culture. In a parallel study using iPSC-derived human AEC2s (iAEC2) [32], both human lung MSC subsets increased the number of iAEC sphere formation. However, only the co-culture with mG+-like MSCs resulted in increased sizes of iAEC spheres (Fig. 4C-E). These data suggest that mouse lung mG+ and human mG+-like MSCs may have superior activity in promoting alveolar growth.

Discussion

Using conventional in vitro approaches, tissue resident MSCs have been isolated and studied although their designation as “mesenchymal stromal cells” versus “mesenchymal stem cells” remains controversial [36]. Although lung tissue MSCs were isolated more than a decade ago, fundamental biological aspects, such as their origins and heterogeneity, remain poorly understood, creating a major obstacle to elucidating the pathogenic mechanisms of pulmonary diseases and advancing MSC-based therapies. This is largely due to the absence of specific cellular markers and the complex nature of the mesenchymal cell compartment. Leveraging a fetal lung mesenchyme-specific reporter mouse model, we identified two distinct subsets of mouse lung MSCs: one derived from embryonic lung mesenchymal progenitors (mG+ MSCs) and another of unknown tissue origin (mT+ MSCs). Distinct transcriptomic profiles confirm their heterogeneity. Importantly, similar subsets of MSCs were identified in human lung. It is unlikely that the mT+ MSCs arise from lung epithelial cells as prior lineage-tracing studies using a lung epithelial reporter showed no evidence of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, even under injury conditions [37]. Our data further show that mT+ MSCs are scarcely present in neonatal lung, but their proportion relative to mG+ MSCs increases with age. It is speculated that this subpopulation has extra-pulmonary origin that is recruited to the lung through pulmonary circulation in postnatal life. Once homing to the lung, these cells may adapt to the lung tissue environment and remain their progenitor status. Interestingly, similar developmental diversity is observed in bone marrow MSCs [38]: nestin-positive MSCs originate from the neural crest and support hematopoietic niches, whereas nestin-negative MSCs are mesoderm-derived and contribute to bone formation. Therefore, further sophisticated lineage tracing experiments are needed to define the origin(s) of the mT+ MSCs. Comparative epigenomic profiling between lung MSC subsets and MSCs from other tissues, such as bone marrow, may also help identify their origin.

Transcriptomic analysis revealed conserved differential gene expression between the two lung MSC subsets in both mice and humans. Gene ontology analysis revealed enrichment of cell cycle and DNA replication genes in both mouse mG⁺ MSCs and their human counterparts mG+-like MSCs, whereas genes associated with adhesion, migration, and extracellular matrix organization were enriched in both mouse mT⁺ MSCs and human mT+-like MSCs. Consistent with these findings, mouse and human mG⁺/mG+-like subpopulations exhibited higher proliferative capacity, while mT⁺/mT+-like subpopulations showed stronger adhesion to specific extracellular matrix proteins. These functional differences likely reflect their distinct roles: mG⁺/mG+-like MSCs better support alveolar sphere growth in 3D culture, indicating a superior niche function. In contrast, mT⁺/mT+-like MSCs may contribute more to tissue retention and immune modulation, including suppression of T cell proliferation.

MSCs have been isolated from multiple tissues including the lung [6–10, 39, 40]. Their heterogeneity appears to reflect both tissue microenvironment and developmental lineage origins [41]. However, many defining MSC markers and properties are derived from in vitro culture [3], in which the adherent culture conditions on plastic dishes may alter their biological properties including surface marker expression seen in vivo [36]. These conventional MSC studies, including ours, have limitation in terms of physiological relevance. Nevertheless, our current work represents an initial step toward defining transcriptional heterogeneity among lung MSCs under standardized in vitro conditions, thereby providing a foundation for identifying candidate subset-specific markers in vivo. The precise localization of resident MSCs within lung tissues remains unclear, largely due to the lack of knowledge on MSC markers in vivo. It has been reported that mesenchymal stromal cells are localized to different anatomic locations such as microvasculature/stroma and the adventitial region of lung arteries (vascular wall MSCs) [42, 43], suggesting the heterogeneity of these cells in their origins, activities, and even functions. Moreover, an age-dependent decline in mouse lung regeneration has been observed in a mouse pneumonectomy model [44]. This decline was not attributable to changes in alveolar epithelial type 2 cells but instead correlated with a loss in lung “fibroblast” clonogenicity. Together, these findings underscore the need for future studies to define molecular markers of distinct lung MSC subpopulations in vivo and to characterize their alterations in both healthy and diseased lungs.

No MSC-based therapy has yet received FDA approval in the United States, despite hundreds of clinical trials using MSCs and their derived products in treating certain diseases have been registered. Transplantation of extrapulmonary MSCs into the lung has been investigated for treating pulmonary diseases such as bronchopulmonary dysplasia, interstitial lung disease, emphysema, and COVID-19, with mixed results. Among the many factors, lack of understanding for the precise cell and molecular biology of endogenous lung resident MSCs represents a critical knowledge barrier. Our studies suggest that these extrapulmonary MSCs may behave quite differently in niche and immune regulatory functions dependent of their origins and heterogeneity. Some may have better tissue retainment and inflammatory suppression, and the other have stronger support for epithelial and vascular stem cell growth and tissue repair and regeneration. Further studies of their secretomes and functional profiles will be essential for advancing mechanistic understanding of lung resident MSCs and optimizing MSC-based therapeutic applications.

Conclusion

Two lung MSC subpopulations with different developmental origins and biological functions have been identified in both mouse and human, which are further supported by their differential gene expression at the transcriptomic level. These findings advance our understanding of the complexity of mesenchymal progenitors in lung injury repair and disease, and lay a foundation for future development of effective stem cell-based therapies for pulmonary conditions.
