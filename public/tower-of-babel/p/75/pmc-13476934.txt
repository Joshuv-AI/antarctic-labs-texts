Oral health findings and dental behaviour in children with familial mediterranean fever: a case–control study
Authors: Alem Coşgun, Burcu Bölükbaşı Akgül, Merve Tatar, Müzeyyen Dilşah Demiray, Halenur Altan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFamilial Mediterranean Fever (FMF) is an autoinflammatory disease that affects children’s emotional well-being due to recurrent pain attacks. It can significantly impact oral health and compliance during dental examinations. This study aimed to investigate whether there were differences between children diagnosed with FMF and healthy children in terms of dental findings (DMFT/dmft, gingival index, plaque index, bruxism, saliva pH), compliance with dental examination (Frankl behaviour scale), and parental dental anxiety (C-DAS). Additionally, the effects of age, gender, age of symptom onset, age at diagnosis, colchicine dosage, attack frequency, hospitalisation, and disease severity on dental findings and compliance with dental examination were investigated using regression analyses in the FMF group.MethodsA case-control study was conducted involving 60 children diagnosed with FMF and 60 healthy children matched for age and gender. The DMFT/dmft index, gingival and plaque index, presence of bruxism, saliva pH, Frankl score, and parental C-DAS scores were recorded for all participants. In the FMF group, Pras disease severity score, age of symptom onset, age at diagnosis, colchicine dose, and attack frequency were evaluated. Pearson’s Chi-Square Test, Monte Carlo-Corrected Fisher’s Exact Test, and Yates’s Correction were used to examine associations between categorical variables and groups. The Mann–Whitney U test was applied to compare differences between two independent groups for variables that did not follow a normal distribution. Linear Regression Analysis was used for normally distributed dependent variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe prevalence of bruxism in the FMF group was significantly higher than in the healthy group (p < 0.001). No significant differences were found between the groups in terms of DMFT/dmft, gingival index, plaque index, saliva pH, Frankl score and parental C-DAS scores (p > 0.05).ConclusionWhile oral health in children with FMF appears comparable to that of healthy children, a higher prevalence of bruxism was observed. Increased disease severity may reduce compliance with dental examinations. To help manage these issues, dentists should consider implementing regular monitoring and management plans for bruxism, such as stress-reduction techniques, oral appliances, and counselling on proper oral hygiene practices. The emotional state, disease severity, and pain experiences should be considered in dental management for children with FMF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-09213-6.Keywords: Familial Mediterranean Fever, Child, Bruxism, Frankl behaviour, Pras disease severity, C-DAS

Introduction

Familial Mediterranean Fever (FMF) is the most common autoinflammatory disease worldwide, characterized by recurrent fever, peritonitis, pleurisy, arthritis, and skin manifestations [1, 2]. The disease usually begins in childhood, and its severity is assessed using the Modified Pras Disease Severity Score. This scoring system includes criteria such as age of disease onset, number of attacks per month before initiation of colchicine treatment, presence of arthritis, erysipelas-like erythema, amyloidosis, and daily colchicine dose needed to control attacks [3, 4]. This Score in FMF is a fundamental tool for determining appropriate treatment approaches, predicting prognosis, and analyzing clinical correlates [5].

Recurrent and intense abdominal pain attacks, which represent the most prominent clinical feature of FMF, are known to negatively affect children’s quality of life and emotional state [6]. Chronic illnesses have significant effects on children not only physically but also psychosocially. Frequent hospital visits, pain experiences, continuous medication use, and lifestyle restrictions can increase stress and anxiety levels in children, thereby affecting behavioural compliance [7]. The Wong-Baker scale, frequently used in dentistry, is a pain assessment tool in which children select the expression that best describes the intensity of pain they are experiencing [8]. The Frankl Behaviour Scale is frequently used to assess dental anxiety and clinical behaviour [9].

Family structure, socio-economic status, and parental education have been widely reported as important determinants of children’s behavioural responses during dental treatment [9]. Children from lower socio-economic backgrounds or with limited parental educational support may exhibit higher levels of dental fear and uncooperative behaviour, potentially due to reduced access to preventive dental care and negative prior experiences. In addition, parental dental anxiety has been identified as a significant psychosocial factor influencing children’s perceptions of dental treatment, with anxious parents more likely to transmit fear-related attitudes and behaviours to their children through verbal and non-verbal cues [10].

Parental dental anxiety has been consistently associated with increased dental fear, avoidance behaviours, and poorer cooperation in pediatric patients. To assess parental dental anxiety in the present study, the Corah Dental Anxiety Scale (C-DAS), a well-established psychometric instrument originally developed for adults, was administered to parents [9]. The C-DAS evaluates anxiety-related responses across four key dental situations: anticipation of a dental visit, waiting in the dental office, sitting in the dental chair, and undergoing dental procedures [11]. Despite being designed for adult populations, the C-DAS has been extensively used in pediatric dentistry research to evaluate parental anxiety, owing to its simplicity, reliability, and strong predictive value for child dental behaviour. Previous studies have demonstrated that higher parental C-DAS scores are significantly associated with increased child dental anxiety, negative behaviour ratings, and the need for advanced behaviour management techniques [12–14].

The literature regarding the relationship between Familial Mediterranean Fever (FMF) and oral health is quite limited. Chronic inflammation and emotional stress are known to indirectly affect parameters such as caries risk, periodontal health, and bruxism. Previous studies have reported that the prevalence of dental caries and periodontal disease is high in children with FMF; notably, the frequency of dental caries has been found to be significantly higher in children who experienced attacks within the last six months compared to those who did not [15]. This suggests that the inflammatory load during active disease periods may affect the oral microenvironment, indicating a biological interaction between oral health and the course of the disease [15].

Furthermore, the genetic background of FMF, specifically mutations in the MEFV gene, has been linked to oral mucosal lesions such as recurrent aphthous stomatitis, which appear to occur more frequently in patients carrying mutations in exon 10 and exon 2 [15]. The processes of oral tissue destruction and inflammation in FMF are attributed to an imbalance in the cytokine network, characterized by increased pro-inflammatory IL-1β and reduced anti-inflammatory mediators such as IL-10 and IL-1ra [16]. How this cytokine activity, which persists even during subclinical periods, together with colchicine treatment, influences the periodontal tissue response remains a controversial issue in the literature [16, 17].

This study aimed to evaluate whether differences exist between children with FMF, who have experienced pain and hospitalization at an early age, and healthy children in terms of dental findings (DMFT, gingival index, plaque index, bruxism, salivary pH), compliance with dental examinations, and dental anxiety in their parents. Additionally, the study aimed to investigate the effects of age, gender, age at symptom onset, age at diagnosis, drug dosage, attack frequency, hospitalization, and disease severity on compliance with dental examinations, parental dental anxiety, bruxism, DMFT/dmft, gingival index, plaque index, and disease-related pain severity. The null hypothesis (H0) of this study was that there are no significant differences between children with FMF and healthy children regarding dental findings, clinical compliance, and parental anxiety, and that disease-related factors do not influence these outcomes.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The ethical and scientific principles of this study were approved by the Tokat Gaziosmanpaşa University Faculty of Medicine Non-Interventional Scientific Research Ethics Committee (2-MOBAEK-423). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Consent forms were signed by both parents and children who agreed to participate in the study.

Power analysis

Power analysis was performed with G*Power 3.1.9.6 to calculate sample size. With 95% confidence (1-α), 95% test power (1-β), and an effect size of d = 0.0890, a minimum of 60 participants per group was determined to be appropriate [18].

Patient selection

This study included 60 children aged 8–15 years who were diagnosed with FMF and had no other systemic diseases. All attended the Department of Pediatric Dentistry, Faculty of Dentistry, Gaziosmanpaşa University, Tokat, for routine dental examination in 2025. Sixty healthy children served as the control group.

Inclusion criteria were parental and child agreement to participate, being in the attack-free period among FMF patients, aged between 8 and 15 years, and having no mental or physical disabilities.

Exclusion criteria were incomplete or missing questionnaire responses from parents or children, and the presence of additional systemic diseases in FMF patients.

Measurements

The age and gender of children in both groups, parental education, and whether parents were related by marriage were recorded. An oral examination was then performed. Concisely, the oral examination was conducted according to the World Health Organization (WHO) Oral Health Surveys Basic Methods [19] by a single examiner (pediatric dentist with 10 years of experience), using a reflector light, an intraoral mirror, and an explorer probe.

To ensure diagnostic consistency and reliability, the examiner underwent internal calibration before the start of the study. Intra-examiner reliability was assessed by re-examining a randomly selected 10% of the sample group two weeks after the initial assessment. In the intra-examiner ICC analysis conducted to determine the measurement reliability of clinical indices, values of 0.810 (excellent agreement) for DMFT/dmft, 0.848 (Excellent agreement) for the Plaque Index, and 0.850 (Excellent agreement) for the Gingival Index were obtained. Intra-examiner reliability was found to be statistically significant for all parameters (p < 0.001)(Table 1).

Dental examinations

DMFT/dmft

Dental caries status was assessed using the DMFT index for permanent dentition and the dmft index for primary dentition, strictly following the diagnostic criteria recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO). The indices were calculated as the sum of Decayed (D/d), Missing due to caries (M/m), and Filled (F/f) teeth. According to this classification, caries prevalence was categorized into five levels ranging from “very low” to “very high” based on DMFT/dmft scores. The specific ranges for these severity levels are presented in Table 2 [20].

Gingival index

The index developed by Löe and Silness was used to assess the severity of gingival inflammation. This index is scored according to the degree of redness, swelling, and bleeding in the gums upon gentle probing [21].

Plaque index

The Silness-Löe index was used to assess oral hygiene. This index evaluates the thickness of dental plaque on the tooth surface near the gumline [22].

Saliva pH

Salivary pH was measured using the GC Saliva-Check BUFFER kit (GC, Tokyo, Japan).

Bruxism

Assessed according to the criteria of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM), which defines bruxism as repetitive jaw-muscle activity characterized by clenching or grinding of teeth and/or by bracing or thrusting of the mandible [23]. The diagnostic methodology followed the AASM minimal criteria for sleep bruxism (code-306.8), requiring criterion A plus criterion B.

Psychometric examination

Frankl behaviour score

For both the study and control groups, children’s behaviour was observed by the examiner during the clinical examination, and at the end of examination, the patient’s behavioural status was recorded by the same examiner according to the Frankl Behaviour Scale (1-definitely negative, 2-negative, 3-positive, 4-definitely positive), which is used to assess a child’s cooperative behaviour in dental settings [24].

Assessment of pain due to FMF

Wong-Baker facial pain scale

Children in the study group were asked during their clinical visit, ‘Can you point to the face that reflects your current most severe pain related to FMF?’ Their pain was then assessed prospectively using the Wong-Baker Facial Pain Scale. On this scale, children rate their pain on a 0–5 scale based on facial expression; higher scores reflect lower pain tolerance, and lower scores indicate more tolerable pain [25].

FMF severity

Modified Pras disease severity scoring system

The modified Pras disease severity scoring system was used to assess FMF severity. It was based on symptoms obtained from the medical history. Disease severity was calculated as the sum of parameter scores. Total scores of 3–5 were classified as mild, 6–8 as moderate, and 9 or higher as severe [26] (Table 3).

Measuring parental dental anxiety

C-DAS scale

This scale was administered to parents of children in both groups. It is a five-point Likert-type scale, in which respondents indicate their level of anxiety for each item from low to high. The C-DAS consists of four questions about feelings before arriving at the dentist, in the waiting area, at the dental unit, and during treatment. Responses range from ‘no fear at all’ to ‘very fearful’. Total scores range from 4 to 20 [27]. The Turkish version of the C-DAS scale has been validated for reliability and validity by Şeydaoğlu et al. [28].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS V23 and R version 4.4.1. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test, which assesses whether a dataset deviates from a normal distribution, was used to test normality. Pearson’s Chi-Square Test, Monte Carlo-Corrected Fisher’s Exact Test, and Yates’s Correction, which are statistical methods used to examine relationships in categorical variables, were applied to assess associations between groups and categorical variables.

The Mann-Whitney U Test, a non-parametric test used to compare differences between two independent groups, was applied to variables not conforming to a normal distribution. Linear Regression Analysis, which evaluates the relationship between dependent and independent variables under the assumption of normality, was used for normally distributed dependent variables. Robust Regression Analysis with the MASS package, which adjusts for non-normal data and outliers, was used for variables that did not follow a normal distribution. Logistic Regression, a technique used for modelling binary outcome variables, examined independent variables related to bruxism.

Results were presented as frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables, and as mean ± standard deviation and median (minimum–maximum) for quantitative variables. The significance level was p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study is one of the first comprehensive dental investigations to systematically compare dental findings, oral health parameters, and dental behaviours in children diagnosed with FMF with those of healthy peers. Studies evaluating the impact of FMF on children’s dental behaviours, oral health, or oral parafunctions such as bruxism are extremely limited. Therefore, our study presents a pioneering approach that examines the potential direct and indirect effects of FMF on dental health by evaluating not only clinical observations but also numerous disease-related variables (such as disease severity, attack frequency, age of symptom onset, age at diagnosis, and colchicine dosage) in analytical models.

The relationship between Pras disease severity and the Frankl behavior score is one of the most interesting findings of this study. In children with FMF, an increase in the Pras disease severity score was associated with a significant decrease in the Frankl behavior score. The disease severity score is determined by evaluating age of onset, attack frequency, colchicine dosage, and the presence of arthritis, erysipelas-like erythema, and amyloidosis [3]. It is known that chronic diseases have a significant impact on pain sensitivity, stress levels, and behavioural adjustment in children. Von Baeyer [29] and Tsze & Hirschfeld [30] have shown that children with chronic pain exhibit increased behavioural resistance and anxiety levels towards medical procedures. In inflammatory diseases with attacks, such as FMF, children’s pain expectations, stress load, and emotional regulation difficulties may also negatively affect dental compliance.

The fact that the age of onset of FMF symptoms and the age of diagnosis showed effects close to statistical significance on the Frankl score suggests that early disease onset may have shaped behavioural responses by creating longer-term stress exposure in children. Peretz and Efrat [31] have also demonstrated that early exposure to chronic disease may increase dental anxiety in children. Conversely, the fact that colchicine dosage, attack frequency, and hospitalization did not affect the Frankl score supports the notion that dental compliance may be more closely related to how the child experiences the disease (disease severity, pain memory, emotional burden) rather than to biological indicators of the disease. Our results showed that increasing age was significantly related to higher Frankl scores, supporting earlier studies reporting improved dental cooperation with age [32]. Indeed, Klingberg and Broberg [33] reported that age is one of the strongest predictors of dental fear and behavioural problems and that as children grow older, they find the dental environment more predictable. Similarly, Cianetti et al. emphasized that behavioural management difficulties are more frequently observed in younger age groups [34].

In the present study, the rate of bruxism was significantly increased in the FMF group. This finding supports previous studies demonstrating that bruxism is associated with psychosocial stress, pain experience, and mood disorders [35–37]. The emotional burden created by recurrent abdominal pain attacks in FMF, together with activation of neurophysiological stress response, may present a possible mechanism mediating bruxism. Bruxism can lead to tooth wear, headaches, muscle pain, temporomandibular joint pain, and limited mouth opening. Therefore, identifying predisposing factors for early diagnosis is crucial [38]. Dentists should be aware that children with FMF may be prone to bruxism, and further studies should be conducted on the etiology of bruxism in children with FMF. Regular dental follow-up every six months is recommended to monitor early signs of bruxism in this patient population. Specific monitoring methods should include regular assessments of dental wear patterns, inquiries into stress levels and sleep quality, and the use of bite registration techniques to detect grinding habits. Early intervention strategies may include providing advice on stress management techniques and, where necessary, prescribing custom-fitted night guards.

The similarity of DMFT, gingival index, plaque index, and saliva pH values between the two groups indicates that FMF may not have a significant direct biological effect on dental health under remission conditions. Although chronic inflammation may have indirect effects on oral tissues, the fact that most of the children in our study were in the non-attack period may explain this result. Kıvrak et al. [39] reported that the effects of systemic inflammatory diseases on oral tissues are more related to disease activity and elevated cytokine levels during flare-ups. The biological interaction between FMF and oral health is primarily driven by an imbalance in the cytokine network, where pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β remain active even during subclinical periods, potentially modulating the periodontal tissue response and altering the oral microenvironment [16]. However, how this persistent cytokine activity, together with colchicine treatment, influences periodontal tissue responses remains controversial in the literature [16, 17].

The study conducted by Unverdi et al. [40] reported that the presence of systemic diseases in children did not have a negative impact on oral health compared to healthy controls. Costa et al. [41] demonstrated that saliva pH and plaque accumulation are more influenced by environmental factors, such as oral hygiene habits, diet, and saliva flow rate, than by the presence of chronic disease. The similarity of gingival indices and DMFT scores in the FMF group to those in the control group is also consistent with this view, as inflammation-related changes in oral mucosal and periodontal structures may remain minimal during periods of low disease activity [42]. Furthermore, the fact that the vast majority of children with FMF in our study were evaluated during the non-attack (remission) period is an important factor supporting this finding. Livneh et al. [43] reported that inflammatory markers in FMF return to near-normal values outside of attack periods and that systemic effects are largely transient. Therefore, it is expected that children evaluated during remission will show no significant differences in caries experience, plaque accumulation, or gingival inflammation. However, our findings do not necessarily indicate that FMF does not affect oral health, particularly since disease activity was not evaluated longitudinally. There is a need for studies examining the oral health of children with FMF during the attack periods.

The strength of our study lies in the comprehensive evaluation of dental findings in children with FMF and in the detailed examination of the effects of the disease’s clinical characteristics on oral health parameters. However, the study has some limitations. Firstly, the relatively small sample size and the single-center design may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader populations. Secondly, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes us from establishing definitive causal relationships between FMF and the observed oral health parameters. Thirdly, although we attempted to standardize our groups, potential confounding factors such as individual oral hygiene practices, specific dietary habits, and socioeconomic status were not fully controlled, which may have influenced the clinical outcomes. Finally, all FMF patients were examined during remission (non-attack) periods. Future longitudinal studies evaluating patients during both attack and remission periods would provide a more comprehensive understanding of disease dynamics and their impact on oral health.

Conclusion

Although the effects of FMF on systemic inflammation, chronic pain, and quality of life are frequently emphasized, data on its effects on oral health and dental behaviour remain limited. Our findings suggest that oral health parameters were not significantly affected by the chronic course of FMF and by patients’ regular medical follow-up. However, the tendency for Frankl scores to decrease with increasing disease severity suggests that the burden of chronic disease may indirectly affect dental compliance. Therefore, disease severity, pain experiences, and psychosocial status should be carefully considered during dental assessment and management of children with FM.
