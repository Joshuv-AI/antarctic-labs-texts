Aberrant structural rich club organization in temporal lobe epilepsy with focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures
Authors: Qiuxing Lin, Wei Li, Yuming Li, Peiwen Liu, Yingying Zhang, Qiyong Gong, Dong Zhou, Dongmei An
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Temporal lobe epilepsy patients had reduction in strength and global efficiency.•Focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures patients had lower rich club connectivity.•Focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures patients had larger abnormal subnetwork.

Introduction

Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) is the most common type of focal epilepsy in adults. Focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures (FBTCS), previously known as “secondarily generalized tonic–clonic seizures”, are characterized as propagation of epileptiform activities from seizure onset to bilateral brain network (Fisher et al., 2017). FBTCS is an important risk factor for sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (Devinsky et al., 2016, Sveinsson et al., 2020) and seizure relapse in patients who reached seizure-free after medical or surgical treatment (Ferreira-Atuesta et al., 2022, Lamberink et al., 2018, Maehara and Ohno, 2011). Hence, it is necessary to investigate the potential mechanisms of specific TLE patients susceptible to FBTCS.

Neuroimaging studies demonstrated that several brain regions may play a role in the occurrence and development of FBTCS (Blumenfeld et al., 2009, Caciagli et al., 2020, He et al., 2020). A single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) study showed decreased cerebral blood flow (CBF) in fronto-parietal association cortex, precuneus and cingulate gyrus and increased CBF in subcortical structures during FBTCS (Blumenfeld et al., 2009). Thalamus was also recognized as a pivotal region promoting the propagation of focal ictal discharges by using structural or functional MRI (He et al., 2020, Peng and Hsin, 2017, Wang et al., 2014). The human brain is characterized as a complex network with highly efficient communication between interconnected regions (Bassett and Sporns, 2017). Therefore, solely focusing on isolated brain regions may oversimplify the complexity of brain network organization.

Rich club organization consisting of a set of core brain nodes has been considered as one of the prominent features of brain network. Rich club organization is characterized as high cost, namely rich club connections could span longer physical distances, high capacity, namely a large proportion of connections pass through the rich club nodes, and high metabolic energy usage (van den Heuvel et al., 2012). This collectively highlights the rich club organization play a crucial role in overall communication within the brain network (van den Heuvel and Sporns, 2011, van den Heuvel et al., 2012, Collin et al., 2014). Disruption of the rich club organization may reduce the functional integration and information flow of brain networks, resulting in brain functional disturbance. Previous studies had reported the disruption of rich club organization in several neuropsychiatric diseases, including schizophrenia (van den Heuvel et al., 2013), obsessive–compulsive disorder (Baldi et al., 2022), Alzheimer’s disease (Dai et al., 2019), major depressive disorder (Liu et al., 2021), and idiopathic generalized epilepsy (IGE) (Li et al., 2016). Herein, we hypothesized that TLE patients with FS only and those with FBTCS may have different whole-brain topological characteristic, abnormal rich-club connectivity, and different abnormal subnetwork.

In the present study, we employed graph-based analysis and Network-based Statistics (NBS) of structural connectomes constructed based on diffusion-weighted imaging data, to investigate whole-brain topological characteristic, rich club organization, and edge-wise connectivity differences in TLE patients with and without FBTCS.

Methods

Participants

One hundred and thirty TLE patients were recruited from the epilepsy center in West China Hospital from January 2014 to January 2019. The diagnosis and lateralization of TLE were determined by a combination of medical history, seizure semiology, scalp electroencephalography (EEG), structural MRI, and positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) if available. The inclusion criteria of TLE patients were as follows: (1) patients age ≥ 16 years; (2) diagnosed as unilateral TLE; (3) no evidence of abnormity in structural MRI other than hippocampal sclerosis (HS) in the ipsilateral epileptogenic temporal lobe. Sixty-eight age- and gender-matched healthy controls (HC) were also recruited. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 16 years; (2) without any history of neurological and psychiatric disorders. The general exclusion criteria were (1) with a history of brain surgery; (2) abnormal manifestation of structural MRI including malformations of cortical development, tumor, traumatic injury, etc; (3) with any other neurological disorders, psychiatric disorders, or serious systematic diseases; (4) contraindications to MRI.

TLE patients who never had any generalized tonic–clonic seizure events were categorized as FS group, while patients who experience one or more generalized tonic–clonic seizures were classified as FBTCS group.

This study was approved by the West China Hospital ethics committee and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Imaging acquisition

All participants underwent MRI scanning by a 3 T scanner (Trio, Siemens) with an eight-channel head coil. T1-weighted images were obtained using 3D magnetization prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR): 2300 ms; echo time (TE): 4.18 ms; flip angle: 9°; field of view (FOV): 256 × 256 mm2; voxel size: 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) data were acquired using spin echo planar imaging sequence with one reference image (b = 0 s/mm2) and 64 non-collinear directions (b = 1000 s/mm2). Other scanning parameters including: TR = 6800 ms; TE = 91 ms; FOV = 240 × 240 mm2; slice thickness = 3 mm, no gap, 50 slices.

Image preprocessing

DWI data were preprocessed using the FMRIB Software Library (FSL version 6.0; https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl) and MRtrix3 (version 3.0.4; https://www.mrtrix.org/). Raw DWI data underwent several preprocessing steps, including denoising (Veraart et al., 2016), Gibbs­ringing artifacts removal (Kellner et al., 2016), head motion and eddy current correction (Andersson and Sotiropoulos, 2016), and bias field correction (Tustison et al., 2010). Then preprocessed images were reconstructed using MRtrix3. Firstly, the response functions were estimated in white matter, gray matter, and cerebrospinal fluid using the Dhollander algorithm (Dhollander and Connelly, 2016). Then, the response functions were used in Single Shell 3 Tissues constrained spherical deconvolution (SS3T-CSD) to estimate the fiber orientation distributions (FODs) in each voxel (Aerts et al., 2019:). FODs were intensity normalized to correct global intensity differences. Subsequently, anatomically constrained tractography (ACT) was performed using the iFOD2 probabilistic tracking method to generate 10 million streamlines for each subject (Smith et al., 2012). Afterward, spherical-deconvolution informed filtering of tracks (SIFT) was applied to reduce the bias of tractography and improve biological plausibility, producing 1 million filtered streamlines (Smith et al., 2013).

Network construction and topological metrics

A brain network was defined with a set of nodes and edges connecting a pair of nodes. Eighty-two network nodes were identified after parcellation of the T1-weighted images based on Desikan–Killiany atlas (Desikan et al., 2006) using FreeSurfer (version 7.3.2; https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) (Fischl et al., 2004). An 82 × 82 interregional connectivity matrix was generated. Each edge was populated by number of streamlines (NOS) that link a pair of nodes. Only NOS higher than 3 were selected, otherwise the edges were set to zero.

Topological parameters computed using Brain Connectivity Toolbox (BCT) for Matlab were used to describe the network characteristics (Rubinov and Sporns, 2010). Graph metrics included connectivity strength (computed as the sum of the weights of all node connections), global efficiency (GE, computed as the average inverse of the shortest path lengths between all pairs of nodes), normalized clustering coefficient (gamma, computed as average likelihood that neighbors of each node are interconnected and then normalized by 1000 random networks), and normalized path length (lambda, computed as the average of shortest path lengths between all pair of nodes and then normalized by 1000 random networks). Moreover, degree of each node, defined as number of binary links connected to each node, was also computed for assessing rich club coefficient.

Rich club organization

The weighted rich club coefficient φw (k) at each level of k, ie. degree, was calculated in each subject. The detailed approach and formula were described in previous study (Opsahl et al., 2008). In short, φw (k) was computed in three steps: first, W>k was calculated by sum of weights of edges in the subgraph with nodes degree greater than k; second, ranked all edge weights in descending order under the entire network, ie. Wranked and calculate top l of Wranked in which the l was number of subgraph edges in step 1; third, φw (k) was calculated as ratio of quantities calculated in step 1 and step 2. Rich club nodes and connections

The network nodes of each subject were ranked according to the level of k. Then, the top 12 % highest-degree nodes (10 nodes) were selected in all subjects and the most consistently ranked nodes were determined as rich club nodes (van den Heuvel et al., 2013). The robustness of rich club nodes selection was tested in a group-average network as a previous study did (van den Heuvel and Sporns, 2011). The group-average network was computed by averaging every edge that presented in at least 75 % of subjects and the rest of edges were set to 0.

Based on the different categorizations of nodes, network connections could be categorized into three different connections: rich club connections, linking rich club nodes; feeder connections, linking rich club nodes to non-rich club nodes; and local connections, linking non-rich club nodes (van den Heuvel et al., 2012). Connectivity strength of different types of connections was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical data was analyzed by IBM SPSS (version 25). Categorical variables were compared by chi-square test. For continuous variables, the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis H test were used to compare the clinical data between groups.

The non-parametric permutation test was implemented to evaluate the differences in observed metrics including rich club coefficient, rich club connections, feeder connections, local connections, connectivity strength, GE, gamma, and lambda. Firstly, actual differences in observed metrics between groups were computed. Then, randomly assigned each participant to random group to form new groups which had the same size as the original groups. We carried out 10,000 times permutations to yield an empirical null distribution. Afterward, a P value was assigned by computing the proportion of the null distribution that exceeds the actual difference. A two-tailed P＜0.05 was considered as statistical significance. Benjamini-Hochberg FDR at the alpha of 5 % was carried out for multiple comparations.

To evaluate the differences in edge-wise connections, a network-based statistic (NBS) analysis was carried out between different groups. In the NBS analysis, age and sex were used as covariates, assigned edge-wise P < 0.001, FDR corrected P < 0.05 and performed 10,000 permutation tests by NBS toolbox in Matlab (Zalesky et al., 2010).

Network-based statistical analysis

The FBTCS group presented more widespread connections with decreased connectivity strength compared to the FS group. In FS group, an abnormal subnetwork was identified comprising 27 nodes sharing 26 edges with a reduction of connectivity strength compared to HC group. Specifically, the affected nodes including 5 rich club nodes (18.5 % in affected nodes and 50 % in total rich club nodes) and the affected edges including 1 rich club connection (3.9 %), 10 feeder connections (38.5 %), and 15 local connections (57.7 %). The abnormal subnetwork predominantly comprised connections of temporal-subcortical (23.1 %), subcortical-subcortical connections (15.4 %), frontal-parietal (11.5 %), and subcortical-occipital (11.5 %), of which temporal-striatum connections were the most frequently involved (4 edges) (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 1). In the FBTCS group, an abnormal subnetwork was also identified comprising 39 nodes sharing 53 edges, specifically, including 9 rich club nodes (23.1 % in affected nodes and 90 % in total rich club nodes) and the affected edges including 2 rich club connections (3.8 %), 28 feeder connections (52.8 %), and 23 local connections (43.4 %). The abnormal subnetwork predominantly comprised connections of temporal-subcortical (26.4 %), frontal-parietal (17.0 %), frontal-temporal connections (17.0 %), and subcortical-occipital (7.5 %), of which temporal-striatum connections were the most frequently involved (10 edges) (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 2). Discussion

In this study, graph theoretical analysis was applied to explore the whole brain topological characteristics alteration and NBS was used to assess edge-wise connection changes to investigate the difference of brain structural connectivity alterations between TLE patients with and without FBTCS. Both the FS and FBTCS group had lower level of whole-brain connectivity strength and GE. For rich club organization, TLE patients with FBTCS were more likely to have reduced connectivity strength in rich club and feeder connections, while TLE patients with FS only showed lower connectivity strength in feeder and local connections. Moreover, the FBTCS group presented wider disruption of network connectivity compared to the FS group, and the majority involved connections were between temporal lobe and striatum regions.

Characteristic path length and GE indicate the ability to communicate between distributed brain regions, namely network integration (Rubinov and Sporns, 2010). While the clustering coefficient represents the ability to quantify the presence of specialized subnetwork, ie network segregation (Rubinov and Sporns, 2010). Previous studies had investigated the whole brain topological properties in TLE patients and displayed a significant increase in clustering coefficient and characteristic path length, but a significant reduction in GE (Wang et al., 2014, Bernhardt et al., 2015, van Diessen et al., 2014), which indicated decreased network integration and increased network segregation. Concordant to the previous study, our study indicated decreased level of GE in both FS and FBTCS group. Only the FBTCS group showed a trend of increased normalized clustering coefficient, despite no statistical significance after FDR correction, it may suggest more segregated network characteristics in TLE with FBTCS. The potential mechanism of alteration of network topological properties is still unknown, it may present the outcome of frequent focal seizure activity and cause progressive reorganization of subcortical-cortical connectivity, or an adaptive mechanism to prevent seizure activity propagate from the epileptogenic zone and generalization (Englot et al., 2016).

In line with previous studies (van den Heuvel and Sporns, 2011, van den Heuvel et al., 2013), we identified 10 cortical and subcortical rich club regions including bilateral precuneus, SFG, SPL, thalamus, and putamen. Despite a reduction of global connectivity was found in both FS and FBTCS group, there still were different patterns of connection alteration between the FS and FBTCS group. In the FBTCS group, the alteration of connectivity strength mainly concentrated in rich club regions, including rich club connections and feeder connections. There may be several reasons for the vulnerability of rich club regions in patients with FBTCS. Firstly, damaging of gray matter in the rich club regions. Rich club regions are characterized by high capacity, high cost, and played a key role in global network integration (van den Heuvel et al., 2013). However, these regions are more vulnerable to pathological attacks including ictal epileptiform discharges. As an ENIGMA study showed that hub regions were overall more susceptible to epilepsy-related atrophy in TLE patients (Larivière et al., 2020). Moreover, patients with FBTCS often showed more widespread epileptiform discharges. Previous studies found that rich club connectivity was selectively affected with local connectivity relatively preserved in IGE patients, indicating the long-term effects of epileptic activities on the central hubs (Li et al., 2016, Liu et al., 2022). As part of the rich club regions, thalamus plays an important role in seizure secondary generalization (He et al., 2020). Several studies had reported additional atrophy and abnormal thalamic circuits in patients with FBTCS (Peng and Hsin, 2017, Chen et al., 2019, He et al., 2015, Yang et al., 2017). In addition, in line with the rich hub regions identified in the current study, previous SPECT study showed decreased ictal cerebral blood flow in precuneus and fronto-parietal association cortices during a FBTCS attack (Blumenfeld et al., 2009). Secondly, it has been confirmed that TLE patients displayed white matter tracts abnormalities, including corpus callosum and cingulum, which often pass through the rich-club regions we identified (Hatton et al., 2020). In our NBS analysis, both the FS and FBTCS group showed decrease in connectivity strength. Particularly patients with FBTCS displayed more widespread reduction of connectivity strength and involved 90 % rich club regions, while the affected connections in FS group were limited and involved fewer rich club node regions. In summary, both damage of rich club regions and white matter fibers that connected rich club regions that jointly induced the abnormity of rich club organization, especially in patients with FBTCS.

Basal ganglia had been shown to be an inhibitor of the propagation of ictal epileptiform activities (He et al., 2020, Aupy et al., 2019, Rektor et al., 2012). Increased synchronization between the striatum and neocortices may play a pivotal role in seizure termination (Aupy et al., 2019). Reduction of basal ganglia–thalamal interactions may induce FBTCS (He et al., 2020). In the current study, majority of the decreasing connections were between temporal lobe and subcortical regions (mostly striatum), especially in patients with FBTCS, suggesting that decreased connectivity strength between temporal lobe and striatum may weaken the inhibition effect of striatum and lead to FBTCS.

The main novelty of this study was that we applied both graph-based analysis and NBS to capture the differences of whole-brain topological characteristic, rich-club connectivity, and abnormal subnetwork in TLE patients with FBTCS and without FBTCS. Moreover, several limitations in this study should be considered. Firstly, only single shell DWI data were used in this study, which may insufficient to assess the crossing fibers (Maier-Hein et al., 2017). Multi-shell data will better track and reconstruct white matter streamlines in future studies. Secondly, we did not acquire phase-flipped images to correct susceptibility-induced artifacts, which may affect the assessment of tractography in temporal pole and orbitofrontal cortex. Thirdly, the nature of the case-control design could not indicate causal-effect inference in this study. Finally, we only utilized one threshold method to construct brain network, specifically by retaining edges with NOS higher than three. Despite other methods for thresholding graphs are also available, but none of these methods are perfect. Therefore, further exploration is needed to find better threshold methods.

Conclusion

Network integration was reduced in temporal lobe epilepsy. TLE with FBTCS selectively disrupted the rich club regions, while TLE with FS only were more likely to affect the non-rich club regions, emphasizing the contribution of rich club organization to seizure generalization.
