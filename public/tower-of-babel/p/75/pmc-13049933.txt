Mitotic spindle–organizing protein 2A (MZT2A) promotes cisplatin resistance through NEMO ubiquitination and NF-κB activation in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Huanxi Wang, Zhe Zhang, Yizhuo Li, Shiping Li, Jingqian Guan, Zongang Liu, Yue Ma, Xianghong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), as a highly malignant tumor, remains challenging because of resistance to chemotherapeutic agents. This study aims to elucidate the regulatory mechanism of mitotic spindle–organizing protein 2A (MZT2A) in chemotherapy resistance in LUAD to identify novel therapeutic targets. We analyzed the expression of MZT2A in LUAD cells/tissues and determined its relationship with hypoxia. We used flow cytometry and cell cisplatin resistance experiments to investigate the role of MZT2A in the apoptosis and chemoresistance of LUAD cells in vitro and in vivo. In addition, the mechanistic interaction between MZT2A, NF-κB essential modulator (NEMO), and OTU deubiquitinase with linear linkage specificity (OTULIN) was explored using coimmunoprecipitation, glutathione-S-transferase pull down, ubiquitination immunoprecipitation assay, and mutational analysis. The results indicated that MZT2A was positively correlated with hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α) and was upregulated in human LUAD, especially in neoadjuvant-resistant tumors. It was also correlated with an advanced disease stage and poor survival. It suppressed apoptosis and promoted cisplatin resistance in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, MZT2A protected NEMO against deubiquitination by binding to the catalytic domain of OTULIN, which activated the NF-κB signaling pathway. These findings suggest that MZT2A can promote chemoresistance in human LUAD by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway. Importantly, we identified a novel mechanism for hypoxia-induced NF-κB that involves the HIF-1α–MTZ2A–OTULIN–NEMO signaling cascade. The study shed new light on the crosstalk between HIF-1α and NF-κB that drives tumor drug resistance. Here, we showed that MZT2A can serve as a therapeutic target for overcoming drug resistance in LUAD.

Non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for up to 85% of all lung cancer cases (1, 2). Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common histological subtype of NSCLC. The incidence of LUAD has gradually increased from 40% to 50% in Western countries, such as the United States and Europe, whereas in Asian countries, over 60% of lung cancers are LUAD, especially among nonsmokers and women (3, 4). The majority of patients with LUAD have advanced disease at the time of diagnosis, and the 5-year survival rate of LUAD is only 15% (5). Early stage LUAD is typically treated surgically, but only those with a clinical stage of T3N1M0 (Union for International Cancer Control TNM classification) or lower have a chance of cure (6, 7). Although immunotherapy and targeted therapy have achieved a certain degree of success in improving patients’ survival (8), resistance to chemotherapy, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and immune checkpoint inhibitors remains a major obstacle to improving the prognosis of patients (9, 10, 11, 12). Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying drug resistance is critical for developing novel therapeutic agents for this aggressive cancer.

Hypoxia is a major driver of resistance to treating solid cancers (13, 14, 15). Hypoxia induces the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α), which reprograms cell metabolism from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis (Warburg effect), thereby promoting cancer cell survival and growth in hypoxic tumor niches (16). In addition, HIF-1α deregulates certain signaling pathways, which can lead to genomic instability, angiogenesis, and resistance to apoptosis (17). Therefore, many HIF-1α inhibitors have been employed in clinical trials in combination with other anticancer agents to overcome resistance to current cancer treatments (18). Notably, β-elemene, a naturally occurring compound that inhibits HIF-1α expression, has been shown to improve the radiosensitivity of LUAD xenograft in vivo (19), supporting the role of HIF-1α as a potential target for overcoming drug resistance in LUAD.

Microtubules are dynamic cytoskeleton structures composed of α-tubulin and β-tubulin heterodimers. Microtubules are needed for mitosis and are critically involved in carcinogenesis. Drugs targeting the tubulin and microtubule system are known as antimitotic drugs. They are a common component of combination chemotherapy for treating several types of cancers (20). Mitotic spindle–organizing protein 2A (MZT2A), also referred to as FAM128A, is a core component of the γ-tubulin ring complex. In normal conditions, MZT2A is localized within the centrosome, where it is involved in spindle formation (21, 22, 23). Downregulation of MZT2A was shown to impair microtubule nucleation and cell proliferation (23). In a previous study, we detected MZT2A upregulation in human NSCLC lesions and found a positive association between MZT2A expression in tumor tissues and poor prognosis. We also found that MZT2A overexpression can promote the viability and invasion of NSCLC cells (24). In the present study, we measured the expression of MZT2A in human LUAD lesions and matched noncancerous tissues and investigated the relationships between MZT2A expression in tumor tissues and LUAD progression and prognosis. We also investigated the role of MZT2A in the apoptosis and chemoresistance of LUAD cells in vitro and in vivo.

Results

MZT2A was induced by HIF-1α in hypoxic human LUAD

In single-cell database analysis, the major cell types were identified based on cross-cohort typical cell type–labeled scores, and epithelial cells were labeled with KRT18, KRT19, and EPCAM (Fig. S1A). In epithelial cells, high-variability genes (median absolute deviation >0.01) were screened using the median absolute deviation, and the soft threshold (scale-free topology fitting index >0.9) was determined. Dynamic tree cut was used to cluster genes into modules, which were labeled as epithelial cell programs LUAD1–11 (Fig. S1B). We used the AUCell algorithm to predict the relationship between the hypoxia signaling pathway and LUAD cells and determine the scores for the HALLMARK_HYPOXIA pathway (Fig. 1A). We found a high degree of overlap between the HALLMARK_HYPOXIA pathway and LUAD6 programmed cells (p < 0.05, false discovery rate <0.25). The correlation results also revealed that the pathway score was significantly increased in the LUAD6 cell program (Fig. 1B). We named this epithelial cell program LUAD6–HYPOXIA. Forty-five randomly selected LUAD specimens from patients who did not undergo neoadjuvant therapy were subjected to immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for MZT2A and HIF-1α. A positive association was found between the two proteins (Fig. 1D, p < 0.001). In A549 cells, hypoxia upregulated the mRNA and protein levels of MZT2A in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 1, E and F). Thereafter, we searched the University of California Santa Cruz genome browser database and the JASPAR database and identified three potential HIF-1α-binding sites in the MZT2A promoter region. We subsequently inserted the MZT2A promoter sequence, which predicted HIF-1α binding sequences and the RE1–3 promoter sequence (ΔRE) into the pGL3 luciferase reporter plasmids (RE1–3, Fig. 1G). We found that HIF-1α overexpression activated the reporter gene expression driven by the MZT2A promoter. Partial reporter gene expression was detected using the RE2 sequence, but the RE1, RE3, or ΔRE sequence did not respond to HIF-1α (Fig. 1H), suggesting that HIF-1α binds to the MZT2A promoter at RE2. These clinical and in vitro results demonstrated that hypoxia upregulates MZT2A in human LUAD through transcriptional regulation by HIF-1α.

MZT2A was upregulated in human LUAD, inhibited cell apoptosis, and promoted cisplatin resistance

The Kaplan–Meier analysis indicated a positive association between MZT2A expression and poor prognosis of patients with LUAD (Fig. 2A, hazard ratio = 1.63, Logrank p < 0.001). Using IHC staining and RT–quantitative PCR (qPCR), we measured MZT2A expression in 179 human LUAD specimens. In the IHC assay, MZT2A was mainly located in the cytoplasm, with a few MZT2A proteins located in the nucleus. The nuclear–cytoplasmic fractionation assay also confirmed that the subcellular localization of MZT2A was consistent with the results of IHC staining (Fig. S2B). Based on IHC staining data, MZT2A was upregulated in tumor tissues compared with normal lung tissues (Fig. 2B). IHC data also revealed that high MZT2A expression was associated with larger tumor size, lymph node metastasis, and advanced TNM stage (p < 0.05, Table 1). No association was found between MZT2A expression and gender, age, or differentiation (p > 0.05, Table 1). Among 27 patients who received neoadjuvant therapy, MZT2A expression was lower in patients who exhibited pathological complete response or major pathologic response (Fig. 2B, lower panel, p < 0.01), indicating an association with chemoresistance. RT–qPCR data confirmed the upregulation of MZT2A in LUAD tissues and showed its association with chemoresistance (Fig. 2C, p < 0.01). Association of MZT2A expression with the clinicopathological data of patients

Flow cytometry showed that under normoxic conditions, the basal apoptosis rate and cisplatin-induced apoptosis rate of A549 cells overexpressing MZT2A were decreased, whereas the basal apoptosis rate and cisplatin-induced apoptosis rate of A549 cells were increased when MZT2A was downregulated. However, under hypoxic conditions, the basal apoptosis rate and CDDP-induced apoptosis rate of cells with MZT2A gene silencing were increased. However, when MZT2A was upregulated, these changes were nonsignificant (p < 0.05, Fig. 2D). The 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide assay showed that MZT2A overexpression induced the resistance of A549 cells to CDDP under normoxia, and the resistance ability of A549 cells was weakened when MZT2A was downregulated. Meanwhile, MZT2A knockdown sensitized A549 cells to CDDP under hypoxia, and this phenomenon was reversed when MZT2A was overexpressed (p < 0.05, Fig. 2E). Collectively, these clinical and in vitro data indicated that MZT2A overexpression protects against apoptosis in human LUAD and promotes drug resistance.

MZT2A activated NF-κB in human LUAD cells by activating IκB kinase

NF-κB is a crucial mediator of drug resistance in cancer and is activated by hypoxia (25, 26) and is also a transcription factor for inflammatory mediators. We measured the levels of major molecular components of the NF-κB signaling pathway by Western blotting to explore the possible mechanisms by which MZT2A promotes cisplatin resistance. Similar to hypoxia, MZT2A overexpression increased IκB kinase (IKK) phosphorylation, downregulated IκB, and promoted the nuclear translocation of NF-κB, whereas MZT2A knockdown showed the opposite effects (Fig. 3, A–C, p < 0.05). These data suggested that MZT2A activates NF-κB by promoting IKK phosphorylation and activation. Mass spectrometry (MS) and BioGRID database (https://thebiogrid.org/576069) were used to analyze the interaction of MZT2A and NF-κB-related proteins in MZT2A-overexpressing A549 cells. Then, NF-κB essential modulator (NEMO) got our view, which was confirmed by coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP)–Western blotting (Fig. 3D). NEMO (also known as IKK-γ) is a regulatory subunit of the IKK complex that is essential for controlling NF-κB activity. We used glutathione-S-transferase (GST)-pull-down assay to validate the direct binding between MZT2A and NEMO (Fig. 3E). MZT2A enhanced NEMO ubiquitination by inhibiting OTU deubiquitinase with linear linkage specificity–mediated deubiquitination

Activation of the IKK complex depends on NEMO ubiquitination in its ubiquitin binding in the ABIN and NEMO (UBAN) domain (27). We found that, similar to hypoxia conditions, overexpression of MZT2A in A549 cells increased NEMO ubiquitination (p < 0.01), without altering the expression levels of NEMO (Fig. 4A). OTU deubiquitinase with linear linkage specificity (OTULIN) is a deubiquitinating enzyme that specifically targets the M1 chain of NEMO to downregulate NEMO ubiquitination (28). Co-IP experiments and GST-pull-down assay confirmed the interaction between MZT2A and OTULIN (Fig. 4, B and C). Next, we cotransfected MZT2A cDNA and OTULIN siRNA into A549 cells and found that although MZT2A interacts with OTULIN, they do not regulate the expression of each other (p > 0.05, Fig. 4D). MZT2A bound to the catalytic domain of OTULIN, thereby inhibiting its deubiquitinating function

Next, we utilized molecular docking to predict the binding dynamics between MZT2A and NEMO (Fig. 5A). PRO10 and LYS77 in the MOZART2 domain of MZT2A were predicted to bind to GLN313 and GLN201 in the UBAN domain of NEMO, respectively. Subsequently, we constructed an MZT2A mutant with the removal of the MOZART2 domain, a NEMO mutant with the removal of the UBAN domain, and an MZT2A mutant only with the MOZART2 domain and a NEMO mutant only with the UBAN domain (Fig. 5B). Co-IP experiments showed that MZT2A interacted with both wildtype NEMO and the UBAN domain by itself but not with the NEMO mutant without the UNAB domain. NEMO interacted with both the wildtype MZT2A and the MOZART2 domain by itself but not the MZT2A mutant without the MOZART2 domain (Fig. 5C). These results confirmed that the interaction between the MOZART2 domain of MZT2A and the UBAN domain of NEMO mediated the binding between MZT2A and NEMO. The interactions between MZT2A and OTULIN predicted by molecular docking are shown in Figure 5E. Specifically, GLN74, MET79, and ASP54 of MZT2A were predicted to interact with the HIS339, TYR91, and LYS94 of OTULIN. Notably, HIS339, TYR91, and LYS94 of OTULIN are located in the catalytic domain of OTULIN, which consists of Leu80–Arg345. Previous studies have identified HIS339, CYS129, and ASN341 as critical residues in the catalytic domain targeted by the deubiquitinating function of OTULIN (28). Thus, we constructed mutant OTULIN proteins with a point mutation at HIS339, CYS129, ASN341, TYR91, or LYS94 and probed the interaction between MZT2A and these mutant proteins using Co-IP. The results showed that the point mutation at HIS339 was abolished, whereas mutations of CYS129 and TYR91 suppressed the interaction between MTZ2A and OTULIN. Low MTZ2A–OTULIN interaction was accompanied by decreased p-IKK phosphorylation (p < 0.05, Fig. 5F). These results indicated that MTZ2A binds to multiple residues in the catalytic domain of OTULIN, thereby inhibiting its deubiquitinating activity.

MZT2A promoted cisplatin resistance in LUAD cells of nude mice

We established a nude mouse xenograft model of human LUAD to validate the in vitro effects of MZT2A and its domain MOZART2 in LUAD. BALB/c nude mice received a subcutaneous injection of A549 cells stably transfected with pNC, pMZT2A, pMZT2A-MOZART2, or pMZT2A-ΔMOZART2, followed by intraperitoneal injection of cisplatin (3 mg/kg) every 3 days for 2 weeks, starting 4 weeks after inoculation. We found that tumors derived from the pMZT2A and pMZT2A-MOZART2 groups grew faster than those derived from the pNC and pMZT2A-ΔMOZART2 groups. Discussion

The present study indicated that MZT2A promotes LUAD progression and chemoresistance in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistic studies revealed that in hypoxic conditions, HIF-1α directly upregulates MZT2A in LUAD cells and promotes IKK phosphorylation, leading to IκB degradation and NF-κB activation. These findings unveiled a novel mechanism linking HIF-1α to NF-κB, two well-recognized drivers of chemoresistance. Our work bridges these two critical pathways by identifying MZT2A—a protein not previously linked to chemoresistance—as a key hypoxia-inducible effector that directly activates NF-κB. This discovery adds a new player to the molecular network controlling treatment failure in LUAD.

Gene mutations contribute to the progression and drug resistance of LUAD through various mechanisms, including modulation of several signaling pathways and interaction with the tumor microenvironment (29, 30). For example, KRAS and epidermal growth factor receptor mutations frequently result in the aberrant activation of signaling pathways, such as Akt and NF-κB, activating Snail/Slug to facilitate epithelial–mesenchymal transition, thereby promoting tumor invasion (31, 32, 33, 34, 35). Therefore, targeting these driver mutations has become a critical component of targeted therapy against LUAD. However, the limited number of participants eligible for inclusion and the emergence of acquired drug resistance pose significant challenges in clinical practice (36, 37). Furthermore, resistance to cisplatin, a first-line broad-spectrum anticancer agent, worsens treatment outcomes for patients with LUAD. Cisplatin exerts its antitumor effects by disrupting microtubule assembly and stability and induces adverse effects, such as peripheral neuropathic pain (38, 39). Gamma tubulins (γ-tubulin) are not a component of the microtubule itself, but they are essential for microtubule nucleation. Inhibition of γ-tubulin was shown to interfere with cancer cell growth without affecting the cytoskeletal structure (40, 41, 42). MZT2A is a recently identified oncogenic factor in LUAD and a member of the γ-tubulin family. A positive association was found between MZT2A expression in tumor tissues and poor prognosis of NSCLC. MZT2A overexpression promoted the viability and invasion of NSCLC cells in vitro and in vivo by activating Akt (24). It has been reported that in hypoxic microenvironments, HIF1α directly binds to the NF-κB p65 subunit, upregulating Bcl-3/cyclin D1, thereby promoting drug resistance in tumor cells (43, 44). In this study, we observed increased MZT2A expression levels in human LUAD tissues, particularly in those exhibiting chemoresistance. MZT2A overexpression accelerated the growth of LUAD cells, inhibited apoptosis, and promoted cisplatin resistance in vitro and in vivo. We hypothesized that targeting MZT2A can sensitize LUAD to anticancer drugs by interfering with microtubule nucleation and inhibiting NF-κB, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target.

In our previous study, we detected increased levels of MZT2A expression in human NSCLC lesions and a positive association between MZT2A expression in cancer and poor prognosis of NSCLC. Furthermore, MZT2A overexpression promoted NSCLC cell viability and invasion in vitro and in vivo by activating Akt (24). In this study, we detected elevated expression levels of MZT2A in human LUAD tissues, particularly in those exhibiting chemoresistance. Moreover, high levels of MZT2A expression in LUAD were correlated with an advanced tumor stage and poor prognosis. MZT2A overexpression accelerated the growth of LUAD cells, inhibited apoptosis, and promoted cisplatin resistance in vitro and in vivo. These results suggest that pharmaceutical inhibition of MZT2A may help overcome drug resistance in LUAD and other subtypes of NSCLC and improve patients’ prognosis.

In our IHC experiments, we found that MZT2A mostly exists in the cytoplasm, but a small proportion of MZT2A is also present in the nucleus. This indeed differs from the data reported in databases such as UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/Q6P582/entry). This phenomenon was also observed in nuclear–cytoplasmic separation experiments and cell immunofluorescence experiments. We believe that the subcellular localization of the protein is subject to a certain degree of dynamic regulation. These findings indicate that the MZT2A protein exhibits a previously under-reported nuclear localization phenomenon, which is closely associated with its known functions or new functions. We will conduct more in-depth mechanistic studies in this regard.

The results of this study showed that MZT2A can promote LUAD by activating NF-κB through the inhibition of OTULIN-mediated NEMO deubiquitination. NEMO is the core regulator of the IKK complex, and its ubiquitination is essential for activating the NF-κB signaling and immune receptors, such as interferons and interleukin-1 (27, 45). In this study, we found that MZT2A was directly upregulated by HIF-1α in LUAD cells in response to hypoxia. Similar to hypoxia, MZT2A overexpression enhanced NEMO ubiquitination and activated IKK and NF-κB. The results of our molecular docking experiment, Co-IP, point mutation experiment, and mass spectroscopy analysis suggested that MZT2A promoted NEMO ubiquitination by binding to the catalytic domain of OTULIN and inhibiting NEMO-mediated deubiquitination (Fig. 7). Previous studies have identified HIS339, CYS129, and ASN341 as key residues in the catalytic domain of OTULIN responsible for the formation of the catalytic center for NEMO-mediated deubiquitination (28). Our molecular docking and point mutation experiments indicated that MZT2A interacts with OTULIN mainly through the catalytic domain residues HIS339, CYS129, and TYR91. Interestingly, the results of mass spectroscopy revealed potential interactions between MZT2A and other ubiquitination-related proteins, such as ubiquitin aldehyde binding 1 (OTUB1) and ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme 5 (UBA5), supporting the role of MZT2A as a general regulator of protein ubiquitination. Moreover, our molecular docking and domain deletion experiments indicated that the MORAZT2 domain of MZT2A directly interacts with the UBAN domain of NEMO. Given that NEMO ubiquitination occurs in the UBAN domain, we speculated that this interaction may regulate NEMO ubiquitination. We aim to investigate the functional significance of this interaction in our future studies.Figure 7Mechanism diagram.

Hypoxia and the NF-κB signaling are major factors driving drug resistance of tumor cells (15, 25). In particular, NF-κB expression in LUAD was found to be associated with chemoresistance (46), and inhibition of the NF-κB pathway can promote the sensitivity of LUAD cells to chemotherapeutic drugs (47). NF-κB is activated by hypoxia; however, how NF-κB becomes activated in hypoxia remains unclear. HIF, factor inhibiting HIF, prolyl-hydroxylases, transforming growth factor activating kinase 1, and the transforming growth factor activating kinase 1–IKK axis appear to be the major factors involved in hypoxia-induced NF-κB activation (26). In this study, we identified a novel mechanism for hypoxia-induced NF-κB activation that interacts with the HIF-1α–MTZ2A–OTULIN–NEMO signaling cascade. This finding sheds light on the crosstalk between HIF-1α and NF-κB that drives tumor drug resistance. Meanwhile, MZT2A overexpression in hypoxic conditions may modulate the tumor immune microenvironment and tumor response to chemotherapy. It has been reported that the ability of tumor cells to survive in hypoxic conditions is closely associated with the escape mechanism of immune cells (48, 49). In this study, MZT2A enhanced the survival of tumor cells by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway, which may lead to immune escape in cancer treatment. This offers new ideas for future strategies of immunotherapy, particularly in targeting hypoxia-induced tumor resistance.

Meanwhile, it should be noted that in the current clinical setting, multiple anticancer drugs are typically used in combination to overcome cisplatin resistance. In this study, we focused on the effect of MZT2A on cisplatin resistance in tumor cells. However, the role of MZT2A in combination with cisplatin has not been investigated so far, which can be a future research direction.

In summary, the present study identified MZT2A as a key mediator of hypoxia-induced NF-κB activation in human LUAD that contributes to chemoresistance. MZT2A can serve as a therapeutic target for overcoming drug resistance in LUAD and improving patients’ prognosis.

Experimental procedures

Single-cell sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

We employed the single-cell LUAD dataset GSE131907 to investigate the associations between hypoxia and LUAD (50). Datasets were renormalized using Seurat (Satija Lab, New York, NY). The principal component analysis was conducted using highly variable genes. Regarding the GSE131907 cohort, the major cell types were identified based on cross-cohort typical cell type–labeled scores. We analyzed the cellular programs of epithelial cells in LUAD primary lesions using high-dimensional weighted gene coexpression network analysis and employed the AUCell algorithm to predict the scores for the HALLMARK_HYPOXIA pathway.

The GSE157692 and GSE222187 datasets were screened to identify genes differentially expressed between chemoresistant and chemosensitive lung cancer cells. The criteria for differentially expressed genes were |log fold change| ≥2 and p < 0.05. Using the AUCell algorithm, we calculated the CDDP-resistant scores for LUAD cells. The Kaplan–Meier method (https://kmplot.com/analysis/) was employed to assess the relationship between MZT2A expression and survival.

Patients and biospecimens

Tissue specimens were obtained from 179 patients with LUAD who were admitted to Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University (Shenyang, China) between June 2022 and February 2023. Of the 179 patients, 27 underwent radical tumor excision combined with neoadjuvant chemotherapy. All patients were diagnosed following the World Health Organization TNM staging system (51). Based on the expert consensus on the pathological evaluation of neoadjuvant therapy efficacy for NSCLC in China (52), a pathological complete response was defined as a resection specimen without residual tumor cells after neoadjuvant therapy. A major pathologic response was defined as a resection specimen with less than 10% residual tumor cells after neoadjuvant therapy. Fifty of 179 cases obtained lung tissue without lesions at a distance of more than 3 cm from the tumor, which were marked as the normal group.

This study was reviewed and approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of Shengjing Hospital (approval number: 2022PS524K). All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent.

IHC staining

The paraffin blocks were cut into 3 μm sections and incubated with antibodies against MZT2A (ThermoFisher), Ki-67 (Proteintech), and HIF-1α (Proteintech). The sections were stained and scored, and the intensity indices (from 0 to 12) were calculated as described previously (24). The expression levels of MZT2A and HIF-1α were categorized as high or low based on cutoff staining indices of 3 and 5, respectively. Ki-67 staining was conducted, and the percentage of stain-positive cells was measured.

Cell culture

Human NCI-A549 cells (hereafter referred to as A549) were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. In the normoxia group, the cells were cultured in RPMI1640 (SevenBio) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Clark Bioscience) and penicillin (100 U/ml)/streptomycin (100 g/ml) (Beyotime) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. In the hypoxia group, the cells were cultured in an anoxic atmosphere containing 1% O2 (hypoxia) and 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 8 to 24 h.

The mouse model of LUAD

Twenty female BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks old) from Charles River Laboratories were housed in a specific pathogen-free barrier facility with temperature maintained at 22 to 24 °C and relative humidity maintained at 50% to 60%. A549 cells stably transfected with pNC, pMZT2A, pMZT2A-MOZART2, or pMZT2A-ΔMOZART2 were injected subcutaneously into the right forelimb armpit (1 × 106 cells, 100 μl), each group with 10 mice. Four weeks after inoculation, half of the mice in each group intraperitoneally received cisplatin (3 mg/kg), every 3 days for 2 weeks. The tumor volume was determined once weekly during the 6-week experiment. At the end of the experiment, all animals were anesthetized with 3% to 4% enflurane and euthanized using a carbon dioxide chamber. The tumors were harvested, photographed, and weighed. The tumor tissues were subjected to HE and IHC staining to detect MZT2A and Ki-67 levels. The animal study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of China Medical University (approval number: CMU2022011) and conducted following the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals issued by the Chinese Council on Animal Research.

Plasmid preparation and cell transfection

Plasmids carrying the human MZT2A cDNA (#RC219963), NEMO cDNA (#RC201743), and their corresponding vector controls were obtained from OriGene. MZT2A-siRNA, OTULIN-specific siRNA, and negative control siRNA were purchased from RioboBio. The siRNA sequences are presented in Table S1. The efficiency was validated based on real-time RT–qPCR and Western blotting. The sequences of siRNAs are presented in Table S1. Plasmids carrying the MOZART2 domain–deleted human MZT2A (designated as MZT2A-ΔMOZART2) were constructed in our research laboratory. Plasmids carrying the UBAN domain–deleted human NEMO cDNA (designated as NEMO-ΔUBAN) were obtained from GenePharma. The plasmids and siRNAs were transfected into A549 cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). The transfected cells were cultured in a medium containing G418 (geneticin sulfate, 400 μg/ml) to select cells stably expressing MZT2A.

Cell apoptosis and drug resistance

Cell apoptosis was measured using a flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson) and an Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining kit (KeyGEN BioTECH) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were inoculated into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and incubated with 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 μg/ml cisplatin (MedChemExpress) at 37 °C for 12 h to evaluate drug resistance. Cell viability was measured using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide reagent (MedChemExpress). Drug resistance was indicated as the percentage of viability relative to control viability ([absorbancepNC/pMZT2A with 1 to 16 μg/ml cisplatin-absorbanceblank]/[absorbancepNC with 0 μg/ml cisplatin – absorbanceblank] × 100%).

RT–qPCR

The total RNA of sample tissues was extracted using the AllPrep DNA/RNA FFPE Kit (#80234, QIAGEN). Total RNA was extracted using the Trizol reagent (TransGen Biotech). The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA using the PrimeScriptRT Master Mix kit (TAKARA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The experimental conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 5 s; 60 °C for 1 min; for 40 cycles continuously. The mRNA levels of MZT2A and GAPDH were measured using qPCR and specific primers (Table S2). The relative mRNA levels were determined using the comparative method (ΔΔCT), followed by normalization to the GAPDH level (an internal control).

Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay

The pGL3 luciferase reporter plasmids carrying the MZT2A promoter sequence or a putative regulatory sequence in the promoter of the MZT2A gene and the plasmids carrying the HIF-1α cDNA were cotransfected into A549 cells. Luciferase activity was measured 48 h after transfection using a dual-luciferase reporter gene assay kit (Promega). The plasmids are listed in the supporting information.

Western blotting

The cells were lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer (Beyotime) containing PMSF (Beyotime). Total protein concentration was measured using a bicinchoninic acid kit (Beyotime). The protein levels were separated in 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Invitrogen). For Western blotting, the membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed dry milk solution at room temperature for 2 h and incubated with a primary antibody (Table S3) at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the membranes were incubated at 37 °C with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse/rabbit IgG at a dilution of 1:20,000 (ZSBio) for 2 h. The protein bands were quantified using the Image Lab version 3.0 software (Bio-Rad).

MS and Co-IP

The total protein was extracted from stably transfected A549 cells. We added PMSF (#329-98-6, Proteintech) and phosphatase inhibitor (HY-K0021, MCE) to the collected protein samples to prevent protein degradation. Proteins interacting with MZT2A were collected using agarose gel beads, and the protein bands were identified by Coomassie brilliant blue staining. MS analysis was conducted at the BGI Center (n = 3, Beijing, China). The Q-Exactive mass spectrometer was used to detect the samples, set in positive ion mode and data-dependent manner with full MS scan from 350 to 2000 m/z, full scan resolution at 70,000, MS/MS scan resolution at 17,500. Raw MS/MS data were converted into an MGF format using Proteome Discoverer 1.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptide identification was conducted on the Mascot software (version 2.3.01, Matrix Science) using the UniProt database search algorithm and the integrated false discovery rate analysis function. For specific parameters, please refer to Table S4. The NEMO–MZT2A and OTULIN–MZT2A complexes formed in A549 cells were isolated based on Co-IP as previously described (24). The antibodies are listed in Table S3. The obtained samples were analyzed using Western blotting.

GST-pull down

We transfected GST-MZT2A plasmids into Escherichia coli. After amplifying the E. coli, we extracted the GST-labeled fusion protein. Thereafter, we conducted chromatography on glutathione agarose beads to purify the GST-MZT2A fusion protein. We extracted protein samples from A549 cells and incubated them with the purified GST-MZT2A protein at 4 °C overnight with gentle rotation. We eluted the bound proteins using an elution buffer (10 mM glutathione in PBS, pH 8.0) and analyzed them using Western blotting.

Immunocytochemistry

A549 cells were placed on coverslips and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The fixed cells were permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100, blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin, and incubated with anti-MZT2A and anti-NEMO antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the cells were dark incubated with a fluorescent secondary antibody, mounted in DAPI Fluromount-G (SevenBio), and observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus). The colocalization relationship of fluorescence was analyzed using the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, NIH, version 1.53e).

Ubiquitination detection

A549 cells were cotransfected with p-Ub-HA plasmids and MZT2A cDNA, MZT2A siRNA, or OTULIN siRNA for 48 h. Thereafter, gliotoxin (10 μM for 9 h, #HY-N6727, MedChemExpress), an inhibitor of linear ubiquitination that prevents ubiquitination-mediated IκB degradation (53), was added to prevent protein linear degradation. Each assay was conducted as described previously (54). After extracting the total cellular protein, the A/G agarose beads were used for blocking. Then, the beads were removed, and the agarose bead–antibody complex was added, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C. After centrifugation, the immune complexes were collected and washed, and the ubiquitination level was measured by Western blotting. Next, the immune complexes were harvested for Western blotting.

Molecular docking

The ZDOCK server was employed to predict the interactions between MZT2A (UniProt accession no.: Q6P582) and NEMO (UniProt accession no.: Q9Y6K9) and between MZT2A and OTULIN (UniProt accession no.: Q96BN8). PyMOL (version 4.3.0) was employed to obtain 3D dynamic biomolecular images showing interactions between specific amino acid residues from the two proteins. AlphaFold3 was employed to predict the crystal structures of MZT2A and OTULIN. The protein binding energy was measured using PDBePISA.

Statistical analysis

All in vitro experiments were repeated three times. All data are presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. Student's t test (parametric) or Mann–Whitney U test (nonparametric) was applied for comparisons between two groups as appropriate. One-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc test (parametric) or Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn's test (nonparametric) was used for multiple group comparisons. The Chi-squared test was employed to measure the relationships between MZT2A expression and the clinicopathological characteristics of patients with LUAD. All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism, version 10.4.1. A p value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.
