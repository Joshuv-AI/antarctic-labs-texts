Structure, Properties, and Applications of Silica Nanoparticles: Recent Theoretical Modeling Advances, Challenges, and Future Directions
Authors: Ben McLean, Irene Yarovsky
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSilica nanoparticles (SNPs), one of the most widely researched materials in modern science, are now commonly exploited in surface coatings, biomedicine, catalysis, and engineering of novel self‐assembling materials. Theoretical approaches are invaluable to enhancing fundamental understanding of SNP properties and behavior. Tremendous research attention is dedicated to modeling silica structure, the silica‐water interface, and functionalization of silica surfaces for tailored applications. In this review, the range of theoretical methodologies are discussed that have been employed to model bare silica and functionalized silica. The evolution of silica modeling approaches is detailed, including classical, quantum mechanical, and hybrid methods and highlight in particular the last decade of theoretical simulation advances. It is started with discussing investigations of bare silica systems, focusing on the fundamental interactions at the silica‐water interface, following with a comprehensively review of the modeling studies that examine the interaction of silica with functional ligands, peptides, ions, surfactants, polymers, and carbonaceous species. The review is concluded with the perspective on existing challenges in the field and promising future directions that will further enhance the utility and importance of the theoretical approaches in guiding the rational design of SNPs for applications in engineering and biomedicine.Keywords: functionalized silica, modelling, silica, silica force field, simulation

Introduction

Silica or silicon dioxide, SiO2, is one of the most widely studied compounds across science and engineering. Naturally, it is often present as quartz or sand, though it exists in a plethora of crystalline and amorphous forms. Silica in its crystalline form has been applied for thousands of years in the production of glass and concrete, while modern applications include high‐temperature ceramics and refractory materials. In crystalline silica, Si is coordinated by four O atoms, and each O bonds with two Si atoms. This forms a connected network of tetrahedra with central Si atoms and bridging O atoms. The Si─O─Si linkage represents the siloxane functional group. The SiO4 tetrahedron represents the building block of silica, with how they are arranged and connected (Si─O─Si bond angle) determining key properties. For example, the density of SiO4 tetrahedron packing into a periodic structure determines whether the properties will be quartz‐like or glass‐like. With Si possessing a formal charge of +4 and O a −2 the structure is neutral overall. The structure becomes “amorphous” when the SiO4 tetrahedrons are irregularly arranged, such that long‐range order is disrupted. This is a consequence of Si─O─Si bond angle variance and thus discrepancy in pore size between tetrahedral units. These cleaved Si─O bonds result in heterogeneity of surface features away from the bulk. This facilitates enhanced functionality at the silica surface. The primary silica surface functionalities are siloxane and silanol (SiOH). The unfilled valences of the exposed Si and O atoms are often satisfied by water, leading to surface hydroxyl groups. They can also be present in the bulk due to incomplete condensation during synthesis, though we focus here on surface functionalities. The presence of surface silanols has been confirmed for almost 75 years,[ 1 ] and they are classified often using Qn terminology, where n indicates the number of Si─O bridging bonds. We show examples of Qn classifications in Figure 1 , with surfaces and individual structural units. Today, surface silanols are still the subject of broad research efforts into the effects of their Qn identity, surface density, surface spacing, atomic charge, pKa, and more, with how they may be functionalized to induce or enhance chemical and physical properties the target for many next‐generation applications.

Currently, there exist significant bodies of literature detailing the properties and applications of the bulk silica and its surfaces and interfaces with various environments,[ 2 , 3 , 4 ] though despite a rich history of characterization and application, there is still a lack of information at the micro and nanoscale. This is particularly true for structurally amorphous silica, which by nature are inherently complex to characterize at atomic resolution. In addition, experimental data is difficult to interpret correctly in some instances where structural information on the studied system is unknown or lacking. To complement the experiments, theoretical modeling of the atomic structure and properties has been applied successfully to various forms of silica over the years, from bulk crystal and amorphous forms to silica surfaces and their interfaces with other materials, including biological molecules.[ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] The combination of theoretical molecular modeling with experiments became even important for atomically precise modern applications of silica where ligation and/or functionalization of the silica surface is performed with atomistic precision to achieve desired properties for electronic or biomedical applications. In the case of ligation for example, unravelling the interactions between silica and the ligand, silica and the solvent and the ligand and the solvent can be very challenging experimentally alone. Even for the bare silica – aqueous interface, there lacks a clear and distinguished picture regarding the ordering and dynamics of water at the interface, from both the experimental and theoretical perspectives, as the environmental conditions become complex.

In this perspective we focus on the theoretical advances enabling atom‐resolved modeling of a form of amorphous silica that exemplifies the importance of these structural characteristics and interactions; silica nanoparticles (SNPs). With the recent advents of nanoscience and nanotechnology, and due to their tailorable structural characteristics and ease of functionalization, SNPs have demonstrated enormous potential for use in biomedicine, pharmaceuticals, industrial coatings, and catalysis, among others. We highlight some key examples of SNP functionalization in Figure 2 . However, fundamental aspects of the structure, properties, and behavior of SNPs lack comprehensive understanding. Due to their relatively small size, it is here that computer simulation presents as a crucial tool to utilize synergistically with experimental investigation to probe the SNP structure(s) with precise control over system conditions and interactions and is hence capable of revealing atomic information that experiments alone could not. We examine the evolution and contributions of computer simulation that have enhanced our understanding of SNP structure, properties, behavior, functionalization, and self‐assembly. We first discuss the simulation methodologies that have been applied to studying various forms of silica, followed by detailing key findings made possible by computer simulation as well as remaining research challenges to target as theoretical efforts continue to complement and guide the experiments. While we present a brief chronology of the method development for simulating silica, we subsequently narrow our focus to advances in the last decade and refer the reader to extensive reviews that discuss the prior theoretical contributions in more detail,[ 5 , 9 ] and those where theory and experiment are used in tandem.[ 2 ] We aim to provide a useful perspective of the current state of the field for a broad audience, including how computer simulation has contributed to experimental and application advancements while also providing computational specialists with key insights.

Evolution of Silica Modeling Approaches

Modeling material structure requires understanding of the fundamental interatomic connectivity, 3D atomic coordinates, and larger‐scale morphology of a bulk material (crystal or amorphous). It is often an important and necessary step toward subsequent modeling of more complex, curved morphologies such as NPs, unless formed via condensation of precursors.[ 10 , 11 ] Capturing the properties of silica as a material requires precise and accurate treatment of the bulk atomic 3D geometry and the structure of the cleaved surfaces and NPs. For relatively small systems, this can be accomplished most precisely with quantum mechanical (QM) computational methods, where explicit electronic structure considerations are involved. We demonstrate this relationship between the spatial and temporal scale and what physicochemical phenomena can be modeled for a range of theoretical methods in Figure 3 . With the increased complexity and length scale of the models, such as amorphous silica surfaces and colloidal NPs, particularly when modeling their behavior in aqueous environments, the surface functionalization and/or response to external stimuli, QM methods become prohibitively computationally expensive. Therefore, in the simulation of relatively larger scale silica models, the QM methods for the majority have been used in the process of developing more computationally accessible methods such as classical molecular dynamics (MD) and mechanics based on interatomic interaction potentials (force fields, FFs). The latter approach enables the efficient modeling of silica on larger spatial and time scales in experimentally relevant environments, while assuming only physical interactions take place with no chemical bonds forming or breaking, thus allowing to forsake the electronic accuracy in the model.

For all methods, periodic boundary conditions are commonly employed to model 3D crystals or the crystalline surfaces. These conditions reduce the size of the system to that of a repeating unit cell to increase computational efficiency. This becomes non‐trivial when modeling amorphous materials, such as silica and is not practical for modeling explicit NPs. Surface slabs are often used to approximate NP surfaces, however. This section summarises the development of FFs aimed at modeling silica‐based materials prior to the last decade, with more recent advances and current developments presented in Section 3. In Section 3 we present recent advances and investigations into the understanding of bare SNPs and functionalized SNPs, distinguishing the nature of the modification to the surface for functionalized SNPs.

Atom‐Resolved Structure and Properties: Classical FF Methods

Initially, modeling silica structure and properties relied on interatomic potentials, FFs, mathematical functions that describes the total potential energy of a molecular system as a function of atomic (x,y,z) coordinates and a set of parameters via an analytical expression.[ 22 ] FFs contain several terms that describe bonded (bond length, angle and torsions) and non‐bonded (van der Waals and electrostatic) interactions of mechanically connected charged particles (atoms). The FF parameters can be based on empirical data or as described above, can be determined via more accurate theoretical calculations using QM methods. These FF parameters and the mathematic terms that make up the overall potential are what separate different FF sets. They are often named for their developers, their purpose, or their functional form. We refer below to “two‐body” and “three‐body” potentials, indicating the number of atoms considered when describing interatomic interactions.

Early classical two‐body potentials, named for their developers, Beest Kramer van Santen (BKS)[ 23 ] and Tsuneyuki Tsukada Aoki Matsui (TTAM),[ 24 , 25 ] saw success at modeling various silica polymorphs. However, the flexibility of the Si─O─Si bond required three‐body terms for better accuracy, e.g., for the vitreous silica models of Feuston and Garofalini, one of several potentials introducing them to represent covalent bonds.[ 26 , 27 , 28 ] Some early potentials focussed on simulating bulk silica properties were based on density functional theory (DFT) calculations of small silica clusters.[ 29 , 30 , 31 ] While in hindsight the BKS potential has been improved upon in the last decade or so, in the 1990′s, a great deal of information regarding quartz structure,[ 23 , 32 ] and phase transitions[ 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 ] was uncovered. The qualitative failures of BKS were remedied by a later potential[ 37 ] parametrized by ab initio calculations[ 38 ] with added polarizability terms for the O atoms, accounting for electrostatic interactions. Early interatomic potential functions parameterized by ab initio methods were capable of large‐scale modeling of 3D bulk silica networks,[ 39 ] though could not capture long‐range coulombic or dispersive interactions that are crucial for appropriately modeling silica surfaces. Fixed‐charge potentials that struggled with the electrostatic environment of silica[ 23 ] were extended by accounting for dipole polarization[ 38 ] and later improved further via reparameterization,[ 40 ] though focussed on bulk structure and properties. Methods developed in the last two decades have extended their focus from the bulk structure to account for H‐bonded features at the silica surface and the interface of silica with water. For crystalline silica surfaces, experiments, and theoretical investigations have reported the observation of ordered, ice‐like water.[ 41 ] On amorphous silica surfaces, the presence of this ordered water structure may be absent, yet the molecular packing of water may differ with the degree and the heterogeneity of surface silanol ionization.

The classical potentials adopted in the 1990′s for simulating silica suffered from the drawback of being unable to accurately model structural, energetic, and vibrational features of the H‐bond capable silanol surface groups. Some early potentials were applied to modeling a range of crystalline surfaces, focusing on water adsorption to siloxane bridges,[ 42 ] though were deemed unfit to predict OH vibrations and H‐bond geometries. Other models extended those used for silica bulk such as BKS in conjunction with popular water models (e.g., single‐point charge, SPC)[ 43 , 44 ] to model the hydroxylated surface,[ 45 ] while the widely used Tersoff potential had also been applied.[ 46 ] Additionally, the Tersoff potential was reparametrized by for silica.[ 47 ] The Tersoff potential incorporates a dependence on the bond order, as opposed to the aforementioned BKS and TTAM potentials. DFT calculations employing the B3LYP functional were used to parametrize the FFSiOH FF;[ 48 ] a partial charge shell‐ion model that capably characterized the bulk and H‐bonding surface features of silica. These models discussed above,[ 23 , 29 ] as well as others based on DFT‐calculated silica nanoclusters,[ 14 ] and surfaces,[ 48 ] differ in regard to several key parameters such as the atomic charge of Si and the ionicity of the Si‐O bond. While these discrepancies may have negative implications when modeling interfacial phenomena and surface chemistry, the 3D morphologies generated by the potentials are still useful, as are the predicted bulk properties of crystalline silicas and glasses.[ 49 ]

Generally, the analytical expressions describing the classical FFs discussed above are not suited to describe chemical reactions, as they do not consider any changes in the electronic structure. Morse functions are a simple option in this regard to study covalent bonds. Morse functions are two‐body potentials that represent the potential energy function as the difference between the interatomic distance and the equilibrium interatomic distance, thus can account for bond breaking, though are not sufficient without an accompanying model to tackle partially ionic systems like silica.[ 50 , 51 ] Charge equilibration (QEq) methods, that predict charge distribution in molecules based on geometry and atomic properties, have been used in conjunction with Morse potentials to remedy this.[ 52 ] Reactive potentials that describe bond cleavage‐formation allow for processes such as hydroxylation, H‐transfer, and NP self‐assembly to be studied. Reactive potentials are so referred as they can model chemical reactions, representing the potential energy of a system as a function of the bond order, which updates on the fly as atom connectivity changes. Electronic interactions are implicitly included in FF parameters. For example, a reactive FF[ 53 ] for modeling silica structures including silica‐based reactionary species and complexes. This FF, ReaxFFSiO has been employed and further parameterized for modeling processes in silicon and silica systems including silica hydroxylation,[ 54 ] the silica‐water interface,[ 55 ] the silica‐gas interface,[ 56 ] ion mobility[ 57 , 58 ] and defect migration.[ 59 ]50 A dissociative potential, MGFF, also demonstrated consistency with experimental data on water adsorption to silica.[ 60 ] Overall, the ReaxFF approach represents a powerful tool for exploring interfacial properties of silica systems though is limited by the need for repeated reparameterization and validation for new and unique systems, often via extensive DFT calculations.

Integrating and/or adapting the parameters of these FFs into popular methods for modeling water and other pertinent molecular and charged systems is vital for accurately studying SNPs. While the functional forms of early FFs were not compatible with water models used for interfacial and biomolecular studies, bonded potentials emerged boasting applicability to study the nanostructure and properties and adsorbed water on silica (notably CHARMM‐consistent expressions),[ 61 , 62 , 63 ] that were compatible with the TIP3P water model.[ 64 ] These however had shortcomings in the stoichiometry of the Si‐O network and when compared to structural experimental data, and could not reproduce measured density values for quartz.[ 5 ] Nonbonded potentials such as CLAYFF[ 36 , 65 ] allowed for large‐scale simulations of silicate‐water interfaces that accounted for energy and momentum transfer between phases. However, by treating these interactions as nonbonded and representing water and hydroxyl via the SPC water model,[ 43 , 44 ] the very nature of chemical bonding within silica is neglected as is charge variance of O and H atoms due to their environment. Comparisons of early silica FFs[ 66 , 67 ] demonstrated discrepancies to experiment and/or ab initio methods regarding surface water structure and inadequacies in describing the nuances of hydrogen bonding at the silica‐water interface. In fact, the degree of surface ionization was neglected for the first few decades of modeling silica with FF methods.[ 68 ] We note that many recent advances or studies of silica utilize modified versions of these FFs, which make up for some of their shortcomings. We discuss existing challenges and future directions for modeling silica in Section 4.

Electronic Structure and Properties: QM Methods

The application of QM methods to model periodic crystalline silica surfaces, small silica clusters and the interactions of pertinent surface sites has been demonstrably effective but somewhat limited due to a much greater computational cost than that of the classical methods described above. Even more so for QM methods, SNPs are approximated with small, periodic unit cells or silica surface slabs. However, the construction of a model surface is important, as surface reconstructions may be required when considering extensive dehydroxylation of the surface silanols. Rozanska et al. studied the dehydroxylation of crystalline silica surfaces as a function of temperature and water partial pressure using PW91‐based DFT to illustrate this.[ 69 ] This makes the unit cell dimensions a key parameter when designing a periodic system for simulation. Patterning artefacts induced by unit cell restrictions and boundaries will have influence on the surface chemistry observed.

Ab initio methods approximate explicit electronic structure within molecules and materials, including a description of the electron correlation and hence struggle to optimize large systems, or dynamically model any significant timescale. This is especially apparent for systems lacking symmetry, such as heterogenous and amorphous silica surfaces (NPs), particularly when solvation is also considered. Hence, ab initio calculations are often performed in vacuo for a single molecule or small molecular clusters. Here we use ab initio to describe calculations performed by methods based on first principles. These include wave function methods employing Møller‐Plesset Perturbation Theory,[ 70 ] which model electron correlation effects, for example the widely used second‐order correction MP2,[ 71 ] and DFT‐based calculations, which approximate the wave function as an electron density.[ 72 ] There exist many “flavours” of DFT which vary in computational complexity and accuracy in approximating the exchange‐correlation energy.[ 73 ] We refer readers to excellent resources on wave function‐[ 74 , 75 ] and DFT‐based[ 76 , 77 ] methods for more detail.

Early analysis of silica structure and surface properties was conducted with the local density approximation (LDA).[ 78 ] BLYP provided a better description of H‐bonding and was employed to probe hydroxylation and silanization,[ 79 , 80 ] as well as water adsorption.[ 81 , 82 ] PW91 was also used to study the reconstruction of crystalline silica, and the adsorption of water layers via ab‐initio MD.[ 83 , 84 ] B3LYP emerged as a promising functional to model both the structure[ 85 ] and the H‐bonding features[ 86 ] of crystalline silica, showing good agreement with experimental data.[ 87 , 88 , 89 , 90 ] Dispersion corrections were found to be a major driving force for adsorption at the silica surface even from the earliest studies employing it in the gas‐phase.[ 91 , 92 , 93 ] Stability increased upon hydroxylation of the surface though surface stability depended strongly on the degree H‐bonding between silanol sites.[ 94 ] Additionally, cooperative bonding of water molecules with surface silanols induced water structure at the interface compared to the bulk.[ 95 ] The reactivity of radical defects with water to hydroxylate the surface and the production of OH· and H· radicals has been investigated with cluster[ 96 ] and 2D surface models.[ 97 ]

Attempts have been made to utilize DFT to construct hydroxylated amorphous surfaces,[ 48 , 98 ] which correlate well with experiment for some temperatures.[ 99 ] Reconstructing the quartz surface structure has long been a topic of interest for first‐principles calculations.[ 78 , 84 , 94 , 100 , 101 , 102 , 103 ] An alternative approach is to approximate the amorphous silica surface using clusters that exhibit the desired surface chemistry. This can allow the optimization of a surface feature using higher‐order methods and explicit solvation. Early clusters were built on silicate ring models, and could predict multiple properties of silica well,[ 104 , 105 , 106 , 107 ] though interfacial interactions are not compatible. By expanding the size of the cluster to account for the local environment of pertinent surface groups, one can retain a cluster‐based approach but more accurately model the silica surface.

Another effective strategy is to employ a hybrid methodology, specifically where a high level of theory treats the inner cluster and desired surface moieties while the outer region is treated with a lower level of theory, often referred to as quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM). This was initially proposed as the integrated molecular orbital molecular mechanics (IMOMM) method[ 108 ] though was extended to allow quantum mechanical approaches as the ONIOM2 method.[ 109 , 110 ] These methods and similar have been shown to be applicable to modeling dense and porous silica morphologies.[ 111 , 112 ] It is however still much more computationally efficient to utilize interatomic potentials for MD of large systems.

A recent methodology to study more representative clusters and amorphous surfaces arose in density functional tight binding (DFTB), with the method applied to studying defects in crystalline silica.[ 113 ] An extended two‐centre Hückel approximation to DFT utilizing a minimal Slater‐type all valence basis set, DFTB approximates the electronic density assuming only small perturbations from a reference density occur. This permits explicit electronic contributions yet allows for dynamic simulation of molecular systems. In addition, a dispersion correction can be applied.[ 114 ] Applying DFTB to studying adsorption at the silica surface allowed for molecular adsorption to surface slabs to be modeled including explicit solvent consideration.[ 115 ]

In summary, while quantum chemical‐derived methods can provide a more accurate energetic description for individual interactions, appropriately modeling SNPs to replicate and/or relate to experiment and to their application requires NPs to be minimum 1–2 nm in diameter and be adequately solvated. This means systems containing thousands of atoms and timescales on the order of hundreds of nanoseconds to microseconds and beyond, rendering DFT, QM‐MM, and DFTB‐based approaches less useful than classical methods. In the last decade, there have been key advances in the development of FF‐based methods that we focus on here, gaining ground on the accuracy of higher‐order methods because of comprehensive parameterization and training.

Recent Simulation Advances for Targeted SNP Applications

We henceforth discuss computational investigations of structural and dynamic properties of SNPs as well as their interactions with the surrounding media, including solvent, (bio)organic molecules, and inorganic materials. We begin by examining classical methods applied to study silica structure and interfaces and follow with QM methods. As described in Figure 3, these methods are applied to modeling silica on different time and length scales, each with their own advantages and limitations. We aimed to focus primarily on SNPs, though it is still common for a SNP to be modeled as a surface slab, mimicking a local region of a large(r) NPs. However, while we note that experiments demonstrate the silica surface ionization to be dependent on the particle size.[ 68 , 116 ] There are almost no atomistic simulations that employ a true to size (and curvature) SNP model. We discuss this as one of the outstanding challenges in the field and future directions in Section 4.

Bare SNPs

Classical Methods

The interatomic interaction potentials for silica described in Section 2.1 have attracted research interest in the last decade, for example, the BKS potential has been adjusted to better represent mechanical behavior,[ 117 ] truncated with Coulomb interactions for better performance,[ 118 , 119 , 120 ] and used to generate a database of amorphous slab models (Figure 4b).[ 121 , 122 ] In some instances it has outperformed a host of other potentials for modeling amorphous silica structure.[ 123 ] ClayFF has also been improved over time and has been valuable in studying material‐water interfaces.[ 124 ] However, earlier potentials suffer from shortcomings that the design of more recent methods have sought to address. This includes but is not limited to the ionicity of the Si─O bond, treatment of surface functionalities like silanols, surface heterogeneity, atomic charges, and the ability to model a silica interfaced with other materials. Parameters optimized via DFT that accurately modeled water on neutral and negatively charged amorphous silica were developed.[ 125 ] Notably, this FF did not rely on fixing the silica structure during simulation, allowing it to be fully flexible. Additionally, atomic point charges were highlighted as crucial for the energy landscape to agree with DFT, including the adjustment of second nearest neighbors for deprotonated sites. The assignment of atomic charges for classical simulations has been emphasized for decades.[ 126 ] It is best to derive and validate charges using a combination of experimental and theoretical evidence. Experimentally they can be difficult to resolve in amorphous systems, and individual theoretical partitioning schemes share unique strengths and weaknesses that lead to uncertainties. The INTERFACE FF features thermodynamic consistency with experimental parameters for a range of compounds, initially including bulk silica and Q4, Q3, and Q2 quartz surfaces.[ 127 ] A silica‐specific FF derived from the same principles as INTERFACE was proposed by the same group in 2014.[ 5 ] This work increased the computed accuracy of many properties of the silica surface (Q2, Q3, Q4, Figure 1) and its interaction with water. The nature of the surface as a function of pH with varied surface silanol ionization was also considered. Thus, the work introduced a database of silica surface models with wide ranges of silica surface environments as demonstrated in Figure 4a. By customizing the degree of ionization, they proposed that SNPs could be approximated by Q3 silica surfaces, with an average area density of surface silanol groups of 4.5–4.9 per nm2. Importantly though, this was demonstrated to be dependent on the size of the NP. Additionally, on Q3 surfaces, multiple water layers were observed, dependent on the water model applied, though the water density profile appeared independent of Na+ content. Various energy expressions of prior FFs created challenges in transferability, so this potential was made compatible with CHARMM,[ 128 , 129 ] AMBER,[ 130 ] CVFFF,[ 131 ] PCFF,[ 29 , 132 , 133 ] COMPASS,[ 134 ] and INTERFACE.[ 127 ] We note several examples in this review where this FF has been broadly applied to studying silica with biomolecules, polymers, and inorganic materials. FFs parameterized by experimental results, and by DFT‐based methods have been applied to model properties of silica‐based systems yet there is still contention between those that study silica and in the broader materials simulation community about the more appropriate methodology for training classical FFs. We return to this discussion in Section 4.

Common practice to approximate an amorphous silica surface is to perform MD on crystalline silica structures at high temperature followed by quenching to kinetically trap the structure where long‐range order is diminished,[ 135 , 136 ] even for more recent FF generations,[ 125 , 137 ] though this practice can introduce artifacts at the interface.[ 138 ] Hence, cleaving prior to the quench has been a recently employed approach (Figure 4c).[ 121 , 139 ] Cleaving prior to the quench resulted in surfaces consistent with experimental diffraction data, reproducing the first sharp diffraction peak in the structure factor. Specific hydroxylated crystalline silica models can also mimic behavior of amorphous surfaces.[ 140 , 141 ] Also notable are bottom‐up approaches to building surfaces where the initial sol‐gel process is simulated.[ 142 ]

Experimental contact angles of a water droplet on Q3 and Q4 silica polymorphs were modeled.[ 5 ] For silanol densities of 4.7, 2.35, 1.2, and 0 hydroxylated silanol per nm2, contact angles of 0°, 28 ± 5°, 34 ± 5°, and 43 ± 5° were observed, respectively. Similar simulations of water droplets on silica with varying surface functionality were performed, in the presence of sequestered CO2.[ 143 ] While the angles obtained could not be directly compared with those of experiment (due to difficulties to determine precise experimental surface functionality), the predicted contact angles on Q3 amorphous silica for 3 M NaCl solutions agreed with that of dewetting droplets. Similar simulations found that ionic strength impacted the contact angle, though surface roughness and surface ionization were not considered.[ 144 ] Prior simulations utilizing the COMPASS FF calculated a Young's contact angle of 22.3 ± 3° for 5.6 SiOH nm−2, an overestimation compared to the experiment though interestingly, despite the heterogeneity of the surface, the contact angle value did not vary with water droplet size.[ 145 ] Interestingly, for patterned surfaces, a relationship between droplet volume and contact angle as a function of surface structure due to contact line pinning has been observed by MD simulations based on the multibody Tersoff potential.[ 146 ] DFT was used to obtain nonbonded parameters for MD simulations of water on silica and found a contact angle of 12.4° for a quartz surface.[ 147 ] It was observed using classical MD that for the same silanol density, spacing of surface silanol groups changed the contact angle of water by 20° and determined whether ordered, ice‐like water was observed on the surface, with more disordered Q3 amorphous surfaces leading to less extensive H‐bonding and no ice‐like water structure forming.[ 143 ] An MD investigation showed contact angles water droplets wetting Q3 silica with contact angles and those calculated by thermodynamic integration by a phantom wall were very similar.[ 148 ] The two methods agreed that hydroxylated silica exhibited enhanced wettability, though the predicted values overestimated the experiment and prior simulations. They demonstrated a linear relationship between interfacial entropy loss and silanol density and a decrease in droplet mobility with increasing surface heterogeneity.

MD simulations of model amorphous silica surfaces interacting with TIP4P‐Ew[ 149 ] water demonstrate marked changes in hydration properties for intermediate silanol densities (2.0–2.9 nm−2), dependent on the spatial distribution of silanol groups on the surface.[ 150 ] A genetic algorithm was then used to investigate patterning of silanol on a crystalline silica surface. In agreement with surface force apparatus (SFA) data, water surface diffusivity decreases with surface silanol density, with MD simulations showing a 10% fluctuation in water diffusivity with altered silanol spacing, as plotted in Figure 5a. The same authors showed that in general, for both hydrophilic and hydrophobic surfaces, interfacial water dynamics are governed by the spacing of surface functionality.[ 151 ] In agreement, ReaxFF simulations of annealed glassy silica‐water interfaces showed that surface silanol density and the spatial distribution of those silanols contribute to the hydrophilic to hydrophobic phase transition of silica.[ 152 ] In fact, they observed a positive linear correlation between the silica–water interface energy and the silanol surface density, indicating that hydrophilicity of silica is indeed controlled by silanol surface density (Figure 5c). Specifically, if silanol density is high, i.e., for a hydrophilic silica surface, each water molecule forms two H‐bonds with the surface, where only one will form for low silanol density, a hydrophobic silica surface. From these simulations, a silica surface would be fully hydrophobic for silanol surface densities of ≈0.6 nm−2. Further, from Figure 5c, surface silanol density decreases with increasing annealing temperature. In the study of bulk water on silica surfaces, experimental evidence has pointed to distinct layers of water that form at the silica‐water interface. The diffusivity of the water closest to the interface is decreased, leading to its referral as ice‐like. MD simulations performed employing the COMPASS FF,[ 134 ] in striking agreement with frequency‐modulated atomic force microscopy (FM‐AFM), demonstrate that there is a single densely bound hydration layer on the surface (at heights less than 0.75 nm), with a discontinuous second layer with decreased density.[ 154 ] With the heterogeneity of the surface silanol groups, the second hydration layer does not contain an ordered network of structured water molecules, while the first layer (within 0.35 nm) is composed of individual water molecules or ions clustering at adsorption sites, covering the whole surface. This markedly changes with the introduction of silica surface ligands, as we discuss in a later section. Given the dynamic nature of interfacial water including exchange reactions, dissociation, and transfer, reactive FFs provide a valuable tool to examine these phenomena through MD compared to other classical potentials. In addition, the formation of and reaction with surface silanols can be examined. For instance, the lifetimes of hydronium ions at the interface are shorter than their lifetimes in bulk water, as observed by reactive FF simulations.[ 156 ] With ongoing reparameterization, continuous improvements have been made to ReaxFF. For example, early DFT cluster calculations used the Minnesota functionals,[ 56 ] though this was aimed at improving gas‐surface interactions. ReaxFF was later optimized to better describe silica‐water interactions[ 54 ] and showed that silanol formation following H3O+ proton donation was more likely at sites of high strain (Figure 7a). Proton donation from H3O+ occurred within 10 ps of simulation to form a silanol at a siloxane bridge (Figure 7b). This was observed to rapidly dissociate into an isolated silanol and an undercoordinated Si atom, free for subsequent hydroxyl formation, though this was not shown. Additionally, increasing the simulation temperature from 900 to 1100 K has been shown to accelerate silanol formation. This ReaxFF parameterization was demonstrated to yield structures, reaction mechanisms, hydroxylation rates, and defect healing pathways[ 157 ] that agreed with DFT‐based MD simulations shown in Figure 7c,d.[ 158 ] Specifically, Figure 7c shows the local structure of deprotonated silanols at the interface of water and amorphous silica. The number of H‐bonds accepted from water and from neighboring silanols varies by Qn identity. Figure 7d presents the five steps of two‐membered ring cleavage during the hydroxylation of a Q4 silanol. ReaxFF (and ClayFF) were unable to replicate experimental surface energies.[ 159 ] Reaction pathways of water bonding with an undercoordinated Si atom at the surface were demonstrated later using the MGFF potential.[ 160 ] MGFF was argued to be less computationally expensive then ReaxFF while providing an accurate description of water properties and water‐silica reactivity than earlier ReaxFF potentials.[ 54 , 161 ] Notably, a second‐generation ReaxFF potential improved the description of water proton transfer mechanisms.[ 162 ] An alternative all atom reactive potential[ 163 , 164 ] has also demonstrated the ability to model proton transfer between interfacial water and silanols and between interfacial water and the bulk.[ 165 ] Additionally, this FF has described silica glass expansion as a result of silanol formation following siloxane rupture rather than intact water molecules.[ 166 ]

Potential application of silica surfaces in microelectromechanical systems (MEMs) such as accelerometers and biosensors, has seen several investigations into tribological properties. As tribological behavior relies on the interaction of surface functionalities, simulations provide a platform to observe molecular origins of friction and wear. The tendency of Si─O─Si bridge formation between silica and silicon surfaces in dry conditions using MD simulations is shown in Figure 9a, with water decreasing atom transfer events across the interface, as silanols (that interact less with silicon) form.[ 169 ] ReaxFF simulations were employed to study the interfacial friction and wear of two fully hydroxylated silica surfaces in dry conditions.[ 13 ] Results demonstrated that increased surface silanol density reduced friction and wear. QM Methods

Describing the silica‐water interface at the QM level is challenging, particularly for amorphous silica. System sizes need to be large enough to capture a sufficient heterogenous surface and dynamics need to be sampled over a long enough time to observe not only equilibration but physical and chemical events such as diffusion and transfer. QM calculations have proved effective at distinguishing individual silanol sites and their interaction energies with water,[ 170 ] indicating preferential H‐bonding to geminal and vicinal silanols over siloxane bridges. Water H‐bonding modes to various silanol sites on amorphous silica as calculated by dispersion‐corrected PBE is presented in Figure 10 . The length of H‐bonds observed varied between 1.60 and 2.20 Å. Water was found to be more likely to adsorb to vicinal silanols rather than geminal.

a) Water‐silanols interaction (O─H bond) types and distances on various sites of the amorphous silica. b) Water interaction and H─bonds distances on the β‐cristobalite silica: (111), (001), and (101) surfaces. Reproduced with permission.[ 170 ] Copyright 2020, Elsevier.

For a lot of FFs, atomic charges and atom types may only consider isolated silanols and not geminal, nor H‐bonded vicinal silanols. The structure of water on amorphous silica was studied by ab initio MD that found all surface silanols to be H‐bond donors to water, while vicinal silanols could also accept H‐bonds.[ 171 ] BLYP‐D2 MD simulations of water on hydrophobic silica models[ 48 ] illustrated weakly H‐bonded water at siloxane oxygens, the number of which correlated with an increase in siloxane sites on the surface and an increase in the contact angle.[ 172 ] In contrast, at a crystalline silica (quartz) surface, the first layer of interfacial water was disordered. The physical arrangement of water at varying silanols is also a consequence of their chemical acid‐base behavior. MD simulations found that silica surface silanols exhibited bimodal acid‐base behavior.[ 173 , 174 ] The calculated pKas of surface silanols ranged from 4.8 (donor SiOH on 1010 β surface) to > 11 for inner silanols on 1011, acceptor SiOH on 1010 β and deprotonated SiO−. Figure 11a demonstrates the bimodal behavior via water incorporation mechanisms. DFT‐based MD simulations employing the BLYP functional show that on quartz, in‐plane and out‐of‐plane silanol surface groups act as H‐bond acceptors and donors, respectively with interfacial water.[ 175 ] Water molecules were observed to prefer to form a single H‐bond with the surface, while maximizing H‐bonding with water in the second near‐surface layer. The authors showed the pKa of the out‐of‐plane silanols to be 5.6. The acidic nature of the silanols indicating they donate H‐bonds to water in the “ice‐like” interfacial layer. The same group of authors found the in‐plane silanols had a pKa of 8.5, and calculated the point of zero charge to be 1.0.[ 153 ] The distinction of silanols and the differing strengths of H‐bonds they formed with water provided an explanation for experimentally observed vibrational sum frequency generation (VSFG) spectra on quartz.[ 176 ] Later the same group[ 167 ] and others examined the amorphous silica‐water interface[ 177 ] with PBE‐based MD simulations to demonstrate that the type of site alone did not determine the acidity of the silanol, as the solvent accessibility and H‐bonding played key roles. Figure 11b shows a H‐bond network on amorphous silica showing non‐acidic silanols accepting H‐bonds with H2O while acidic silanols donate H‐bonds. They found pKa values of 2.9 for a convex Q2 and 8.9 for a concave Q2 geminal silanol, and a pKa of 2.1 for Q3 vicinal silanols in a hydrophilic environment and 16.2 for Q3 vicinal silanols in a hydrophobic environment, while isolated Q3 silanols had a pKa of 10.3. In agreement with experimental measurements, the pKa of these silanol sites increased with the addition of salt in accordance with the Hofmeister series of ions.[ 178 ] With ions present, the H‐bond network around the silanols is weakened, stabilizing the O‐H silanol bond and reducing the chance of ionization. Ultimately, these findings by QM calculations question the simplification of silanol classification that may be employed in a FF. While large systems and long timescales can be achieved with the classical MD, the FF parameterization may need to become more rigorous, as QM uncovers more information about specific silanol behaviors in different environments. We return to this discussion in Section 4.

Disruption of the covalent Si‐O network results in a range of reactive surface sites being exposed. When designing classical methods and assigning charges to surface silanol groups, it's important to properly characterize the nature of the silanol. Exposed surface sites, particularly in amorphous systems, may require further distinction than simply protonated or deprotonated. Though without electronic consideration, these distinctions may not be significant, though this has not been comprehensively studied. Recently, amorphous silica surfaces cleaved from bulk with controlled surface roughness were presented.[ 122 ] The authors defined a “roughness parameter” that controls the width of a Gaussian distribution which sets the cleaving plane. The slabs, generated by the BKS FF, were re‐equilibrated and interfaced with water using ReaxFF. The rougher the surface, the higher the concentration of silanols formed at the surface, inducing bimodal acidity, a phenomenon described previously via DFT calculations.[ 177 ]

Alternatively, QM has been a powerful tool in describing silica systems. A cluster‐based approach employed B3LYP with a 6–31+G(d,p) basis set to optimise small molecules that represented exposed silica surface sites.[ 179 ] From homolytic cleavage of Si─O, radical siloxy and silylene sites were derived. Heterolytic cleavage leads to charged surface sites, silyl and silylene cations and siloxy anions as well as deprotonated geminal silanediols. These calculations suggested that homolytic cleavage of Si─O was more favorable than heterolytic, however did not account for stabilising electrostatic interactions and hydroxylation when relaxing surface sites in slab models.[ 94 ] Later embedded cluster models employing B3LYP demonstrated that the radical species’ singly occupied orbital centres on one of the 2p orbitals of O.[ 180 ] This work also showed that silyl and siloxy neutral defects mentioned above can also form negatively charged defects, behaving as deep electron traps. The significance being that these compete for electrons and weaken and elongate Si─O bonds, leading to oxygen vacancies and interstitial O2−,[ 181 ] that preferentially reside at the silica boundaries.[ 182 ] Extra electrons can also be trapped at intrinsic precursor sites in amorphous silica,[ 183 , 184 ] all giving rise to experimentally observed optical bands that were unable to be explained by experiment alone.[ 185 ] Figure 12a shows the spin density within amorphous silica. Figure 12b plots the calculated optical absorption spectrum, indicating transitions of the unpaired electron at O─Si─O bridges to the a‐SiO2 conduction band and an electron from non‐bonding O p orbitals to O─Si─O. The migration pathways and barriers for these oxygen vacancies, which are dependent on material strain,[ 186 ] have also been revealed with DFT,[ 187 ] and ReaxFF,[ 59 , 188 ] potentially explaining the causes of dielectric breakdown in resistive silica devices,[ 187 ] which are promising materials in this area.[ 189 ] DFT calculations also afford studies of complex interfaces for these devices such as SiO2‐TiN, demonstrating incorporation and diffusion of O ions at the grain boundary.[ 190 ]

The DFTB approach, shown to faithfully describe the proton transfer energetics of water in different chemical environments,[ 191 ] offers a computationally efficient method that can attain results on par with DFT methods.[ 192 , 193 ] Recently, DFTB has been employed to investigate proton transfer on quartz surfaces. Having used DFTB to reveal defects at the quartz(101)‐water interface,[ 15 ] Surface defects, which cause the ratio of vicinal to geminal silanols to differ from the pristine quartz surface, have been shown to lead to a marked effect on the range of pKa values calculated.[ 194 ] Interestingly, strong H‐bonding environments do not necessarily yield an acidic surface site, with a wide spectrum of reaction pathways observed on the defective quartz(101) surface for proton transfer from various O sites to water.

Functionalized SNPs: Exploring the Structure, Dynamics, and Potential of Functional Groups for Applications in Technology and Biomedicine

Even for bare SNPs as discussed in the previous section, modelling amorphous systems requires larger system sizes than explicit electronic consideration currently allows due to their inherent heterogeneity. Modification of the surface to impart additional functionality, modelling chemical reactions on the surface or adsorption of biomolecules from solution enhances the complexity of the system in multiples. For one example, functionalization of silica with carboxylic (‐COOH) and amino (‐NH2) groups was simulated using ReaxFF and DFT, with varying surface coverage.[ 195 ] At higher surface coverages as expected, grafted functionalities self‐interact and form a H‐bond network, with increasing density as coverage increases. However, this was done in the absence of solvent, aiming to design a functional material primed for biomolecule adsorption.

The inclusion of a solvent significantly increases the complexity of such systems, particularly for quantum chemical descriptions. For this reason, classical methods are most often chosen for studying functionalization and chemistry with SNP surfaces and will thus be the focus here. Analogous to the importance of the model structure when attempting to simulate amorphous silica, the design of the modified silica surfaces would appear to be a vital step for studying adsorption processes and changes in properties. While this is true, careful design of the modified surface may preclude the additional modeling of the adsorption and equilibration processes of the modification itself. Notably, it was demonstrated that protein interactions with free polyethylene glycol (PEG) chains correlated with experimental adsorption data,[ 196 ] meaning that the properties and behaviors of modified silica surfaces may be evaluated and predicted without explicitly modeling silica modification to obtain the initial structure. Ligand features for protein adhesion resistance of silica were identified using the all‐atom MD models of ligated silica surfaces in solution[ 197 ] and applied machine learning (ML) methods[ 198 ] to demonstrate alternative ways to classify the efficiency of functional ligands for specific applications without resorting to complexities of the all‐atom large‐scale models.

Due to scope and computational expense, it is not common for studies of modified silica to model both the functionalization mechanisms of silica and the resulting modified system properties. It is worth mentioning that such an investigation would likely require multiple simulation methodologies. As we have discussed, variations in simulation methodologies may render such a study less useful, given that one potential may reach a structure via equilibration that another may not, or may treat nonbonded interactions differently, or assign contrasting atomic charges, to name a few. As such, we distinguish subsections here by the type of material used to modify a silica surface rather than the computational approach. Additionally, as mentioned, we do not discuss in detail the applications of functionalized mesoporous silica structures, which exhibit great potential in biomedicine; this would indeed require its own review.[ 199 , 200 , 201 , 202 ]

Contamination Protection Ligands for Antifouling Silica Surfaces

Silica surfaces decorated by chemically attached ligands have demonstrated potential as contamination‐resistant surfaces, for both the carbonaceous and bio‐organic contaminants, making them appropriate candidates for biomedical applications such as biosensing and drug delivery and as industrial coatings. Resolving the molecular origins of contamination resistance and the influence of ligation on the silica‐contaminant interface is challenging with experiments alone. MD simulations have been instrumental in understanding the mechanisms of functionalization and ligation of silica, including how these modifications influence key surface wettability and contaminant adsorption properties.[ 10 , 203 , 204 , 205 , 206 , 207 ] More recently, via a combination of the classical all‐atom MD and dynamic AFM approaches, aqueous interfaces of bare and ligated silicas were studied and considerable differences between the structure of water at the surface for silica modified with hydrophilic glycidoxypropyltrimethoxysilane (GPS), and that of bare silica were observed.[ 154 ] Due to the H‐bonding capability of the GPS ligand, the diffuse layer of water between the closest surface‐associated water and the bulk solvent was not as well defined as a typical hydration layer, but was rather quite mobile and contained occupancy voids (Figure 6). Terminal hydroxyl groups of the GPS ligands associated strongly with water in this diffuse later, meaning these water molecules were not restricted to certain surface sites like for bare silica. The lability of the diffused water layer caused by the short functional ligands was shown by the follow‐up MD studies to correlate with the biocontamination resistance (protein repellence capacity) of the ligated silica surfaces.[ 18 ]

A study employing ReaxFF MD correlated stronger forces in the AFM force profiles to specific H‐bonding between water and surface silanols on bare silica (quartz (100)).[ 208 ] This observation and simulated densities of these regions correlated well with FM‐AFM force profiles in this region. This was further explored by tuning the density of the GPS ligation and demonstrated that regions of the surface with lower GPS coverage exhibited similar behavior to bare silica and would act as adsorption sites for proteins and larger biofouling species.[ 154 ] Subsequent simulations by the same group employed steered MD with the CHARMM36 and CGenFF36 potentials with TIP3P water to model protein (Ubiquitin) adsorption and water contact angle on GPS‐modified silica in all‐atom detail.[ 197 ] The model silica surfaces were modified with hydrophobic CF3‐capped ligands, with both GPS and CF3 and GPS ligands alternating with an NH2 terminated ligand to identify the effects of the ligand functionality and spacing on the surface wettability. While difficult to directly compare the simulation‐predicted contact angle estimates with experiment, qualitatively the experimental trend of enhanced hydrophobicity of the modified silica leading to decreased surface wettability was followed. Interestingly, enhancing the surface hydrophilicity via more hydrophilic ligands did not produce the improved wettability effect, possibly due to the hydrophilic silica surface already maximising the degree of H‐bonding at the interface and the additional entropic penalty due to the hydration of the flexible ligands. Prior simulations of PEGylated silica surfaces showed that enhancing the hydrophilicity of fully hydroxylated silica does not result in any significant difference in wetting behavior,[ 145 ] though with a two‐fold increase in grafting density decreasing the contact angle by 10%.[ 18 ] The simulated contact angles of water droplets on modified silica[ 18 ] compared to those calculated on charge neutral silica[ 5 ] are presented in Figure 13a,b. The contact angle simulated for additional ligated silica surfaces[ 197 ] is shown in Figure 13c. Interestingly, this contact angle decrease was not observed for MPOX (Poly(2‐methyl‐2‐oxazoline))‐modified silica. It was demonstrated that modification of silica with ligands of alternating length disrupted protein‐ligand H‐bonding thus hindering protein adsorption (biofouling). This agrees with simulations of modified silica surfaces by the same group,[ 204 ] though it depends on the structure of the protein also and whether ligand patterning of modified surfaces can be accommodated. An increase in protein detachment events overall was observed for the GPS:NH2‐modified silica and at certain release points for GPS:CF3 compared to a surface modified solely with GPS, though the hydrophobic regions of the surface were prone to biofouling. Comparing PEG‐, PEO (polyethylene oxide)‐, and POX (poly(2‐oxazoline))‐modified silica surfaces with varying grafting densities (CGenFF with INTERFACE and TIP3P), the greatest antifouling efficacy came from lower grafting densities of MPOX that balance surface hydrophilicity, heterogeneity, and enhanced H‐bonding between ligands and water at the interface.[ 18 ] Simulations of PEGylated silica indicated that with higher grafting densities, there was an opportunity to trap water molecules near the interface.[ 209 ] The all‐atom modelling demonstrated that as the adsorbing protein molecule approaches the ligated surface, the PEG chains compress and release this water; inducing a thermodynamic penalty for adsorption. Modified surfaces were broadly classified by the authors as dual action, single action or no action, depending on whether they repelled proteins based on the hydration and/or steric exclusion mechanisms. It's clear from this suite of investigations into antifouling properties of ligated silica that, while challenging to characterize with classical FF methods, entropic barriers imparted by the dynamics and mobility of surface ligands would contribute to contamination resistance and should be further explored in the design of contamination resistant functional surfaces.

Peptide Adsorption and Functionalization of Silica Surfaces for Biomedical Applications

The use of silica in applications of biomedicine has long been explored, originating from the analytical chemistry‐based peptide/protein separation techniques such as reverse‐phase HPLC (RP‐HPLC) where the n‐alkyl ligand functionalized silica surfaces serve as stationary phases for peptide purification. The RP‐HPLC relevant ligand silica surfaces were modeled by classical MD from mid 1990s,[ 205 , 206 ] followed by simulations looking at the interaction of amino acids and small peptides with silica surface groups and ligands.[ 91 , 207 , 210 , 211 , 212 , 213 ] Amino acid‐silica adsorption has also been the focus of the development of new FFs aimed at reproducing ab initio calculations.[ 214 ] Common drugs such as aspirin initially served as model compounds to study silica‐biomolecule interactions,[ 215 ] though a wide range of peptides and proteins have since been investigated, and in general the simulation of inorganic‐bioorganic interfaces is developing and evolving still as a valuable tool.[ 216 ] An important, combined experimental and theoretical study investigated the adsorption of a cationic and noncationic peptides on fully and partially hydroxylated silica surfaces.[ 68 ] They used MD simulations employing PCFF‐SILICA, their FF for silica interfaces in water parameterized according to experimental measurements. They demonstrated formation of ion pairs of peptide ammonium groups and ionized silanols and the capacity for H‐bonding with peptide polar residues. This study highlighted several peptide residues important for silica adsorption. We refer the reader to an excellent review for discussions of this work and those preceding it that studied peptide and amino acid adsorption to silica,[ 6 ] while we focus primarily on studies in the last decade.

Studying molecules such as peptides that exhibit a large conformational space requires appropriate exploration of this space to make comments on favorable adsorption mechanisms. Enhanced sampling methods have demonstrated enormous potential for elucidating physicochemical phenomena for large systems.[ 217 ] One such method that has been effective is replica‐exchange MD (REMD), and has been used to study adsorption and tune FF parameters for peptide‐silica interactions.[ 218 ] Here, MD simulations of the same system for given temperature are simulated in parallel using different Hamiltonians, with atomic coordinates exchanged as potential energies of the replicas overlap, creating a generalized ensemble.[ 217 ] The first REMD study of peptide‐silica binding, employing the CHARMM forcefield and TIP3P, determined that peptide flexibility plays a key role in binding behavior on quartz surfaces.[ 219 ]

Peptide and protein adsorption on silica and other biomedically attractive nanomaterials such as Au and Ag, has attracted widespread research attention for decades, and we discuss key examples where silica and SNPs are the focus. An exciting recent example is SNPs facilitating the stacking of 2D peptoid nanosheets functionalized by proteins.[ 220 ] However, we refer the reader to comprehensive reviews on the topic for further reading.[ 221 , 222 ] Employing REMD allowed for explicit representations of these large biomolecular adsorption systems on quartz that were consistent with experiment.[ 222 ] The use of circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy is often applied to reveal polypeptide secondary structure,[ 223 ] though it can be difficult to resolve all conformations for small peptides as they interact with NPs in solution. REMD simulations combined with metadynamics[ 224 ] allowed for the prediction of changes in secondary structure upon silica adsorption to be modeled and the full CD spectra to be resolved, in agreement with experiment (Figure 14a). REMD simulations have also assisted in estimating the energetics of polypeptide adsorption on amorphous silica, combined with AFM‐based force spectroscopy.[ 225 ] The adsorption of cell‐penetrating peptides (CPPs) onto Q3 amorphous silica surfaces was performed using the silica INTERFACE FF coupled with a well‐tempered (WT) metadynamics scheme.[ 226 ] Figure 14b shows adsorption of the MAP peptide (an amphipathic chemically synthesized peptide made of alanines, leucines, and lysines) to various silica surfaces. The degree of ionization of the silica surface played a key role in the binding affinity of peptides to the surface, as did the CPP sequence. Cationic peptide residues and N‐terminals interacted strongly with the negative silica surfaces. In addition to non‐covalent interactions contributing to the binding affinity, structural factors such as peptide conformational flexibility and the structure of the water at the interface played roles. Specifically, the C‐terminal residues drove the adsorption mechanism for fully protonated silica surfaces, while N‐terminal residues are the first to interact for deprotonated silica. As shown in Figure 14b, the MAP peptide adopts a parallel conformational state to silica when fully adsorbed. Other peptides examined, such as CADY, performed conformational twists to maximize interactions between the charged C‐terminal residues and the negatively charged silica. The TAT peptide preferred to interact with water rather than silica, unless the surface was largely deprotonated, where the interaction was stabilized by ARG residues.

Other enhanced sampling methods have been applied to studying peptide adsorption to silica such as PTMetaD‐WT[ 227 ] for R5 (19‐residue segment of the native silaffin peptide), demonstrating pH dependency on the binding behavior.[ 228 ] Umbrella sampling has been employed for studying alanine and arginine binding, indicating that electrostatic interactions were dominant, but the strength was more dependent on the negative repulsive group rather than the positive.[ 229 ] The WT ensemble[ 230 ] was also used to obtain a diverse set of structures for classical MD simulations of silica‐peptide intercalation.[ 21 ] Additionally, MD simulations augmented with WT metadynamics studied adsorption of DDD and DSS peptides as well as those modified with phosphorylated serine residues in line with mineral precipitating proteins.[ 231 ] Electrostatic interactions were observed to be a strong driving force for adsorption, mediated by the ions at the interface (Na+ adsorbed at negative silanol groups. The arrangement of the pS residues in the peptide also influenced binding affinity.

Enhanced sampling methods have greatly improved the capability of MD simulations to model peptide‐surface interactions and dynamics,[ 217 ] and should play a role in modeling these systems going forward.[ 232 ] Recently employed parallel bias metadynamics simulations demonstrated SNP nucleation and growth as triggered by self‐assembled peptoids that adhered strongly to the silica surface via amino groups.[ 233 ] Prior classical MD simulations of lysozyme and chymotrypsin proteins on amorphous silica have demonstrated favorable adsorption motifs[ 20 ] though can only sample a limited time frame and conformational space without enhanced sampling. Still, with large biomolecule systems such as these proteins, a wealth of information can be obtained from the simulations regarding which protein residues interact with the surface, and the nature of the interacting protein itself. Figure 15a shows binding motifs of lysozyme adsorbed on amorphous silica, indicating specific peptide binding motifs. Regions of water density and binding residues are distinguished by charge and polarity. Coupling MD simulations with replica exchange solute tempering (REST) and metadynamics allowed the same group to examine structural changes of chymotrypsin on silica. The enzyme demonstrated tertiary structure perturbations as aromatic moieties interact with silica, demonstrated in Figure 15b, though even with enhanced sampling, only a portion of the protein was sampled.[ 234 ] Sampling of the true conformational space remains a great challenge for larger biomolecules at surfaces.

Studying the interactions of CPPs with SNPs is difficult experimentally due to the peptide namesake, as it crosses the cellular membrane. MD simulations performed using the CHARMM36 FF for the peptides and the INTERFACE FF for silica, allowed for adsorption mechanisms of the peptides on Q3 silica as a function of the surface ionization.[ 226 ] The cationic peptides interacted strongly with the dehydrogenated silanol groups on the surface in addition to H‐bonding. For negatively charged peptides, residues interacted with hydroxylated silica via the OH‐adsorbed Na+ ions as revealed by MD simulations.[ 231 ] This phenomenon has also been observed recently at pH 9 with negatively charged peptide residues attracted to surface‐bound Na+ ions and positively charged residues attracted to the quartz surface.[ 235 ] These simulations focus on the use of peptides as potential flotation collectors. Repulsive self‐interactions in the peptides improved binding propensity to the quartz surface.

Silica parameters interfaced with CHARMM investigated the adsorption of a neutral, cationic, or anionic peptide as a function of pH and particle size, as approximated by Q3 and Q2/Q3 surfaces with varying degrees of silanol ionization and adsorbed Na+ concentration.[ 236 ] Optimal conformations of the peptides on the surface and their adsorption energies were reported, with results largely in alignment with experimental adsorption isotherms. The conformations of three adsorbing peptides are presented in Figure 16a, with key peptide‐surface interactions circled. Electrostatic and H‐bonding interactions contributed to selective peptide‐silica binding over hydrophobic reactions, which became significant as the surface became more hydroxylated/pH became more acidic. With increasing surface ionization/pH, the positively charged lysine residues and the N‐terminals were most strongly associated with the surface. Overall, the sequence similarity between peptides that bind to silica at pH 3 compared to pH 7 is less than 10%.[ 237 ] Notably, Na+ ions at the interface organize to accommodate the adsorbing peptide. Umbrella sampling employed with steered MD simulations by[ 238 ] also showed that the degree of silanol ionization/pH influenced the adhesion of positive peptide residues. At low ionization, the adhesion energies had the trend of arginine > histidine > lysine, though this was lysine > arginine > histidine for increased surface ionization.[ 238 ] Even for small peptide sequences, the conformational space is expansive, and many peptide‐surface interactions are possible. The implementation of enhanced sampling methods can provide great insight into specific interactions that may be manipulated for tailored applications of functionalized silica.

Positively charged peptide sequences designed to bind with silica have recently been termed silica binding peptides (SBPs). One such example is polyhistidine, which binds strongly to negative charged silica surface groups.[ 240 ] Additionally, H‐bonding and hydrophobic interactions play roles in histidine‐silanol binding.[ 239 , 241 ] Figure 16b shows binding mechanisms between cationic, neutral, and anionic peptides with SNPs for neutral and acidic pH conditions, and varying concentration of peptide in solution. These binding mechanisms range from H‐bonding, van der Waals interactions, and electrostatic interactions for cationic peptides for silica at pH 7.4, and only H‐bonding and van der Waals contributions at pH 3. DFT calculations have also demonstrated the ability for lysine to bind strongly to silanol groups via H‐bonding with side‐chain amine groups.[ 242 ]

Recent coarse‐grain simulations demonstrate that the SBP‐silica interaction is significantly influenced by the orientation of the peptide amino acids, tuning the magnitude of the electrostatic and H‐bonding interactions.[ 19 ] The interaction was also dependent on the pH and the ionic concentration. Coarse grain simulations have also been utilized to model protein clusters[ 243 ] and DNA on silica, with atomistic simulations for biomolecules of this size computationally prohibitive. More work is needed in this area to gain a better understanding of the atomic mechanisms of SBP binding, and the interactions of biomolecules with silica in general. It has been highlighted throughout this review, and in past works,[ 5 , 236 ] how important appropriate selection of a surface model is even prior to sampling conformational space of the interacting (adsorbing) species. There still exist many challenges in appropriately modeling peptide‐surface interfaces,[ 221 , 232 , 244 ] as we discuss further in Section 4.

Effect of Ions on Silica Surface Functionality and Interactions

It's important to note that in many potential applications thus far discussed for silica, e.g., in biomedicine or as surface coatings, the surface‐solution system will also contain salts – free‐flowing ions – the effects of which play a key role in influencing silica functionality and interactions. It's well understood that ions affect the structure of water in solution.[ 245 ] There have been many studies both experimental and theoretical into how ions affect the structure of water at surfaces, including silica, and it's important that these methods are utilized in tandem.[ 246 ] As discussed in this review, simulation methodologies differ in how they treat atomic charges and how these interact, giving rise to transferability issues between bulk and surface investigations, notably, the assignment of partial charges for a FF. This means that during MD simulations, the charge of a species will not change as it is redistributed to an interface. Nonetheless, simulations have provided atomic‐scale mechanisms for ion‐silica adsorption that experiments could not,[ 247 ] predictive models for surface potentials,[ 248 ] and made observations that align with experiment.

Figure 17a shows Na+ cations in water and in various inner‐sphere complexes with silica from ReaxFF MD simulations.[ 249 ] Five complexes were identified, with Na+ cations coordinating bridging oxygen atoms to have the longest residence time, though formed only at concentrations > 0.5 m.[ 249 ] PBE0 DFT MD simulations demonstrated that for monovalent cations, only Li+ formed an inner‐sphere complex with H2O at the quartz‐water interface, at deprotonated silanols.[ 250 ] Na+, K+, and Rb+ formed outer‐sphere complexes with both deprotonated and protonated silanols. The geometry‐optimized ions close to the quartz interface are shown in Figure 17b. Calculation of the ion exchange energy allowed for comparison with in situ flow microcalorimetry experiments, with resulting trends in agreement, though absolute energy values higher in magnitude. Calculated coordination numbers also agreed well with experimental data though less accurately for the larger ions with looser hydration shells such as K++ and Rb+.

With the number and spatial distribution of negatively charged surface groups (i.e., at different pH) influencing water structure and the adsorption of ions, FFs such as ClayFF were modified to better model the surface charges.[ 251 ] The adsorption of Na+, Rb+, and Sr2+ ions on quartz (101) surfaces as a function of pH showed inner‐ and outer‐sphere complexing between Na+ and silanol and closer ion adsorption to the surface compared to the larger ions, contrasting earlier experimental results based on material dielectric properties.[ 252 ] In the absence of any adsorbing species, the adsorption density of cations on quartz increases with ion size, regardless of valency.[ 253 ] Modified ClayFF[ 254 ] simulations demonstrated the capacity of monovalent cations to orient water molecules and unprotonated surface groups proceeded in the order Li+ > Na+ > K+ > Rb+ > Cs+ >> water. The same trend was followed for neutralization of the surface, occurring ≈2 nm from the zero plane, as plotted in Figure 18a. Lateral density profiles for the monovalent cations on silica are shown in Figure 18b. The first three adsorption peaks were analyzed for distances 0–0.362, 0.362–0.430, and 0.430–0.642 nm. For the first peak, there are two distinct adsorption sites for Na+. These are for a Na+ cation directly above an inner silanol oxygen and for a bidentate site with Na+ cations equally distributed between inner and outer silanol oxygens. Interestingly, there was a preference for protonated silanols over deprotonated silanols, attributed to the higher solvation of deprotonated silanols. For Rb+ cations there existed a more continuous distribution of adsorption sites, with bidentate and tridentate sites, while Sr2+ cations strongly preferred monodentate sites on outer deprotonated silanols. DFT calculations of monovalent cation exchange with Li+ at the silica‐water interface disagree with these simulations however, showing ions preferring to reside in outer‐sphere orientations.[ 250 ] This phenomenon is notably concentration‐dependent as a highly negative surface will not necessarily be compensated by a strong inner sphere of Na+ as the cations will repel each other, leading to a more condensed layer farther from the surface, as observed by MD simulations and second harmonic scattering measurements.[ 255 ] Unmodified ClayFF was used to perform simulations showing K+ adsorb to the ionized silanol sites with OH− accumulating in the second near‐surface layer.[ 12 ] The modified ClayFF[ 254 ] employed on (101) quartz showed that the diffusion of water molecules decreases with increasing surface charge.[ 256 ] Ions behaved similarly in that their axial diffusion decreased with increasing surface charge. Additionally, ions diffused slower at the interface than the bulk and slower for low concentrations. The diffusion of water increased with increasing ionic radii (Rb+ > K+ > Na+). Later MD simulations revealed that as temperature is increased from 298 to 373 K, Na+ adsorbs less to the interface, resulting in less effective surface charge screening and a larger zeta potential.[ 257 ] MD simulations employing the CHARMM27 FF of NaCl and CsCl on silica showed that Na+ ions adsorb closer than Cs+ and without their hydration shells.[ 16 ] Conversely, Cs+ ions retained their hydration shells as the H in H2O was observed to be closer to the surface, as indicated by the density profiles in Figure 18c. The distribution of the Cl− counterion also differs between ions, with density depleted for Cs+ compared to Na+.

Importantly, the adsorption of ions reorientates water molecules at the interface. The degree of this reorientation is ion‐dependent while the extent of the adjacent, diffuse layer of water is independent of the cation that screens the charge, and appears more reliant on the ion concentration and the distribution of charge on the surface.[ 16 ] MD simulations of the (0001) quartz‐electrolyte (NaCl, KCl, NaBr, KBr) interfaces for 2 ≤ pH ≤ 9 demonstrated the influence of ions on the structure of water.[ 262 ] Irrespective of pH, water formed three distinct layers on (0001) quartz. As previously observed, the closest layer of water is strongly ordered and H‐bonded, particularly for low pH/fully hydroxylated silica surface. Notably, the pH and the proportion of ionized silanol sites play roles in the behavior of the ions. At pH = 6, MD simulations showed that the Na+ cations associate closer than K+, with anions behaving similarly, though becoming pronounced near the surface in this regime. At pH = 9, orientation of water molecules is heavily influenced by K+ rather than Na+. Smaller anions were observed to conceal the first cation layer, increasing the calculated zeta potential. PBE DFT MD simulations showed that the classifications of “structure‐making” and “structure‐breaking” should not be applied as a complex relationship exists between the influence of ions on the water structure and the specific nature of the surface and the proximity to the surface.[ 261 ] Representative surface configurations observed are presented in Figure 19d. Cations that may adsorbed strongly to a charged interface and reorient water molecules there, disrupt water structure in solution away from the interface. For hydroxylated surfaces, anions can also adsorb at the interface, with a minor effect on water structure, though cations and anions adsorbing together as a pair enhance the freedom of water at the interface. The origin of the reorientation of water at the interface for hydroxylated silica surfaces is that both cations and anions adsorbing to the surface can fulfill their hydration shells with surface silanols.[ 263 ] This breaks silanol‐water H‐bonds, thus influencing water capacity to H‐bond with itself within the interfacial layer.[ 264 ] This phenomenon is similar to that which gives rise to hydrophobic patches of silica.[ 172 ]

The presence of ions at the silica‐water interface, through the adsorption of ions at the interface to the reorientation of water molecules, affects how potential modifiers of the silica surface will interact and behave. The ClayFF, AMBER 99 sb, and SPC/E FFs were applied to a quartz‐saltwater system to examine polyacrylamide adsorption behavior to the silica surface with ions present.[ 265 ] The adsorption of cations onto the quartz surface directly correlated with the Hofmeister series of ions while adsorption of monovalent cations onto the partially hydrolyzed polyacrylamide followed an inverse Hofmeister series. Monovalent ions better neutralized the surface charge and that of the polymer, leading to less polymer‐silica interaction, while some salt combinations with larger ions increased the adsorption contact between polymer and silica. These results and the principles of how ions affect the silica‐water interface are important considerations for facilitating silica functionalization in targeting specific interactions for diverse applications.

Interaction of Silica Surfaces with Surfactants

Whether used in froth flotation, oil recovery, or biotechnology, the interaction of mineral (i.e., silica) surfaces with surfactants is of vital significance. Simulations investigate individual adsorption and interaction mechanisms in silica‐surfactant systems that shed light on tuning the surfactant chemistry and conditions of operation. The adsorption mechanisms in versatile and widely used industrial processes such as flotation still lack understanding,[ 266 ] but have been discussed in a comprehensive prior review.[ 267 ] MD simulations employing silica INTERFACE FF, OPLS and SPC/E for silica, cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) and water respectively, studied the CTAB‐silica system.[ 268 ] The cationic headgroup was electrostatically attracted to the negative silica surface while the surfactant tail displaced weakly adsorbed interfacial water. Additionally, the balance of CTAB surface coverage via electrostatic attraction and the hydrophobic aggregation of surfactant tails can be tuned by surfactant concentration. Density profiles for key atom types, the nitrogen headgroup (N), the tail‐end methyl group (CH3), and the water oxygen atoms (Ow) are plotted and the morphology of CTAB aggregates at varying CTAB concentrations are shown in Figure 20a. As the number of adsorbed surfactants (Ns) increases, indicating the saturation of the surface by headgroup adsorption. The structural change in the surfactants as the concentration increases is indicated by the changes in the CH3 peak as Ns increases above 96, with hemimicelles forming at Ns = 196 and micelles forming at Ns = 360. Later MD simulations showed the mechanism of hydrophobic particle collection from the silica surface using CTAB molecules.[ 269 ] The formation of a water channel between the surfactants and the silica surface was a key requirement for particle detachment, with water able to diffuse to the oil‐water interface to form H‐bonds at the interface, reorienting the alkanes in the hydrophobic particle to be perpendicular to the surface rather than parallel. The silica surface swells and forms a surface gel layer. During the motion of the water‐oil‐solid contact line (Figure 20b), oil molecules are replaced by H2O and the contact line shrinks.

MD simulations based on the ClayFF, CVFF, and SPC FFs examined the adsorption of amine‐ and amide‐based mineral flotation collectors on quartz surfaces in aqueous solution. Simulations allowed the varying selectivity for collector‐quartz adsorption in iron ore reverse flotation to be explained via the adsorption mechanisms and the competition between H‐bonding and alkyl chain repulsion as a function of surface silanol protonation.[ 270 ] The nature of the surfactant structure matters for the adsorption onto silica. The ClayFF and Dreiding FFs[ 271 ] were coupled to study amine surfactant adsorption, with results showing electrostatics predominantly determines adsorption followed by H‐bonding and dispersion interactions. As a function of pH, the results aligned well with experimental flotation recoveries. Notably, an amphoteric surfactant, LAO, was predicted to perform well across a range of pH values though it has not been utilized in quartz flotation applications. MD simulations aimed at modeling flotation,[ 272 ] showed that increased ionic strength led to enhanced adsorption of anionic polyelectrolyte on quartz. However, with polyelectrolyte collapse this limited bridging capacity diminished hence particle aggregation occurred.

Silica‐Polymer Interfaces

SNPs have been modified extensively with polymers to form nanocomposites with tunable properties,[ 273 ] however the mechanisms behind the relationships between polymer structure, surface chemistry, and thermomechanical properties of the composite are not well understood.[ 274 , 275 , 276 ] Due to the attractive properties of such composite materials however, the adhesion of polymers to silica has attracted extensive research effort due to the potential for industrial coatings and contamination‐resistant films.[ 277 , 278 , 279 ] Epoxy‐silica interactions have thus been investigated given epoxy's wide use in the coating industry.[ 280 ] While the silica‐epoxy adhesion is strong, it is susceptible to interfacial water, as demonstrated by several MD studies. Figure 21c shows structures for detachment simulations for an epoxy from the silica surface in dry and wet conditions.[ 281 , 282 ] though the design of such systems is computationally complex and the influence of silica surface heterogeneity was not fully explored. Epoxy‐modified silica was also studied using a combined DFT‐ReaxFF methodology to build the atomic models.[ 283 ] It was found that the silica surface roughness modulated the friction and shear properties of the grafted epoxy, with stronger frictional pressure for rougher surfaces.

Several other polymers have been studied via theoretical methods and have provided insight into binding mechanisms that would be difficult to elucidate experimentally. MD simulations studied poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) chains (with CGenFF) between amorphous silica slabs (INTERFACE FF). An increase in the Tg of the PVA due to strong H‐bonding interactions with the hydroxylated silica, surface, reaching a Tg maximum at 75% surface hydroxylation was also demonstrated.[ 274 ] Following this, toward full surface hydroxylation, competition between the PVA‐silanol and silanol‐silanol interactions reduce the H‐bond network strength. Steered MD simulations employing ReaxFF have demonstrated the strong binding of polyimides to the silica surface, and provided insight into failure mechanisms for polyimide‐silica films.[ 284 ] The same group used an INTERFACE‐CVFF FF to demonstrate the stronger affinity of polyimide monomers for hydroxylated silica surfaces compared to dehydroxylated surfaces.[ 285 ] Additionally, it appeared that polyimides with higher O density would provide stronger binding to the silica, with the carbon backbone and surface silanols responsible for the adhesion. Coarse‐grained approaches have also been used to examine larger polymer‐silica model systems, and have been useful in this area.[ 287 ] A multiscale approach to build coarse‐grained potentials from atomistic simulations has been developed, and is able to reproduce thermomechanical and morphological properties of polymer melts.[ 288 ] Poly(vinyl butyral‐co‐vinyl) (PVB) melts (random‐blocky copolymers) in contact with amorphous silica were studied with coarse‐grained MD with models derived from both atomistic MD and macroscopic data.[ 289 ] Increased vinyl alcohol content led to a stronger adhesion, though the arrangement of the blocks, the blockiness, played a significant role in the adhesion strength. The heterogeneity of amorphous silica allowed for the blocks of the polymer to adsorb to their preferred sites, however unexpectedly the random copolymers with a high degree of blockiness adsorbed more strongly than simply deblock copolymers. Silica Surface Interactions with Carbonaceous Materials

Silica‐based coatings have long been considered for protection of underlying (polymer/paint) surfaces from carbonaceous contamination, including graphitic and amorphous carbon‐based particle contaminants originating from atmospheric pollution.[ 279 ] The inherent hydrophilicity and ease of functionalization of silica surfaces provided opportunities to use classical MD simulations to explore carbon‐resistant surfaces mimicking nature's lotus leaf effect. As such, classical MD simulations have enhanced the understanding of the fundamental aspects of remarkable contamination resistance exhibited by bare silica and silica ligated with PEG and POX.[ 17 , 209 , 278 , 290 , 291 , 292 ] Figure 22 highlights MD simulation results showing how silica functionalization modifies the adhesion of carbonaceous materials, and the key relationship between the nature of ligand modification and the entropic penalties of adsorption.[ 17 ] These simulations provided invaluable atomistic insight into the application of polymer coatings on silica for carbon repellence, still considered the gold standard for protection from carbon adhesion today. Additionally, and importantly, they contributed significantly to the current understanding of entropic penalties of adhesion for ligated surfaces, as discussed in Section 3.2.1.

Benzene adsorbed to an amorphous, hydroxylated silica surface was modeled using INTERFACE FF[ 236 ] and TIP4P‐Ew.[ 149 ] The pure silica surface as expected induced changes in the three‐body angle distribution of water molecules compared to bulk water, while the benzene‐modified surface increased tetrahedral ordering, closer to that of bulk water.[ 296 ] With the hydrophobic modification, the interfacial water molecules had reduced configurational entropy, similar to that observed on hydrophobic polymer surfaces.[ 297 ] This change in water mobility differs from the change brought on by a scarcity of surface silanols, indicating that a structurally heterogenous surface affects hydration dynamics. The benzene modification led to local ordering of interfacial water. Amorphous SNPs functionalized by CH3 were modeled as hydrate inhibitors at the water‐oil interface.[ 298 ] These hydrophobic NPs did not act as nucleation sites for hydration but shifted the nucleation position toward the interface. The NPs reduced the diffusivity of water thus forming semi‐ordered layer/s that could seed the formation of water‐oil hydrates. However, the authors noted that the simulations could not account for nucleation sites being inaccessible after the nanoparticle‐covered area expands, leading to the likelihood that experiments would yield different results.

In this review, we have covered a range of simulation methodologies and their application to studying properties of bare and functionalized silica surfaces and SNPs. FF methods have yielded significant insight into silica‐based systems, as have ab initio calculations themselves, providing fundamental information unattainable by experiments alone. Overall, experimental results have been supported, mechanisms have been elucidated to explain puzzling observations, and predictions have been made that may guide functional applications in the future. Conversely, theoretical investigations alone could not have landed the field where it is today, with spatial and temporal scales of many physical phenomena not yet achievable computationally, qualitative disagreement on physicochemical trends, and orders of magnitude discrepancies in quantitative measures between methods. It is vital that theoretical investigations remain systematic, use accurate models and employ methods that sufficiently describe the system and explore phenomena at the relevant timescales. Enhanced sampling methods have enabled investigation into adsorption and (self) assembly processes of biomolecules such as peptides and proteins, and coarse‐graining has allowed even larger systems to be studied. Limitations of fully atomistic, unbiased simulations revolve around accessibility of physically relevant spatial and temporal scales. However, advances in computing architecture and parallelization may prime these methods to further elucidate and predict structure‐property relationships on the nanoscale, as well as model assembly and aggregation mechanisms in addition to adsorption. By the same token, advances in high‐performance computing promote the use of QM‐based approaches to more precisely model specific adsorption chemistries and quantify enthalpic and entropic reaction barriers.

Multiscale approaches provide an avenue for a comprehensive description of SNP systems, whether by application of different levels of theory for kinetics and thermodynamics or by hybrid QM/MM methods. The potential of multiscale methods in the simulation of SNPs has not been fully realized, yet promising studies have emerged. Qi et al. incorporate atomistic MD representations of silica with macroscopic DLVO forces and experimental results.[ 233 , 299 ] They modeled the self‐assembly of SNPs with adsorbed protein containing cationic peptides on either end. The behavior of this system quantitatively agreed with experimental results, such that pH‐dependent and reversible assembly was observed, while considering the influence of particle curvature and the directionality of protein interactions on a scale of 100 nm. While the observed process and simulation conditions were strictly regulated and engineered, it is an exciting step in developing multiscale approaches that can simulate increasingly scalable and experimentally matched systems. With the promise of SNPs in drug delivery and biomedicine, multiscale simulation methods offer routes to simulate biocompatibility with large protein and membrane structures.

For FF methods thus far employed to study silica surfaces and SNPs, we observe a clear and on‐going trend of FF reparameterization with the goal of better representing the fundamental details of a specific system of focus, not always necessarily making it transferrable and more universally applicable. It is unclear still whether the way forward is by parameterizing FF functions with experimental data, by high‐order ab initio calculations, or both. Today there is no truly universal FF available to accurately study all aspects of the silica‐water system, or any system for that matter, which makes some computational comparisons difficult. More consistent comparative studies may yield insight into which methods are most suitable for systems extending beyond the silica‐water interface. Benchmarking of the FF methods discussed here and examining differences in evaluating experimentally observed phenomena would be beneficial for future investigators. This is particularly pertinent for using FF methods as predictive tools. If it is clear a certain method accurately reproduces ligand‐adsorption behavior for example, simulations can be utilized as a high throughput screening technique, with reliable predictive capability for guiding future experiments.[ 300 ] Alternatively, a range of environmental conditions such as temperature, pH, and concentration can be explored more systematically. A recent example demonstrates a model that rapidly estimates the binding free energy of peptides to silica, though can be extended to any solid surface.[ 301 ] The model displayed remarkable accuracy and robustness, showing that polypeptide binding can be estimated from the free energy contribution of each residue, reducing the computational cost to 3% of similar studies using enhanced sampling.

In recent years, there has also been significant research effort to increase the accuracy and computational efficiency of FF methods by developing machine‐learning‐based interatomic potentials. This moves away from continued reparameterization and extension of the models toward a more flexible form that can be trained by large sets of systematic DFT data. Early machine‐learning potentials in the forms of a neural network[ 302 ] and a Gaussian approximation[ 88 ] have been applied to silicon. More recent efforts were able to inexpensively provide accurate structural and energetic information on phases of amorphous silicon[ 139 , 303 ] and even reveal structural transformations previously beyond the reach of computational modeling techniques due to the speed‐up and scaling made possible by ML.[ 304 ] There are many examples of these machine‐learning interatomic potentials successfully navigating the materials science and chemistry landscape,[ 305 , 306 , 307 , 308 , 309 ] and it is likely that the computer simulation of silica will be the subject of future machine‐learning potentials. However, care is required when selecting one for use as many factors including their training, parameters, functional form, descriptors and more can influence their accuracy and efficiency in modeling a specific system.[ 310 , 311 , 312 ] ML has also started and will continue to be used for direct prediction of properties of silica‐based materials, with accumulation of sufficient datasets related to properties of interest. For example, ML has been shown to enhance the accuracy in predicting chemistries of biocontamination protective ligands for silica (and other) surfaces using combined experimental and theoretically (MD) developed datasets and descriptors, relative to empirical models.[ 198 ]

We concede the future direction of simulating SNPs should focus on more than the method itself. As evident in this review, SNPs have historically been approximated by a silica surface slab in most previous and current molecular models. Yet, experimental results have demonstrated that the extent of SNP functionalization and the resultant properties depend on the particle size.[ 313 , 314 ] A logical step forward is the simulation of curved surfaces, i.e., actual SNPs. The consideration of curvature inherently adds additional layers of complexity to amorphous, heterogenous silica surfaces. This first requires an adequate atomically resolved simulation model including the geometries (diameter/curvature) of the SNPs themselves and representative surface silanol/charge/ligand distributions. Additionally, FF methods such as those discussed here, have been trained and tested on silica surfaces, thus may require reparameterization to account for curvature and atomic surface roughness and heterogeneity. However, the nature of the water‐silica interface for curved surfaces is fundamental for understanding the behavior of ions, ligands and adsorbed species in SNP systems leading to a better understanding of larger‐scale physical and chemical processes such as aggregation, deposition, adhesion, and repulsion/rejection. Studying curved surfaces could also provide further insight into entropic effects influencing adsorption/desorption of species. Ultimately, more closely matching the simulated form of silica, a SNP, to that which is used in experiment, further bolsters to ability of simulations to guide experimental design and correlate with observations. This is essential for designing and engineering SNP properties for various applications.

It is also important to stress the need for coupling of simulation results that focus on fundamental interactions and interfacial mechanisms with the performance of the modeled materials in real applications. A more synergistic relationship between those that examine underlying mechanisms and those that utilize SNPs in self‐assembly, in biomedicine, in surface coatings, or in any application is crucial for the future successful exploitation of the silica‐based particles in science and industry.

We have highlighted here our perspective on key successes, challenges, and future directions for theoretically simulating SNP properties and behavior for a variety of practical applications in biomedicine and industry. We anticipate theoretical simulation of SNPs will continue to evolve and be utilized to enhance our understanding of these important materials. It is crucial that collaborations between theoretical and experimental researchers continue to grow so that their potential can be fully realized.
