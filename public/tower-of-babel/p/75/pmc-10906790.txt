Influence of implant design and length on stress distribution in immediately loaded implants in posterior maxilla – A two-dimensional finite element analysis
Authors: Rika Singh, Shrikar R. Desai, R. G. Shiva Manjunath
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim:The aim of this two dimensional (2D) finite element analysis study was to evaluate the influence of implant design (step and tapered) and length on stress distribution at bone implant interface, when placed in maxillary posterior bone under immediate loading protocol.Materials and Methods:2D finite element models were developed to simulate the two types of implant designs, i.e., Tapered and step implants of 3.75-mm – Diameter and 6 and 10 mm lengths, respectively. Maxillary posterior bone quality (D4 bone) was simulated with a very thin cortical bone (0.5 mm) for the placement of implants. The respective material properties were assigned. The implant designs incorporated microthreads at the crestal part and rest of the implant body incorporated Acme threads. Forces of 100 Newtons were applied vertically and in oblique direction (at an angle of 45°) to long axis of the implants. Total deformation (micromovements), stresses and strains were evaluated at the bone implant interface.Results:The results of micromovements, stresses and strains were found to be lower for long implants as compared to short implants. Also, all the values were found to be higher for oblique loads as compared to vertical loads. The von mises stress values were highest for 6 mm step implants and lowest for 10 mm step implants.Conclusion:In the presence of low bone density with optimal bone height, standard diameter long step implants can be used. Incorporation of microthreads at the crestal portion and acme threads for body portion of the implant presents a good option to be used under immediate loading protocol.

INTRODUCTION

The tooth implant fixture functions as a device that bears the masticatory loads and transfers it to the surrounding peri-implant bone. According to the old school of thought, osseointegration at implant bone interface can be obtained by submerging it during the healing phase. This was also thought to be necessary to prevent the dental implant from infection and also gingival epithelium downgrowth.[1] The micromovements (if in excess) occurring at the bone implant interface during the healing phase, could lead to the formation of fibrous tissue at the interface rather than osseointegration and can ultimately lead to implant failure.[2]

For the benefit of the patient, the delay between the surgical phase and the prosthetic phase should be minimized. Thus the concept of immediate functional loading of dental implants came into consideration.[3] There is significantly shortened time lapse between implant surgery and prosthetic rehabilitation following immediate loading of implants. Also the patients do not undergo the emotional and functional stress of being edentulous. Successful osseointegration can be achieved for immediately loaded implants if micromovement at the bone-implant interphase are kept minimal.[4]

Both quality and quantity of the bone is an important aspect to be considered while placing dental implants. The type of bone determines the implant selection, its primary stability and the loading time.[5] The classification scheme as given by Lekholm and Zarb divides the bone quality into four groups and has been accepted by many clinicians and investigators.[6] Porous and poor quality bone leads to more failure rates of dental implants as compared to dense bone quality. Jaffin and Berman reported that 55% of all failures occurred in D4 bone, with an overall 35% failure.[7]

It has been reported that the bone quality also affects the choice of implant length. For good density bone, diameter and length of implants are not such significant factors to be considered. However with poor bone quality situations, optimal choice of implant length as well as geometry becomes an important factor. Inadequate bone volumes or localized anatomical limitations led to the use of short and/or narrow implants.[8] The studies are controversial regarding the choice of implant length. While some studies state that the use of short implants leads to increased failure rates others say that implant length does not significantly affects its survival rates.[91011] However in the presence of poor bone quality, avoiding short implants and usage of wide diameter implants is being recommended.[12]

Preservation of marginal bone is a desirable factor for survival of an implant. The microthreads maintain strain levels at the “steady state zone” around the implants and thus counteract marginal bone resorption.[1314] In various clinical situations, to increase the functional and structural implant-bone integrity, the morphology of surrounding bone should be evaluated and considered. Based on its ability to withstand and accommodate the stresses, the design and shape of the implant should be chosen. More uniform stress distribution in the surrounding bone is seen by the usage of stepped cylindrical design of implant as reported by Holmgren et al.[12] The use of tapered implants is also recommended due to enhanced primary stability especially in low density bone areas. When the available bone quantity is less (due to proximity of vital structures or during risk of perforations), tapered implants are preferred.[15]

The present study was undertaken to evaluate the biomechanical response of standard diameter tapered and step form implants of varying lengths (6 mm and 10 mm) incorporating microthreads for crestal portion and acme threads for body portion placed in D4 bone quality under immediate loading protocol.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The solid modeling and finite element analysis (FEA) were performed using ANSYS classic, version 11. The bone model used in the study was generated for the maxillary posterior region. Two dimensional (2D) Finite Element models were created with a cancellous core D4 bone surrounded by a 0.5 mm thick cortical layer [Figure 1a-d].

(a) Step 6mm implant, (b) Step 10mm implant, (c) Tapered 6mm implant, (d) Tapered 10mm implant

Implant design

The simulated implants structure was of 6 mm and 10 mm length with standard diameter of 3.75 mm. Tapered and step implant designs were used. For 10 mm step implant the steps length was 3.33 mm each with a total of three steps and for 6 mm step implant, three steps were created with a length of 2 mm each. The width of step 1 was 3.75 mm, for step 2 it was 3.25 mm and for step 3 it was 2.75 mm for both 6 mm and 10 mm lengths, respectively. The implants were designed to incorporate microthreads at the crestal part with 0.2 mm screw pitch and 29° thread angle. The rest of the implant body was incorporated with acme threads with 0.8 mm screw pitch and 29° thread angle.[16] [Figure 2].

All the four implant designs incorporated micro threads at the crestal part with 0.2mm screw pitch and 290 thread angle. The rest of the implant body incorporates Acme threads with 0.8mm screw pitch and 290 thread angle

Interface condition

A frictional coefficient of 0.6 was applied at bone implant interface to simulate the interface of an immediately loaded implant.

Elements and nodes

The finite element method model composed of 4195 nodes and 4087 elements for tapered 6 mm implant and 3046 nodes and 2958 elements for step 6 mm implants. For 10 mm tapered implant the nodes were 5856 and elements were 5732 and for 10 mm step implant 3770 nodes and 3678 elements. For 6 mm implants, the height and width of bone block was 10 mm × 20 mm and for 10 mm implants, 15 mm × 20 mm, respectively [Figure 3].

RESULTS

The values of total deformation for vertical loading were 0.16 mm and 0.0085 mm for stepped short and long implant, respectively and 0.12 mm and 0.08 mm for tapered short and long implant, respectively. For oblique loading the values for total deformation were 0.13 mm and 0.11 mm for stepped and tapered 6 mm implant and 0.017 mm and 0.083 mm for stepped and tapered 10 mm implant, respectively [Figure 5a and b]. The values were found to be minimum for step long implant and maximum for step short implant using vertical loads [Figure 6a and b].

For tapered implants with vertical loads, Von mises stress values were 94.2 MPa for 6 mm implant and 47.2 MPa for 10 mm implant [Figure 7a and b] and Von mises strain values were 0.018 and 0.014 for 6 mm and 10 mm implants, respectively [Figure 8a and b]. Stress values in tapered implants were more for oblique loading than vertical loads, i.e., 306.6 MPa and 204.4 MPa for 6 mm and 10 mm implants, respectively [Figure 9a and b]. Similar was the condition with strain values in oblique loading and they were 0.034 for 6 mm implant and 0.020 for 10 mm tapered implant [Figure 10a and b]. For stepped implants the Von mises stress values for vertical loads were more, i.e., 188 MPa for 6 mm implant and 35.44 MPa for 10 mm implant. For oblique loading also the values were more for 6 mm implant, i.e., 319.2MPa and less for 10 mm implant, i.e., 96.9MPa. The strain values for stepped implants were 0.025 and 0.017 for oblique loading and 0.016 and 0.0039 for vertical loading for 6 mm and 10 mm length implants, respectively. When lengths were compared, stress as well as strain values were found to be lower for long implants. The stress values were found to be highest for step 6 mm implant and lowest for step 10 mm implant among all four models with both vertical as well as oblique loads.

DISCUSSION

The purpose of this study was to use 2D FEA model to analyze the effect of varying designs and lengths of implants placed in D4 bone quality under immediate loading protocol. The method of FEA helps to obtain the solution of complex problems by subdividing them into smaller and simpler problems. These simpler problems can be solved using numerical techniques and a combined solution can be obtained. This method allows the stress and strain evaluation of the complex dental implant and bone system. Thus the prediction of success of a particular implant is increased.[19] Instead of the complex three-dimensional models, the 2D models are being considered for the present study for the ease of manipulation, simplicity, efficiency and cost-effectiveness.

Immediate functional loading has gained considerable importance and comparable results are being obtained. Controlled loading conditions as well as good primary stability plays a major role in this.[20] To simulate the clinical conditions of occlusal forces, loading should be evaluated in both vertical as well as oblique direction. The vertical loads (parallel to long axis) are considered to be normal and can be well sustained, however oblique loads are considered as being more realistic masticatory movements and it has been demonstrated to cause the highest stress in cortical bone for dental implants.[21] In the present study, a 100 N force was applied along the long axis of implant and also at 45° to the long axis of implant. Occlusal loading creates micromotions at the bone-implant interface. The differentiation hypothesis by Frost says micromovements (if within certain limits) create a local mechanical environment that helps in the proliferation and differentiation of the osteoblasts which will help in periimplant bone formation. However if these microstrains reach to higher values it results in immature bone mineral formation and a fibroblastic cell pattern.[22]

In the present study, the value of total deformation (micromovements) was found to be lower for tapered long implants. The values were 36.43% and 26.9% lesser for 10 mm implants as compared to 6 mm short implants with vertical and oblique loading forces, respectively. Among all the four models of implants, the total deformation (micromovements) values were found to be least for 10 mm step implant and highest for 6 mm step implant. The value of total deformation was 89.59% and 79.59% lower for step 10 mm implant as compared to tapered 10 mm implant with vertical and oblique loading, respectively. The microstrains, stresses and micromotions generated at the bone-implant interface are dependent on implant design and geometry and also the bone quality.[23] Implant failure rates have been found more with weaker bone quality (D4).[2425] Thus understanding the peri-implant bone effects in poor bone quality is important in proper implant selection and also long-term success.

This study also focuses on the problems encountered by the clinician about the choice of implant design in the presence of low bone density (D4). Stepped implant design has been shown to distribute the loads favorably as it mimics the natural root form.[26] Also, tapered implant design has a good survival rate and directs the stress away from the crestal bone portion.[2728] When considering the implant lengths longer implants have been found to act better than the shorter ones.[2930] However the jaw anatomy and reduced alveolar bone height limits the choice of implant length. In these situations the use of short length implants is more appropriate.[31] The results of our study are in accordance with other studies with lower stress values for long implant than short implants. Also among all the four models evaluated here, step long implant (10 mm) showed minimum stress as well as strain distribution at bone implant interface with both vertical as well as oblique loading forces.

The use of threaded implants helps in achieving good primary stability and optimal stress distribution.[13] Criteria for the selection of thread design especially in the presence of low bone density should be the one which maintains strain levels at the “steady state zone” and stimulates bone preservation.[32] In the present study the use of microthreads at the crestal module and acme threads for the rest of the implant body gave optimal results, especially for long implants.

The results obtained in our study also emphasize the importance of direction of loads affecting the biomechanical aspects for alveolar bone and implants. The values of stress and strains was found to be higher for short implants especially with the use of oblique loading forces than axial forces. Our study was not able to demonstrate favorable results for stress and strain distribution at bone implant interface for standard diameter short implants in low bone density. In areas with low bone density, the survival rates of short implants may be made comparable to those of longer implants with standard diameters either with the increase in diameter of short implants or with the incorporation of other variable thread forms other than microthreads and acme threads as used here.

Several assumptions were made in this computer based analytical study such as the loading conditions (axial and oblique) simulated in this study were not realistic and approximated roughly to simulate the complex balance between masticatory forces and their reactions. The structures in the model were all assumed to be homogenous, isotropic and to possess linear elasticity. However, the properties of living tissues are completely different. In this study, no cement thickness was taken into consideration.[33]

Within the limitation of this 2D finite element study, the following conclusions can be drawn:
