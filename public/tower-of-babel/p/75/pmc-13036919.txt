Association rule analysis of physical activity and multimorbidity in middle-aged Korean adults with diabetes: an evidence to promote active lifestyles
Authors: Yingcheng Huang, Zhongwei Sun, Jin Lan, Yali Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetes mellitus is a prevalent chronic disease that often coexists with other conditions, especially in middle-aged adults, complicating disease management and increasing health risks. As physical inactivity is a known contributor to both diabetes and related comorbidities, this study examines how physical activity (PA) influences the clustering of chronic diseases in middle-aged Korean adults with diabetes. Using data from 15,517 individuals aged 40–64 from the Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2016–2021), participants were categorized as physically active or inactive based on the WHO guidelines. In this cross-sectional study, association rule analysis was conducted to identify patterns of comorbidity. The most common co-occurring conditions were obesity, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and hypertriglyceridemia. Association rule identified more diabetes-related comorbidity rules in physically inactive individuals than in physically active individuals (20 vs. 14), with generally stronger rule metrics in the inactive subgroup (maximum lift: 2.7 vs. 2.4). Unique associations also emerged, such as cataract with diabetes in inactive individuals and depression with diabetes in active individuals. These findings suggest that regular PA may help prevent or weaken harmful disease clusters, reducing the overall burden of multimorbidity. Promoting physical activity should be a central strategy in public health efforts to improve health outcomes and support healthy aging in diabetic populations.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a major chronic disease characterized by either insufficient insulin production by the pancreas or the body’s inability to effectively utilize the insulin it produces [1]. According to the International Diabetes Federation Diabetes Atlas, the global prevalence of diabetes among individuals aged 20–79 years was estimated at 10.5% (536.6 million people) in 2021 and is projected to increase to 12.2% (783.2 million people) by 2045 [2]. South Korea exemplifies this global trend. Based on the 2012–2023 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, the prevalence of diabetes among Korean adults aged 19 years and older rose from 9.7% in 2012 to 13.2% in 2023 [3]. However, only 15.9% of adult patients with diabetes have achieved comprehensive management goals [4]. These suboptimal management outcomes, together with the high prevalence, may substantially contribute to the growing burden of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) attributable to diabetes in South Korea [5].

In South Korea, middle age is commonly defined as 40–64 years, a period characterized by the onset of physiological aging while individuals remain at the peak of their social and economic productivity [6]​. Epidemiological evidence indicates that physical inactivity is highly prevalent among middle-aged adults globally [7]. The development of chronic diseases during this stage may increase health risks later in life [8]. Therefore, middle-aged adults represent a key population for public health research, particularly in the context of ongoing demographic aging.

Diabetes in middle age commonly coexists with other chronic conditions, such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, obesity, and cardiovascular disease, forming complex comorbidity profiles [9, 10]. Approximately 90% of individuals with diabetes have at least one additional chronic disease, with many experiencing multiple comorbidities [11], The term “multiple chronic diseases” describes such patients, regardless of the types of chronic disease. These clustered conditions substantially increase disease management complexity and are associated with higher risks of disability, hospitalization, and mortality [12, 13].

Physical activity (PA) is a well-established, modifiable behavioral factor that plays a crucial role in preventing or delaying the onset of various chronic diseases, including diabetes. Beyond its protective role, regular PA can also attenuate the interconnections among different chronic conditions, thereby reducing the incidence of multiple chronic diseases in individuals [14, 15]. Conversely, physical inactivity is strongly associated with an increased risk of developing diabetes and other non-communicable diseases (NCDs). For instance, an epidemiological analysis conducted in Brazil estimated that approximately 3% of diabetes-related deaths could have been prevented through sufficient PA [16]. Similarly, findings from 13 population-based health surveys conducted in Germany between 1997 and 2018 indicated that the prevalence of physical inactivity was significantly higher among individuals with diabetes compared to those without the disease [17]. These findings collectively support the notion that physical inactivity is strongly associated with diabetes and is also linked to an increased mortality risk among affected individuals.

While the independent associations between physical inactivity and adverse diabetes outcomes are well established, less is known about how PA levels may influence the broader patterns of multiple chronic diseases among individuals with diabetes. In particular, it remains unclear whether middle-aged individuals with diabetes who engage in recommended levels of PA exhibit different comorbidity patterns compared to their physically inactive counterparts. Addressing this knowledge gap is crucial for developing tailored and effective intervention strategies. Therefore, the present study aims to examine how comorbidity patterns differ among middle-aged Korean adults with diabetes based on their PA status. We apply association rule analysis to identify common clusters and interrelationships of chronic conditions in individuals with diabetes who meet versus do not meet PA recommendations. By illuminating these patterns, our study seeks to enhance the understanding of how PA habits relate to the complex landscape of comorbidities in middle-aged diabetes patients, thereby informing more targeted public health strategies in the context of an aging society.

Methods

Research subjects

The data for this study were obtained from the Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES), conducted by the Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (KCDC) from 2016 to 2021 [18]. Ethical approval for this survey was granted by the Research Ethics Review Committee of the KCDC. After merging, cleaning, and filtering the data collected over the six-year period, a total of 15,517 participants were included in the final analysis. Chronic diseases

In this study, we examined a total of 22 non-communicable chronic diseases. These include diabetes, hypertension, obesity, and dyslipidemia—comprising both hypercholesterolemia and hypertriglyceridemia stroke and cardiovascular disease (myocardial infarction and angina) arthritis (including osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis), osteoporosis, asthma, thyroid disorders, and various forms of cancer (such as stomach, liver, colon, breast, cervical, lung, thyroid, and others), depression, allergic inflammatory conditions (such as atopic dermatitis, allergic rhinitis, sinusitis, and otitis media), chronic eye diseases (including cataracts, glaucoma, and macular degeneration), kidney disease; and liver cirrhosis.

Among these, hypertension, obesity, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, and hypertriglyceridemia were diagnosed based on objective clinical examination criteria. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure of ≥ 140 mmHg and a diastolic blood pressure of ≥ 90 mmHg, or the current use of antihypertensive medication. Obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) of ≥ 25 kg/m². Diabetes was defined as a fasting blood glucose level of ≥ 126 mg/dL, a physician’s diagnosis, or current use of hypoglycemic medication or insulin injection. Hypercholesterolemia was defined as a total cholesterol level of ≥ 240 mg/dL or current use of cholesterol-lowering medication. Hypertriglyceridemia was defined as a triglyceride level of ≥ 200 mg/dL. The diagnoses of the remaining 17 diseases were based on self-reported data collected through structured questionnaires. Participants who answered “yes” to both questions were considered to have the corresponding chronic condition in this study.

Physical activity status

In this study, data on PA status were collected using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ), which enabled a comprehensive assessment of participants’ PA patterns. Based on their responses, participants were categorized according to their adherence to the World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations for PA. Participants who met or exceeded the WHO guidelines of engaging in at least 150 min of moderate-intensity PA per week, or 75 min of vigorous-intensity PA per week, or an equivalent combination of both were classified as “physically active.” According to the equivalency principle, 1 min of vigorous-intensity activity is considered equal to 2 min of moderate-intensity activity. Conversely, participants who did not meet these thresholds were categorized as “physically inactive.”

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0, while graphical representations and figures were generated using OriginPro2021. To evaluate differences between groups, chi-square tests were applied for categorical variables, and independent samples t-tests were used for continuous variables. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Association rule analysis

Association rule analysis was employed in this study as it is able to uncover complex, non-obvious relationships among multiple chronic conditions, without relying on the restrictive assumptions typically required by traditional regression-based methods such as linearity, variable independence, or a predefined model structure [19]. In recent years, this analytical approach has been increasingly applied in public health and medical research to identify hidden disease clusters and to support data-driven decision-making in healthcare policy and practice [13, 20].

In the present study, association rule mining was performed using IBM SPSS Modeler version 18.0. This method identifies frequent co-occurring itemsets and extracts strong relational rules among them. The relationships are typically expressed in the form of A → B, where A (the antecedent) and B (the consequent) represent one or more items (i.e., chronic diseases). These rules are evaluated using three primary metrics: support, confidence, and lift, which together quantify the strength and significance of the identified associations. The calculation methods for these three metrics were consistent with those used in previous studies [15].

Individuals with diabetes were older than those without diabetes (p < 0.01). Regarding residential characteristics, participants with diabetes were more likely to reside in rural areas than those without diabetes (p < 0.01). The parameters for association rule mining included a minimum conditional support of 0.5%, a minimum confidence of 20%, a lift greater than 1.5, and a maximum of five antecedent items per rule. In the physically active subgroup, a total of 14 association rules related to diabetes were identified (Table 2). Among the 10 chronic conditions identified in the antecedents, the most frequent were hypertension and hypercholesterolemia (7 times each), followed by obesity (6 times), hypertriglyceridemia (5 times), arthritis (2 times), and depression (1 time). Among these identified rules, the one with the highest lift was “Obesity + Hypertension + Hypercholesterolemia → Diabetes” with a lift value of 2.4, indicating that individuals with this combination of conditions were 2.4 times more likely to have diabetes than the general population. Further analysis showed that 13 out of the 14 rules in Table 2 also appeared in physically inactive subgroup (Table 3), while the rule “Depression → Diabetes” appeared only in the physically active group.

In the physically inactive subgroup, a total of 20 association rules related to diabetes were identified (Table 3). Among the 11 chronic conditions identified in the antecedents, the most frequent was hypertension (10 times), followed by hypercholesterolemia (9 times), obesity (8 times), hypertriglyceridemia (6 times), arthritis (5 times), and cataract (1 time). Among all the rules, the one with the highest lift was“Hypertension + Hypertriglyceridemia + Hypercholesterolemia → Diabetes”, with a lift value of 2.7, indicating that individuals with this combination of conditions were 2.7 times more likely to have diabetes than the general population. Further analysis showed that 13 out of the 20 rules in Table 3 also appeared in physically active subgroup (Table 2), while several rules—such as “Cataract → Diabetes” and “Hypertension + Hypertriglyceridemia + Hypercholesterolemia → Diabetes” total 7 association rules were found only in the physically inactive group.

Discussion

This study used association rule analysis to examine diabetes-related multimorbidity patterns among middle-aged Korean adults, with comparisons by physical activity status. Overall, diabetes frequently co-occurred with metabolic conditions such as obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and hypertriglyceridemia, often appearing in clustered patterns. Subgroup analyses indicated that physically inactive individuals exhibited more complex and stronger diabetes-related disease associations than their physically active counterparts.

Middle age (40–64 years) is often regarded as a life stage associated with peak economic productivity [22]. However, it also marks a stage when health becomes increasingly vulnerable to chronic conditions, among which diabetes is a major health challenge for middle-aged adults [23]. The interaction between disease burden and socioeconomic disadvantage is therefore critical for understanding health disparities in this population. Our findings indicate that individuals with diabetes tend to have lower socioeconomic status and poorer living conditions compared with the general population. These results suggest an association between socioeconomic disadvantage and the occurrence of diabetes. Potential contributing factors include limited health literacy, unhealthy dietary habits, and inadequate access to healthcare services. Although overall diabetes control in South Korea has improved in recent years, comprehensive management remains suboptimal. Only 15.9% of patients have achieved comprehensive diabetes management—defined as simultaneous control of blood glucose, blood pressure, and blood lipids [4], representing an improvement from 9.7% in 2019–2020 [24]. Given the complexity and increasing burden of diabetes, regular monitoring of national diabetes indicators is essential for informing and refining public health policies. South Korea must strengthen efforts to raise public awareness, promote early screening and prevention, and ensure equitable access to comprehensive diabetes care. National-level interventions should prioritize high-risk and underserved populations and incorporate socioeconomic factors into policy design and resource allocation.

Independent of the physical activity status, the results of our association rule analysis indicate that obesity, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and hypertriglyceridemia frequently appear in various association rule combinations, with a significantly higher frequency than other chronic diseases (Tables 2 and 3). This result is not surprising. Compared with other chronic conditions, these four diseases are not only closely related to diabetes but are also among the most common metabolic disorders in middle-aged populations. Notably, the association rule “Obesity + Hypertension + Hypercholesterolemia → Diabetes” exhibited the highest confidence and lift values in the physically active subgroup(Table 2), indicating that 27.2% of individuals with this metabolic risk factor cluster had diabetes, and that the probability of diabetes was 2.4 times higher than expected based on the baseline prevalence within the same subgroup. Although the relationship between hypertriglyceridemia and diabetes remains somewhat controversial in certain studies [25], the co-occurrence of these conditions often signals the presence of metabolic dysregulation, which may serve as an early warning sign for the development of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases [26]. These findings lend support to the concept of Metabolic Syndrome, also known as Syndrome X, which was introduced by the WHO as early as 1998 [27]. Metabolic Syndrome refers to the coexistence of multiple metabolic abnormalities, and it is designed as a tool for identifying individuals at high risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, with the goal of improving their quality of life and reducing mortality [28]. Over time, although diagnostic criteria have varied somewhat among different countries and institutions, they are all based on a common framework that includes five key indicators: central obesity, hypertension, elevated fasting blood glucose, hypertriglyceridemia, and low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol. In addition, arthritis also appeared multiple times in our data mining results. The relationship between diabetes and arthritis is not a new topic; the incidence of insulin resistance among arthritis patients is significantly higher than in the general population [29], and insulin resistance is a key marker of Type 2 Diabetes. Although most well-conducted studies support a link between arthritis and diabetes, the association is generally considered weaker than that between arthritis and insulin resistance. Moreover, arthritis patients often share other risk factors for diabetes, such as treatment with glucocorticoids, obesity, lifestyle factors, and exposure to disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs that may promote the development of Type 2 Diabetes [30].

Through subgroup comparisons, we observed that the Physically Inactive subgroup not only exhibited a greater number of association rules, but also, as shown in Fig. 2, the rules identified in this group tended to have higher support and confidence values compared to those in the Physically Active subgroup. This finding suggests that adequate PA is associated with a lower probability of comorbidities among individuals with diabetes. Of particular interest is the association rule “Cataract → Diabetes,” which emerged exclusively within the Physically Inactive subgroup. This observation prompts further attention to the potential relationship between cataracts and diabetes. It is well established that diabetes, as a systemic chronic metabolic disorder, leads to pathological changes in multiple ocular tissues and is characterized by microvascular complications [31]. Cataract is one of the leading causes of visual impairment in individuals with diabetes [32], and studies have reported that individuals with diabetes are up to five times more likely to develop cataracts, especially at an earlier age [33]. Notably, this specific rule did not appear in the Physically Active subgroup, indicating that individuals with diabetes who engage in sufficient PA may not exhibit a significant association between cataract and diabetes. Given the existing evidence suggesting associations relationships between PA and both diabetes [34] and cataract [35], we have reason to hypothesize that adequate PA may play a critical role in disrupting this pathological linkage. Several plausible mechanisms have been proposed to explain the protective effect of PA against cataracts. Among these, oxidative stress has received particular attention, as the lens is highly susceptible to oxidative damage that can lead to the development of opacities.

PA may alleviate oxidative stress by activating endogenous antioxidant enzymes [36]. In addition, PA promotes the production of high-density lipoprotein [37], which is instrumental in transporting lipophilic antioxidants [38]. Elevated HDL levels may enhance the delivery of antioxidant compounds from the plasma to the lens, thereby preventing oxidative damage and inflammation in the ocular lens [39]. Moreover, PA may also reduce circulating levels of C-reactive protein, a systemic inflammatory marker that has been associated with an increased risk of senile cataract [40]. Additionally, it is noteworthy that the association rule “Depression → Diabetes” also appeared within the physically active subgroup. Current evidence does not suggest that regular PA increases the risk of either diabetes or depression; rather, extensive research indicates significant protective effects of PA against both conditions [41, 42]. Therefore, we hypothesize that at this life stage, individuals who possess sufficient physical capability and economic resources are more likely to engage in regular PA to manage or improve their existing chronic conditions, such as diabetes or depression. Consequently, some individuals already diagnosed with diabetes or depression may proactively adopt adequate PA as a non-pharmacological intervention, reflecting a higher health awareness. As a result, a considerable number of comorbid individuals appeared in the “physically active” subgroup, thereby generating the unexpected association rule mentioned above.

This study has several limitations. First, although association rule analysis can identify frequent co-occurrence patterns among chronic diseases, it cannot provide information about temporal sequence or causal relationships, nor can it effectively account for the influence of confounding factors such as age and income. Second, the use of minimum support and confidence thresholds may have excluded low-prevalence but clinically important disease combinations. Lastly, the study sample consisted of Korean adults aged 40–64, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other age groups, countries, or ethnic populations. Future studies should consider longitudinal designs, objective measurements, and alternative analytical approaches to verify and extend our findings.

Conclusions

Based on association rule analyses conducted across physical activity subgroups, obesity, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and hypertriglyceridemia consistently emerged as the most common co-occurring chronic conditions among middle-aged Korean adults with diabetes, often appearing in clustered patterns. Subgroup analyses further indicated that comorbidity profiles differed by physical activity level, with physically inactive individuals exhibiting a larger number of diabetes-related association rules and stronger associations among chronic diseases. These findings suggest that maintaining regular PA plays a critical role in reducing the occurrence of comorbidity multimorbidity in individuals with diabetes. Therefore, public health interventions should place particular emphasis on promoting PA among middle-aged individuals with diabetes to improve their overall health outcomes.
