Usefulness of pegfilgrastim for allogeneic peripheral blood stem cell collection from healthy donors
Authors: Tomoshige Shimizu, Tomoyasu Jo, Noriyoshi Yoshinaga, Takashi Sakamoto, Hiroyuki Matsui, Toshio Kitawaki, Junya Kanda, Momoko Nishikori, Kouhei Yamashita, Miki Nagao, Akifumi Takaori‐Kondo, Yasuyuki Arai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGranulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF)‐based mobilization is essential for peripheral blood stem cell (PBSC) collection for allogeneic transplantation. Conventional daily G‐CSF requires repeated dosing and dose adjustment and often necessitates leukapheresis over multiple consecutive days, increasing donor burden. Single‐dose pegfilgrastim has emerged as an alternative; however, real‐world data on its impact on CD34+ cell collection in healthy donors remain limited.Study Design and MethodsHealthy related or unrelated donors who received either a single dose of pegfilgrastim or daily filgrastim and subsequently underwent PBSC collection at Kyoto University Hospital between July 2014 and September 2025 were included. CD34+ cell harvest outcomes and donor burden were compared.ResultsA total of 39 donors were analyzed (20 pegfilgrastim, 19 filgrastim), with comparable baseline characteristics. At the start of leukapheresis, peripheral blood white blood cell counts were significantly higher in the pegfilgrastim group. Pegfilgrastim achieved greater total CD34+ cell yield (median 398.0 × 106 vs. 302.4 × 106 cells, p = .025) and higher yields per 10 L of processed blood (median 407.8 × 106 vs. 302.4 × 106 cells, p = .032). Single‐session leukapheresis was more frequent with pegfilgrastim (95.0% vs. 63.2%, p = .002), and the estimated processing time required to collect 2 × 106/kg CD34+ cells was shorter (median 62.7 vs. 111.1 min, p = .040). No severe adverse events were observed.DiscussionThese findings suggest that single‐dose pegfilgrastim enables more efficient PBSC collection with reduced donor burden and may represent a practical mobilization strategy in healthy allogeneic donors.Keywords: allogeneic donors, CD34+ yield, filgrastim, PBSC mobilization, pegfilgrastim

INTRODUCTION

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo‐HSCT) is widely performed as potentially curative therapy for relapsed or refractory hematological diseases. 1 Hematopoietic stem cell sources include bone marrow, peripheral blood stem cells (PBSCs), and cord blood. PBSC transplantation (PBSCT) offers several advantages compared with bone marrow transplantation. It enables faster hematopoietic engraftment in recipients and precludes the need for preoperative autologous blood collection and general anesthesia for donors, and generally imposes less physical burden. Thus, PBSCT is widely performed worldwide 2 , 3 and has become increasingly common in Japan. 4

For PBSC collection, donors are mobilized by administration of granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF) to induce hematopoietic stem cell release into peripheral blood, followed by leukapheresis to collect a sufficient number of CD34+ cells. 5 Traditionally, short‐acting G‐CSF formulations such as filgrastim have been administered daily for 4 to 6 consecutive days prior to leukapheresis. 6 , 7 However, this approach requires that donors come to the clinic on multiple, consecutive days and that clinical staff members adjust G‐CSF doses in accordance with guidelines for appropriate use of G‐CSF. Moreover, in some donors, a single leukapheresis session fails to yield an adequate number of CD34+ cells. In such cases, additional, consecutive leukapheresis procedures become necessary, resulting in prolonged schedules, increased donor burden, and occasionally insufficient graft cell doses for recipients. 8 Although plerixafor is available as a rescue mobilization agent in autologous transplantation, its use is not approved for healthy donors, 9 leaving few options to improve mobilization efficiency in this setting. With diversification of transplantation techniques, including widespread use of haploidentical transplantation with posttransplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy), the optimal CD34+ cell dose required for transplantation has tended to increase. 10 , 11 Consequently, improving the efficacy of PBSC mobilization has become an urgent clinical need.

Recently, single‐dose subcutaneous administration of a long‐acting G‐CSF, pegfilgrastim, has attracted attention as a PBSC mobilization strategy for healthy donors. 12 A prospective clinical trial performed in Japan reported that when healthy adult donors received a single 7.2‐mg subcutaneous injection of pegfilgrastim, all individuals achieved peripheral blood CD34+ cell counts ≥20 × 106/L by day 7 post‐administration, indicating robust mobilization of CD34+ cells to the peripheral blood. 13 Based on these results, pegfilgrastim was first approved in Japan in 2022 for stem cell mobilization in healthy donors. However, real‐world data demonstrating that pegfilgrastim mobilization actually translates into improved CD34+ cell collection yields and enhanced leukapheresis procedural efficacy in healthy donors remain limited.

Therefore, in this study, we compared CD34+ cell harvest outcomes between single‐dose pegfilgrastim and conventional daily filgrastim mobilization in healthy PBSC donors, so as to determine the clinical utility of pegfilgrastim as a mobilization strategy in real‐world PBSC collection.

METHODS

Study population

We retrospectively analyzed healthy donors who underwent allogeneic PBSC collection using either pegfilgrastim or filgrastim for mobilization at Kyoto University Hospital between July 2014 and September 2025. From July 2014 through August 2023, stem cell mobilization was performed using either filgrastim or lenograstim, with the two agents chosen at random. For the present analysis, only donors who received filgrastim were included in the filgrastim group. From September 2023 onward, pegfilgrastim was used exclusively for mobilization. Donors fulfilled the Japan Marrow Donor Program (JMDP) donor eligibility criteria, 14 and donors with body mass index (BMI) ≥30 kg/m2 or body weight <40 kg were not included. Donors included both related and unrelated donors. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Kyoto University and was conducted in accordance with principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Peripheral blood stem cell mobilization

The first day of filgrastim or pegfilgrastim administration was defined as day 1. Donors in the pegfilgrastim group received a single subcutaneous injection of 7.2 mg/body pegfilgrastim (G‐Lasta®; Kyowa Kirin Co., Ltd.) on day 1. Donors in the filgrastim group received filgrastim (Gran®; Kyowa Kirin Co., Ltd.) subcutaneously at 400 μg/m2 donor body surface area, administered either once daily or divided into two doses, and continued daily until completion of leukapheresis as per prescribing information. Donor body surface area was calculated according to Fujimoto's formula based on body weight and height, as follows 15 : body surface area (m2) = 0.008883 × height (cm)0.663 × weight (kg)0.444. Actual body weight was used for this calculation, without adjustment for high or low body weight. During filgrastim administration, blood tests were performed daily prior to dosing. The dose was reduced by half if leukocyte counts exceeded 50,000/μL or if platelet counts fell below 100,000/μL. Administration was discontinued if leukocyte counts exceeded 75,000/μL or platelet counts decreased below 50,000/μL, in accordance with guidelines for appropriate use of G‐CSF in Japan. 16 To mitigate G‐CSF–associated musculoskeletal pain, acetaminophen was administered prophylactically, with additional acetaminophen or loxoprofen on an as‐needed basis.

Peripheral blood stem cell collection

PBSC collection began on day 4 or 5 after G‐CSF administration in the pegfilgrastim group and on day 4 in the filgrastim group. In the pegfilgrastim group, the choice between day 4 and day 5 was determined by logistical factors such as scheduling and day‐of‐week considerations, not influenced by donor clinical parameters. Throughout the entire study period, all leukapheresis procedures were performed using Spectra Optia® (Terumo BCT) in mononuclear cell (MNC) mode, with citrate‐dextrose solution A used for anticoagulation. Blood volume processed was determined according to the Japanese Marrow Donor Program (JMDP) Manual for Peripheral Blood Stem Cell Collection. 5 CD34+ cell counts in collected products were measured using BD Stem Cell Enumeration Kits (Becton, Dickinson and Company) with flow cytometry performed on a FACS Canto II (Becton, Dickinson and Company) single‐platform assay at Kyoto University Hospital. 17 In a subset of donors, measurements of peripheral blood hematopoietic progenitor cells (HPCs) were performed using an XR‐9000 (Sysmex Corporation). 18 The target CD34+ cell dose was defined as ≥4 × 106/kg recipient body weight for recipients undergoing PTCy‐based transplantation, and ≥2 × 106/kg recipient body weight for all other transplantation settings. Leukapheresis was continued for up to two consecutive days until the target CD34+ cell dose was achieved.

Assessment parameters and statistical analysis

Donor demographics, including age, sex, weight, and height, as well as the total G‐CSF dose, were collected and compared between the two groups. Peripheral blood parameters were also evaluated both prior to the start of G‐CSF administration and just before leukapheresis on the first collection day. Blood volumes processed, CD34+ cell yields, and leukapheresis processing times were assessed using data from the first day of leukapheresis. CD34+ cell yield per 10.0 L of blood volume processed was derived by dividing the total CD34+ cell yield by the blood volume processed (L) and multiplying the result by 10.0 L. The estimated processing time to achieve a CD34+ cell dose of 2 × 106/kg was calculated as the actual collection time divided by the actual number of CD34+ cells collected per kg of donor recipient body weight, multiplied by 2 × 106/kg. Adverse events were monitored and recorded for all donors throughout the mobilization and collection period, and subsequently compared between the groups. Continuous variables were expressed as median and range, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Statistical comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student's t‐test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Statistical significance was defined as a p‐value <.05. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 19 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA), and figures were created using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA).

RESULTS

Donor characteristics and peripheral blood parameters

Twenty donors in the pegfilgrastim group and 19 donors in the filgrastim group were included in the analysis. There were 10 donors (50.0%) aged ≥35 years in the pegfilgrastim group and 13 (68.4%) in the filgrastim group, with no significant difference between the groups (p = .333). The pegfilgrastim group comprised 14 males (70.0%) and 6 females (30.0%), whereas the filgrastim group included 11 males (57.9%) and 8 females (42.1%), with no significant difference in sex distribution between the groups (p = .431). There were also no significant differences in height, weight, body surface area, or donor–recipient weight difference between the groups (Table 1). Peripheral blood parameters before G‐CSF administration, including white blood cell count, platelet count, hemoglobin, and hematocrit, showed no significant differences between the groups. On the first day of leukapheresis, white blood cell counts were significantly higher in the pegfilgrastim group than in the filgrastim group (median 55.20 × 103/μL vs. 39.84 × 103/μL, p = .011) (Table 2). In a subset of donors in whom peripheral blood HPC counts were available, median HPC counts were higher in the pegfilgrastim group than in the filgrastim group (171/μL [range, 71–219], n = 10 vs. 28/μL [range, 18–64], n = 3; p = .005). Pegfilgrastim was administered as a fixed dose of 7.2 mg in all donors, whereas filgrastim dosing was reduced in 9 donors (47.4%) because of leukocytosis before the completion of leukapheresis; no dose reductions were due to thrombocytopenia.

Collection outcomes

The median blood volume processed on the first day of leukapheresis was 10.0 L (range, 7.0–15.0) in the pegfilgrastim group, and 10.0 L (range, 5.2–10.0) in the filgrastim group, with no significant difference (p = .646). The pegfilgrastim group achieved a significantly higher CD34+ cell yield (median 398.0 × 106 cells; range, 155.5–1343.0 × 106) than the filgrastim group (median 302.4 × 106 cells; range 85.8–555.1 × 106) (p = .025) (Table 3, and Figure 1A). CD34+ cell yield per 10.0 L of blood volume processed was also significantly higher in the pegfilgrastim group (median 407.8 × 106 cells; range 129.6–1343.0 × 106) than in the filgrastim group (median 302.4 × 106 cells; range 85.8–688.6 × 106) (p = .032) (Table 3, and Figure 1B).

In the filgrastim group, dot plots of CD34+ cell yield and processing time suggested the presence of a subgroup of donors with particularly poor collection efficiency (Figures 1A,B, and 2). In a post hoc analysis stratified by CD34+ cell yield (<150 × 106, n = 8 vs. ≥150 × 106, n = 11), baseline donor characteristics were largely comparable between the two subgroups, whereas filgrastim dose reduction tended to be more frequent in the low‐yield subgroup than in the high‐yield subgroup (75.0% vs. 27.3%, p = .070; Supplemental Tables S1 and S2). Consistent with the definition, collection outcomes were worse in the low‐yield subgroup, including significantly longer processing time (Supplemental Table S3). Importantly, no distinct low‐yield subgroup was observed in the pegfilgrastim group, while CD34+ cell yield and processing time showed a broad distribution.

Frequent adverse events included back pain, headache, nausea, arthralgia, and mild laboratory abnormalities. The incidence of these events was similar between the pegfilgrastim and filgrastim groups. All adverse events were manageable with symptomatic treatments such as analgesics, and no adverse events requiring unscheduled treatment or hospital visits were observed in either group (Supplemental Table S4).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we compared CD34+ cell collection outcomes between single‐dose pegfilgrastim and conventional daily filgrastim for PBSC collection in healthy allogeneic donors. Our findings indicated that pegfilgrastim offers clear advantages in CD34+ cell collection performance and procedural efficiency, without an increase in adverse events.

First, with comparable baseline donor characteristics between the two groups, pegfilgrastim demonstrated superior CD34+ cell harvest outcomes compared with filgrastim. Pegfilgrastim, as a long‐acting G‐CSF, allows sustained and robust mobilization of hematopoietic stem cells with a single injection, 19 , 20 and previous reports have shown that such mobilization regimens offer equivalent or superior results compared to conventional G‐CSF protocols. 13 , 21 , 22 Our real‐world findings demonstrating increased CD34+ cell yields with pegfilgrastim are consistent with these earlier studies reporting improved mobilization efficacy. While peripheral CD34+ counts were not measured in this study, considering the absence of differences in donor characteristics or collection procedures, the substantial increase in actual CD34+ cell yield and the higher yield per processed blood volume suggest that pegfilgrastim enhances mobilization of stem cells to peripheral blood. Consistent with this, at the time of leukapheresis, donors who received pegfilgrastim exhibited significantly higher peripheral leukocyte counts, as well as HPCs, which are reportedly correlated with CD34+ cell counts. 18 Importantly, yield‐distribution analysis revealed a key advantage of pegfilgrastim: the absence of the low‐yield subgroup observed with filgrastim. This suggests that pegfilgrastim provides more consistent mobilization and reduces the risk of unexpectedly poor collection yield. Such improvements in stem cell mobilization and collection are beneficial for recipients as well, and are particularly valuable in contemporary transplantation settings, in which higher CD34+ targets, especially in PTCy‐based haploidentical transplantation, are increasingly recommended. 23 , 24 , 25

Pegfilgrastim offers important practical benefits. It is administered as a fixed single dose, eliminating the need for daily dosing adjustments. In contrast, filgrastim dosing requires careful dose modification based on leukocyte and platelet counts, as reductions are sometimes necessary to comply with appropriate‐use guidelines. 16 In fact, in this study, 9 donors (47.4%) in the filgrastim group required dose reduction. Such dose reductions carry a potential risk of compromising stem cell yield and represent a well‐recognized practical challenge in routine donor collection. 26 Notably, filgrastim dose reduction was more frequent in the low‐yield subgroup identified in our yield‐distribution analysis (Figure 1A,B, and Supplemental Table S2). Although exploratory, it suggests that dose reduction may have contributed to reduced collection efficiency in some donors. In this context, the fixed‐dose pegfilgrastim regimen, which precludes the need for dose reduction, may help to overcome the risk of insufficient CD34+ cell yield.

In the present study, pegfilgrastim was associated with a markedly higher likelihood of completing leukapheresis in a single day and with a significantly shorter estimated processing time to reach a CD34+ yield of 2 × 106/kg. By reducing the likelihood of low‐yield collection, pegfilgrastim supports a “one‐time dosing and one‐day collection” strategy, which enhances its clinical and operational value in real‐world settings through improved donor experience, more predictable transplant scheduling, and reduced demand for leukapheresis machines and personnel time.

In this study, no adverse events requiring medical intervention were observed in either group, which is consistent with previous reports documenting the safety of pegfilgrastim in healthy donors. Since several studies reported rare but serious complications such as vasculitis and arteritis, 27 , 28 , 29 continued vigilance and long‐term safety monitoring is important.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted as a single‐center study with a limited number of donors, which may have potentially biased analysis and limited generalizability of our findings to broader donor populations. Studies incorporating multicenter data will be essential to validate these results in more diverse settings. Second, although this study evaluated CD34+ cell yield and processing time, peripheral blood CD34+ cell counts were not measured. Consequently, we could not directly assess mobilization kinetics or collection efficiency. Incorporating sequential peripheral CD34+ measurements in future studies would allow a more comprehensive evaluation of mobilization dynamics and collection performance. Third, this analysis did not address differences in cost or evaluate posttransplant outcomes, both of which warrant investigation in future studies.

Collectively, our results indicate that single‐dose pegfilgrastim represents a highly promising alternative to traditional filgrastim mobilization. Pegfilgrastim offers improved and reliable CD34+ cell harvest outcomes, reduced donor burden, and streamlined clinical workflow, thereby enhancing overall PBSC collection performance. Future studies are warranted for a more comprehensive evaluation that includes donor background, cost, safety, and engraftment outcomes.
