Context, mechanisms, and outcomes of smoke-free school hours in social and health care vocational schools: a realist informed qualitative analysis
Authors: Marie Pil Jensen, Marie Broholm-Jørgensen, Dina Danielsen, Susan Andersen, Rikke Fredenslund Krølner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComprehensive school tobacco policies can reduce young people’s smoking, but evidence from upper secondary school settings with high smoking prevalence is limited. A smoke-free school hours policy was tested across different Danish vocational school types with the intended outcome of reduced smoking only observed among students at social and health care vocational schools. This study examines the contextual factors and mechanisms shaping smoking-related outcomes in this setting.MethodsWe analyzed interview transcripts from six semi-structured focus group interviews with social and health care vocational students (N = 41) conducted in 2018–2019. Using a realist evaluation perspective, we did a thematic analysis followed by Context–Mechanism–Outcome configurations to examine how outcomes were generated through interactions between contextual conditions and underlying mechanisms. To analyze and identify mechanisms, we used the heuristic tool of distinguishing between the resources offered by the intervention, and the reasoning of the students in response.ResultsWe identified five overall Context-Mechanism-Outcome configurations illustrating pathways through which smoke-free school hours produced different outcomes. Contextual conditions such as students’ professional orientation and expectations of future smoke-free workplaces activated mechanisms of trust in schools’ intentions and perceived policy meaningfulness, leading to acceptability and reduced smoking during school hours. Conversely, contexts characterized by strong beliefs in smoking as an individual responsibility and weak enforcement activated mechanisms related to perceived loss of autonomy, low perceived need for intervention, and low perceived risk of sanctions, resulting in policy disapproval, resistance, continued smoking, and increased smoking during school hours.ConclusionsThe findings show that the implementation of smoke-free school hours is highly context-dependent and operates through mechanisms that can generate both intended and unintended outcomes. Successful implementation in vocational school settings requires alignment with students’ professional identities, consistent enforcement, and communicative strategies to consider students’ attitudes and beliefs. Realist evaluation adds critical insight into how and why smoke-free school policies succeed or fail to inform future context-sensitive implementation strategies.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27231-5.Keywords: Realist evaluation, CMO, Cigarette smoking, Complex interventions, Young adults

Background

In recent decades, rates of smoking initiation and transition to daily smoking during early adulthood have been increasing [1], giving priority to tobacco control among this age group [2]. School tobacco policies are often used as means to prevent and reduce cigarette smoking and can, under the right circumstances, be viable tools in tobacco control, but the implementation process is essential [3, 4]. Thus, when comprehensive policies are consistently enforced, strictly sanctioned, and combined with preventive initiatives, the intended effects of reduced smoking can be achieved [3, 4]. Conversely, poorly implemented policies can lead to reverse effects, i.e., increased smoking [4]. Barriers to the implementation of school tobacco policies into routine practice across Europe have been reported [5–7]. For example, staff experience resistance and opposition to the rules from socially disadvantaged students and students with persistent smoking habits [5], which complicates the potential effectiveness of such policies among those most in need of smoking prevention. The current evidence of whether and how school tobacco policies work is primarily based on adolescents in secondary schools (13–16 years as the dominant ages) [3, 4], while the impact of school tobacco policies on smoking behavior among older adolescents and young adults in upper secondary education institutions such as high school and vocational school (from the age of 16 and above in Denmark) is under-investigated. Across Europe, the prevalence of daily smoking differs between tracks of upper secondary education, i.e., more students in vocational education smoke daily than students in academic educational tracks [8] and, in Denmark, 29% of students in vocational school smoked daily in 2019 compared to 9% in academic high schools [9].

In response to the high smoking prevalence in vocational schools in Denmark, the “Focus” intervention was developed to reduce smoking through a comprehensive school tobacco policy, smoke-free school hours, and five additional supportive components [10]. This policy banned smoking for students, staff, and visitors during the school day, both on and off school premises [10]. The intervention’s effectiveness was evaluated in a cluster-randomized controlled trial in 2018–2020 showing no overall effect of the intervention on the students’ smoking behavior [11]. However, the intervention was implemented to a greater extent in one of the included school types, social and health care vocational schools, compared to the two other included school types [12] and subgroup analyses indicated that the intervention reduced regular smoking among students in social and health care vocational schools (OR: 0.38, 95% CI: 0.14–0.98) [11]. Nevertheless, a ‘black box’ remains in terms of how the intervention produced change in this particular school type, i.e., the mechanisms [13].

Studying such mechanisms of impact is a key task of process evaluation of complex interventions [14]. Quantitative assessment of mechanisms may include testing of the hypothesized causal pathways outlined in the program theory, i.e., pre-specified mediating variables. However, qualitative assessment of mechanisms may explore more complex and unanticipated mechanisms that emerge when participants interact with the intervention activities in the real world [14]. Moreover, mechanisms are central to realist evaluation, a type of theory-based evaluation based on the idea that interventions can work differently for different people depending on the contextual conditions in which they are implemented [13, 15]. Theory-based evaluation perspectives are central to the evaluation of complex interventions on a par with efficacy, effectiveness, and systems-thinking research perspectives [16], as they reflect real-life contexts and conditions enhancing the usefulness of evaluations for practice and policy makers [16]. In realist thinking, mechanisms are seen as people’s responses to intervention activities in the real world [13, 17]. Thus, change is not produced by the intervention itself, but is produced when the participants interact with the intervention in a context [13]. Studies evaluating school-based interventions have similarly found great variation between schools in the level of implementation [18–21], underlining the importance of exploring the interplay between intervention activities and the context of implementation. Regarding school-based smoking prevention, mechanisms of change have been investigated in mediation analyses using pre-specified mediating variables [22–24] and in realist evaluations of smoking cessation interventions [25, 26]. The mechanisms of school tobacco policies have been explored in realist reviews [4, 6, 27] mainly focusing on adolescents up to 16 years [4] or on the mechanisms of school staff’s enforcement practices [6, 27]. To our knowledge, no studies have explored the mechanisms of how school tobacco policies impact student populations in the upper secondary education system nor vocational student populations prone to high smoking prevalence [9].

Thus, to seek explanations to the contextual variation in implementation and effectiveness of the “Focus” intervention between school types [11, 12], the realist evaluation perspective using qualitative methods is suitable for exploring how and why the observed outcomes were generated in the specific setting of social and health care vocational schools. This paper aims to explore the mechanisms emerging among students in social and health vocational schools to generate outcomes of smoke-free school hours, and how this process was influenced by contextual factors.

Methods

Setting: social and health care vocational schools

Danish social and health care vocational schools offer education and training for social and health care assistants for future employment in e.g. eldercare, hospitals, or daycare institutions [28]. The education consists of a basic and main program. The basic program takes place at the school and is divided into two parts. The first part of the basic program (20 weeks) is intended for students who recently finished compulsory education (appr. 16–18 years) and offers an opportunity for trying different educational programs before deciding on a direction. Students who have finished compulsory education more than two years ago start directly at the second part of the basic program (20 weeks), so the age range is much wider here. During the main program (appr. 3 years), the students shift between schoolwork and internships in municipal or regional workplaces such as elder care, home care, hospitals etc.

The “focus” intervention

The intervention was systematically developed based on theory, evidence, and a comprehensive needs assessment using the Behavior Change Wheel (BCW) and the Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation model of Behavior change (COM-B) [10, 29, 30]. The final intervention program was underpinned by 23 selected Behavior Change Techniques (BCTs) including restructuring the social and physical environment, social support, instruction on how to perform a behavior, and behavior substitution etc. [10]. The multi-component intervention program included the introduction and enforcement of a smoke-free school hours tobacco policy as the main component, stating that students, staff, and visitors were not allowed to smoke during the school day on or off school premises, a teacher-delivered curriculum, a staff course in facilitating short conversations about smoking with students, a class competition, free access to telephone-based smoking cessation support, and an edutainment session about smoking [10]. The program theory (Supplementary file 1) outlines how the intervention was expected to work. The components were intended to interact and complement each other to achieve the intended effects of preventing smoking uptake among non-smokers, reduce number of daily cigarettes among daily smokers, and prevent progression to daily smoking among occasional smokers [10]. For example, the tobacco policy was intended to provide physical and social opportunity for students to avoid smoking during school hours, the students’ physical capability of not smoking was addressed by providing access to telephone-based smoking cessation support and by support from staff trained in short conversations about smoking. Further, students’ reflective motivation to abstain from smoking was addressed by the curriculum and edutainment session [10].

Study design

This study is based on data from a process evaluation conducted alongside the “Focus” trial in 2018–2020 [10, 11, 31]. Three types of schools were included in the trial: social and health care vocational schools offering programs within the area of Care, health, and pedagogy (e.g., social and health care assistant), technical or commercial vocational schools offering programs within the areas of Technology, construction and transport (carpenter, electrician etc.), or Administration, commerce, and business service (office assistant, sales assistant etc.), and preparatory basic education and training institutions which offer programs targeting young people below 25 years who need to improve professionally, personally, and socially to proceed to the labor market or upper secondary education such as vocational schools. The intervention was tested during one school semester, i.e., from August to December. The process evaluation consisted of a qualitative study including ethnographic field work at selected schools, focus group interviews with students and teachers, and individual interviews with principals and a quantitative study including questionnaire data collected among all students at intervention schools and the teachers and principals who were involved in the intervention [12, 31, 32].

Participants and data collection

We used data from six focus group interviews with students (N = 41) conducted during the intervention period in 2018–2019 at the three social and health care vocational schools (Table 1). The sample of students was purposively selected to capture the experiences and viewpoints of a wide variety of students. School staff helped ask students within the same class to participate in an interview during school hours and tried to gather students who smoked and did not smoke. Four of the focus groups included students at the first part of the basic program aged 16–19 years, while two interviews included students at the second part of the basic program aged 18–54 years. In one of these interviews, five out of six students were above the age of 50 years. While these students are not a part of the age group of ‘young adults’ we find it important to include the views and experiences of these students because the general student population of social and health care vocational schools includes a wide age range. Most of the students were female which reflects the gender composition of social and health care vocational schools. The focus group interviews had a one-hour duration and were conducted in a room at the school by DD, MPJ, and a research assistant. The semi-structured interview guide (Supplementary file 2) focused on the students’ experiences with the intervention with a special focus on norms and attitudes related to smoking and smoke-free school hours, students’ everyday life at the school, social interactions in the class, and the role of smoking in relation to this. To stimulate discussions and reflections around the students’ beliefs and attitudes related to smoking and tobacco policies, position cards with statements of common smoking-related attitudes among young people were used in the interviews. Thus, data from the interviews provide access to the contextual circumstances of the students. This study focuses on the smoke-free school hours tobacco policy, and not the entire intervention program, because the policy was the component most present in the everyday school lives of the students, and thus, the dialogues in the interviews revolved mostly around this component. The interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. As the interviews were conducted with the original purpose of exploring students’ experiences with the intervention in general [32, 33] and not explicitly to perform a realist analysis, the present study can be characterized as a secondary analysis [34].

Analytical approach

Realist evaluation

The central question of realist evaluation is: “what works for whom under which circumstances and how?” [15]. Situated in the philosophy of realism, realist evaluation relies on a ‘generative’ causality understanding [13]. As opposed to a successionist understanding of causality assuming that “a causes b”, realist evaluation focus on the underlying generative mechanisms that interact with the context to bring about change. This understanding of causality implicates that it is not the task of realist evaluations to produce universally applicable findings. Rather, the aim is to produce context-sensitive evaluations useful to practice and policy makers [15]. Context-Mechanism-Outcome configurations (CMOs) are the constructs that illustrate the generative processes by which inputs (such as an intervention) causes outcomes. They are used to explicate the relationships between the key concepts of realist evaluation: context, mechanisms, and outcomes. Thus, we used CMOs in the present study as an analytical tool to unravel the processes by which the intervention produced change among students in social and health care vocational schools.

Context, mechanisms, and outcomes

Context

In line with the RAMESES II project, we understand context as features of the preexisting conditions into which interventions are introduced that enable or disable mechanisms [35, 36]. Thus, contextual factors are understood in relation to mechanisms, and as analytically distinct yet interconnected elements of the CMOs [35, 37]. Further, context can be conceptualized as consisting of multiple layers [35, 37]. Thus, to operationalize context in our analysis, we were inspired by Pawson et al.’s (2004) classification of four layers of context: 1) Individual capacities of key actors such as attitudes, knowledge, and skills, 2) Interpersonal relationships required to support the intervention, 3) Institutional setting in which the intervention is delivered such as norms and culture, and 4) Infrastructural system such as political circumstances and resources [38]. Although the four layers informed our initial conceptualization of context, the analytical approach was ultimately data-driven, allowing contextual factors to emerge inductively from the interview material rather than being pre-classified according to predefined categories.

Mechanisms

We base our analysis on Dalkin et al.’s disaggregation of mechanisms into resources provided by the intervention and the reasoning of the participants in response [13]. Despite mechanisms being one of three key elements of process evaluation of complex interventions, no clear operationalizations are offered by the MRC guidance. Dalkin et al.’s framework offers a useful operationalization to analyze mechanisms, and disentangle the intervention program and activities from mechanisms, which is inherently difficult [13]. In contrast to traditional lessons learned evaluations, which primarily summarize experiences and recommendations, our realist evaluation explicitly examines how the provision of resources interacts with participants’ reasoning to generate outcomes within specific contexts. By doing so, we move beyond descriptive learning and provide an explanatory account of generative processes.

Outcomes

In line with the realist tradition, we employ a broad approach to outcomes in the analysis to explore, in part, what explained the outcome pattern of decreased regular smoking as observed in the effectiveness study of “Focus” [11], but also other potential positive or negative outcomes resulting from the activation of mechanisms [39]. Inspired by a realist review of how school tobacco policies affect secondary students’ smoking behavior, we are interested in both cognitive outcomes such as attitudes, and behavioral outcomes such as changed or continued cigarette smoking [4].

Data analysis

In the analysis of the data, we sought to employ ‘retroductive’ reasoning. Retroduction refers to identifying the hidden causal forces behind patterns of change [40]. Thus, we intended to analyze and identify the underlying generative processes of how smoke-free school hours produced change. Retroductive reasoning draws upon both inductive and deductive logic as well as the insights and common sense of the researcher [40]. First, in an iterative process of movement between an open data driven approach and our research objectives, we conducted a thematic analysis of the interview transcripts. The first author used Braun & Clarke (2006)’s six-step guide of thematic analysis to identify patterns and themes in the interview transcripts [41]: familiarized herself with the data by reading the interview transcripts, generated initial codes in NVivo 12 software, searched for, reviewed, and defined themes, and wrote up the analysis [41]. In the next step, more explicitly guided by the research objectives, the first author returned to the full original transcripts and conducted a theory-informed analysis to identify patterns of possible contextual factors, mechanisms, and outcomes. We then analyzed these patterns, and the interactions between the identified contexts, mechanisms, and outcomes to theorize the underlying generative processes. We used “if then because” statements to analyze and gain a better understanding of the relationships and interactions, and we used Dalkin et al.’s (2015) heuristic tool to distinguish between the resources of the intervention and the reasoning of the participants in response [13]. Next, the identified relationships were structured more explicitly into Context-Mechanism-Outcome configurations. Eleven possible CMOs were identified in this step. In a collaborative process, the first author and MBJ discussed, adjusted, and combined related CMOs, and arrived at five CMOs reflecting the overall findings of the analysis. We decided to structure the findings section by the identified mechanisms because the identified contextual factors were tightly interrelated. Based on the findings, we produced a program theory of how the intervention worked, under which circumstances, and why, in social and health care vocational schools.

Findings

In the following, we elucidate the identified CMO configurations. As the contextual factors activating the mechanisms were closely interlinked, we structure the findings based on the identified mechanisms. Thus, we illustrate and explain how the mechanisms and outcomes were activated by different contextual factors.

CMO 1. Perceived violation of personal freedom and autonomy

This CMO describes a mechanism reflecting students’ experience that smoke-free school hours violate personal freedom and autonomy. This mechanism generated outcomes of disapproval of or opposition to the policy and, in some cases, continued smoking during school hours among students (Fig. 1). In this section, we explain how different contextual factors activated this mechanism.

CMO 1. Perceived violation of personal freedom and autonomy

Smoking as an individual responsibility

When presented with position cards during the focus group interviews, most students declared agreement to the statement: “Smoking is your own responsibility” and disagreement to the statement: “The school has the right to make rules about smoking”. The students seemed to perceive cigarette smoking and its health consequences as an individual responsibility. They consider the school as an institution for education that should not interfere with the students’ personal life choices:

“(…) You must decide for yourself what you do and how you will take care of your health. It is not the school’s problem. It is your own problem. If you want to destroy your lungs, you do it. If you don’t want to, you don’t (laughs). (…) That is the thing… That you do it with your very own will.”

These attitudes and beliefs about smoking were widespread among the students, and the following citation illustrates how the comprehensive tobacco policy made the students experience that their personal freedom and autonomy were being disregarded which sparked negative emotions about the policy:

“It’s our own life, so they can’t decide what we do. (…) I don’t think they can tell us that we can’t go out to smoke. I can understand that we can’t smoke on school’s ground. But if we go to the other side of the road, then I think it’s ridiculous that we can’t smoke.”

Adults with professional experience

Further, some students at the second part of the basic program experienced that the school undermined their position as adult people and future health professionals capable of making their own decisions:

“We’re about to be educated as professionals of authority, we hear about legislation and rules all the time (…) when you have said something once (…) it is not necessary to say it again, because then we know. We are adults.”

These experiences seemed to be related to how the teachers communicated the intervention in the everyday situations in the classroom. Students in a class where the “Focus” project had been extensively emphasized by the teachers expressed that they were tired of hearing about the project. The massive focus on the tobacco policy, and intervention in general, could sometimes spark protest and opposition to the rules:

“It’s all we hear:”Remember not to go out to smoke in the breaks… Remember, remember (…)”. We are reminded all the time and you get the urge to say: “You (swears) shouldn’t decide this, I’m over 18, I will go out and smoke if I want to”.

Breaks perceived as personal time

The students at the first part of the basic program who normally smoked at school attached a special significance to breaks during the school day. While the classroom sessions were generally experienced as time dedicated to schoolwork and accepted as smoke-free time, breaks were experienced as their own personal time. The function of breaks seemed to be shaped by the students’ view of breaks in the workplace:

“I think when you are a professional at the workplace, you have to be professional in what you are doing. Same with the school. When you’re at school, you’re at school. In the breaks, it doesn’t matter because you don’t have classes. It’s the same with work. When you work, you’re at work. Breaks – you can do what you want.”

“Breaks are our own time, I think. Like at a workplace, right?”

"Considerate" smoking

The smoke-free school hours policy entailed that the students were not allowed to smoke in their breaks as usual, regardless of place; on or off the school’s property. Most students respected that school’s ground was smoke-free because they sympathized with the school’s presumed wish to avoid the mess from cigarette butts and to avoid influencing younger students to start smoking. Prior to the intervention, students who smoked would usually leave the school’s ground to smoke in consideration of the school and other students. These students, who seemed to view themselves as “considerate smokers” were therefore dissatisfied that the smoking ban applied beyond school’s ground during their breaks:

“I understand that they don’t want us to smoke on their ground because it’s annoying to clean up and collect cigarette butts every day. But the fact that we can’t smoke if we leave school’s ground… That’s… They shouldn’t decide that.”

“ … But I think it should be your own responsibility. It’s fair enough that you can’t smoke at school’s ground, but then you should be allowed to go near the supermarket or something like that. After all, it’s our free-time.”

Thus, when the smoking ban applied even when the students respected the policy on the school’s ground in consideration of others, they perceived the policy as irrational and unfair and therefore continued their smoking practice off school’s ground.

CMO 2. Perceived meaningfulness and trust in the school’s intentions

The next CMO focuses on mechanisms reflecting that students perceived the policy as meaningful in the light of the future profession and had faith in the school’s intentions behind introducing the policy, i.e., to prepare students for internships and future employment (Fig. 2). These mechanisms facilitated the acceptance of and adherence to the policy.

CMO 2. Perceived meaningfulness and trust in the school’s intentions

Orientation towards the future profession

We identified a pattern of orientation towards the future vocational profession among the students. This orientation was expressed as a strong motivation for the education and profession, a self-identification with the professional role as social and health care assistant or pedagogical assistant, and a perception of the school setting as comparable to the cultures and norms of a workplace. This orientation shaped how they experienced the tobacco policy. For example, some students accepted the policy from a professional point-of-view; you sign a contract with the school as you do with an employer and enter the school on a set of terms and conditions including a restrictive tobacco policy:

“But you have to think of the school as a workplace. (…) So, the rule is there (the tobacco policy), and that is what you put your signature on when we are employed or accepted at this school (…) We also do it at the workplace.”

Moreover, students who already had professional experience within the field and had personal experience with smoke-free workplaces found it easier to adhere to the policy during school hours. Thus, this professional orientation facilitated the acceptance of and adherence to smoke-free school hours.

Smoke-free work hours at future workplaces

Most of the municipal and regional workplaces in Denmark such as hospitals, nursing homes, kindergartens etc. have a smoke-free work hours tobacco policy. This means that all employees are not allowed to smoke during work hours inside or outside on or off the workplace’s property including during self-paid breaks. The anticipation of smoke-free workplaces seemed to play an important role for the acceptability and meaningfulness of smoke-free school hours for the students. The students seemed to interpret the school’s introduction of the policy as a helping hand to prepare them for the internships and future employment opportunities, that is, being smoke-free or used to cope with a smoke-free workday:

“We have to learn to respect this because, for example, when we will work in kindergartens or such, we are not allowed to smoke. So, I think that the school is trying to help students who smoke to stop because when we start the internships, we are not allowed to smoke either.”

“For my part, I think it is really annoying that we cannot smoke in our breaks here, but on the other hand, many municipalities have implemented smoke-free work hours (…) So we learn here that when we enter the real professional life, we cannot smoke at work”

Thus, when students believed that the school’s intention was to help them become successful in their future professional activities, they accepted the restrictions to their smoking at school.

Social influence and responsibilities associated with the professional role

Despite the prevalent belief of smoking as an individual responsibility, the students generally recognized that one person's smoking habits could socially influence others. This perspective of social influence also emerged in relation to the future professional role, especially among students at the beginning of the education. They associated the professional role with a responsibility towards the citizens such as children and elderly people and reflected on this position as role models and their potential social influence on the citizens in terms of adverse health behaviors:

“We have to think about this in the future. (…) If I go to a kindergarten to work, and I smoke in my break, and the kids can see it. Then they will stand there and look at it and think “I will do that too when I grow up” or something like that. I know that many children might come from families of smokers, that they smoke in their homes. But if they are to see it in the kindergarten, then it has a whole other… Because we are someone they can look up to.“

Thus, these students seemed to accept the rationale behind the introduction of smoke-free school hours, finding it meaningful in the light of their future positions as role models to citizens.

CMO 3. Perceived non-relevance of a smoking-related intervention

CMO 3 describes an identified mechanism reflecting that students essentially did not believe that they needed an intervention to help them become smoke-free, which led students to disapprove of smoke-free school hours interfering with their behavior and individual choices (Fig. 3).

CMO 3. Perceived non-relevance of a smoking-related intervention

Experiencing the school and workplace settings as contrasting

As described in CMO 2, some students compared the school setting to the culture and norms of a workplace which facilitated the acceptability of the tobacco policy. However, other students experienced the school and workplace as completely different settings. The workplace seemed to be attached with a collectively oriented set of values while the school life seemed to be associated with an individual-focused set of values:

“(…) When you are at the workplace, it is something else. You have other people to think about. Here (at school), you only have yourself, now I sound really selfish, but you only have yourself and it is only your own learning you have to consider. I might as well not care about all of you [other students], whether you learn anything or not.”

These individual-focused values associated with the school seemed to be closely related to the belief that smoking is a person’s own responsibility as seen in CMO 1. We found that these different perceptions of the school and work settings inhibited the acceptance of smoke-free school hours as preparation for internships and employment:

“Interviewer: Do you think about it as preparation for internships or work? (…) Does it help you a little, that you can’t smoke here?

Students: No (several students agreeing)

Interviewer: What’s the difference?

Student 1: This is an education. I don’t think they should decide this. I can understand it when you are at the labor market (…) that you can’t smoke there. (…) We will probably learn it ourselves. It’s logical thinking that you can’t smoke when you are with children or elderly people. (…) I think they forget that smoking is your own responsibility. (…) It’s not because we are together with a small child or an elderly man [at school]. Then it doesn’t matter that (…) they have introduced a policy in another place (…) It cannot really go together, that you blow smoke into their heads. I think it’s clear that you can’t smoke in the playground where the children are.”

Although these students were also motivated for the upcoming internships and future profession, they did not seem to believe that they needed help to prepare for this task in advance in terms of their smoking.

Beliefs about own capability of smoking cessation

In the citation above, the students described it as self-evident that they would be able to stop smoking at their future workplace in the presence of the citizens because smoking is viewed as incompatible with being responsible for children or elderly people. This seemed to be related to an underlying belief about own capability of smoking cessation, i.e., that they can stop smoking at any time. Different beliefs about the students’ perceived capabilities of smoking cessation emerged from the dialogues. Some students seemed to believe that they were able to stop smoking at any time, while others believed it to be very hard and demanding to stop smoking:

“If you decide for yourself to start (smoking), then you can also stop again. Almost.”

“You can’t say: ‘Well. Today I’m going to quit’. You can’t. It’s too hard”

These beliefs seemed to influence whether the students believed there was a need for the intervention and thereby whether they accepted the rationale for introducing smoke-free school hours. Thus, when students experienced the school and work as completely different settings and believed they were able to avoid smoking once they started the internship or future job, they did not see a need to introduce smoke-free school hours and interfere with their smoking during the school day. This made them disapprove of the policy.

CMO 4. Low perceived risk and consequences of getting sanctioned for smoking

CMO 4 focuses on an identified mechanism that students do not experience risks or personal consequences of getting sanctioned if they smoke during school hours (Fig. 4). This mechanism could lead to continued smoking during school hours and students finding new places to smoke instead of smoking less during the school day. In the following, we describe how this mechanism could be activated from weak enforcement and sanctioning practices of the school staff, and from a lack of interest in the educational track.

CMO 4. Low perceived risk and consequences of getting sanctioned for smoking

Weak enforcement and sanctioning practices

The students describe different experiences with enforcement and sanctioning of the tobacco policy from mild sanctions such as warnings, having a talk with a teacher etc. to teachers deliberately overlooking students’ smoking. Some students expressed that the teachers’ enforcement was declining over time during the intervention period. The level and modes of enforcement had an impact on the students’ reactions to the policy. When the policy was weakly enforced by the staff, they described how they often went for smoking breaks without even hiding it from the teachers:

“Student 1: Many people have passed by [name of teacher] and have been told, “you can’t do that [smoke]”. But then you go out anyway, and she knows that you go, and then the smokers come back and say “we’ve just gone out for a cigarette” (…)

Interviewer: They look the other way, or…?

Student 3: If you make a rule, a school rule, then you have to hold on and really go through with it instead of being like “we have a rule and you can’t smoke and there will be warnings”, but no warnings are given.

Student 5: And then they have one eye closed, but they look with the other one.”

Thus, the students experienced contradictory messages when the teachers did not enforce the policy in practice as notified. Adding to the contradictory message, some students expressed seeing teachers smoking during school hours despite the policy encompassing staff’s smoking. Further, a student described that the lack of enforcement and sanctions made them “dare” to smoke at school despite the tobacco policy:

“Interviewer: Do you know of anyone who got a warning? (…)

Student 1: Not at all. No. (several students agreeing)

Student 2: I think that’s why you dare to smoke.

Interviewer: Because nothing happens?

Student 2: Because we don’t hear about anyone getting these warnings”

Furthermore, when asked whether they would smoke less during school hours if the policy was enforced to a greater extent, different responses emerged. Some students thought they would smoke less, but other students would probably find other places to smoke:

“Interviewer: What if there were, in fact, sanctions? (…)

Student: Then I would probably go someplace else because they sort of know where we usually smoke (laughs). So, I would probably (…) find a new place to smoke”

“Interviewer: What if there were greater consequences of getting caught smoking?

Student: You always find a way to smoke.”

Lack of interest in the educational track

Among the younger students at the first part of the basic program, the motivation for the educational track, or lack thereof, played a role in their adherence to the tobacco policy. Students who were not really motivated for proceeding at the educational track to be educated as social and health care assistant or pedagogical assistant seemed to be indifferent about getting sanctioned for smoking:

Student 2: But it depends on whether you have the interest (…) I don’t care. But if you want to proceed to the second part of the basic program, then I think you would [stop smoking during school hours] … Yes.

Student 5: (…) As I said, it depends on what your plans are afterwards. Since I’m not doing any of this [proceed to the second part], I might as well smoke. Otherwise, I’m not really motivated to quit.”

Thus, younger students who were not motivated for the educational track were likely to continue their smoking during school hours because they did not perceive a personal consequence of getting sanctioned.

CMO 5. Triggered urge to smoke

CMO 5 describes a mechanism of a triggered urge to smoke leading to continued smoking during school hours (Fig. 5). In the following, we explain how this urge to smoke was triggered by contextual factors.

CMO 5. Triggered urge to smoke

An important finding was that some of the highly nicotine dependent students experienced an enhanced urge to smoke when the teachers repeatedly talked about the tobacco policy during the school activities. Thus, reminding the students not to smoke had the opposite effect, even when the students had a positive disposition towards the intervention:

“Student: It’s also annoying that when the teachers talk about smoking, you really want to smoke a cigarette. I think it’s unfair that they keep on talking about it (…) Everything is about smoking, every time they say something. And it’s annoying because you sit like this (shakes fingers on the table).

Interviewer: And want to smoke a cigarette?

Student: Yes. It doesn't help (…) that they try to help us not focus on smoking when they keep on talking about it.”

Thus, when the teachers talked too much about smoking, the tobacco policy, or the intervention in general, nicotine-dependent students’ potential intentions to comply to smoke-free school hours were counter-acted by triggering an enhanced urge to smoke.

Figure 6 shows a refined program theory based on our empirical findings illustrating the identified contexts, mechanisms, and outcomes of how and why smoke-free school hours worked among social and health care vocational students (Fig. 6). The green dashed arrows illustrate how the mechanisms of trusting the school’s intentions for introducing the policy, and perceived meaningfulness of the policy, can counter-act the negative pathway of perceived violation of personal freedom and autonomy generating disapproval, resistance and/or continued smoking during school hour, and instead, lead to the acceptance of and/or adherence to the policy.

Discussion

Main findings

In this realist-informed qualitative analysis, we identified five Context-Mechanism-Outcome configurations that contribute to a nuanced understanding of how and why smoke-free school hours worked among social and health care vocational students. We identified mechanisms that generated negative outcomes of disapproval of the policy, opposition to the policy, continued smoking during school hours, and increased smoking during school hours: perceived violation of personal freedom and autonomy, low perceived need of a smoking-related intervention, low perceived risk and consequences of getting sanctioned for smoking during school hours, and triggered urge to smoke, shaped and influenced by contextual factors such as strong beliefs about smoking being an individual responsibility, beliefs about own capabilities of smoking cessation, and the school’s communication, enforcement, and sanctioning practices. We also identified mechanisms that facilitated positive outcomes of acceptability and adherence to smoke-free school hours: having faith in the school’s intentions behind introducing the policy as preparation for future professional activities and experiencing the policy as meaningful in the light of the future profession as role models to citizens, that were triggered by contextual factors of orientation towards the future profession, smoke-free work hours at future workplaces, and the social influence and responsibilities associated with the future professional role. These contextual conditions and mechanisms facilitating positive outcomes could disrupt the negative pathways generated from violated personal freedom and autonomy.

Comparison to other studies

In line with findings from other studies, we found a pattern of disapproval of a comprehensive school tobacco policy (in our case smoke-free school hours) related to pre-existing beliefs and attitudes centered around smoking being a matter of personal freedom. Hewer et al. similarly found that 15-year old students from 17 schools across seven European countries justified their smoking behavior at school by reference to their personal autonomy and free will [43]. Similarly, Schreuders et al.’s realist review of how school tobacco policies impact secondary school students’ smoking behavior found that “smoking is an individual’s personal choice and legal right” was the dominant view which could lead to disrespect and disregard of the policy, and opposition to the school’s authority [4]. In addition to the individual-focused beliefs about smoking and tobacco policies, we found that the school environment was associated with a highly individualized set of values that inhibited the acceptability of the policy. These findings may be understood in relation to Katznelson’s analysis of institutional individualization of the Danish educational system [44]. She describes a societal and political process during recent decades characterized by a massive increase in the number of options for young people, and a discourse and tendency that life choices, responsibilities, and the potential consequences thereof, rest on the individual person along with enhanced requirements to be active agents in this process [44]. This strong focus on the individual, she argues, has also entailed conscious individualization processes among the young people in the educational system [44]. Ingholt et al., in a qualitative study, similarly found that Danish vocational students (and teachers) experienced the school setting as highly individualized as shaped by a political reform of 2000 emphasizing individual educational programs and learning processes [45]. Thus, from a sociological point of view, these findings of individualized smoking-related attitudes and views of the school setting as fundamentally individualized might reflect this process of individualization.

An important finding of the present study was that we identified contextual factors and mechanisms that seemed to counter-act, or at least compete, the mechanism of violated personal freedom and autonomy leading to continued smoking, disapproval, and resistance. We found that the students’ professional orientation activated mechanisms of perceived meaningfulness and trust in the school’s intentions which justified the policy and facilitated acceptability and adherence. Schreuders et al. [4] found that aspects of school tobacco policies’ implementation such as strict enforcement facilitated reduced smoking. To our knowledge, no other studies identified contextual factors activating mechanisms to counteract the students’ resistance to school tobacco policies. Further, parallels can be drawn between the motivation and orientation towards the future profession, i.e., working in positions as role models for vulnerable groups, and the concept of ‘vocational identity’. While this concept has roots in developmental psychology [46], vocational or professional identity has previously been described in studies of vocational education and training [47–49]. Ferm et al. investigated the vocational identity formation among Swedish vocational students in relation to the industrial sector [47] and found that this identity was shaped in a dynamic process by the students’ social backgrounds, perceptions of workplace-based learning, and thoughts about the future vocation [47]. Further, Solberg et al. found that having a strong vocational identity contributes to positive mental health among vocational students [49]. These studies imply that vocational identity formation occurs across the spectrum of vocational programs and not only in social and health care vocational schools. As we found that contextual factors related to the future profession and work life facilitated positive mechanisms and outcomes, these contextual conditions may represent potential for successful implementation of smoke-free school hours across vocational school types.

We identified a contextual factor, the notion of “considerate" smoking, contributing to the mechanism of violated personal freedom and autonomy from smoke-free school hours because smoking during breaks became prohibited. The students believed they were not bothering anyone and respected school’s rules and other students by smoking out of sight off school’s ground. Hewer et al. similarly found a “considerate smoking” narrative reflecting, they argue, that students internalize regulatory norms and comply to smoke-free school ground policies, while forming new persistent smoking practices off school’s ground [43]. Thus, this narrative of being a “considerate smoker” might call for special attention since these students may be more prone to resistance to smoke-free school hours.

Most of our CMOs were related to the acceptability of smoke-free school hours. Acceptability as described by Proctor et al. is an important implementation outcome and determinant of implementation success [50]. Further, Sekhon et al. propose a multi-faceted framework of acceptability including the following constructs: affective attitude, burden, perceived effectiveness, ethicality, intervention coherence, opportunity costs, and self-efficacy [51]. The resistance to the policy related to the individualized attitudes and beliefs may be understood in relation to the construct of ethicality, which is defined as “the extent to which the intervention has good fit with an individual’s value system” [51]. Indeed, there were tensions between the participants’ value systems in terms of individualized beliefs and attitudes, and smoke-free school hours restricting their smoking behavior. Thus, working more extensively with the ethicality aspect of students’ acceptability might be a way forward in supporting the implementation of smoke-free school hours into routine practice in vocational schools.

We identified mechanisms related to the staff’s enforcement, sanctioning, and communication practices. In line with Schreuders et al., we found that low perceived risk of getting sanctioned led to continued smoking during school hours and displacement of smoking [4] closely related to experiencing contradictory messages from the staff. Schreuders et al. identified a mechanism activated from experiencing contradictions between schools’ anti-smoking message and actual enforcement practices, i.e., students internalizing the belief that smoking is not all too bad. A similar generative mechanism might be part of the explanation of why students continued their smoking practices at school in our study. We also found that the teachers’ communication of the tobacco policy and the intervention in general sparked an urge to smoke, a mechanism explaining continued and/or increased smoking among highly nicotine dependent students. This unintended outcome can be characterized as a paradoxical effect, i.e., when interventions increase adverse outcomes, they seek to prevent [52]. This finding suggests that the intervention component of providing access to support to handle a smoke-free school day did not reach the highly nicotine dependent students as intended despite a high level of implementation (delivery and receipt) among social and health care students [12].

Methodological considerations

The realist approach allowed for context-sensitive knowledge of the generative processes by which smoke-free school hours produced change among the students. However, the interviews were not conducted with the purpose of realist evaluation. For this reason, we were only able to use CMOs in the analysis and not in the collection of data. This study can be characterized as a secondary analysis of data collected for a previous study to explore new research questions and analytical strategies [34]. In line with recommendations to improve the rigor of secondary analysis [34], a postdoctoral researcher who conducted the original study (DD) and a senior researcher not involved in the original study with expertise in realist evaluation (MBJ) were involved in the analytical work, and the uncoded data material was analyzed. Moreover, the first author conducted one of the focus group interviews and thus was familiar with and had firsthand knowledge of the data material. A strength of the analytical process was that all co-authors were involved in the analytical work in a collaborative analysis [53]. An advantage of the data material was the application of the semi-structured focus group interview and the use of position cards to stimulate dialogues between the students. This approach allowed for insights into the interpersonal dynamics between the students, generating responses that might not have come to mind in individual interviews [54]. Our access to knowledge about context is shaped by the prompts of the semi-structured interview guide. Other questions could have provided access to other aspects of context such as the wider socioeconomic circumstances, social relations, or the students’ engagements outside the school environment etc. We did not develop an initial program theory with explicit pre-defined CMOs. As the rationale for this study sprung from a previously identified outcome pattern [11], our approach was focused on understanding the underlying generative processes of this outcome pattern, and thus, our analytical strategy was more data-driven. In recent years, the field of realist evaluation has evolved greatly and methods such as realist interviewing have appeared [55] where participants are presented with program theories which are discussed in a “teacher-learner cycle” [56]. Such methods can enhance evaluators’ theoretical insights through applying theory-building phases: theory gleaning, theory refining, and theory consolidation [55]. Using such interview techniques could have provided access to other aspects of the participants’ interactions with and perspectives on the intervention through direct involvement of the participants in a collaborative approach of exploring and refining program theories. A limitation of the present study is that it focuses exclusively on the school type where the intervention was most successfully implemented. Comparisons with schools with less successful trajectories could have captured a wider range of barriers and challenges alongside favorable factors. Such systematic comparisons are beyond the scope of this paper but could have added valuable insights. However, focusing on the most successful school type allows for an in-depth exploration of mechanisms and enabling conditions that could be useful to support real-world implementation of similar interventions in similar settings.

Implications for practice and future research perspectives

The findings of the present study carry important implications for practice. In Denmark, smoke-free school hours were mandated by law as of 2021 in all Danish education institutions with students under 18 years. Thus, this tobacco policy is now mandatory in Danish vocational and preparatory schools marking a significant step for school smoking prevention in Denmark. However, recent studies indicate poor implementation of the policy in these schools [57]. For example, 71% of daily or occasional smokers in vocational and preparatory school still smoked cigarettes during school hours in 2023 [58]. This indicates a potential to support the implementation of the policy into routine practice to ensure the intended outcomes of reduced smoking. Thus, our findings can be used to develop and tailor implementation strategies [59, 60], or adapt existing initiatives, to the contextual conditions of social and health care vocational schools to enhance the implementation of smoke-free school hours. Moreover, stakeholders may use the findings in everyday efforts to implement the policy. Our findings point to several relevant aspects to be considered in such processes. First, our findings suggest that addressing the students’ acceptability should be a key priority. Especially the tension between students’ beliefs of smoking as a personal matter and the comprehensive restrictions to their smoking. Our findings point to a potential for schools to work with communicative strategies, e.g., related to the professional orientations and motivation of the students to disrupt the negative pathway of perceived violated autonomy. For example, teachers and staff could facilitate class discussions about the social influence of the professional role in terms of adverse health behaviors etc. Secondly, our findings call for more private workplaces to implement comprehensive tobacco policies to complement and support the effectiveness of smoke-free school hours at vocational schools. We found that the anticipation of attending a future workplace that was smoke-free facilitated the acceptability of and adherence to the school’s policy. A strategy could be for schools or municipalities to establish partnerships between vocational schools and related local workplaces to strengthen this relationship and urge companies to take part in the responsibility for supporting smoke-free environments for young people. Finally, this study provides important insights about how asmoke-free school hours policy should be communicated to students at vocational schools. On one hand, and in line with previous research, this study shows that strict and consistent enforcement and sanctioning is crucial to achieve the intended effects of reduced smoking, but on the other hand, our findings suggest that the policy can be extensively communicated, e.g., teachers talking too much about smoking and reminding students not to smoke during class activities, leading to unintended consequences of negative emotions and continued or increased smoking. In line with previous studies, enabling social activities during breaks as alternatives to smoking might be a useful strategy to keep students’ focus off the cigarettes [48, 61]. Ideally, implementation strategies should be tailored to the unique contextual conditions of each school [59]. However, our findings may be transferable to other school settings nationally and internationally with similar contextual conditions [37]. For example, some of the identified contextual conditions shaping the mechanisms and outcomes may be universal across other types of vocational schools such as the ‘vocational identity formation’ [47], institutional individualization process [44], and attitudes and beliefs of smoking being a personal matter [5, 43]. Preparatory schools, that are not directed at a specific vocation, and target students in need of extra support and upgrading to proceed in the educational system or labor market, may be characterized by different contextual conditions. Thus, further research is needed to gain an understanding of the context of preparatory schools and students. Future research should also investigate a broader spectrum of vocational schools considering their unique contextual conditions – especially those with the greatest challenges of implementing school tobacco policies.

Conclusions

This study provides concrete insights into how and why a smoke-free school hours policy produces outcomes among vocational students in social and health care education. We found that the policy triggered disapproval and continued or increased smoking particularly in contexts characterized by strong smoking norms and limited perceived legitimacy of the policy. In contrast, acceptability and adherence were facilitated in contexts where the policy aligned with professional identity formation and was consistently enforced. These findings demonstrate that the effects of comprehensive school tobacco policies are not uniform but depend on how policy mechanisms interact with existing social norms, institutional practices, and student identities. Importantly, the study shows that strategies focusing solely on formal policy adoption are insufficient; attention must also be paid to contextual readiness, perceived fairness, and the availability of supportive resources. By applying a realist evaluation approach within a randomized controlled trial, this study illustrates the value of qualitative, theory-driven inquiry for uncovering mechanisms that explain both intended and unintended policy outcomes.
