The case for contextually grounded executive function measurement
Authors: Mateus M. Mazzaferro, Jelena Obradović, Sabine Doebel, Sharon Wolf, Yuko Munakata, Lisa B. Thorell, Michael Willoughby, Steven J. Howard
Published: 2026

It is now well established that traditional executive function (EF) tasks developed in Western contexts do not validly assess EF in non-Western contexts. Extensive literature documents why that is the case and how to address it (1–4). We appreciate that Kroupin et al. (5) (hereafter the authors) showed how such EF tasks also failed to yield valid data in a nonschooled context, but we question their conclusion that “we must decide between using the term EF to describe 1) universal capacities or 2) the culturally specific skill set reflected in performance on typical tasks.” (5). We argue that an integrated model of developmental universality with specificity is more psychometrically sound and useful for science and practice (6).

Under classical test theory, psychological assessments should maximize construct variance and minimize error in a given context. When contexts change, the proportion of construct-relevant to construct-irrelevant variance in assessments shifts, potentially rendering the test score uninterpretable. Previous research on theory of mind (ToM) exemplifies this idea (7). If directly employed in nonschooled settings, the original Sally-Anne ToM test would yield scores reflecting little construct-relevant (ToM) and much irrelevant (e.g., familiarity with names, narrative conventions, social norms) information. It would be premature to conclude based on those hypothetical data that we must decide between using the term ToM to describe a universal capacity or a culturally specific skill set reflected in performance on that task. Similarly, the authors’ results are not sufficient to tease universal and specific aspects of EF apart. Cross-cultural ToM researchers recognize this limitation and work to ensure that assessments are contextually adapted before suggesting universality or specificity of ToM (7).

In support of the universal relevance of EF, we amplify the authors’ recognition that the cognitive capacities under the umbrella of EF evolved early in the human phylogenetic tree and are shared across many species, especially primates (8, 9).* Importantly, this evidence does not conflict with the idea that EF varies in how it develops and is deployed according to social and cultural demands (3, 10). While these cognitive capacities are fundamental and widely shared, “culture-agnostic” EF assessments are neither possible nor desirable. Whereas an American child may engage EF to learn long division, a Himba child may engage EF to learn herding. Adequately measuring EF and understanding both its shared and unique applications is critical for designing effective practices and supports that promote such culturally relevant competencies (2, 6).

In sum, it does not follow that “EF [...] can describe universal capacities or culturally specific performance on typical tasks—but not both” (5). Rather, the authors’ results contribute to a large body of evidence suggesting that contextually misaligned tasks fail to capture how children engage EFs. Our aspiration, thus, should be to adapt and expand our measurement approaches, ensuring assessments capture construct-relevant variance locally to accurately assess EF and its applications across diverse settings and goals.
