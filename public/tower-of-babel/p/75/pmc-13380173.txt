Preliminary effectiveness of the OurFutures alcohol and cannabis prevention program in German schools
Authors: Jeanette Röhrig, Lorenz Sutter, Lena Rademacher, Vanessa-Emily Müller-Schoch, Franziska Träuble, Antonia Groß, Danja Bogdan, Sabrina Stebe-Frick, Hannes Schierle, Lauren A. Gardner, Katrina E. Champion, Maurice Cabanis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsychoactive substance use often begins during adolescence, and early initiation increases risks of problematic use and adverse health outcomes. Few evidence-based, technology-supported substance use prevention programs for adolescents exist in Europe. OurFutures (previously Climate Schools) is a web-based substance use prevention program developed in Australia that has been shown to improve substance-related knowledge and strengthen refusal and harm-minimization skills. This study aimed to adapt the OurFutures Alcohol and Cannabis course for German schools and to evaluate its feasibility and preliminary effectiveness. To strengthen the link between prevention and early intervention, the program was connected to the Mobile Online Portal for Questions on Addiction (MOFA), enabling students to access counseling services via chat, email, or telephone.MethodsThe Alcohol and Cannabis course was adapted for the German school and addiction systems, and the online portal was created. Effectiveness and implementation were assessed in four German secondary schools using a controlled pilot study with quantitative surveys, complemented by exploratory qualitative feedback from a focus group (n = 2) and three individual interviews.ResultsAnalyses of covariance demonstrated that the intervention group's knowledge about alcohol (n = 62) and cannabis (n = 63) increased significantly more than of controls (n = 28 and 31). No significant differences were observed for attitudes or intentions to use. Most students evaluated the Alcohol module positively and relevant. Qualitative findings indicated satisfaction among students and teachers, with suggestions for school implementation.ConclusionThis blended-learning program increased substance-related knowledge and may strengthen school-based prevention in Germany. Wider implementation requires coordination between education and addiction systems.

Background

According to the World Health Organization (WHO; Charrier et al., 2024), the use of psychoactive substances in childhood and adolescence continues to represent a major global public health concern. Substance use disorders (SUDs) impose a substantial health burden on adolescents and young adults aged 10–24 years and are characterized by marked demographic and geographic inequities (Chen et al., 2025). The initiation of alcohol, nicotine, cannabis, and illicit substance use typically occurs during adolescence or early adulthood (World Health Organization, 2018; World Health Organization. Regional Office for Europe, 2019). The WHO reports that the timing of first use critically shapes subsequent health and social outcomes of drug use (United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime, 2025). Early onset of substance use has been linked to later dependence, psychiatric comorbidities, and adverse psychosocial outcomes (Behrendt et al., 2009; Dawson et al., 2008; Gobbi et al., 2019; Hamaoui et al., 2025; Subodh et al., 2019). It is also associated with academic and occupational difficulties (Volkow et al., 2014; Zhu et al., 2025), internalizing and externalizing problems (Griffith-Lendering et al., 2011), vehicular accidents (Schulte and Hser, 2013), and engagement in risk behaviors (Gardner et al., 2024; Poulin and Graham, 2001; Shorey et al., 2015). Neurobiological evidence further suggests that the heightened vulnerability of the still developing adolescent brain may exacerbate these detrimental effects of early substance exposure (Albaugh et al., 2021; Guerri and Pascual, 2010; Hamidullah et al., 2020; Hoch et al., 2025; Meruelo et al., 2017).

The importance of prevention and early intervention for substance use during childhood and adolescence is well established (Bühler and Bühringer, 2018; Horn, 2020). The International Standards on Drug Use Prevention (United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime and the World Health Organization, 2018) highlight that effective prevention begins early, is developmentally tailored, and operates across contexts (family, school, community, policy). These standards emphasize that interactive, competence-based programs with strong quality assurance show the most durable effects.

A systematic literature review commissioned by the German Federal Centre for Health Education identified evidence-based measures across multiple intervention settings (Bühler et al., 2021). Schools represent the most extensively studied context, with intervention effectiveness varying by age, substance, and approach. Overall, prevention programs are found to be most effective when targeted to specific age groups using age-appropriate methods (Onrust et al., 2016). Evidence-based school prevention programs typically focus on life skills training, resilience building, parental involvement, and peer education.

Digital approaches have gained increasing attention in recent years because technology-based delivery formats offer potential advantages in terms of reach, acceptance, and cost-effectiveness (Murray, 2012; O Rourke et al., 2016; Riper et al., 2018). They are also associated with greater user engagement, destigmatization, accessibility, and need-based targeting (Monarque et al., 2023). However, current digital interventions, especially in school settings, for adolescents remain limited in number and scope, as highlighted by recent systematic reviews (Champion et al., 2016; Distelkamp et al., 2021; Hopson et al., 2015; Ondersma et al., 2019).

The Australian OurFutures prevention model, previously known as Climate Schools (Newton et al., 2009a), is one of the most extensively evaluated digital, school-based substance use prevention programs. It combines social influence theory, knowledge dissemination, and harm reduction principles to enhance students' knowledge and skills to prevent or reduce substance use and improve their physical and mental health. The program includes several modules addressing alcohol, cannabis, vaping, psychostimulants, MDMA and emerging drugs, mental health, and broader health behaviors. Each course consists of 4 to 6 lessons combining an online comic with teacher-facilitated classroom activities. The efficacy of the OurFutures prevention model has been evaluated in 11 cluster-randomized controlled trials (RCTs) including over 29,000 students across 326 Australian schools. All studies utilized active control groups receiving the standard health education curriculum, which is mandatory in Australia. Two RCTs of the Alcohol module demonstrated significant improvements in alcohol-related knowledge, reductions in consumption, and decreases in binge drinking among students who received the intervention (Newton et al., 2009b; Vogl et al., 2009). Five additional RCTs evaluating the Alcohol and Cannabis course (Alcohol module + Alcohol and Cannabis module) reported enhanced substance-related knowledge, lower frequency of use, decreased binge drinking, increased resistance to peer pressure, reduced psychological distress, and decreased school absenteeism as a result of the intervention (Champion et al., 2016; Newton et al., 2010; Newton et al., 2014a; Teesson et al., 2020). Most recently, a cluster RCT of the OurFutures Vaping module in 40 schools demonstrated a 65% reduction in the odds of past-12-month e-cigarette use after 12 months among students who received the program versus control (Gardner et al., 2025). OurFutures has also been adapted and implemented beyond Australia, including in the United Kingdom (Newton et al., 2014b) and Colombia (Egan et al., 2024), suggesting it may also offer a useful prevention model in the German context.

Despite the growing evidence supporting school-based and digital prevention approaches, the number of rigorously evaluated programs implemented in European school settings remains limited (Agabio et al., 2015). In Germany, the implementation of school-based substance use prevention is characterized by considerable structural heterogeneity. Education policy, including health education and prevention in schools, is largely the responsibility of the federal states, resulting in substantial variation in prevention activities across regions (FINDER Akademie, 2020; Kultusministerkonferenz, 2012). While national institutions provide general recommendations and guidelines, the selection and implementation of prevention programs are typically decided at the level of individual schools (Deutsche Beobachtungsstelle für Drogen und Drogensucht, 2022). Consequently, schools may choose from a wide range of programs that differ in content, quality, and level of scientific evaluation (Pütz et al., 2010). This decentralized structure can lead to inconsistent implementation of evidence-based prevention and highlights the need for rigorously evaluated, scalable programs that can be implemented more systematically within the German school context. Most recently, the partial legalization of cannabis in 2024 brought the need for effective regulation and addiction prevention strategies back into focus (Robert Koch-Institut, 2025). Together, these structural characteristics highlight the need for scalable, evidence-based prevention programs that can be systematically implemented in the German school context. Programs developed in other countries require careful evaluation when implemented in new educational and cultural contexts (Castro et al., 2004).

Objectives

The present study aimed to (1) evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of implementing the OurFutures Alcohol and Cannabis course in German secondary schools (German: Gymnasien), and (2) explore preliminary effects on substance-related knowledge and behavioral outcomes using a controlled pilot design. These two modules were selected because the two substances are (next to nicotine) the most commonly used among adolescents, and the vaping module was not yet available at the time the study was initiated.

Methods

Guidelines and ethical considerations

The study adhered to the TREND (Transparent Report ing of Evaluations with Non-randomized Designs; Des Jarlais et al., 2004) guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Study design

The project was originally planned for a duration of 36 months (March 1, 2021, to February 29, 2024). Due to scheduling delays, implementation took place between May 1, 2021, and April 30, 2024.

To ensure expert guidance and explore strategies for long-term implementation and sustainability, a project steering committee was established (see Supplementary).

The project was initially structured into four substudies. Substudy 1 was conducted as a controlled pilot trial to evaluate program effectiveness. Substudies 2 and 4 were originally planned as focus group studies conducted before and after the intervention to inform program adaptation and further development of the digital platforms. Due to resource constraints, these two substudies were merged into a single study (substudy 2), and focus groups were conducted only after completion of substudy 1. Substudy 3 evaluated the training sessions provided for teachers and peer advisors. The present paper reports the methods and results of substudies 1 and 2. Findings from substudy 3 will be reported elsewhere.

Substudy 1 (controlled trial)

Substudy 1 was designed as a controlled pilot trial evaluating the effectiveness of the OurFutures program in German schools. Twelve school classes from four secondary schools (three classes per school) participated in the study. Six classes were allocated to the intervention condition, while six classes served as controls and continued to receive the standard prevention activities provided by their schools (see Supplementary). Allocation to the intervention condition was based on organizational considerations and the availability of teachers to implement the program during their lessons.

Students in both groups completed online surveys using the online survey platform SoSci Survey (SoSci Survey GmbH, Munich) at five measurement points over a one-year period (Fig. 1). Measurement points were aligned with the intervention schedule in the intervention group: T0 represented baseline before the Alcohol module; T1 followed completion of the Alcohol module; T2 was a follow-up assessment conducted approximately 18 weeks after the Alcohol module and before the Alcohol and Cannabis module; T3 followed completion of the Alcohol and Cannabis module; T4 was a final follow-up approximately 18 weeks after the Alcohol and Cannabis module.Fig. 1Overview of the study design and assessment schedule across the two-year study period.Fig. 1

Overview of the study design and assessment schedule across the two-year study period.

Baseline assessments (T0) were conducted at three different time points due to the staggered implementation of the study: late 2021 (three classes), spring 2022 (four classes), and late 2022 to early 2023 (five classes).

In the preregistration, the study design was mistakenly described as a stepped-wedge design. However, as the study included a control group that did not receive the intervention during the study period, this classification was inaccurate. To account for potential differences resulting from staggered baseline assessments, the starting time of each class was included as a covariate in the analyses.

Schools and participants

Schools were recruited in several stages. Initially, twelve secondary schools (Gymnasien) in neighborhoods near the center of Stuttgart were contacted via email with information about the project and an invitation to participate, but no schools expressed interest. In a second step, the school social workers from the respective schools (n = 7) were contacted. However, all of them declined participation due to limited capacity. In a third step, the school principals of the twelve schools were contacted by telephone. Ultimately, four schools (33.3%) agreed to participate, while others declined due to time constraints.

Prior to implementation, preparatory meetings were held with school representatives (e.g., principals, school social workers, or prevention officers) to discuss integration of the program into existing school structures. These meetings were conducted online due to COVID-19-related restrictions and organizational considerations.

Eligible participants were students aged ≥14 years enrolled in grades 9 or 10. Teachers informed students about the study during class and distributed study information materials. Participation in the evaluation was voluntary and required written informed consent from the students' legal guardians. The project was also presented during parent information meetings to inform parents and guardians and address potential questions.

Procedures

Intervention

The OurFutures Alcohol and Cannabis course was selected, consisting of the six-lesson (45 min each) Alcohol module and the six-lesson Alcohol and Cannabis module. The course content can be found in Newton et al., 2009a, Newton et al., 2009b.

Each module includes two components. First, students engage with an online comic that communicates evidence-based information on substance use and promotes assertiveness and refusal skills through storylines reflecting adolescents' everyday experiences. Second, teachers facilitate classroom activities designed to reinforce the content, including discussions, role plays, and recap exercises. At the end of each comic episode, students complete a short quiz assessing their understanding and receive a summary of the key learning points.

For implementation in German schools, all materials were translated into German by a professional translation agency and subsequently adapted by the project team to the German educational and addiction care context. Adaptations included adjustments to cultural references, legal frameworks, terminology, statistics, and references to local addiction support services.

The lessons were delivered via a secure digital learning platform, which also integrated the MOFA platform (German: Mobiles Online-Portal für Fragen zu Abhängigkeitserkrankungen, English: Mobile Online Portal for Questions on Addiction Disorders). MOFA was developed by the project team and offered students direct and confidential access to professional addiction counseling services provided by Klinikum Stuttgart via email, chat, or telephone with the aim of strengthening the link between prevention and early intervention. The platform served as a low-threshold, evidence-informed contact point that could be used anonymously if desired. Students could use MOFA to ask follow-up questions related to classroom content, seek advice regarding their own substance use, or obtain support in helping peers or family members. In each comic episode, students were informed about the availability of the MOFA platform.

In addition to the core prevention program, interested students were invited to participate in an online peer advisor training. This short intervention consisted of a 45-min presentation including brief role-play exercises aimed at enabling students to support peers and encourage them to seek professional help when needed.

Depending on the organizational structure of the participating schools, lessons were facilitated by homeroom teachers or biology teachers during homeroom periods, regular classes, or designated prevention sessions.

Students assigned to the intervention group received an individual access link via email allowing them to log into the online platform. The start page introduced the two program modules and provided an overview of the program sequence (lesson introduction → comic → quiz → classroom session; see Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Each module was designed to be completed over an 8-week period, with approximately one session per week. Students were required to complete the Alcohol module before accessing the Alcohol and Cannabis module.

In addition, students who completed a module were invited to evaluate the program using a questionnaire adapted from the OurFutures evaluation instrument. Moreover, participants' contact with the support system (via email, chat, or telephone) was recorded. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27). The analytical approach differed from the preregistered protocol because the preregistration mistakenly described the study as a stepped-wedge design. In addition, there were more missing values due to non-participation at individual measurement time points than previously expected. Given the high rate of missing values and the small sample size, the imputation of missing data can be prone to bias. Thus, to reduce data loss, mean scores were calculated for the measurement points before the intervention and after the intervention for each student. These aggregated scores were used to examine changes over time. To assess the clustering of students within school classes, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) at baseline was calculated. Analyses of covariance (ANCOVA) were conducted with the post-intervention score as dependent variable, group as between-subject factor, and the pre-intervention score as covariate. To account for potential differences resulting from staggered study entry, another covariate representing the three baseline start periods (end of 2021, spring 2022, end of 2022/ beginning of 2023) was included in the analyses. In addition, independent-samples t-tests or chi-square tests were conducted to compare intervention and control groups at each of the five individual measurement points. As sample sizes varied across time points due to missing data, these analyses should be interpreted cautiously and are reported as descriptive complements to the ANCOVA results rather than as indicators of longitudinal change.

Substudy 2 (focus groups)

Substudy 2 aimed to explore the perceptions and experiences of students, teachers, peer advisors, and parents/guardians regarding the OurFutures program and its implementation within the German school context. The qualitative findings were intended to complement the quantitative evaluation and to inform potential program adaptations for future implementation.

Sample size and recruitment

Four focus groups with four participants each were originally planned (students, teachers, peer advisors, and parents/guardians). Separate inclusion criteria were defined for each group. Students were required to have participated in the Alcohol and/or Alcohol and Cannabis module. Teachers had to have completed the teacher training and implemented at least one module. Peer advisors were required to have participated in the peer advisor training and the program modules. Parents/guardians had to be the parent or guardian of a participating student.

Recruitment for the focus groups took place at the end of the intervention phase through direct outreach to potential participants. Following the T3 and T4 assessments, students, peer advisors, and implementing teachers were informed by the evaluation staff from substudy 1 about the opportunity to participate in substudy 2 and, if interested, received more detailed information. In addition, students (n = 98), peer advisors (n = 12), and teachers (n = 10) were invited via email. To recruit parents/guardians, contact persons representing the teachers at the four participating schools (n = 4) were asked to forward an email invitation to class parent representatives, who distributed it to parents/guardians. Individuals who expressed interest (students: n = 2; peers: n = 1; teachers: n = 2; parents/guardians: n = 1) were subsequently contacted by the research team via email to arrange participation in individual interviews or the focus group. Participation was voluntary.

Data collection

Semi-structured interview guides were developed for each participant group. The guides covered program design and technical implementation, program content, experiences with the comics and classroom activities, the role of peer advisors, the use of the MOFA platform, and implementation in everyday school practice.

The interviews and focus group were conducted by a psychologist with clinical expertise in addiction prevention who was specifically employed for the qualitative component of the study. All sessions were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Transcripts were checked for accuracy and pseudonymized.

Qualitative analysis

Qualitative data were analyzed using MAXQDA Plus 2020 (VERBI Software, 2022). A qualitative content analysis with a summarizing approach following Mayring (2022) was applied. Coding was primarily deductive, based on the predefined thematic areas of the interview guides, and complemented by inductive coding for emerging themes not captured by the predefined categories.

Results

Substudy 1

Substudy 1 began in December 2021 and was completed at the end of April 2024. Students from two intervention classes completed the first survey (T0) in April 2022. These students finished the Alcohol module in July 2022 and the Alcohol and Cannabis module in June 2023. The final survey (T4) was conducted with students of one class in November 2023 and with those of the other class in February 2024. Students of four additional intervention classes commenced in December 2022 (T0), completing the Alcohol module between May and July 2023. In three of these classes, the Alcohol and Cannabis module was completed in December 2023 and the final survey (T4) was carried out in April 2024. In the fourth class, the Alcohol and Cannabis module could not be delivered due to teacher availability. Students of control classes started with the first survey in December 2021, April 2022 or December 2022 and finished the final survey between May 2023 and April 2024.

Satisfaction with the alcohol module

A total of 21 students evaluated the Alcohol module. 76.2% of students liked the comics (see Supplementary Fig. 5 for details). 65.0% of students rated the comics as relevant to current or future experiences in their lives or in the lives of their peers and as a generally entertaining way to learn the theory. 61.9% of students said that the information in the comics was not new to them. Nevertheless, 70.0% of the students planned to apply the information they learned in the prevention program in their own lives.

Contact with the support system

The use of direct contact with MOFA via the online platform was also evaluated among the secondary target criteria. During the project period, one student was successfully referred to the regional addiction support system after contacting MOFA.

Substudy 2

Data collection took place between January and February 2024. Despite extended recruitment efforts, participation remained limited. Ultimately, one individual student interview (n = 1), two individual peer advisor interviews (n = 2; one online and one face-to-face), and one online focus group with two teachers (n = 2) were conducted. A planned parent interview could not be realized.

The focus group lasted 66 min, and the individual interviews lasted approximately 35 min. Two members of the research team independently coded the transcripts and discussed discrepancies until consensus was reached. Across all transcripts, 355 coded statements were identified. The resulting coding framework comprised four main thematic domains: program design, program content, implementation in school practice, and overall evaluation of the program.

Program design

Participants evaluated the design of the prevention program positively. In particular, the visual presentation, accessibility, and usability of the online platform and comics were appreciated by teachers, peer advisors, and the participating student. One teacher noted that the program was “very easy to handle and visually very accessible for students.” Similarly, the student and peer advisors described the comics as easy to understand and relatable to everyday situations. A peer advisor also highlighted the classroom activities, stating: “I really liked the role-playing exercise because it was something different from the typical school routine.” At the same time, some participants indicated that certain elements of the storyline were not always perceived as fully realistic for all student groups. Technical barriers were mentioned occasionally, particularly difficulties related to forgotten passwords.

Program content

Participants were generally satisfied with the content of the prevention program and considered the selected topics relevant and covering a broad range of substance-related issues. The provision of new information was perceived positively. As one peer advisor noted, “I was actually very satisfied. You learned a lot that you hadn't heard about before.” A small number of suggestions for improvement were raised. For example, one peer advisor recommended placing greater emphasis on help-seeking options and available support services.

Implementation in schools practice

Participants expressed mixed views regarding the integration of the program into everyday school practice. Some considered the program well suited for grades 9 and 10, noting that students at this age are already confronted with alcohol and substance use in their everyday environment and therefore show strong interest in the topics. As the participating student explained, the program worked well in a stable class group where “it is easier to talk about such topics.” At the same time, participants highlighted organizational challenges. In particular, teachers noted that implementing the program required replacing regular teaching time, which sometimes raised concerns about covering the required curriculum. Suggestions for improvement included introducing the Alcohol module earlier (e.g., in grade 8), adjusting the scheduling and length of modules, and providing clearer information on the expected time requirements. Participants also noted that support from school leadership and dedicated time resources (e.g., homeroom or prevention lessons) could facilitate implementation.

Overall evaluation of the program

Prior to the intervention, participants reported mostly neutral expectations or curiosity about the program, particularly regarding the cannabis-related content. Overall, the program was evaluated positively. Participants highlighted the relevance of the topics, the interactive learning approach, and the amount of information provided. They also appreciated that the program addressed substance use over a longer period of time rather than as a single, isolated prevention activity.

However, some barriers were also mentioned, including limited time resources, the perceived simplicity of the comic format for some classes, and the additional workload for teachers. Suggestions for improvement included expanding the range of teaching formats (e.g., videos or expert input), covering additional substances, and integrating the program more firmly into the school curriculum, for example through dedicated prevention days or regular school activities.

Discussion

This pilot study provides preliminary indications for the effectiveness and acceptability of the OurFutures Alcohol and Cannabis prevention program when implemented in the German school context. Students who received the intervention demonstrated significantly greater knowledge about both alcohol and cannabis compared with students in the control group, with large effect sizes. However, effects were very small to small and statistically not significant for alcohol or cannabis consumption, attitudes, or intentions. Overall, the Alcohol module was well received by the students, and qualitative findings also indicated positive perceptions of the program.

The finding of increased alcohol- and cannabis-related knowledge in the intervention group is aligned with the results of previous studies that evaluated the English-language version of the Alcohol and Alcohol and Cannabis modules in Australia (Champion et al., 2016; Newton et al., 2009a, Newton et al., 2014a; Newton et al., 2010; Teesson et al., 2020). However, the present findings could not demonstrate statistically significant reductions in alcohol or cannabis consumption among students who received the intervention as previously shown in some of the Australian studies (Newton et al., 2009b; Vogl et al., 2009). One factor that may have played a role here is that the intervention and control group already differed at baseline with the intervention group reporting higher levels of substance use (small to medium effect size). Over the course of the study, the groups became more similar, with the control group slightly surpassing the intervention group in reported consumption at later time points, but the effect size of this difference was small and statistically non-significant. Given the small sample size and limited statistical power of this pilot study, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Longer-term follow-up studies are needed to examine behavioral changes in response to preventive interventions. The questions regarding consumption referred to the past three months, so changes in this indicator are expected to be delayed.

Regarding changes in attitude towards cannabis, we found only a small, statistically not significant difference between intervention and control groups. This is consistent with studies from Australia which could not detect significant effects at 6- and 12-month follow-ups (Newton et al., 2009a; Newton et al., 2010). In addition, the effects on intention to use were very small for alcohol and small for cannabis, and neither was statistically significant. This aligns with Australian evidence showing no significant group differences. One possible explanation is that the primary aim of school-based prevention programs is not necessarily to achieve complete abstinence from substance use, but rather to promote a responsible and reflective approach to alcohol and cannabis. From this perspective, pronounced differences between intervention and control groups may not be expected. However, it should be noted that the values, particularly for the intention to use cannabis, were overall very low. This suggests a potential floor effect and may indicate that the intention to use cannabis was generally low in the sample and remained stable over time. Furthermore, significant differences between groups were already present at baseline, which further complicates the interpretation of the findings.

The evaluation of the Alcohol module revealed that participants liked the comic format and perceived the content as relevant to their everyday lives. Although students indicated that the information presented was not entirely new to them, most stated that they intended to apply the knowledge gained from the program in their own lives. This finding is consistent with results from Australia, which also showed very positive evaluations (Newton et al., 2009a; Newton et al., 2010; Vogl et al., 2009). The focus group and individual interview findings confirmed the evaluation results, with respondents expressing a positive impression of the program. In addition, they provided supplementary insights into the perception and potential implementation of the program. Due to the qualitative methodology and the small number of participants, these findings cannot be considered generalizable but provide valuable indications for further development and future implementation strategies. Participants suggested, for example, introducing the Alcohol module at an earlier stage and integrating the program as a fixed component of school prevention curricula with dedicated instructional time. They also highlighted the value of addressing substance-related topics over a longer period of time rather than through isolated prevention activities. Furthermore, participants emphasized that administrative and time-related barriers, particularly those associated with evaluation procedures during the study period, should be minimized in future implementations. Data about the utilization of the contact form to the addiction counseling center revealed that one student was successfully connected to local addiction support services through the integrated online counseling platform. It remains unclear whether additional students sought support through alternative pathways (e.g., directly contacting services recommended in the program materials), as this could not be systematically assessed within the scope of the current evaluation. Given the small number of documented referrals, these findings do not allow for conclusion regarding the effectiveness or overall reach of MOFA, but may be considered a first preliminary indication that integrated digital components can facilitate access to support services in individual cases (Murray, 2012; O Rourke et al., 2016; Riper et al., 2018; Romero et al., 2026).

Several limitations must be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study deviated from the preregistered protocol. Due to limited financial and personnel resources, substudies originally planned as separate components were merged, and analyses could not be performed in the manner described. Second, the sample size was small in both substudies. In substudy 1, this resulted in limited statistical power to detect intervention effects, particularly for behavioral outcomes such as substance use. Recruitment and retention of schools and students proved challenging, partly due to the difficulty of integrating research activities into routine school operations or the high ethical requirements (written consent from both parents/guardians). These challenges were further exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic, which disrupted regular teaching schedules and reduced available time resources within schools. Participation in the evaluation surveys was particularly challenging in the control group, which appeared less motivated to participate in repeated assessments. As a result, the control group sample was smaller than the intervention group at most measurement points. Only at the final measurement point participation could be increased by providing an additional incentive (shared pizza during the survey administration in the classroom). These differences in participation rates should be considered when interpreting the results. In substudy 2, the small sample size limits the breadth of perspectives captured and reduces the interpretability and transferability of the qualitative findings. Third, due to ethical regulations, only students aged 14 years and older were eligible for inclusion. However, prior research suggests that the optimal timing for prevention is at a younger age and that there are developmental periods in which prevention may have no effect or even adverse effects (Onrust et al., 2016). Therefore, future projects should consider a lower or more differentiated age threshold. Fourth, the study could not randomly assign classes as participation depended on the availability and willingness of individual teachers to implement the intervention during regular lessons. This may have led to selection bias. Because participation was voluntary, both teachers and students who agreed to participate may have differed systematically from those who did not, for example, in their motivation or interest in prevention. The intervention and control group differed on several outcome measures at baseline, including risky alcohol use, the attitude about cannabis use, and the intention to use alcohol and cannabis, which complicates the interpretation of the changes over time. Fifth, the implementation of the intervention varied across schools. Because the German school system is highly decentralized, schools differ substantially in organizational structures, available resources, and prevention practices. Even within the same city and school type, considerable heterogeneity in implementation conditions was observed. These differences required extensive coordination with teachers and school administrators and resulted in varying implementation contexts. Sixth, the sample consisted exclusively of students attending a Gymnasium. In Germany, compared to other types of schools, this type of school is typically associated with a student body that is socioeconomically more advantaged and has a lower prevalence of substance use. This socioeconomic selection may have overestimated the effectiveness of the program, and the results may not be generalizable to students in other educational tracks. Seventh, the interval between modules differed from the schedule used in the original Australian trials. While the program was originally designed to deliver modules approximately 6–12 months apart, logistical constraints resulted in shorter intervals in the present study. These differences may have influenced the observed outcomes. Finally, due to limited funding, it was not possible to conduct long-term follow-up assessments at 6 or 12 months or longer after completion of the intervention. Previous evaluations of the OurFutures program have shown that some behavioral effects emerge at later follow-up points (Teesson et al., 2024). The absence of longer-term follow-up assessments in the present study may therefore have limited the ability to detect delayed intervention effects.

Conclusion

This study provides initial support for the feasibility and potential effectiveness of a culturally adapted version of the evidence-based Australian OurFutures prevention program in the German school context. The blended-learning approach appears to be well accepted by students and can contribute to improving substance-related knowledge among adolescents.

Given the decentralized structure of the German school system, broader implementation of such programs will require close coordination between the education sector and the addiction prevention and treatment system. Future studies with larger samples, randomized designs, and longer follow-up periods are needed to further evaluate the program's effectiveness and its potential for wider implementation.
