Global burden, regional disparities, and decomposition analysis of metabolic risk-attributable diseases among women of reproductive age: GBD 2021
Authors: Liping Hu, Hui Li, Chen Gao, Wenqiang You, Mingxing Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMetabolic disorders are a growing global concern, especially among women of reproductive age (15–49 years), where their dual impact on maternal and long-term health remains underexplored. This study aims to quantify the burden and trends of diseases attributable to metabolic risk factors in this population using data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study.MethodsWe extracted GBD 2021 data on six metabolic risk factors (high body mass index [BMI], high fasting plasma glucose, high systolic blood pressure, high low-density lipoprotein [LDL] cholesterol, kidney dysfunction, and low bone mineral density) from 204 countries and territories, and their attributable disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and deaths from 1990 to 2021. Analyses included age, Socio-demographic Index (SDI), and region-specific trends; frontier and inequality assessments; and decomposition by aging, population growth, and epidemiological shifts.ResultsIn 2021, diseases attributable to metabolic risks caused 30.3 million DALYs and 383,874 deaths among women aged 15–49, corresponding to age-standardized rates (ASRs) of 1556.2 and 19.7 per 100,000 population, respectively. From 1990 to 2021, DALYs exhibited a modest but persistent increase (estimated annual percentage change [EAPC]: + 0.28%), while death rates declined slightly (EAPC: –0.25%), suggesting an evolving but inadequately controlled risk landscape. The diseases most associated with metabolic risks included cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and kidney disorders. High BMI and high fasting glucose were the leading contributors. The greatest disease impact was observed in South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa, especially in low- and middle-SDI countries. Frontier and inequality analyses revealed marked inefficiencies and persistent disparities, while decomposition analyses showed that population growth (75.0%) and aging (32.6%) were the main drivers of DALY increases.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the urgency of integrating routine metabolic screening and management into reproductive health services, particularly in low- and middle-income settings. Tailored, sex- and age-specific interventions are critical to mitigating long-term metabolic morbidity and enhancing maternal and intergenerational health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04005-6.

Introduction

Metabolic disorders—including obesity, hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and dyslipidemia—have become increasingly prevalent worldwide, imposing a growing burden on health systems and population well-being [1–3]. Women are particularly vulnerable to the health impacts of these conditions, and recent global data show a concerning upward trend in both the prevalence and severity of metabolic-related disease among females [2, 4–6]. However, despite the recognized impact of metabolic risk factors on adult populations, there remains a notable gap in large-scale epidemiological research specifically focused on women of reproductive age (15–49 years) [2]. This lack of data is especially critical given that metabolic abnormalities during this life stage may not only impair general health but also adversely affect reproductive outcomes, maternal well-being, and neonatal health [6–9]. Conditions such as gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, and infertility are closely linked with pre-existing metabolic risks, emphasizing the need for timely detection and management in this population [10–12].

While several countries have implemented health promotion strategies targeting metabolic risks in adult women, few have tailored interventions specifically for those in their reproductive years [9, 13]. In high-income settings, initiatives such as metabolic screening during prenatal visits or lifestyle interventions for polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) show promise (e.g., Japan’s annual metabolic checkups or Australia’s NDSS programs) [14–16]. However, many such programs lack integration into reproductive and maternal health services and are often implemented without adequate sex-specific or age-disaggregated monitoring [17]. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), the problem is compounded by limited resources and weak health infrastructure [18]. Large-scale data describing the effectiveness or coverage of metabolic risk prevention among women aged 15–49 years are sparse, hampering informed policymaking [12, 19, 20]. Therefore, a global epidemiologic profile of metabolic risk-attributable disease burden among reproductive-age women is urgently needed to bridge this critical evidence gap.

This study aims to fill that void by providing a comprehensive, regionally disaggregated analysis of the metabolic risk-attributable burden among women aged 15–49 years using the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 dataset. By describing trends over time and across geographies, this research offers critical insight into the unmet health needs of reproductive-age women, identifies areas of high burden and policy failure, and lays a theoretical and empirical foundation for region-specific interventions. Such evidence is crucial to support the development of targeted, efficient, and equitable health policies that not only improve women’s metabolic health but also enhance maternal and neonatal outcomes on a global scale.

Materials and methods

Data sources

This study utilized data from the GBD Study 2021, a comprehensive, systematic analysis providing detailed information on global health metrics. Specifically, we extracted data concerning the metabolic risk factors attributable to diseases among women aged 15–49 years from 1990 to 2021. The GBD 2021 estimates used in this study are not based on raw surveillance data alone, but are derived through a suite of advanced statistical modeling techniques that synthesize data from heterogeneous sources with variable quality and completeness. Specifically, the GBD framework employs tools such as DisMod-MR 2.1—a Bayesian meta-regression modeling platform—to generate internally consistent estimates for disease burden across time, geography, and demographic groups. Detailed modeling strategies and data input descriptions are publicly available through the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) [4–6].

Data were categorized by age groups (in 5-year intervals), socio-demographic index (SDI) levels (high, high-middle, middle, low-middle, low), and GBD-defined regions (High-income; Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania; South Asia; Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia; North Africa and Middle East; Latin America and Caribbean; Sub-Saharan Africa) [4]. Data included numbers, rates, and age-standardized rates of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and deaths. The Institutional Review Board of the University of Washington examined and authorized a consent waiver for the GBD research.

Definition

The metabolic risk factors analyzed in this study included high systolic blood pressure, high body-mass index (BMI), kidney dysfunction, high fasting plasma glucose, low bone mineral density, and high low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol. Diseases attributable to these metabolic risk factors were categorized by the Level 2 GBD 2021 classification system, encompassed diabetes and kidney diseases, transport injuries, unintentional injuries, self-harm and interpersonal violence, respiratory infections and tuberculosis, digestive diseases, sense organ diseases, neurological disorders, neoplasms, chronic respiratory diseases, cardiovascular diseases, and musculoskeletal disorders [5]. The associations between specific metabolic risks and disease outcomes were derived directly from the GBD 2021 comparative risk assessment framework, which systematically quantifies risk–outcome pairs based on epidemiological evidence and modeling [4].

Study approach

Our research approach comprised six main analytical sections: A comprehensive global descriptive analysis of metabolic risk-attributable DALYs and deaths among women aged 15–49 in 2021, stratified by age groups, SDI, and GBD-defined international regions, and an assessment of temporal trends from 1990 to 2021 using the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC). A detailed analysis exploring the specific metabolic risk factors and associated diseases, with comparative descriptive statistics and EAPC trends from 1990–2021, alongside visualization via stacked pyramid charts for the years 1990 and 2021. Geospatial analyses presenting age-standardized rates of DALYs and deaths for the year 2021 and EAPC from 1990 to 2021 across 204 countries and regions using thematic world maps. Frontier analysis, employing iterative modeling to benchmark the performance of each country or region in controlling metabolic risk-attributable disease burden. In this analysis, the “effective difference” was defined as the vertical gap between a country’s actual age-standardized burden and the estimated frontier curve at a comparable SDI level. This gap quantifies the extent to which a country underperforms relative to the best achievable outcome given its development context. Benchmarking in this manner allows for identifying performance inefficiencies and prioritizing improvement targets across comparable socio-demographic environments. Health inequality assessment utilizing slope index of inequality (SII) and concentration index (CI) metrics to evaluate disparities across different nations and regions, comparing the years 1990 and 2021. Decomposition analysis attributing changes in disease burden to factors such as aging, population growth, and epidemiological shifts, elucidating their relative contributions to the observed variations in disease burden.

Statistical analysis

DALYs and deaths were expressed as counts, crude rates per 100,000 population, and age-standardized rates per 100,000 using the GBD world standard population. Temporal trends from 1990 to 2021 were analyzed by calculating the EAPC and its 95% uncertain intervals, using Linear regression models with a log transformation. Continuous variables are equidistributed by quartile spacing when mapping. Frontier analysis involved 100 iterations of benchmarking using the frontier package in R, defining the frontier as the lowest attainable age-standardized rate. Health inequalities were quantified using SII and CI through linear and robust regression analyses, testing heteroscedasticity using Breusch-Pagan tests. Decomposition analyses employed a standardized demographic decomposition method, attributing the overall change in disease burden to aging, population growth, and epidemiological transitions, with results presented in absolute and percentage terms.

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2) and Stata/MP (64-bit, version 17.0). The following R packages were employed: “dplyr”, “purrr”, and “sf” for data manipulation and filtering; “ggplot2”, “ggsci”, “ggrepel”, “patchwork”, and “ggbrace” for data visualization; and “MASS”, “car”, “splines”, “broom”, and “mgcv” for robust regression analysis, heteroscedasticity testing, and functional model fitting. Stata package “conindex” was employed. P < 0.05 was defined statistical significance.

Results

Global burden and trends

In 2021, metabolic risks were responsible for approximately 30.3 million (95% UI: 24.4 to 36.2 million) DALYs among women aged 15–49 years worldwide. This corresponds to an ASR of 1,556.2 DALYs per 100,000 population (95% UI: 1250.8 to 1856.3), indicating that for every 100,000 women of reproductive age, more than 1,500 years of healthy Life were lost due to metabolic-related conditions. Although the ASR has shown a relatively modest annual increase of 0.28% (95% CI: 0.22 to 0.33) between 1990 and 2021, the absolute number of DALYs rose considerably—primarily due to population growth and demographic shifts, as later confirmed in the decomposition analysis. The metabolic risks attributable to deaths numbered about 383,874 cases (95% UI: 338,678 to 431,444), and the ASR was 19.7 per 100,000 (95% UI: 17.38 to 22.14). Interestingly, the EAPC of deaths exhibited a decreasing trend at −0.25% (95% CI: −0.32 to −0.17) over the same period (Table S1 and Table S2). Burden by metabolic risk factors, disease causes, and age distributions

The major contributors among metabolic risks for DALYs in 2021 were high body-mass index (BMI) and high fasting plasma glucose, followed closely by high systolic blood pressure and high LDL cholesterol, particularly among the older reproductive-age groups (35–49 years). For example, among women aged 45–49 years, the DALY burden surpassed 10 million, with an age-standardized rate (ASR) exceeding 4,400 per 100,000. In contrast, the steepest rise in DALY rates over time occurred in the 20–24 age group, which saw a moderate annual increase, while the 45–49 group experienced a notable decline. Regarding mortality, women aged 45–49 accounted for the highest number of deaths—over 160,000 cases—with the highest ASR across all age groups, despite showing a substantial yearly reduction in recent decades (see Tables S1 and S2 for detailed estimates).

Analysis of specific causes revealed that in 2021, cardiovascular diseases and diabetes and kidney diseases remained the leading contributors to both DALYs and deaths, while neoplasms became particularly prominent in older age groups. Across metabolic risks, high BMI and high fasting plasma glucose showed consistent growth in both DALYs and deaths over time, in contrast to low bone mineral density, which exhibited a downward trend. The trends between 1990 and 2021 depicted by stacked pyramid charts (Fig. 1C-F) demonstrate a marked increase in the contributions from high BMI and high fasting plasma glucose, especially among women aged 35–49 years, underscoring significant epidemiological shifts. Trends in DALYs and deaths due to different metabolic risk factors are plotted in Figures S1-S6, and different diseases due to them in Figures S7-S18. Among them, the subgroup stacked pyramid plots for cardiovascular diseases and cancers are in Figures S19-S20, respectively.

Regional and national level burden and trends

Regional disparities were profound, with the highest DALYs and deaths rates observed in middle and low-middle socio-demographic index (SDI) regions, while high SDI regions exhibited comparatively lower rates. The middle SDI region bore the heaviest DALY burden, exceeding 10 million cases, with an age-standardized rate (ASR) approaching 1,700 per 100,000. In contrast, the high SDI region had a substantially lower burden, with around 3 million DALYs and a notably lower ASR. Regarding deaths, the low-middle SDI region showed the highest mortality rate and death count, while the high SDI region exhibited the lowest ASR across all strata (see Tables S1 and S2 for detailed estimates).

Across GBD super-regions, South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa carried the highest absolute burden of both DALYs and deaths. In 2021, South Asia alone accounted for over 8.7 million DALYs and over 117,000 deaths, while Sub-Saharan Africa reported 6.9 million DALYs and 85,000 deaths. Temporal trends from 1990 to 2021 indicated increased DALYs primarily in low-middle and middle SDI regions, with the most considerable increments observed in South Asia (EAPC = 0.49) and North Africa and the Middle East (EAPC = 0.42). In 2021, the effective difference for DALYs was the largest in American Samoa (4681.02) and Marshall Islands, indicating the greatest inefficiency relative to the best achievable frontier (Table S3). High-income countries such as Finland and Japan were located close to the frontier curve, reflecting superior control over metabolic risk-related burdens (Fig. 3A-B). Health inequality analysis

The SII and CI revealed persistent but slightly improving inequalities from 1990 to 2021. For DALYs, the slope index increased from −556.66 (95% CI: −869.61 to −243.71) in 1990 to −619.33 (95% CI: −1080.7 to −157.96) in 2021 (Table S5). Concentration indices indicated a slight narrowing of inequality, shifting from −0.0775 (95% CI: −0.1029 to −0.0520) in 1990 to −0.0551 (95% CI: −0.0817 to −0.0284) in 2021 (Table S7). Similar patterns were observed for deaths, with the CI for deaths worsening slightly from −0.1446 to −0.1538 during the same period (Fig. 4B and D). Decomposition analysis

Decomposition analysis showed that from 1990 to 2021, global increases in DALYs were largely driven by population growth (75.0%) and aging (32.6%), while epidemiological change offset the burden by −7.6% (Table S8). Regionally, the impact of population growth was most pronounced in South Asia, whereas aging contributed more significantly in high-SDI and high-income regions (Fig. 4E). For deaths, similar decomposition patterns were observed, though the relative contribution of aging was even more prominent compared to DALYs, highlighting the dual role of demographic transitions and shifting risk profiles in shaping metabolic risk-attributable disease burdens.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the global burden of metabolic risk factors among women of reproductive age (15–49 years) from 1990 to 2021, utilizing data from the GBD 2021 study. The findings reveal a significant increase in DALYs attributable to metabolic disorders in this demographic, underscoring the urgent need for targeted public health interventions. Notably, BMI and high fasting plasma glucose emerged as the leading contributors to the disease burden, particularly among women aged 35–49 years. These trends highlight the critical importance of addressing metabolic health in women during their reproductive years to prevent long-term health complications.

The study’s results indicate a rising trend in metabolic risk factors among women of reproductive age globally. This demographic shift is concerning, as metabolic disorders during reproductive years can lead to adverse pregnancy outcomes and increase the risk of chronic diseases later in life [1, 21]. Comparative analyses with other age groups and male counterparts reveal that women in this age bracket are experiencing a disproportionate increase in metabolic-related health issues. For instance, the prevalence of obesity and T2DM has escalated more rapidly among women aged 15–49 years compared to men of the same age group, emphasizing the need for gender-specific health strategies [22].​ To address this trend, public health programs should prioritize targeted interventions focusing on the most impactful modifiable risks, particularly high BMI and elevated fasting plasma glucose. Evidence-based lifestyle interventions—such as culturally adapted dietary counseling and structured physical activity programs for women aged 20–40—can substantially reduce long-term risk trajectories. School-based education for adolescent girls and workplace wellness campaigns targeting reproductive-age women are especially promising in LMICs, where early prevention is often more feasible than late-stage treatment. Furthermore, the intersection of reproductive health and metabolic disorders presents unique challenges. Conditions such as PCOS and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) not only affect fertility but also predispose women to long-term metabolic complications [23, 24]. Recent studies have highlighted the bidirectional relationship between reproductive health and metabolic disorders, suggesting that interventions targeting metabolic health can have positive implications for reproductive outcomes and vice versa [15]. The rise in DALYs among women of reproductive age reflects not only an increased disease burden but also broader consequences for maternal health, productivity, and intergenerational outcomes. A high DALY burden in this demographic may translate into reduced workforce participation, increased demand for chronic disease care, and poorer neonatal health in future generations.

The implementation and effectiveness of policies addressing metabolic risk factors among women of reproductive age have shown stark contrasts across regions. High-income countries have largely made significant strides through well-established national strategies combining public health initiatives, community-based interventions, and clinical programs. For instance, Finland’s North Karelia Project successfully reduced cardiovascular risk factors by integrating diet and lifestyle interventions across entire communities, serving as a model for similar programs globally [25, 26]. Japan, through the “Metabo Law,” mandates annual metabolic syndrome screenings for adults aged 40–74 years, resulting in substantial improvements in obesity and hypertension control rates [27]. Similarly, Australia has implemented the National Diabetes Services Scheme (NDSS) and preventive initiatives targeting Indigenous populations, demonstrating modest but meaningful reductions in obesity and T2DM incidence [28]. In contrast, LMICs face considerable challenges. In South Asia, despite national programs like India’s NPCDCS (National Programme for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, Cardiovascular Diseases and Stroke), evaluations show that early screening and sustained lifestyle modifications remain limited, especially in rural populations [29, 30]. In Bangladesh and Pakistan, national efforts to address metabolic diseases often lack integration into maternal health services, leading to fragmented care for women of reproductive age [18]. Sub-Saharan Africa demonstrates an even more concerning trend. Although initiatives such as WHO’s Package of Essential Noncommunicable Disease Interventions (PEN) have been adapted in countries like Kenya and Ghana, resource limitations, healthcare workforce shortages, and cultural barriers continue to impede implementation [31, 32]. Notably, studies from Nigeria and Ethiopia reveal that women are less likely to access metabolic screening or treatment services, exacerbating the burden of undiagnosed hypertension and diabetes [33, 34]. Latin America offers a mixed picture. Countries like Chile and Mexico have pioneered sugar-sweetened beverage taxes and mandatory front-of-package labeling to combat obesity [35]. These policies have been associated with reductions in consumption of unhealthy foods among young populations. However, their specific effectiveness in addressing reproductive-age women remains underexplored. Meanwhile, Brazil’s Family Health Strategy emphasizes primary healthcare outreach but struggles with scaling up targeted metabolic health interventions [36]. The Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, facing rapidly rising rates of obesity and diabetes among women, has launched ambitious programs such as Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 health initiatives [37]. Yet, cultural restrictions, gender disparities in healthcare access, and persistent stigma around weight issues continue to hinder comprehensive program success. Integrating metabolic screening and management into existing reproductive and maternal health platforms—such as antenatal visits, preconception checkups, or family planning services—offers a practical and cost-effective avenue for early detection. For example, incorporating HbA1c and blood pressure checks into routine maternal health visits could capture undiagnosed metabolic disorders in time to prevent adverse pregnancy outcomes. Digital health tools (e.g., SMS-based reminders or teleconsultation for glucose monitoring) can further enhance accessibility in resource-constrained settings. Despite numerous policy efforts, many regions show a critical gap between policy formulation and effective implementation. Tailoring interventions to the socio-cultural, economic, and healthcare realities of each region—and specifically integrating metabolic risk screening into maternal and women’s health services—remains an urgent global priority. Beyond national strategies, empowering regional hubs for women’s health can play a pivotal role in tailoring scalable solutions. Establishing collaborative networks that bridge ministries of health, academic institutions, and community leaders enables better alignment of intervention design with sociocultural contexts. For instance, pilot programs led by local female health workers in South Asia have demonstrated success in improving adherence to dietary guidelines and follow-up care for gestational diabetes—offering a model for other regions with similar constraints.

The decomposition analysis in this study reveals that population growth and aging are significant contributors to the increasing DALYs associated with metabolic risk factors. However, epidemiological changes, including shifts in lifestyle behaviors and environmental factors, also play a crucial role [38, 39]. The rise in sedentary lifestyles, unhealthy dietary patterns, and increased stress levels contribute to the escalating burden of metabolic disorders [40–42]. Understanding these underlying factors is essential for developing effective prevention and intervention strategies tailored to specific populations and regions.

This study is subject to several Limitations. First, the reliance on secondary GBD 2021 data may introduce inaccuracies due to heterogeneity in data quality across regions. As such, some country-level data may reflect estimation rather than direct measurement, especially in resource-limited settings. Besides, the observational nature of the GBD framework precludes causal inference, and our findings should be interpreted in this context. Second, certain contextual factors such as genetic predisposition, socioeconomic status, and healthcare accessibility were not comprehensively accounted for, which could affect the interpretation of regional variations. Lastly, causal relationships between reproductive health outcomes and metabolic risks were beyond the scope of this analysis. Future research should prioritize prospective cohort studies focusing on reproductive-age women, especially in LMICs, to establish clearer causal pathways. Additionally, integrated care models linking reproductive, metabolic, and chronic disease services should be evaluated for effectiveness in diverse healthcare settings.

Conclusions

The escalating burden of metabolic risk factors among women of reproductive age is a pressing global health concern. This study underscores the necessity for targeted, gender-specific interventions and policies that address the unique health challenges faced by this demographic. Proactively addressing these risks—beginning in adolescence and continuing throughout the reproductive years—through early screening, promotion of physical activity, and nutritional interventions, offers a transformative opportunity to reshape women’s health trajectories. Such efforts can improve health outcomes for women and future generations while helping to reduce the global burden of metabolic-related diseases.
