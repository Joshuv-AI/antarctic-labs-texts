Evaluation of hospice and palliative care policies in China: a Theme-Instrument-Effectiveness (TIE) framework based on text analysis
Authors: Xinran Li, Lu Ke, Shuai Zheng, Haoran Qu, Jingkai Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChina is undergoing rapid population aging, which has substantially increased the demand for hospice and palliative care (HPC). Systematic and quantitative evaluation of China’s HPC policy corpus remains limited. This study addresses this gap through an integrated three-dimensional analytical framework examining policy themes, policy instruments, and text-based policy effectiveness.MethodsA corpus of 95 national-level, provincial-level, and selected sub-provincial pilot HPC policy documents (January 2011–May 2026) was analyzed using a Theme-Instrument-Effectiveness (TIE) framework combining three approaches: (1) Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic modeling optimized by coherence score and perplexity, with labels reviewed by two independent coders until consensus; (2) NVivo-assisted content coding with substantial inter-coder reliability for policy-instrument coding (Cohen’s κ = 0.75); and (3) a Policy Modeling Consistency (PMC) Index Model applied to 28 analytically representative policies across nine dimensions using binary coding with equal-weight aggregation.ResultsEight latent topics were identified, covering cancer prevention and control, digital economy, medical and health care, medical insurance management, elderly health, elderly care services, hospice and palliative care, and population development. Equity-oriented themes concerningrural elderly care, disabled and dementia elderly groups, grassroots service accessibility, rare disease prevention and treatment, and regionally balanced medical-resource allocation were not represented as standalone topics. Content coding of 1,784 coded text segments revealed a supply-dominant instrument structure (supply-side: 51.18%; environment-side: 29.99%; demand-side: 18.83%), with public awareness (1.01%) and financial support (2.86%) being the least-used subcategories. PMC evaluation yielded a mean score of 6.73 (Excellent); Policy Instruments (X4 = 0.98) and Policy Objectives (X5 = 0.87) performed strongest, while Policy Timeliness (X2 = 0.33) and Policy Perspective (X3 = 0.60) received comparatively lower scores, although the uniform X2 score was partly attributable to its operational definition.ConclusionsChina’s HPC policy system has established a broad institutional foundation but retains structural gaps in equity coverage and instrument balance. Future policies should strengthen legal safeguards, rebalance instruments toward demand- and environment-side mechanisms, expand coverage of underserved populations, and embed performance evaluation provisions to improve the internal coherence, operability, and accountability of policy texts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12904-026-02236-4.Keywords: Hospice and palliative care, Policy evaluation, LDA topic modeling, PMC index model, Policy instruments, Theme-instrument-effectiveness, Universal health coverage, Health equity

Background

The accelerating pace of global population aging, coupled with profound epidemiological transitions, has placed hospice and palliative care (HPC) at the forefront of contemporary health policy discourse. Guided by global authoritative frameworks and public health strategies, HPC has been widely recognized as a core component of equitable, people-centered health systems [1, 2]. Ensuring the comfort, dignity, and quality of life of terminally ill patients has emerged as a critical benchmark for evaluating both societal progress and the comprehensiveness of national health systems [3, 4]. HPC—a model of care dedicated to alleviating the physical, psychological, social, and spiritual suffering of patients with life-limiting illnesses through holistic, person-centered approaches—is increasingly institutionalized within modern health care systems [5, 6]. Global Atlas of Palliative (2nd Edition) systematically maps global HPC resources, access gaps and developmental priorities, providing a foundational reference for national policy formulation [1]. Meanwhile, a public health strategy for HPC has been proposed as a core approach to scale up population-based, equitable HPC services worldwide, emphasizing integration into mainstream health systems and targeted support for vulnerable groups [2]. By emphasizing respect for the natural course of life and prioritizing quality of life over mere prolongation of survival, HPC aligns with the contemporary medical paradigm that champions integrated, life-cycle-oriented care [5, 7–10].

China’s rapid demographic transition has rendered the development of HPC services particularly urgent. By the end of 2025, China’s population aged 60 years and above reached 323.38 million, accounting for 23.0% of the total population. The population aged 65 years and above stood at 223.65 million, representing 15.9% of the national total, which indicates that China has entered a deeply aging society [11]. In response to escalating demand for HPC, the Chinese government has accorded growing priority to HPC development. A series of national and provincial policy initiatives has been launched to expand service access, enhance care quality, and standardize service delivery.

The Outline of the “Healthy China 2030” Plan, issued by the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China and the State Council in 2016, explicitly called for strengthening palliative and other forms of continuity of care. Subsequently, the former National Health and Family Planning Commission launched the first batch of national HPC pilot programs in October 2017, followed by the second batch launched by the National Health Commission in May 2019. To further scale up and standardize HPC services nationwide, the National Health Commission issued a notice launching the third batch of national HPC pilot programs in April 2023, steadily expanding the coverage of high-quality HPC across the country. A consistent core principle has been established throughout the three rounds of pilot initiatives: improving the quality of life of terminally ill patients through systematic psychological support and humanistic care for both patients and their families. Collectively, these efforts signify the institutionalization of HPC within China’s national health reform agenda and reflect the government’s commitment to integrating HPC into a broader, people-centered health strategy.

Despite these notable advances, the development of HPC in China continues to face multifaceted challenges spanning policy, sociocultural, and resource domains. At the policy level, the existing regulatory framework remains fragmented, with goals, implementation instruments, and operational mechanisms frequently misaligned or overlapping between central and local governments. Such misalignment between policy objectives and implementation instruments has resulted in operational inefficiencies, uneven geographical coverage, and ambiguous policy priorities [12]. At the sociocultural level, deeply entrenched traditional beliefs—particularly the perception that HPC equates to “abandoning active treatment”—continue to impede public acceptance of HPC services, thereby hindering their widespread uptake [13]. With respect to resources, critical bottlenecks persist: a shortage of specialized healthcare professionals, inadequate institutional capacity, and persistent financial constraints collectively restrict the provision of accessible, high-quality HPC [14]. Consequently, many institutions providing HPC services struggle to maintain sustainable operations in the absence of robust funding mechanisms and targeted policy support.

Scholars have made considerable efforts to address these challenges. In the domain of policy analysis, researchers focusing on the Chinese context have mapped the evolutionary trajectory of HPC policies, identified coordination gaps among government departments, and proposed optimization strategies for policy implementation [15]. Studies of European health systems have offered comparative perspectives for China’s policy development, emphasizing the importance of integrating HPC into universal health coverage frameworks [16, 17]. Regarding sociocultural dimensions, China-based empirical studies have explored determinants of public attitudes toward HPC, including regional cultural variation and health literacy levels [14]. On resource constraints, existing scholarship has primarily focused on quantifying workforce gaps and assessing the financial sustainability of HPC institutions, with some scholars advocating for diversified funding mechanisms encompassing government subsidies, charitable donations, and medical insurance coverage [18].

Nevertheless, this body of literature exhibits notable limitations that warrant further scholarly attention. First, although recent policy studies have examined China’s HPC policies from the perspectives of policy instruments, participating entities, policy evolution, and text-based policy evaluation, they have paid less attention to the interactive dynamics among policy fragmentation, evaluation mechanisms, implementation conditions, and resource allocation efficiency [15, 19]. Second, existing quantitative evaluation tools are mostly single-dimensional; conventional qualitative analyses or single-indicator quantitative approaches are insufficient to capture the complex, multi-dimensional structure of HPC policies, thereby impeding the precise identification of policy optimization leverage points [15, 19]. Third, studies on sociocultural perceptions and resource constraints tend to be conducted in isolation, with limited attention to the dynamic interrelationships among cultural attitudes, resource availability, and text-based policy effectiveness—an oversight that considerably constrains the practical relevance of proposed solutions [14, 19, 20].

Recent quantitative studies have begun to evaluate China’s HPC policy system using policy-instrument analysis, textual quantitative methods, and PMC-based frameworks [15, 19]. These studies have advanced the empirical assessment of HPC policies by moving beyond purely descriptive policy review. However, two limitations remain. First, existing studies have generally focused on either policy instruments, policy evolution, or PMC-based structural evaluation, with limited integration of thematic structure, instrument configuration, and text-based policy effectiveness within a single analytical framework. Second, few studies have used latent topic modeling to identify the implicit thematic architecture of HPC policy documents and to examine whether specific policy concerns are concentrated as independent themes or embedded within broader governance narratives. To address these gaps, this study collects and analyzes national-level, provincial-level, and selected sub-provincial pilot HPC policy documents in China from January 2011 to May 2026. It proposes a three-dimensional Theme-Instrument-Effectiveness (TIE) framework that integrates Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic modeling, NVivo-assisted content coding, and the Policy Modeling Consistency (PMC) Index Model. Within this framework, the theme dimension identifies the major policy priorities, the instrument dimension examines the configuration of policy instruments, and the effectiveness dimension assesses text-based policy design quality, including internal consistency and structural completeness, rather than implementation outcomes. This integrated design enables triangulated interpretation across thematic, instrumental, and evaluative dimensions. Accordingly, this study pursues three objectives: (1) to clarify the thematic structure and policy priorities of China’s HPC policy system; (2) to assess the distribution and balance of policy instruments; and (3) to evaluate the internal coherence, structural completeness, and design weaknesses of selected policy texts, thereby providing evidence for future policy optimization.

Materials and methods

Data sources

To improve the systematicity, transparency, and analytical relevance of the policy corpus, HPC policy documents issued in China between January 2011 and May 2026 were systematically retrieved from six authoritative sources: (1) the Peking University Law and Regulation Database; (2) the State Council Policy Document Repository; (3) the official websites of the National Health Commission and other relevant central ministries; (4) official websites of provincial health commissions; (5) provincial government portals; (6) provincial regulatory normative document databases. The search was conducted using core Chinese-language keywords, including “安宁疗护” (hospice care), “临终关怀” (end-of-life care), “姑息治疗” (palliative care), and “医养结合” (integrated medical and elderly care). The corpus was primarily composed of national-level and provincial-level HPC policy documents, supplemented by selected sub-provincial pilot documents where they contained substantive policy-design, implementation, or governance provisions relevant to HPC development. Municipal- and county-level documents were not systematically retrieved as an independent analytical stratum, because many such texts are implementation-oriented documents derived from higher-level policy mandates and may introduce substantial textual redundancy. Selected sub-provincial documents were therefore included only when they provided analytically relevant implementation details or local pilot arrangements not fully reflected in higher-level documents—a scoping decision consistent with comparable Chinese health policy text analyses [21, 22].

Given that HPC policy in China remains nascent and is frequently embedded within broader health and social governance frameworks, the following inclusion criteria were applied: (1) Policy type—substantive regulatory or programmatic documents, including laws, regulations, administrative measures, implementation opinions, and plans; purely technical standards, project approvals, and routine announcements were excluded unless they contained substantive policy-design, regulatory, or implementation provisions relevant to HPC governance; (2) Issuing authority—documents issued by the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China, the State Council, national-level ministries, provincial-level authorities, and, where analytically relevant-sub-Provincial pilot agencies; (3) Document currency—where multiple versions of a document existed, only the most recent version was retained; (4) Policy relevance—documents explicitly referencing HPC or containing substantive provisions pertaining to its service delivery, financing, regulation, or institutional development.

After applying these criteria, 95 eligible policy documents were identified. Each document was reviewed in full, and a structured metadata record was compiled for each, cataloging the policy title, issuing authority, document number, and publication date. Analytical framework

A three-dimensional analytical framework is proposed to systematically examine China’s HPC policy system. The framework comprises three interrelated dimensions—policy themes, policy instruments, and text-based policy effectiveness—which together offer a comprehensive perspective for analyzing the logic, structure, and internal coherence of policy design. This approach enables an integrated understanding of what policies prioritize, how they operationalize their goals, and the structural completeness and design quality of policy texts within the national governance system.

Policy theme dimension

The policy theme dimension identifies the core priorities and strategic emphases embedded in HPC governance. It captures the major policy domains that receive sustained attention, including patient comfort and symptom management, family psychological support, service network construction, professional capacity building, and financial security. This thematic approach is consistent with recent Chinese HPC policy analyses that examine policy priorities, policy instruments, participating actors, and implementation orientations within a multidimensional framework [15, 19]. By clarifying the thematic focus of policies, this dimension reveals how policymakers conceptualize the mission and value of HPC within broader health reform, aging governance, and social welfare agendas. Furthermore, it illuminates the evolution of HPC from a narrowly conceived medical service into a multidimensional public policy concern.

Policy instrument dimension

The policy instrument dimension focuses on the means and mechanisms through which policy objectives are translated into action. Following the classical typology of policy instruments, instruments are categorized into three types [23]: (1) Supply-side instruments, which provide tangible resources such as infrastructure, service systems, technical investment, workforce and team development , and financial support; (2) Environment-side instruments, which establish enabling conditions through legal protection, target planning, performance evaluation, educational outreach, and pilot demonstration; and (3) Demand-side instruments, which stimulate participation and service utilization through support for vulnerable groups, medical insurance coverage, institutional protection, and public awareness. This dimension emphasizes the structural composition and coordination of instruments that collectively shape the policy implementation pathway.

Text-based policy effectiveness dimension

The text-based policy effectiveness dimension evaluates the internal coherence, logical rigor, and operational soundness of policy design. Specifically, it assesses whether policy documents articulate clear objectives, maintain logical consistency, and specify actionable implementation mechanisms [24]. By examining structural integrity, target precision, and practical feasibility, this dimension gauges the text-based maturity of the HPC-related policy system. In this study, text-based policy effectiveness is operationalized as the internal quality and structural integrity of policy documents, given that directly observable implementation and outcome data remain unavailable for this policy cohort; this operationalization is consistent with established PMC index methodology and should not be interpreted as evidence of actual service-level effectiveness.

In summary, the three dimensions form a cohesive analytical system: policy themes establish the strategic orientation, policy instruments specify the operational mechanisms, and text-based policy effectiveness evaluates the internal coherence and implementability of policy texts. This integrated framework provides a robust conceptual basis for analyzing the evolution, internal dynamics, and potential optimization of China’s HPC-related policies, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Research methods

Grounded in the three-dimensional analytical framework, this study adopted a mixed-methods research design to systematically evaluate the characteristics, structure, and evolution of China’s HPC policy system. The research process was organized into three sequential stages: (1) policy text identification and coding, (2) dimension-based analysis, and (3) comparative interpretation. This staged approach ensures methodological coherence and enhances analytical rigor.

Topic extraction and model optimization

To uncover the latent thematic structure of China’s HPC policy corpus, LDA topic modeling was applied to the assembled documents. Text preprocessing followed established procedures. This study employed the Jieba library for Chinese word segmentation and constructed a custom domain-specific dictionary for HPC policies to ensure accurate recognition of specialized terminology such as “hospice and palliative care”, “integrated medical and elderly care”, and “hospice care”. Because Jieba tokenization combined with a custom dictionary may not fully capture domain-specific compound terms, this study adopted a two-layer term extraction scheme: basic word segmentation was first implemented relying on Jieba and the custom dictionary; then, Gensim Phrases was utilized to automatically mine bigrams and trigrams from the full corpus based on word co-occurrence regularities, supplementing the terminology omitted by the dictionary.

During the subsequent text cleaning phase, a deliberate departure from the conventional high-frequency word removal strategy was adopted. Given that most high-frequency words in the HPC policy corpus (e.g., “health”, “nursing”) are highly relevant to the research topic and serve as the core foundation for thematic semantic generation, indiscriminate removal could result in the loss of substantively meaningful thematic information. On this basis, a curated stop-word list was first applied to remove function words and other non-informative tokens. Subsequently, the scope of the stop-word list was optimized through multiple rounds of iteration, supplemented by round-by-round manual verification, to precisely eliminate high-frequency background words with low relevance to policy themes (e.g., “service”, “contract”, “management”).

The LDA topic modeling process was implemented based on the Gensim open-source library in the Python programming language. The Dirichlet hyperparameters (alpha = 0.0091 and eta = 0.1250) were automatically estimated during model training. To determine the optimal number of latent topics, multiple rounds of iterative model testing were conducted, with the C_V topic coherence score adopted as the core evaluation metric (a higher coherence value corresponds to a more semantically stable and interpretable thematic structure).

Meanwhile, to verify the robustness of the preprocessing parameter settings, this study further tested the variation pattern of topic coherence under different document frequency filtering thresholds (no_above). It is critical to distinguish between two distinct mechanisms for removing non-informative terms employed in this study:

Domain-specific stop-word list: This manually curated and iteratively optimized list was specifically designed to eliminate high-frequency background words with low thematic relevance that appear ubiquitously across policy documents but contribute little to distinguishing different policy themes, as described in the preprocessing section above.

The results showed that the topic coherence score exhibited a monotonically increasing trend with the rise of the no_above threshold, reaching a peak value of 0.57 when no_above = 1 (i.e., no document frequency filtering was applied), as shown in Fig. 2. This finding does not contradict the goal of removing background words, as the truly non-informative background terms had already been precisely eliminated through the domain-specific stop-word list prior to this step. Many of the remaining high-frequency terms represent core domain concepts that are essential for constructing semantically coherent topics. Applying any additional document frequency filtering would inadvertently remove these critical thematic terms, leading to degraded model performance.

Robustness Check: topic coherence across different document-frequency filtering thresholds

Robustness comparisons supported the interpretability of the final eight-topic solution, although exact topic composition and term rankings varied across preprocessing specifications, including different no_above thresholds and the additional removal of manually identified candidate background terms. These checks support the semantic stability of the final 8-topic solution and reduce the risk that topic labels were driven by corpus-wide non-discriminating vocabulary. Topic labels were not assigned on the basis of the top-five terms alone. For each topic, two coders additionally examined the 20 terms with the highest probabilities, the most representative documents (highest topic loadings), and the intertopic distance map, and resolved labeling discrepancies through discussion. This procedure reduces the risk that labels are driven by a small number of high-frequency, non-discriminating terms.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the coherence score fluctuated markedly in the early stage (2–7 topics), rose sharply to its peak value at 8 topics, then declined at 9 topics and did not return to the peak level thereafter. Model fit was further evaluated using perplexity (Eq. 1), a standard metric for assessing the fitting performance and generalization capacity of LDA models, where lower logarithmic perplexity values indicate better model performance. The perplexity analysis revealed an overall increasing trend as the number of topics increased from 2 to 14, indicating that fitting performance weakened as thematic granularity became finer. After balancing semantic interpretability (coherence score) and generalization performance (perplexity), the 8-topic solution was selected for subsequent analysis.

Policy text coding procedure

For the research and analysis on the dimension of policy instruments, this study used systematic content analysis to code and analyze the policy texts. First, all HPC-related policy documents were imported into NVivo 15 for structured coding. Based on an established analytical framework for policy instruments, this study conducted a meticulous sentence-by-sentence and line-by-line interpretation and manual coding of all policy documents, and extracted relevant policy-instrument information from the texts in strict compliance with standardized coding protocols.

This study took “policy number-clause content” as the basic analytical unit, screened the core sentences and paragraphs associated with policy instruments in the texts, and categorized each text unit into the pre-established classification system of policy instruments. A four-level hierarchical coding rule was employed in this research, with the unified coding format defined as parent node-child node-policy code-clause serial number; Table 2 presents illustrative examples of the coding procedure.

To enhance the objectivity and accuracy of the coding outcomes, this study adopted a rigorous coding quality assurance model with two trained coders. Both coders had received two years of professional training in qualitative text analysis and health policy research.

One coder completed the full coding of all policy documents independently. To assess inter-coder reliability, the second coder independently coded a randomly selected 10% subsample of the policy texts. A pilot coding round was first conducted on an additional 5% of the documents to refine the coding framework and resolve initial ambiguities before the formal coding process.

Coding disagreements between the two coders were resolved through consensus discussion; unresolved cases were referred to a third senior researcher with extensive experience in health policy analysis for final adjudication.

After coding was completed, inter-coder reliability was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient. The final Cohen’s κ value was 0.75, indicating substantial agreement between the two coders according to the criteria established by Landis and Koch [25]. This result indicates satisfactory inter-coder reliability in the policy text coding process [26].

PMC index modeling approach

Variable assignment and parameter setting

Building upon the conceptual framework originally proposed by Ruiz Estrada and incorporating insights from subsequent applications in digital health and social policy evaluation, the model comprises nine primary variables and 44 secondary variables [24]. These variables encompass nine key dimensions of policy design: policy nature, timeliness, perspective, instruments, objectives, content, target groups, policy review, and incentive constraints. Selection of policy samples

The PMC Index Model is designed to assess all indicators objectively without imposing special requirements on the units of evaluation [19]. To adequately capture variation in HPC policies across time periods, administrative levels, and functional types, this study adopted the following purposive sampling strategy:

Although the full corpus covers January 2011–May 2026, the PMC sample selection was organized from 2016 onward because dedicated national-level HPC policy development became more explicit after the launch of the Healthy China 2030 agenda; earlier documents were retained in the full corpus for thematic and instrument analysis but were not selected as landmark PMC samples.

Second, across the three policy development stages—the exploratory period (2016–2019), the standardized development period (2020–2022), and the high-quality advancement period (2023–May 2026)—samples were selected according to the principle of prioritizing national-level policies while supplementing with provincial documents. Specifically, 6 national-level policies were drawn from the exploratory period, 3 national-level and 3 provincial policies from the standardized development period, and 9 national-level and 7 provincial policies from the high-quality advancement period, accounting for functional variation across administrative levels.

Notably, very few provincial-level HPC policies were issued during the exploratory period from 2016 to 2019, and most relevant provincial provisions during this stage were embedded within broader elderly care policies under the term “end-of-life care.” Accordingly, only national-level framework and pilot policies were selected for the PMC sample in this early phase. From 2020 onward, dedicated provincial HPC policies were progressively issued, allowing national- and provincial-level documents to be included in the subsequent policy development stages. In total, 28 HPC policy documents were ultimately selected for PMC analysis, covering the full policy development cycle and major policy functional categories. To ensure comparability in administrative authority, institutional scope, and policy function, the PMC evaluation sample was restricted to national- and provincial-level policy texts. To distinguish them from the full-corpus serial numbers (No. 1–95 in Table 1), the 28 sampled policies are labeled P1-P28 throughout the PMC analysis. The sample is analytically representative of the national- and provincial-level policy documents in the full corpus: national-level policies account for 64.3% of the sample and provincial-level policies for 35.7%; documents from the exploratory period account for 21.4%, those from the standardized development period for 21.4%, and those from the high-quality advancement period for 57.1%. For clarity of presentation, these samples are ordered from national- to provincial-level policies.

Evaluation framework and index construction

Adapting the PMC index grading criteria originally proposed by Ruiz Estrada to the HPC policy context, a four-tier classification scheme was established: PMC scores of 7.00–9.00 are classified as Perfect, 5.00-6.99 as Excellent, 3.00-4.99 as Acceptable, and below 3.00 as Poor. This multi-dimensional evaluation system enables a holistic assessment of the degree to which individual policies align with national strategic objectives and implementation requirements in HPC. Results

Policy theme results

Using the LDA model with k = 8, eight latent topics were identified from the HPC-related policy corpus.The topic labels and the five highest-weighted terms for each topic are presented in Table 6. Collectively, the eight topics encompass cancer prevention and control, digital economy and industrial development, basic medical and public health services, medical insurance management and institutional collaboration, elderly health and traditional Chinese medicine services, elderly care and aging response, hospice and palliative care services, and population development and fertility–employment support policies.

Several terms recur across topics—Medical institution (Topics 3, 4, 7), Health (Topics 1, 3, 5), and Elderly people/Elderly (Topics 5, 6). Rather than reading this recurrence as direct evidence of thematic centrality, we treat these as corpus-level high-frequency terms whose thematic meaning is assigned only where they co-occur with more specific, topic-discriminating words (e.g., Medical insurance and Designated in Topic 4; Hospice and Palliative Care and Nurse in Topic 7). On this basis the eight topics remain interpretable as distinct policy domains spanning medical-institution management, public health, and elderly-care provision, consistent with the progressive advancement of the Healthy China initiative and the active response to population aging.

To avoid conflating corpus-wide prevalence with within-topic semantic concentration, the maximum weighted term value was used only as an auxiliary indicator of semantic concentration within each topic. Corpus-wide prominence was interpreted together with the marginal topic distribution in pyLDAvis and representative documents.

Topic 8 (Population Development) yielded the largest maximum term weight (0.062 for the term “Population”), followed by Topic 4 (Medical Insurance Management, 0.052 for “Medical insurance” and “Medical institution”), Topic 6 (Elderly Care Services, 0.048 for “Elderly people”), and Topic 7 (Hospice and Palliative Care, 0.046 for “Hospice and Palliative Care”). Though these four topics show marginal proportions below 5% in the pyLDAvis visualization, their prominent internal term weights mark them as the semantically concentrated topics within the policy corpus.

In contrast, Topic 3 (Medical and Health Care, maximum weighted term value 0.025) and Topic 2 (Digital Economy, maximum weighted term value 0.015) had comparatively lower maximum weighted term values indicating lower within-topic semantic concentration rather than a peripheral position in the policy agenda (Table 5).

Discussion

Methodological contributions

This study developed a three-dimensional TIE framework—integrating LDA topic modeling, NVivo-assisted content coding, and the PMC Index Model—to systematically evaluate 95 national-level, provincial-level, and selected sub-provincial pilot HPC policy documents issued between January 2011 and May 2026. Moving beyond the descriptive narrative reviews that have dominated HPC policy research, it adopts a replicable, data-driven approach that maps the thematic structure of China’s HPC policy attention, instrument distribution, and internal design quality. The integration of machine-assisted topic extraction, structured manual coding, and standardized policy scoring achieves a dual focus on breadth (system-level coverage of multi-tier texts) and depth (dimension-level inspection), enhancing the transparency with which unstructured policy texts are converted into analyzable evidence and aligning with emerging health policy text-analysis paradigms that combine computational tools with qualitative coding [15, 19].

The central innovation lies in the coherent logical linkage among the three TIE dimensions, which addresses a key gap in extant Chinese HPC policy research. Prior studies have largely examined fragmented aspects—clinical service models, implementation pathways, or qualitative readings of core documents—without a unified framework capturing simultaneously what policies aim to address (themes), how they operationalize these goals (instruments), and how well they are structurally designed to support intended outcomes (text-based effectiveness) [15]. By contrast, the TIE framework establishes an analytical pathway that is both cross-document comparable and cross-dimension interpretable, bridging qualitative understanding with quantitative rigor. This is especially valuable for HPC, where normative goals (e.g., patient dignity, quality of life, health equity) intersect with operational demands (e.g., workforce training, financing, service delivery) and complex governance conditions (e.g., inter-departmental coordination, accountability, public acceptance) [15], and it offers a replicable toolkit for other health and aging domains characterized by multi-sector, multi-actor, multi-layer governance.

Policy themes: from narrowly clinical to nationally strategic

LDA-based thematic mapping and intertopic distance analysis indicate that China’s HPC policy has expanded from a clinically oriented service domain into a broader system embedded in health governance, aging agendas, and social welfare arrangements—a trajectory consistent with global trends that frame HPC as an essential component of universal health coverage and emphasize equitable access [16, 17]. The eight topics collectively construct a three-tier agenda spanning macro-level strategic planning, meso-level institutional construction, and micro-level frontline service delivery, resonating with recent scholarship on healthy ageing and integrated care in China [27, 28]. This architecture is empirically supported by the intertopic distance map and keyword weights: Topic 1 (Cancer Prevention and Control), the most prevalent topic, is anchored by Cancer (0.044), and the adjacent clustering of Topics 2 (Digital Economy) and 3 (Medical and Health Care) around it reflects linkages among hospice development, grassroots healthcare, and digital-driven transformation. The presence of Topics 5 (Elderly Health) and 6 (Elderly Care Services) reflects the embeddedness of HPC policymaking within healthy-aging strategies. Although “Medical institution” recurs across Topics 3, 4, and 7, we interpret this not as direct evidence of thematic centrality but as a corpus-wide high-frequency term whose policy meaning emerges only where it co-occurs with topic-specific vocabulary (e.g., medical insurance and designated institutions in Topic 4), consistent with the role of medical institutions as a principal implementation setting for HPC [15]. Against the backdrop of the “Healthy China 2030” agenda, the prominence of elderly health and care indicates that HPC development is deeply tied to long-term care system construction and community-oriented services [15, 27, 29, 30]. The emergence of Topic 2 (Digital Economy) reflects the penetration of “smart healthcare” narratives [31–33], whereas the high weights of Medical insurance (0.052) and Medical institution (0.052) in Topic 4 underscore payment-mechanism reform and designated-institution management as core drivers of standardized HPC development [19, 34]. Finally, the emphasis on Nursing (0.044) and Nurse (0.014) in Topic 7 signals a shift toward strengthening frontline nursing capacity and extending HPC beyond tertiary hospitals to community and primary care settings [35, 36].

Despite this breadth, the analysis reveals two structural imbalances. First, signals targeting vulnerable and hard-to-reach groups—rural residents, low-income households, persons with disabilities, and those facing geographical or socioeconomic barriers—do not emerge as standalone topics, remaining embedded within broader medical-health and elderly-care narratives. This equity gap reflects a foundational feature of China’s HPC discourse: HPC is framed primarily as a systemic public good for aging governance and health-system construction rather than as a mechanism for protecting individual health rights, which privileges building a universal service system over differentiated interventions for specific populations. Consistent with this, prior research finds only a small number of equity-related terms in HPC texts and a near-absence of structural factors such as gender, cognitive impairment, and intersectional vulnerability, leading scholars to argue that the neglect of equity may be more profound than currently measurable [19, 37, 38].

This under-representation of equity is not unique to China but recurs in global HPC policy formulation, where similar gaps have been documented [19]; it also mirrors documented urban-rural and socioeconomic disparities in the distribution of China’s HPC resources [19, 20].

Second, Topic 7 (Hospice and Palliative Care) occupies the lower-right quadrant of the intertopic distance map, relatively distant from supporting topics, suggesting a separation between the core HPC service theme and themes of medical insurance, digital health, and elderly care. This likely mirrors the administrative division of governance—clinical services under health administration, insurance under healthcare security authorities, elderly care within civil affairs, and digital health under health-informatization authorities—which can generate parallel policy discourses and implementation misalignment when cross-departmental coordination is weak [15, 39]. Accordingly, topic modeling and keyword co-occurrence jointly indicate that medical insurance payment, digital technology, elderly-care integration, and HPC clinical practice are framed as parallel emphases rather than a coherent package with explicit links among instruments, settings, and operational steps [19, 40, 41]. This fragmentation is compounded by an overrepresentation of macro-level governance language and a scarcity of micro-level operational detail—frontline workflows, scenario-specific guidance, and practice norms [15, 42]—yielding a corpus broad in agenda-setting but uneven in thematic coherence and operational feasibility, which limits translation into equitable, sustainable, and actionable HPC pathways [19, 30].

Policy instruments: supply-side dominance, environment-side support, and demand-side lag

Content coding of 1,784 text segments reveals a pronounced supply-side dominance: supply-side instruments account for 51.18% of the total, environment-side for 29.99%, and demand-side for only 18.83%. This contrasts sharply with a prior study covering a longer historical period (1994–2024), in which environment-side instruments reached 57.7% across 36 documents while supply-side instruments represented only 23.3% [15]. The reversal stems partly from the different study periods (January 2011–May 2026 here, reflecting deeper policy systematization) and indicates that, as HPC moved from exploration to standardization, the policy focus shifted from establishing a normative environment toward expanding service-delivery capacity. While this conforms to the general logic of policy-system evolution, it also implies that the relative lag in demand-side activation—particularly public awareness and grassroots mobilization—may become the most pressing bottleneck once supply-side accumulation saturates [14, 15, 19]. Notably, this configuration differs from international HPC systems, which typically balance supply-side investment (e.g., workforce, infrastructure), demand-side incentives (e.g., insurance, public engagement), and environment-side regulation (e.g., legal and regulatory safeguards) within a synergistic ecosystem [1, 43].

Within the supply-side category, service system development (29.82%) is overwhelmingly dominant, followed by infrastructure (6.61%) and technical investment (6.45%), while talent and team building (5.44%) and direct financial support (2.86%) are markedly underemphasized—a structure that, although differing from prior research reporting environment-side dominance, similarly signals an imbalanced policy-tool mix in need of optimization [15]. The emphasis on service system development aligns with China’s strategy of building a multi-level HPC service network and accessible pathways for older adults in need, whereas the low share of talent-building instruments echoes the long-standing HPC workforce shortage that prior studies identify as a core barrier to sustainable services [18, 20].

For environment-side instruments, legal protection (9.42%) is primary, followed by target planning (7.06%) and performance evaluation (5.04%), while educational outreach (4.32%) and pilot demonstration (4.15%) are marginal—a marked shift from the early stage dominated by promotional and exploratory measures [15]. The prominence of legal protection reflects a focus on institutionalizing HPC through top-level legal norms, consistent with the global trend of embedding HPC in legislative systems to ensure implementation rigidity [1, 2]. Unlike jurisdictions that have enacted dedicated legislation (e.g., the Hospice and Palliative Care Act in Taiwan, China), China’s framework remains largely attached to the Law of the People’s Republic of China on the Protection of the Rights and Interests of the Elderly, though the steady refinement of HPC-related provisions over the study period has laid a preliminary legal foundation. However, the low share of educational outreach and pilot demonstration indicates that institutional construction has not been matched by sufficient grassroots promotion and localized experimentation, risking a gap between top-level design and on-the-ground implementation [14, 15, 19].

On the demand side, tilt towards the weak (8.18%) and medical insurance coverage (6.50%) dominate, while institutional protection (3.14%) and especially public awareness and engagement (1.01%) remain least utilized—reflecting a focus on formal financial protection and equity support over grassroots mobilization, a critical gap given that low public awareness remains a key barrier to HPC uptake in mainland China [44, 45]. The priority on vulnerable-group tilt and insurance coverage aligns with global HPC concerns about financial protection and equitable access for low-income and disadvantaged older populations [16, 46]. Yet the minimal investment in public awareness is a notable oversight: without education and engagement to raise social acceptance, even well-developed supply-side systems and environment-side norms may fail to achieve intended effects, since the preferences of older adults and their families ultimately determine HPC utilization [45, 47].

Taken together, the instrument allocation follows a clear developmental logic—prioritizing basic service pathways and delivery capacity through administrative and organizational measures, supplemented by environment-side institutional construction, rather than a balanced multi-faceted ecosystem with strong demand-side activation [15, 19, 47, 48]. The moderate attention to technical investment aligns with China’s digital health strategy and global trajectories of technology-enabled HPC [15, 32, 49], and the moderate use of environment-side legal instruments is consistent with the global trend of institutionalizing HPC through legal, regulatory, and policy safeguards [1, 43].

Nevertheless, the severe under-representation of demand-side instruments—especially public awareness and engagement—signals a critical weakness: a relative absence of demand-activation mechanisms to match expanding supply capacity, which may produce a supply-demand mismatch and ultimately hinder the system’s sustainability [19, 45, 47]. This pattern confirms the broader tendency of China’s health policies to prioritize supply-side capacity building over demand-side activation and environment-side refinement [31, 50].

Text-based policy effectiveness: high overall quality but marked dimensional unevenness

The PMC evaluation of 28 analytically representative HPC policy documents reveals overall high design coherence, with scores ranging from 4.29 (Acceptable) to 8.08 (Perfect) and a mean of 6.73 (“Excellent”); fourteen policies were rated Perfect, thirteen Excellent, and only one Acceptable, with none Poor—a pattern consistent with PMC evaluations of other Chinese health policy domains (e.g., long-term care, public health), which likewise report strong overall design quality alongside uneven dimensional performance [51, 52].

Dimension-level analysis shows a clear hierarchy: Policy Instruments (X4 = 0.98) scored highest, followed by Policy Objectives (X5 = 0.87), Policy Content (X6 = 0.82), Policy Nature (X1 = 0.81), Policy Review (X8 = 0.79), and Policy Targets (X7 = 0.77); Incentive Constraints (X9 = 0.75) sat at a moderate level, while Policy Perspective (X3 = 0.60) and especially Policy Timeliness (X2 = 0.33) were markedly weak. PMC surface plots (Fig. 5) confirm this heterogeneity across most dimensions, whereas Policy Timeliness remained uniformly low across all evaluated policies. Policy Timeliness (X2) showed no cross-document variation (mean 0.33), reflecting the binary operationalization of time attributes: none of the evaluated policies simultaneously specified short-, medium-, and long-term timelines. Similar PMC studies likewise identify timeliness as a weak dimension, suggesting that limited temporal specification is a common feature of policy text design rather than unique to HPC [53, 54]; in the Chinese context, validity periods and milestones are often implied by document titles (e.g., “Five-Year Plan”) rather than stated as explicit time-bound clauses, depressing timeliness scores in quantitative coding [53, 54]. Future PMC applications in this context may therefore benefit from crediting implicit timeliness expressions.

The weak-to-moderate scores on these dimensions reflect three observable design limitations. First, low Policy Timeliness under the current operationalization indicates a general absence of explicit, time-bound roadmaps and evaluation timelines, undermining the accountability and operationalizability of policy commitments. Second, the moderate Incentive Constraints score reveals a relative scarcity of concrete, enforceable incentives and disincentives—rooted in China’s decentralized health governance, where central HPC policies are typically issued as guiding “opinions” and “notices” rather than binding regulations, limiting the enforceability of incentive provisions [39, 55, 56]. Local governments retain wide discretion, and HPC service quality has not been incorporated into official promotion assessments, weakening frontline motivation. This “goal specification without enforcement constraints” is a recurring feature of China’s incremental health reform and poses particular challenges within its multi-level governance system. Third, the low Policy Perspective score reflects limited integration of multi-stakeholder viewpoints (e.g., patients, family caregivers, grassroots workers) and an overreliance on top-down governmental perspectives—particularly salient in HPC, where implementation depends on coordination among multiple actors across the health and social care sectors [19, 39].

Comprehensive analysis: an institutionally consolidated but internally uneven policy system

These findings can be contextualized against a core parallel study that applied the PMC method to China’s HPC policies [19]. Whereas that study relied solely on PMC scoring over a broader dataset, the present integration of systematic text coding and PMC evaluation surfaces an additional structural feature—“broad thematic coverage but fragmented implementation pathways”—that single-method PMC approaches are not designed to capture [19]. In scope and outcome, the parallel study evaluated 112 documents including local-level texts (mean PMC 7.20), whereas the present study analyzed 28 representative policies drawn from 95 national-level, provincial-level, and selected sub-provincial pilot HPC policy documents (mean 6.73) [19]. The difference partly reflects that local-level documents often contain more operational provisions rewarded by PMC indicators, while national and provincial policies show stronger top-level design and strategic coherence that does not by itself guarantee grassroots implementation. Synthesizing across all nine dimensions, China’s HPC policy system is best characterized as institutionally consolidated but internally uneven—consistent with the broader evolution of China’s national health policy over the past decade [12, 56]. At the system level, China has built a broad, multi-domain HPC agenda aligned with national health governance and active-aging strategies, supported by a supply-dominant toolkit centered on service system development, infrastructure expansion, and technological innovation, and has achieved high internal design quality in instrument specification, goal articulation, and content coverage—reflecting the capacity of its top-down model to rapidly scale policy attention and establish a foundational framework for a country facing the world’s most demanding aging challenge.

At the same time, the system’s internal unevenness—limited multi-stakeholder integration, a supply-dominant instrument imbalance, and dimension-level weaknesses in timeliness and grassroots implementation guidance—marks the unfinished agenda of HPC policy development. These are not isolated shortcomings but reflect deeper structural features of China’s health policy-making: prioritizing macro-level system building over micro-level operational detail, organizational capacity over tangible resource investment and regulatory institutionalization, and top-down governance over multi-stakeholder engagement and equity for vulnerable populations. Addressing them is critical to transforming China’s HPC framework from a formally established institutional system into one capable of delivering equitable, high-quality HPC to all who need it.

Limitations

This study has three main limitations. First, the policy corpus primarily comprises national- and provincial-level HPC policy documents, supplemented by selected sub-provincial pilot documents only where they provided analytically relevant implementation content. The PMC evaluation was limited to national- and provincial-level policies to maintain comparability in administrative authority and policy function. Therefore, this study does not systematically examine municipal- and county-level implementation documents, and local implementation challenges, operational variation, and geographically tailored policy adjustments may not be fully captured.

Second, the PMC Index Model relies on binary coding and equal-weight aggregation. This approach improves transparency and comparability across policy documents, but it cannot fully capture differences in policy strength, enforceability, or the relative importance of individual indicators. Future studies could incorporate graded scoring schemes, expert weighting, or sensitivity analyses to refine the measurement of text-based policy effectiveness.

Third, this study is based on cross-sectional policy text analysis and evaluates text-based policy effectiveness rather than actual implementation performance. It therefore cannot establish causal links between policy design and service-level outcomes such as HPC coverage, utilization, workforce capacity, or patient and family experiences. Future research should combine policy text analysis with longitudinal data, local implementation evidence, and service-outcome indicators to assess how policy design translates into practice.

Conclusion

This study applied a three-dimensional Theme-Instrument-Effectiveness (TIE) framework, integrating LDA topic modeling, NVivo-assisted content coding, and the PMC Index Model, to evaluate 95 national-level, provincial-level, and selected sub-provincial pilot HPC policy documents issued in China between January 2011 and May 2026. The findings indicate that China’s HPC policy system has established a broad institutional foundation and shows relatively strong text-based design quality, particularly in policy instruments, objectives, and content coverage. However, structural weaknesses remain in policy timeliness and multi-level policy perspectives, with incentive-and-constraint mechanisms and demand-side activation only moderately developed. Future policy design should specify clearer implementation timelines and review milestones, incorporate patient-, family-, provider-, and grassroots-level perspectives, strengthen enforceable incentive and accountability provisions, and rebalance policy instruments toward public awareness, service utilization support, and underserved population coverage. These improvements would enhance the internal coherence, operability, and accountability of HPC policy texts.
