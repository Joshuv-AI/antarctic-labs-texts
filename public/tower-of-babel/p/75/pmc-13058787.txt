Clinical Factors Associated With Endoscopic Decompression Failure and Recurrent Sigmoid Volvulus: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Seoyoon Choi, Hye Kyung Hyun, Jihye Park, Soo Jung Park, Hyun Chul Lim, Jae Hee Cheon, Tae Il Kim, Hye Sun Lee, Jae Jun Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionAlthough sigmoid volvulus is a potentially life‐threatening condition in elderly patients, its prognostic factors are not well‐known. This study aimed to evaluate clinical outcomes and identify prognostic factors in patients with sigmoid volvulus.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 96 patients diagnosed with sigmoid volvulus by abdominal CT between January 2005 and January 2023 at two tertiary referral centers. After patients who underwent emergent surgery, refused endoscopic treatment, or experienced spontaneous decompression were excluded, 75 patients were analyzed. Logistic regression identified factors linked to endoscopic decompression failure, while Cox regression analyzed recurrence factors.ResultsAmong the 75 patients who underwent endoscopic decompression, 60 (80%) showed improvement, whereas 15 (20%) did not. In the logistic regression analysis, younger (≤ 65 years) age (odds ratio [OR], 10.21; 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.21–47.09) and larger (≥ 85 mm) maximum cross‐sectional diameter of distended colon (OR, 5.06; 95% CI, 1.12–22.87) were associated with failure of initial endoscopic treatment. Among the 60 patients who initially improved with endoscopic treatment, cumulative recurrence rates were 30% at 1 year and 36.7% at 3 years. In the Cox regression analysis, a larger (≥ 85 mm) maximum cross‐sectional diameter of distended colon (hazard ratio [HR], 3.27; 95% CI, 1.25–8.59) and a greater (≥ 230 mm) longitudinal axis length of spiraled colon (HR, 5.18; 95% CI, 1.67–16.05) at diagnosis were related to volvulus recurrence.ConclusionsYounger age (≤ 65 years) and severe colonic dilatation (≥ 85 mm) were associated with endoscopic decompression failure, while severe colonic dilatation (≥ 85 mm) and marked longitudinal elongation (≥ 230 mm) were independently related to recurrence. These findings may guide early surgical consultation for high‐risk sigmoid volvulus patients and subsequent resection planning after successful decompression.

Introduction

Sigmoid volvulus, the twisting of the sigmoid colon around its axis, can cause intestinal obstruction and life‐threatening complications, including intestinal infarction and necrosis. Risk factors for sigmoid volvulus include male sex, advanced age, a vegetable‐rich diet, chronic constipation, prior abdominal or pelvic surgery, dolicomesentery, elongated bowel loops with narrow mesosigmoid parietal attachments, and psychiatric and neurological disorders, such as Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, diabetes, and hypokalemia [1, 2, 3]. It accounts for 2%–3% of intestinal obstructions and 5%–10% of colonic obstructions. Sigmoid volvulus has a high recurrence rate of approximately 30%–90% and a mortality rate of 25%–50%, often due to complications such as bowel ischemia and sepsis [1].

The initial management strategy for patients presenting with sigmoid volvulus often involves endoscopic decompression followed by emergent surgery in cases of endoscopic treatment failure [1]. Considering the high recurrence rate associated with endoscopic decompression and the ongoing risks of ischemia and perforation with each recurrence, delayed elective surgery is considered after improvement. Initial endoscopic decompression not only stabilizes patients for elective surgery but also improves anastomosis success and reduces colostomy rates compared to emergent surgery [2].

Despite the aforementioned usefulness of endoscopic treatments, endoscopic decompression as a universal first step has drawbacks. It can delay emergent surgery in patients with severe ischemia and lead to fatal outcomes. Some patients with sigmoid volvulus fail endoscopic treatment and require immediate surgery, making communication with the surgical team during the periendoscopic period crucial. Although elective surgery is advised to prevent sigmoid volvulus recurrence [3], surgical risks are considerable in patients with debilitated sigmoid volvulus, such as elderly patients and those with multiple comorbidities.

Accordingly, we analyzed the factors associated with endoscopic decompression failure and investigated the factors of recurrence following endoscopic decompression. We hypothesized that specific demographic and radiologic factors, particularly colon measurements on imaging studies, would be significantly associated with both endoscopic treatment failure and recurrence risk.

Methods

Patients

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Severance Hospital (approval number: 4‐2024‐0668). This retrospective study reviewed the medical records of adult patients diagnosed with sigmoid volvulus by using abdominal computed tomography (CT) at Severance Hospital and Yongin Severance Hospital between January 2005 and January 2023.

During the study period, 96 patients were diagnosed with sigmoid volvulus. Among these, seven patients experienced spontaneous decompression under medical observation during the preoperative period [4]. One patient refused the recommended endoscopic procedure due to advanced age and mental confusion. Thirteen patients underwent emergency surgical intervention due to signs of bowel ischemia or peritonitis. The main analysis focused on 75 patients who initially received endoscopic decompression (Figure 1).

Flowchart on patient selection process.

Data Collection

Data collection involved reviewing electronic medical records. The following data were collected: demographic information (sex and age at diagnosis); radiologic measurements (maximum cross‐sectional diameter of the dilated colon as determined by abdominal X‐ray and CT at diagnosis, along with the longitudinal and transverse dimensions of the tortuously extending colon across the midline by CT imaging); clinical milestones (dates of diagnosis, recurrence, surgical intervention, and last follow‐up); diagnostic findings (presence of air–fluid levels on initial abdominal X‐ray, possibility of bowel ischemia on CT scans); functional status (history of nursing home residency); and medical history (comorbidities, neuropsychiatric disorders, prescribed medications, previous abdominal surgeries, previous episodes of sigmoid volvulus).

CT Findings of Sigmoid Volvulus

An experienced radiologist identified sigmoid volvulus on the initial abdominal CT scans. Radiological signs suggestive of bowel ischemia on initial CT were evaluated. CT findings interpreted as suggestive of possible ischemia included decreased mural enhancement of the sigmoid colon, diffuse bowel wall thickening consistent with edematous changes, pericolic or mesenteric fat stranding, pericolic fluid collection or ascites, and mesenteric whirling with associated signs of reduced perfusion. Some patients also showed obliteration or reduced flow of the distal inferior mesenteric artery, as well as newly developed ascites in the setting of a markedly distended bowel loop. In one patient, pneumatosis coli was noted without definite bowel wall defect. The maximum cross‐sectional diameter of the colon was measured on axial CT images perpendicular to the colonic longitudinal axis, with the measurement site determined with reference to the colonic configuration on coronal images. The longitudinal and transverse dimensions of the colon as it spiraled across the midline were measured on coronal CT images (Figure 2). The coronal image with the longest longitudinal axis was selected as the primary measurement. The transverse axis was defined as the line perpendicular to the identified longitudinal axis in the same image. When the longitudinal axis was not perfectly linear, terminal points were established, and a hypothetical straight line connecting these points was used to measure the longitudinal axis. All measurements were performed by an experienced gastroenterologist (S.Y. Choi).

Measurement of computed tomography–related parameters.

Colonoscopic Detorsion Procedure

Patients diagnosed with sigmoid volvulus on radiographic and CT imaging underwent endoscopic procedures in the endoscopy unit after the administration of glycerin and/or warm saline enemas. The procedures were performed using a flexible colonoscope in the left lateral position. When distal stenosis due to torsion was observed, the colonoscope was advanced to decompress the sigmoid colon by aspirating air and fluids while assessing mucosal viability. Emergent surgery was advised in cases of necrotic or ischemic colonic mucosa. In other cases, the colonoscope was advanced past the proximal stenosis to achieve further decompression. All endoscopic procedures were performed by experienced gastroenterologists (senior fellows and faculty members). Endoscopic detorsion was performed in patients without definite bowel ischemia on CT, as it allows for rapid intervention before surgery and is a relatively safe procedure when performed by an experienced specialist. Endoscopic detorsion was not performed in patients with definite bowel ischemia on CT. Ischemic changes were identified endoscopically by bluish‐purple mucosal discoloration, but no cases of severe ischemia or gangrene were observed.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics Version 26, Chicago, IL, USA). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of endoscopic decompression were compared using Student's t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify risk factors for endoscopic decompression failure in patients with sigmoid volvulus. Risk factors for recurrence after reduction were analyzed using the Cox proportional hazards model and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. Multivariable analyses were performed using a backward variable selection method. Statistical significance was defined as p ≤ 0.05 using two‐tailed tests.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Among 75 patients who underwent primary endoscopic decompression, the mean follow‐up duration was 706.8 days. The cohort was predominantly male (65.3%) with a mean age of 67.5 years. Patients with successful endoscopic decompression were significantly older than those with unsuccessful decompression (70.7 vs. 55.8 years, p = 0.011). The mean maximum cross‐sectional diameter of the bowel was 98.85 mm on abdominal X‐rays and 83.0 mm on CT. The tortuous colon crossing the midline on CT had a mean longitudinal axis length of 223.4 mm and a transverse axis length of 145.2 mm. Air–fluid levels were present on the initial abdominal radiographs in 45.3% of the patients. Radiological findings indicating a possible risk of bowel ischemia on CT were found in 13.3% of patients and were significantly more frequent in patients with unsuccessful endoscopic decompression (p = 0.023) (Table 1). Factors Associated With Endoscopic Decompression Failure

Among 75 patients treated for sigmoid volvulus at Sinchon Severance Hospital and Yongin Severance Hospital, endoscopic decompression was successful in 60 (80%) and unsuccessful in 15 (20%). Among the 15 patients who failed endoscopic decompression, 11 underwent semi‐elective surgery during the same hospital admission. The remaining four patients, despite failed decompression, exhibited partial symptomatic improvement and clinical stability, allowing for discharge and subsequent semi‐elective surgery [5]. Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with endoscopic decompression failure (Table 2). Univariable analysis revealed four significant risk factors: younger age at diagnosis (p = 0.001), maximum colonic diameter ≥ 85 mm on CT (p = 0.032), evidence of bowel ischemia on CT (p = 0.018), and history of bowel surgery (p = 0.019). In multivariable analysis, younger age at diagnosis (odds ratio [OR], 10.21; 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.21–47.09; p = 0.003) and maximum colonic diameter ≥ 85 mm (OR, 5.06; 95% CI, 1.12–22.87; p = 0.035) remained significant factors associated with treatment failure. Among the 15 patients who required emergent surgery after failed endoscopic treatment, 13 (86.7%) showed clinical improvement, while two (13.3%) experienced recurrence. Factors Associated With Recurrence After Endoscopic Decompression

The baseline characteristics of patients with successful endoscopic detorsion are summarized in Table S1. Among the 60 patients who were successfully treated with endoscopic decompression, 22 experienced recurrences. The median follow‐up period was 544 days (interquartile range: 65.5–807.3 days), and cumulative recurrence rates were 30.0% at 1 and 36.7% at 3 years (Figure 3). Figure 4 shows the cumulative recurrence rates stratified by maximal bowel distension and the longitudinal axis length of the spiraled colon. Patients with colonic distension ≥ 85 mm showed significantly higher recurrence rates than those with colonic distension < 85 mm (p = 0.0038, log‐rank test). Similarly, longitudinal axis length ≥ 230 mm was associated with significantly higher recurrence rates than < 230 mm (p = 0.0029, log‐rank test).

Among 22 patients with recurrence, 13 underwent elective or emergent surgery. Among the surgical patients, 10 (76.9%) showed clinical improvement, whereas three (23.1%) died from perioperative complications. Among the nine patients managed nonsurgically, four (44.4%) achieved resolution after a single additional endoscopic decompression, four (44.4%) experienced multiple recurrences (range: 2–6), and one died during emergent surgical planning. Among those with multiple recurrences, one patient died from aspiration pneumonia related to recurrent volvulus, complicated by advanced age (91 years) and comorbidities.

Discussion

To date, studies on recurrence factors after endoscopic decompression for sigmoid volvulus remain limited. Previous research has suggested that sigmoid colon extension and maximum diameter are linked to recurrence; however, these findings were based on univariable Kaplan–Meier analysis, with multivariable analysis limited by uncontrolled time variables [6]. Another study reported CT‐based recurrence factors but did not specify whether the initial treatment involved conservative management or endoscopic decompression, relying solely on univariable analysis without multivariable adjustment [7]. A separate study conducted correlation analyses using a relatively large sample size but did not perform a multivariable analysis [8]. In contrast, this study includes the largest sample size among those analyzing recurrence factors after endoscopic treatment and identifies factors independently associated with recurrence while adjusting for multiple confounding variables.

Sigmoid volvulus requires prompt detorsion via endoscopic or surgical intervention. While endoscopic decompression has a reported success rate of 55%–90%, recurrence remains high at 30%–85% after successful decompression [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. In our study, endoscopic decompression was successful in 80% of the cases, with a 3‐year cumulative recurrence rate of 36.7%. These findings are consistent with previously reported outcomes.

Previous studies have explored the factors associated with endoscopic decompression failure. Surek et al. [16] reported that previous abdominal surgery and cecal distension (> 10 cm) increased failure risk due to increased intra‐abdominal pressure, mesenteric edema, and restricted colonic mobility. Iida et al. [15] identified absence of tenderness, laxative use, and prior surgery as factors associated with successful decompression. Regarding laxative use, they reasoned that chronic use leads to intestinal elongation, making endoscopic reduction technically easier than for sudden‐onset volvulus without colonic elongation. Previous studies reported conflicting results regarding the risk of endoscopic treatment failure in patients with a history of abdominal surgery. In our study, a history of abdominal surgery was associated with failure in univariable analysis but was not significant in multivariable analysis.

A key novel finding of our study is that younger age (≤ 65 years) was independently associated with endoscopic failure (OR, 10.21), a factor not previously reported. We hypothesize that age‐related changes contribute to this phenomenon. With aging, the incidence of dolichosigmoid, a dilated sigmoid colon with a mesentery that is wider than it is long, typically with a narrow mesenteric base, increases as intracolonic gases expand, while the elastogenesis of the colonic wall deteriorates, leading to the development of dolichocolon. Recurrent episodes of sigmoid volvulus may cause mesenteric fibrosis, resulting in a narrow‐based mesentery, which predisposes patients to both frequent recurrence and spontaneous decompression [17]. Dolichosigmoid may explain why younger patients experience a higher failure rate of endoscopic decompression than elderly patients. Additionally, aging leads to decreased elasticity and strength in connective tissues, primarily due to alterations in collagen and elastin fibers [18, 19]. Age‐related connective tissue weakening, fat redistribution, and muscle loss may affect colonic mobility and mesenteric tension. These findings align with Iida et al.'s [15] observations that patients with chronic colonic elongation, such as long‐term laxative users, had higher success rates of endoscopic decompression.

Another significant factor associated with endoscopic failure in our study was colonic distension ≥ 85 mm (OR, 5.06). A large colonic diameter increases intra‐abdominal pressure and mesenteric congestion, limiting the maneuverability required for successful endoscopic reduction. Surek et al. [16] similarly reported that cecal distension > 10 cm was associated with failure, further supporting the role of colonic diameter as a critical factor. Given the increased failure risk in these patients, early surgical consultation should be considered rather than repeated endoscopic attempts.

In our cohort, seven patients experienced presumed spontaneous decompression, a rate exceeding prior reports. These patients were generally older and had smaller colonic diameters, differing from the risk profile for decompression failure identified in our study. While a direct relationship remains speculative, this suggests that lower risk anatomical profiles may allow spontaneous improvement in selected patients. Further investigation is warranted.

While identifying factors linked to endoscopic failure is important, another clinical concern is determining which patients require elective surgery and which can be safely monitored following successful endoscopic decompression. In our study, the recurrence rates were 30% at 1 year and 36.7% at 3 years, which is lower than previous reports of 30%–60% at 1 year and 60%–90% at 3 years after endoscopic decompression [13, 14, 15, 16, 20, 21]. This discrepancy is likely due to our shorter median follow‐up (544 days), which may have underestimated recurrence. Although prophylactic elective surgery is recommended in current guidelines [2, 20, 22], many patients are elderly and frail, making personalized surgical risk assessment essential. Identifying high‐risk individuals for recurrence is critical to tailoring treatment strategies.

Several studies have attempted to define recurrence risk factors. Korkut et al. [17] reported elderliness, early onset, maleness, high‐fiber diet habit, high altitude, and most likely chronic constipation may be the practical clinical parameters of recurrent sigmoid volvulus. Two previous studies investigated CT‐based recurrence factors associated with recurrence after endoscopic decompression in patients with sigmoid volvulus. Kusunoki et al. [6] analyzed 30 patients who underwent successful endoscopic decompression and identified sigmoid colon extension, defined as a tortuous colon abutting the diaphragm or liver in transverse CT images and sigmoid colonic distension (> 8.9 cm), as factors associated with recurrence within 1 year. Humbert et al. [7] reported that smaller volumes of Liver Segments 2 and 3 (< 150 cm3) and significant colon distension (> 10 cm) were associated with an increased risk of sigmoid volvulus recurrence within 6 months.

More recently, Loria et al. developed and validated a predictive model for recurrence using a large cohort of 2078 Medicare patients managed nonoperatively [23]. They reported a 1‐year recurrence rate of 36.1%, which is comparable to the 30% observed in our study. In contrast to our study, which identified anatomical risk factors from CT imaging, their model predicted recurrence based on readily available clinical and demographic data, including age, sex, race, palliative care consultations, and various comorbidities. This approach highlights the value of patient‐level clinical characteristics in risk stratification, complementing our findings on the importance of radiological measurements.

In our study, two key factors were independently associated with recurrence: a maximum colonic diameter ≥ 85 mm (HR, 3.27) and a longitudinal axis length spiraled colon ≥ 230 mm (HR, 5.18). These parameters likely reflect a larger intra‐abdominal space, allowing volvulus to reoccur more easily. Unlike assessing tortuous colon abutting the diaphragm/liver or assessing the liver segmental volumes, which require radiological expertise, colonic dimensions can be readily measured using CT, making them practical and accessible prognostic indicators.

These findings have significant clinical implications, as patients with larger colonic diameters and elongated spiral axis have a 3.27‐ to 5.18‐fold increased recurrence risk. In such cases, elective surgery should be strongly considered after initial decompression to prevent life‐threatening complications. These findings provide valuable prognostic indicators that can guide clinicians in determining the necessity of surgical intervention after endoscopic treatment of sigmoid volvulus, balancing the benefits of elective surgery against surgical risks in frail patients.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective design, it may be prone to inherent biases. Second, despite including patients from two referral centers over an 18‐year period, the rarity of sigmoid volvulus resulted in a sample size too small to detect minor effect sizes or conduct adequately powered subgroup analyses. In addition, the relatively small number of outcome events limits the statistical power and necessitates cautious interpretation of the results, as it may increase the risk of overfitting or unstable estimates in multivariable analyses. Nevertheless, this remains the largest study to date analyzing factors associated with recurrence while adjusting for multiple confounders. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes and more robust event counts are warranted to validate these findings and refine risk stratification models.

In conclusion, younger age (≤ 65 years) and larger distended colon diameter (≥ 85 mm) are associated with endoscopic decompression failure in sigmoid volvulus patients. Additionally, larger distended colon diameter (≥ 85 mm) and greater longitudinal axis length of the spiraled colon (≥ 230 mm) are significant factors associated with recurrence. Patients at low risk of recurrence may be safely monitored after successful decompression, whereas those with high‐risk features should be considered for elective surgery to prevent recurrent volvulus and its complications. A tailored management approach based on these associated factors may help optimize patient outcomes.
