Global disease and economic burden of main mycobacterial infections in the working-age population from 1990 to 2021 with a forecast to 2045
Authors: Jiaxu Gu, Jiaming Wang, Long Zhong, Bingcheng Lu, Hongqiang Xie, Bo Yu, Yang Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Declining Leprosy, Rising TB in Elderly: Leprosy prevalence dropped (14.426–5.942/100,000), while TB cases increased in older adults (50–64 years).•Socioeconomic Gaps Drive Burden: Low-income regions face higher leprosy burdens (CI: −0.35); health spending (ρ = −0.557) and UHC (ρ = −0.785) reduce disparities.•Drug Resistance Threatens Progress: MDR/XDR-TB and HIV co-infections challenge gains; targeted funding and global cooperation urged.•Novel Predictive Modeling: Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort forecasts trends to 2045, revealing slowing declines and persistent inequalities.•Economic Factors Key to Control: GDP, health expenditure, and UHC strongly correlate with reduced disease burdens globally.

Introduction

Diseases caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Mycobacterium leprae contribute to a considerable proportion of the burden from mycobacterial infections. For instance, Mycobacterium tuberculosis causes fever, wasting, hepatomegaly, pulmonary findings, painless swelling in the cervical region, periventricular hemorrhage, and other symptoms,1, 2, 3 whereas Mycobacterium leprae leads to physical disabilities and increases psychological burden.4,5

Mycobacterial infections are among the most common diseases in people of all ages and impose a significant burden on global health.6 The incidence of mycobacterial infections is highest in impoverished areas.7 Over the past decades, countries and governments have used medications such as dapsone and rifampicin to treat mycobacterial infections; nevertheless, substantial disease burden has remained.8

In this study, we describe the disease and economic burden and trends of mycobacterial infections in the global working-age population (15–64 years) explore potential associated factors, and predict the disease burden until 2045. Our findings could aid in the development of targeted prevention and treatment measures.

Materials and methods

Overview and data collection

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study, an expansive and in-depth global epidemiological investigation, delivers a comprehensive evaluation of health detriments across 204 nations and regions. The study provides a longitudinal analysis from 1990 to 2021 of a spectrum of 369 distinct diseases and injuries and 88 identifiable risk factors.9,10 This metric is vital for health research, policy, and resource allocation.

We examined the tuberculosis and leprosy datasets of GBD 2021 (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/), which represent the main types of global mycobacterial infections sourced from the Global Health Data Exchange. Focusing on the key working-age population worldwide, we divided the data by age, ranging from 15 to 64 years, with each group covering 5 years. We also stratified the data by sex and age to accurately capture global demographic patterns.

Our geographical coverage of 204 countries and territories was extensive and provided a comprehensive global perspective. By incorporating 95% confidence intervals into our analysis, we accounted for natural fluctuations in the data, thereby enhancing the validity and reliability of our research findings. Notably, we recently added five new Socio-demographic Index (SDI) regions to the study. This expansion further enriched our dataset and improved our analytical capabilities across socio-economic contexts.

Statistical analysis

Predictive analysis

In our study, we integrated the Bayesian age–period–cohort model with a random-effects exponential link function to allow predictive analytics within a Bayesian statistical framework. We chose the integrated nested Laplace approximations method given its superior computational efficiency in performing approximate Bayesian inference; the method was a compelling alternative to conventional Markov chain Monte Carlo methods.11 Our predictive model forecasted age-standardized prevalence rates (ASPRs) and age-standardized disability-adjusted life year rates (ASDRs) for mycobacterial infections from 2022 to 2045. Additionally, we performed an in-depth stratified analysis after categorizing age groups in 5-year increments from 15 to 64 years. The Bayesian age–period–cohort model calculation method is as follows:(1)log(Yabc)=β0+αa+λp+τC+∈abc

Yabc denotes the observed value, such as ASPR or ASDR, for a specific age group a, period b, and birth cohort c. The parameter β0​ corresponds to the model intercept, representing the overall baseline value. The term λp reflects the period effect, accounting for temporal trends influencing all age groups simultaneously. The term αa signifies the age effect, capturing variations associated with different age groups. The term τC indicates the cohort effect, attributable to the influence of shared early-life experiences among individuals from the same birth cohort. Finally, ∈abc represents the random error term, which accommodates over-dispersion and captures variability not explained by the fixed age, period, or cohort effects.

Health inequality analysis

Health inequality remains a pivotal concern in global public health that highlights the inequitable distribution of health outcomes across various socioeconomic groups.12 In this study, we focused on the determinants of unequal access to healthcare services and their impact on health outcomes by calculating the concentration index for global mycobacterial infections. The concentration index quantifies the distribution of a health variable, such as disease incidence or mortality, across socioeconomic groups and usually measures the degree of inequality in health outcomes based on socioeconomic status. A negative concentration index indicates that health outcomes are more likely to occur in groups with low socioeconomic status, and the absolute value of the index reflects the severity of the inequality.

The concentration index calculation method is as follows:(2)CI=2n∑i=1nyiRi−(n+1)n represents the total number of individuals in the sample, yi denotes the health outcome variable for the i-th individual, Ri​ indicates the fractional rank of the i-th individual in the socioeconomic distribution, ranging from 0 to 1.

The concentration index allows researchers to identify groups that face inequalities in accessing healthcare services and how this affects their health outcomes. This analysis helps policymakers and public health experts design and implement targeted interventions that reduce health inequalities and improve the health of the population as a whole.

Correlation analysis

Additionally, we downloaded relevant economic and health indicators such as the current health expenditure per capita (Current United States), the gross domestic product per capita purchasing power parity (GDP per capita PPP; International United States), and the universal health coverage (UHC) service coverage index from the World Bank database.13 Using a Spearman correlation analysis, we evaluated the correlation between the ASPR and the ASDR with various indicators across 204 countries and territories. All of the relevant indicators were transformed into logarithm base 2 to more clearly elucidate trends in data changes. This analysis aimed to identify potential factors that influence the burden of disease to provide actionable recommendations for disease prevention and control.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A p value of less than 0.05 in a two-tailed test was deemed to indicate statistical significance.

Result

Global and regional burden of mycobacterial infections

Leprosy

A significant downward trend in the global prevalence of leprosy was observed: the overall global prevalence of leprosy dropped from 14.426/100,000 in 1990 to 5.942/100,000 in 2021. Although the reduction in prevalence was greater in men (a decrease of 10.81/100,000), the burden of disease was consistently higher in men (7.42/100,000) than in women (4.46/100,000) in 2021. Significant differences in the prevalence of leprosy were observed across countries and regions. For instance, the prevalence in low-SDI regions declined markedly from 48.382/100,000 in 1990 to 14.620/100,000 in 2021. The prevalence in high-SDI regions was relatively low, decreasing from 0.178/100,000 in 1990 to 0.129/100,000 in 2021 (Table 1). Furthermore, differing patterns were observed in the prevalence of leprosy across regions such as the Caribbean and Central America, the Persian Gulf, Balkan Peninsula, Southeast Asia, West Africa, the Eastern Mediterranean, and Northern Europe. High-income areas such as Northern Europe reported near-zero cases, whereas moderate rates were observed in Latin America (e.g., Dominican Republic, 3.40/100,000). The highest burdens were observed in Southeast Asia (The Republic of India, 16.17/100,000) and West Africa (The Federal Republic of Nigeria, 8.66/100,000), and extreme rates were detected in the Pacific Islands (The Republic of Kiribati, 93.25/100,000). Notably, China demonstrated substantial progress in leprosy control. The ASPR of leprosy in China decreased to 0.455/100,000 (95% uncertainty interval, UI: 0.340–0.598) in 2021. A significant downward trend in disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) from leprosy was observed from 1990 to 2021. Globally, DALYs decreased from 0.710 in 2000 to 0.312 in 2021, with an average annual percentage change (AAPC) of −2.63 (95% UI: −2.70 to −2.55). Notably, the decline in leprosy DALYs was most pronounced (−4.00; 95% UI: −4.16 to −3.83) in low-SDI regions (Table 1). The disease burden of leprosy remained relatively high in some countries. For example, 0.85 DALYs per 100,000 people were observed in The Republic of India (95% UI: 0.52–1.30; Supplementary Table S2).

Tuberculosis

The prevalence of tuberculosis was consistently lowest in high-SDI locations in 1990 (24,112.02/100,000) and 2021 (15,595.34/100,000). However, the incidence decreased most prominently in low-SDI countries (from 41,056.27/100,000 to 29,897.99/100,000) over the past 21 years, followed by low-middle-SDI countries (from 37,689.54/100,000 to 32,800.67/100,000). Although the global prevalence of tuberculosis did not significantly decline (from 35,009.04/100,000 to 27,744.54/100,000), DALYs dropped by more than half (from 1554.50/100,000 to 608.00/100,000). Whereas DALYs in high-SDI countries decreased in 2021 by more than 80% compared with 2000 in terms of proportion, low-SDI locations experienced the largest decrease in quantity (from 5246.00/100,000 to 1923.35/100,000; Table 1). Data from 2021 showed that the global prevalence of tuberculosis, like that of leprosy, varied significantly across regions. The Republic of Kiribati had the highest prevalence of tuberculosis (50,758.27/100,000; 95% UI: 39,917.11–58,447.83), whereas Spain had a low prevalence rate (2752.41/100,000; 95% UI: 1769.48–3984.43; Supplementary Table S3).

From the standpoint of DALYs, tuberculosis inflicted substantial health losses globally. DALA data were not optimistic in some countries with a large population base. For instance, DALYs were 1231.54/100,000 (95% UI: 1038.49–1520.27) in The Republic of India. China (71.77/100,000; 95% UI: 54.79–93.98) and Republic of Indonesia (1115.06 per 100,000 people; 95% UI: 878.89–1416.67) also had relatively high DALYs (Supplementary Table S4).

Tuberculosis

Unlike leprosy, although the ASPR of tuberculosis has shown an overall downward trend between 1990 and 2045, the number of cases fluctuated significantly, with some age groups showing an upward trend. For instance, the number of cases for the 35–39-year age group increased from 12,951,053 in 1990 to 17,211,930 by 2024, and then declined to 14,994,106 by 2045. For example, the number of cases for the 55–59 age group rose from 6811,273 in 1990 to 15,046,250 by 2045, representing a 2.2-fold increase (Fig. 1C).

The ASDR in individuals of all ages, especially those aged 45–64 years, declined rapidly from 1990 to 2021. However, the rate of decline slowed down significantly from 2022. The number of DALYs followed a similar pattern to that of leprosy (Fig. 1D).

Discussion

Our findings showed that the ASPR and the number of leprosy cases decreased annually, whereas the ASPR from tuberculosis generally declined, with fluctuations and even increases in some age groups. A worldwide downward trend in the ASDR for mycobacterial infections was observed. Although fluctuations or increases in the ASDRs from both leprosy and tuberculosis were observed from 2000 to 2021, they were followed by rapid declines. Based on the results of predictive modeling, we conclude that control and prevention efforts for leprosy have had a positive impact globally, whereas tuberculosis remains a major public health challenge.

The global trend of declining disease burden from mycobacterial infections is attributed to various factors. For instance, the multidrug therapy for leprosy that was proposed in the last century involves the simultaneous use of multiple anti-leprosy drugs.14 This approach not only reduces the risk of drug resistance but also shortens the duration of infectiousness. Additionally, the administration of a single dose of rifapentine to household contacts of leprosy patients reduces the cumulative disease incidence by 84%,15 further diminishing the transmission of leprosy and helping to control the emergence of new leprosy cases. Furthermore, to reduce the disease burden, the government can better monitor the incidence of new cases and historical data by establishing a special fund.16

The widespread administration of the bacillus Calmette–Guérin vaccine has effectively controlled tuberculosis in children, thus reducing the global burden of mycobacterial infections.17 Moreover, the current development of new tuberculosis vaccines, such as the adjuvanted protein subunit vaccine and viral-vectored vaccines, may further decrease the global burden of tuberculosis.18,19 In addition to vaccines for prevention, classic anti-tuberculosis drugs such as isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol are widely used in clinical treatment.20 The introduction of novel drugs such as linezolid, bedaquiline, and pretomanid in recent years has significantly improved outcomes in multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) cases of tuberculosis.21, 22, 23

In addition to vaccines and drugs, new diagnostic methods have helped reduce the disease burden of mycobacterial infections. The enhanced nanobiosensor, an advanced detection tool for identifying Mycobacterium tuberculosis, produces linearly increasing detection results within a protein concentration range of 1 fg/mL to 100 µg/mL. The entire testing cycle of this sensor lasts only 30 s.24 Additionally, a novel type of single-walled carbon nanotube-based field-effect transistor for the diagnosis of tuberculosis has further contributed to the reduction of the global disease burden of mycobacterial infections.25 Importantly, molecular techniques such as the GeneXpert MTB/RIF and interferon-gamma release assays (e.g., T-SPOT.TB) have become essential tools for rapid and accurate diagnosis, particularly in resource-limited settings.26,27 These innovations enable the early detection and timely intervention that are critical for controlling transmission.

Despite the declines in the ASPR and ASDR of tuberculosis, the increasing trend in the number of affected individuals that was revealed by our prediction models was shaped by multiple challenges. First, the rise in non-communicable comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus exacerbates tuberculosis susceptibility, because hyperglycemia impairs immune responses to Mycobacterium tuberculosis.28,29 Second, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) co-infection remains a critical concern, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where HIV-associated tuberculosis accounts for > 30% of cases.30 The immunosuppressive effects of HIV accelerate tuberculosis progression and amplify transmission risk.31 Third, although the prevalence of drug-susceptible tuberculosis declined, MDR and XDR strains proliferated, with rifampicin mono-resistance in South Africa rising from 15.3% in 2011 to 21.4% in 2014.32 One study found that the prevalence of drug-susceptible tuberculosis declined over the years and was replaced by MDR or even XDR tuberculosis infections, indicating that the treatment of tuberculosis has become more challenging.33 In another global burden of disease study, the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) and ASPR of MDR-tuberculosis increased significantly.34 These trends underscore the need for integrated strategies that simultaneously address comorbidities, co-infections, and drug resistance. As with tuberculosis, although the ASPR and ASDR of leprosy significantly decreased, the rate of decline slowed down after 2022, possibly due to the emergence of drug resistance. This phenomenon has already been observed in the Brazilian Amazon region.35 In addition to causing drug resistance, HIV infection leads to a decline in immunity, which has also contributed to the rise in the number of mycobacterial infection cases and deaths.36 From 1990 to 2019, the worldwide ASIR of HIV-XDR-tuberculosis increased significantly by an average of 14.77% annually, and the ASIR of XDR tuberculosis associated with HIV also increased.37 Another study showed that the incidence of leprosy increased in countries with a high prevalence of HIV.38 Drugs that address these situations are expensive and difficult to approve.39

Furthermore, the projected 2.2-fold increase in tuberculosis cases between 1990 and 2045 among 55–64-year-olds reflects a complex interplay of biological and socioeconomic factors. The immunosenescence-age-related decline in immune function, particularly in T-cell responses, significantly increases susceptibility to tuberculosis reactivation and progression; studies have shown that older adults have a higher risk of developing active tuberculosis than younger adults.40 This vulnerability is further compounded by the high prevalence of comorbidities (such as diabetes) in aging populations that independently impair anti-mycobacterial immunity.41 Concurrently, socioeconomic factors and diagnostic challenges associated with atypical clinical presentations in older patients create synergistic risks that sustain tuberculosis transmission in this demographic. These findings highlight the urgent need for age-specific tuberculosis control strategies that integrate comorbidity management with improved diagnostic approaches in high-risk older adult populations.

The health inequality analysis and the Spearman correlation analysis based on indicators selected from the World Bank database revealed significant disparities in the disease burden of mycobacterial infections across countries and regions with varying economic levels. For instance, although the health inequality resulting from tuberculosis prevalence was not pronounced, a significant inequality in DALYs was observed. Despite the decreasing trend in health inequity caused by leprosy and tuberculosis between 1990 and 2021, disparities persisted, and resource-limited areas likely required additional support and intervention measures. A study revealed that tuberculosis cases in the Federative Republic of Brazil increased significantly during the economic recession.42 Another study indicated that a 2% reduction in global tuberculosis deaths is equivalent to a reduction of $17.5 trillion in economic losses.43 In addition, a research team in China found a valid correlation between public health expenditure and the incidence of tuberculosis;44 our results were consistent with those of this study. All of these findings indicate inequalities in global mycobacterial infections across regions and the significant relationship between local economic level and health expenditure.

Strengths and limitations

This study supports that the burden of major mycobacterial infections worldwide cannot be ignored. Remarkable regional health inequalities were observed, and predictions were made about the next 20 years. Nevertheless, the study has some limitations. First, the GBD database did not incorporate data on non-tuberculous mycobacterial infections, which also cause some global burden. Second, some countries do not collect epidemiological data on leprosy, which may lead to an underestimation of the disease burden. Moreover, the disabilities defined by the GBD only cover symptoms such as physical and functional limitations and exclude mental illness that results from secondary psychological burdens, secondary infections, and other complications. Additionally, the burden of leprosy may be further underestimated in regions with incomplete surveillance systems or missing epidemiological data. To improve accuracy, future studies should prioritize integrating alternative data sources such as Non-Governmental Organizations reports or local health surveys.

Suggestions

Tuberculosis has received a high level of global attention in discussions regarding global mycobacterial infections.45,46 In contrast, leprosy has not garnered the high level of attention it deserves, despite continuing to impose a significant global health burden.47 Our findings indicated significant regional disparities in the disease burden of mycobacterial infections among individuals aged 15–64 years and a concerning upward trend in the prevalence of tuberculosis cases. Leadership by the World Health Organization and national health agencies is of paramount importance to ameliorate this situation.

For instance, international organizations such as the World Health Organization and the United Nations Development Programme should continuously monitor the prevalence of mycobacterial infections across countries and regions and offer guidance and recommendations accordingly.48,49 Furthermore, transfer payment mechanisms are essential for increasing health expenditures in underdeveloped areas50 to reduce the incidence of new cases and alleviate the disease burden caused by infections in these regions. Second, an intensified effort in the research and development of new drugs and vaccines is required to mitigate the burden of drug-resistant mycobacterial infections.51 Finally, enhancing specialized nursing training and elevating the professional caliber of dermatologists and infectious disease specialists can improve detection rates and enhance the medical experience for patients.52

Conclusions

Although the global burden of mycobacterial infections among individuals aged 15–64 years is easing, significant health inequities persist across regions. Given the lack of reminders and reliable guidance, the current state is being overlooked. To enhance the quality of life for patients and alleviate the economic and social burdens of the disease, increasing public health investment and oversight is strongly recommended.
