Influence of organic growing medium supplemented with jeevamrit on physiological and biochemical responses of ornamental kale under subtropical conditions
Authors: Shabnam Pangtu, Puja Sharma, Sita Ram Dhiman, Mast Ram Dhiman, Anju Sharma, Shilpa Kamal, Poonam Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrganic farming is an influential practice for minimizing the environmental and ecological impact of sustainable development. The use of organic materials in agricultural practices can reduce the adverse effects on the environment by preserving its natural cycles during the recovery process. The study evaluated the effects of different organic growing media and liquid organic fertilizer i.e. Jeevamrit on foliage expansion, aesthetic appeal and nutritional properties of ornamental kale genotype ‘Kt OK-2’. The growing media tested included a combination of leaf mould collected from ban oak soil and cocopeat in a (1:1, v/v), cocopeat alone and leaf mould alone. Jeevamrit was soil drenched at weekly, fortnightly and monthly intervals and was compared with a control using the recommended dose of fertilizers. Results indicated that the growing medium of leaf mould and cocopeat (1:1, v/v) demonstrated superior qualitative and quantitative effects on plant growth compared to the other media, owing to its high nutritional content. Jeevamrit @ 20% at weekly intervals further enhanced plant performance. This combination of growing medium and jeevamrit led to the highest measurements of plant height (22.06 cm), spread (35.75 cm), number of leaves (46.43), rosette diameter (23.55 cm), percentage of green and coloured leaves per plant (36.84% and 67.73%) and pot display life (68.55 days). Furthermore, total chlorophyll content (32.88 mg/100 g), anthocyanin content (0.64 mg/100 g) and total carotenoid content (2.13 mg/100 g) of the plant was recorded highest for the same treatment combination. Additionally, the study assessed physico-chemical properties of the soil. The results underscore the effectiveness of using a balanced growing medium with regular organic manure applications to optimize plant health and aesthetic quality in ornamental kale.Keywords: Carotenoids, Cocopeat, Drenching, Jeevamrit, Leaf mould, Soil physico-chemical properties

Introduction

Ornamental kale (Brassica oleracea var. acephala DC.) is a crop with untapped potential in the floral industry. Its striking appearance, with vibrant central leaves in shades of pink and red, and its ease of cultivation [1, 2], makes it a promising addition to the industry. It is one of the oldest members of the cabbage family, originating in the eastern Mediterranean region [3–5]. Although it is closely related to cabbage, ornamental kale is characterized by its unique growth pattern, as it does not form a compact head at the top of the plant [6, 7]. Its cultivation requirements are similar to those of other Brassica vegetables, with its distinctive coloured leaves being the main decorative feature. Leaf colour, influenced by growing conditions, becomes more vibrant at cooler temperatures due to reduced chlorophyll production [8]. Additionally, ornamental kale is rich in essential nutrients and offers significant health benefits as a good source of dietary carotenoids [9].

Standardization of production practices is of utmost importance for the commercial cultivation of ornamental kale, specifically when grown as a potted plant. The crop’s nutritional requirements play a crucial role in the production of high-quality foliage. Although nutrients can be supplied from both organic and inorganic sources, excessive use of inorganic fertilizers can pose health risks and contribute to environmental pollution. On the other hand, liquid organic fertilizers offer a range of benefits. They have been found to enhance soil characteristics by increasing aggregate stability, hydrophobicity and total porosity, while simultaneously reducing bulk density and soil penetration resistance [10]. Moreover, they play a vital role in crop development by supplying necessary nutrients, improving soil moisture levels, promoting microbial activity, enhancing soil buffering and reducing soil toxicity. Organic liquid formulations, such as the well-known jeevamrit used in organic farming, are fermented products made from readily available materials and are specifically designed to enhance plant growth [11]. The adoption of organic methods has been proven not only to enhance crop productivity but also to preserve the physicochemical properties of the soil [12].

In addition to nutrient management, the selection of an appropriate growing media is essential for the successful production of ornamental kale as a potted plant. An effective growing medium directly affects root development, offers stable support for the plant, acts as a reservoir for nutrients and water, facilitate oxygen flow to the roots and enables gaseous exchange between the roots and the surrounding atmosphere [13]. The growing medium commonly used at many nurseries is top soil and containers filled with top soil can be heavy to handle and transport. To address these issues, identifying a lighter alternative for growing medium is essential. Cocopeat emerges as a viable option in this context and is increasingly recognized as a sustainable alternative to sphagnum peat in horticulture [14, 15]. Compared with other growing media, cocopeat offers favourable physical properties, including high total pore space, substantial water retention, low shrinkage, low bulk density and slow biodegradation [16]. In addition to cocopeat, other growing media such as leaf mould, formed from decayed leaves, act as a nutrient-rich reservoirs and show promising potential as a medium for plant growth. Similarly, forest soil serves as another potential growing medium, although physical and chemical attributes of forest soils can exhibit considerable variation. Factors responsible for these variations include climate, topography, weathering, vegetation and other living and non-living elements [17, 18]. Forest soils play a significant role in shaping the composition of forested areas, ground cover and the growth rates of trees [19, 20]. The soil profile, pH and nutrient cycling are crucial factors in evaluating the quality of forest sites. For example, soils up to 10 cm depth under oak forests contain higher levels of organic carbon (3.4–4.2%), total nitrogen (0.3–0.46%), and available phosphorus (0.01–0.012%) compared to soils under chir pine (Pinus roxburghii) and sal (Shorea robusta) forests. The C/N ratio is approximately 10:1, and the pH is slightly acidic (5.8–6.8) [21]. Research on forest soils in the Himalayan region suggests that these soils may be suitable as growing media for crops. Moreover, findings indicate that combining different growing media components is more effective for plant growth than using a single material alone [22].

Therefore, this study aims to assess the effects of liquid organic manure and various organic growing media on the growth, appearance and nutritional value of ornamental kale. The objective of the study is to establish standardized recommendations for the use of liquid organic manure and growing media to improve pot plant production for ornamental kale, thereby contributing to a more sustainable agricultural future.

Methods and materials

Experimental location

The field experiment was conducted at the Research Farm of the Department of Floriculture and Landscape Architecture, Dr. Yashwant Singh Parmar University of Horticulture and Forestry, Nauni, Solan (HP) − 173230. The research Farm is located at 30o51’0’’ N latitude and 77o11’30” E longitude at an elevation of 1276 m (above mean sea level) at Nauni, approximately 14 km away from the Solan city (Himachal Pradesh).

Crop husbandry and media preparations

The seeds of the ornamental kale (Brassica oleracea var. acephala DC.) genotype ‘Kt OK-2’ (pot plant) were procured from the ICARI-IARI, Regional Station, Katrain (Kullu-Valley) H.P. ‘Kt OK- 2’ is an inbred line developed at the station with “Nagoya” as commercial variety for pot plant production. The seeds of the genotype for pot plant production were sown in trays containing a mixed medium (Soil: Sand: FYM) (1:1:1, v/v). The seeds were treated with fungicide and sown in the trays. Seedlings become ready for transplanting in 25–30 days. Different growing media i.e. leaf mould, cocopeat and mixed (Leaf mould: Cocopeat) (1:1, v/v) were prepared. Leaf mould was collected from Ban oak forest at a depth of 5–10 cm from the upper layer of the forest floor. Cocopeat was washed thoroughly to remove phenolic compounds, reduce EC and dried under the sun before use. The ingredients were mixed thoroughly before growing as per the treatment combinations. These media were filled in plastic pots of 10 inches in diameter. Healthy, disease-free seedlings of uniform size and vigour were planted in pots (one seedling per pot) in the evening hours. Pots were irrigated immediately after transplanting. All the pots were kept under naturally ventilated polyhouses throughout the study. Watering was done on a regular basis during the active vegetative growth stage. However, during the winter months plants were irrigated manually twice a week depending upon the soil moisture content and prevailing weather conditions.

Preparation and application of jeevamrit

Jeevamrit (Chemical formulation- Table 1) was prepared by dissolving cow dung (10 kg/200 L), cow urine (10 L/200 L), jaggery (2 kg/200 L), pulse flour (1 kg/200 L) and a handful of soil [23]. All the ingredients were mixed in a plastic drum; the mixture was stirred clockwise and covered with a wet jute bag before being stored in the shade [24, 25]. The mixture was continuously stirred in a clockwise direction every morning and evening for seven days. On the seventh day, the solution was ready for the soil drenching and was used as per the treatment schedule. Application of jeevamrit was done in the form of soil drenching at intervals of 7, 15 and 30 days, starting after 15 days of transplanting. Jeevamrit was diluted to 20% before use by dissolving 20 L of jeevamrit solution in water to make a final volume of 100 L. Drench application of jeevamrit (250 mL per pot) was done manually.

Treatment details

The experiment was laid out in a factorial completely randomized design (CRD) with three replications and number of pots per replication were five. For quality pot plant production, genotype ‘Kt OK- 2’ of ornamental kale was grown in growing media i.e. M1: Leaf mould collected from ban oak soil: Cocopeat (1:1, v/v), M2: Cocopeat and M3: Leaf mould. Plants were drenched with 20% jeevamrit at different intervals (T1: weekly, T2: fortnightly, T3: monthly and T4: Control) (Table 2). In control treatment T4 N: P:K @ 30:20:20 g/m2 was applied as the recommended dose of fertilizer by mixing in the growing medium. Before laying out the experiment, different growing media were prepared and analysed for various physicochemical properties. The methods employed and results obtained for important characteristics (initial) of the experimental media are presented in Table 3. After harvesting of the crop, again analysis was done for different growing media during both the years and pooled data are presented in Fig. 3.

Biochemical estimations

Biochemical analysis was done after the fully mature stage using a composite plant sample. Total chlorophyll content was extracted in 80% acetone and the absorbance at 663 nm and 645 nm was read using a spectrophotometer against blank following standard protocols [31, 32]. Total anthocyanin content was determined by the spectrophotometric method and total carotenoid content of products was estimated by the solvent extraction method [33, 34].

Agrometeorological data

Monthly mean meteorological data related to temperature, rainfall and humidity were obtained from the Department of Environmental Science, Dr YS Parmar University of Horticulture and Forestry Nauni, Solan (HP). The data for the cropping period (September to February) are presented in Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis

The experiment was carried out in a factorial completely randomized design (CRD) and the results were presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to determine significant differences among treatments. Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test was used to compare mean values at p ≤ 0.05. Pooled data analysis was performed using SPSS version 16.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results and discussion

Vegetative parameters

The data for plant height are depicted in Table 3. It is evident that among treatments T1, T2, T3 and T4, the tallest plant height was recorded in M1 (22.06 cm, 20.34 cm, 20.22 cm, and 18.92 cm), while the shortest was observed in M2. Among different growing media (M1, M2 and M3), the highest plant height was seen in treatment T1 (22.06 cm, 15.28 cm and 18.37 cm), which is statistically similar to T2 and T3. In comparison, the lowest plant height was noted in T4.

Regarding plant spread, in all treatments (T1, T2, T3 and T4), the widest plant spread was observed in M1 (35.75 cm, 34.25 cm, 32.02 cm and 31.55 cm), which was statistically similar to M3. However, in M1, M2 and M3 the widest plant spread was observed in T1 (35.75 cm, 29.30 cm and 33.70 cm), which was statistically similar to T2, and the minimum spread was noted in T4. The combination of cocopeat and leaf mould, along with weekly drench application of jeevamrit, resulted in the maximum plant height and spread. This effect may be attributed to the conducive conditions created by the interactive effects of media and treatments, which facilitated better growth and development of the plants. These results are similar to a study in geraniums, where using leaf mould and peat-growing substrates resulted in maximum plant growth [35]. Similar findings were reported, showing excellent growth of perennials in leaf mould and peat substrate, with the addition of rock wool, leading to maximum plant height [36]. Furthermore, the present results corroborate the findings in Alternanthera, where cocopeat and other amendments improved the vegetative parameters [37].

Decorative parameters

The pooled data indicate that the minimum number of days required for colour development, early rosette formation, percentage of coloured leaves per plant, percentage of green leaves per plant and total number of leaves per plant was observed in T1M1. This treatment combination involved the jeevamrit drenching at weekly intervals, along with a mixture of Cocopeat and Leaf mould in a 1:1 (v/v).

The data in Table 4 shows that among treatments T1, T2, T3 and T4, the earliest colour formation was observed in M1 (40.80 days, 42.44 days, 44.15 days and 44.92 days). On the other hand, the maximum number of days taken for colour development was noted in M2. The days taken for colour formation in different growing media, M1, M2 and M3, were earliest in T1 (40.80 days, 49.26 days, and 45.48 days) and longest time was noted in T4. This could be attributed to the increased availability and utilization of nutrients, as well as the optimal doses of liquid organic manure. As a result, the plants nutrient uptake efficiency also increased, allowing them to achieve the required vegetative growth in a shorter period [38].

Regarding the percentage of coloured leaves per plant in all treatments (T1, T2, T3 and T4), the maximum percentage was observed in M1 (67.73%, 66.64%, 65.29% and 63.00%), while the minimum was noted in M2. In terms of the growing media (M1, M2 and M3), the maximum percentage of coloured leaves per plant was observed in T1 (67.73%, 60.56% and 64.92%), while the minimum was noted in T4. The data in Table 3 shows that among treatments T1, T2, T3 and T4, the highest percentage of green leaves per plant was observed in growing media M1 (36.84%, 34.16%, 32.16% and 30.14%), while the lowest was seen in M2. For the growing media M1, M2 and M3, the maximum percentage of green leaves per plant was recorded in T1 (36.84%, 33.17% and 34.08%), and the minimum was observed in T4.

The data in Table 3 indicate that in all treatments (T1, T2, T3 and T4), rosette formation occurred earliest in M1 (71.09 days, 72.41 days, 73.86 days and 75.35 days). However, the longest time for rosette formation was observed in M2. In terms of growing media (M1, M2 and M3), the earliest rosette formation was recorded in T1 (71.09 days, 78.68 days and 75.12 days), while the longest time for rosette formation was noted in T4.

Based on the pooled data, it is clear that among the treatments T1, T2, T3 and T4, the rosette diameter was largest in M1 (23.55 cm, 20.41 cm, 18.94 cm and 16.66 cm) and smallest in M2. For the growing media M1, M2 and M3, the maximum rosette diameter was observed in T1 (23.55 cm, 17.93 cm and 20.46 cm) and the minimum was observed in T4.

Similarly, the total number of leaves varied among treatments T1, T2, T3 and T4. The maximum number of leaves was recorded in M1 (46.43, 44.35, 43.10 and 41.33), and the lowest was recorded in M2. For growing media M1, M2 and M3, the maximum number of leaves was recorded in T1 (46.43, 32.48 and 39.58), while the lowest was recorded in T4.

The increased production of leaves per plant in the growing media can be attributed to its improved physico-chemical and biological properties, which contribute to the production of more biomass and longer shoots. The addition of cocopeat improved plant morphology, which positively impacted the number of leaves. The study results showed that the maximum number of leaves was obtained in the growing media containing Soil, Cocopeat and Leaf mould in equal proportions (1:1:1 v/v). This finding is consistent with the research in roses [39] and antirrhinum [40], indicating that leaf mould can be a recommended plant growth medium compared to others. In a study on zinnia [41], found significant results for leaf numbers in a growing media consisting of silt, leaf manure and coco coir substrates. Further, these results are supported by study on ornamental plants, where potting media influenced the vegetative growth [23]. Additionally, the positive effects of jeevamrit were noted in different field crops [42–44]. These findings align closely with the earlier research in petunias [45, 46], tuberose [47] showing that the jeevamrit treatment provided the best results in terms of the number of leaves. A higher number of leaves is positively correlated with a larger photosynthetic area, leading to increased food reserves and ultimately better rosette size.

Pot display life is an important parameter, and it is affected by various parameters. Among treatments T1, T2, T3 and T4, the highest pot display life was observed in M1 (68.55 days, 65.85 days, 65.24 days and 62.48 days), while the lowest was seen in M2. Among different growing media M1, M2 and M3, the maximum pot display life was recorded in T1 (68.55 days, 52.46 days and 59.23 days), with the lowest in T4. The pot display of the genotype ‘Kt OK-2’ indicates that the maximum pot display was observed in T1M1, which involved using jeevamrit as a drench application at weekly intervals along with a mixture of Cocopeat and Leaf mould (1:1, v/v). This might be attributed to the vigorous growth of the plant in the media, leading to enhanced nutrient and water uptake and ultimately increased yield [48, 49]. The duration for which a potted plant remains visually appealing is known as pot display life, and healthy plants generally have the longest pot display life.

Biochemical parameters

The chlorophyll, carotenoids and anthocyanin levels, which are the main pigments in leaves, provide valuable information about the plant’s physiological condition. The anthocyanin content in treatments T1, T2, T3 and T4 was highest in M1 (0.64 mg/100 g, 0.61 mg/100 g, 0.53 mg/100 g and 0.41 mg/100 g) and lowest in M2 (Fig. 2). Additionally, the anthocyanin content in the growing media M1, M2 and M3 was highest in T1 (0.64 mg/100 g, 0.58 mg/100 g and 0.61 mg/100 g) and lowest in T4. For total chlorophyll content in treatments T1, T2, T3 and T4, the highest values were observed in M1 (32.88 mg/100 g, 32.49 mg/100 g, 26.09 mg/100 g and 23.76 mg/100 g), with the lowest in M2. Among the growing media M1, M2, and M3, the maximum total chlorophyll content was noted in T1 (32.88 mg/100 g, 25.76 mg/100 g and 28.42 mg/100 g) and the minimum in T4. According to different studies, jeevamrit is an organic liquid formulation which boosts carotenoid content in treated plants and also has the potential to enhance chlorophyll a and b levels in treated plants, as reported in marigold [50] and sweet basil [24]. Additionally, it was found that application of jeevamrit along with vermicompost in Amaranthus caudatus enhances the leaf chlorophyll content of the leaves [51]. Furthermore, the carotenoid content in all treatments T1, T2, T3 and T4 was highest in M1 (2.13 mg/100 g, 2.09 mg/100 g, 2.04 mg/100 g and 1.89 mg/100 g) and lowest in M2. The carotenoid content in the different growing media M1, M2, and M3 was highest in T1 (2.13 mg/100 g, 2.02 mg/100 g, and 2.10 mg/100 g) and lowest in T4. It was suggested that applying jeevamrit doses at weekly intervals can enhance the growth, development and physicochemical attributes of the plant by providing essential nutrients [52]. Kinetin, found in jeevamrit, can increase the chlorophyll content in leaves, thereby improving photosynthetic activity, growth and yield [53].

Soil physicochemical parameters

Soil pH

The data on various soil physicochemical properties data are shown in Fig. 3. As regards pH in all the treatments T1, T2, T3 and T4 were found maximum in M3 (5.83, 5.89, 5.92 and 5.98) and the lowest was observed in M2. In different growing media M1, M2 and M3 higher values of pH were noted in T1 (5.83, 5.71 and 6.05) and the lowest pH was noted in T4. The higher soil pH was recorded in Leaf mould whereas, lower in Cocopeat. Cocopeat lowers the soil pH and makes it more appropriate for plant development, as for several plants a slightly acidic to neutral pH is favourable. A balanced growing medium of Leaf mould and Cocopeat showed better results for plant growth compared to other treatments. Furthermore, the application of jeevamrit resulted in reduction of soil pH. This might be due to the increased permeability of the soil by the application of jeevamrit [54].

Soil organic carbon

In all the treatments T1, T2, T3 and T4, maximum organic carbon content was found in M3 (2.62%, 2.49%, 2.36% and 2.28%) and the minimum was noted in M2. It is also evident from Fig. 3 that in different media M1, M2 and M3 maximum organic carbon content was found in T1 (2.62%, 1.93% and 3.10%) and minimum was noted in T4. Organic carbon was observed highest in M3 (Leaf mould collected from ban oak soil) and T1 (weekly application of jeevamrit). The global carbon cycle depends heavily on forests, which sequester a large portion of the atmosphere’s carbon stock. Depending on the specific management practices and activities employed, the carbon accumulation in forests can operate as both a source and a sink of carbon [55, 56]. The application of liquid manure has increased the incorporation of organic materials and stimulated root growth, which has increased soil organic carbon. Applying jeevamrit may have caused a drop in bulk density, which could be linked to higher levels of organic carbon content, corroborating earlier findings [57, 58].

Soil electrical conductivity (EC)

In all the treatments (T1, T2, T3 and T4), the highest electrical conductivity (EC) value was found in the M2 growing media, with respective values of 0.65 ds/m, 0.65 ds/m, 0.67 ds/m and 0.69 ds/m. Conversely, the lowest EC value was observed in the M3 growing media. Among different growing media (M1, M2 and M3), the maximum EC value was observed in T4, with values of 0.45 ds/m, 0.69 ds/m and 0.20 ds/m, while the minimum was noted in T1. Notably, the EC was observed to be at a minimum in the M3 growing media, specifically in Leaf mould collected from ban oak soil, and also in T1, attributed to the weekly application of jeevamrit. A low EC value means that the medium did not include an excessive quantity of salt, which may have damaged the plants due to salinity, but it also does not contain enough nutrients to allow for the healthy growth of the plants [59]. The EC of the media mixtures increased with increasing proportions of cocopeat [60].

Nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium content

The content of nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) differs according to the growing media. The nitrogen content in all the treatments (T1, T2, T3 and T4) was observed to be highest in M1 (0.51%, 0.50%, 0.48% and 0.46%) and lowest in M2. Among the different growing media (M1, M2 and M3), the highest nitrogen content was noted in T1 (0.51%, 0.48%, and 0.43%), while the lowest was noted in T4. Research indicates that Cocopeat contains nitrogen (0.43%) and phosphorus (0.38%), but the nutrient composition varies depending on the degree of decomposition and the retting process [61]. However, using cocopeat in higher proportions (> 50%) as a growing medium can lead to nitrogen immobilization, which is an inevitable outcome of microbial activity consuming the available carbon in the medium [62].

Based on the data presented in Fig. 3, it is clear that in all the treatments (T1, T2, T3 and T4) the highest phosphorus content was observed in growing media M1, with values of 0.35%, 0.32%, 0.30% and 0.27% respectively. The lowest phosphorus content was observed in growing media M2. In growing media M1, M2 and M3, the highest phosphorus content was observed in treatment T1, with values of 0.35%, 0.17% and 0.25% respectively, while the lowest was observed in treatment T4. The maximum potassium content was recorded in M2T1 treatment combination. Cocopeat, a growing medium, is composed of soluble chloride and sodium salts, along with higher levels of potassium maintaining an optimal nutritional balance for plant growth, cocopeat should be supplemented with appropriate organic composts. Adding organic manures to the soil enhances the accessibility of nitrogen, thereby promoting microbial growth and increasing basal respiration [63, 64]. The decomposition of organic materials stimulates soil microbial activity, leading to the production of organic acids that solubilise phosphates, consequently elevating the soil’s phosphorus concentration [65].

The potassium content was measured in different treatments (T1, T2, T3 and T4), with the highest potassium content found in M2 (0.84%, 0.82%, 0.80% and 0.78%) and the lowest recorded in M3. It is also evident from Fig. 3 that media M1, M2 and M3 had the highest potassium content in T1 (0.62%, 0.84% and 0.40%), with the lowest in T4. Moreover, organic matter and manures may interact with clay to generate K2O ions, thereby increasing the availability of potassium in the soil [66]. Additionally, leaf mould obtained from ban oak soil contains nutrients beneficial for both plant growth and soil health. Vegetation has a significant impact on the soil’s physicochemical qualities [67–69]. The nutrient thus returned to the soil has a strong feedback effect on ecological processes [70–72].

Conclusion

The use of a balanced growing medium comprising Leaf mould collected from ban oak soil and Cocopeat in a (1:1, v/v), combined with weekly drenching of jeevamrit, proved to be an effective plant growth stimulant for quality pot plant production. This approach enhanced biological efficiency and improved nutrient availability, thereby fostering healthier plant growth and improved soil health. By leveraging the strengths of these components, this method creates an optimal environment for robust ornamental kale development and promotes sustainable and eco-friendly horticultural practices. These findings suggest that combining organic media and jeevamrit can be a feasible approach for producing high-quality and aesthetically pleasing ornamental kale plants, making it highly applicable for both commercial as well as home gardening utilization.
