Reliability of intraoral scanning for capturing gingival morphology and color in periodontitis patients
Authors: Linlin Li, Wei Li, Shengyuan Huang, Dong Dai, Jing Zhao, Jiang Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntraoral scanners (IOSs) can directly capture the three-dimensional (3D) morphology and color of soft tissue and dentition, but a few studies have tested the reliability of IOSs for recording gingival tissues of periodontitis patients in vivo. The aim of this study was to evaluate the reliability of intraoral scanning for recording the morphology and color of gingival tissues in the anterior teeth region of periodontitis patients.MethodsDirect and indirect digital maxillary models of ten periodontitis patients were acquired by an IOS and a conventional impression (CI), respectively. Superimposition of the digital models was performed to calculate the 3D deviation of the gingival tissues of the anterior teeth between pairwise digital models. Color values (L*a*b*) were recorded at thirteen anterior gingival sites, and the color differences (ΔE*) were calculated. Precision was analyzed using paired-samples t-test, color precision with Mann-Whitney U test, and ΔE among different points with one-way ANOVA.ResultsThe IOS demonstrated a significantly lower 3D deviation (42.5 ± 17.9 μm) compared to CIs (105.3 ± 40.8 μm) (Ρ = 0.001). The overall ΔE* was 5.69 ± 2.63. The ΔE values of the gingival margins and middle gingival tissue were 6.24 ± 2.62 and 5.07 ± 2.46, respectively, and a significant difference was found (Ρ = 0.009). The ΔE* values of different points were not equal.ConclusionsIntraoral scanning provides a highly reliable representation of the gingival morphology of periodontitis patients. The precision of the IOS to reproduce color information of the middle gingival tissue is better than that of the gingival margins.Trial registration IDThe Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, No. ChiCTR2200058275 (04/04/2022).

Background

Periodontal disease is characterized by the loss of alveolar bone, the formation of periodontal pockets, and alterations in the color, shape, and texture of gingival tissue [1]. The clinical assessment of gingival inflammation relies on morphological changes [2, 3] as well as color variations in gingival tissues, which are usually performed visually by clinicians in clinic. However, visual inspection is subjective and difficult to quantify depending on individual experience. Therefore, there is a need for non-invasive, objective, and precise methods to record and quantify changes in gingival tissues, which could facilitate the evaluation of morphological variability, early detection of periodontal conditions, and identification of treatment needs [4, 5].

Several methods have been used for the measurement of gingival morphology. Subjective visual inspection and evaluation of probe transparency through the gingival sulcus are straightforward, but exhibit relatively poor reliability [6]. The transgingival probing method can measure gingival thickness but may cause slight swelling at the puncture site, with accuracy influenced by the probe angle and force [7]. Ultrasonic measurement is a noninvasive method for measuring gingival thickness [8, 9]; however, its clinical application is limited due to challenges in positioning, technical sensitivity, and high cost. The plaster models enable measurement of gingival tissue changes, but deformation caused by impression material pressure during acquisition can impact the results [10]. Additionally, tearing and distortion of the elastic impression material during removal [11], as well as the deformation of plaster models during storage, can affect the measurement results.

Intraoral scanners (IOSs) can directly capture the three-dimensional (3D) morphology and color of soft tissue and dentition with enough detail in a contact-free manner, effectively eliminating potential force to soft tissue [4, 5, 12, 13]. Intraoral scanning has been proven to be time-efficient, patient-friendly, and able to avoid potential deformation (expansion, shrinkage, distortion) of impressions [12–14]. Digital models obtained with IOSs can be magnified and viewed from different directions for detailed inspection [15]. Additionally, IOSs can serve as a diagnostic tool to evaluate disease presence and progression [16, 17].To date, few studies have tested the reliability of IOSs for recording gingival tissues in vivo [18]. Ender [19] focused on a small area extending approximately 1 mm below the free gingival margin, whereas Wei [20] focused on the anterior teeth of participants without periodontitis, and Kuralt [5] focused on specific teeth. Further assessment is needed to evaluate the reliability of the gingival surface acquired through the IOS in individuals with periodontitis in vivo.

Clinical colorimetric assessment of gingival color is commonly performed via visual estimation, but the lack of a standard and effective color chart and plate for soft tissues poses a challenge [21]. The influence of environmental light on visual estimation is significant. Seshan [22] and Mayer [23] evaluated changes in gingival health using standard non-invasive gingival indices and reported that digital images can be utilized to document gingival inflammation through alterations in redness. However, digital images captured by cameras can be affected by surrounding lighting conditions, and capturing reproducible digital images remains highly challenging. Variations in angulation, lighting, and magnification are all factors that influence the presentation of a lesion’s size and color across different photographs [4, 12]. Spectrophotometric measurements and other instrumental methods are currently widely employed for direct color measurement. Nevertheless, these instruments require contact with soft tissue, potentially impacting the accuracy of the results because of probe compression [24].

Currently, the IOS can obtain not only 3D morphological information of the tissue but also texture information, allowing for the acquisition of nearly true colors of intraoral tissues [25]. The IOS has its own source of light, such that no additional light is needed and it is not affected by surrounding lighting conditions. Additionally, it captures the oral cavity in 3D, providing a panoramic view of the tooth rather than observing it from a specific angle or direction [4]. It has great potential for efficient and accurate data capture, facilitating remote evaluation and data verification. Accurately recording gingival tissues is essential. Therefore, the present study aimed to validate the reliability of a real-color intraoral scanner in recording the morphology and color of the anterior teeth gingiva of participants with periodontitis in vivo.

Methods

Participant recruitment

This study was an observational study. Ten participants with periodontitis (8 women and 2 men) aged 22–50 years and with an average age of 34.4 ± 8 years from Beijing Tongren Hospital Affiliated to Capital Medical University, were recruited. The sample size calculation was based on the mean 3D deviation values of the pilot results using the paired-samples t-test. The sample size was 2, which was calculated by PASS software (NCSS, V11.0, US) based on a calculated effect size of 5.086, Type I error at α = 0.05, and Type II error at 1-β = 0.90. All participants provided their consent. The inclusion criteria for this clinical study were as follows: patients diagnosed with stage II and stage III periodontitis based on 2018 International classification of periodontal diseases and implant diseases; within class I malocclusion of Angle’s classification; and good compliance. The exclusion criteria were as follows: orthodontic treatment; obvious pigmentation; upper lip frenulum with low attachment; temporal mandibular disease; severe systemic illness, mental illness or Parkinson’s disease; and an abnormal degree of mouth opening. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Bioethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital Affiliated to Capital Medical University, China (TREC2022-KY016), and was recorded in the Chinese Clinical Trial Register (ChiCTR2200058275).

Digital model acquisition

After calibration according to the manufacturer’s instructions, an IOS (Aoralscan 3, Shining 3D Co., Ltd., China) was used to capture the direct digital impressions by an experienced operator following the recommended strategy. The scan region included teeth from the right to left premolars and labial/buccal soft tissues of the anterior teeth, as well as part of the alveolar mucosa, to ensure that the gingiva was fully captured. The same scanning strategy was used for all participants (Fig. 1). The scanning procedures were repeated 3 times without interruption for consistency (I1, I2, I3). The digital models were saved and exported in standard tessellation language (STL) file format and Wavefront object (OBJ) file format.

Digital models scanned by the IOS (a) and scanning strategy (b)

Then, conventional polyvinyl siloxane impression material (SilagumPutty ISO4823: 2000 Type 0 and Silagum AutomixLight ISO4823: 2000 Type 3, DMG Medical Devices Co., Ltd. Germany) was used to take maxillary impressions according to the two-step impression method (conventional impression: CI). The extent of the impression matched that captured by the IOS. The procedure was repeated 3 times with a ten-minute interval to ensure the rebound of gingival tissue. Before the model was prepared with type V dental stone (PEMACO Inc., USA), impressions were disinfected for 5 min and stored for at least 2 h. All plaster models were digitalized using a calibrated high-precision laboratory scanner (Activity 880, Smart Optics Sensortechnik GmbH, Germany, with an accuracy of 0.010 mm), and the digital models were exported in STL format (M1, M2, M3).

Superimposition and processing of digital models

Superimposition of the digital serial models was performed using Geomagic software (Geomagic Studio 2013; 3D Systems Inc., USA). In this study, the position of I1 remained unchanged. The other digital models were registered to model I1 using the n-point registration method to achieve global registration. The crown boundaries of I1 were subsequently drawn more than 1 mm away from the gingival margin and the interproximal contact area (Fig. 2). These boundaries were then projected onto other digital models. The areas within these boundaries were selected for executing the “best-fit registration” command to finish the pairwise registration.

Fig. 2Boundary drawing (a) and selection of crown area (b) to carry out the superimposition of digital models

Boundary drawing (a) and selection of crown area (b) to carry out the superimposition of digital models

The gingiva includes the free gingiva, attached gingiva and gingival papilla. The free gingiva and gingival papilla are in contact with the teeth, surrounding the cervical and interproximal areas of the teeth. The contact area with the teeth together forms the coronal margin of the gingival tissue. The root side of the attached gingiva is the alveolar mucosa, and there is a clear boundary between the two, known as the mucogingival junction. The attached gingiva is pink in color, while the alveolar mucosa is dark red.

The surface curvature between teeth and gingiva varies, which is the basis of many tooth segmentation algorithms. The gingival margin was independently defined on all digital models for the buccal side with a landmark curve based on surface curvature values to automate detection. The mucogingival junction line of model I1 was drawn according to the morphology and color of the soft tissue (Fig. 3), and the line was projected onto all the other digital models. The area between the gingival margin and mucogingival junction line of the anterior teeth was selected as the gingival tissue for 3D deviation analysis. Additionally, the gingival tissues of a single anterior tooth were specifically chosen for evaluation.

To ensure the accuracy of the measurement, 5 groups of the data were again analyzed after 2 weeks by the examiner and by another examiner proficient in dental imaging software to assess intra-and inter-examiner reliability. The inter-class correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.99 were achieved for both inter- and intra-examiner reliability.

Color precision analysis

The digital models I1, I2, and I3 were adjusted to display the central incisors, left side, and right side, and then images were captured in the three views separately. The images were imported into Adobe Photoshop (V22.4.2, Adobe Systems Inc., USA) and converted to L*a*b* color mode. The sites included the gingival papilla (GP) between the central incisor teeth, gingival margins along the long axis of the right and left central incisors (RCIGM and LCIGM), middle points of the gingival tissue along the long axis of the right and left central incisor teeth (RCIMP and LCIMP), gingival margins and middle points of the gingival tissue of the right lateral incisor and canine (RLIGM, RLIMP, RCGM, and RCMP), and gingival margins and middle points of the left lateral incisor and canine (LLIGM, LLIMP, LCGM, and LCMP).

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test for normality and Levene’s test for equality of variances were performed. Paired-samples t-test or Wilcoxon signed rank test was used to analyze the precision of two impressions using a statistical software program (SPSS Statistics, v19.0; IBM Corp, USA). Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze the precision of single tooth to determine the influence of tooth position and impression-taking methods. Two-sample T test or Mann-Whitney U test was used to analyze color precision. The ΔE* between different points were tested by one-way ANOVA and post hoc least significant difference (LSD) tests. The significance level was set at P < 0.05 for all tests.

Results

The RMS values of the gingival tissues of the anterior teeth were 105.3 ± 40.8 μm for the CIs and 42.5 ± 17.9 μm for the IOS. The RMS were normally distributed with homogeneous variance. Paired-samples t-test was carried out, and a significant difference of RMS values was found between the 2 impressions (Ρ = 0.001). The RMS values of the gingival tissues of a single anterior tooth are shown in Fig. 6. The results revealed that impression-taking methods had a significant influence on the RMS values of 3D deviation (P < 0.001) and the tooth position had no significant influence.

Discussion

The present study validated the reliability of an IOS in capturing the morphology and color of the gingiva of the anterior teeth. Compared with conventional impressions, intraoral digital impressions exhibit superior precision for obtaining gingival tissues of anterior teeth. Owing to its non-invasive acquisition and high reliability, the IOS could be used to record the morphology of the gingival tissues of periodontitis samples.

The results indicated that the precision for recording the gingival morphology was 42.5 ± 17.9 μm, which was similar to that reported in a previous study [20]. However, the precision of the conventional impressions was 105.3 ± 40.8 μm, which was worse than that reported in the aforementioned study (63.66 ± 17.19 μm). This difference may be attributed to the inclusion of participants with periodontitis in our study, whereas in Wei’s study, participants with good periodontal conditions were included. The presence of gingival inflammation made the gingiva more susceptible to impression pressure, resulting in larger errors, particularly in papilla areas, as shown by color-coded mapping. In patients with periodontitis, the elastomeric impression material may flow into undercuts of the interdental areas due to attachment loss. Owing to the elasticity of the material, tearing and distortion of the material may be observed during the removal of the impression. By conventional impressions, interdental areas are poorly acquired, representing a significant limitation of indirect digital acquisition [11, 29]. Furthermore, deformed gingival tissue was difficult to completely recover for its viscoelasticity, even if a ten-minute interval was carried out. An important advantage of IOSs is their ability to capture papilla areas, although their accuracy in those areas is slightly limited [5]. So, in the present study, the reliability of intraoral scanning for capturing gingival morphology in periodontitis patients was measured to establish whether it can record gingiva of periodontitis more precisely than the conventional impression.

Unlike teeth and rigid structures, the characteristics of soft tissue are difficult to imitate with artificial materials in vitro. A previous study demonstrated that the precision of intraoral digital impressions to capture gingival tissues of participants with good periodontal conditions was better than that of conventional impressions [20]. Therefore, in the present study, the precision through repeatability was tested, and the trueness was not assessed, due to the challenges in accurately measuring the real dimension of the gingiva in vivo.

Selecting stable and reliable reference areas for superimposing digital models of subjects with periodontitis is challenging because of the destruction of teeth’s supporting structures and tooth mobility. When the region of interest is used for both superimposition and evaluation, the magnitude of the actual changes is reduced because the best-fit algorithm aims to minimize deviation between corresponding regions [30]. With whole model selection, including teeth with drifting and mobility and changing soft tissues, the errors of teeth and soft tissues will be averaged, potentially confounding outcomes [18]. Kuralt M [30] assessed the different reference areas for superimposition and reported that using teeth as a reference area provided the highest accuracy for superimposing serial 3D intraoral scans of patients with advanced periodontitis for volumetric soft tissue evaluation, despite their possible mobility and drifting. Therefore, in the present study, crown regions more than 1 mm away from the gingival margin and the interproximal contact area were selected for superimposition.

The Commission Internationale de l′Eclairage, L, a*, b* (CIELAB) system is most commonly used in current oral color research, and it can compare color differences [31]. The CIELAB is a 3D system with L* representing lightness, coordinate a* representing a measure of greenness (negative a*) or redness (positive a*), and coordinate b* representing a measure of blueness (negative b*) or yellowness (positive b*). The CIELAB color coordinates are beneficial for characterizing color ranges and the distribution of human gingiva [28].

The results indicated that ΔE ranged from 4.11 to 7.12, which was greater than the color acceptability threshold of ΔE = 4.6 [32]. Both the distance and angle between the handpiece and the scanning surface affect the projected light, which in turn affects the color presentation. The distance and angle make it difficult to guarantee complete agreement in repeated scanning procedures, and a positioning guide plate could be applied to avoid this problem in the future. The quality of the images may influence the evaluation outcome. The images for assessment of color precision were captured via Geomagic software rather than the original images depicted by the IOS. The image quality of the Geomagic software may have been lower than that of the IOS software, potentially influenced by variations in built-in light sources between different software platforms. A previous study demonstrated that dentists can perform valid tele-screening of oral soft and hard tissues based solely on approximate true color intraoral scans taken via the IOS. However, the agreement between remote diagnosis and clinical diagnosis for periodontal assessments is moderate to low. The sensitivity and specificity values were 0.61 and 0.39 for gingivitis and 0.67 and 0.33 for periodontitis, respectively [33]. This may be related to the quality of color images that the software uses for the colorimetric augmentation of the 3D file. The image quality of the tablet software may have been lower than that of the IOS software, emphasizing that hardware and software may influence the accuracy of remote screening. The precision of intraoral scanning to record color information should be improved, and a standard color card can be scanned to calibrate the color of the intraoral scanning image and the color displayed by different software. The ΔE values of the gingival margins were greater than those of the middle points. Intraoral scanning systems create direct digital impressions by matching up single optical shots. The gingival margin is at the junction between the tooth and the gingiva, and the color of the gingival margin may be affected by the tooth color. In clinical practice, the gingival margin can be regarded as the shoulder of the preparation, and the “fine scanning” command can be used. On the other hand, inflammation in most patients with periodontitis and gingivitis is present near the gingival margin and is characterized by redness and swelling of the gingival margin. The precision of intraoral scanning may be influenced by changes in gingival morphology and texture.

With the IOS, 3D images of teeth and soft tissues can be rapidly captured and stored accurately. The sites for health and inflammation appeared to have great clarity and were straightforward to differentiate. The color information in conjunction with the texture information may be used to aid remote screening for early diagnosis and identification of treatment needs. Sinéad Dalya [4] compared gingival inflammation scores obtained chairside using the non-invasive modified gingival index (MGI) with MGI scores from an IOS, and the results conclusively demonstrated that there was very close agreement between clinical MGI and IOS color/texture scores. This study confirmed that the IOS accurately captured gingival contour images to allow the clinical evaluation of health or inflammatory gingival status using MGI scores.

A direct digital model can be recorded by IOSs at the patient’s first visit to form the basis for comparisons with future intraoral models. If the scans are repeated at subsequent appointments, the subsequent data will offer valuable, highly accurate information on dental and gingival surface changes over time. This can aid the dentist to rapidly assess the complete dentition and periodontium and monitor gingival health, as well as facilitate future research and improve clinical decision making [4].

The establishment of a digital intelligent evaluation system for periodontal soft tissue was our aim. To achieve the aim, three core studies was included: analyzing the reliability of intraoral scanning in capturing gingival morphology and color among periodontitis patients; measuring changes in gingival morphology and color in these patients using intraoral digital models; and identifying surface image features of gingival inflammation to establish a digital intelligent evaluation system. In the present study, the reliability of intraoral scanning for capturing gingival morphology and color was analyzed, confirming that this technique provides a highly reliable representation of gingival morphology in periodontitis patients. In addition, our team successfully developed an automatic identification method for surface characteristics of gingival inflammation based on deep learning and IOS technology, providing a standardized and automated auxiliary tool for clinical gingival inflammation examination with high recognition accuracy, with a Dice coefficient of 77.8%, an intersection over union of 65.4%, and a pixel accuracy of 93.7% [34].

The present study has several limitations. First, only the gingival tissues of maxillary anterior teeth were scanned and assessed. The precision of the IOS in recording the gingiva of posterior teeth will be assessed in future studies. Only one IOS and reverse engineering software were included, and the precision of more IOSs to scan and of more software to depict the color should be compared. Second, the present study requires a certain level of computer knowledge and experience with 3D analysis software; with the development of IOS, 3D deviation and color analysis of regions of interest in the present study should be integrated and automated in future software. Third, the intraoral scanning was carried out by an experienced operator, and the whether the reliability will be different for beginners was not analyzed.

In the future study, the intraoral scanning data will be combined with the CBCT-acquired data to measure gingival thickness of patients with periodontitis. The influence of scanning proficiency, tooth color, and light conditions on the results will be analyzed. The morphological and color change after supportive periodontal therapy can be monitored using sequential intraoral scanning.

Conclusions

Based on the results of the present study, intraoral scanning offers a highly reliable representation of the gingival tissue morphology of maxillary anterior teeth in periodontitis patients. The precision of the IOS in reproducing color information of the middle points of the gingival tissue is better than that of the gingival margins.
