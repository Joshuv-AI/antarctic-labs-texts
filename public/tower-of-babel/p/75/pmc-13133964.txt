Unperturbed dye-based imaging of spontaneous synchronized calcium activity in iPSC-derived neuronal cultures
Authors: Nina Dirkx, Bob Asselbergh, Peter Verstraelen, Jonas Van Lent, Els De Vriendt, Vincent Timmerman, Winnok H De Vos, Sarah Weckhuysen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySynchronous calcium (Ca2+) bursting is a hallmark of neuronal network maturation. While microelectrode array (MEA) recordings are routinely used to generate population-averaged measurements on this functional network activity, live cell Ca2+-imaging offers single-cell resolved, contextual data. Unfortunately, most electrophysiologically active cells are hypersensitive to medium exchange, which is standard practice in most sensor dye-based Ca2+-imaging protocols. Here, we found that the use of conditioned imaging medium preserves spontaneous network activity of iPSC-derived glutamatergic and motor neuron cultures. The effect was consistent across different cell lines and seeding densities and allowed for the faithful detection of disease-specific phenotypes, as shown using a KCNQ2-related epilepsy model. Our findings thus provide a simple, robust strategy to measure spontaneous network activity in Ca2+-imaging experiments, broadening the utility of this technique for functional phenotyping, disease modeling, and drug screening with cellular resolution.Subject areas: imaging methods in chemistry, neuroscience

Introduction

One of the hallmark features of neuronal network maturation is the emergence of synchronous activity across the network. This behavior is characterized by alternating phases of coordinated episodes of high-frequency neuronal firing across the neuronal network, followed by a period of silence, a phenomenon referred to as network bursting. In the developing brain, network bursting plays a critical role in shaping synaptic architecture and supporting the refinement of neuronal connections across the brain.1,2,3 Likewise, in in vitro neuronal cultures, including those derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), network bursting serves as a key indicator of neuronal maturation and synaptic integration.4,5,6,7,8,9 iPSC-derived models are especially valuable as they allow personalized studies of patient-specific mutations, the use of isogenic controls, and the differentiation into diverse neuronal and glial cell types. The ability of such in vitro models to develop network bursts and to capture neurodevelopmental processes over time makes them an attractive platform for disease modeling and drug screening, as alterations in network bursting have been reported in a range of neurodevelopmental disorders.8,10,11,12 Network bursting can be measured using micro electrode arrays (MEAs), which directly measure voltage changes in the extracellular space, or calcium (Ca2+) imaging techniques, which monitor intracellular Ca2+ dynamics as an indirect proxy of neuronal firing. While low-density MEA typically averages signals across several cells, high-density (HD) MEAs overcome this limitation by providing improved resolution. Nonetheless, both MEA approaches lack exact spatial (e.g., (sub-)cellular or contextual) or phenotypic (e.g., cell type) information. Ca2+-imaging, by contrast, can offer this additional spatial insight at the (sub-)cellular level. Interestingly, despite this advantage, fewer studies have employed dye-based Ca2+-imaging than MEA technology to study network bursting. A key difference between dye-based Ca2+-imaging and MEA lies in the experimental interventions required for Ca2+-imaging, such as dye loading and the use of specific imaging media. Ca2+ indicators are known to introduce intracellular Ca2+ buffering that may alter the amplitude and timing of Ca2+ signals.13 while variations in extracellular ion concentrations (e.g., Ca2+, K+, Na2+, and Mg2+) and osmolarity in the imaging media influences network excitability.14,15,16,17,18 While some studies reported spontaneous network bursting in 2D neuronal cultures using dye-based Ca2+-imaging,19,20,21,22,23 the majority focus on individual spiking activity and typically lack evidence of network bursting, even under physiological ionic conditions.24,25,26,27,28,29,30,31,32 Furthermore, some studies that combine MEA recordings and dye-based Ca2+-imaging, demonstrate network bursting using MEA but the accompanying Ca2+ traces lack evidence of clear network bursting.33,34

In this work, we address a key factor contributing to the limited detection of spontaneous network bursting during dye-based Ca2+-imaging experiments in the literature: The disruptive effect of media changes on the network bursting of neuronal cultures. We present a robust and straightforward method that enables reliable detection of spontaneous network bursting in iPSC-derived neuronal cultures using dye-based Ca2+-imaging. We further show that this protocol faithfully captures the disease-specific network phenotype observed with MEA recordings of an epilepsy model. This method offers an accessible and robust strategy for functional phenotyping and drug screening, particularly when spatial information is of interest.

Results

iNeurons show network bursting recorded by micro electrode arrays

To assess network activity, glutamatergic iPSC-derived neurons (iNeurons) from two iPSC lines (iPSC1 and iPSC2) were cultured for four weeks on HD-MEAs. Representative raster plots and network plots, of IPSC2, from DIV14, DIV21, and DIV28 illustrate the time-dependent evolution of functional network activity (Figure 1A). At DIV14, neuronal firing was limited to isolated, asynchronous spikes without evident network bursting. From DIV21 onwards, iNeurons exhibited network bursts and increased firing. As cultures matured, we observed a progressive increase in the percentage of active electrodes, mean firing rate (MFR), network burst frequency (BF), and the proportion of spikes occurring within network bursts, hallmark features of synaptogenesis in a developing neuronal networks (Figure 1B).8

Calcium imaging medium exchange abolishes spontaneous network bursting despite preserved neuronal firing

We investigated whether the spontaneous network bursting detected with MEA recordings could also be captured using Ca2+-imaging with the fluorescent sensor dye Fluo-4 AM. To align with the MEA experiments, iNeurons were cultured in Neurobasal medium supplemented with B27 (NB).35 Based on the MEA recording results showing frequent spontaneous network bursts on DIV28, Ca2+-imaging was also performed at this time point. A Fluo-4 AM protocol was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions (see STAR Methods and, Figure 2). Initial experiments were performed using Krebs imaging buffer; however, no synchronized bursting was observed (Figures 3A and 3B, iPSC1: n = 9/2). To minimize variability due to batch differences in homemade imaging buffers, we switched to commercial BrainPhys Imaging (BPi) medium for the loading and imaging steps. BPi is a commercially available formulation optimized for neuronal activity assays, which has been shown to be equivalent to the gold standard, aCSF, in maintaining electrophysiological activity in neuronal cultures.15,33 For clarity, we refer to this protocol as the “NB-BPi protocol” throughout the remainder of the manuscript (Figure 3A). We quantified the number of wells showing network bursting activity. Wells were considered active if at least three network bursts occurred within a 5-min recording window, as this minimum threshold allows for reliable averaging of network burst parameters. Both iPSC1 and iPSC2-derived iNeurons displayed spontaneous firing activity (representative raster plot of iPSC1 shown in Figure 3B and Video S1). However, under baseline conditions, network bursts were largely absent, with only one out of 25 recordings exhibiting a single network burst during the recording period (Figures 3B and 3C). To confirm that the neuronal networks were functionally capable of generating network bursting, we applied Forskolin (FSK), a compound known to increase intracellular cAMP levels and promote network bursting.36 Following FSK treatment, all cultures exhibited robust network bursting (Figures 3B and 3C, Video S2), confirming the viability and functional integrity of the networks. To evaluate whether the lack of network bursts during Ca2+-imaging was attributable to the NB-BPi protocol, we replicated the experimental setup on HD-MEA using iPSC1 iNeurons (Figure 3D). Under baseline conditions, all cultures displayed consistent spontaneous network bursting, (100% active wells; MFR 0.93 ± 0.09 Hz; mean network burst frequency (MBF) 0.068 ± 0.007 Hz; Figures 3D–3F). Notably, following the medium exchange after dye loading to fresh BPi imaging media, network bursting activity was completely abolished across all wells during the 2-h recording (network burst count: 0; MBF: 0, Figure 3F). Networks, however, still showed persistent spontaneous firing activity (MFR: 0.95 ± 0.23 Hz, Figure 3E), confirming network viability. These results demonstrate that the medium changes associated with the NB-BPi protocol disrupt the synchronized network bursting, while leaving overall neuronal firing intact.

Conditioned imaging media preserve network bursting

Abrupt switching from NB to BPi medium introduces marked differences in osmolarity and ionic composition, which is known to impose osmotic stress and can drive neurons into sustained depolarization and transient depolarization block. This could be a reason for the absence of spontaneous network bursting in the NB-BPi protocol.15,33 Therefore, to better replicate the stable conditions present during MEA acquisition and minimize abrupt alterations in media composition, we adopted a gradual transition to BPi during neuronal maturation. Importantly, BPi is formulated to support long-term neuronal viability and electrophysiological activity, enabling its use as a chronic maintenance medium and thus allowing such a gradual adaptation strategy. NB was gradually replaced with BPi starting from DIV10 by performing 50% media changes twice a week, matching our standard maintenance schedule. This approach ensured that the frequency of media replacement remained consistent with normal culture handling, preventing confounding effects of altered media change rates while still enabling a complete and gradual transition to ∼100% BPi by DIV28, the day of recording (see protocol Figure 4A). We referred to this approach as the “BPi-BPi protocol” throughout the remainder of the manuscript. All optimization experiments described below were performed in iPSC 1 iNeurons.

Using HD-MEA, we first validated that a gradual transition from NB to BPi did not affect neuronal viability, as indicated by the absence of a significant medium × time interaction in mixed-effects model analysis of the percentage of active electrodes (Figure S1A). Similar to the NB-BPi protocol, all cultures displayed firing activity; importantly, network bursting was observed in 27.2 ± 9.72% recordings, representing a clear improvement compared to the NB-BPi protocol (Figures 4B and S1B, Video S3). The replication of this setup on the HD-MEA further confirmed our Ca2+-imaging findings: Network bursting was observed across all wells during baseline recordings but decreased to 40 ± 20% during the imaging period (Figures 4B and S1C).

Video S3. iNeurons show spontaneous network bursts using the gradual exchange Fluo-4 AM Ca2+-imaging protocol, related to Figure 4B

Because full media changes during the Ca2+-imaging procedure, even when using the same media such as the BPi-BPi protocol, still disrupt network bursting, we hypothesized that the transition to fresh media itself may contribute to this effect. To address this, we further minimized changes in the culture environment by imaging the iNeurons in their own conditioned culture media. Referred to as BPi-ConBPi protocol throughout the remainder of the manuscript (Figure 4A). Specifically, when removing the culture media to start the Fluo-4 AM loading (Figure 1A), this conditioned media was collected and maintained in an incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 and then re-applied to the corresponding well after removal of the loading dye. Neuronal cultures showed a substantial improvement in maintaining their spontaneous network bursting activity, with 98% ± 1.69 being active (Figures 4C and S1B). At the single-cell level, using the BPi-ConBPi protocol significantly maintained intrinsic firing characteristics, including the fraction of active cells, mean peak amplitude, mean peak interval, and firing frequency (Figure S2A). Moreover, neuronal synchronicity across the network was substantially higher in conditioned BPi compared with fresh BPi, indicating enhanced preservation of synaptic communication (Figure S2A). In addition, the variability of single-cell firing was reduced in the BPi-ConBPi protocol, as reflected by lower coefficients of variation for both peak interval and peak amplitudes, further supporting more stable and homogeneous network activity. The replication of this setup on the HD-MEA confirmed the Ca2+-imaging findings: Network bursting was restored across all wells during the imaging period (Figure 4C). HD-MEA network parameters did not show significant differences between BPi-BPi and ConBPi, this is likely due to the low sample size of the BPi-BPi protocol (n=3), as only active wells were used for this analysis (Figure 4C). Next, we validated the BPi-ConBP protocol using Ca2+-imaging in three additional iNeuron lines and one iPSC-derived motor neuron line, observing network bursting in 94.4 ± 5.56% for iPSC2, 100 ± 0% for iPSC3, 100 ± 0% for iPSC4 and 88.2 ± 8.05% for the motor neuron cultures (Figure 4D).

Increased seeding density preserves network bursting in fresh imaging media but alters network burst characteristics

The density of cultured neurons strongly shapes both the connectivity and the functional dynamics of a neuronal network.4,37,38,39 Therefore, we investigated if the outcome of the Ca2+-imaging protocols would vary when using different network densities. We performed the BPi-BPi and the BPi-ConBPi protocol on the HD-MEA platform, comparing three seeding densities: a low density (50E5 iNeurons), the original density (150E5 iNeurons), and a HD (500E5 iNeurons). Interestingly, seeding density had a minor effect on the percentage of active wells for the BPi-ConBPi protocol; the low-density condition showed a modest 1.18-fold reduction in network bursting compared to medium density (with medium and HD both resulting in 100% active wells) (Figure 4E). In contrast, the impact of seeding density was more pronounced for the BPi-BPi protocol: Low-density cultures exhibited a 3.25-fold reduction in percentage active wells, whereas HD cultures showed a 2.47-fold increase, reaching 100% active wells (Figure 4E). Although increasing seeding density for the BPi-BPi protocol restored the percentage of active wells to 100%, we next asked whether the properties of network bursts were similarly preserved. To assess this, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) on network parameters extracted from the HD-MEA recordings under high seeding conditions, comparing baseline recordings to the mock imaging recordings from the BPi-BPi and the BPi-ConBPi protocol. All PCA analyses were performed considering the first three principal components (PC1, PC2, and PC3) to evaluate potential batch effects, and the 2D projections showing the greatest overlap between batches were chosen to best visualize group differences (Figure S1D). PCA revealed that baseline and the BPi-ConBPi protocol clusters largely overlapped, indicating similar network burst characteristics, whereas the BPi-BPi protocol cluster showed more variability and tended to separate from the other two clusters, suggesting altered network bursting behavior mainly driven by a higher coefficient of variation in the network burst parameters (Figure 4F).

Optimized calcium imaging protocol captures disease phenotype in KCNQ2-G290D iNeurons

Using the BPi-ConBPi protocol, we evaluated whether we could capture disease-relevant changes in network bursting in iNeurons previously described with MEA recordings. We generated iNeurons harboring the pathogenic dominant-negative loss-of-function G290D variant in KCNQ2 (KCNQ2-G290D) alongside its isogenic control (iPSC2). KCNQ2 encodes the Kv7.2 subunit of the voltage-gated potassium channel Kv7, which mediates the M-current. The heterozygous KCNQ2-G290D variant reduces the M-current, resulting in developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (KCNQ2-DEE) in patients. At DIV28, KCNQ2-G290D iNeurons exhibited a significant increase in both MBF and network burst duration (BD) compared to their isogenic control, as measured by Fluo-4 AM Ca2+-imaging and HD-MEA, consistent with previous KCNQ2-DEE studies using similar models (Figure 4G).40,41,Dirk et al. in prep At the single-cell level, Ca2+-imaging showed that the proportion of active neurons and neuronal synchronicity was significantly reduced in the mutant line, whereas peak amplitude, firing frequency, and peak interval did not show a significant difference (Figure S2B). Furthermore, PCA of both HD-MEA network parameters and Ca2+-imaging parameters revealed clear genotype-specific clustering in both datasets (Figure 4G). These results demonstrate that conditioned medium-based Ca2+-imaging preserves pathophysiological network bursting features and effectively captures the functional hyperexcitability associated with KCNQ2-DEE, supporting robust in vitro disease modeling.

Discussion

iPSC-derived neuronal cultures are capable of developing synchronous network bursts, seen in the developing human brain as well, making them an attractive model for studying disease pathology or for drug screenings. Fluo-4 AM Ca2+-imaging is a powerful tool for measuring neuronal physiology, and its advantages: immediate use without genetic modification, fast kinetics, large dynamic range for detecting both sparse and intense activity, and subcellular resolution, make it particularly attractive for studying these network dynamics. However, the literature reveals a notable gap: Spontaneous network bursts are infrequently captured using this technique, despite its theoretical suitability.

Our study identifies and resolves a critical methodological barrier to capturing robust spontaneous network bursting in human iPSC-derived neuronal cultures via Fluo-4 AM Ca2+-imaging. By systematically dissecting the effects of media changes, we show that the NB-BPi and BPi-BPi protocol, particularly the 100% media change inherent to Fluo-4 AM Ca2+-imaging, profoundly disrupts network synchrony, despite preserving neuronal viability and asynchronous firing.

Traditional Ca2+-imaging demands two full media exchanges shortly before imaging (one change to the Fluo-4 AM loading media and one to the imaging media). The limited time frame during which Fluo-4 AM imaging can reliably be performed, typically within one to one and a half hours after dye incubation due to progressive dye internalization and declining signal-to-noise ratio, offers no opportunity for the neurons to condition the new environment. Fresh media environment is significantly different from conditioned media as this contains a complex mixture of neuron- and astrocyte-secreted neurotrophic factors, extracellular matrix components, and metabolic byproducts that are essential for culture survival and maturation.42,43 Moreover, a full media change can disrupt the culture environment by altering osmolarity,17 pH,44,45,46 and extracellular glutamate levels,47,48,49 all of which are known to influence neuronal excitability.

By contrast, we show that the use of conditioned BPi medium as imaging media, retained from the original culture and temporarily set aside during dye loading, fully restores physiologically relevant network bursting in the majority of neuronal cultures. This preservation is reproducible across multiple iNeuronal lines and motor neuron cultures and is robust to variations in seeding density.

We acknowledge that conditioned media will inevitably vary between wells due to technical and biological factors such as evaporation, pipetting variability, and differences in cell density. Crucially, however, for the BPi-ConBPi protocol, the conditioned medium from a given well is used exclusively for that same well during imaging. As a result, for each individual culture there is no change in medium composition between the pre-imaging period and the imaging period. This within-well invariance is the critical aspect of our approach. Differences between wells are, to some extent, unavoidable and already present during maturation, and would therefore also affect cultures even if all wells were imaged in the same fresh medium. In that sense, the between-well variability observed is comparable to the technical and biological variability seen in MEA experiments.

Although we do not claim that BPi is the only possible medium for this workflow, the approach fundamentally requires an imaging medium that can chronically support neuronal cultures to generate conditioned imaging media. Thus, traditional imaging buffers such as aCSF or Krebs, lacking the necessary trophic support for long-term cell viability, are not compatible with this setup.

Together, these manipulations likely explain the infrequent observation of spontaneous network bursts in the literature using Fluo-4 AM Ca2+-imaging, in contrast to the more consistent detection of such activity via MEA platforms. MEA recordings avoid the acute perturbations associated with complete medium exchanges and, thanks to their non-invasive nature, enable longitudinal measurements. However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of MEA technology. Modern HD MEAs, when combined with spike detection, sorting, and filtering pipelines, provide robust and reliable population-level electrophysiological readouts. However, MEA measurements remain inherently constrained to neurons that generate detectable extracellular spikes and therefore provide limited information about electrically silent or weakly active cells. In lower-density MEA configurations, signals may also be spatially undersampled, resulting in population-level representations rather than true single-cell resolution. Nonetheless, both MEA approaches lack exact spatial (e.g., (sub-)cellular or contextual) or phenotypic (e.g., cell type) information. In contrast, chemical Ca2+ indicators such as Fluo-4 AM enable single-(sub)cellular resolution, but their use is typically restricted to acute, single-session measurements because repeated loading over multiple days is not well established and may be inadvisable due to documented effects of AM-ester dyes on cellular physiology.13,50 Thus, MEA and Ca2+-imaging approaches should be considered complementary, each offering distinct insights into neuronal network function.

An alternative to chemical Ca2+ indicators is the use of genetically encoded calcium indicators (GECIs), such as GCaMP, delivered via viral vectors, which are widely applied in neuronal cultures. These approaches allow repeated single-cell measurements from the same culture without dye loading but require viral transduction, which can result in heterogeneous expression, extended preparation times, and reported cytotoxic effects in neuronal cultures, even at relatively low viral doses.51,52 Additionally, Adeno-associated viral vectors have been shown to provoke a DNA damage response in the cell.53 Lastly, chronic intracellular Ca2+ buffering due to the calcium indicator itself is known to alter the neuronal physiology.13 The BPi-ConBPi protocol presented here does not replace GECI-based imaging but instead improves the performance of dye-based Ca2+-imaging in settings where viral transduction is undesirable or impractical.

The timing of the gradual NB-to-BPi exchange was chosen to ensure complete adaptation to BPi by the day of recording rather than to target a specific developmental window. Initiation at DIV10, combined with 50% medium exchanges twice weekly, allowed a full transition to ∼100% BPi by DIV28 while preserving standard culture handling and avoiding abrupt changes in media composition. Importantly, for experiments conducted at later stages of neuronal maturation, this protocol can be readily shifted such that five consecutive 50% medium changes are completed prior to the experimental time point, ensuring full BPi exposure at the time of recording without altering overall maintenance frequency.

Our findings also reveal that although increasing neuronal density can partially mitigate the loss of spontaneous network bursting in fresh media, the network burst characteristics observed under these conditions are still altered compared to baseline activity. This alteration compromises the physiological relevance of the data, underscoring that fresh media conditions are not ideal for accurately interpreting network function during Ca2+-imaging.

Beyond methodological advances, we show that the BPi-ConBPi protocol enables the modeling of disease-relevant network phenotypes, such as in KCNQ2-related epilepsy iNeurons.40,41,Dirkx et al. in preparation In our study, we replicated this hyperexcitable phenotype using the BPi-ConBPi protocol, validating its utility for disease modeling and pharmacological screening. This approach is particularly valuable in settings where access to MEA technology is limited, where information of inactive cells is of interest, or where simultaneous imaging of network dynamics and cell or nuclear markers is required, for example, to distinguish activity patterns of different neuronal cell types.

Recent developments in wash-free calcium indicators, such as cell-permeant, self-quenching dyes (e.g., Calbryte or Fluo-8 no-wash formulations), offer a promising evolution of chemical Ca2+-imaging that eliminates media exchange steps entirely. These reagents could potentially preserve synchronized network bursting activity by avoiding the acute environmental disruptions identified in our study, while maintaining the non-invasiveness and rapid implementation of AM dyes. However, many of these approaches were originally designed for high-throughput screening or bulk biochemical assays rather than detailed imaging of spontaneous activity in neuronal networks, and may involve trade-offs in background signal, dye kinetics, and compatibility with extended live imaging. Although their performance in long-term human iPSC neuronal cultures remains to be systematically validated, such no-wash dyes represent a useful direction for future optimization of chemical Ca2+-imaging workflows.

In conclusion, our work provides a mechanistic explanation for the frequent absence of spontaneous network bursts in Fluo-4 AM Ca2+-imaging studies and introduces a straightforward solution. By preserving physiological network bursting via the use of conditioned imaging media, this approach enhances the utility of Fluo-4 AM Ca2+-imaging for phenotypic screening, disease modeling, and drug testing in human iPSC-derived neuronal cultures.

Limitations of the study

While our BPi-ConBPi Ca2+-imaging protocol reliably preserves spontaneous network bursting in iPSC-derived neuronal cultures, some limitations should be considered. First, although we tested multiple iNeuron and motor neuron lines, the generalizability of this approach to other neuronal subtypes or more complex co-culture systems remains to be established. Second, our study focuses on Fluo-4 AM as the Ca2+ indicator; while the principles likely extend to other dye-based indicators, further validation is required to confirm applicability across different dyes and imaging modalities.

iPSCs were cultured in StemFlex medium on Geltrex-coated plates at 37°C, 5% CO2. Passaging was performed at a 1:6 ratio using ReleSR. Antibiotics were omitted to allow bacterial contamination monitoring. Mycoplasma testing was performed quarterly using LookOut One-Step PCR kit. Authentication was performed by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and Sanger sequencing of mutant lines. This work is not part of or doesn’t involve a clinical trial.

Mouse primary astrocytes

Mouse astrocytes were isolated from healthy postnatal day 0–3 C57BL/6 pups according to ethical approval ECD 2020-076. All protocols complied with EU Directive 2010/63/EU. Sex of the mouse astrocytes is not available as isolations from several mouse brains were mixed to reduce the risk for bias. Mouse astrocytes were maintained in glia media (DMEM, 10% FBS, 100x Glutamax, 50 U/ml P/S, 1 mM Sodium Pyruvate) on Geltrex coated T75 flasks. Animal experiments were approved and conducted in accordance with the policies and guidelines set forth by ethical Committee of the University of Antwerp (NAE 2020-076).

Method details

iPSC generation and authentication

Lines iPSC1, iPSC3, iPSC4, and KCNQ2-G290D were reprogrammed from PBMCs using episomal OCT4, c-MYC, SOX2, and KLF4 vectors by Radboud UMC Stem Cell Technology Center. The isogenic iPSC2 line was generated at the ICM Stem Cells Core Facility (Paris). Pluripotency and genomic integrity were validated by qPCR, immunostaining (Nanog, Sox2, Oct4), and trilineage differentiation. MN iPSC was reprogrammed from fibroblasts and characterized by the Stem Cell Institute, University of Leuven.

All iPSC clones were screened for recurrent chr20q11.21 duplication using an in-house Multiplex Amplicon Quantification (MAQ) assay. Negative clones were subjected to SNP microarray analysis (HumanCytoSNP-12 v2.1).

Multiplex Amplicon Quantification

To exclude the presence of the recurrent duplication of chr20q11.21 in the corrected clonal lines,55,56 we used our in-house Multiplex Amplicon Quantification (MAQ) method (Agilent). This method includes multiplex PCR amplification of fluorescently labeled target and control amplicons, followed by fragment analysis. The assay targets six amplicons situated in and around the chr20q11.21 region, along with five control amplicons situated at random genomic positions outside the chr20q11.21 region and other known CNVs. These 11 amplicons were PCR-amplified in a single reaction using 20 ng of genomic DNA template. The peak areas of the target amplicons were normalized to those of the control amplicons. Comparisons of the normalized peak areas between clonal lines and reference samples were used to produce a dosage quotient (DQ) for each targeted amplicon, computed by the MAQ-S software package (Agilent). DQ values exceeding 1.25 were considered indicative of a duplication.

Stable Neurogenin (NGN)-iPSC generation

For NGN1/2 integration, TALEN plasmids targeting the CLYBL locus with doxycycline-inducible NGN1/2 and constitutive mCherry57 were nucleofected as described.40 mCherry+ cells were FACS-sorted and subcloned. Clonal lines with Homozygous insertion, confirmer via PCR, were validated for CNVs (MAQ + SNP-array). STR authentication and Sanger sequencing confirmed integrity.

Generation of iNeurons

NGN-iPSCs were dissociated using Accutase and 1E6 cells were seeded per well in a 6 well plate with 2 mL StemFlex supplemented with 10 μM Y-27632 rho kinase inhibitor and 2 μg/mL doxycycline. After 24 h, 1 mL of media was removed and 2 mL of StemFlex supplemented with 2 μg/mL doxycycline added. After 48 h, 2 mL media was again removed and replaced with 2 mL of StemFlex supplemented with 2 μg/mL doxycycline. 72 h after initial induction, these pre-differentiated day 3 neurons became post-mitotic and were frozen in freezing media (90% KnockOut Serum Replacement (KOS), 10% DMSO).

For final differentiation, thawed day 3 neurons were co-cultured with primary mouse astrocytes in Complete Seeding Medium (Neurobasal Plus, 50X B27 Plus, 100XGlutaMAX, 10 ng/mL BDNF, 10 ng/mL NT-3, 2 μg/mL doxycycline, 2 μM Ara-C, CEPT Cocktail). Half-medium changes were performed twice weekly from day 7 using Differentiation Medium (Neurobasal Plus, 50X B27 Plus, 100XGlutaMAX, 10 ng/mL BDNF, 10 ng/mL NT-3).

iPSC-derived motor neurons

Motor neuron differentiation followed previously published protocols.58 Briefly, iPSCs maintained in Essential 8 medium were transitioned to a neuronal induction medium composed of Neurobasal medium, DMEM/F12, N2 supplement (100X), B-27 supplement without vitamin A (50X), GlutaMAX, β-mercaptoethanol, and ascorbic acid, supplemented on the first day with RevitaCell Supplement (100X) to enhance survival. For the first two days, dual SMAD inhibitors SB431542 and LDN-193189, together with the WNT agonist CHIR99021, were added. Thereafter, half-medium changes were performed using differentiation medium containing retinoic acid (RA), smoothened agonist (SAG), BDNF, glial cell-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), γ-secretase inhibitor DAPT, ciliary neurotrophic factor (CNTF), and laminin. Cells were plated on poly-L-ornithine/laminin-coated plates at 1×105 cells/ml. To increase motor neuron purity, a single treatment with 1 μM Ara-C was performed on day 14.

HD-MEA preparation

Prior to cell plating, HD-MEA plates were pre-treated with 1 mL of 1% tergazyme for 1 h, washed 3 times with PBS and sterilized in 70% ethanol for 30 min. After removing ethanol and washing, 1 mL of Neurobasal Plus basal media with B-27 Plus supplement (50x) was added per well and incubated for 48 h (37°C) to condition the chips. Afterward, medium was removed, and the chips were washed once with 1 mL PBS. The chips were coated first with 50 μL 0.14% PEI in borate buffer for 1 h, then extensively washed four times with 1 mL Milli-Q water followed by airdrying overnight. The following day (=DIV0), the chips were coated with 20 μg/mL CellAdhere Laminin-521 in DMEM for 0.5 to 1 h at 37°C. During this incubation time cell suspensions for plating were prepared. Frozen day 3 neurons were thawed at 37°C in a warm water bath, and in parallel, fresh mouse astrocytes of passage 2 were dissociated using 0.05% Trypsin. A mixture of 1.5E5 day 3 neurons and 2.5E4 astrocytes was added directly to the chips in a 50 μL cell suspension composed of Complete Seeding media supplemented with Primocin (100 μg/mL) and incubated for 30 min in a 5% CO2 cell culture incubator at 37°C. After cell adherence, an additional 2 mL Complete Seeding media supplemented with Primocin was gently added to the wells. Cells were grown on the MEA plates until DIV28.

HD-MEA recordings

All MEA recordings were performed on a MaxTwo (MaxWell Biosystems AG, Zurich, Switzerland), using MaxLab Live assays as described previously by.59 Activity Scan Assays and Network Assays were performed three times a week during from DIV14 to DIV28 at 5% CO2 and 37°C. For all the recordings, a 300 Hz high-pass filter was used and a spike detection threshold set to 5.0 standard deviations. For the Activity Scan Assay, the checkerboard configuration was used with a recording time of 30 s for each configuration. For the Network Assay, 1024 electrodes were selected using the Maxlive neuronal selection algorithm which selects the electrodes with the highest amplitude while also maximizing the distance between the individual electrodes. This reduces the potential for redundant recording of the same cells with multiple electrodes and therefore increases the total number of measured cells. All HD-MEA media changes took place immediately following recordings to allow >48 h of media conditioning. Burst parameters analysis was performed using an 80% peak to baseline cut-off. Recorded data were plotted in R and analyzed for statistical significance. Network parameters used for PCA included mean spikes per burst (SPB), SPB coefficient of variation (CV), mean network burst duration (BD), BD CV, Burst peak (BP), BP CV, Inter burst interval (IBI), IBI CV, mean inter spike interval within burst (bISI), bISI CV, number of bursts, Burst frequency (BF).

Fluo-4 AM calcium imaging

A mixture of 1E5 day 3 neurons and 1E4 astrocytes were cultured on 96-well glass bottom plates coated with 0.14% PEI and 20 μg/mL CellAdhere Laminin-521. At DIV 28 iNeurons were loaded with the Ca2+-sensitive fluorescent dye Fluo-4 AM to monitor intracellular Ca2+ dynamics. A stock solution of Fluo-4 AM was prepared by dissolving the dye in DMSO and then diluted to a final concentration of 4 μM in imaging buffer. To improve dye solubility and cell loading efficiency, 0.02% (w/v) Pluronic F-127 was added to the dye solution. Culture medium was removed, and Fluo-4-AM loading solution was applied to the cultures, followed by incubation for 30 min in a portable, temperature- and CO2-controlled incubator (Cellbox) located adjacent to the microscopy setup. After incubation, the dye solution was carefully removed, and imaging media was added. Cells were allowed to equilibrate for an additional 30 min on the prewarmed (37°C) microscope. This procedure minimizes variability caused by transporting cultures between the incubator and microscope before imaging and ensured stable physiological conditions throughout the imaging process. Ca2+-imaging was performed immediately after equilibration on a PerkinElmer UltraVIEW VoX system, with Nikon Ti microscope and Yokogawa SU-X1 spinning disk. Recordings were made using a 20X objective (NA 0.75) and the light path for GFP (excitation wavelength 488 nm, and emission band-pass 525(W50)nm). In each well, one 5-min time-lapse recording was acquired, at a temporal resolution of 2 frames per second (450 ms exposure time). To limit phototoxicity, camera binning was set at 2x2 and laser power was kept below 15% (same setting across all wells of a plate). For each plate, all recordings were made within a window of 60 min, allowing for the acquisition of ∼10 wells per plate. See Figure 2A for workflow.

To pharmacologically induce network bursting, 10 μM forskolin (FSK) was applied to neuronal cultures following the spontaneous Ca2+-imaging recording. FSK was initially dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 10 mM stock solution. Immediately prior to experiments, a 10× working stock (100 μM) was prepared by diluting the 10 mM FSK stock into imaging medium. To achieve a final concentration of 10 μM, 22 μL of the 10× stock was added to the imaging well containing 200 μL of imaging medium.

Analysis of Ca2+ traces

Image stacks were processed using FIJI with the custom macro Neuroconnectivity_Func.ijm, available from the De Vos Lab repository on GitHub: github.com/DeVosLab/NeuroConnectivity.60 Cell body segmentation in the time-projected Fluo-4 images was performed using the StarDist algorithm.61 Subsequent signal and data analyses were conducted in R with a modified version of NeuroConnectivity_Func.R (GitHub: github.com/DeVosLab/NeuroConnectivity). For each segmented cell, fluorescence intensity traces over time were extracted and corrected for photobleaching via linear regression on log-transformed data. Baseline fluorescence levels were automatically detected, and transient calcium events were defined as peaks exceeding two standard deviations above baseline. Peaks with amplitudes below 30 gray values were excluded from further analysis. The mean peak frequency (in Hz) was calculated by averaging the number of valid peaks across all neurons within each recording. Neurons exhibiting at least three valid peak during the 5 min acquisition period were classified as active, and the active fraction was defined as the proportion of active regions of interest (ROIs). To quantify network synchrony, a cell-by-cell Pearson correlation matrix was constructed for each recording, and the mean correlation coefficient was used as a measure of synchronicity. All fluorescence traces were then averaged to suppress non-synchronous fluctuations, isolating synchronous network events. The synchronous peak frequency was determined by applying the same detection criteria (≥2 SD above baseline and >30 gray values) to this averaged trace. Recordings that had at least 3 network bursts during the 5-min recording time were defined as active wells, allowing for direct comparison with HD-MEA data. See Figure 2B for pipeline.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in R v4.3.0. Comparisons between two groups were made by two-tailed Student’s t test or a two-tailed Mann–Whitney U test, after normality checks. PCA was run on z-score–normalized network parameters using prcomp() (stats package). Data are presented as mean ± SEM. p-values <0.05 were considered significant (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.005, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.001). n-values are denoted in figure legends as wells (a)/independent differentiations (b).
