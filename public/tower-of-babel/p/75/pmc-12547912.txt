Mild Dilatation of the Main Pancreatic Duct Is a Risk Factor for Progression to Pancreatic Cancer in High-Risk Individuals
Authors: Elizabeth Abou Diwan, Helena Saba, Amanda L. Blackford, Mohamad Dbouk, Linda Chu, Jin He, Richard Burkhart, Ralph H. Hruban, Michael Goggins, Marcia Irene Canto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsMild dilatation of the main pancreatic duct (MPD) can be an early indicator of an obstructing mass, but its clinical significance in asymptomatic high-risk individuals (HRIs) undergoing surveillance has not been defined. We determined the prevalence and incidence of MPD dilation in HRI and assessed its association with neoplastic progression (to high-grade dysplasia or pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC)) in HRI.MethodsWe analyzed prospectively collected data from HRIs enrolled in the Cancer of Pancreas Screening cohort studies. We determined the risk of neoplastic progression associated with MPD dilation (defined as ≥4, ≥3, ≥2 mm diameter, in the head, body, tail, respectively) using Cox regression and time-varying covariate analysis. We estimated time-to-progression from baseline endoscopic ultrasound and from baseline duct dilation by the Kaplan–Meier method.ResultsOf 641 HRIs followed for a median 3.6 years (interquartile range 1.9–7.2), 97 (15%) had MPD dilation without evidence of an obstructing mass lesion; 10 of whom (10.3%) were diagnosed with neoplastic progression within a median of 2 years after dilation was first detected. The cumulative probability of high-grade dysplasia/PDAC in HRI with baseline MPD dilation was 16% at 5 years and 26% at 10 years. HRI with any MPD dilation were 2.6 times more likely to progress (P = .05), particularly those with ≥ 3 pancreatic cysts during surveillance (adjusted hazard ratio = 9.07, P < .001).ConclusionMPD dilation without an apparent obstructing lesion is an independent risk factor for neoplastic progression in HRI.

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is projected to become the second-leading cause of cancer death in the United States by 2030.1 Screening for PDAC is not advocated in the general population because of its low incidence (1.7% lifetime, 1 in 57).2 However, the International Cancer of the Pancreas Screening (CAPS) Consortium, American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy, and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network currently recommend surveillance for individuals with an estimated average lifetime risk of developing PDAC of 5% or more.3, 4, 5, 6 These high-risk individuals (HRIs) either carry deleterious germline variants in the cancer susceptibility genes that increase the risk of PDAC, and/or have a strong family history of PDAC with at least one pair of affected first degree blood relatives. There is now clear evidence that pancreatic surveillance can improve overall clinical outcomes and HRIs with surveillance-detected PDAC are especially likely to achieve long-term survival.7, 8, 9

Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)/magnetic retrograde cholangiopancreatography are the main imaging modalities used for pancreatic cancer surveillance3,4 Pancreatic surveillance can lead to increased resection rates of early-stage PDAC,7,10 but some patients develop interval cancers despite annual surveillance with MRI or EUS or both MRI and EUS.9,11

Identifying imaging characteristics that predict neoplastic progression may help decision-making regarding surveillance intervals and surgical treatment. One such characteristic is the presence of main pancreatic duct (MPD) dilation. Typically, MPD secondary to duct obstruction (tumor, chronic pancreatitis, papillary stenosis) or to mucin secreted into the main duct by intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs). MPD dilation has been observed in HRIs undergoing pancreatic surveillance in the absence of a visible mass.11,12 Canto et al reported MPD dilation at baseline was more common in progressors and was one of the worrisome features that predicted progression, but it was not an independent risk factor for neoplastic progression.12 Timely recognition of MPD dilation without a visible obstructing lesion could potentially predict PDAC development in HRIs and impact patient management.

The aims of this study were to determine the baseline prevalence and incidence of any MPD dilation in HRI undergoing surveillance, assess the association of MPD dilation with neoplastic progression to high-grade dysplasia (HGD) or PDAC, and describe the natural history and patterns of MPD dilation over time in a well characterized prospective high-risk cohort. We also examined the clinical characteristics of patients, pancreatic imaging abnormalities, and clinical outcomes associated with MPD dilation and progression.

Methods

Patients and Study Design

We analyzed prospectively collected data from ongoing and completed Cancer of Pancreas Screening (CAPS 1–5) cohort studies at The Johns Hopkins Hospital from 1998 to 2022 (ClinicalTrials.gov https://www.clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT02000089).7 HRIs were enrolled based on either family history criteria, which included individuals who had at least 1 first-degree relative with PDAC and who were part of a kindred with at least 1 pair of affected first-degree relatives (familial pancreatic cancer kindred), or, a germline deleterious variant associated with hereditary pancreatic cancer (ataxia telangiectasia mutated, BRCA1, BRCA2, CDKN2A, partner and localizer of BRCA2, or STK11). All CAPS studies were approved by the Johns Hopkins Institutional Review Board, and all enrolled patients signed informed consent for baseline evaluation and prospective follow-up.

Patients underwent pancreatic surveillance in accordance with CAPS International Consortium consensus guidelines.3,4 For this study, we included subjects who either had a baseline EUS in addition to a second follow-up imaging test (EUS, MRI, or computed tomography [CT] scan) who were followed for at least 12 months, unless they were diagnosed with PDAC, or had a pancreatic resection within the first year of follow-up.

We excluded patients who were being evaluated for concerning symptoms or who presented with MPD dilation due to an obstructive lesion. We also excluded all patients with a history of pancreatic resection involving the head or body of the pancreas and those with initial screening visit in 2022 onward to enable a minimum of 2 years of follow-up.

Surveillance Methods, Clinical Management, and Final Diagnosis

All HRIs underwent baseline screening with EUS following informed consent and completion of a detailed questionnaire Follow-up surveillance combined the use of EUS and MRI/magnetic retrograde cholangiopancreatography, with CT imaging reserved for patients with claustrophobia or an indeterminate pancreatic lesion. The surveillance interval was typically 1 year but was adjusted based on individual patient clinical factors (age, germline mutation status, and family history), and/or imaging abnormalities in accordance with published international guidelines.3,4

Measurements of the MPD were routinely obtained at each EUS from the head, body and tail, and recorded as part of the surveillance protocol. A dilated MPD was defined by a diameter of ≥ 4 mm in the head, ≥ 3 mm in the body, or ≥ 2 mm in the tail of the pancreas. These cutoffs are at least 1 mm larger than the normal MPD diameter measurements of 3 mm, 2 mm, and 1 mm in the head, body, and tail, respectively, based standard accepted EUS and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography criteria.13,14 EUS was used as the imaging modality of choice because MPD measurements were routinely performed during EUS and recorded. To evaluate the significance of a dilated MPD, HRIs were divided into an “ever-dilated” group (those who had dilation of the MPD detected by EUS at any point during surveillance in any anatomic pancreatic location), and a “never-dilated” group (those who never had any MPD dilation during surveillance).

We also analyzed the presence and number of pancreatic cysts, pancreatic solid masses, and pancreatic abnormalities defined by the Rosemont criteria (echogenic foci, echogenic strands, lobularity, cyst(s), MPD dilation, MPD irregularity, echogenic MPD, stone(s) in duct, and side branch dilation),13 which were routinely noted during surveillance procedures. Four or more Rosemont criteria features were considered meeting criteria for chronic pancreatitis.13 We also examined MRI, CT, and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography data for the presence of a pancreatic duct stricture and pancreas divisum.

We classified MPD dilation as mild (MPD 4–5 mm in the head, 3–4 mm in the body and/or 2–3 in the tail), and moderate to severe (MPD ≥6 mm in the head, ≥5 mm in the body, and/or ≥4 mm in the tail). We defined an “isolated MPD dilation” as any MPD dilation that was not concurrently associated with a visible lesion (cyst, mass, stone, or stricture). Any other MPD dilation with a prevalent cystic lesion or solid mass was referred to as “lesion-associated MPD dilation”. “Neoplastic progression” was defined as the development of pathologically proven PDAC or HGD in precursor neoplasms (pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN) or IPMN).

All clinical and imaging data were prospectively tracked over time from each HRI’s baseline surveillance visit to the last follow-up to December 2022 at The Johns Hopkins Hospital, Baltimore, Maryland. Data were recorded in an online secure database (Microsoft Access or REDCap).

Most patients with MPD dilation were recommended to undergo their next surveillance at an earlier time point, such as 6 months, rather than 1 year. HRIs with worrisome findings on imaging or cytology were offered surgical resection after multidisciplinary review of clinical history and radiologic findings. Final decisions were made on an individual basis, using shared decision-making and CAPS international consensus guidelines. Most surgeries were performed at the Johns Hopkins Hospital by experienced pancreaticobiliary surgeons.4,12,15

The final diagnoses were established by surgical pathology or cytology (percutaneous or EUS-guided fine needle aspirates). For cases with several pancreatic lesions in the resection specimen, the endpoint analysis was restricted to the lesion with the highest pathologic grade. Older cases designated as having intermediate-grade dysplasia were recategorized as low-grade lesions based a revised consensus classification of dysplasia within pancreatic precursor neoplasms (IPMNs and PanINs) as either high-grade dysplasia or HGD.16

We also examined the temporal pattern of MPD dilation from the baseline MPD diameter (first or baseline visit) to the last imaging visit, or to the prediagnostic visit for HRI that had pathology-proven diagnoses. We further classified patients with "ever-dilated" MPDs into those with “transient” or “persistent/recurrent” MPD dilation. We considered a change of MPD diameter greater than 0.5 mm at any given location to be a noteworthy increase or decrease in diameter. MPD dilation pattern in “ever-dilated” HRI with persistent or recurrent dilation was further classified into 3 types: (a) “worsening”, if the MPD went from nondilated to dilated, or if it increased by more than 0.5 mm in the same or another location; (b) “stable” if there was no difference, or if the difference between MPD measurements was ≤ 0.5 mm; or (c) “improving or resolved” if the diameter decreased by more than 0.5 mm, or normalized. If the dilation was improving or stable in one location of the pancreas and worsening in another location, we classified the MPD dilation trend as “worsening”.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized with means and standard deviations and categorical variables were summarized as absolute and relative frequencies. Time to neoplastic progression was calculated as the time from the baseline EUS (time zero) to the most recent diagnosis date based on surgery or biopsy (censored) or the date of progression (event) in HRI who did not undergo resection using the Kaplan–Meier analysis.

Cumulative probabilities of neoplastic progression at 5 and 10 years in HRIs found to have any MPD dilation detected by EUS at their baseline surveillance visit were estimated for the entire cohort as well as relevant subgroups.

The primary study objective was to estimate the association of MPD dilation with neoplastic progression to HGD or PDAC. We estimated hazard ratios (HRs) using Cox proportional hazards models for time-to-progression with a time-varying covariate specification for presence of an MPD dilation at each visit. The model adjusted for baseline covariates: age, gender, diabetes, high-risk category (mutation carrier status and family history), alcohol consumption (ever/never), and smoking (ever/never), and included the presence of ≥ 3 pancreatic cysts specified as time-varying based on each visit. Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the association between baseline characteristics and time-to-duct dilation. Analyses were completed using R version 4.4.1.17

Natural History and Pattern of MPD Dilation

The natural history of MPD dilation in the 97 ever-dilated patients was highly variable as illustrated by the swimmer’s plot in Figure A1. In 10 HRI with MPD dilation ultimately diagnosed with HGD/PDAC, 50% maintained stable, persistent, or recurrent MPD dilation in their prediagnostic exams, 20% exhibited improvement or normalization, and 30% had worsening dilation.

MPD Dilation at Baseline and Risk for Neoplastic Progression

The cumulative risk of neoplastic progression in our entire cohort (n = 641) at 5- and 10 years was 4% and 8%, respectively (Table 2). Out of 97 HRI with any MPD dilation, 10 developed neoplastic progression (10.3%). The cumulative probability of progressing to HGD or PDAC in the HRI with a dilated MPD at baseline (n = 57) was 16% at 5 years and 26% at 10 years (Table 2). Among all HRI with 3 or more cysts, (irrespective of MPD status) the cumulative risk for neoplastic progression was 11% and 25% at 5 and 10 years, respectively (Table 2). MPD Dilation at Any Time and Risk for Neoplastic Progression

We estimated the risk for progression using time-varying covariate models and considered the risk associated with any MPD dilation either at baseline or during follow-up surveillance EUS (Table 3). The presence of MPD dilation conferred a higher risk for progression (unadjusted HR 4.0, 95% CI: 1.7, 9.6, P = .002) at any time point, compared to no dilation. When we considered other possible factors (baseline age, risk group, smoking, alcohol, smoking, EUS features of chronic pancreatitis) associated with neoplastic progression, the presence of ≥3 pancreatic cysts at any visit (adjusted HR 3.2, 95% CI: 1.4, 7.3, P = .006) was the only significant independent risk factor for neoplastic progression (Table 3). However, when we considered both MPD dilation and pancreatic cyst number, the presence of MPD dilation with ≥3 pancreatic cysts at any time conferred a 10-fold elevated risk for developing HGD/PDAC (unadjusted HR 10.3, 95% CI: 3.8, 27.6, P < .001, Table 3). Time to Progression

Twenty seven of 641 HRI developed incident HGD or PDAC (Figure A2), with a median time to progression of 2.6 years. Ten of these progressors with baseline or new MPD dilation had a shorter median time to progression (2 years, Q1 = 0.7, Q3 = 4.4), compared to the other 17 never-dilated HRI (2.6 years, Q1 1.1, Q3 6) (Table A2). The median time from the first dilation to progression was 1.8 years for ever-dilated HRI (Q1 = 0.7, Q3 = 3.8) (data not shown).

Description, Diagnosis, Tumor Stage, Management, and Outcomes in Ever-Dilated Progressors

The clinical and imaging characteristics, final pathology diagnoses, tumor stage, management, and clinical outcomes of the 10 progressors who had any MPD dilation detected are summarized in Table 4. The 10 patients had an age range of 68–80 years diagnosis, 90% had only mild MPD dilation, and 80% were eventually diagnosed with tumors in the pancreatic head. Seven of the 10 progressors developed HGD/PDAC more than a year from baseline. Eight of the 10 patients that presented with MPD dilation were diagnosed with HGD (n = 4, patients 1–4) or resectable PDAC (n = 4, patients 5–8). Importantly, these 8 patients had prolonged survival after treatment, with only 2 dying of PDAC, 1 (patient 5) dying of primary lung cancer, and the remaining 5 (patients 1–4, 5) alive for 6.5–18 years. Discussion

Pancreatic imaging abnormalities are commonly detected on EUS and/or MRI in HRIs undergoing pancreatic surveillance for PDAC.15,18, 19, 20, 21 While pancreatic cysts are observed in nearly half of HRI,15,22 MPD dilation without an apparent obstructive lesion raises concern for an obstructing tumor to be later identified as the cause of the dilation. In this study, we found that ever-dilated HRI had about a 4-fold increase in their risk for neoplastic progression and their time to diagnosis is shorter than those that progress without MPD dilation (mean of 1.8 years). The cumulative incidence of progression to HGD or PDAC in HRI with baseline dilation is high, specifically 16% at 5 years and 26% at 10 years from baseline. The highest risk for progression was in HRI with baseline MPD dilation and more than 3 cysts at any time during surveillance.

Several studies have investigated prediagnostic MPD dilation in non-HRI who subsequently progress to PDAC. In a retrospective case-controlled study of 595 patients with sporadic PDAC matched to healthy individuals, investigators found that upon imaging review a pancreatic mass was evident in about half the cases with prediagnostic MPD dilation detected by CT or MRI.23 In addition to MPD dilatation (>3 mm), MPD interruption, focal atrophy, and features of acute pancreatitis were strongly associated with PDAC. In another retrospective study, Martin et al recently reported on patients who underwent pancreatic resection for concerning imaging findings and also had MPD dilatation of uncertain origin on CT.24 Most patients with MPD dilation had a neoplasm; 41% had PDAC or HGD (2% PDAC, 37% HGD in IPMN (mainly mixed type), and 2% HGD within PanIN). Another retrospective study from Japan reported mild MPD dilation by transabdominal ultrasonography (≥2 mm) was detected in the 65% of 39 subjects with incident PDAC that developed more than 4 years later.25 When compared with an age-matched control group of over 10,000 subjects, the odds ratio for PDAC development was 32.5. In a follow-up prospective study, Tanaka et al reported a >6-fold higher risk of PDAC in subjects with slight MPD dilation by transabdominal ultrasound in the pancreatic body (>2.5 mm) or a cyst ≥ 5 mm.26 The 5-year cumulative risk for incident PDAC was even higher when subjects had both findings (age- and sex-adjusted HR 27.5, P = .002).26

Few studies have investigated the significance of isolated mild MPD dilation in HRI. One prospective study of a high-risk cohort of CDKN2A-p16-Leiden mutation carriers undergoing surveillance with MRI (and optional EUS) reported prior MPD dilation without evidence of tumor as the first manifestation of PDAC in 5 of 7 HRI undergoing surveillance.27 The PDACs that developed in these patients were small (mean size 12.3 mm) and all were resectable. In contrast to our current study, our prior report of 354 HRI prospectively followed with MRI and EUS in our CAPS 1–4 studies, we found that minimal MPD dilation at baseline EUS (defined as > 3.5 mm in the head, > 2.5 mm in the body and/or > 1.5 mm in the tail, but <5 mm in any area) was not significantly associated with neoplastic progression.12 With a large sample size, additional follow-up, and specific analysis of MPD dilation as a time-varying factor in addition to baseline assessment, we now demonstrate risk estimates for progression that consider MPD changes over time.

Mild MPD dilation is one of the presenting signs of early-stage PDAC and potentially provides an opportunity for early diagnosis and treatment. In a retrospective study of 200 cases of Stage 0 and Stage I PDAC, dilatation of the MPD and local fatty changes were associated with early-stage PDAC.28 Furthermore, Kurihara et al also showed that for Stage 0 and Stage I PDAC, MPD dilation was detected in 75% of patients by EUS, 63.8% by CT, and 70.4% by MRI.29

The accumulated data and our current study with long-term follow-up results underscore the importance of MPD dilation on EUS without apparent obstruction as risk factor for progression, particularly when coupled with ≥3 pancreatic cysts. Close follow-up of MPD dilation is important and provides an opportunity for early detection and improved outcomes. The time to progression to HGD or PDAC from first detection of MPD dilation to diagnosis (approximately 2 years) points to a crucial window of opportunity to detect HGD or early PDAC. We showed that 8 of the 10 ever-dilated progressors with resectable neoplasia had overall survival ranging from 2.6 to 18 years.

The International CAPS Consortium published guidelines in 2020 recommended repeat imaging in 3 months or surgical resection of IPMNs with MPD ≥ 10 mm if an MPD stricture is detected, similar to the Fukuoka guidelines.4 However, when we applied these guidelines to our CAPS cohort, the threshold recommendations for intervention were not sufficiently sensitive for predicting neoplasia.30 Many familial/genetic HRIs undergoing pancreatic surveillance have pancreatic cysts but these are usually small, with relatively few patients having large cystic IPMNs that can cause MPD dilation from spread of the IPMN and/or mucin secretion into the main duct. For this reason, we used more stringent MPD diameter cut-offs than those recommended for patients with sporadic IPMN.31,32

MPD dilation may be due to a number of factors. Mechanical obstruction of the duct by the solid neoplasm may result in segmental obstruction and upstream dilation. Mucus production and main duct involvement by IPMN can also cause MPD dilation. Chronic pancreatitis is also a benign cause of MPD dilation. EUS features of chronic pancreatitis were concomitant with dilation in 73% of our ever-dilated HRI (P = .004). These mild chronic pancreatitis changes commonly reported in HRI undergoing surveillance are not associated with alcohol use12,15,33 and potentially related to PanIN and associated lobulocentric parenchymal atrophy (acinar cell loss, fat replacement, and fibrosis).34 Finally, other causes of MPD dilation include pancreas divisum,35 papillary stenosis, cystic fibrosis, stricture due to prior abdominal trauma (eg seat belt pancreatitis),36 autoimmune pancreatitis,37,38 and annular pancreas.39

Our study has several strengths, including prospective cohort design, well-characterized asymptomatic high-risk patient population with standardized long-term follow-up, and large sample size. High resolution EUS has better visualization of the MPD than CT or MRI,40 and our EUS MPD diameter measurements included the head, body, and tail at each EUS visit, which allowed us to detect MPD dilation with greater sensitivity. In addition, the statistical analysis of repeated measures over time and time-varying variables (MPD diameter, cyst size) provides robust estimates of long-term progression risk.

Our study has limitations, including single-center, and a patient population selected for their familial and/or inherited genetic risk. The number of patients in our cohort who developed neoplastic progression was also small, as it is in nearly all studies of HRI undergoing surveillance.9,41,42

Conclusion

Minimal MPD dilation without an apparent obstructing lesion is significantly associated with a high risk of neoplastic progression in asymptomatic HRI undergoing pancreas surveillance. HRI with isolated MPD dilation without an apparent obstructing lesion should be considered for short-interval surveillance, particularly if 3 or more cysts are present.
