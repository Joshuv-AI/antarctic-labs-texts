Upadacitinib for the Treatment of Cutaneous Crohn's Disease Presenting as Lymphangioma Circumscriptum
Authors: Benjamin Gerstein, William C. Lau, Avi Bitterman, Jonas A. Adalsteinsson, Zoe Gottlieb, Rachel Kassenoff, Ahuva Cices
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMetastatic Crohn’s disease refers to cutaneous Crohn’s disease noncontiguous with the gastrointestinal tract. Vulvar lymphangioma circumscriptum is a rare manifestation of metastatic Crohn’s disease, presenting as pink papillomatous or vesicular papules and plaques. Treatment options are limited and often invasive, and clearance is difficult to achieve. We report a novel case of vulvar lymphangioma circumscriptum secondary to Crohn’s disease successfully managed with oral and topical Janus kinase inhibitors in combination with procedural therapies.

INTRODUCTION

Metastatic Crohn’s disease (MCD) refers to cutaneous Crohn’s disease (CD) of the skin that is noncontiguous with the gastrointestinal tract and often occurs independent of luminal CD activity.1 Secondary lymphangioma circumscriptum (LC) is a rare, benign condition characterized by dilated lymphatic vessels that can result from inflammation or obstruction of lymphatic channels. LC has been previously reported as an uncommon presentation of vulvar MCD.2,3 Vulvar LC presents as pink papillomatous or vesicular papules and plaques on the vulva that can resemble extensive condyloma acuminata.2,3 This condition can be disabling, with symptoms of lymphedema including pain, burning, pruritus, and heaviness that can impair quality of life.2,4 Although the pathogenesis of MCD remains unclear, treatments indicated for luminal CD have been effective for MCD.5 Janus kinase (JAK)-mediated signaling is implicated in CD, and upadacitinib is an oral JAK-1 selective inhibitor FDA approved for the treatment of CD.6 Ruxolitinib 1.5% cream is a JAK-1 and JAK-2 selective topical therapy approved for the treatment of atopic dermatitis and nonsegmental vitiligo.7,8 We present the case of a 31-year-old woman with vulvar LC in the setting of well-controlled CD successfully treated with combination therapy of upadacitinib, topical ruxolitinib, cryosurgery, and intralesional triamcinolone (ILTAC).

CASE REPORT

A 31-year-old woman with a 17-year history of difficult to control fistulizing perianal CD presented to dermatology clinic with a worsening vulvar rash for several years duration. At the time of presentation, her luminal CD was in deep remission on ustekinumab, and her perianal disease required regular exams under anesthesia with seton placements. Prior therapies included infliximab, adalimumab, and mercaptopurine. Pelvic MRI 1 month before presentation at dermatology demonstrated branching transsphincteric and intersphincteric perianal fistulas without evidence of active inflammation or infection. She was referred by gynecology in the setting of trying to conceive for an itchy and painful vulvar rash that responded minimally to twice-daily application of clobetasol 0.05% ointment for several months and a 10-day course of cephalexin. Histology of 2 biopsies of the labia performed by gynecology and read by gynecologic pathology demonstrated hyperkeratosis and chronic inflammation. Exam was notable for erythematous patches and plaques in the perianal and vulvar area as well as multiple skin-colored and pink vesiculopapules coalescing into cobblestone-like plaques (Figure 1). Given her history of CD and exam with lymphangiomatous changes and edema of the vulva, the patient was clinically diagnosed with MCD.9 The histology of the prior biopsies was reviewed by dermatopathology and demonstrated dilated lymphatic channels consistent with vulvar LC (Figure 2), in the absence of granulomatous inflammation or vasculopathy.

Given the patient's strong desire to conceive and disease impact on quality of life, aggressive treatment with twice-daily application of topical ruxolitinib 1.5% cream and monthly sessions of cryosurgery with ILTAC 10 mg/mL injections was initiated (Figure 3). After 2 months with significant improvement of inflammatory skin changes, but only mild improvement of LC changes, upadacitinib was initiated by consensus among dermatology, gastroenterology, and gynecology with the goal of expediting vulvar disease control to allow for pregnancy planning. Upadacitinib was chosen due to its known efficacy in CD, rapid onset of action, ease of initiation and discontinuation, previous failed therapies, and the patient's preference to avoid invasive interventions. Upadacitinib 45 mg daily was initiated, the starting dosage indicated for gastrointestinal CD. After completing the 12-week loading dose, marked symptomatic relief was noted, and the patient transitioned to the maintenance dose of 30 mg daily (Figure 4). At week 16, there was continued improvement on exam (Figure 5), and the patient reported significant improvement in quality of life and resolution of symptoms. Given the significant clinical and patient-reported improvement after 4 months of treatment with upadacitinib, gastroenterology, gynecology, and dermatology collectively agreed to continue therapy for an additional 2 months for a total treatment course of 6 months. At that time, the patient remained significantly improved, and under multidisciplinary consensus, therapy was discontinued; she was subsequently cleared to conceive. Of note, she is now 10 weeks pregnant, and her vulvar MCD has remained well controlled with monthly cryosurgery.

DISCUSSION

The skin is a common site of extraintestinal inflammatory bowel disease affecting as many as 44% of patients with CD.10 Cutaneous manifestations of inflammatory bowel disease include MCD, perianal CD, inflammatory skin diseases (e.g., psoriasis and hidradenitis suppurativa), reactive skin diseases (e.g., erythema nodosum, pyoderma gangrenosum, and cutaneous vasculitis), as well as treatment emergent side effects (e.g., TNF-induced psoriasis and acne).10 MCD is rare and its true incidence is likely underestimated given its varied clinical presentation and lack of familiarity across specialties.10 Among patients with MCD, LC is an uncommon feature and infrequently reported in the literature.2

In this case, we highlight the efficacy of combination therapy with upadacitinib, an oral JAK-1 selective inhibitor, a topical JAK 1,2 selective inhibitor, cryosurgery, and ILTAC for LC secondary to vulvar MCD. A small number of cases have shown efficacy of JAK inhibitors for MCD, including some with vulvar involvement; however, to our knowledge, none have described features of LC.11–14 Reported treatment approaches for acquired vulvar LC are predominantly surgical, including vulvectomy and laminectomy, or abrasive methods (laser therapy, electrocoagulation, cryosurgery, and sclerotherapy).2,3 However, surgical interventions have significant risks, including infection, scarring, and recurrence, underscoring the need for alternative treatment modalities, particularly for young females of childbearing potential.4 This case of comanagement by gastroenterology, gynecology, and dermatology highlights the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration to improve patient care, adequately address patient needs, and minimize the morbidity of complex, debilitating diseases.

Upadacitinib is a selective JAK-1 inhibitor currently U.S. FDA approved for the treatment of multiple autoimmune and inflammatory disorders including CD and ulcerative colitis.6 Recent case reports suggest that upadacitinib may also be an effective treatment option for MCD.11–14 As a targeted immunomodulator and activator of transcription signaling pathways, upadacitinib may address the underlying immune dysregulation contributing to LC in MCD.15 To our knowledge, we report the first case of upadacitinib as an effective treatment for vulvar LC as well as the first case of topical ruxolitinib for inflammatory lesions of MCD. Given the inconsistent response of MCD to conventional luminal CD therapies and the limited noninvasive treatment options for acquired LC, upadacitinib, both as an adjunct or monotherapy, may be a valuable treatment strategy for clinicians managing cases of genital involvement of MCD and topical ruxolitinib may be a useful topical therapy for inflammatory lesions of MCD. Additional studies are needed to validate the efficacy of oral and topical JAK inhibitors for cutaneous MCD.
