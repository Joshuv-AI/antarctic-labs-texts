US professional medical societies’ public statements regarding violence in Palestine and Israel from October to December 2023: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Carmen Conroy, Sophia Bylsma, Alaha Nasari, Sarah Alsamman, Christian Adair Powers, Maydha Dhanuka, Noor Falah, Sabreen Akhter, Yara M Asi, Bram Peter Wispelwey, David M Mills
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to analyse the quantity and content of publicly discoverable statements that US-based medical societies issued regarding the 2023 escalation of violence in Palestine and Israel.DesignWe conducted a mixed-methods study using an explanatory sequential design.MethodsInclusion criteria were defined as US-based medical societies from the American Medical Association House of Delegates, Council of Medical Specialty Societies (CMSS), or Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) Council for Academic Specialty Societies, yielding 186 US-based professional medical societies. Descriptive statistics and oods ratios (ORs) were used to assess the association between society characteristics and odds of statement issuance. A qualitative inductive and deductive thematic approach was employed to identify themes from public statements made from 7 October 2023 to 7 December 2023.Results22 professional medical societies (N=22/186, 11.83%) issued 20 unique public statements regarding violence in Israel and Palestine. Exploratory OR analyses suggested associations between statement issuance and several society characteristics, though CIs were wide and thus underscored uncertainty regarding the magnitude of these associations. Exploratory OR analyses suggested that statement issuance was associated with possession of a lobbying arm (OR 26.83, 95% CI 3.53 to 204.25, p<0.01); a society diversity, equity, and inclusivity initiative (OR 6.57, 95% CI 1.48 to 29.04, p=0.01); a society global health or international initiative (OR 3.11, 95% CI 1.23 to 7.86, p=0.02); a society land acknowledgment or indigenous health initiative (OR 8.47, 95% CI 1.60 to 44.99, p=0.01); and prior issuance of a statement about the Russo-Ukrainian War (OR 38.83, 95% CI 10.63 to 141.75, p<0.01). Of the 20 unique, publicly discoverable statements found, 25% (N=5/20) used verbiage indicating exclusive support for Israeli or Jewish civilians, while none expressed sole support for Palestinian civilians. Our qualitative analysis revealed three main thematic categories of statement content: (1) those expressing support for or condemnation of a specific group, (2) those providing historical context, and (3) those describing a society’s approach to statement issuance.ConclusionMost professional medical societies did not make a public statement regarding violence in Palestine and Israel, and societies that previously engaged in political or international advocacy were more likely to issue a statement. Statements generally avoided any historical, contextual, or geopolitical framing and were more likely to reference Israeli victims than Palestinian victims of violence as well as violence by Hamas than Israeli state violence.Keywords: Health policy, PUBLIC HEALTH, Health Equity, Health Workforce, Knowledge

Introduction

The occupied Gaza Strip (colloquially referred to as ‘Gaza’) is facing a humanitarian crisis of unprecedented proportions. Following the October 7th attack on southern Israel by Palestinian militant groups—which killed nearly 1,200 people and resulted in the capture of approximately 240 hostages—Israel declared war.1 2 The already dire conditions in occupied Gaza worsened with the ongoing violence enacted by the Israeli military, as well as the imposition of a total siege restricting entry of food, water, electricity, and fuel.3 4 This collective punishment resulted in devastating consequences for the more than 2 million Palestinians residing in Gaza. The enactment of a full blockade, evacuation orders leading to forced displacement, and an unprecedented bombing campaign left over 25, 000 Palestinians confirmed dead during this study period (7 October 2023 to 7 December 2023); as of October 2025, this number has climbed past 68 000.5 6 Another 1.9 million have been displaced during this time, and Gaza’s healthcare system has completely collapsed.4,8

In its totality, Israel’s campaign in Gaza has been recognised as a genocide by multiple legal and human rights authorities and organisations, including several UN Special Rapporteurs’ investigations, Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, the World Organization Against Torture, the International Association of Genocide Scholars’, and Israeli organisations including B’Tselem and Physicians for Human Rights: Israel.9,13 The level of devastation led some prominent international medical and public health organisations, including the WHO and Médecins Sans Frontières, to advocate for a ceasefire.14,16 Many Western health organisations, however, have remained silent with regards to the growing humanitarian crisis as well as the attacks on health workers and infrastructure in Gaza, despite the financial and political support of their countries to Israel.17,20

The US have powerful global influence, particularly on Israel, and US-based professional medical societies leverage their own power on US policy.21 22 These societies sometimes issue public statements in response to topical social and political issues, especially ones with clear public health implications, including statements on the gun violence epidemic, the Black Lives Matter movement, and the overturning of Roe v. Wade.23,26 At times, these societies also assert views on the international level, notably the ongoing Russo-Ukrainian War.27 The importance of these public statements has been the subject of academic study, as professional medical societies leverage unique cultural influence in the form of their vast membership, lobbying power, and professed commitment to the practice of medicine and improvement of public welfare.28 29 30 Previous analyses have posited that academic organisations shape knowledge production; in particular, the framework of epistemic injustice has been applied in illuminating how lived experiences and knowledge of marginalised communities are often discredited in legal systems, healthcare settings and the broader dissemination of information by academic institutions.31,36 Thus, professional societies’ publicly issued statements regarding different sociopolitical movements or events can shape public discourse around these topics, either reinforcing or correcting epistemic wrongs.

While the stated aim of many professional societies is to promote patients’ welfare and members’ professional interests, statements issued by societies may not always be congruent with these goals in the context of competing financial, political and commercial pressure.37,40 Societies that represent health-related disciplines have also been criticised for releasing statements exclusively in response to discrete events, rather than using their platforms to keep ongoing health-related issues in public conversation.41 This paper aims to numerically quantify as well as qualitatively characterise the response of medical societies to violence in Israel and the Gaza Strip in the 2 months following 7 October 2023.

The urgency of such an analysis stems from the gravity of the humanitarian crisis which unfolded in the Gaza Strip following 7 October 2023.42 During this time, Israeli forces bombed at least 20 hospitals and killed more than 1000 healthcare workers, in addition to precipitating a blockade of food, water and medical aid, thus fomenting conditions for the spread of infectious disease and engendering a population of severely injured people in need of urgent and long-term medical care.43,46 With a current estimated civilian death toll surpassing 68,000—a significant proportion of which consists of women and children—and thousands more reported missing, the situation demands a closer examination of the broader healthcare community’s response.447,49 Furthermore, the disproportionate violence against Palestinians in the form of settler colonialism—defined by scholars as colonial domination with the goal of eliminating indigenous communities and overtaking their land—spans many decades. This historical violence coupled with the more recent escalation of state-sanctioned war crimes raises questions about the role that healthcare organisations, including professional medical societies, play in perpetuating or alleviating such disparities.50,53

This study examines two primary research aims with several components. First, we assessed whether professional medical societies issued any statements following the episode of violence in Israel and Palestine on 7 October 2023. We also explored whether there were specific society characteristics that were associated with society statement issuance. As far as the authors are aware, no professional organisation analysed in this study is mandated to comment on or release statements regarding global affairs. Our aim was to find if a statement was released (particularly in comparison as to whether a statement was released on the Russo-Ukrainian War) and, within released statements, to assess their contents. For societies who did release statements, we examined the content of the statements through a framework of epistemic injustice, referring to injustices that occur in the production and dissemination of knowledge.31 On analysis, we discuss the implications of our findings and what they may indicate about those societies who issued them, as well as the medical community in the US more broadly.

Methods

Study design

We used a sequential mixed-methods study design to present our findings.54 55 In phase one, we queried website and social media accounts of professional medical societies to quantify publicly discoverable statements. We then performed quantitative analysis employing logistic regression to estimate the odds of statement issuance and association with various institutional demographic characteristics in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for quantitative research.56 In the second phase, we analysed textual data from the statements gleaned in phase one by conducting content and thematic qualitative analysis using the framework described by Fereday and Muir-Cochrane.57 Statement language and thematic content was used to categorise excerpts from each statement as expressing support for or condemnation of a specific group, providing historical context, or describing the society’s approach to statement issuance. Select qualitative findings were then correlated with select demographic characteristics, again using logistic regression analysis. We presented our findings in accordance with the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative studies (COREQ).58

Study team composition

The multidisciplinary study team consisted of physicians, public health researchers, and medical students at the time of this study.

Society inclusion and exclusion criteria

Following an adaptation of methods previously published by Levy et al, inclusion criteria were defined as US-based medical societies from the American Medical Association (AMA) House of Delegates, Council of Medical Specialty Societies (CMSS) or Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) Council for Academic Specialty Societies.2659,61 Statements from an organisational leader on behalf of the society on a public society platform, as well as collective statements issued by a board or group of society members, were included. Exclusion criteria included statements, letters, or blog posts made by self-identified society members which were published on a personal platform (such as on a personal social media account), instead of on an official society publication with explicit endorsement from the society. We also excluded internally circulated, private society statements, as we did not have access to them.

Data searching and collection

Between 7 October 2023 and 7 December 2023, the authors queried societies’ websites and social media channels following an adaptation of methods previously published by Levy et al and McNeilly et al to gather statements regarding ongoing violence in Palestine issued within the 2 months immediately following the Hamas attack which took place on 7 October 2023.27 62 Search terms were combined to create Boolean searches including terms such as ‘October 7’, ‘Palestine’, ‘Israel’ and ‘Gaza’. A complete list of search terms is more explicitly enumerated in online supplemental file 1. All study authors involved in data collection completed searches for statements on a horizontal search engine (that is, Google), as well as vertical search engines, such as official society social media accounts (that is, Twitter, Instagram and LinkedIn). Findings determined if any of the above identified professional medical societies made publicly discoverable statements and updates to original statements found. Existence of statements and statement updates were tabulated in binary, as existing or not existing, based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria specified above. At minimum, two independent study team members repeated this search for each society during the above specified date range. In cases where the two members found discordant results, a third member repeated the search with the same terms to establish a consensus.

Society characteristics

In addition to searching for statements, we also queried publicly available information about characteristics of sampled societies. These characteristics were chosen as a reflection of a society’s professional influence and reach, influence on public policy, commitment to health equity and global health related activities. These characteristics and their definitions are listed below:

Membership count: The number of active registered members in a particular professional medical society, as reported on the official society website.

Presence of a society lobbying arm: Existence of a discoverable public record of medical societies’ lobbying spending published by any government source within the past calendar year prior to 7 October 2023

Non-profit status: Registration of a particular society as a tax-exempt 501(c)3 organisation or existence of a non-profit 501(c)3 organisation affiliated with the society employing society members in leadership roles.

Global health initiative within the society: Existence of a society global health initiative (including promotion of international membership or concerted society efforts to provide healthcare abroad).

Society indigenous health initiative or public land acknowledgement statement: Existence of an indigenous health initiative or public land acknowledgement statement on an official society website or social media account.

Society diversity, equity, and inclusivity (DEI) initiative: Existence of a DEI initiative, committee, or leadership role within a particular society.

Content of the society’s mission statement: inclusion of ‘equity’, ‘diversity’ and/or ‘inclusivity’ in a society’s core values or mission statement, as listed on their official website or social media accounts.

Society statement regarding the war in Ukraine: Existence of a publicly issued society statement (made by society leadership) regarding the Russo-Ukrainian War as discoverable on their official website or social media accounts following the Russian assault on Ukraine which took place on 23 February 2022. See online supplemental file 1 for specific search terms employed.

We compiled and tabulated all data in Microsoft Excel.

Quantitative analysis

We then performed a quantitative analysis to test the relationship between society characteristics and statement issuance, using a logistic regression model to estimate an OR correlating demographic characteristics with statement issuance. Following our qualitative analysis as reported below, we again used a logistic regression model to calculate an OR testing the relationship between select demographic variables and select qualitative statement content findings. Coefficients for all covariates are interpreted as conditional associations and not as causal effects. Given the small number of societies that issued statements, the OR analyses were intended to be exploratory and hypothesis-generating. We did not interpret these models as providing precise estimates of association magnitude, particularly where CIs were wide. Lastly, we used descriptive statistics to compare the relative proportion of a subset of themes represented in original statements compared with statement updates for relevant societies. All analyses were completed using Stata V.17.0 Basic Edition. Quantitative analysis was completed in accordance with STROBE guidelines for reporting quantitative data (online supplemental file 2).56

Qualitative analysis

We next used content and thematic analysis as described by Fereday and Muir-Cochrane to code characteristics of statement content both deductively, on a priori determination of domains highlighting opportunities for the perpetuation of epistemic injustice, and inductively, on reading the content of the statements found.57 63 64 In the deductive phase of our process, codes were developed which included the mention of specific involved parties, historical context for the events of 7 October and different possible approaches to statement issuance. Statement content was analysed for the presence of or reference to these codes. Next, within the collected statements, content was inductively coded to determine specifically which parties and settings were mentioned, whether action by the mentioned parties was supported or condemned, specific settings, features of the historic events leading to 7 October and ensuing consequences, and specific language explaining statement issuance. A codebook of themes was created collaboratively by the lead coders, and themes were refined on iterative review with the study team throughout the process of statement collection. Qualitative analysis was verified after completion by assessing accordance with COREQ guidelines for reporting qualitative data (online supplemental file 3).58

Qualitative analysis validation

Three study team members independently coded all statements to ensure intercoder reliability. Differences were resolved by majority consensus and discussion with the study team. Additionally, we have provided substantial excerpts from most of the discovered statements in online supplemental file 4 for transparency.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Demographics

A total of 186 professional medical societies—representing an estimated 1.7 million members and at least 92 distinct lobbying groups, which possess over US$10 million in lobbying capital—were discovered in our search.65 These included 93 (50.00%) societies with a lobbying arm; 174 (93.55%) with non-profit status; 119 (63.98%) with a publicly professed DEI initiative; 66 (35.48%) that listed ‘equity’, ‘diversity’ or ‘inclusivity’ in their society core values or mission statement; 73 (39.25%) with a global health or international initiative; 6 (3.23%) with a publicly published land acknowledgement or indigenous health initiative; and 42 (22.58%) which had previously issued a public statement regarding the Russo-Ukrainian War. Of the 20 unique statements, 5 (25.00%) came from self-identified, individually named society leaders, while 15 (75.00%) were signed anonymously or by a leadership board on behalf of the collective society members. In one instance, a society initially published an online public statement which was no longer accessible publicly at the time of qualitative analysis, as it had been deleted. This statement has thus been excluded from all qualitative analysis, though it was included in the above-mentioned demographic analysis. In another instance, a single statement from a parent society was also publicly issued by another affiliate organisation reporting to this parent society. The affiliate society was categorised demographically as its own distinct entity that did issue a statement, but the statement issued by the parent organisation was only analysed thematically once. Of note, one society issued an update and recanted its original statement, which is no longer publicly accessible, leaving the update published in its stead. As there was no baseline original statement against which to compare the update, the update is counted as the original statement for this society and all qualitative findings relevant to this statement have been tabulated as such.

Of the 22 statement-issuing societies, 4 societies (18.18%) issued updates to their initial statements. The average number of days that lapsed between the original statement issuance and the update was 11.20 days with a SD of 7.04 days. All updates were issued between 31 October and 9 November 2023.

Content of public statements

Through the development of deductive a priori codes highlighting possible instances of epistemic injustice and the complementary generation of inductive themes which emerged from the statements discovered, three overarching thematic categories were developed from the 20 unique publicly accessible statements: (1) expression of support for or condemnation of a specific group, (2) statements providing historical context for the ongoing war, and (3) language that described societies’ approaches to statement issuance or assuming a political stance.

Themes expressing support for or condemnation of a specific group

Themes expressing support for or condemnation of a specific group included those that identified discrimination against a discrete group (such as invoking intolerance of antisemitism or Islamophobia) or those that explicitly mentioned a group of people implicated in the ongoing war, ranging from directly impacted civilians to society members themselves. To this end, we found that 8 of the 20 unique public statements (40.00%) mentioned the word ‘Palestine’ or ‘Palestinians’, while the majority of statements (N=17/20, 85.00%) mentioned ‘Israel’ or ‘Israelis’ by name. Furthermore, our analysis demonstrated that 1 of the 20 public statements (5.00%) was concerned with decrying Islamophobia, while 3 (15.00%) of the statements were concerned with denouncing antisemitism. Additionally, 50% (N=10/20) of statements mentioned the impact of the conflict on ‘all civilians’ without naming a group, while another 35% (N=7/20) explicitly mentioned the impact of the violence on both Israeli and Palestinian civilians. 25% (N=5/20) held concern exclusively for Israeli citizens, and none (N=0/20, 0.00%) of the statements focused solely on Palestinian civilians. Similarly, 8 statements (40.00%) alluded to the impact of the war on all society members without explicitly naming the involved parties, while 2 of the 20 statements (10.00%) made mention of the war’s impact exclusively on Israeli or Jewish society members; none (N=0/20, 0.00%) of the statements expressed concern solely for Palestinian, Muslim or Arab society members.

This thematic category also included themes decrying the actions of certain groups, including naming a specific group as a perpetrator of violence. 9 (45.00%) of the 20 statements found condemned the actions of Hamas or identified Hamas as an instigator of violence on 7 October. None (N=0/20, 0.00%) of the statements identified the state of Israel or the Israeli military as perpetrators of violence, neither historically in the decades leading up to the events of 7 October, nor currently as perpetrators of retaliatory violence.

Themes providing historical context for the ongoing war

The second thematic category encompassed statement content which provided historical context for the events that unfolded on 7 October and the resultant siege on Gaza. We first assessed how the organisations characterised the primary actors; in line with the US State Department designation, 40% (N=8/20) of the statements referred to Hamas as a terrorist organisation.62 66 However, the rest of the subthemes from this category yielded negative results. For example, none (N=0/20, 0.00%) of the statements alluded to Israel as the Occupying Power in the occupied Gaza Strip and West Bank (which is the internationally accepted status of the Palestinian territories, including by the USA), nor made mention of apartheid, a term increasingly used by Israeli, Palestinian and international government and non-profit organisations to describe Israel’s system of racial domination over Palestinians.67 68 Additionally, despite the clear implications for the provision of healthcare, none (N=0/20, 0.00%) of the professional medical society statements made mention of the blockade of food, water, medicine or other aid into Gaza.

Themes describing society approaches to statement issuance or assuming a political stance

The final thematic category included language which described the society’s approach to issuing statements about sociopolitical events. This included language that described a professional obligation to address the ongoing war by invoking a Hippocratic duty (N=2/20, 10.00%), stated diversity and inclusivity as guiding society values (N=2/20, 10.00%), made directives for society member discourse (N=1/20, 5%), and mentioned the need for humanitarian aid (N=9/20, 45.00%). Interestingly, though nearly half of societies alluded to the urgent need for humanitarian aid, none of the societies (N=0/20, 0.00%) promoted their own humanitarian initiatives to address the war or made mention of potential society-funded donations to the cause. Instead, nearly half (N=8/20, 40.00%) of all statement-issuing societies directed members to resources or recommendations for individually participating in relief or advocacy efforts. Furthermore, one fifth of statements analysed (N=4/20, 20.00%) invoked both the urgency of the humanitarian crisis and simultaneously outsourced humanitarian action items, resources or recommendations to individual society members.

Associations between institutional characteristics and statement issuance

The following OR analyses were exploratory and intended to identify patterns in statement issuance across publicly identifiable society characteristics. Because of the small number of statement-issuing societies, wide CIs, and potential for confounding, these estimates should not be interpreted causally or as precise measures of association magnitude. A comprehensive summary of the ORs for the relationship between statement issuance and each demographic characteristic is summarised in table 2. The tables and formulas used to calculate these ORs can be found in online supplemental file 5A.

Lobbying arm

Professional medical societies with a lobbying arm had 26.83 times greater odds of issuing a statement than those who did not (OR=26.83, 95% CI 3.53 to 204.25, p<0.01). Whereas 21 out of 93 (22.58%) societies with a lobbying arm issued a statement, 1 out of 93 societies (1.08%) without a lobbying arm issued one. Because only 22 societies issued statements overall, several comparisons contained small cell counts and produced wide CIs. Accordingly, these ORs should be interpreted as evidence of possible association rather than as precise estimates of the magnitude of association.

Nonprofit status

Medical societies with non-profit status were 1.51 times more likely to issue a statement than those that are for-profit entities (95% CI 0.19 to 12.30, p=0.70). Almost all statements came from societies with non-profit status; while 21 of 174 (12.17%) societies with a non-profit arm issued a statement, 1 out of 12 societies (8.33%) without non-profit status issued a statement.

Society DEI initiative

Medical societies with a DEI initiative were 6.57 times more likely to have been statement issuers than those without such an initiative (95% CI 1.48 to 29.04, p=0.01). Whereas 20 of 119 (16.81%) societies with a DEI initiative issued a statement, 2 out of 67 societies (2.99%) without such an initiative issued a statement.

Core values

Medical societies that enumerated ‘diversity’, ‘equity’ or ‘inclusivity’ as core values or in their mission statements on their public websites were 1.98 times more likely to have issued statements (95% CI 0.81 to 4.86, p=0.13). Of the 66 societies explicitly enumerating these core values, 11 of them (16.67%) made statements, whereas 11 of the 120 (9.17%) societies that do not explicitly name these core values offered a statement.

Global health or international initiative

Societies with publicly discoverable global health or international initiatives were 3.11 times more likely to have been statement issuers than those that did not have such programmes (95% CI 1.23 to 7.86, p=0.02). Among the 73 societies queried who did have a global health or international initiative, 14 (19.18%) made statements; however, for the 113 societies without such an initiative, 8 weighed in publicly (7.08%).

Society land acknowledgement or indigenous health initiative

Medical societies that have publicly issued land acknowledgments or have their own indigenous health initiatives were 8.47 times more likely to have issued statements than those who have not made such acknowledgments or do not have such initiatives (95% CI 1.60 to 44.99, p=0.01). Six societies had publicly listed land acknowledgments or indigenous health initiatives, but of those 6, 3 (50.00%) offered publicly discoverable statements about the war in Palestine. In contrast, 19 of the 180 societies (10.56%) with no such land acknowledgement or indigenous health initiative made a publicly discoverable statement.

Society statement about Russo-Ukrainian War

Lastly, medical societies that have previously made a publicly discoverable statement about the Russo-Ukrainian War following 23 February 2022 had 38.83 times greater odds of issuing a statement than those which did not publicly opine about the war in Ukraine (95% CI 10.63 to 141.75, p<0.01). 42 societies made public statements about the war in Ukraine, with less than half (N=19, 45.24%) of those same societies offering public commentary about the war in Palestine. Of the 144 societies that offered no statement about the Russo-Ukrainian War, 3 (2.08%) opted to publicly opine about the war on Gaza.

Associations between institutional characteristics and content of public statements

We assessed the ORs for the relationship between certain qualitative content statement findings and select demographic characteristics. In particular, we analysed the relationship between society DEI initiative and the espousal of DEI values in public statements, and the association between societies that mention DEI in their mission statement or core values and the presence of DEI values in public statements. The tables and formulas used to calculate these ORs can be found in online supplemental file 5B and are summarised below.

Among statement issuers, professional medical societies with a DEI initiative had 0.43 times lower odds (95% CI 0.01 to 13.69, p=0.63) of issuing a statement that mentioned ‘diversity’, ‘equity’ or ‘inclusivity’.

Analysis of all publicly discoverable statements issued also demonstrated that professional medical societies which profess these core values had 0.13 times lower odds (95% CI 0.01 to 3.11, p=0.21) of invoking these same essential values in their statements regarding the war in Palestine.

Language used in updates compared with language used in original statements

Among the 20 unique original statements, there were 4 individual updates (20.00%) issued by 4 unique societies in the weeks following. We observed some shifts in language and thematic composition of the statement updates compared with the original statements, although the small sample size of update-issuing societies (N=4/20) provided limited ability to interpret these findings. Two times as many statement updates (N=4/4, 100.00%) as original statements (N=2/4, 50.00%) issued by the same societies mentioned the impact of the war on both Israeli and Palestinian civilians by name. None of the updates expressed unilateral support for Israeli civilians, whereas half (N=2/4, 50.00%) of the original statements had; additionally, none of the statement updates expressed sole support for Israeli or Jewish society members, with the majority instead expressing support for all society members (N=3/4, 75.00%), regardless of national or religious identity. While three-fourths (N=3/4, 75.00%) of the original statements both named Hamas as a perpetrator of violence and labelled Hamas as a terrorist group, 25.00% (N=1/4) of the statement updates did the same. As far as addressing the society response to the humanitarian crisis prompted by the war, one update (N=1/4, 25.00%) pointed society members to a society initiative related to relief efforts, while others took the approach of cautioning respectful conversation (N=1/4, 25.00%) or invoking the importance of free speech (N=1/4, 25.00%).

Discussion

This study finds that most professional medical societies based in the US (N=164/186, 88.17%) did not issue any public statements regarding violence in Palestine and Israel in the 2 months following 7 October 2023; in other words, among these 186 societies, 22 (11.83%) issued 20 unique public statements. By comparison, one study demonstrated that 72 out of 187 (38.50%) professional medical societies surveyed issued public statements in the 2 weeks following the overturning of Roe vs Wade.26 While this discrepancy may be explained as a difference between a domestic issue compared with an international one, our own investigation suggests that over the past 2 years, 42 of the same 186 societies queried (22.58%) have issued statements regarding the Russo-Ukrainian War. Further study is needed to understand why approximately half as many professional medical societies weighed in on the violence in Palestine and Israel compared with the Russo-Ukrainian War, though this finding may be due at least in part to the discrepancy in length of time lapsed between these events and the end of the study period. Future studies may query which demographic characteristics were most likely to be associated with Russo-Ukrainian War statement issuance compared with statement issuance about the October 7th attacks.

In our exploratory quantitative analysis, several society demographic characteristics were associated with a statistically significant likelihood of public statement issuance, including existence of a society lobbying arm, society DEI initiative, society global health or international initiative, society land acknowledgement or indigenous health initiative, and a previous public society statement about the war in Ukraine; meanwhile, other demographic characteristics proved not to be significantly associated with public statement issuance, including society non-profit status and naming of ‘diversity’, ‘equity’ or ‘inclusion’ in the society’s core values or mission statement. However, these estimates were imprecise, with wide CIs, and should be interpreted as evidence of probable associations rather than as reliable estimates of association magnitude. Unmeasured confounding is likely and may be an important limitation of these analyses. For example, society size, public visibility, leadership structure, political orientation, member pressure, prior advocacy practices, and institutional capacity may influence both the measured characteristics and the likelihood of statement issuance. Our quantitative findings are therefore best interpreted as hypothesis-generating and complementary to our qualitative findings, rather than as indisputable evidence of direct causality. In spite of these caveats, our findings suggest that societies with a record of engaging in political and international advocacy were more likely to issue statements.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, societies analysed do not have institutional obligations to release statements regarding global affairs. Notably, many of these organisations have not released statements on a litany of public issues directly related to health, or even on issues with explicit relevance to their particular specialty. As these organisations wield immeasurable influence in guiding medical research priorities, industry standards and ethics, and advocacy efforts, the role of public statements on core issues relevant to the medical profession or their (sub)specialties should be understood as a powerful tool of communication. While we do not argue that all professional societies must be compelled to make public statements on all issues, they may consider developing transparent and consistent guidelines about when such statements will be released, if at all, and what the purpose and content of those statements should be.

Among the 20 unique publicly discoverable statements, none of the statements provided evidence-based historical or geopolitical context for violence in Palestine and Israel, including naming the ongoing Israeli occupation and seizure of Palestinian land deemed illegal by the International Court of Justice in July 2024, what leading human rights organisations have described as the apartheid reality for all Palestinians living in the region, the extensive previous attacks on healthcare facilities and personnel, the pre-existing blockade of the Gaza Strip, or the more restrictive blockade of food, water, and medical aid into Gaza that was imposed by Israel starting on 11 October 2023.34 45 69,71 The relevance of such context is corroborated by the fact that many health and human rights organisations have released data meticulously capturing the multifaceted health effects of the occupation and blockade, including the WHO, which was also among the first global actors to call for a ceasefire.3,59 However, none of the major professional medical associations based in the US echoed this call or acknowledged the considerations the WHO used in its plea for an end to violence in Gaza.

Regarding the current violence at the time of statement issuance, discrepancies in society statements were found. While the sample size is small (n=20), the statements were more likely to reference Israeli victims than Palestinian victims of violence, and similarly, they were more likely to reference Hamas violence than Israeli state violence. Almost half of the statements (45.00%) name Hamas as a perpetrator of violence, and 40.00% of the statements condemn Hamas explicitly as a terrorist group; meanwhile, none of the statements make mention of Israel or the Israeli military as perpetrators of violence, despite the extraordinary destruction and loss of life this violence had already enacted by early December 2023.6 7 47 73 This discrepancy in naming victims and perpetrators of violence, while requiring cautious interpretation, is in line with the historical and ongoing silence regarding the health impacting policies and actions of the Israeli state vis-à-vis Palestinians.74,76

The word ‘Israel’ or ‘Israeli’ was mentioned more than twice as often (85.00%) as the word ‘Palestine’ or ‘Palestinian’ (40.00%), which may also reflect disproportionate attention when considered through the lens of epistemic injustice.

Though only a limited number of societies issued updates to their original statements (20.00%), there were some readily observable trends in language used in the updates compared with original statements. This included a shift from expressing sole concern for one party of civilians or society members to all civilians or society members. Additionally, fewer updates offered a unilateral explanation for the instigation of violence. As statement updates may indicate a tacit acknowledgement that an original statement was insufficient, these changes in language may represent societies moving to correct language they perceived may have been too one-sided or inaccurate. It may also indicate that associations received critical feedback regarding the initial statements, although this cannot be confirmed by the data available. To ensure that any future statements are evidence-based and grant appropriate consideration to unequal systems of power, professional medical societies should consider creating transparent standards for future statement issuance. This may help attenuate some of the observed inconsistencies in this data set.

While it is beyond the scope of this study to identify all possible reasons that these US-based associations may have released statements that differentially recognised victims and perpetrators of violence, emerging research has indicated that racism distinct from Islamophobia or anti-Arab racism may be expressed against Palestinians as a group; for example, anti-Palestinian racism includes denial of Palestinian history or narratives, unwillingness to recognise power inequities between Palestinians and Israelis, and generalised consideration of Palestinians as terrorists or terrorist sympathisers, particularly when Israel or its allies justify collective punishment against Palestinians or the widescale killing of civilians.77 78 Notably, scholars of genocide have highlighted that the crisis in their own field wrought by Israel’s destruction of Gaza speaks to an ‘unwillingness to accept the full humanity of Palestinians’ that is endemic within Western sites of knowledge production, with Western institutional and scholarly contributions ensuring that ‘the condition of Palestinians remains outside of the normative priorities’, in turn providing narrative justification for their material destruction.79 80 Another factor potentially influencing the nature of these statements is the broader rhetorical and financial support the USA offers Israel. Since the end of World War II, the USA has provided Israel with US$300 billion in aid, making Israel the top global recipient of American aid.80Organisations based in the USA may have incentives to echo hegemonic views of foreign policy, especially in the case that they rely on funding or other support from the US government. Lastly, the sociopolitical complexity of this international conflict may play a role in how quickly societies were able to appraise threats to society members and patient populations.

Strengths of this study include the comprehensive nature of surveying 186 professional medical societies, as well as the mixed-methods approach to providing both quantitative and descriptive findings. Additionally, while qualitative studies may be subjective in nature, we employed three study team members to independently code the statements to achieve consensus, allowing for a more robust interpretation of the data. Moreover, the analysis of these medical societies’ statements through the lens of their role in actualising knowledge production and influencing public discourse provides an important framework for holding institutions accountable to their espoused values and the public which they aim to serve.

One limitation of this study is the observational, cross-sectional nature of the statistical models used. As such, the reported ORs throughout reflect conditional associations and may be influenced by unmeasured institutional factors; they should not be interpreted as causal effects. Put differently, there may be possible confounding factors not elucidated here which influence both society characteristics as well as likelihood of statement issuance. For this reason, the OR analyses should be interpreted as exploratory evidence of association rather than as causal findings or precise estimates of effect magnitude. Another limitation of this study is that it provides insight into the approaches of AMA House of Delegates, CMSS and AAMC Council for Academic Specialty Societies member societies, all of which are US-based organisations. This may limit the generalisability of these findings beyond the scope of American professional medical societies. However, our study demonstrated that over one-third (39.25%) of these societies have global health or international initiatives, and many more of the societies boast international membership with chapters across multiple countries outside the USA. Additionally, the USA has played a well-documented role in financially and politically enabling the actions of the state of Israel, which may be influencing global discourse about Israeli violence against the Palestinian people.22 Lastly, it is possible that since statements were collected in the 2 months immediately following 7 October 2023, some societies may have opted to release statements beyond 7 December 2023, which will therefore not be captured by our analysis; additionally, though our methods of searching for public statements were thorough and reproducible by multiple study members, our list of key search terms was not exhaustive and it is therefore possible that we did not discover some existing statements.

Conclusions

Most professional medical societies did not issue public statements in the wake of violence in Palestine and Israel after 7 October 2023. Of those societies that did issue statements, none of the statements provided historical context prior to the events of October 7. The statements were more likely to reference Israeli victims than Palestinian victims of violence and were more likely to reference Hamas violence than Israeli state violence. Further research is needed to understand the reasons for and content within societal statements released about violence in Israel/Palestine.
