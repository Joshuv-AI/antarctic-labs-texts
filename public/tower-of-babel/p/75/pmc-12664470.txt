TRIM36 inhibits lung fibroblast activation and pulmonary fibrosis through the degradation of phospho-AKT1
Authors: Yajun Li, Yuexia Yang, Yingjie Wang, Feifan Shi, Yue Zhang, Hongzhi Wen, Xin Pan, Cong Xia, Zhongzheng Li, Kaisa Cui, Guojun Zhao, Huibing Liu, Qiwen Wang, Lan Wang, Guoying Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) critically regulates protein stability and function. E3 ligase dysregulation plays a crucial role in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). TRIM36, a RING-finger E3 ligase with emerging tumor-suppressive roles in various cancers, remains poorly characterized in IPF. In this study, we found that TRIM36 is significantly upregulated in IPF patients, and its expression is negatively associated with the disease severity. TRIM36 inhibits lung fibroblast proliferation, migration, and differentiation into myofibroblast in vitro. Mechanistically, TRIM36 mediates K48-linked polyubiquitination of phospho-AKT1 (T308/S473), leading to its proteasomal degradation and consequent inhibition of AKT signaling. Overexpression of TRIM36 markedly ameliorates bleomycin (BLM)-induced mouse pulmonary fibrosis. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that TRIM36 inhibits pulmonary fibrosis by degrading phospho-AKT1 (T308/S473) to inhibit AKT1 signaling, offering valuable insights for IPF treatment.

Introduction

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a common interstitial lung disease with rising prevalence, particularly in the elderly, characterized by excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and progressive lung interstitial remodeling.1,2 Repetitive epithelial injury initiates the complex interactions among alveolar epithelial cells, mesenchymal cells, immune cells, and endothelial cells, ultimately leading to the fibroblast activation through enhanced proliferation, migration, and differentiation.3 Myofibroblasts, which are differentiated from fibroblasts, persistently secrete ECM, driving irreversible promotion of lung interstitial remodeling and fibrosis.4 Elucidating the molecular mechanisms of IPF pathogenesis has crucial significance for developing novel therapeutic options.

Multiple signals, including phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT), transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), and Wnt/β-catenin pathway, are frequently dysregulated in IPF. The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway plays crucial roles in multiple cellular processes, such as cell survival, metastasis, and metabolism.5,6 Classically, AKT activation consists of two steps: First, AKT is transferred from cytoplasm to cell membrane in response to stimuli, such as growth factors or insulin; Second, AKT is phosphorylated by PDK1 and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) (functioning as PDK2). Activated AKT dissociates from cell membrane and translocates to nucleus or cytoplasm, where it phosphorylates downstream substrates, such as murine double minute 2 (MDM2), glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β), mTOR, and forkhead box O (FOXO) proteins, thereby generating diverse biological effects.7,8 The abnormal activation of PI3K/AKT signaling plays a profound role in fibrosis-related cellular behaviors, including lung fibroblast activation (proliferation, migration, and differentiation) and alveolar epithelial type 2 cell (AEC2) epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT).9 AKT activity is tightly regulated by post-translational modifications, including but not limited to ubiquitination and phosphorylation. Notably, several E3 ubiquitin ligases, such as breast cancer susceptibility gene 1 (BRCA1), carboxyl-terminus of Hsc70-interacting protein (CHIP), and mitochondrial ubiquitin ligase activator of NF-κB (MULAN), have been reported to mediate ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of AKT, particularly phospho-AKT1 (T308/S473).10,11,12

TRIM36, a poorly characterized member of the tripartite motif (TRIM) protein, was reported to play a crucial role in acrosome reactions and fertilization.13,14 Although predominantly expressed in the testis, emerging evidence suggests that TRIM36 dysregulation contributes to various diseases. In an Indian familial study, homozygous deficiency of TRIM36 resulted in severe neural pore closure defect, also known as anencephaly.15 TRIM36 gene hypermethylation, which leads to the suppression of gene transcription, has been reported in lung cancer.16 TRIM36, as a tumor suppressor, promotes the degradation of β-catenin and FOXA2 in colorectal cancer.17,18 In addition, TRIM36 negatively regulates MAPK/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling, thereby inhibiting pancreatic cancer.19

Here, we demonstrate that TRIM36 is significantly upregulated in IPF patients and negatively associated with pulmonary fibrosis severity. Mechanistically, TRIM36 directly interacts with phospho-AKT1 (T308/S473) and mediates its K48-linked polyubiquitination at K268, thereby degrading AKT1 and inhibiting fibroblast activation. Importantly, TRIM36 overexpression ameliorates experimental pulmonary fibrosis in vivo. Collectively, our findings unveil a key mechanism by which TRIM36 suppresses fibroblast activation and pulmonary fibrosis through the degradation of phospho-AKT1 (T308/S473), which will help in developing effective approaches for IPF treatment.

Results

TRIM36 is significantly upregulated in IPF and negatively associated with disease severity

To identify dysregulated TRIMs in IPF, we analyzed TRIM expression profiles from the GSE47460 dataset. TRIM36, a poorly characterized RING-finger E3 ligase, showed significantly increased expression in IPF tissues compared to normal lung tissues (Figure 1A). This upregulation was also consistently observed in multiple independent IPF datasets, including GSE32537, GSE24206, GSE110147, and GSE53845 (Figures 1B and S1A). Next, based on GSE47460 datasets, we examined the correlation between TRIM36 expression and pulmonary fibrosis severity, which was evaluated by lung function. Intriguingly, TRIM36 was upregulated in mild IPF patients, who maintained relatively fine lung function parameters, including diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) and forced vital capacity (FVC) (Figure 1C). Moreover, the expression of TRIM36 positively correlated with DLCO and FVC values in IPF patients (Figure 1D). To further investigate TRIM36’s role in pulmonary fibrosis, we examined its expression in IPF patients and bleomycin (BLM)-induced fibrotic mouse lungs. immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis revealed elevated TRIM36 protein levels in both IPF and BLM-treated mouse lungs (Figures 1E–1G). Consistently, TRIM36 mRNA levels were significantly upregulated in BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis mice (Figure 1H). To identify the specific cell types exhibiting elevated TRIM36 expression, immunofluorescent staining assays were performed and revealed elevated TRIM36 protein levels in both IPF and BLM-treated mouse lungs. Notably, TRIM36 was expressed in IPF lung fibroblasts, as evidenced by the localization of TRIM36 and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) (Figures S1B and S1C). TRIM36 inhibits lung fibroblast proliferation, migration, and differentiation

Myofibroblasts, differentiated from fibroblasts, plays a pivotal role in IPF progression through the secretion of ECM. To investigate the function of TRIM36 in lung fibroblasts, we generated TRIM36-overexpressing Medical Research Council Cell Strain-5 (MRC5) and Institute for Medical Research-90 (IMR-90) human lung fibroblast cell lines (Figure 2A). The cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8), 5-Ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EdU), and Transwell assays were performed, and the results indicated that ectopic TRIM36 expression significantly inhibited the proliferation and migration of both MRC5 and IMR-90 cells (Figures 2B–2G). In addition, TRIM36 overexpression attenuated TGF-β1-induced fibroblast-to-myofibroblast differentiation, as demonstrated by the downregulation of myofibroblast markers, including Fibronectin 1, Collagen 1, and α-SMA (Figures 2H–2K). To investigate the role of TRIM36 in lung fibroblasts, primary human lung fibroblasts (PHLFs) were isolated and transfected with TRIM36 short hairpin RNA. TRIM36 depletion facilitated TGF-β1 induced myofibroblast differentiation, as evaluated by myofibroblast related proteins (Fibronectin 1, Collagen 1, and α-SMA) (Figures S2A). TRIM36 destabilizes AKT1

The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway plays a crucial role in pulmonary fibrosis pathogenesis. We found that ectopic TRIM36 expression reduced the abundance of AKT1, phospho-AKT1 (S473), and phospho-ERK1/2 in MRC5 cells (Figure S3A). Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays were performed and demonstrated specific interaction between TRIM36 and AKT1, but not with ERK1/2 (Figure S3B). We further confirmed the endogenous TRIM36-AKT1 interaction (Figure 3A). To identify the region responsible for the interaction, various deletion mutants of TRIM36 and AKT1 were generated. A time-course assay following cycloheximide (CHX) treatment, which intercept protein synthesis, showed that TRIM36 overexpression significantly shortened the half-life of endogenous AKT1, phospho-AKT1 (T308), and phospho-AKT1 (S473) in both MRC5 and IMR-90 cells (Figures 3D and 3E). Additionally, ectopic TRIM36 expression inhibited TGF-β1-induced phosphorylation of AKT1 at both T308 and S473 residues in MRC5 and IMR-90 cells (Figure 3F). Given the well-established role of PI3K/AKT signaling in insulin response,20 we next explored whether TRIM36 influenced insulin-stimulated AKT1 activation. Strikingly, ectopic TRIM36 expression also inhibited insulin-induced phosphorylation of AKT1 at both T308 and S473 residues (Figure S3C). These findings collectively indicate that TRIM36 interacts with AKT1 and destabilizes it.

TRIM36 promotes proteasomal degradation of AKT1 via K48-linked ubiquitination at lysine 268

Since TRIM36 interacts with AKT1 and destabilizes it, we hypothesized that TRIM36 mediates AKT1 ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation. Consistent with this, ectopic TRIM36 expression inhibited the expression of AKT1, phospho-AKT1 (T308), and phospho-AKT1 (S473) in MRC5 and IMR-90 cells, while the phenomenon was reversed by the proteasome inhibitor Z-Leu-Leu-Leu-CHO ( MG132) but not the lysosomal pathway inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) (Figures 4A and S4A). To further elucidate the mechanism, we generated TRIM36 inactivation mutants, which were constructed by either deleting the RING-finger domain or introducing point mutations (C33/36A).14 Notably, full-length TRIM36 but not its ΔRING-finger or C33/36A mutants promoted AKT1 polyubiquitination and degradation in a dose-dependent manner (Figures 4B, 4C, and S4B). To determine the ubiquitination mechanism, we performed a ubiquitination assay and found that TRIM36 mediated K48-linked polyubiquitination of AKT1 (Figure 4D). Based on the PhosphoSitePlus database, several potential ubiquitin residues (K268, K276, K284, K301, and K377) of AKT1 were identified, and corresponding lysine-to-arginine (K-to-R) mutants were generated using homologous recombination. Notably, although AKT1 wild type (WT) and other mutants were destabilized by TRIM36 in a dose-dependent manner, the K268R mutant, which is evolutionarily conserved, remained stable (Figures 4E and S4C). Consistently, TRIM36 promoted polyubiquitination of all mutants of AKT1 except K268R (Figure 4F). Collectively, these data indicate that TRIM36 promotes K48-linked ubiquitination of AKT1 at K268, leading to its proteasomal degradation.

TRIM36 preferentially ubiquitinates phospho-AKT1 (T308/S473)

Given the fact that several well-known E3 ubiquitin ligases of AKT, including BRCA1, CHIP, and MULAN, preferentially target phospho-AKT1 (T308/S473),10,11,12 we speculated that the phosphorylation of AKT1 is critical for its interaction with TRIM36. Indeed, the AKT1-TRIM36 interaction was significantly attenuated upon λ protein phosphatase treatment (Figures 5A and S5A), supporting the essential role of phosphorylation in mediating this interaction. Furthermore, MK2206, a selective AKT phosphorylation inhibitor, damaged the interaction of AKT1 and TRIM36 (Figures 5B and S5B). To further investigate the role of phosphorylation in the interaction between AKT1 and TRIM36, we constructed AKT1 phospho-mimetic (T308D/S473D) and phospho-deficient (T308A/S473A) mutants. Interestingly, TRIM36 destabilized AKT1 WT and its T308/S473D mutant in a dose-dependent manner but had no effect on the T308A/S473A mutant (Figure 5D). Consistent with this, TRIM36 mediated polyubiquitination of AKT1 WT and its T308/S473D but not T308/S473A mutant in vivo (Figure 5E). Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and GST pull-down assays further confirmed that TRIM36 preferentially interacts with AKT1 T308/S473D mutant (Figures 5F, 5G, and S5D). Moreover, the endogenous interaction between TRIM36 and phospho-AKT1 (S473) was validated in MRC5 and IMR-90 cells (Figure 5H). Collectively, these data indicate that the phosphorylation of AKT1 at T308 and S473 dramatically enhances its interaction with TRIM36.

TRIM36 inhibits lung fibroblast activation by suppressing AKT1 activity

Given that abnormal AKT signaling activation promotes lung fibroblast proliferation, migration, and differentiation, we examined whether TRIM36 inhibits lung fibroblast activation through AKT1 degradation. In MRC5 and IMR-90 cells, TRIM36 overexpression critically suppressed cell proliferation and migration, while reducing levels of AKT1, phospho-AKT1 (T308), phospho-AKT1 (S473), and phospho-GSK3β (S9), a canonical AKT substrate (Figures 6A–6E and S6A–S6G). Notably, this inhibitory effect was largely abolished when treated with MK2206 (an AKT phosphorylation inhibitor) but not SC79 (an AKT1 phosphorylation agonist) (Figures 6A–6E and S6A–S6O). To directly assess TRIM36’s AKT1-dependent mechanism, we co-expressed TRIM36 with either wild-type AKT1 or its phospho-deficient mutant (T308A/S473A). Ectopic AKT1 expression but not its inactivation mutant (T308/S473A) significantly damaged the inhibition of lung fibroblast activation mediated by TRIM36 (Figures 6F–6J and S6P–S6V). TRIM36 ameliorates experimental mouse pulmonary fibrosis

Due to the unavailability of lung fibroblast-specific adeno-associated viruses (AAVs), we employed AAV6 to knockdown TRIM36 in mouse lungs. We established a BLM-induced mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis to investigate TRIM36’s role in IPF pathogenesis in vivo (Figure S7A). AAV6-mediated TRIM36 silencing significantly exacerbated BLM-induced weight loss and further upregulated the expression of fibrotic proteins, including Fibronectin 1, Collagen 1, and α-SMA (Figures S7B and S7C). Additionally, lung hydroxyproline content and total cell counts in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were remarkably elevated in BLM-treated mice compared to saline group, while TRIM36 silencing further enhanced these fibrotic responses (Figures S7D and S7E). Histological analyses (Hematoxylin and Eosin [H&E] and Masson’s trichrome staining) and microcomputed tomography (Micro-CT) imaging demonstrated that TRIM36 depletion aggravated BLM-induced fibrotic changes (Figures S7F and S7G).

To verify whether TRIM36 exerts an anti-fibrotic role, we employed AAV6 to overexpress TRIM36 in mouse lungs (Figure 7A). AAV6-mediated TRIM36 overexpression significantly inhibited BLM-induced weight loss and the expression of fibrotic proteins, including Fibronectin 1, Collagen 1, and α-SMA (Figures 7B and 7C). Additionally, TRIM36 overexpression suppressed BLM-induced the elevation of lung hydroxyproline content and BALF (Figures 7D and 7E). Histological analyses (H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining) and Micro-CT imaging demonstrated that TRIM36 overexpression attenuated BLM-induced fibrotic changes (Figures 7F, 7G, and S7H). Given that MK2206 significantly inhibited lung fibroblast activation, we investigated its therapeutic potential in pulmonary fibrosis. Remarkably, intraperitoneal MK2206 administration potently suppressed AKT1 activation and exhibited prominent anti-fibrotic effects (Figures 7A–7G and S7H). Discussion

Pulmonary fibrosis represents the end-stage manifestation of various lung diseases and is associated with poor clinical prognosis.21,22 The ubiquitin-proteasome system plays a pivotal role in IPF pathogenesis, with multiple E3 ubiquitin ligases, including STIP1 homology and U-box containing protein 1 (STUB1), E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase NEDD4-like (Nedd4L), and F-box and WD repeat domain-containing protein 7 (Fbxw7), being implicated in fibrosis development.23,24,25 TRIM36, located at chromosome 5q22.3, is frequently deleted in multiple malignancies, including non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), colorectal cancer (CRC), and esophageal cancer (EC).13 Epigenetic silencing through hypermethylation and consequent TRIM36 downregulation have been observed in various cancers, including NSCLC, HCC, and breast cancer.16 Functionally, TRIM36 exhibits tumor-suppressive activity through targeted degradation of oncoproteins including FOXA2 and β-catenin.16,17,18 Our study provides compelling evidence that TRIM36 is upregulated in IPF and its expression negatively associated with disease severity. This suggests that TRIM36 upregulation in IPF may represent a protective response against fibrotic progression.

Lung fibroblasts are one of the principal effector cells in IPF pathogenesis. During physiological wound repair, fibroblasts migrate to alveolar injury sites in response to specific inflammatory factors.26,27 Upon stimulation, these fibroblasts undergo proliferation and differentiate into myofibroblasts, which play a critical role in secreting ECM, including Collagen and Fibronectin.28 While this reparative process typically resolves through fibroblast apoptosis, IPF is characterized by persistent fibroblast activation and aberrant ECM deposition. In this study, we demonstrated that TRIM36 overexpression effectively inhibits both lung fibroblast activation in vitro and BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis in vivo, suggesting that TRIM36 is a potent negative regulator of fibrotic processes.

The PI3K/AKT pathway plays a crucial role in regulating cell proliferation, survival, migration, and metabolism, and its dysregulation significantly contributes to the development of IPF.7 The activation of AKT signaling facilitates fibroblast proliferation and enhances Collagen secretion.29 In this study, we identified AKT1 as a previously unrecognized substrate of TRIM36 that undergoes TRIM36-dependent ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation. AKT1 activation requires sequential phosphorylation at Thr308 and Ser473 residues.30 Several E3 ubiquitin ligases, such as TRAF6, BRCA1, STUB1, and MULAN, have been reported to mediate AKT ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation10,11,12,31; most exhibit preference for phospho-AKT1 (T308/S473).32 We demonstrated that TRIM36 specifically interacts with phospho-AKT1 (T308/S473) and mediates its ubiquitination. Notably, unlike the known MUL1-mediated AKT1 ubiquitination at K284,33 we reveal that TRIM36 ubiquitinates AKT1 at K268. This represents a key report establishing the critical role of K268 in regulating AKT1 protein stability.

The therapeutic potential of AKT signaling inhibition spans multiple diseases, such as cancer and IPF. Several AKT inhibitors (e.g., omipalisib, PX-866, and LY294002) have been investigated for both cancer and IPF treatment.6 MK2206, an orally bioavailable allosteric AKT inhibitor, has shown therapeutic promise in asthma, glioblastoma, and various cancers.34,35 Preclinical studies demonstrate that MK2206 obviously improves atherosclerosis by suppressing vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation, motility, and inflammation.36 Clinical trials have supported its therapeutic potential in lymphoblastic lymphoma, with a phase II study reporting favorable efficacy and safety profiles.37 Despite these advances, MK2206’s application in IPF remains largely unexplored. Our study provides compelling evidence that this selective AKT inhibitor potently suppresses both lung fibroblast activation and BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that TRIM36 is upregulated in IPF and its expression is negatively associated with disease severity. Ectopic expression of TRIM36 significantly inhibits fibroblast activation in vitro. TRIM36 inhibits AKT1 signaling and attenuates pulmonary fibrosis through K48-linked polyubiquitination of phospho-AKT1 (T308/S473) and subsequent proteasomal degradation. These findings indicate that TRIM36-mediated AKT1 inhibition represents a promising therapeutic strategy for IPF.

Limitations of the study

This study demonstrates that TRIM36 functions as a suppressor of AKT signaling and exerts inhibitory effects on IPF. While AAV6-mediated TRIM36 upregulation in mouse models is a valid and widely accepted experimental approach, it lacks tissue-specific targeting. Therefore, genetically engineered mouse models will be essential to precisely elucidate the role of TRIM36 in IPF pathogenesis. Furthermore, we observed that TRIM36 inhibits EMT in A549 cells, which serve as a surrogate for AEC2s. Given that AEC2 dysfunction is a well-recognized key driver of IPF progression, future studies should focus on investigating the specific regulatory role of TRIM36 in AEC2s. In the BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis model, male mice exhibit greater sensitivity to BLM and develop more severe lung fibrosis. Therefore, male mice were exclusively used in the present study. This exclusive use of male mice represents a limitation, as it may restrict the generalizability of our research findings.

Experimental model and study participant details

Clinical human IPF specimens

All experiments using human samples were approved by the Henan Provincial Chest Hospital Medical Research Ethics Committee (No. 2021-03-04). Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects. The IPF samples are surgical remnants of lung biopsies or explants. Controls are normal histology tissue obtained from the disease-free margin of lung cancer resection specimens. Five IPF and normal lung tissues were used for IHC and immunofluorescent staining. After all the subjects were diagnosed according to the “Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (2022 Edition)”, they were randomly assigned to the experimental group and the control group using a random number table.

Cell culture

Human HEK293T, MRC5, and IMR-90 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Rockville, MD, USA). Cells were cultured in DMEM (HEK293T and PHLFs) or MEM (MRC5 and IMR-90) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Animals

All animal procedures were approved by and performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Henan Normal University (No.SMKX-2118BS1018). Male C57BL/6 mice (eight-week-old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China). Mice were maintained in an environment free of specific pathogens (22-26°C, 40-60% humidity, a 12-hour light cycle, free to eat food and drink water). The BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis mouse model was established as described previously.38 Briefly, the newly purchased mice were randomly grouped and acclimated to the housing environment for one week. BLM (1-1.5 U/kg) was administered via intratracheal injection. Three days later, mice were injected with AAV6 virus. Twenty-one days after BLM injection, mice were euthanized and lung tissues were collected for analysis.

Method details

Cell proliferation assay

The cell proliferation abilities were measured using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (BL1055B, Biosharp, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, indicated cells were seeded into 96-well plates (1-2×103 cells/well) based on the cell growth rate. At the specified time points, 10 μL CCK-8 reagent was added in 96-well plates. The optical density at 450 nm was measured using Microplate Reader (Thermo Scientific, USA). The EdU staining were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions (C10310-1, RIBOBIO, China). Briefly, indicated cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5-7×103 cells/well) and incubated with 10 μM EdU for 2 hours. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde, followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-10. Afterward, the Cells were incubated with the Apollo dye mixture in the dark for 30 minutes. Finally, the images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Cell migration assay

The cell migration capacity were measured by Transwell assays. Briefly, indicated cells were resuspended with serum-free medium and seeded into the upper chambers of 24-well Transwell plates at a density of 3-5×104 cells/well, with the seeding density adjusted based on the cell growth rate. The medium containing 10% FBS was placed in the bottom layer of the Transwell chambers. After incubation for 24-36 hours, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes and stained with crystal violet for 10 minutes. Finally, images of the migrated cells were captured by inverted microscope (Leica, Germany).

Western blotting

Indicated cells and tissues were collected and lysed with RIPA lysis buffers (P0013B, Beyotime Technology, Shanghai, China). Total proteins were separated via SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Then, the PVDF membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk (dissolved in PBST) at room temperature for 1 hour, followed by incubation with the indicated primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. After incubation, PVDF membranes were washed three times with PBST, and then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies, including goat anti-mouse (115-035-003, Jackson, USA) and goat anti-rabbit (111-035-003, Jackson, USA) second antibodies. Protein signals were visualized by ECL Chemiluminescence Detection Kit (PK10003, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Finally, images of the target protein bands were acquired by Odyssey Fc imaging system (Gene, Hong Kong, China) and protein expression intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

qRT-PCR and plasmids

Total RNA was extracted from samples using TRIzol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen, USA). Subsequently, reverse transcription was performed to synthesize complementary DNA using a cDNA synthesis kit (RRO36A, Takara, Japan). The Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed with SYBR Green qPCR Mix (PC3301, Aidlab, Beijing, China) using a Real-Time PCR device (Roche LightCycler 480 II, Germany). The relative expression levels of the tested genes were calculated using the classical 2-ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as a normalizer. All primers used in qRT-PCR assays are listed in Table S1 and were synthesized by Genecreate Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). For plasmids construction, the human TRIM36, AKT1, and ERK1/2 coding sequences were amplified from HEK293T cell-derived cDNA and cloned into the following vectors: pcDNA5-HA, pHAGE-CMV-MCS-PGK-3×HA, pHAGE-CMV-MCS-PGK-3×Flag, pET-32a(+)-His, and pGEX-4T-GST-His vectors (C117-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Mouse TRIM36 coding sequences were amplified from mouse lung tissue-derived cDNA and cloned into pAAV-EF1a vectors. Mutations in TRIM36, ubiquitin, and AKT1 were introduced by homologous recombination or overlap extension PCR (C216-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). All primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Table S1 and were synthesized by Genecreate Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Protein half-life assays

Cells expressing the indicated vectors were seeded in 6-well plates. When cells reached approximately 70% confluence, the protein synthesis inhibitor CHX (HY-12320, MedChemExpress, USA) was added to the cells and incubated for the specified durations. Cells were then harvested and analyzed by Western blotting with the indicated antibodies.

Plasmid transfection

When cells reached approximately 80% confluence, they were transfected with the indicated plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) or PEI (40815ES03, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For lentivirus package, overexpression plasmid and packaging plasmids were cotransfected into HEK293T cells using PEI according to the manufacturer’s protocol. 6 hours post-transfection, the old DMEM medium was replaced with fresh medium. At 24–48 hours post-transfection, the culture medium containing the virus was collected. For stable transfection, the collected lentiviruses were used to infect the indicated cells for 48 hours. The infected cells were then treated with 5 μg/mL puromycin (ST551, Beyotime Biotech, Shanghai, China) to select for stable transfection cells.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

Immunohistochemistry was performed as previously described.41,42 Briefly, lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4°C overnight, then washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, lung tissues were was dehydrated using an alcohol gradient and embedded in paraffin following standard protocols. Paraffin-embedded lung tissues were sectioned into 4-7 μm slices and mounted on the glass slides. The sections were dried in an oven at 60°C for 2 hours. For immunochemistry, lung sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated using a graded alcohol series. After antigen retrieval with sodium citrate buffer, lung sections were treated with 3% H2O2 to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Then, lung sections were blocked with QuickBlock™ Blocking Buffer (P0260, Beyotime Biotech, Shanghai, China) and incubated with the anti-TRIM36 primary antibody (1:200, Proteintech, Cat:25913-1-AP, RRID: AB_2880293) at 4 °C overnight. Following this, sections were incubated with HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (P0615, Beyotime Biotech, Shanghai, China), and the chromogenic reaction was performed using DAB substrate (P0202, Beyotime Biotech, Shanghai, China). Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Imaging was performed using a Zeiss microscope (Zeiss, Germany). For immunofluorescence, lung sections were washed and blocked as described in the immunohistochemistry protocol. Then, lung sections were incubated with primary antibodies against TRIM36 (1:200, Proteintech, Cat:25913-1-AP, RRID: AB_2880293) and α-SMA (1:200, Proteintech, Cat: 67735-1-Ig, RRID: AB_2918504) at 4 °C overnight. After washing, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology; Cat. No.: 4416) and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology; Cat. No.: 8890) secondary antibodies at 37°C for 1 hour. Moreover, sections were stained with DAPI at room temperature for 10 minutes and mounted with antifade mounting medium (P0126, Beyotime Biotech, Shanghai, China). The images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Germany) (Zeiss, Germany).

Immunoprecipitation and ubiquitination assays

Immunoprecipitation (IP) were performed as previously described.43 In brief, indicated cells were lysed in RIPA buffer [50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 0.25 mM EDTA, 1% Nonidet P-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 10 μg/ml aprotinin, 10 μg/ml leupeptin, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride]. After centrifugation at 12000 rpm for 10 minutes, the supernatant was collected and incubated with overnight at 4°C with magnetic beads—either Protein A/G beads (B23201, Selleck, USA) conjugated with the indicated antibodies, or anti-Flag/HA beads (B26102/B26202, Selleck, USA). Then, the magnetic beads were washed three times with Wash buffer [20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1% Nonidet P-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 10 μg/ml aprotinin, 10 μg/ml leupeptin, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride]. To elute the immunocomplexes, magnetic beads were incubated in RIPA buffer and boiled for 10 minutes. Immunocomplexes were analyzed by immunoblotting using the indicated antibodies. Ubiquitination assays were performed as previously described.41,44 Briefly, when HEK293T cells reached 80% confluence, they were transfected with the indicated plasmids using PEI. At 48 hours post-transfection, cells were treated with 20 μM MG132 for 5 hours. Cells were then harvested, lysed in RIPA buffer, and boiled for 5 minutes to denature proteins. IP and immunoblotting analyses were performed as described above.

GST pull-down assays

The prokaryotic protein expression protocol was performed as previously described.42 Briefly, Rosetta E. coli cells were transformed with indicated plasmids and cultured in LB medium. When the optical density at 600 nm reached 0.8-1.0, isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added to the LB medium to induce proteins expression. Rosetta E. coli cells were then lysed in RIPA buffer and incubated with Ni-charged magnetic beads for protein purification. For the interaction assay, AKT1 or its mutant proteins were incubated with either GST or GST-TRIM36 fusion protein bound to GST-beads. The complexes were collected and analyzed by immunoblotting using the indicated antibodies.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The independent IPF microarray datasets (GSE47460, GSE32537, GSE110147, GSE53845, and GSE24206) were obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). The GSE47460 dataset comprises two platforms: GPL6480 and GPL14550. To eliminate the batch effect, the gene expression data from GPL6480 and GPL14550 were integrated using bioinformatics approaches with the SVA R package.45 TRIMs with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significantly dysregulated. p-values were calculated using the Mann-Whitney test. Other data were presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using two-tailed Student's t-tests (between two groups) and one-way ANOVA (between three or more groups), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001).
