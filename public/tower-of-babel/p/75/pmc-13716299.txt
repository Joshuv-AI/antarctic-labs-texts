Comparative effectiveness of Orsiro, Xience, Synergy, and Resolute stent in patients with acute myocardial infarction undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention: a nationwide registry study
Authors: Hyun Woong Park, Yong Kyun Kim, Seok-Woo Seong, Jae-Hwan Lee, Mi Joo Kim, Pil Sang Song, Seon-Ah Jin, Kye Taek Ahn, Youngkeun Ahn, Sang Hyun Park, Jin-Yong Hwang, Chang-Hwan Yoon, Jin-Ok Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHead-to-head comparisons of contemporary drug-eluting stents (DES) in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remain limited. We sought to compare the long-term clinical outcomes of four widely used DES platforms: Orsiro, Xience, Synergy, and Resolute.MethodsFrom the nationwide prospective Korea acute myocardial infarction registry-V, we identified 9284 patients with AMI undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention with one of the four study devices (Orsiro, n = 1634; Xience, n = 2576; Synergy, n = 2811; Resolute, n = 2263). The primary endpoint was target lesion revascularization (TLR). Inverse probability of treatment weighting was performed to adjust for baseline differences.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 723 days, the cumulative incidence of TLR differed significantly among groups. In IPTW-adjusted analyses, compared with the Orsiro group (reference), the risk of TLR was significantly higher in the Synergy [hazard ratio: 2.30; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.20–4.41, P = 0.013] and Resolute groups (hazard ratio: 2.30, 95% CI: 1.18–4.47, P = 0.014). The Xience group showed a comparable risk of TLR to Orsiro (hazard ratio: 1.40, 95% CI: 0.69–2.82, P = 0.346). Target vessel revascularization followed a similar pattern, favoring Orsiro. However, there were no significant differences in composite major adverse cardiovascular events or all-cause mortality among the four groups.ConclusionIn this large-scale, real-world AMI cohort, the ultrathin-strut, biodegradable-polymer Orsiro stent was associated with a lower risk of repeat revascularization compared with the Synergy and Resolute stents. Xience demonstrated comparable outcomes to Orsiro. Stent selection remains a crucial determinant of vessel-level outcomes in patients with AMI.

Introduction

Drug-eluting stents (DES) have become the standard of care for percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) [1]. Compared with bare-metal stents, early generation DES substantially reduced restenosis and the need for repeat revascularization, but at the cost of concerns regarding late and very late stent thrombosis, delayed endothelialization, and chronic vascular inflammation related to durable-polymer coatings [2–4]. These safety issues prompted the development of newer-generation DES that incorporate thinner struts, more biocompatible or biodegradable polymers, and refined antiproliferative drug kinetics to optimize the balance between efficacy and safety [2].

In particular, AMI is characterized by high thrombus burden, intense inflammatory activity, and dynamic plaque morphology [5], all of which may accentuate device-specific differences in strut thickness, polymer behavior, and healing response.

Despite substantial progress in DES technology, direct, head-to-head comparisons among newer DES stents in real-world AMI patients remain limited. Most prior studies have focused on pairwise comparisons (e.g. Orsiro vs. Xience, Synergy vs. Promus, or Resolute vs. other zotarolimus-eluting stents) or have enrolled mixed populations in which AMI constituted only a subset of the overall cohort [6–8]. Consequently, clinicians lack comprehensive comparative-effectiveness data on how these four commonly used DES platforms perform against each other within a dedicated AMI registry under routine clinical practice conditions.

Given the prognostic importance of both acute procedural success and long-term stent performance in AMI, understanding device-specific differences in restenosis, revascularization, and thrombotic risk is crucial for optimizing stent selection.

Therefore, in this nationwide AMI cohort, we sought to compare the long-term clinical outcomes associated with four contemporary DES platforms – Orsiro, Xience, Synergy, and Resolute.

Methods

Study population (Fig. 1)

This was an observational cohort study based on a nationwide, multicenter registry of patients with AMI undergoing PCI (Korea acute myocardial infarction registry-V; 1 January 2016–30 June 2020; 33 centers in Korea). During the study period, consecutive patients enrolled in the registry who presented with AMI were screened. Both stent thrombosis-segment elevation AMI and non-stent thrombosis-segment elevation AMI were eligible, and the diagnosis of AMI was established according to contemporary guideline criteria, including typical symptoms, electrocardiographic changes, and elevation of cardiac biomarkers. The overall study design and registry protocol have been described previously [9] and were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards of all participating centers, and the need for written informed consent was obtained according to local regulations.

Study flow diagram. The flow chart depicts patient selection for the present analysis. Patients who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention with implantation of one of four DES (Orsiro, Xience, Synergy, or Resolute) were screened. After applying prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria, the final analytic cohort was defined and categorized by stent type. DES, drug-eluting stents.

For the present analysis, a total of 13 150 patients with AMI were available in the registry during the study period. We first excluded patients who underwent index PCI with DES platforms other than the four study devices (Orsiro, Xience, Synergy, or Resolute), leaving 10 672 patients treated with one of the four contemporary DES platforms. Patients were classified according to the DES implanted in the infarct-related artery at the index procedure. To allow clear attribution of outcomes to a single stent platform, patients who received a mixture of greater than or equal to two of the study DES types during the index PCI were excluded. We further excluded patients with missing information on stent type, key baseline covariates required for propensity score estimation, or clinical outcomes, as well as those lost to follow-up immediately after discharge. The final propensity score cohort consisted of 9284 patients who underwent PCI with one of the four study DES platforms.

Stent groups

All four DES platforms analyzed in this study are widely used new-generation coronary stents that were commercially available in Korea during the study period. Orsiro (Biotronik, Bülach, Switzerland) is an ultrathin-strut (60–80 μm) cobalt–chromium sirolimus-eluting stent with a biodegradable-polymer coating. Xience (Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, California, USA) is a thin-strut, durable-polymer everolimus-eluting stent that has served as a benchmark device in multiple pivotal trials. Synergy (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, Massachusetts, USA) is a thin-strut platinum–chromium stent with an abluminal, biodegradable everolimus-eluting polymer, and Resolute (Medtronic, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) is a zotarolimus-eluting stent with a durable, biocompatible polymer designed for sustained drug release.

Patients were categorized into four groups according to the DES implanted in the infarct-related artery: Orsiro, Xience, Synergy, and Resolute. The choice of stent type, PCI strategy (e.g. multivessel PCI and bifurcation technique), and adjunctive pharmacotherapy was left to the discretion of the treating interventional cardiologist and institutional practice, in line with contemporary AMI guidelines.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was target lesion revascularization (TLR). TLR was defined as any repeat, clinically indicated PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting of the index lesion, including the stent and its 5-mm proximal and distal segments, driven by symptoms, objective ischemia, or documented restenosis on angiography.

Secondary outcomes included: cardiac death: any death because of cardiovascular disease; stent thrombosis: definite or probable stent thrombosis defined according to the Academic Research Consortium criteria, occurring in the territory of the infarct-related artery; target vessel revascularization (TVR): any repeat revascularization (PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting) of the target vessel, irrespective of lesion segment; and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE): cardiac death, recurrent myocardial infarction, and stroke.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are summarized as counts (percentages) and were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range), according to their distribution, and were compared using Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Event-free survival for each clinical endpoint was estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods, and differences between groups were compared with the log-rank test. Cumulative incidence curves for TLR, TVR, cardiac death, stent thrombosis, and MACE were plotted over the entire follow-up period, and global P values for group differences were reported.

To account for baseline imbalances in patient and lesion characteristics across the four DES groups, we constructed propensity score-based weights. A multinomial logistic regression model was used to estimate the probability of receiving each DES type, including all clinically relevant baseline variables (demographics, cardiovascular risk factors, hemodynamic status, laboratory values, infarct-related artery, lesion complexity, and discharge medications) as covariates. Specifically, the propensity score model included age, BMI, left ventricular ejection fraction, number of stents, mean stent diameter, total stent length, sex, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, current smoking, history of myocardial infarction, angina, heart failure, or stroke, final diagnosis (stent thrombosis-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) or Non-stent thrombosis-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI)), discharge use of aspirin, clopidogrel, potent P2Y12 inhibitors (ticagrelor or prasugrel), calcium channel blockers, beta-blockers, statins, renin–angiotensin system blockers, optical coherence tomography, intravascular ultrasound use, multivessel PCI, left main or left anterior descending artery as the infarct-related artery, ACC/AHA lesion type B2/C, stent count, stent diameter, and stent length. From these propensity scores, we derived inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) using stabilized weights, with truncation at the 1st and 99th percentiles to reduce the influence of extreme weights. Balance was evaluated before weighting and after application of stabilized IPTW with truncation at the 1st–99th percentiles. An absolute standardized mean difference (SMD) less than 0.10 was considered indicative of adequate balance, and Love plots were generated to visually inspect covariate balance across the stent groups (Supplementary Figure S1, Supplemental digital content 1, https://links.lww.com/MCA/A811).

Weighted Cox proportional hazards models with robust (sandwich) standard errors were then used to estimate hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each outcome. Global P values from the weighted Cox models were reported to assess overall differences among the four DES. Covariate balance across the four stent groups was assessed using absolute SMDs, summarized as the maximum SMD across all pairwise treatment comparisons. As a sensitivity analysis, IPTW-adjusted Cox proportional hazards models were fitted with robust sandwich variance estimators clustered by participating center. Calendar year derived from the index date available in the analytic dataset was, in addition, included as a covariate to account for temporal trends during the study period.

In addition, pairwise comparisons between stent types were performed using log-rank tests and Cox models, with P values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method. All analyses were performed in R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using standard packages (e.g. survival and rms).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among 9284 patients presenting with AMI and treated with PCI, four contemporary DES – Orsiro group (n = 1634), Xience group (n = 2576), Synergy group (n = 2811), and Resolute group (n = 2263) – were compared (Fig. 1). In the unweighted cohort (n = 9284), baseline characteristics were generally comparable across stent types. Significant between-group differences were observed for final diagnosis and dyslipidemia prevalence (P = 0.001 and P = 0.040, respectively). Several biomarkers differed modestly, including creatine kinase-myocardial band, troponin I, and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (P = 0.005, P = 0.001, and P = 0.016). Procedural characteristics varied, with differences in mean stent diameter and total stent length (P < 0.001 and P = 0.003). Discharge antiplatelet regimens also differed across groups (clopidogrel and potent P2Y12 inhibitor use; both P < 0.001) (Table 1). After IPTW, distributions across all major clinical and procedural variables were effectively harmonized, indicating appropriate reduction of confounding (Supplementary Table S1, Supplemental digital content 1, https://links.lww.com/MCA/A811).

Clinical outcomes

In unweighted analyses (Table 2; Fig. 2), vessel-related endpoints differed significantly by stent type. Compared with Orsiro, Synergy and Resolute were associated with higher risks of TLR (Synergy: hazard ratio: 2.39, 95% CI: 1.36–4.20, P = 0.003; Resolute: hazard ratio: 2.27, 95% CI: 1.27–4.06, P = 0.006) and TVR (Synergy: hazard ratio: 1.90, 95% CI: 1.22–2.98, P = 0.005; Resolute: hazard ratio: 1.74, 95% CI: 1.09–2.79, P = 0.020), whereas Xience was not significantly different from Orsiro for these outcomes. Stent thrombosis was higher with Synergy and Resolute versus Orsiro, although absolute event rates were low. MACE did not differ materially across stent types. In unweighted analyses, Xience showed lower bleeding and mortality versus Orsiro, while myocardial infarction and stroke were similar among groups.

After IPTW weighting with robust standard errors and additional adjustment for high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (Table 2; Fig. 3), the TLR and TVR differences persisted, with higher TLR for Synergy and Resolute versus Orsiro (both hazard ratio: 2.30, P = 0.013 and P = 0.014, respectively) and higher TVR (Synergy: hazard ratio: 2.03, P = 0.009; Resolute: hazard ratio: 1.98, P = 0.015). Repeat revascularization also remained higher with Synergy and Resolute (hazard ratio: 1.45, P = 0.046; and hazard ratio: 1.52, P = 0.026). In contrast, the major bleeding and mortality differences were attenuated and no longer statistically significant after weighting (e.g. all-cause death for Xience vs. Orsiro: hazard ratio: 0.73, P = 0.082; bleeding: hazard ratio: 0.72, P = 0.126). Stent thrombosis showed a similar direction but did not reach conventional statistical significance after weighting (Synergy: hazard ratio: 7.28, P = 0.056; Resolute: hazard ratio: 5.66, P = 0.100).

In IPTW-weighted subgroup analyses, the lower risk of TLR with Orsiro compared with Synergy and Resolute was generally consistent across major clinical and angiographic subgroups, including older age, sex, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, STEMI versus NSTEMI, long stent length, complex (B2/C) lesions, and LM/LAD as the infarct-related artery (Supplementary Table S2, Supplemental digital content 1, https://links.lww.com/MCA/A811). A nominal interaction was observed for hypertension in the Orsiro-versus-Resolute comparison (P for interaction = 0.035).

In sensitivity analyses using IPTW-adjusted Cox models with center-clustered robust standard errors and calendar-year adjustment, the main findings for repeat revascularization were consistent with the primary analysis. Compared with Orsiro, Synergy and Resolute remained associated with higher risks of TLR and TVR, whereas Xience showed no significant difference. No significant global differences were observed for cardiac death or MACE (Supplementary Table S3, Supplemental digital content 1, https://links.lww.com/MCA/A811).

Discussion

In this large, nationwide cohort of 9284 patients presenting with AMI and treated with PCI, we observed consistent and clinically relevant differences in long-term outcomes among four widely used contemporary new-generation DES platforms. By synthesizing results from unweighted analyses and IPTW, we found that Orsiro was associated with the most favorable vessel-related profile, characterized by the lowest risks of TLR and TVR. In contrast, Synergy and Resolute were consistently linked to a higher burden of repeat revascularization, whereas Xience demonstrated outcomes broadly comparable to Orsiro in our cohort.

Mechanistic considerations and device-level interpretation

The link between stent performance and device design is supported by mechanistic data on both strut thickness and polymer characteristics. In an experimental model, Kolandaivelu et al. [10] showed that thinner-strut platforms are substantially less thrombogenic than thicker-strut designs under high-risk flow conditions, highlighting reduced flow disturbance and platelet activation as key mechanisms.

Similarly, Ijichi et al. [11] demonstrated in a porcine coronary model that biodegradable-polymer DES exhibit less fibrin deposition and faster endothelial coverage than durable-polymer stents, indicating attenuated chronic inflammation and more favorable healing. Human autopsy data from Joner et al. [12] further linked durable-polymer first-generation DES to delayed endothelialization, persistent fibrin, and late stent thrombosis.

From early to newer-generation devices, DES have progressively improved both safety and efficacy compared with bare-metal stents in patients with AMI, particularly those undergoing PCI for myocardial infarction [13–15]. Newer-generation DES that combine thin-strut platforms with more biocompatible durable-polymer coatings have been associated with further reductions in repeat revascularization and stent thrombosis in patients undergoing PCI for AMI, as compared with early generation, thicker-strut durable-polymer DES [14]. Newer-generation DES with thinner struts and biodegradable or highly biocompatible polymer coatings were specifically designed to lessen thrombogenicity and vascular injury, facilitate endothelialization, and thereby improve clinical outcomes [10].

Among contemporary DES, several platforms have been widely adopted and extensively investigated. Xience, a durable-polymer everolimus-eluting stent, has served as a benchmark device in numerous pivotal randomized trials and registries, with robust evidence supporting its long-term safety and effectiveness across a broad spectrum of coronary artery disease, including left main and complex lesions [16,17]. More recently, Orsiro, an ultrathin-strut, biodegradable-polymer sirolimus-eluting stent, was engineered to further enhance vascular healing by minimizing strut thickness and limiting long-term polymer exposure [18]. Randomized trials such as BIOSCIENCE, BIOFLOW V, and BIOSTEMI have reported favorable outcomes with Orsiro compared with durable-polymer everolimus-eluting stents, particularly with respect to target lesion failure and stent thrombosis, including in high-risk AMI populations [8,18,19].

Synergy and Resolute represent additional widely used new-generation DES platforms. Synergy employs a thin-strut platinum–chromium backbone with an abluminal, biodegradable everolimus-eluting polymer, whereas Resolute utilizes a zotarolimus-eluting, durable-polymer platform designed to provide sustained drug release and improved biocompatibility [20,21]. Both stents have demonstrated noninferiority to earlier everolimus-eluting devices in selected trial populations; however, comparative performance across different DES families may vary depending on clinical setting, lesion complexity, and follow-up duration [7,17].

Interpretation of device-specific outcomes and clinical implications

The advantage of Orsiro in vessel-specific endpoints is biologically plausible and concordant with mechanistic insights from DES technology. Orsiro combines an ultrathin-strut cobalt–chromium backbone with a biodegradable polymer that is resorbed after drug release, thereby limiting chronic polymer exposure. This configuration is thought to attenuate local inflammation, facilitate more rapid and homogeneous endothelial coverage, and reduce neointimal hyperplasia. Prior randomized trials and registry analyses have suggested that ultrathin-strut, biodegradable-polymer sirolimus-eluting stents are associated with lower rates of restenosis and late thrombotic events compared with thicker-strut, durable-polymer devices [8,18,19]. The fact that the superiority of Orsiro in TLR and TVR persisted across multiple analytic strategies in the present study supports the interpretation that these findings reflect intrinsic device-level properties rather than residual confounding alone.

The observation that Xience was also associated with a lower risk of TLR and TVR requires a nuanced interpretation, particularly given that Xience has historically served as the benchmark DES in multiple pivotal trials and large registries and is widely regarded as one of the safest and most effective stents in contemporary practice [16,22]. Xience is a durable-polymer everolimus-eluting stent with an extensive safety track record, including robust data on late and very late outcomes [16,22]. Its stable polymer chemistry, predictable drug-release kinetics, and the high degree of operator familiarity may contribute to a lower incidence of catastrophic adverse events such as fatal stent thrombosis or myocardial infarction, even if the need for repeat revascularization is not minimized to the same extent as with Orsiro.

By contrast, the higher TLR risk observed with Synergy and Resolute in our AMI cohort may reflect the interaction between device design and the thrombus-rich, highly inflammatory milieu of AMI lesions [10]. Although Synergy employs a biodegradable polymer, its rapid resorption (3–4 months) contrasts with the prolonged degradation of Orsiro (12–24 months); this rapid dissolution might be less favorable for controlling the intense, protracted inflammation inherent to AMI. Furthermore, the strut thickness of Synergy (74 μm), while thin, remains greater than the ultrathin Orsiro profile (60 μm), potentially exacerbating flow disturbance in thrombus-laden lesions [11,23]. Resolute, which incorporates a thicker-strut platform with a durable polymer, may be even more susceptible to delayed healing, persistent inflammation, and neoatherosclerosis in this setting [12,23]. In such high-risk environments, relatively subtle differences in strut geometry, polymer behavior, and implantation technique can be amplified and manifest as clinically meaningful differences in TLR and stent thrombosis across DES platforms [10,24].

Importantly, the present findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating broad clinical superiority of one DES platform over another. The observed differences were mainly driven by repeat revascularization outcomes, particularly TLR and TVR, whereas mortality and overall MACE did not differ significantly after adjustment. Moreover, repeat revascularization may be affected by follow-up intensity, angiographic surveillance practice, and physician decision-making. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as suggesting potential differences in repeat revascularization outcomes rather than definitive superiority across hard clinical endpoints. This suggests a divergence between lesion-level and patient-level risk. On the one hand, vessel-related outcomes appear sensitive to stent engineering, polymer behavior, and local vessel biology. On the other hand, improvements in guideline-directed medical therapy, advances in PCI techniques, and standardized AMI care pathways may attenuate the downstream impact of stent-specific differences on composite outcomes that integrate death and recurrent myocardial infarction [25]. In other words, contemporary AMI management may buffer the influence of device selection on global cardiovascular risk, even while stent choice remains a key determinant of local vessel durability. Although Synergy and Resolute showed higher hazard estimates for stent thrombosis in the sensitivity analysis, the total number of stent thrombosis events was small. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be regarded as definitive evidence of superiority for stent thrombosis prevention.

From a clinical perspective, the observed heterogeneity in TLR, TVR, and stent thrombosis is far from trivial. Patients with complex coronary anatomy, high thrombus burden, diffuse disease, or anticipated long life expectancy may derive substantial benefit from stents that minimize long-term risks of repeat revascularization and thrombosis. The consistent pattern favoring Orsiro across all analytic frameworks in our study suggests that its ultrathin-strut, biodegradable-polymer design may confer a durable advantage in such high-risk scenarios, in line with prior randomized data on ultrathin-strut, biodegradable-polymer sirolimus-eluting stents.

Limitations

Several limitations merit consideration. First, the observational nature of this registry-based analysis inherently introduces the possibility of residual confounding, despite the use of extensive multivariable adjustment and IPTW. Unmeasured factors such as detailed lesion morphology and precise stent deployment techniques were not captured and may have influenced outcomes. Second, we did not have systematic quantitative coronary angiography or intravascular imaging data to directly evaluate mechanisms such as stent underexpansion, malapposition, or neoatherosclerosis, which could differentially affect stent platforms. Third, because stent selection was determined by the treating operator and participating center rather than random assignment, unmeasured factors such as operator preference, center volume, stent availability, reimbursement policy, lesion morphology, implantation technique, postdilatation strategy, and dual antiplatelet therapy duration may have influenced both stent choice and clinical outcomes. Although IPTW adjustment was used to balance measured covariates, residual confounding by unmeasured procedural factors cannot be excluded. Also, although the sensitivity analysis accounted for center-level clustering and calendar-year trends, residual confounding by unmeasured center-, operator-, lesion-, and procedure-related factors cannot be excluded. Fourth, given the relatively low absolute event rates for stent thrombosis and TLR in this contemporary DES era, the study may have been underpowered to detect modest differences in rare safety endpoints such as very late stent thrombosis. Fifth, because all patients were enrolled in Korean centers, generalizability to other ethnic populations or health care systems may be limited.

Finally, differences in background pharmacotherapy – including P2Y12 inhibitor type, treatment adherence, and duration of dual antiplatelet therapy – may have modulated late thrombotic risk and were not fully accounted for in the current analysis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in this large, real-world AMI cohort treated with contemporary DES, Orsiro was associated with a lower risk of repeat revascularization, particularly TLR and TVR, compared with Synergy and Resolute across multiple analytic approaches, whereas Xience showed outcomes comparable to Orsiro. However, no significant differences were observed in mortality or overall MACE, indicating that the observed differences were mainly driven by vessel-related repeat revascularization rather than hard clinical endpoints. Although stent thrombosis events were numerically less frequent in the Orsiro group, the small number of events and limited statistical power preclude any definitive conclusion regarding superiority for stent thrombosis prevention. These findings support the potential importance of individualized DES selection in AMI PCI, while emphasizing the need for further adequately powered studies to confirm whether differences in repeat revascularization translate into clinically meaningful long-term benefits.
